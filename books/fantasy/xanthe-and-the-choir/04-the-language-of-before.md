## 4. The Language of Before

The child did not sleep that first night. Neither did Petra.

They had put her in the loft above the drying room, on Maren's spare pallet among bundles of lavender and yarrow that perfumed the dark like a memory of summer. From below, Petra could hear her moving — not restlessly, but deliberately, as though she were taking inventory of the dark: a hand trailing along the rafters, a knee settling, breath measured out like something rationed.

Petra lay in her own narrow bed and listened, and thought about names.

It was a hedge-witch's habit, thinking in names. Maren said everything under the moon had one, and half of witchcraft was simply learning to say it correctly. But this child had no name they knew of, and no words they knew of either, and she had arrived at their door carrying a scrap of royal purple and a burn scar shaped like a crown pressed into her palm — and Maren had dropped a full jar of valerian at the sound of her voice, which was not a thing Maren did. Maren had not dropped anything in thirty years of practice. Her hands were famous for it.

So Petra lay awake and tried the language on in her head, syllable by syllable, the way you'd test ice before trusting it with your weight. She'd caught perhaps a dozen of the child's words since dusk. They didn't sound like anything from the peddler songs or the few tattered stories of the outer world that survived inside the stones. They sounded older than that. They sounded like the river sounded if the river could decide to be a word instead.

Near dawn, she gave up on sleep and climbed to the loft.

The child was sitting up, watching her come through the hatch without surprise, as if she'd been waiting to be found. In the grey light her eyes were an unsettling gold, the color of the last honey in the jar, and her mud-caked hair had dried into a wild pale thicket. She looked, Petra thought, like something the wind had planted.

"I'm going to call you Xanthe," Petra said. "For your hair. It means yellow-gold, where I'm from. Or near enough." She hesitated, then added, "You don't have to keep it."

The child considered this with a gravity far too old for her thin face. Then she touched her own hair, looked at her fingers as though checking the color against some inner standard, and said, in careful valley-tongue, learned apparently in a single evening from listening through a floorboard:

"Xanthe."

Not *yes*. Not *that will do*. Just the name itself, spoken back like a stone set down on a boundary line — *this is now true*.

Petra felt the word land somewhere beneath her ribs. Later, much later, she would understand why. But that morning she only laughed, surprised by the sound of her own laugh in the tense house, and Xanthe's mouth twitched at one corner, almost a smile, quickly put away again like a coin someone might steal.

Downstairs, Maren was already grinding comfrey, and pretending she hadn't been standing at the foot of the ladder listening.

* * *

For three days they barely left the kitchen.

Maren had declared it would rain until the equinox, and it did — a soft, relentless autumn rain that turned the herb garden silver and made the whole valley smell of wet stone and coming winter. Inside, while the fire hissed and spat, Petra spread sheets of butcher's paper across the table and began the work that would define the rest of her life, though she didn't know it yet.

She called it mapping. Maren called it madness. Both of them kept doing it anyway.

Here is what Petra had, to start: a child who spoke a language no book in the valley named, a handful of words caught at random, and a suspicion she couldn't yet shape into sense. So she did what she always did when the world wouldn't explain itself. She made a chart.

Across the top of the first sheet she wrote the child's sounds as best she could render them, using the peddler's phonetics Maren had taught her for reading foreign herb-labels. Down the side she wrote what each word seemed to *do*. And it took less than a day for the pattern to emerge — the pattern that made the hair on Petra's arms stand up and refuse to lie down again.

Because the words didn't describe things. The words *did* things.

Take the word Xanthe used for the kettle — four syllables, liquid, rising at the end like a question that answered itself. When she said it, pointing, meaning nothing more than *that thing there*, the water in the kettle grew hot. Not warm. Hot, fast, steaming, though the fire hadn't been fed in an hour. Xanthe had frowned at the kettle, annoyed, and said something shorter and sharper, and the water cooled.

"It's not her word for kettle," Petra whispered, ink on her fingers, heart hammering. "Maren. It isn't a word *for* anything. It just — happens."

"Say it again," Maren said quietly, and did not look up from her mortar.

Xanthe shook her head. Firmly. She folded her hands in her lap and became very interested in the floorboards, and something in the set of her small shoulders made Petra think of a door being closed, and locked, and the key swallowed.

That was the other thing Petra was beginning to map, the column she kept in the margin because she didn't know what else to do with it: how rarely the child spoke. A girl her age should have been a fountain of chatter, especially a girl newly safe, newly fed, newly named. Instead Xanthe spent whole hours silent, communicating in gestures, in expressions, in pointed looks of astonishing precision. When she did speak, it was as though each word were drawn from a purse she guarded with her life.

"She's rationing," Maren said on the second night, after Xanthe had gone up to the loft. The old woman sat looking into the fire, and her face in its light was older than Petra had ever seen it. "Rationing her words. Like a sailor rationing water." She rubbed her eyes. "I've seen hoarding before, girl. Greed, fear, spite — those I know. This is different. This is someone who knows exactly what each word costs, down to the last drop."

"Costs *what*?"

"That," said Maren, "is the question I've been avoiding since she knocked on our door."

* * *

On the fourth day, the goat died.

Or nearly. Nettle was the eldest of Maren's three goats, a gray-jawed, ill-tempered creature of twelve winters who had survived colic, foxes, and one memorable attempt by Petra at age seven to teach her to fetch. That morning they found her down in the pen, sides heaving in shallow fluttering breaths, gums white as chalk. Poison-plant, most likely; she'd never been able to resist the milkweed that crept in from the wood's edge, and the ward-stones that stopped magic cold also stopped nothing so ordinary as an old goat's bad judgment.

"There's nothing to be done," Maren said, kneeling in the straw. Her voice was flat, professional, and underneath it, grieved. "Her gut's turned. Even outside the stones I doubt I could—" She stopped. Outside the stones. Everything came back to that now. Everything came back to the line they lived behind. "She's old, Petra. Let her go comfortable."

But Petra was watching Xanthe.

The child stood at the fence rail, motionless, her golden eyes fixed on the dying animal with an intensity that seemed to bend the rainy air around her. Petra saw her lips move once — testing something, tasting it. Saw her glance sideways, quick as a sparrow, at Maren. At Petra. At the house, as if measuring the distance to witnesses.

Then Xanthe climbed the fence, dropped into the straw, and knelt by the goat's head.

"No," Maren started, "child, she's past—"

Xanthe spoke.

Petra would spend years trying to write down what it sounded like, and never fully succeed. It was four syllables and then a fifth that wasn't a sound so much as a *shape* the air agreed to hold. It was low and high at once, the way a chord is several notes pretending to be one. It did not echo off the barn boards. Sound echoes. This *settled*, the way snow settles, everywhere at once, final.

And the world obeyed it.

There was no flash. No drama. What happened was quieter and far more terrible than that: the goat's breathing deepened, evenly, the way a tide comes in. Color rose in her gums like dawn rising over the ridge. The fluttering shallow heave of her sides smoothed into long, easy bellows-breath, and Nettle — ancient, poisoned, dying Nettle — opened one yellow eye, decided she was hungry, and began gnawing Xanthe's sleeve.

The silence afterward lasted three full heartbeats. Then Maren made a sound Petra had never heard from her, a small cracked noise, and gripped the fence post with both hands as though the farm had tilted.

Xanthe swayed. Petra lunged through the gate and caught her before she fell.

Up close, the cost was visible at once. All the color had gone out of the child's face, leaving her skin the grey of wood ash. Her breath came thin. There was blood at her nose, one bright thread of it, and her hand — the hand with the crown-shaped burn scar — trembled against Petra's arm like a bird's heart.

"You *knew*," Petra breathed, holding her up, furious and awed and frightened all at once. "This is what it costs. This is what you've been rationing. Words are *blood* to you—"

Xanthe looked up at her. And smiled — a real one this time, unhidden, exhausted and luminous — and said nothing at all, and leaned her forehead briefly against Petra's shoulder, and let herself be carried to the house.

Behind them, Nettle stood up, stretched, and bit the fence.

* * *

That night Petra filled six pages and slept two hours, and woke knowing three things with absolute certainty, the way you wake knowing frost has come.

First: the child's tongue was not a language. Languages describe. This one decreed. Every word was a small law, and speaking it enforced the law upon the world, and the enforcement drew payment from the speaker's own flesh and years. It was grammar the way a blade is iron — technically true, and missing the entire point. It was a binding grammar. Words that make things true.

Second: Xanthe was not merely fluent in it. She was the last of whoever had kept it. You could see it in how precisely she spoke, how carefully, how she held each word like the last ember of a fire whose fuel was gone. A living archive. A choir of one.

Third — and this was the thought that sat on Petra's chest in the dark and refused to move — third: a scrap of royal purple. A burn scar in the shape of a crown. A kingdom beyond the stones where, according to every fragment of story that leaked through, a king sat glassy-eyed on his throne while something behind his eyes wore him like a glove. Maren had said the name once, in the confession that still curdled Petra's nights: *a crown-spirit, a parasite, feeding on the dead king's memories, growing hungrier.*

And what does a thing that eats memories fear most?

Not armies. Armies forget. Not priests; priests remember only what suits them. It would fear the one voice in the world whose words could *unbind* — whose grammar could reach into the fabric of things and say *this is no longer so*. It would fear the last singer of the language of before the way a thief fears the one witness who can read the ledger.

It wouldn't want her dead, Petra realized, staring at the rafters. Dead was wasteful. It would want her *silenced*. Kept. Hollowed and worn, or caged and starved of speech until the grammar rotted in her throat unused. It would send riders. It had probably already sent riders.

She got up, lit the lamp, and looked in on the loft. Xanthe slept curled small among the lavender, one fist closed tight around the scrap of purple cloth, her burned palm open toward the ceiling. Even asleep she looked like someone guarding a door from the inside.

"We're going to get you somewhere it can't reach," Petra whispered — and then stopped, hearing herself, hearing how empty the promise was. Somewhere it can't reach. The whole valley existed because someone had believed such a place possible, and Maren had already told her the truth about that place: the ward was fraying, the boundary found, the safety leaking out at the edges like light under a door.

There was nowhere it could not reach. There was only forward, and faster, and together.

In the morning, Petra sharpened her charcoal, laid out fresh paper, and went back to work. If Xanthe's words were weapons, then Petra would learn to read the armory — every syllable, every rule, every cost. She would build a map of the grammar of before, page by page, and pray she finished before anyone came asking questions at their door.

She was copying out the healing-word, fifth shape and all, when she noticed Xanthe standing rigid at the window, looking north toward the ridgeline, where the rain was lifting off the hills.

The child's face had gone white as ash.

Far off, at the very edge of sight, something glinted along the ridge road — metal, catching the new sun. More than one piece of it. Moving, unhurried, down toward the valley.

Toward the stones.
