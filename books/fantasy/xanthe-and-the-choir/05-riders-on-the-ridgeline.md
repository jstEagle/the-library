## 5. Riders on the Ridgeline

The first sign was the birds.

Petra had been in the garden since dawn, tying up the last of the summer's yarrow bundles under Maren's exacting instruction — *stems even, girl, or the tincture will strain crooked* — when she noticed that the hedgerow had gone silent. Not quiet. Silent. The wrens and finches that usually quarreled over the elderberries had simply stopped existing as sound, as though someone had drawn a wet cloth across the morning.

She straightened, twine still in her fingers, and looked up.

They were coming over the ridgeline in a line so neat it looked rehearsed: nine riders on grey horses, in grey cloaks, beneath a sky gone pale as scoured linen. Even at this distance Petra could see the livery — no crest, no color, nothing but grey on grey, like the riders were less an escort than a weather front given legs. Their banners, if you could call them banners, hung limp and colorless in the still air.

Nine. She counted them twice, because her hands wanted something to do besides shake.

"Xanthe." Her voice came out low and rough. The child was sitting on the back step, sorting dried beans by some private logic into three clay bowls, humming her strange wordless hum. "Xanthe. Inside. Now."

The girl looked up at her with those dark, unhurried eyes — she never startled, Petra had learned that much in six days; she moved through the world like water finding its level — and then, without question, gathered a bean bowl under each arm and went inside. That obedience frightened Petra more than the riders did. Xanthe didn't obey out of trust. Petra suspected she obeyed out of practice, the way small creatures learn to move when large shadows pass overhead.

"Maren!" Petra dropped the twine and crossed the yard at a run. The herb-house door stood open; inside, Maren stood at her worktable with a pestle frozen mid-grind, staring north through the little window. Her face had gone the color of the ash she used for lye soap.

"I see them," Maren said, before Petra spoke. "Boots off. Quietly."

"Maren, who are they?"

"Crown riders." The words came flat and dry, like stones turned over after a long winter. "Grey livery. They haven't ridden this road in thirty years, and they've certainly never come down into the valley." She set the pestle down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown. "The ward is fraying faster than I thought. Or they found another way to smell her."

*Her.* Petra's stomach tightened around the morning's bread. Six days ago a mud-caked child had appeared on their doorstep speaking a language that made Maren drop a jar of valerian, carrying a scrap of royal purple and a burn scar shaped like a crown in the palm of her hand. Six days of hiding her in the loft, of teaching her to fold beans and carry water, of mapping a grammar that made things true. And now the world outside their valley — the world Petra had never seen, that no memory in the valley seemed able to hold — had come looking.

"What do we do?"

Maren's answer was to move. Thirty years of hedge-craft expressed itself not in panic but in procedure: she banked the fire, covered the almanac's hiding place with the false brick, swept the floor of anything that might tell a story. Then she took Petra by both shoulders, and her hands were trembling after all.

"Listen to me. You'll take her down the herb tunnels — under the drying shed, past the old root cellar, come up behind the millpond. You know the way; I've made you learn it every year since you could walk, and don't think I didn't notice you complaining."

"You said it was for floods."

"It is for floods. It is also for this." Maren pressed a satchel into her arms — already packed, already packed, which meant it had always been packed, which meant Maren had been waiting for this day the whole of Petra's life. Bread, cheese wrapped in wax, a flask, dried herbs in labeled twists, a knife with a horn handle, and at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a small iron key. "The root cellar at the millpond has a second door. Don't stop until you're through it. Don't look back."

"You're coming with us."

"I am going to stand at my own door," Maren said, "and greet my guests."

"No." Petra heard her own voice crack and hated it. "No, you can't — they'll —"

"They'll ask me questions, and I'll answer them, and then they'll leave, because there's no child here, only a foolish old woman and her apprentice, who is fetching water from the spring and will be back before dusk." Maren's chin came up, and for a moment Petra saw not her mentor but the woman Maren must have been at twenty, the one who had helped ward a valley against the hunger of kings. "Go, Petra. *Now.*"

But it was already too late to go anywhere quietly. Hooves sounded on the near road — soft, unhurried hooves, the gait of people who had never once needed to hurry — and then a voice, pleasant and clear as a bell rung in an empty room:

"Goodwoman! Might we trouble you for water?"

* * *

Petra got Xanthe into the tunnels, barely. It meant leaving Maren standing alone on her own doorstep with her hands folded and her spine straight while nine grey riders dismounted in her yard like a slow tide coming in. It meant pressing Xanthe's small cold hand against the trapdoor under the drying shed and mouthing *down, down, wait for me,* and watching the child descend into the dark without a sound.

It meant staying, one heartbeat longer than wisdom allowed, to watch through the gap in the shed boards.

There were nine of them in the yard now, and none of them moved quite right. Petra couldn't have said how at first — they sat their horses well, they walked like men, they held their reins like any soldier — but something about the *timing* of them was wrong, the way a puppet show is wrong, all the motions human-shaped but arriving a half-breath late, as if each rider consulted some inner ledger before permitting himself to lift a foot. Their horses were worse. The animals walked without tossing their heads, without flicking at flies, without any opinion whatsoever about the world.

And then the tenth figure came forward from the gate, though Petra would have sworn only nine had crested the ridge, and she understood that the riders were merely the frame, and this was the picture.

She was tall, and dressed in grey so fine it might have been woven from fog, and she was smiling. She smiled the way a portrait smiles — completely, correctly, and without any change behind the eyes. Chancellor Vey, Petra would learn later, though she knew it instantly in her bones anyway: this was the head, and everything else in the yard was body. The woman's hair was silver-white and bound simply, her face unlined and ageless in a way that wasn't youth. Youth wants things. This face had finished wanting, long ago, and had settled into perfect satisfaction like a pond settling into ice.

It was her eyes that made Petra's mouth go dry. When Vey turned her head, the light followed a half-moment too long — her eyes held the sun a beat after she'd faced away from it, gleaming faintly, like a cat's caught by a lantern, like glass reflecting a room that wasn't there.

"Goodwoman." Vey stopped an arm's length from Maren and inclined her head graciously. Her voice carried perfectly to the shed, pitched for exactly that. "We apologize for the intrusion. We ride on crown business, and the roads have been dusty."

"The roads generally are, this time of year." Maren's voice was steady as a plumb line. Petra marveled at it even as her heart slammed. "Water's in the well. Help yourselves."

"Kindly." One of the riders dismounted and drew water, and the bucket rose and poured, and the sounds were all correct, all ordinary, and Petra hated how ordinary they were. "We're looking for a child," Vey said. "A girl, perhaps eight years, perhaps ten — they age strangely, these children. Dark-haired. Found wandering, most likely. Mud about her. There may be burns on her hands." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "She's ill, goodwoman. Very ill. She needs care we can provide."

"She hasn't passed this way."

"You're certain."

"I'd know a child on my land the way I'd know rain on my roof."

Vey smiled wider. The smile reached every part of her face except the part of her face where smiles actually live. "Of course you would." She turned, slowly, surveying the yard, the gardens, the drying shed — her gaze crossed the gap in the boards and Petra felt it physically, a cold line drawn across her chest, and she stepped back from the light with her heart in her teeth. "Lovely valley," the chancellor said. "So quiet. So very quiet. One forgets places like this exist."

"Most people manage it."

"Do you know why, goodwoman?" Vey's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, terrible. "Places like this are quiet because someone paid for the quiet. Nothing in this world is free — not silence, not safety, not forgetting. Someone always pays." A rustle of fine grey cloth; Petra risked one eye to the gap. Vey had drifted closer to Maren, close enough that Petra could see the chancellor studying her face with what looked almost like tenderness. "You seem like a woman who understands accounts."

"I keep my books," Maren said. "They balance."

"Mm." Vey let the silence stretch until it sang. Then, brightly: "We'll water the horses and be on our way. Thank you for your courtesy." She turned toward the well — and stopped. Turned back. Tilted her head, birdlike, and Petra saw the light pool wrong in those eyes again, saw them gleam like coins at the bottom of a dark well. "One thing more. The apprentice. Yours, is she?"

Every drop of blood in Petra's body went cold.

"My sister's girl," Maren said. "Come to help with the harvest. She's down at the spring. She'll be sorry to have missed the crown's own riders — she's never seen such grand folk."

"No doubt." Vey regarded Maren for a long, long moment. The wind moved the grey banners. Somewhere far off, absurdly, a crow finally remembered how to make noise. "You're lying," the chancellor said pleasantly.

Petra's soul left her body.

"But that's your right," Vey continued, in exactly the same tone, as though she'd remarked on the weather, "and lying to protect a child is the oldest lie there is. I respect it. Truly." She drew something from her sleeve — a strip of grey cloth, a cord? — and Petra saw Maren's hands curl at her sides. "The crown forgives goodwomen their small treacheries. But accounts must balance, goodwoman. They always balance. If a child passes through this valley — *when* a child passes through this valley — you will send word to the capital within the day. And this—" she pressed whatever it was into Maren's palm, and closed the old woman's fingers over it gently, almost lovingly, "—will remind you. Forgive me. It stings, at first."

Maren's breath hissed once through her teeth. Whatever it was, she didn't cry out. Petra bit her own knuckle until she tasted copper, because crying out was the one thing she absolutely could not do.

"We ride south," Vey announced to her riders, loud and gracious again. "There's a village beyond the ridge that hasn't seen the crown in years. Let's not disappoint them."

* * *

Petra waited until the hoofbeats faded to nothing, until the crows came fully back to the hedgerow, until she could breathe — and then she climbed down into the dark.

The herb tunnels ran cool and close beneath the farmyard, walls of packed earth threaded with roots, smelling of loam and dried lavender and the ghost of forty harvests. Petra had known them all her life the way she knew the backs of her own eyelids. She lit no lamp. She found Xanthe by touch instead, a small warm shape pressed against the tunnel wall, utterly silent, waiting the way stone waits.

"Follow my voice," Petra whispered. "Hold my hem. Step where I step."

The child's hand found hers instead of her hem — small fingers closing around hers with startling firmness, thumb brushing once across Petra's knuckles in that deliberate way she had, her private language of touch that Petra was only beginning to read. *I'm here. Go.*

Behind the root cellar, behind the shelf of empty crocks, behind the door Maren had shown her a hundred times and sworn her to secrecy over — Petra fumbled the iron key from the satchel with shaking hands. It turned. The second door opened onto gray daylight and the reed-choked margin of the millpond, and Petra pulled Xanthe through and shouldered the door shut and drove the key home, and only then did she allow herself one glance back at the house above the hidden tunnel mouth.

Smoke was rising from Maren's chimney. Thin, white, domestic smoke — the smoke of a banked fire tended, of a kettle put on, of an old woman making tea for guests who had already gone. Maren had told them the truth in every particular that mattered: there was no child here. There was only a foolish old woman, and her lies, and whatever grey thing the chancellor had pressed into her hand to make sure the account stayed open.

*She lied with her life,* Petra thought. *That's the coin she paid. She bought us the road with herself.*

Xanthe tugged her sleeve. The girl was looking at the thin thread of smoke too, and her dark eyes were full of something old and knowing and unbearably sad, and then she lifted her free hand and touched her own sternum, twice, and pointed south — toward the ridge, toward the road, toward whatever waited beyond the boundary stones that Petra had never once in her life crossed.

*It knows where she is. We go, or it comes back for all of us.*

"Yes," Petra said aloud, her voice cracking on it. She hitched the satchel higher on her shoulder and took the small cold hand again. "Yes. South. Before they circle back."

They went along the pond's edge with the water whispering at their left and the ridge rising black-green ahead, two small figures in a landscape that had been the entire world and was suddenly, terrifyingly, only a doorway. Behind them Maren's chimney smoke bent southward in the wind, following.

At the foot of the ridge, where the valley grass gave way to the old road, Petra stopped. The boundary stones stood ahead in their ancient ring — and between two of them, faint but unmistakable, the air shimmered like heat off a forge, like a wound in the world that hadn't finished healing.

Beyond it, on the road, in the dust, she could see tracks. Fresh ones. Nine horses, and one set of fine boots walking apart from the rest.

Leading away. Not south.

Back toward the valley.
