## 6. Crossing the Stones

The boundary stones stood in a ragged crescent at the valley's northern throat, and Petra had spent her whole life pretending they were ordinary.

She knew every one of them the way she knew her own teeth. The leaning one with the lichen like spilled milk. The split-topped one where she'd hidden a tin of honey drops when she was nine and found it, years later, gone to sugar-glass and ants. The tallest, which Maren called the Old Man and which never gathered frost, even in deep winter, as though something inside it breathed warmth. As a child she had climbed them, napped against them, cursed them without knowing she was cursing them. They were furniture. They were geology. They were the edges of everything.

Tonight they were a door, and doors only meant something when you were afraid of what stood on either side.

Petra ran with Xanthe's wrist locked in her grip, and the girl came willingly — that was the strange mercy of it — her bare feet finding the sheep path without fumbling, her breath coming quick but not panicked, as though being hunted through the dark by men who burned houses was simply another kind of weather. Behind them the glow of Maren's burning house painted the low clouds the color of an open wound. Smoke rolled down the valley slope in a slow grey flood, and beneath its bitterness Petra could still smell valerian, chamomile, the scorched ghost of the drying loft — twenty years of harvest going up in a single hour.

*Don't look back,* she told herself, and looked back anyway, because she was her mother's daughter and apparently stubbornness survived even when memories didn't.

The house was fully alight now. She could see figures at its edge — small, unhurried, grey — moving through the herb rows with the calm of gardeners inspecting blight. One of them bent to examine the cold-frame where Petra had transplanted the feverfew that very morning. It straightened. It did not chase them. None of them chased them, and somehow that was worse than pursuit, because it meant the riders already knew where Petra and Xanthe were going. There was only one door out of the valley, and everyone standing in the smoke knew it.

"They're not following," Petra gasped, "why aren't they—"

Xanthe made a sound — not a word, Petra realized later, or maybe the truest word there was: a short falling note, like the last syllable of a lullaby. *Because they don't have to.* That was what the note meant. Petra understood it the way you understand a slap.

They came over the last rise and the stones rose around them, taller than she remembered, taller than they had any right to be, and for the first time in her life Petra saw them clearly.

That was the first wrongness. In all her years of climbing and picnicking and sulking against them, she had never once actually *seen* the boundary stones — not the way you see a tree or a fence post. Her eyes had always slid off. Now, with the ward fraying and Maren somewhere behind her pouring herself into the failing spell like wine from a cracked jug, the stones stood naked in the moonlight, and they were not stones at all. Or they were not *only* stones. Under the moon they were veined with light, faint and patient, the way river ice holds the memory of the current that made it. And carved into each one — so worn she should never have been able to read them, and yet she read them effortlessly, the way you read your own name — were lines of script. Rows and rows of it, spiraling up the flanks of every stone, dense as moth wings.

It was writing. It was her mother's handwriting.

"I don't—" Petra's voice cracked. She touched the nearest stone and the letters moved under her fingers like minnows. "This is the ward. This is *her*. Maren said — Maren said my mother sang the ward, I thought she meant — I thought it was a story."

Xanthe pressed both hands flat against the stone beside hers. The child's burn-scarred palm — the crown-shaped mark that had made Maren drop the jar, the mark that had started everything — pressed white against the rock, and where it touched, the script brightened. Not just near Xanthe. Near *Petra* too. The letters recognized something in her blood and woke to greet it, dim gold rising along the veins of light, and the whole crescent of stones shivered like a struck bell too low to hear.

Then, behind them, down the long dark throat of the valley, a second light answered.

Not fire. Fire was red and greedy. This was white and steady and utterly concentrated — a pillar of it rising from the direction of Maren's house, from the herb garden, from the place where Maren must be standing, because Petra knew that posture even at this distance, knew those planted feet and that squared chin, had seen it a thousand times facing down colic in goats and debt collectors and once, memorably, the reeve of the lower village over a disputed fence line.

Maren was holding the ward.

"No," Petra said. It came out very small. "No, no, she's supposed to come *after*, she said she'd follow, she said—"

Maren had said nothing of the sort. Maren had shoved the satchel into Petra's arms, had kissed her forehead with lips that tasted of bitterroot tincture, had said *run north, don't stop, don't wait, whatever you hear behind you, don't wait* — and Petra, seventeen years old and furious with grief before the grief had even happened, had obeyed, because there had been no time to do anything else, and obedience was the cruelest thing she had ever done.

The white pillar flickered. Somewhere below, a grey figure turned toward it, unhurried, and raised one hand, and the flickering steadied — held — the way a drowning man holds a rope he cannot climb.

"Come." Xanthe tugged her hand. The child spoke in gesture and pressure and those rare, ruinously expensive notes of sound, and every part of her said the same thing: *through, now, through.* She stepped between the two nearest stones, into the gap, and stopped there, looking back at Petra with an expression far too old for her face. Waiting to see if Petra would cross. Waiting to find out if she was alone again.

And Petra understood, in one cold flash, why the child waited. Because crossing was the whole question, wasn't it? Everything on this side of the stones was Petra's life — the garden, the kitchen, the smell of bread and yarrow, the crooked shelf of jars, the locked almanac with her mother's name inked out, Maren's voice calling her in from dusk. Everything beyond the stones was a land that ate people, a kingdom of harvested minds and roads that remembered the dead, a hunt with her name on it. On one side: everything familiar and ending. On the other: everything terrifying and true.

There was no time. There was never any time. That, she suspected, would be the lesson of her entire life.

"Wait for me," Petra whispered toward the distant white light — toward Maren — knowing the woman couldn't hear her, knowing words spent backward were wasted words, spending them anyway. "You hold on. You hold on, you impossible old woman, and you follow me, and if you die in that garden I will dig you up and *kill you*—"

Her voice broke entirely. She let it. Then she took Xanthe's hand, fixed her eyes on the gap between the stones, and stepped through.

---

Magic hit her like cold water.

Not gently. Not like a swimmer slipping into a lake. Like falling through ice into a February river — a full-body shock that stopped her heart, restarted it, stopped it again. Every hair on her arms lifted. Her lungs forgot their trade. She had lived seventeen years in a place where magic could not exist, had been raised in silence the way other children were raised in faith, and now the silence ended all at once, everywhere, like a deaf ear suddenly hearing an orchestra mid-crescendo.

The world was *loud*.

The grass beyond the stones sang — an actual sound, thin and green, a note held by ten thousand blades at once. The wind had texture; she could feel it thinking. The moonlight fell on her skin with weight, with intention, like a hand testing fruit at market. Beneath her feet the road — the sheep track widening into the old north road — hummed with a deep, layered murmur, and she would learn within days what that murmur was (footsteps, thousands of years of footsteps, the road remembering everyone who had ever walked it), but in that first moment she only knew that the ground was speaking and she could almost, *almost* understand it.

She staggered. Went down to one knee on the far side of the stones. Vomited bile into the ditch grass, because her body had opinions about having a soul after all these years, and her soul had just been dunked.

"Petra." Not a word — a note, a descending pair of tones, warm and worried, and she heard them *as meaning*, directly, the way you feel heat from a flame. Xanthe crouched beside her, small hands hovering, afraid to touch.

"I'm fine," Petra rasped, and then laughed, wet and wild, because fine was absurd. "I'm — oh, saints, is this what it's like? Is this what Maren felt? Did she stand in that garden all those years *remembering* this?" She wiped her mouth and pushed her hair from her face and looked back through the stones.

The valley looked the same. That was the obscenity of it. Same dark fields, same sleeping village, same thread of woodsmoke — except now she could see the ward itself, arched overhead like a ceiling of faint gold glass, and she could see it *fraying*. Threads of it hanging loose. Holes. And through the largest hole, at the valley's southern end, a darkness was pressing — patient, vast, interested. Something on the other side of the sky, leaning its weight against her mother's unfinished song.

And at the base of the ward, holding it up like Atlas in an apron, one small fierce figure of white fire that could only be Maren.

"She can't leave," Petra said. The knowledge arrived whole, the way the worst knowledge always does. "If she lets go, it comes through. If she comes with us, the ward falls and the crown-thing takes the whole valley while we're walking away. She's trading herself for our head start." Her fists closed in the ditch grass until the stems snapped. "She didn't even *ask* me."

*Xanthe* — the girl touched her own chest, then pointed back down the valley, then laid her palm over her heart and drew it slowly away, and the gesture needed no translation. *Some things are given, not asked.* Or maybe: *she chose.* Petra couldn't tell which. Both hurt equally.

She got up. Her legs shook. She made them work.

"Then we make the head start worth it," she said, and was surprised to find her voice came out level, hard, someone's voice she hadn't met before. Grief, it turned out, could be forged into a tool if you hammered it fast enough, before it cooled. "We walk tonight. We walk until the road ends or my legs do."

They walked.

---

They walked through the rest of that night and into a dawn that Petra had no framework for.

Magic saturated everything past the stones, and magic, it turned out, was color-hungry. The sunrise came up not gold but rose and copper and a green she had no name for, gilding the hills until they looked like the scales of some enormous sleeping beast. Mist pooled in the hollows and *moved with purpose*, drifting in slow spirals that were almost certainly alive. A fox watched them pass from a fencepost with eyes like coins, and when it blinked, it blinked sideways, and Petra decided firmly not to think about that.

But it was the little things that undid her. A spiderweb strung between two hawthorn branches, beaded with dew, humming faintly in a chord she felt in her back teeth. Wildflowers that turned to follow Xanthe as she passed, not the sun. At a stream crossing, the water paused — actually paused, a heartbeat of held silver — as if listening to Xanthe's footsteps, before deciding to go on being water.

"It's all true here," Petra murmured. "Everything's *true* here. Nothing's just — background."

Xanthe glanced at her sidelong and gave the smallest shrug, the universal gesture of children explaining that yes, obviously, and isn't it funny that adults ever believed otherwise.

They were three miles out — Petra counted by counting her own staggering steps, because the sun moved strangely here, hesitating at the horizon like a guest unsure of welcome — when Xanthe stopped dead in the middle of the road and held up one hand.

Petra froze. Three nights of learning the child had taught her that much: the raised palm meant *listen*, or sometimes *danger*, or sometimes *wait*, and the difference lived in the angle of the fingers, and Petra was perhaps forty percent fluent and getting worse under pressure.

Then she heard it herself. Hooves. Far behind. Not many — two horses, maybe three, moving at a canter up the north road. Coming from the valley.

Coming from *Maren*.

Petra's heart performed a complicated maneuver involving hope and terror in equal parts. Riders. Riders from the valley. Which meant the riders had gotten *past* the ward, which meant either Maren had fallen — and the thought arrived like a blade between her ribs, sharp, immediate, unbearable — or Maren had held, had bought the ward's seams enough time to mend, and had sent—

The hoofbeats crested the rise behind them, and it wasn't Maren. Of course it wasn't. It was two of the grey riders, mud-flecked, unhurried, their horses' breath steaming in the early cold, and between them, walking freely, hands unbound, face serene —

Chancellor Vey.

Petra had seen him for less than a minute through a window while his soldiers questioned Maren, and she would know him anywhere and forever. He was a tall man dressed in dove-grey, and he was the most beautiful person Petra had ever seen, and his beauty was the beauty of a perfectly kept tomb. His eyes reflected the dawn light *wrong* — the light went into them and did not come back out the same, came back older, came back arranged. He reined in ten paces away and regarded them across the morning with an expression of gentle, bottomless patience.

"Petra Nakamura," he said. Her name sounded different in his mouth. Owned. Catalogued. "Daughter of Sera Nakamura. Ward-singer's get. How fortunate." He smiled, and the smile reached every part of his face except anything behind it. "And the little songbird. We've been looking for you both for such a long time."

Xanthe's hand found Petra's sleeve and gripped. And then the child did something Petra had never seen her do, something that would haunt the edge of her dreams for weeks afterward.

She opened her mouth, and she *spoke*.

One word. A single true-word, torn out of her like a tooth, and the world *rang* with it — the dew flashed to steam, the hawthorns shed their leaves all at once, the horses screamed and reared, and Chancellor Vey's beautiful face went, for one unguarded instant, absolutely blank with something that on a human face would have been pain.

The word had no shape Petra's ears could keep. But its meaning slammed into her whole body at once, simple and colossal:

***STOP.***

The road itself seized. The mist froze mid-spiral. For three heartbeats the entire morning stood still as a held breath, and in that stillness Xanthe grabbed Petra's hand and *ran* — dragging her off the road, down into a fold of the hills, through bracken and gorse, while behind them the word's power drained away like a tide going out and the world resumed its business and Vey's voice floated after them, unhurt, unhurried, almost fond:

"Run, then, little bird. The road remembers everyone who walks it. Sooner or later, it remembers *you*."

They ran until the horses' sounds faded, until Vey's voice faded, until Petra's lungs burned and her stitch felt like a knife and Xanthe stumbled and Petra caught her and they went down together in the bracken, breathing like bellows.

Xanthe's palm was bleeding. Petra grabbed it — the crown-scarred one — and found a fresh cut across the old burn, thin and precise, welling red. The cost of the word. The child had *paid* for those three seconds of stillness out of her own flesh, out of her own — Petra remembered Maren's grey-faced dread, remembered the phrase the hedge-witch had used, *every word shortens her* —

"You can't do that," Petra panted. "You can't spend yourself like that, not for me, not for—"

Xanthe looked at her. And smiled — a small, crooked, exhausted, utterly human smile — and tapped Petra's chest once, right over the heart, then pointed back the way they'd come. Toward the valley. Toward Maren.

*Worth it.*

Petra sat down hard in the bracken. The grief she had been hammering into a tool since the stones finally cooled and cracked, and it came out of her in great ugly sobs — for Maren standing alone in white fire, for the house that smelled of bread and yarrow, for the mother whose handwriting covered the boundary stones and whose face she would never see, for the child bleeding silently beside her who thought love was a currency you paid in flesh.

Xanthe let her cry. After a while, awkwardly, the girl put one small arm around Petra's shoulders, the way you might comfort something large and wounded and not yet certain whether it bites.

When the sobs finally burned down to embers, Petra wiped her face on her sleeve, looked east toward the hazy line of hills that hid the capital, the court, the crown, the king who gardened without memory — and made herself say it aloud, because saying it made it real and real things could be fought.

"We're going to the capital. We're going to find out what my mother started. And we're going to finish it."

Beside her, Xanthe went very still.

Then, slowly, the child raised her scarred hand and traced one careful sign in the air — a sign Petra had never seen, a spiral closing inward, a door shutting — and pointed east, and her eyes were full of a fear so old it predated language.

Something in the capital already knew they were coming.
