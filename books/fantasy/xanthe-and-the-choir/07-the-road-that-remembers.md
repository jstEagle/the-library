## 7. The Road That Remembers

The world beyond the stones did not look different.

That was the first cruelty of it. Petra had spent her whole life imagining the boundary as a wall — that on the other side the trees would burn blue, or the sky would have teeth, or the grass would whisper her name in voices she didn't want to hear. Instead there was a road. A plain, rutted cart-track running east through birch and bracken, dappled with afternoon light, so ordinary it felt like an insult.

She stood at the edge of it with Xanthe's small hand locked in hers, both of them still smelling of smoke, and waited for the magic to do something terrible.

It did something else instead.

The light moved. Not the sun — the light *underneath* things, a pale shimmer rising out of the packed earth like heat off a summer stone, except it was autumn and cold and the shimmer had a rhythm to it, a slow pulse, like something breathing under a blanket. Petra crouched. Pressed her palm flat to the road.

Cold water again — the same shock as crossing the stones, but gentler now, diluted, as if the road were letting her in by degrees. And beneath her hand she felt... movement. Footfalls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Pressed into the clay like fossils pressed into shale, each one carrying a ghost of weight, a ghost of warmth, a ghost of whoever had made it.

*Oh,* she thought. *The road remembers them.*

Xanthe made a sound beside her — not a word, never a word if she could help it, but a soft clicking of the tongue against the teeth that Petra had begun to understand meant attention. The child knelt too, laid her scarred palm against the dirt, and her eyes went wide and wet at once. Whatever she heard in the road, it was loud.

"Don't listen too long," Petra said, though Xanthe couldn't understand the words. She said them anyway; she was learning that saying things aloud kept them true for herself, even when no one could answer. "We don't know what listens back."

They walked east because Maren had told her once — years ago, in one of her rare unguarded moods, stirring the valerian tincture with her back half-turned — that all roads from the valley ran toward the capital, because the valley had been made by people fleeing the capital, and fear leaves tracks that outlast everything. Petra hadn't understood then. She thought about it now with every step.

The footprints came clearer as the light failed. Where the road dipped into shadow, the old impressions rose up faintly luminous, layered like rings in cut wood: boots, bare feet, hooves, wheel-ruts worn deep and shining. And every so often, walking beside her own fresh prints, Petra would see another pair keeping pace — translucent, silvered, a stride or two off — the replay of someone long dead who had walked this exact line on some other evening, in some other year, going somewhere they'd never arrive.

The first time one passed through her, she screamed.

It didn't hurt. That was worse, somehow. The dead woman's footsteps simply overlapped her own for three paces — Petra felt the ghost-weight settle into her legs, felt the rhythm of a stranger's gait take over her body like borrowed clothing — and then it slid free and faded, and Petra stood in the middle of the road shaking while Xanthe gripped her sleeve with both hands, alarmed, asking something urgently in the old grammar, the syllables coming out fast and worried before the child caught herself and swallowed them.

"I'm all right." Petra's voice cracked. "I'm all right. It just — walked through me."

She thought of Maren, then. Standing at the boundary stones with the ward blazing around her, telling them *run*, staying behind to hold a door shut against Chancellor Vey and his grey riders and whatever lived inside his eyes. Petra had not looked back. She had made herself not look back, because looking back would have stopped her feet, and stopping would have killed them both.

Now, alone on a haunted road with a child who spoke in truths, she let herself feel it — one breath, two, three, grief arriving like weather — and then folded it away, because grief was heavy and they had miles yet and no food and night coming down.

*Later,* she promised herself. *When we're safe.* Knowing, even as she promised, that safe might be a place that no longer existed anywhere in the world.

* * *

They sheltered that night in the hollow of a lightning-split oak, wrapped in the one blanket Petra had grabbed from the burning house — it still smelled of woodsmoke and dried thyme, of Maren's kitchen, and Petra held it to her face in the dark until the smell threatened to undo her, and then held it anyway.

Xanthe slept curled against her side like a coal, radiating heat. Petra stayed awake and watched the road.

By moonlight the memories glowed stronger. She began, without meaning to, to read them. The way you learn anything: by staring until the pattern surrenders. Fresh prints shone brightest. Old ones dimmed by depth — but depth also meant *many*. A print that had been walked over ten thousand times sank deep and gathered the echoes of everyone who'd ever crossed it, and these crowded places hummed almost audibly, like a hive. Lonely stretches of road held only a few scattered impressions, bright and isolated, and these were somehow sadder — a single pair of small bare feet, decades old, walking alone at midnight, going nowhere near any town.

Petra found herself cataloguing gaits. A limping man with his left heel dragging, repeated over and over across a decade, growing slowly worse, then stopping forever at a bend where the road forked. Two sets of prints that walked together for a mile and then only one set continued, the other turning off toward a field path, and Petra invented and discarded a dozen stories about them before catching herself and stopping, ashamed. These weren't stories. They were people. The road remembered them because no one else did anymore.

*Is this what the crown does?* she wondered. *Eats the remembering, keeps the steps?*

And then, because she couldn't help it — because the thought arrived fully formed, the way important thoughts do, wearing its own dread like a coat —

*If the road remembers everyone who ever walked it.*

*Then it remembers my mother.*

She sat up so suddenly that Xanthe stirred and murmured. The moon was high. The road ran pale and patient through the dark, glittering faintly with its sediment of the dead, stretching east toward the capital like a sentence waiting to be finished.

Petra did not sleep again.

* * *

In the morning she taught herself properly.

They walked slowly — Xanthe's legs were short, and Petra refused to hurry her, and besides, hurrying would ruin the reading. She'd worked out the trick of it overnight: press your bare palm to the earth (shoes muted it; she walked the whole day with her stockings stuffed in her pocket, wincing at every stone), breathe slow, and let your own memory go quiet. The road answered quietness. It had nothing to say to a mind full of noise.

What came up through her hand was not pictures, exactly. It was more like the feeling of recognizing a tune after hearing only three notes — a shape of presence, an impression of someone, rendered in pressure and rhythm rather than sight. A heavy man who whistled while he walked, she could tell, because his prints carried a bounce. A mother who carried a child on her hip, the left prints deeper than the right. A soldier, or what used to be a soldier, whose stride was parade-perfect even forty years dead.

"You could find anyone on this road," Petra whispered. "Anyone who ever traveled it."

Xanthe looked up at her, mud on her chin, understanding nothing of the words but everything of the tone. Then the child pointed east, and then pointed at Petra, and then pressed her own small hand flat against Petra's sternum, directly over her heart, and held it there.

The gesture needed no translation.

"Yes," Petra said, and her throat closed around it. "Her."

They searched all morning. It was harder than she'd expected — the road was crowded with the dead, layer upon layer, and most of what came through her palm was noise, a riverbed of anonymous passage. She learned to narrow it: not *anyone*, but *a hedge-witch's daughter, raised in a warded valley, who left and never came back*. She held that description in her mind like a key held up to a lock, and walked, and listened.

Past noon, at a stretch where the road climbed a long ridge between hawthorn hedges, she felt it.

A gait like her own.

Not exactly — no one walks exactly like anyone else — but close the way family is close. A slight inward roll of the right ankle that Petra caught herself doing when she was tired and had never known where it came from. A stride a little too long for the height of the walker, impatient, always leaning toward wherever she was going. The prints were old — dim, sunk deep beneath newer layers, twenty years deep at least — but they burned through everything above them the way a coal burns through ash.

Petra stopped in the middle of the ridge. Her hand was still pressed to the ground. She couldn't lift it.

"She's here," she said. "Xanthe. She's — I found her. I found her *steps*."

The child came back at a run and dropped to her knees beside her, and together they followed the prints northward along the ridge, away from the capital road, onto a field path Petra would never have noticed. The prints grew brighter as they went — fresher in the memory, less buried — which meant her mother had walked this stretch many times, or recently, or both. Through a broken wall. Across a pasture gone to seed. To a low stone well overgrown with bramble, standing alone in a fold of hillside where no house remained.

At the well, the prints stopped.

They didn't fade. They *stopped* — a final pair planted firmly in the clay beside the well-mouth, toes pointing toward the water, as clear and bright as if they'd been made yesterday. As if the walker had stood there a long time. As if she'd meant to come back and finish the walk.

Petra knelt and put both hands on those last prints, and the road gave her what it had: the inward-rolled ankle, the impatience, and underneath it, unmistakable, a grief so worn and habitual it had become part of the walking itself, carried in the body the way a stone is carried in a shoe. Twenty years of it. Her mother had grieved her way along this road for twenty years, and the road had kept every step of the grieving.

"She never got there," Petra said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Or she got there and—" She couldn't finish it. She looked at the well instead. Looked at the bramble-choked mouth. Thought, absurdly, of dropping a coin in. Wishing on it. Wishing what?

That her mother had turned around? That she hadn't?

Maren had said the valley's ward was bought with her mother's memories. Had said it like a price already paid, a transaction completed, a door closed. But the road said otherwise. The road said a woman had walked east year after year with grief in her ankles, and stopped at a well in the middle of nowhere, and never came home — and Petra's inheritance, she understood now, was not a cottage in a safe valley. It was this unfinished walk. This question with a well at the end of it.

Xanthe touched the last footprint too. The child went very still. Then she looked up at Petra with an expression Petra had never seen on her before — not wonder, not fear, but a kind of fierce, urgent recognition, as if the child had just confirmed something she'd suspected since the night she'd appeared at Maren's door trailing royal purple and the smell of burning palaces.

Xanthe pointed at the prints. Then east, toward the capital. Then at the crown-shaped scar on her own palm.

"No," Petra said.

Xanthe pointed again. Prints. East. Scar. Her eyes were steady and terrible.

"No," Petra said again, weaker. Because the child was right and they both knew it. Her mother had been a singer of unbinding rites — Maren had said that much before the smoke took the rest. The crown had eaten singers. Her mother had fled singers. And here were her mother's footprints, pointing east with the patience of the dead, having waited twenty years for someone to follow them.

The wind moved through the hawthorn, and far down the eastern road, where the land fell away toward the plains and the distant smudge that might have been the capital, Petra saw something that made her stomach drop.

Riders. Grey, unhurried, spread wide across the road like spilled ink. Not searching the countryside — they didn't need to. They were riding the one road that remembered everyone, the road that led, eventually, to everyone.

One of them, at the head, sat very straight, and even at this distance the light seemed to bend strangely around him, reflecting wrong, serene as still water.

Chancellor Vey had not lost their trail. He had simply known, all along, where all roads went.

Petra took Xanthe's hand. Behind them lay the well and the unfinished walk and twenty years of her mother's grief, printed into the earth, patient as scripture. Ahead lay the riders and the capital and whatever waited there wearing a king's face.

"Stay off the main road," she told the child, pulling her down into the bracken, away from the ridgeline, her heart hammering a new rhythm. "We cut across country. We stay off anything that remembers."

But even as she said it, Petra knew the truth the road had taught her: there was no path in this kingdom that didn't remember, and no memory that didn't lead somewhere. Her mother's footsteps ended at a well. Hers, she was beginning to suspect, ended at a throne.

And somewhere behind them, unhurried as erosion, the grey riders came on — following not two fugitives, but the road itself, which remembered everything, and forgave nothing, and never forgot the direction home.
