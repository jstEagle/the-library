## 8. The Tinker's Toll

The road remembered them by now.

Petra had learned that on the second day outside the stones: the way the packed earth seemed to shift beneath her boots, the way old footprints rose like bruises in the mud — a farmer's hobnails from forty years gone, a soldier's nailed heel, a child's bare toes pressed small and quick as birdsong. The road did not forget anyone who walked it. That had been beautiful for about half a morning, before she understood what it meant: that everything here was being written down, kept, hoarded by something, and that she and Xanthe were adding to the ledger with every step.

They had turned off the main road when Petra heard bells behind them — not temple bells, but the thin, wrong-sweet chime she'd begun to associate with grey livery — and cut west through birch country where the trees grew pale as candle-wax and the wind carried the smell of iron and cold water. Which was how, near dusk on the fourth day of exile, they came upon the tinker's wagon parked in a hollow like a fat brown beetle that had died mid-journey.

It was an enormous thing, the wagon — more house than cart, its curved roof shingled in copper gone green with age, its sides hung with pots and pans and birdcages and bundles of dried herbs that clinked and rustled in the breeze without any wind touching them. Lanterns burned at all four corners though no one had lit them. A sign swung above the folded steps, carved in letters that rearranged themselves whenever Petra wasn't looking directly at them; when she forced her eyes to hold still, they resolved into:

*OSKAR. PASSAGE, PROVISIONS, REPAIRS.*
*FAIR PRICES. MEMORY NOT ACCEPTED.*

The last line was scratched out so many times, and rewritten so many times, that the wood beneath had gone soft as bread.

"He wards it," Petra murmured. She could feel it now, the same pressure against her skin that the boundary stones had always made, fainter but familiar — the hum of something that did not want magic to pass. "Like the valley."

Xanthe stood at her shoulder, small and still, her grey eyes moving over the wagon the way she looked at everything: as though reading it. Her hand, the one with the crown-shaped burn scar, was closed tight around the scrap of royal purple she never let go of. She hadn't spoken a true-word since the goat, since the moment Petra had watched life pour back into a dying animal out of nothing but sound — and Petra had stopped asking her to. Some part of her understood already, though she couldn't have said how, that each of those words cost the child something she could not get back.

Still, Petra needed passage. The road ahead climbed into hill country where the memories lay thick — she'd seen it from the ridge, whole fogbanks of the stuff rolling through the valleys like weather, replaying harvests and funerals and first kisses to no one. Walking through that unguarded would be like wading into a river in flood. And behind them, sooner or later, would come Vey's riders.

She knocked on the wagon door with the iron knocker shaped like a swallow.

The door opened before the third knock landed.

The man who filled it was enormous and cheerful and entirely wrong. He was broad as a doorway, with a beard braided into three plaits hung with tiny copper bells, and a smile of such uncomplicated warmth that Petra felt her shoulders drop half an inch before she noticed his shadow. His shadow sat wrong. It pooled behind him like spilled ink, too dark, too still, and it did not move when he moved.

"Well!" he boomed. "Travelers! Two travelers, and such travelers —" His bright black eyes flicked from Petra's mud-caked hem to Xanthe's face, and something moved behind them, quick as a fish under ice. "Come in, come in, out of the road's earshot. It does gossip so."

"I'm looking for passage," Petra said carefully. "West, through the hills. I can pay. Coin, work —"

"Mm." Oskar sucked his teeth. "Coin I have. Work I need less than you'd think; the wagon mostly repairs itself and blames me for the dents. Come in anyway. Tea costs nothing." He paused. "Nothing *yet*," he added, with the air of a man making a joke only he found funny, and stepped aside.

* * *

Inside, the wagon was bigger than outside. Petra had given up being surprised by this somewhere around the boundary stones, but it still caught at her: the interior went back and back, shelves climbing into gloom, kettles hanging from rafters she couldn't see the tops of, a stove the size of a cathedral's heart radiating heat that smelled, strangely, of Maren's kitchen — of rosemary and woodsmoke and scorched honey — which made her chest hurt before she'd even sat down.

Oskar poured tea from a pot with legs. He gave Xanthe hers in a cup painted with stars, and Xanthe wrapped both hands around it and watched him over the rim with the unblinking attention she gave to everything, and Petra saw the tinker notice, and file it away.

"So," Oskar said, settling onto a stool that creaked under him like a ship. "Passage through the hills. You know what's up there, do you? The memory-fogs?"

"I've seen them."

"Seen them, yes, from safe high ground. Walking in them is different. The fogs don't just show you things, girl. They *give* you things. Old griefs, other people's griefs, griefs from people two hundred years dead. Folk walk in carrying their own sorrows and walk out carrying eleven more and no map home." He sipped his tea. "My wagon passes through clean. Warded. I make the trip twice a month, and I take passengers, because I am generous, and soft, and my mother would rise from wherever she is and scold me if I didn't." His shadow did not laugh when he did. "Which brings us to prices."

"Coin," Petra said. "I have some. Not much, but some."

"Keep your coin. Buy bread with it in Thistlefall." He waved a huge hand. "No. For passage through the hills, I take a different toll." He set down his cup, and the cheerfulness drained out of his face like water from a cracked bowl, leaving something older and wearier underneath. "A memory."

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well.

"No," Petra said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to hear the terms."

"Everyone says that." Oskar leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the lamplight caught his eyes and she saw they were not quite black but very, very dark brown, the brown of old varnish, the brown of furniture in houses where everyone had died. "Sit and listen anyway. It's rude to refuse a man before he's finished talking, and worse, it's foolish. A small memory. One taste. Nothing that makes you *you* — I don't take those, wouldn't know where to store them, they're too big for my jars. A small one. A meal. An afternoon. The smell of a particular rain." He spread his hands. "In exchange, you cross sixty miles of haunted country dry-shod and asleep in a proper bed, and arrive on the far side with everything else you own intact."

"And the memory? What happens to it?"

"I keep it." He said it simply. "Jars, mostly. Some barrels. I drink a little now and then — nothing that matters, a sunset here, a mother's voice there — to stay... anchored." His gaze drifted up toward the shelves climbing into the dark, and Petra followed it and realized, with a slow cold turning in her stomach, that the jars lining those shelves were not empty. Each held a curl of light like smoke trapped under glass, and each jar bore a label in a careful, cramped hand. *Rain on the roof, blue slate. First snow, age six. The dog, before.* Hundreds of them. Thousands.

"You drink other people's lives," Petra said.

"I *keep* them," Oskar said, with sudden sharpness. "Do you know what happens to a memory nobody holds? Out here?" He jerked his chin at the walls, at the road beyond. "The road takes it. The fogs eat it. It goes to feed whatever it is that grows in the deep places and wears dead men's faces. At least in my jars they're *safe*. Warm. Occasionally listened to." He caught himself, breathed, and the cheerfulness came back up like a lamp being relit. "But I'm boring you, and you want to say no, and you'll say no until the riders come, and then you'll say yes, and I'll charge extra for making me wait. Everyone does. Sit. Drink your tea."

Petra did not sit. But she did not leave either, and Oskar smiled his warm wrong smile, because he knew it too.

Xanthe touched her sleeve.

Petra looked down. The child was holding out her cup of star-painted tea, offering it, and her grey eyes were fixed on Petra's face with that terrible reading attention — and then she lifted her scarred hand and pressed it flat against Petra's chest, over her heart, and made a small gesture: fingers closing, drawing inward, plucking something out. Then opening. Empty.

*I understand*, the gesture said. *Something leaves. Something is missing after.*

"How does she —" Oskar began.

"She doesn't speak," Petra said quickly. "Not in words you'd know."

"Hm," said Oskar, in a voice that suggested he suspected otherwise, and that the words the child knew were not words at all.

* * *

Petra took the rest of the evening to decide, which was to say she spent the evening deciding and pretending otherwise. She checked their provisions — half a loaf, a heel of cheese, the last of the feverfew tea. She looked at the map Oskar unrolled across his table, the hills rendered in faded ink with little warnings inked along the passes in a dead woman's handwriting: *do not stop singing here. do not answer voices. the ford lies about depth.* Sixty miles of that, on half a loaf, with riders somewhere behind.

And she thought about Maren's kitchen.

That was the trap of it, she realized. Oskar had said *a small one, a taste*, and her mind, treacherous and practical, had immediately begun inventorying itself, laying memories out on a counter like herbs to be weighed. And most of what she owned that was small enough to sell was bound up in that kitchen — because she had grown up in it, because every ordinary afternoon of her life had happened inside its stone walls, and because the ordinary things were exactly the ones she could bear to lose. Or thought she could. The big ones — her mother's inked-out name, the stones, the night the ward was made — those she kept. Those were load-bearing.

"An afternoon," she said finally. "One afternoon in Maren's kitchen. Not the important parts. Just — an afternoon. Chopping herbs. The light through the window. That kind of afternoon."

Oskar's face went very still, in a way that told her he understood precisely what she was doing and what it would cost, and that he was going to let her do it anyway, because that was the trade, and the trade was the trade.

"One afternoon," he agreed softly. "You choose which. Though—" and here he raised one thick finger, "—a warning, free of charge. Memories aren't shelves, girl. They're threads. Pull one thread out and the cloth shifts. You won't lose the afternoon. You'll lose what it was *woven into*. Other days will have a hole shaped like it. You'll know something's missing and not know what. That's the honest price. Most folk don't believe me until after."

"Most folk pay anyway."

"Most folk are in a hurry." He stood, joints popping like green firewood, and went to the shelves, running his huge fingers along the jar labels until he found an empty one — squat, thick-glassed, its cork sealed with red wax stamped with a keyhole. He set it on the table between them. From somewhere in the wagon's impossible depths he produced a coil of fine copper wire and a small brass funnel.

"Now," he said. "Breathe slow. Think of the afternoon — all of it, start to finish, don't flinch from the middle parts, the middle parts are what hold the ends together. When it's bright in your mind — really bright, so you can smell it — breathe out into the funnel. Long and slow. Like blowing out a candle you want to stay lit."

"That's not how anything works."

"It's exactly how this works." He smiled, and for once the smile reached all the way to his eyes, and what lived there was tired and kind and very old. "I've done nine hundred of these. More than any man should. Breathe, girl. The road isn't getting shorter."

Petra closed her eyes.

She chose the afternoon in early summer, two years ago, when she was fourteen and lazy and the garden had come in heavy with lemon balm, and Maren had set her to stripping the leaves while Maren herself sat in the window grinding valerian root, humming — off-key, tuneless, a sound Petra used to complain about and would have given a finger to hear right now — and the light came slantwise through the south window and turned the dust to gold, and the cat — the old orange cat, long dead, whose name Petra suddenly could not remember, whose name had been —

— whose name had been —

She held it. She held the whole afternoon, all of it, the sticky resin on her fingers, the bee bumbling at the sill, Maren saying *"you strip it like you're angry at it, girl, it's a plant, not a debt"* — she held it until it blazed, until she could feel the warmth of that room on her real skin here in the cold wagon, and then she bent to the brass funnel and breathed out, long and slow, like blowing out a candle she wanted to stay lit.

It left her the way blood leaves a shallow cut — not pain, exactly, but a wrongness, a lightening, a sense of having become slightly less than she was. She felt it go through her mouth and throat and out, and heard it land — a soft chime, like a drop of water falling into glass — and opened her eyes.

The jar glowed. Faint gold, swirling gently, and inside it, if she leaned close, she could see shapes: a window. Slanting light. Two figures, one bent over mortar and pestle, one hunched over a basket of green leaves, and the light moved between them like something alive.

"There," Oskar said quietly, corking it, sealing the wax, writing the label in his cramped careful hand: *Lemon balm. Summer. The cat's name is Bramble.* He hesitated over the label a moment — Petra saw him add the last line, saw him decide to give it back to her someday, maybe, or maybe just to keep the record honest — and set the jar among its thousands of brothers on the shelf.

And the hole opened.

It was not like forgetting. Petra had forgotten things before — names, errands, where she'd left her scissors. This was a hole with edges. She reached for yesterday, for the walk along the river, and it was there, intact. She reached for the winter, the fever she'd had, Maren sitting up all night with compresses — there, intact. But somewhere between them, where the summer afternoon should have connected to all the other summers, all the other afternoons in that kitchen, there was a smooth blank patch, like a wall where a door used to be, and she kept brushing her mind against it the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, again and again, unable to stop.

She knew she had chopped lemon balm once. The knowledge was there, dry as a ledger entry. But the light was gone. The smell was gone. The humming was gone. Somewhere in the shelves of this wagon, a girl of fourteen stripped leaves in golden dust while an old woman sang off-key, and Petra could see the jar glowing on the shelf, and she could not feel it anymore.

"Oh," she said. Her voice came out small. "Oh, it's — you said woven —"

"I did say." Oskar didn't gloat. He pushed the refilled teacup back into her hands, and his huge rough hand covered hers around it for a moment, briefly, almost gently. "Drink. It helps. Not much. But it helps." He turned to the shelves, rummaging, his voice businesslike again. "We roll at first light. There's a bunk behind the stove for you and a pallet for the little one, and I warn you, the wagon snores. Now — food? I've a stew on that's mostly edible."

Xanthe had not moved.

She was standing beside the shelf, looking up at the jar — at *their* jar, the gold light swirling inside it, the window, the two figures — and her scarred hand was raised toward the glass but not touching it, hovering, trembling very slightly. And then she turned and looked at Petra, and her face — usually so still, so watchful, so much older than its years — was wet-eyed and stricken, and she made the gesture again, the closing fist, the plucking, the empty open palm, but slower this time, and then she pressed her own small hand flat against her own chest, hard, as though holding something in.

Then she pointed at the jar. And then at the shelf. All the shelves. The thousands of lights in the thousands of jars.

And slowly, deliberately, she shook her head — once, twice — and drew one finger across her own throat.

Petra understood, and the understanding went through her like cold water: *this is what was done to you. Someone paid this toll for you. Many times. Whole shelves' worth.*

"Xanthe," she started, "it's all right, it was my choice, it was —"

But the child had turned back to Oskar, and she did something she had never done in all the days Petra had known her. She spoke. One word, aloud, in that language older than kingdoms — a single syllable, low and resonant, that made the lantern flames stand straight up and made every jar on every shelf, all through the wagon's impossible depths, ring softly, like glasses touched at a wedding, like a choir clearing its throat.

Oskar went white. All three braids of his beard trembled with their little bells.

"What," he whispered, "what did she say?"

"I don't know," Petra lied — because she did know, or half-knew, the way you half-know a dream: the word had meant *remember*, or perhaps *be remembered*, or perhaps something vaster that both of those were only shadows of. And every jar in the wagon had heard it. And every jar, for one impossible moment, had glowed brighter — all those kept afternoons, all those saved sunsets and mothers' voices, straining toward the child like plants toward a window.

Oskar stared at the shelves. Then at Xanthe. Then, last and longest, at the new jar with its fresh red seal, in which the golden afternoon had gone quiet again — but was it Petra's imagination, or did the two little figures in the light stand a fraction closer together than before?

"First light," Oskar said hoarsely, backing toward the stove. "I said first light, and I keep my word, and I'll take you through the hills, and I'll take no further payment, and I would thank you —" his voice cracked and steadied — "I would *thank* the young lady not to sing in my wagon again. Things in the jars have ears. Things in the jars have *hopes*." He busied himself violently with the stew. "Bunks are behind the stove. Sleep well."

Petra slept badly, in the narrow heat behind the iron stove, the tooth-shaped hole in her memory aching whenever her mind wandered near it. But sometime deep in the night she surfaced halfway from sleep and heard, from the front of the wagon, the tinker's great voice, low and private, talking to someone — or to something — and she heard the words *choir*, and *palace*, and *the ones I couldn't save*, and then a sound that might have been weeping, or might have been a thousand small lights in a thousand jars, all sighing at once.
