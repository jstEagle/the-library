## 9. Xanthe

The girl-with-the-scratchy-voice — Petra — had fallen asleep with her mouth open, one hand still curled around a stub of pencil. She had been drawing my words again. Not my words exactly. Her marks for them: little hooked shapes like bird feet, like roots, like the way water decides which way to go around a stone.

I sat very still on the narrow bed and counted what I had left.

This was an old habit. I had done it in the palace nursery, lying in my cot beneath the window where the thirteenth moon used to come through, and I did it now in a hedge-witch's house that smelled of dried valerian and woodsmoke and burnt porridge. Counting is not the same as spending. Counting costs nothing. I was very good at things that cost nothing.

*Four hundred and eleven,* I told myself, though the number was a guess, and guessing was all I had ever been allowed to do about it. The old ones could feel their words the way you feel coins in a closed fist — each one distinct, weighted, warm from your own keeping. My teacher Sefa could hold up her hand and say *I have two thousand and six*, and she would be right to the word. But Sefa's generation had been taught whole. Mine had only been taught to hide.

The fire ticked. Outside, the valley lay under its bowl of quiet, and even the wind seemed to move carefully here, as if it too were afraid of waking something.

I looked at Petra sleeping, and I thought — carefully, in pictures rather than in the old tongue, because thinking in the old tongue was itself a kind of almost-speaking — *she does not know what I am paying for every day she spends trying to learn me.*

That was the cruelty of it, the joke the crown would have enjoyed if crowns could laugh. Everyone believed I had been struck silent. Even kind people believed it. They saw a mud-caked child who made no sound, who answered questions with her hands and her eyes and her terrible patience, and they built a small soft story around me: *poor thing, something happened to her, we must be gentle.* And gentleness was fine. Gentleness was welcome. But the truth was worse and simpler and no one, not even Petra, had thought to guess it:

*I am choosing.*

Every word I knew was true. That is what the old tongue was — not a language the way Petra's language was a language, a coat you put over things, but a grammar that made things so. When the choir sang *the wheat stands tall*, the wheat stood tall. When they sang *the fever breaks*, somewhere a mother stopped wringing her hands. Words were not descriptions. Words were hinges, and behind every hinge a door, and behind every door whatever you dared to name.

And every door opened outward through *you*. Every true-word spent took something out of the singer and did not put it back. A day, sometimes. An hour of laughter, sometimes. Once, when old Yenna sang the storm away from the harvest and saved four villages, she sat down afterward on the threshing floor and forgot the name of her first love, and smiled, because she no longer knew there was anything to forget.

That was how the crown had eaten us. It had learned that it did not need to hunt us with riders and knives — although it did that too, eventually, when we began to run. It needed only to make us speak. Every rescue we sang was a meal it ate later. Every healing, every blessing, every small merciful hinge swung open, and the crown stood behind us with its cup held out, drinking down the days we poured away.

So the last of us learned the hardest lesson any singer had ever learned.

We shut our mouths.

* * *

Sefa found me in the laundry court three days after the night the singers died.

I have kept that scene longer than most, because keeping it felt like a duty, the way you keep a debt-mark even when the person who owes you is ash. Sefa came through the steam with her sleeves rolled and her forearms wet to the elbow, and when she saw me crouched behind the linens where I had hidden since the screaming started, she did not gather me up or weep over me. She knelt. She put her mouth against my ear, and her breath smelled of lye and fear, and she said in the old tongue — softly, softly, the way you handle a blade by the flat —

*"Say nothing. Not one word. Whatever you see tonight, whatever comes for you, keep your teeth closed. Your silence is the last song left in this palace. Guard it."*

Then she pressed something into my hands: a scrap of royal purple, cut from a curtain, still smelling of the king's own rooms, and she folded my fingers over it and said, in Petra's language this time, plain and rough, *run, little bird, run east until the stones stop you, and then find someone who will lie for you.*

I ran. I have been running since. And in all that time I spoke perhaps forty words aloud, and I could recite you every one of them like a miser reciting her hoard, except that a miser's coins do not bleed.

There was the night the wolf-pack circled me in the birch woods, gaunt with the wrongness that lives outside the valley, and I spent *go* — one word, three days of my life gone like water through a cracked jar, and the wolves went, and I lay in the leaves feeling myself become thinner in some place that has no name.

There was the ferryman whose boat was breaking apart in the flood-current, his two children clinging to the mast, and I spent *hold* — and the river held the boat together long enough, and a week of my life went into the water, and the ferryman never knew he had been paid for out of my flesh. He gave me bread. I ate it. It was fair trade, and I have never decided whether that made it better or worse.

There was the dying goat, later, in Maren's kitchen, when Petra's face was so open and so full of hope that I could not bear to teach her the first lesson of my life — *that everything costs* — without giving her one wonder first. So I spent *whole*, and the goat stood up, and Petra looked at me as though I were a lamp lit in a dark country, and I felt the price leave me quietly, like a coin slipping from a torn pocket, and I thought: *worth it. Worth it. Say nothing else.*

She was learning my grammar. She drew her bird-foot letters and asked me questions with her eyebrows and her pencil, and she was quick, quicker than any of the palace tutors had been with the boys, quicker than Sefa herself at that age if Sefa told the truth about it. And every hour she grew more able to use what I taught her, I grew more afraid — not of her, never of her, but of the shape of what we were building between us. Because a singer needs a reason to sing, and Petra was becoming mine.

If the crown's riders came over that ridge tomorrow, I knew exactly what I would spend to save her. I knew to the word.

That was the trap. That had always been the trap. The crown did not need to catch singers. It only needed to give us someone we loved in front of a danger, and wait.

* * *

Petra woke with a start, the way she always did, as if sleep were a country she'd been exiled from. "*Sorry — did I — how long—*" She blinked at the window, at the grey light, at me sitting upright with my hands folded like a good little statue.

I signed: *long enough. You snore.*

"I do *not*—" Then she caught my expression, whatever it was — my face does things without my permission; it is the one part of me that spends freely — and she laughed, and rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, and said, "Right. Lesson. Before Maren's back. Show me the word for *water* again."

I showed her. Not the true-word — never that, not yet, though she didn't know there was a difference, and I hadn't decided whether telling her would protect her or break something in her that I liked unbroken. I showed her the everyday grammar, the scaffolding-words, the ones that only shaped meaning among speakers and moved nothing in the world. Those were safe. Those were the tongue with its teeth drawn, and I fed them to her spoonful by spoonful, and she swallowed them greedily and asked for more.

She was making signs now too — clumsy, wonderful signs, her big farm-girl hands turning my small precise gestures into something like weather. We talked that way while the fire woke and Maren's kettle began its low mutter. *Hungry? Yes. Afraid? Yes. Of the riders? No. Of what?*

Of that question I signed nothing at all, and Petra watched my empty hands for a long moment, and I watched her decide not to press, and I loved her a little for it, the way you love a door that chooses not to open into a room you're not ready to show.

Here is what I could not tell her yet, though I rehearsed it nightly in the dark like a woman rehearsing a confession:

Every true-word I teach her brings the rite closer, and the rite needs a vessel, and I am the vessel, and vessels are for pouring out. Sefa's fragments — the stolen pieces of the unbinding song, sewn into my memory before we fled, before the burning, before everything — those fragments are meant to be sung by a choir. But choirs are dead. I am the choir. And when the thirteenth moon rises and the song must be sung, either I sing it and pour myself out to the dregs, or I stay silent, and the crown eats the world one memory at a time forever, wearing King Aldric's smiling glassy face until the last person who remembers him truly also dies.

There was no version of the arithmetic where I survived it. I had known that since the laundry court. Sefa had known it when she kissed my ear and told me to guard my silence — not to save my life, but to save my death for the right moment, the way you save the best wood for the coldest night.

So I rationed. Four hundred and eleven words, maybe fewer now, maybe fewer. Each one a day or a week or a name I'd never get back. And the strange mercy inside all of it — the part that shamed me and warmed me at once — was this: because I spent so rarely, every word I had ever spoken blazed in my memory like a festival. Other people's lives blurred past them, days sticking together like wet leaves. Mine stood apart, each one numbered, each one paid for in advance. I remembered the wolf morning leaf by leaf. I remembered the ferryman's children's faces, the exact chipped tooth of the smaller one. I remembered the goat staggering up on new legs while Petra laughed and cried at once.

A short life, but a counted one. Sefa said the oldest singers called that *singing thin* — pouring the wine out drop by drop so the cup stays bright. She said it was holy. She said it was also the loneliest thing a person can be.

She was right about both.

* * *

Maren came back from the boundary stones at midday, moving fast for an old woman, and the look on her face emptied the kitchen of comfort.

"They've been asking in the lowland farms," she said, without greeting anyone. "Riders. Grey livery. Polite as priests." She set her staff down with a click that sounded louder than it was. "Asking after a child."

Petra went white. I went still, which is my whiteness.

"How far?"

"A day. Maybe less, if they ride through dusk, and they will. Things like that don't mind the dark." Maren's eyes came to me then, and for the first time since I'd arrived she looked at me the way Sefa had looked at me in the laundry court — not as a child to be sheltered but as a thing being weighed, a blade being judged for balance. "Girl. If they come through that door, can you—"

"*No,*" I signed, fast, sharp enough that my hands shook. *No. You don't know what I am. You don't know what I'm worth spent.*

"She means can you fight," Petra translated unnecessarily, her voice cracking. "Xanthe, if it's you or—"

But Maren was shaking her head. "No," she said slowly. "That's not what I was going to ask." She crossed the kitchen and crouched before me, joints popping, until her faded eyes were level with mine, and she said, in a voice stripped of everything but care: "Can you *run*, child? Truly run, all the way to the capital, if it comes to that? Because I've been reading the old signs on my boundary stones, and the ward is fraying, and I think — I think the valley was only ever a pause in your story, not a home for it."

Outside, far off on the ridgeline, thin as a needle-thread of sound, a horn blew once.

Not a hunting horn. A courteous horn. A horn that said: *we see the chimney smoke, we are in no hurry, we are certain of our welcome.*

Maren rose and began pulling boards off the pantry wall, and behind them I saw the mouths of tunnels breathing cold earth-smell, and Petra grabbed her cloak and my wrist, and I let her.

I counted as we went down into the dark. Four hundred and nine.

I had not spent anything. I simply wanted, one last time in a safe place, to know the number — the way you look back at a lit window before you walk into the winter, not because looking helps, but because you want to remember there was light, and how much of it, and exactly where.
