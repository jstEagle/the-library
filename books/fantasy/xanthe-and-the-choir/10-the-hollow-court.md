## 10. The Hollow Court

The capital announced itself before they saw it — a smell like cold candle wax and cut flowers, sweet and wrong the way a fruit is wrong when it has begun to rot from the inside. Petra smelled it from the crest of the last hill, where the road bent and the whole city of Vell opened below them like a fan of white bone: towers, spires, the great shell of the palace catching the afternoon sun so that it seemed to glow from within, as if something inside it were burning without smoke.

Xanthe stopped walking. She had done little enough talking since the tinker's wagon — her words were rationed like water in a drought, and Petra had learned not to ask for them — but she made a sound then, low in her throat, and her hand found Petra's sleeve.

"I know," Petra said. "I see it."

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, and it would keep being the first thing all the way to the throne room: the capital was *beautiful*, more beautiful than anything Petra had ever imagined, and every lovely surface of it was lying.

They came down through the outer quarters, where the streets were wide and swept and the people smiled at strangers. That was the second wrongness. In the valley, no one smiled at strangers; you narrowed your eyes at strangers, you counted your spoons after they left. Here, everyone smiled, and the smiles were identical — the same curve of lip, the same crinkle at the corner of the eyes, passed from face to face like coins worn smooth by too many hands.

"Good day to you," said a baker, handing Xanthe a bun she hadn't asked for and hadn't paid for. He didn't seem to expect payment. He didn't seem to expect anything. "Good day to you," he said again, to the next customer, with exactly the same inflection, the same tilt of the head, the same pause between the words as if he were reading them off an inner page.

Petra pulled Xanthe onward. Behind them, the baker said, "Good day to you," to a third person, and Petra did not look back to see whether his mouth kept smiling when the words ran out.

The market square was worse, or lovelier, depending on which way you chose to be broken by it. Stalls overflowed with pears and ribbons and copper pots; fountains played; musicians played on the corner — the same eight bars, over and over, and when Petra stopped to listen she realized they were not playing the same piece badly. They were playing it perfectly. Perfectly the same. Every repetition identical down to the breath the flautist took before the fourth bar, the small hesitation of the bow before the final note, as though the whole performance had been copied — not remembered, but *copied*, ink pressed to paper, again and again.

A woman in a green dress walked past them laughing at a joke. Ten steps later, she laughed again. Petra watched her circle the fountain twice, laughing each time at nothing, at some joke that had happened years ago and was still happening, would always be happening, inside her.

"What do they remember?" Petra whispered to Xanthe, though she already knew. Oskar had taken one taste of her memory and she had felt the hole for days, a socket where a tooth had been, her tongue returning to it helplessly. What must it be to have the whole mouth emptied?

Xanthe looked up at her with those dark, unhurried eyes, and slowly raised one hand, and drew one finger across the other, once, twice — their private sign, invented on the road, meaning *taken*. Then she turned her palm upward and spread her fingers: *everything*.

* * *

They found lodging above a chandler's shop because it was the only door whose keeper didn't smile at them. The chandler was an old man with a scarred cheek and a suspicious squint, and Petra could have wept with gratitude at the sight of ordinary distrust.

"Festival tomorrow," he grunted, taking their coin. "Court holds open audience for the equinox. Whole city turns out." His squint deepened. "You're not from here."

"No," Petra said.

"Huh." He counted their coins twice, bit one. "Don't stare at the courtiers. They don't like being seen." And he shuffled off up the stairs, muttering, leaving Petra to wonder what *seen* meant in a city where nobody seemed to see anything.

That night Petra lay awake under the eaves while Xanthe slept curled against her side, and listened to the city. Somewhere past midnight she heard it clearly for the first time: beneath the wind and the distant fountains, the capital murmured. Hundreds of voices, soft, overlapping, coming from the great house districts and the palace hill — conversations, laughter, snatches of song, all of it faintly wrong in pitch, all of it repeating. Not insomnia. Not ghosts. A city talking in its sleep, replaying itself, a music box wound too tight and never allowed to stop.

In the morning they dressed plainly and joined the river of festival-goers flowing up the processional avenue toward the palace, and Petra understood why the chandler had warned her.

The court received them in the Hall of Nine Windows, a vaulted space so full of light it seemed built out of it, nine vast windows of tinted glass pouring amber onto a floor of pale stone, onto silk gowns and silver collars, onto the most beautiful gathering of people Petra had ever seen. Courtiers lined the walls in ordered ranks like orchard trees, and they *were* beautiful — every one of them groomed and graceful, faces composed into expressions of serene, attentive pleasure.

And they were empty.

Petra felt it before she understood it, a crawling behind her breastbone, the same instinct that made her distrust the smiling baker. She watched a lady in dove-grey greet a visiting lord with warm delight: *"Lord Cassel! Years, surely — how is your mother?"* — and then turn away, take three steps along the wall, and greet another lord with the same warm delight: *"Lord Cassel! Years, surely — how is your mother?"* The second lord answered with equal warmth, word for word the same answer the first had given, and both of them smiled through it, and neither of them knew.

Near the third window, two young men argued amiably about a horse race. Petra lingered, listening, and realized with a slow cold turning in her stomach that the race they argued about had happened — she caught the date from their looping exchange — eleven years ago. Their opinions were old. Their jokes were old. One of them quoted the other's reply half a beat before it came, like a man reciting a poem he'd loved to death.

"They're wearing yesterday," Petra breathed. "All of them. They're parading around inside other years."

She looked for faces that flickered, mouths that stumbled, anyone who might still be home behind their own eyes. There were a few — an elderly chamberlain whose hands shook, a young maid who flinched when the doors boomed open, a fat merchant whose smile slipped repeatedly into confusion, as though he kept waking somewhere unfamiliar and being smoothed back to sleep. But they were few, and they hid their waking like a shameful illness, and the rest of the hall shone around them, polished, hollow, humming with borrowed life.

And above it all, on a dais beneath the central window, sat the King.

* * *

Petra had heard Aldric's name all her life, the way you hear the names of weather. The good king. The king who kept the roads safe, who ended the border wars, who loved his gardens. Maren had told stories about him once, in the years before everything went grey — stories with jokes in them, a king who got mud on his boots. Petra had imagined an old man with a laugh.

What sat the throne was a man perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, dressed in deep blue and cloth-of-gold, a slim circlet — not even a crown, only a circlet, and somehow that was worse, that the thing could ride something so small — resting on greying hair. His face was handsome. His hands, resting open on the arms of the throne, were gardener's hands, scarred and capable. Everything about him was dignified, kingly, calm.

His eyes were glass. Not blind-glass, not the dull film of the dead. This glass was *alive* — it reflected the window light in tiny sharp points, and it moved, tracking the crowd, tracking the courtiers, tracking, at the last, Petra herself. And there was nothing behind the movement. It was the motion of a face in a mirror: perfect, responsive, and entirely on the surface.

The King rose to give festival blessing. His voice was rich and kind, and it carried to every corner of the hall without effort.

"People of Vell. Another year gathered safely in. Another year of the kingdom's long peace, which is my pride, and my joy, and the whole labor of my reign." A pause, precisely measured, and gentle smiles moved down the ranks of courtiers in a wave, like wind through wheat. "My ancestors built this kingdom. I will keep it. Nothing that is yours will be lost to you. Nothing that you love will be taken from you. Remain, and be kept."

The hall sighed — an actual sigh, hundreds of breasts releasing together, a sound of such contentment that it should have been lovely and instead raised every hair on Petra's neck. *Remain, and be kept.* The words settled over the crowd like a blanket over a birdcage.

But it was not the words that stopped Petra's heart. It was this: as the King spoke the last line, his glassy gaze — drifting, mirror-moving, empty — crossed Petra's face in the crowd.

And stopped.

And the eyes did not change. The face did not move. The King went on smiling his mild royal smile at the hall at large, exactly as before, his voice continuing in its warm cadence —

— but behind the glass, behind the reflection, at a depth no eye had any right to reach, something *looked* at her.

She felt it the way you feel a finger laid on the back of your neck. It considered her. It considered the girl beside her, small and quiet, head bowed, radiating with practiced skill the perfect unremarkable blankness of a servant child. It considered them both, unhurried, and then — the eyes unchanged, the voice uninterrupted, the blessing flowing on like honey — something behind the King's gaze *smiled*.

Not on the mouth. The mouth stayed regally mild. The smile happened underneath, in the place where a soul ought to sit, and it came up through the glass of the eyes like light through deep water, and it was delighted.

*There you are,* said the smile. *I wondered when you would come to be kept.*

Petra's legs nearly failed her. Xanthe's hand closed on her wrist — hard, harder than the small fingers should manage — and Petra understood that Xanthe had felt it too, had perhaps been feeling it for years, hunted across half a kingdom by that patient subterranean delight.

The King finished the blessing. The hall applauded in waves, each rank clapping in identical rhythm. The King sat. The glass eyes resumed their mild drift across the crowd, and did not find her again, and somehow that was worse — being found, and then dismissed, filed away, *kept* for later.

* * *

They did not run. Running was a language everyone in Vell would understand and report. They walked — slow, festival-paced, two more anonymous faces in ten thousand — down the processional steps and into the gardens the public was permitted to tour on equinox day, because Petra's thoughts had snagged on a single splinter and she could not leave it until she'd drawn it out.

*Nothing that you love will be taken from you.*

Oskar had shown her the mechanism. Take a memory, and the person doesn't miss it — not truly, not for long. The tongue returns to the socket and finds the socket smooth, and forgets there was ever a tooth. The courtiers did not mourn their yesterdays. They *replayed* them. They lived inside the best hours of lives they no longer possessed, warm and painless, forever.

"It's not stealing," Petra whispered. They stood beside a hedge of winter roses, and she kept her lips barely moving, a woman murmuring to a child. "Stealing leaves a hole. This — this is different. He wasn't lying. *Nothing will be taken from you.* They gave it. Or they were made to want to give it. They're happy, Xanthe. That's what's in the hall up there. Everyone is happy."

Xanthe's face, turned up toward her, was pale and set. Slowly, deliberately, the child took Petra's hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, and then moved Petra's hand to Petra's chest, and held it there. Then she pointed back up toward the bright windows of the hall, and shook her head once.

*Nothing in there.*

"I know." Petra swallowed. "I know. Happy isn't the same as alive."

But even as she said it, the temptation brushed her, light as moth wings — because she had paid Oskar one kitchen, one ordinary evening of Maren's bread and humming, and she still reached for it twenty times a day and found smooth socket, smooth socket. What would she not give, some dark honest part of her asked, to never feel that reaching again? To live inside a preserved evening forever, bread warm forever, Maren humming forever, no hole, no hunger, no road, no burning house? *Remain, and be kept.* The crown-spirit did not need to force anyone. It only needed to make the cage comfortable enough, and souls would line up at its door.

The garden path curved toward a high wall, and beyond the wall — glimpsed through a wrought-iron gate, guarded and private — lay a second garden, smaller, wilder, unmistakably tended by hand rather than decree. Roses gone slightly legible with neglect. Bean rows staked with careful crooked stakes. A wheelbarrow.

And a figure in shirtsleeves among the bean rows, broad-shouldered, grey-haired, kneeling in the dirt with his scarred hands in the soil.

Petra's breath left her. The circlet was gone, the blue and gold gone, but she knew the shoulders, knew the shape of him from the dais, knew — impossibly, intimately, from Maren's old fireside stories — the mud on his boots.

The King of Vell was on his knees in his garden, planting something, alone, and even at that distance Petra could see that his movements were different here. Not glass-smooth. Halting. Searching. As if the gardening was the one thread of himself the crown had never needed to harvest — the one room in the house too humble to loot — and inside it, some small stubborn remnant of Aldric still knelt in the dirt, planting beans in the dark, waiting.

As she watched, the King lifted his head and looked directly at the gate. At her. And this time there was nothing behind the look at all, no depth, no smile, only the mild glassy drift — but his hand, his scarred gardener's hand, closed slowly around a young bean plant and crushed it, and crushed it, and crushed it, and his face never changed at all.

From the gate behind them came a voice, smooth as poured cream.

"You'll forgive me. Guests are not permitted past this point."

Petra turned. A tall figure in grey stood on the path, serene and beautifully dressed, eyes reflecting the daylight at a slight, impossible angle, like a cat's.

"But then," said Chancellor Vey, smiling gently, "you are not really guests, are you? I've been waiting for you both a very long time."
