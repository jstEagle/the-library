## 11. The Chancellor's Kindness

They made it eleven streets into the capital before the city itself betrayed them.

Petra had thought they were being clever — hood up, Xanthe's hand tucked in hers, moving with the market crowds the way Maren had taught her to move through a crowded fair: not fast, not slow, never looking at anything twice. But the capital did not need eyes. The cobblestones remembered everyone who walked them, and somewhere beneath her boots she felt a shiver pass through the stone, a ripple of recognition traveling ahead of them like word sent to a householder that guests were coming.

The alley mouth at the end of Copperlane simply stopped being an exit.

Grey-cloaked riders filled it, unhurried, their horses standing with the terrible patience of animals that had been spelled still. More appeared behind. When Petra turned, the crowd had thinned around them in a perfect circle of empty cobbles, as though the market itself had been asked politely to step back.

And through the circle came Chancellor Vey.

Petra had seen her from across the great hall two nights ago, when they'd slipped into the palace's outer court among petitioners — had seen the serene grey figure beside the throne and thought, *that is a woman who has never once been surprised.* Up close, the impression was worse. Vey was beautiful the way frost on a window was beautiful: precise, patterned, and formed by cold. Her hair was silver shot through with iron, bound in a coil so neat it looked drawn rather than grown. And her eyes —

Her eyes reflected light wrong. Not like an animal's, which caught the glow and threw it back doubled. Like polished metal reflecting a room that wasn't there. When she looked at you, you had the sense of something behind the look, adjusting its angle.

"You've come a long way," Vey said, "to die tired in my city."

"I'm not tired," Petra said, which was true, and stupid.

"No." Something moved at the corner of Vey's mouth — not quite a smile; more the memory of where smiles went. "You're frightened, hungry, and protecting a child who could end the world as I have built it. But not tired. I'll grant you that." Her gaze slid past Petra and settled on Xanthe, and every hair on Petra's arms rose. "Hello, little singer."

Xanthe pressed against Petra's side. She didn't speak. She hadn't spoken a true-word since the tinker's wagon, and Petra understood now why — understood what each syllable cost her — and so Petra stepped squarely between the child and the chancellor, arms out, heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door.

"If you touch her—"

"Touch her?" Vey sounded genuinely puzzled, and that was somehow the most frightening thing yet. "Child, do you have any idea what you're carrying? What she is? You've been dragging the last voice of the old choir through mud and hedgerows for weeks like a sack of turnips." She lifted one grey-gloved hand, and the riders closed in without hurry, without menace, the way water closes over a dropped stone. "No one here will harm her. No one would *dare*."

The last thing Petra saw clearly before the hood came down was Xanthe's face — furious, terrified, and mouthing one silent word at her over and over:

*Run. Run. Run.*

She did not run. She should have. Later, in the long grey hours, she would take the memory of that moment out and examine it from every side: the moment when running was possible and she chose instead to let the guards take her arm, because letting go of Xanthe's hand felt like a thing no version of her could survive doing.

* * *

They were not taken to a dungeon.

That was the first wrongness. Dungeons were what you expected, and dungeons were honest — stone, dark, damp, cruelty with its collar unbuttoned. Instead the guards walked them through corridors of pale marble into a tower wing that smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, and put them in a room with a fire already lit, a table laid with bread and honey and pears, and two beds with clean linen folded at their feet.

"Chancellor Vey asks your pardon for the roughness of the escort," said the servant who admitted them — a woman with kind eyes and a strange, placid blankness behind them, like a room swept clean of furniture. "She will call on you after supper."

Then the door shut, and Petra heard the soft, unmistakable click of a lock, and stood in the middle of all that comfort feeling like a fly that had been invited into honey.

"They're feeding us," she said aloud. "Why are they feeding us?"

Xanthe had gone to the window. It was barred, but finely barred, wrought into climbing vines, and beyond it the capital spread out in the last copper light of evening — spires and rooftops and, rising above everything, the half-built skeleton of a vast structure on the far hill, cranes standing against the sky like the ribs of something being born. Xanthe touched the glass with two fingers and drew a shape in the condensation of her own breath. A crown. Then she crossed it out.

"I know," Petra said quietly. She sat down on the edge of the bed nearest the door. "I know what they want. They want your voice. That's what Maren—" Her voice snagged. Saying Maren's name out loud, here, in this soft lavender-smelling cage, made the loss new again. Maren holding the ward. Maren getting smaller and smaller behind the boundary stones as they ran. Maren, alive or not, Petra did not know. "That's what they want you for. So don't give it to them. Whatever they ask. However they ask. Even if they're kind. Especially if they're kind."

Xanthe turned from the window. In the firelight her face was old in a way no child's face should be — the face of someone rationing herself down to nothing, spending herself coin by coin. She crossed the room, took Petra's hand, and traced on her palm, slowly, deliberately, the signs they had begun building together on the road: *listen*. Then: *learn*. Then, hesitating, a third sign Petra didn't know, which Xanthe repeated until Petra guessed at it — something like *watch*, something like *wait*, something like *there are doors I can see and you cannot*.

"Fine," Petra said. "We listen. We learn. We wait." She squeezed the small hand. "But we don't say yes."

Xanthe almost smiled.

Supper came on trays — actual supper, roast fowl and roots and a pot of honeyed tea — and Petra ate because refusing food in enemy territory was a luxury only the well-fed could afford, and because Xanthe was watching her eat with the grave attention of a scholar taking notes. Somewhere between the fowl and the tea, exhaustion ambushed her, and she slept in the chair by the fire with Xanthe's head heavy on her shoulder, and dreamed of green things growing, and woke to a knock.

Chancellor Vey entered alone.

No guards. No grey riders. Just the woman herself, carrying a covered dish and a decanter, wearing robes of dove-grey wool without a single ornament, and Petra scrambled upright with her heart in her throat while Vey set the dish down — candied ginger, of all things — and poured tea into three cups as if she were any hostess in any house in the kingdom.

"Sit," Vey said. "Please. I promise you, the chairs in this room have never eaten anyone."

Xanthe did not sit. Petra didn't either, but Vey seemed entirely unbothered; she took her own cup and settled by the fire with the sigh of a woman ending a long day.

"Do you know what I was, before the crown?" she asked the flames. "A hospital matron. In Marrowfield, during the flux winter. Have you heard of the flux winter?"

"No," Petra said.

"Of course not. It's forgotten. Everything is forgotten; that is the whole tragedy of being human." Vey turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "I watched four hundred people die in five months. Children mostly. And do you know what I remember of it now? Nothing. The crown took it — the screaming, the smell, the little hands. It harvests pain first, you see; pain is the richest vintage. And what it left me instead was—" she paused, searching, "—peace. I remember that I suffered. I remember it the way you remember a story someone told you. I can hold the fact of it without bleeding."

"That's not peace," Petra said. "That's just forgetting with better manners."

Vey looked at her then, fully, and for a moment the wrongness in her eyes flickered and something very old and very tired looked out. "Yes," she agreed softly. "That is exactly what I said. At first. Sit down, girl. I'm not going to hurt the child, and I am not going to hurt you, and I would like very much to stop being shouted at with silences."

Against all sense, Petra sat. Xanthe remained standing, planted between Petra and the chancellor like a small grey-eyed sentinel.

"I'm going to tell you what the crown wants," Vey continued, "because you've imagined something monstrous, and the truth will serve us both better. The crown does not want the child dead. Dead voices sing nothing. The crown wants her *voice* — the last whole instrument of the old grammar — because with it, the work can be finished." She gestured vaguely toward the window, toward the skeleton on the hill. "Do you know what we're building up there?"

"A cathedral," Petra said. "We saw it. Everyone says it's for yesterday."

"Everyone says that because everyone remembers being told that, which is not quite the same thing." Vey smiled faintly at her own joke, and it was a real smile, which unsettled Petra more than the metal eyes had. "It is a cathedral, yes. But not of stone. Stone is just the scaffolding. We are building a cathedral of *memory*, child. Every harvested memory — every grief, every terror, every last hour of every dying man in this kingdom — is preserved. Held. Kept safe in the crown's keeping, replayed in beauty forever. No one suffers anything twice. No one loses anyone truly, because everyone they've lost is kept, whole, singing in the vaults. Death becomes a door into a library instead of a hole in the world."

"And the people those memories were torn out of?" Petra asked. Her voice shook. "The courtiers repeating conversations from years ago? The king sitting on his throne with glass eyes?"

"The donors," Vey said, without flinching, "live comfortably. Better than comfortably — painlessly. That is the entire point." She leaned forward, and her voice lost its polish and became, horribly, sincere. "You think I serve a parasite. I know what you've been taught on whatever hedge-road brought you here. But consider, honestly, what the crown is. It found a kingdom drowning in suffering and it began to drink, yes — drink is the ugly word — but what else would you call mercy that works? Every soul it takes a memory from sleeps better that night. Every grief it swallows is a grief *ended*. It is not a hunger, whatever your witch-tales say. It is a surgery. And surgeries require—"

"Instruments," Petra whispered.

"Instruments," Vey agreed. She turned to Xanthe at last, and her expression gentled into something so nearly warm that Petra's stomach turned over. "The old grammar is dying. This girl is the last vessel of it, and every word she speaks burns years off her life — oh, yes, I know that too; I know more than you think. Left alone, she will spend herself down to silence and die unknown in a ditch, and the language dies with her, and nothing is saved. But under the crown?" Vey spread her hands. "Under the crown, her voice is kept. Every word she has ever spoken, preserved eternally. Her sacrifice made *permanent*. She would become the final instrument of the greatest mercy this world has ever known — remembered forever, honored forever, singing forever. Sanctuary, child. That is what I am offering. Real sanctuary. The only sanctuary that lasts."

The fire popped. Xanthe had gone very still, and Petra realized she was trembling — not with fear, Petra thought, but with something worse. Recognition. Because Vey was not lying. That was the unbearable thing. Every word of it was true, or true enough, and the vision was beautiful the way the cathedral on the hill was beautiful, and somewhere underneath, Petra felt the awful pull of it herself: *no one suffers anything twice. Everyone you've lost is kept.*

Maren would be kept.

"You're almost right," Petra heard herself say. Her voice came out low and steady, and she marveled at it. "That's the worst part. You're almost right. But memories aren't *things*, Chancellor. They're not grain in a granary that you can move from barn to barn. A memory taken isn't preserved. It's killed and stuffed. There's a difference between keeping a bird and keeping its song, and you—" she stood, "—you're building a cathedral full of taxidermy."

Something flickered across Vey's face. Hurt, maybe. Or its memory.

"Your mother said something very like that," Vey said quietly.

The words landed like ice water. "What did you say?"

"Your mother. Lira Nakamura. Royal singer, second voice of the unbinding choir, fled mid-rite nineteen years ago with an unfinished song in her mouth." Vey rose too, smooth as water finding its level, and collected the teacups as though they had merely concluded a pleasant visit. "Yes, we know who you are. We've known since the boundary stones sang your name. The crown remembers everything, child — even the things people ran away to forget." She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back with those eyes that reflected a room that wasn't there. "Think about the offer. Both of you. Think about how much easier it would be to stop running. And when you're ready to talk — really talk — ring the bell. Someone will always answer."

The lock clicked behind her.

Petra stood in the lavender-scented silence, her hands shaking now that it was safe to shake, and Xanthe crossed the room and took her face in both small hands and made her look at her.

"She knew Mother," Petra breathed. "Xanthe — the ledger — the archives — if Vey knows my mother's name, then there's a record, there's a *place*, there's—" Hope lit in her chest like a struck match, reckless and bright. "There's proof of what Mother paid. Of what she was. Maybe even of what she left unfinished."

Xanthe's grip tightened. Slowly, deliberately, she traced on Petra's palm: *trap*.

"Yes," Petra said. "Probably a trap. Almost certainly a trap." She looked at the barred window, at the sleeping draught she now suspected was in the honeyed tea, at the elegant lock on the elegant door, and felt the shape of the maze close around them — and then felt, at its edges, something the chancellor had not accounted for. "But Xanthe — traps have walls. And walls go somewhere. Down, usually."

Xanthe tilted her head.

"Old palaces have crypts," Petra said, and the match-flare of hope steadied into something harder and hotter. "And crypts have doors that even chancellors forget."
