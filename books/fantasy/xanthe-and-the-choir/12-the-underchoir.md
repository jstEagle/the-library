## 12. The Underchoir

### Petra

The tower stairs went down.

Petra had expected up — up toward the roof, the gutters, some desperate scramble across the palace's spine — but Xanthe had taken her wrist at the landing on the ninth floor and pulled her *down*, past the sleeping servants' quarters, past a door that breathed cold air like a sleeping animal, into a spiral of stone so old that the steps had worn into shallow bowls, as if a thousand years of feet had been ladling them out one grain at a time.

"You know this place," Petra whispered. It wasn't a question. The child moved through the dark like water remembering its channel.

Xanthe didn't answer. She never answered when an answer would cost. But her hand tightened around Petra's wrist, and her pace quickened, and above them — muffled by stone and distance but unmistakable — came the sound of Chancellor Vey's voice, still courteous, still gentle, calling down the stairwell in tones of perfect reason:

"There is no shame in coming back, little singer. I have kept your room exactly as it was."

The words slid down the spiral after them like oil poured slowly. Petra felt them trying to settle into her skin, trying to make themselves reasonable. *Your room. Exactly as it was.* For one vertiginous moment she could almost see it: a small chamber, a narrow bed, a window with a view of gardens she had never seen, waiting for a child who had never lived there—

"Stop," she said aloud, to herself, to the words. "It's not true. It's not my memory."

Xanthe looked back at her, eyes catching what little light there was. And in that look Petra saw something that frightened her more than Vey did: recognition. The child knew exactly what those words did. Knew from the inside.

They reached the bottom. A door of black wood, iron-banded, with no lock that Petra could see — only a recessed hollow shaped, absurdly, like a mouth. Xanthe pressed her palm flat against it and spoke.

One word. Petra didn't catch it — she was learning that Xanthe's true-words were like that, slipping past the ear the way a fish slips past a net, leaving only the sense of having missed something. But the door heard it. The iron bands shivered. The mouth-hollow exhaled a breath of air that smelled of dust and candle-tallow and, beneath both, something green — the smell of living things growing where no one tended them.

The door opened inward onto darkness, and the darkness opened onto singing.

Not real singing. Not yet. More like the *memory* of it — a hum threaded through the air, half-heard, the way you might hear a melody years later in the sound of wind through a broken window. Petra stood on the threshold, her heart hammering against her ribs, and understood that they had just closed the door between themselves and the world above, and that whatever waited below had been waiting a very long time.

"Xanthe," she whispered. "What is this place?"

For once, the child answered — not in her own tongue, which would have cost her, but in the trade-speech she was slowly assembling from Petra's lessons, each word chosen like a coin counted out of a nearly empty purse.

"Where the choir went," she said, "when it decided not to be eaten."

* * *

### Xanthe

Down, down, down, and the dark grew warm, and the dark grew full.

Xanthe knew these stones. Not this palace — she had never walked this palace; the nursery she remembered was the old one by the river, before the crown ate its way east — but all palaces were built atop the same bones. Every kingdom stacked itself upon the graves of the singers who taught its first kings to speak. Her teachers had told her so, in the years when she had teachers, in the language that made things true: *We do not live above the dead. We live inside their mouths.*

So when she had seen the recessed door behind the tapestry of the hunt, she had known it for what it was: a crypt-door, and crypt-doors remember their makers.

She had spent a true-word on it. One word, gone now, folded into the wood forever, and the wood would hold the door shut behind them until it rotted or the world ended. She felt the absence of the word already — a small smooth gap somewhere behind her sternum, like a tooth lost too early. She was rationing what remained. Eleven words left, perhaps twelve if she was careful, and each one bought less time than the last. She did not tell Petra this. Petra carried enough.

But the singing — the almost-singing in the dark ahead — that was worth spending breath on, even ordinary breath.

Because she recognized it.

Not the tune. Not any particular voice. What she recognized was the *shape of the damage*. Every choir learns the sound of a voice that has torn out its own grammar. It has a specific raggedness, a hole where a note should resolve, the way a man missing two fingers still grips a cup but differently, carefully, ashamed. The hum in the dark ahead was full of holes. Full of deliberate holes.

Someone down here had done to themselves what the crown wanted to do to all of them — and had done it first, and worse, and on purpose, to become invisible to the thing that listened for true-words.

Her throat ached. She put her hand over it, feeling the pulse there, the small stubborn drumbeat of everything she still contained.

*Hold on,* she thought, though she did not say it. Saying it would cost, and thinking it was free. *You held. I'm coming.*

* * *

### Petra

The crypts unfolded like the inside of a shell.

Corridors branched and doubled back; alcoves held stone coffins whose lids bore names worn to whispers. Someone had hung lamps here once — hundreds of them, little clay saucers of tallow — and someone still lit them, because a scattering of flames burned along the walls at intervals, each one guttering in air that should have been too stale to feed it. The light made the passages feel inhabited rather than haunted, though Petra couldn't have said why that distinction mattered. Perhaps because hauntings don't need candles.

"They're alive," she murmured. "The people down here. They're actually—"

A voice answered her from a side passage, and it was the most broken thing Petra had ever heard speak.

"Alive is a strong word for it."

The speaker stepped into the lamplight, and Petra bit back a cry.

She was older than Maren — sixty, seventy, impossible to say, age in the deep places of the earth moving strangely — and she was thin the way roots are thin, all sinew and endurance. But it was her throat that stopped Petra's heart. Scars crossed it in deliberate rows, precise as stitching, five pale lines where the vocal cords must run beneath, interrupted and re-interrupted, a lattice of self-inflicted wounds healed long ago into permanent ruin. When she spoke, her voice came out ground and splintered, like a wheel rolling over gravel, like a bell cracked and still insisting on ringing.

Beside her, in the shadows of the passage, others were gathering. Petra counted eight, then eleven, then lost count — men and women, mostly old, some young, all moving with the same careful silence. And every one of them bore the same scars at the throat. Some had cut other things besides. A man whose hands were wrapped in rags, the fingers beneath them bent wrong. A woman whose tongue, when she opened her mouth to peer at them, was a pale stump. They had amputated their own music, piece by piece, and the pieces had never grown back.

"The Underchoir," Xanthe said softly, in trade-speech, staring.

"We called ourselves that as a joke, at first." The scarred woman's ruined voice ground out the words with visible effort. "The choir beneath the choir. The ones who wouldn't sing." She turned her head, slow as a door on rusted hinges, and looked at Xanthe for a long, long moment. "And now look what walks into my crypt. A whole voice. An *unbroken* voice."

Petra stepped between them without deciding to. Her body simply refused to let anything with scars like that come closer to Xanthe. "We're passing through. We don't want trouble."

"Trouble found you already, girl. I can smell the Chancellor's courtesy on you. It clings." The woman smiled, and it was a terrible smile, cracked down the middle like her voice. "I'm Sefa. I was First Soprano of the Royal Binding Choir, back when it still had a royal to bind to. Before Aldric put the crown on his head and asked us to make the crowning *true*." Something moved behind her eyes — grief, or its fossil. "We sang him a king. And then we watched what he became, and we understood what we'd sung into the world. So we ran. And when running wasn't enough — when the crown's listening started finding us anyway, city by city, safehouse by safehouse — we stopped running and started *cutting*."

She lifted her chin, showing the lattice of scars fully, unflinching.

"A true-word needs an intact instrument. Break the instrument finely enough, in the right places, and the grammar can't pass through. The crown hears nothing. To the thing upstairs, we are silence wearing old robes." Her gaze flicked to Xanthe again, hungry and horrified at once. "Silence survives. That was the lesson. Silence survives, and singing dies, and you learn to live with the arithmetic."

"How long?" Petra asked quietly.

Sefa considered. "Since before you were born, I'd guess, by the look of you. Valley-born?" Petra flinched. Sefa saw it, and her cracked smile softened into something almost kind. "Ah. Then you know about payments too. Everyone who survived this reign paid something. Memory. Voice. Children." She spat, delicately, into the dark. "Come. If you're going to cross the crypts, you'll do it fed, and you'll do it under our lamps, because there are things in the deep galleries that even we don't disturb."

* * *

### Xanthe

They gave her bread and broth and a seat near the lamp, and Xanthe sat among the broken voices and felt like a jewel lying in a beggar's bowl.

Not because they stared — though they stared, all of them, unable to help it, the way starved people look at bread. But because of what she could hear underneath their silence. Every one of them still *contained* their music. Cut throats, mangled hands, a sacrificed tongue — none of it had removed the grammar from within them. It sat in them still, whole and starving, the way a song sits in a stringless instrument's memory. She could feel it radiating off them like heat off stones after sundown. Eleven mutilated singers, and every one of them a locked cathedral.

Sefa settled across from her, folding her long frame down onto a crate, and studied Xanthe with eyes that had clearly done decades of studying in the dark.

"Say something," Sefa rasped. "Anything. I want to hear how it moves in you."

Xanthe shook her head.

"Not a true-word, girl. I'm not a fool, and I'm not the crown. Just speech. Ordinary speech. I want to hear the shape of your training underneath it."

So Xanthe said, in trade-speech, "The broth is good."

And Sefa laughed — a horrible, wonderful sound, gravel and bells — and slapped her knee. "*Listen to it.* Listen! Even in butcher's trade-talk she puts the weight on the right syllables. Even asking after soup, she speaks like the words are load-bearing." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped, grinding lower. "That's the Old Grammar. The binding tongue. The speech that was old when the mountains were soft. My mother's mother's line carried fragments of it, and I carried fragments of hers, and I cut mine out rather than let the crown drink them — but I never forgot the *shape*, child. You don't forget the shape of the thing you amputated."

She reached out, slowly, giving Xanthe time to pull away, and touched two scarred fingers to Xanthe's throat — lightly, over the pulse.

"You're the vessel," she said. "The last one. The nursery smuggled you out — I heard rumors for years, a child with a whole voice moving north, always one step ahead of the grey riders. We prayed for you down here. Prayed with our ruined throats, silently, every night." Her fingers trembled against Xanthe's skin. "And now you've walked into my crypt with a hedge-witch's apprentice and eleven words in your chest, and you're spending them on *doors*."

Xanthe went very still. Beside her, she felt Petra tense too — *how does she know* — but Sefa waved the question away before it could form.

"The door at the top of the stairs sealed itself with a binding-grammar ward. There are maybe four grammars in the world that could write it, and three of them are buried in this room." She withdrew her hand. "Count your words carefully, little vessel. Because I think you came down here looking for a road. And I think the road you're looking for runs through a rite."

"The unbinding," Petra said hoarsely. "My mother's rite. She fled it mid-song. The valley ward is her unfinished—"

"I know what the valley ward is." For the first time, Sefa's ruined voice cracked clean in two, and what showed through the crack was raw. "I was standing next to the woman who wrote it. I was holding her hand when she ran." She closed her eyes. "Lira Coldharbour's daughter. Of course. Of course you have her jaw. Sit down, child. Both of you. Sit down, and let me show you what your mother left unfinished — because I have spent thirty years keeping the pieces of that rite safe in the only vault the crown will never search."

She tapped her own scarred throat.

"Myself."

* * *

### Petra

Later — much later, in the lamplit hollow the Underchoir used for sleep, wrapped in blankets that smelled of tallow and stone-dust while Xanthe slept against her side like a small warm coal — Petra lay awake and turned Sefa's words over and over, looking for the trap in them, and finding none, and being more frightened by that than by any trap.

The pieces of the unbinding rite were here. In this room. Carved into scarred flesh and ruined voices, hidden in the one place the crown could never listen. Sefa had shown her the first fragment tonight, tracing it in the dust with a stick because speaking it aloud, even brokenly, was more risk than the old woman would take: a rising sequence of marks, half-familiar, and Petra had known it instantly, the way you know your own name shouted across a field. It was her mother's handwriting translated into sound. It was the valley. It was home, written in the language home was secretly made of.

Refuge among the ruined, she thought. And they had given it freely — bread, blankets, lamps, and now the rite itself.

But she lay listening to the Underchoir breathe around her in the dark, eleven people who had destroyed their own music to survive it, and she understood the shape of the bargain forming, the one nobody had spoken aloud yet. Broken voices cannot sing a binding rite whole. Xanthe cannot spend enough words to carry it alone. Somewhere between the fragments in Sefa's throat and the words left in Xanthe's chest, there was a gap — and Petra's instincts, honed by Maren's kitchen and sharpened by everything since, told her that gaps like that are never filled for free.

Above them, far above, through fathoms of stone, Chancellor Vey was still searching. Still courteous. Still patient.

And in the morning, Sefa had promised, she would show them the rest of what her generation had amputated to stay alive — and Petra would finally learn exactly what her mother had run from, and exactly what had been left behind, unfinished, waiting thirty years for a daughter to arrive and pay the balance.

Somewhere in the dark, one of the broken singers began, silently, to hum.

Petra felt it more than heard it — a vibration in the stone floor, faint as a heartbeat under snow — and beside her, in her sleep, Xanthe's hand curled closed around a fistful of Petra's sleeve, and held on.
