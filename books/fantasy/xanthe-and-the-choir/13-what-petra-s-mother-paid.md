## 13. What Petra's Mother Paid

The crypt archives smelled of cold stone and older grief.

Sefa had led them down a stair that Petra was certain had not existed when they passed it the first time—a seam in the wall that opened only when Sefa pressed her ruined palm against it and breathed a word with half its letters missing. The word still worked. That was the horror of it, Petra thought as they descended. Even amputated, the old tongue worked. It simply worked crooked, like a door hanging on one hinge.

Below the Underchoir's sleeping hollows, below the shelves of bones sorted by reign, the archives opened into a vaulted chamber where candles burned without being lit. They had been burning, Sefa said, since before she was born. The crown paid for light. It wanted everything in its kingdom readable.

"Ledgers are along the east wall," Sefa rasped. Her voice came out like wind through a broken flute—she had cut the singing muscles of her throat herself, decades ago, and what remained was speech stripped to its sinew. "Everything the crown has ever taken is written there. Names. Debts. What was harvested, from whom, and why." She paused at the edge of the candlelight, and her eyes—milky at the rim, sharp at the center—found Petra's. "You said your mother's name was Ilse Nakamura."

"Ilse," Petra said. "Yes."

"Then look for her under *singers*, not under *debtors*. There is a difference." Sefa turned away. "And girl—if you find her, do not read it standing up. Whatever it says, sit down first."

Petra did not sit down. She told herself this was because sitting would mean accepting that there was something to fall from.

* * *

Xanthe wandered the aisles beside her, small fingers trailing along the ledger spines. The child had been silent for two days—not her rationing silence, which had a texture to it, a held-breath quality, but something softer. Since the Underchoir had recognized her grammar, since Sefa had knelt in the dark and spoken the fragments of a greeting in the old tongue and Xanthe had answered in full, whole words that made three grown singers weep without noise—since then, Xanthe had been quiet the way a person is quiet after crying. Petra had stopped trying to guess at it. She carried the child's silences now the way she carried her own.

The east wall rose higher than any library Petra had imagined. Ledgers were stacked in niches carved into the stone, each spine marked with a year of some calendar older than the valley's. She began low, where the oldest volumes sat, their covers made of something that was not leather and did not want to be touched. The script inside was the crown's administrative hand—precise, patient, terrible. It recorded everything like weather. *Harvest: minor. Source: cooper's daughter, age nine. Yield: first snowfall, mother's lullaby, the smell of bread.* Page after page. A kingdom eaten politely.

She found the singers' registers in the eleventh niche, set apart, bound in violet-dyed hide that had faded almost to grey.

There were forty names in the oldest book. Each entry was longer than the debtor pages, more careful, written by a different hand—one that hesitated, that pressed too hard, as though the clerk had hated the writing of it. Petra read the names without understanding them. *Maera of the Ninth Choir. Ondreth. Lissabet Vane. Sereth.* Each name followed by a word she didn't know yet, a title in the old language rendered into common script with visible difficulty:

*Rite-singer. Unbinding.*

Her mouth went dry. She knew the word *unbinding*. Maren had used it exactly once, on the night the child arrived, whispered over the crown-shaped burn scar on Xanthe's palm—*if she's what I think she is, someone will have to unbind it, and gods help whoever tries.* Maren had said it like a diagnosis. Like a death.

Petra ran her finger down the column of names. Thirty-eight rite-singers across two hundred years. And then, near the bottom of the page, in an entry whose ink was darker than all the others, as if the clerk had finally stopped hating the work:

**Nakamura, Ilse. Rite-singer, Third Voice. Status: fled mid-rite. Payment incomplete. Ward outstanding.**

The candle flames did not flicker, but everything in Petra did.

*Fled mid-rite.*

She heard Sefa's voice: *sit down first.* Her knees folded. She sat on the cold floor with the ledger open across her lap like a thing that had teeth, and she read the entry again, and again, willing the words to rearrange themselves into something survivable. They would not.

Her mother had not been a hedge-witch's friend who'd died young, as Maren always implied. Not a wanderer who had come to the valley seeking safety like everyone else. Her mother had been a singer of the royal unbinding rite—the very rite that could crack the crown—and she had run from it. Run from the palace, from the choir, from the work itself. And the ward around the valley, the stones, the blessed safe stillness Petra had chafed against her entire life—

"That's her," Petra said aloud, to no one. "The ward is her."

*Payment incomplete. Ward outstanding.* The valley wasn't a refuge that happened to exist. It was a song her mother had started and never finished. Every day of Petra's childhood—the herb garden, the boundary stones, the way no spell would light and no memory of outside survived crossing in—every day had been bought with an unfinished verse. With a woman who had fled halfway through saving the world and hidden her daughter behind the wreckage.

The knife of it went in slow. Inheritance, Petra thought, is supposed to be a gift. A house. A craft. A name. Something you carry forward because someone loved you enough to leave it. Instead she had inherited an obligation with her mother's fingerprints on it, half-done, humming in the stones, fraying at the edges because the crown had finally found where the song trailed off.

Maren had known. Maren had inked out the name in the almanac rather than let Petra see it. Had guarded the secret for sixteen years, had ordered her to hide the child, had lied to Chancellor Vey with her life—all of it to keep Petra from ever reading these words.

*You refused to be protected twice,* Petra reminded herself, and the memory of her own voice saying it steadied her hands. Fine. Then don't be protected now. Read it all.

* * *

She read until the candles guttered and relit themselves, which they did without comment, and Xanthe curled against her side somewhere in the third hour, asleep with her scarred palm open against Petra's arm. The register gave up its secrets grudgingly.

Ilse Nakamura had entered the royal choir at fourteen, a foundling with perfect pitch and the rarest gift: she could hold a true-word steady while others' voices broke around it. Third Voice—the harmony position, the one that anchored the rite. The unbinding rite required three voices and a vessel of willing memory; Petra parsed this from the marginal notes, from a training document bound into the back of the register like an appendix nobody meant laypeople to read. The crown-spirit had been bound once, long ago, by a choir whose names were lost even to this ledger. The binding was not eternal. It needed renewal every hundred years, sung at the rising of the thirteenth moon. And for two centuries the choirs had sung it—until the crown learned to eat singers faster than the palace could raise them.

By her mother's generation, four remained. Three fled or were taken. Ilse Nakamura stood at the rite-stone with the moon rising and sang the opening verses alone, and then—here the clerk's handwriting actually shook—she stopped. Mid-verse. The record did not say why. There was a marginal note in a different hand, older, angrier: *asked for the vessel's consent. Refused to sing without it. Removed from the rite by order of the Chancellor. Fled before dawn.*

Petra read that line eleven times.

*Asked for the vessel's consent.*

The unbinding rite needed a vessel of willing memory. Someone who chose, freely, to give up what they loved most. Her mother had stood at the stone with the thirteenth moon climbing and demanded to know whether the vessel truly understood, truly consented—and when the answer was no, or when the Chancellor refused to allow the question, she had stopped singing. Stopped mid-verse, in front of the crown itself, knowing what disobedience cost. And then she had run, and poured everything she had left into a ward around a valley, a song of hiding, a shelter built from the unfinished rite itself—and she had raised her daughter there, and she had died there, and the last thing she had left her was a question she'd never gotten answered:

What does it cost, and who agreed to pay?

"Oh," Petra said quietly. Her breath fogged in the crypt cold. "Oh, Mama. You weren't a coward. You were the only honest one in the room."

Xanthe stirred against her shoulder. The child's eyes opened—grey-green, feral-calm—and fixed on the ledger without surprise, as though she had known what was written there before Petra found it. Perhaps she had. The old tongue heard things. Petra was learning that the true-words were less like spells and more like listening so hard that reality confessed.

Xanthe reached out one finger and touched the words *ward outstanding*, then drew a shape in the air above the page: two hands, cupped, passing something between them.

"What?" Petra asked. "Show me again."

Xanthe repeated the gesture, slower, and then pointed at Petra, and then at the ledger, and then made the passing shape again—and Petra felt the meaning arrive the way Xanthe's true-words arrived, whole and undeniable. *This was given to you. It is yours to finish.*

"No," Petra said. Her voice cracked in the empty vault. "No, I'm not a singer. I can't hold a note in the bath. My mother had the gift and she—" She stopped, because the lie collapsed even as she shaped it. Her mother hadn't lacked anything except an answer. And Petra—Petra had spent her whole life humming to the herbs, humming to the goats, humming the tuneless little melody she'd never been able to get out of her head, the one Maren always went strange and silent whenever she caught her at it.

The valley's ward-song. Her mother's unfinished verse, living in her daughter's blood like a splinter working toward the surface.

Xanthe watched her understand. The child did not smile. She took Petra's hand instead, turned it palm-up, and traced one line across it—the same gesture, Petra realized with a chill, as the crown-shaped burn on her own palm. Then she closed Petra's fingers over the invisible mark, gently, the way you close someone's hand around a seed.

*It's planted in you already. All you have to decide is whether to grow it.*

* * *

Sefa found them there at what might have been morning, though the crypt kept no hours. The broken-voiced woman lowered herself onto the steps with the care of someone whose body had been at war with her for decades, and she looked at the open ledger for a long time before speaking.

"You found her."

"I found her." Petra's voice came out flat and strange. "Third Voice. Fled mid-rite. My whole life was her unfinished homework." She laughed, and hated the sound. "All those years I begged Maren to tell me about my mother, and the truth was sitting under an ink blot in a kitchen almanac."

"Maren hid it because the Chancellor's agents read minds through questions," Sefa said. "A girl who knew her mother was a rite-singer would have asked different questions than a girl who knew nothing. Questions have weight. Vey's people harvest weight." She leaned forward. "But that isn't what matters now."

"Then tell me what matters."

"The vessel." Sefa's milky eyes were steady. "Your mother stopped the rite because the vessel hadn't consented. Do you know who the vessel was, that night?"

Petra shook her head.

"It was her own daughter." The words fell into the crypt like stones into water. "You were six months old. The crown had eaten the other candidates, and the rite requires a vessel of willing memory, and there was no one left but a baby who couldn't consent to anything—which is to say, the Chancellor intended to consent *for* you. To give the crown your memories before you'd even made any worth taking. Your mother stood up in front of the thirteenth moon and refused to feed her infant to the god-thing in the crown, and then she ran, and she built you a valley where nothing could ever be asked of you." Sefa paused. "She traded the world's salvation for your childhood. I have never decided if it was the most selfish or the most loving thing I ever saw. I sang Second Voice beside her for six years. I was there, that night. I ran too, later, when I understood what we'd been part of."

The crypt was very quiet. The candles burned their unwavering flame.

"She didn't trade it," Petra said finally. Her eyes were wet and she let them be. "She deferred it. *Ward outstanding.* She left the debt to me—with interest." She looked down at Xanthe, asleep again against her ribs, the child's breath slow and rationed even in sleep, every hour of her life measured out in true-words she couldn't afford to spend. "Except it isn't a debt anymore, is it? Because the vessel doesn't have to be a baby. The rite needs someone who chooses. Freely." Her hand closed over the invisible mark Xanthe had drawn there. "Someone who knows exactly what it costs."

Sefa said nothing. In the silence, the ledger lay open between them, and the entry seemed to breathe: *payment incomplete, payment incomplete, payment incomplete.*

"And there's a moon left," Petra went on, because saying it aloud made it real and real was better than true. "One moon until the thirteenth rises. One chance. And I can't sing, and Xanthe can't spend what she doesn't have, and your people—" She stopped, gentling. "Your people gave what they had to the dark years ago."

"We gave our voices," Sefa said. "Not our memories." Something moved behind her ruined face—hope, maybe, or its fossil. "Girl. Sit with the ledger tonight. Read the appendix. Your mother annotated the rite in the margins—her teacher's notes, her objections, her revisions. If anyone ever wrote down how the unbinding could be finished honestly, it was the woman who refused to finish it dishonestly." She rose, joints cracking like the stair itself. "And when you've read it, come find me. Because I think I remember the melody. I've had thirty years to forget it, and I couldn't."

She climbed back up into the dark. Petra stayed.

She turned to the appendix, to her mother's cramped, furious, brilliant marginal hand—the same slanted script Petra suddenly recognized from the single letter Maren kept hidden beneath the kitchen floor, the one thing she'd never been allowed to see—and began to read.

Outside the crypt, far above, through a mile of palace stone, the cathedral bells rang a hour that did not exist, and somewhere in that wrongness, Petra was almost certain, something behind the Chancellor's smiling eyes turned its attention downward, toward the archives, and began—patiently, hungrily—to listen.
