## 14. The King in the Garden

The garden had been Petra's idea of a rumor for most of her life. Maren had spoken of royal gardens only twice, both times in the flat voice she used for things that hurt — a garden where roses grew in shapes no hedge-witch could coax, where fountains ran without pipes, where kings walked at dusk among flowers bred over centuries to bloom in colors that did not exist anywhere else. Maren had seen it once, as a young woman, before the valley, before everything. She had described it the way people describe a beautiful animal they know is dangerous.

Petra found it by following the smell of thyme.

That was the absurdity of it, the thing she would remember afterward and marvel at: after all the locked doors and guarded stairwells, after Vey's tower with its soft-voiced servants and its windows that looked out on painted skies, the way into the king's private garden was simply a door left unlatched behind the old kitchens, opening onto a corridor that smelled of earth and cut stems. No guards. The crown did not need guards here. Whatever it was doing to Aldric, it had clearly decided the garden was part of the cage, not an escape from it.

She went through the door with her heart hammering and Xanthe's hand gripping hers so tightly that the bones ached.

They had come down from the crypts three nights ago, up through a disused cistern and into the palace's servant warrens, where Sefa's people still had eyes. The Underchoir moved them like contraband, from shadowed landing to shadowed landing, and every step deeper into the living palace felt like walking into a mouth. Here the courtiers smiled with borrowed memories. Here the chandeliers burned oil harvested from someone's childhood summers — or so Sefa had said, bitter as ash, before pressing a scrap of map into Petra's hand and telling her which door would be unlocked, which night the garden would be empty of everyone but him.

*Him.* The king. The ruined man on the glass throne whom Petra had glimpsed once across the great hall, and who had looked back at her with something that was not entirely him.

"You don't have to come," Petra whispered now, crouching at the edge of the moonlit lawn, Xanthe beside her. "You could wait. If something happens—"

Xanthe's grip tightened. She shook her head once, firmly, the way she refused all offers of protection, and then she did something worse: she touched two fingers to Petra's wrist, the gesture they had built together between languages, the sign that meant *I stay. I choose.*

Petra breathed out. "All right," she said. "Together."

They crossed the lawn in the open, because there was nowhere to hide and hiding would have been a kind of lie. The moon was one night shy of full — the twelfth moon, Sefa called it, counting down — and it laid the garden out in silver and black: hedges trimmed into impossible spirals, beds of night-blooming flowers that gave off a scent like warm honey and cold stone, a fountain at the center whose water fell upward for a moment before remembering gravity. Magic lived here, unhidden, unrestrained, and Petra felt it press against her skin like the memory of cold river water, like the moment she had first crossed the boundary stones and the world outside had roared into her.

At the fountain, kneeling in the dirt with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, was an old man planting bulbs.

He did not look up when they approached. His hands moved with the slow certainty of long practice — trowel, hole, bulb, soil, press — and around him the ground was dark with turned earth, row after row of it, far more than any gardener could plant in a single night. He planted the same bed, Petra realized slowly, watching the pattern of disturbed soil. He planted the same bed over and over, and each time he finished, he forgot, and began again.

"Your majesty," Petra said.

The trowel stopped.

For a long moment the old man did not move at all, and Petra saw the wrongness of him even from behind — the stillness too complete, the shoulders held like a puppet whose strings were being considered rather than pulled. Then he set the trowel down with exaggerated care, the way a drunk man sets down a glass he does not trust himself to hold, and turned.

His face was handsome the way a portrait is handsome, preserved and slightly wrong, the skin too smooth over the bones beneath. His eyes were the color of rainwater and just as clear and just as empty — except that they weren't empty. Something *looked* out of them. Petra had felt it across the great hall and felt it again now, doubled and close: a vast attention settling over her like a hand, curious, patient, hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food.

"Visitors," the king said, and his voice was pleasant, cultured, mild. "How lovely. It has been so long since anyone visited." He smiled. The smile reached every part of his face perfectly and none of it truly. "Are you gardeners?"

"No," Petra said.

"Such a shame. Everyone should garden." He gestured at the beds with a small, gracious sweep of his arm. "Gardens need no memory. That is their great virtue. A bulb does not care what you have forgotten. You put it in the ground and it becomes what it becomes, whether you watch or not. The only honest work left." He tilted his head, studying her, and for an instant — half a heartbeat, no more — the pleasantness dropped away like a mask slipping, and underneath it his face was *terrified*, wide-eyed, mouthing something soundless. Then the mask rose again, smooth as pond ice. "Forgive me. I haven't introduced myself. I am Aldric. I am told I am king."

"I'm Petra," she said carefully. "And this is Xanthe."

"Xanthe." The name came out strangely. Not pronounced wrong — pronounced *carefully*, each syllable placed like a foot on thin ice. And behind his eyes, that vast attention flared, leaned forward, and Petra understood with a lurch of horror that the crown had heard the name and recognized it, the way a spider recognizes the trembling of its web.

She stepped sideways, putting herself between the king and Xanthe, and made her voice light. "We're lost. We were looking for the kitchens."

"Were you?" Aldric said pleasantly. "Everyone is always looking for the kitchens. Or the library. Or their mothers." He picked the trowel back up, resumed digging, and said, conversational, to the hole in the earth: "There is a girl in this palace. Small. Dark-haired. She sings in a language the chancellor finds very interesting. Have you seen her?"

"No," Petra said.

"Mm," said the king. He pressed a bulb into the soil with surprising tenderness. "I hope someone finds her soon. Before the winter. The chancellor says the winters here are very long."

*Run,* Petra wanted to say to Xanthe. *Turn around, walk back through the door, don't look at him.* But running would confirm everything, and there was something else now, threading through her fear — because the mask had slipped once already, and she was watching for it. She sat down on the fountain's rim, deliberately, insolently, as if she had every right, and said, "Do you mind if I rest? We've walked a long way."

"Not at all," said the king graciously. "Gardens are for resting."

*

They stayed. It was either the bravest or the stupidest thing Petra had ever done, sitting in the moonlight making conversation with the hollowed king while the thing inside him listened through his ears, but she could see — she became more sure of it with every minute — that the mask slipped when the attention elsewhere. When Aldric talked about the garden, about soil and bulbs and the angle of the late sun, the thing behind his eyes grew bored and receded, and the man surfaced in fragments, gasping like a drowning swimmer.

"The crocuses come first," he said, in that mild courtly voice. "Always first. Even through snow." A pause. His hands stilled. When he spoke again the voice was different — rougher, quieter, worn at the edges like a much-handled tool. "*Girl.* Listen. Don't answer me. Nod if you hear."

Petra nodded, barely.

"He watches when I think of the palace. Of Vey. Of the throne. He sleeps, almost, when I am in the soil." The words came low and fast, poured out like water from a cracked jug. "I get — moments. Less each year. He eats forward, do you understand? Not backward. Everyone thinks he eats the past. He eats the *future*. Every year I have fewer years left in me to lose. Soon there will be nothing ahead of me to take, and then—"

Then the mask slid back. Aldric smiled at a flowerbed. "Lovely soil this year," he said. "So generous."

Petra's hands were shaking. Beside her, Xanthe had gone very pale and very still, and her fingers were moving against her thigh — signing, signing, a stream of gestures too fast for Petra to follow, and when Petra caught her eye Xanthe stopped and signed slowly, deliberately: *He is still in there. Deep. Deep deep.*

"How long?" Petra asked softly, to the garden, to the air, hoping the question sounded idle.

Aldric dug. Aldric planted. "Twenty-two years," he said mildly. And then, rough and ragged, barely a breath: "Twenty-two years since the coronation. Twenty-two years since I put it on and it said *yes, this is right, this is yours* and I believed it. My father wore it. His mother before. We thought it was ours. We thought we were wearing it." A broken laugh. "It was wearing us. It wears us like — like coats. Like skins. Girl. *Girl.*"

He looked up, and for this moment the terror was not hidden at all; both faces, the pleasant king and the buried man, were present at once, layered like a double exposure, and it was the most horrible thing Petra had ever seen.

"When I am gone — and I am nearly gone, there is so little of me left that he bothers to chew — he will not stop. Do you understand? A crown is never finished. There is always another head. He will wear whoever comes next. He has *practiced*. Vey thinks she serves him. Vey thinks there will be a heaven of yesterdays at the end of it, everyone safe in their best memories forever." His voice dropped to a rasp. "There is no end of it. That is what mercy looks like to a hunger. More."

Petra thought of Vey's serene face, of her cathedral rising over the city, of her genuine, terrible conviction. She thought of Sefa's ruined voice, of the ledger with her mother's name in it, of the unfinished song folded up inside the valley stones. Her throat ached. "What do you want me to do?" she whispered.

"Nothing for me." The words came out immediate and fierce, the most alive sound he had made all night. "Don't waste anything on me. Don't spend your song pulling me out. Hear me?" He gripped the edge of the fountain, knuckles white, and his rainwater eyes fixed on hers with the whole force of the man he had been before. "I am a coat he has nearly finished wearing through. Save what's under the crown or don't — but make sure—" His voice cracked. He steadied it. "Make sure the thing does not wear my face forever. Whatever is left of me when it breaks, let it break with me. Let me be *finished*. Let me be a man who ended, instead of a mask that lasts." He laughed, wet and short. "I planted ten thousand bulbs, girl. Ten thousand springs I'll never see. Do you know why? Because it is the only work where the doing and the forgetting are the same act. For twenty-two years I have practiced disappearing. I am very good at it now."

Beside Petra, Xanthe made a sound. Small, involuntary, halfway between a sob and a note — and the whole garden heard it. The night-blooming flowers shivered though there was no wind. The fountain's upward-falling water stuttered.

Aldric's head turned toward her slowly, like a great ship coming about.

The thing behind his eyes opened fully, and Petra felt it look at Xanthe the way winter looks at the last green field. When the king spoke again, the pleasant voice was back, but it was stretched thin over something enormous, and the words came out reverent and awful:

"There she is. There's the last of the choir. Oh, little singer. Little vessel. Do you know how long I have been *hungry*?" He rose from the soil, bulbs scattering, and took one step, and the shadows in the garden bent toward him. "One word from you and I would never be hungry again. One true word and every yesterday would be safe forever, every grief mended, every dead man kept. Isn't that mercy? Isn't that *kindness*? Your mother thought so. Your mothers, all down the line, singing their little songs of unbinding—"

"*No.*" Xanthe's voice. Hoarse, rusty, spent like a coin from a nearly empty purse — and it was not a plea. It was a true-word, or close enough to one, and it hit the garden like a stone thrown into a bell.

Aldric stopped. Stood swaying. And in the silence, the mask tore — really tore, top to bottom — and for three heartbeats the old man stood alone in his own face, gaunt and mortal and weeping, looking at Petra with absolute clarity.

"Now," he breathed. "Whatever you came to find — find it *fast*. He knows her voice now. He'll tear the palace apart stone by stone. Go. Go, girl. And when it ends—" His hand fumbled at his breast and pressed something into hers, quick as a pickpocket, folding her fingers shut around it: a small iron key, worn smooth, stamped with the sigil of the royal crypts. "Sefa will know the door. Tell the broken singers the last door of Aldric opens for them. Tell them—" the mask was closing over his face like water over a drowned man, "—tell them I remembered them. All these years. In the garden. That was mine. That was the part he couldn't reach. *Tell them I kept them.*"

The pleasant smile settled back into place. The king looked down at his scattered bulbs with mild regret and knelt to gather them up.

"What strange children," he said kindly, to the soil. "Come to see an old man's garden. Well. The gate is behind the yew hedge, if you've lost your way. Mind the moat stones; they're slippery."

They went. At the yew hedge Petra looked back once. The king of the realm knelt alone in the moonlight, planting the same bed he had planted ten thousand times, humming tunelessly, patiently, a man rehearsing his own disappearance — and above his bowed grey head, unseen by anyone, the air itself seemed to lean toward the palace's tallest tower, where a light had just come on behind Chancellor Vey's window.

Petra closed her fist around the key until the teeth bit her palm, and ran.
