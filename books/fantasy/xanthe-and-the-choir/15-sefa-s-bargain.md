## 15. Sefa's Bargain

The dark beneath the palace had its own seasons, Sefa thought, and it was winter now.

She felt it in the way the others gathered — not in the loose, drifting clusters of ordinary weeks, but pressed close around the low fire of tallow-ends and candle stubs they were never meant to have. Forty-three of them in the deep crypt below the forgotten chapel, in the chamber the palace builders had hollowed out and then pretended they never had. Forty-three singers of the old tongue, each one carrying a hole in their throat where a word used to live.

Sefa had cut hers out herself, forty years ago. She could still feel the shape of it when she swallowed wrong: a jagged absence just left of center, like a stone that had been pried from a wall and never replaced. The word for *unmaking*. She had taken a hot iron to her own voice rather than let the crown hear her sing it, and she had never once regretted it — except in dreams, where her throat was whole again and the crown was listening, and she woke screaming into hands and shushing voices until the dream let go.

Now there was a girl sleeping against the far wall with mud still in her hair, and the whole fragile architecture of forty years of hiding was balanced on whether or not to wake her.

"She's the last one," said Corvin, who had been a nursery-singer before his hands had learned to shake. He sat closest to the girl, Xanthe, and watched her the way you watch a fire you're afraid will spread. "The last choir. You all heard what the Nakamura woman said. Everyone else is dead or eaten."

"Eaten," echoed Little Wren, bitterly. She was the youngest of them, sixty-one years old, and she had spent six decades perfecting a laugh that contained no true-words at all. "That's a gentle word for it. I saw them take Marisol. They didn't eat her. They *hollowed* her. There was nothing left but a shape that smiled."

"And if we help this child," said Hespera from the shadows by the water channel, "that shape comes looking for us. All of us. Every tunnel, every bolt-hole, every crack we've spent our lives learning." Her voice was flat as slate. "I didn't carve out my own tongue so that some runaway apprentice could ask me to hand the crown my name."

"Nobody's asking you to do anything," Corvin said.

"No," Hespera agreed. "But somebody is going to. Somebody always does. That's what Sefa's here for."

Every head turned toward her. Sefa let them look. She was old enough now that being looked at no longer stung; it simply was, like weather. She sat on the stone bench that generations of hidden singers had worn smooth, her ruined throat wrapped in wool even though the crypt was warm, and she waited until the silence had grown heavy enough to hold weight.

"You want me to say it's simple," she rasped. "It isn't, so I'll make it complicated instead."

A few of them almost smiled. In forty years she had learned that the best way to lead broken people was to promise them honesty instead of hope.

"The crown has eaten a kingdom's worth of memory. It ate King Aldric first, and it's been working outward ever since — through the court, through the city, down through the harvest-tithes of forgetting that Vey collects like rent. It grows fat. And things that grow fat grow slow." She held up one finger, crooked with age. "That's argument one for hiding. We stay in the dark, we keep our holes plugged, and maybe it finishes gorging itself before it ever thinks to look under its own throne room. Maybe we die old and safe in the ground, which is more than most people get."

"And argument two?" asked Wren.

Sefa looked at the sleeping girl. Xanthe had curled up small, knees to chest, one hand tucked under her cheek — and even asleep, that hand was closed around something. When Petra had finally pried it open earlier, they'd found a scrap of royal purple cloth, ancient, stiff with age, stitched at one corner with a thread of gold.

"Argument two," Sefa said slowly, "is that I knew her mother."

The crypt went very quiet.

"Ilset Nakamura. Royal singer, third chair of the unbinding choir, trained beside me from the time we were seven years old." Sefa's voice caught on the ruin in her throat and came out rough as gravel. "We sang the same exercises. We got whipped for the same mistakes. When the crown began eating singers, Ilset was the one who figured out what was happening first. She came to the rest of us with her face white as bone and told us what she'd found in the rite-chambers: that the unbinding song wasn't failing because it was weak. It was failing because someone kept *cutting verses out of it.*"

"We know," Hespera said. "You've told us. Our generation amputated the rite to hide it—"

"Not just hid it." Sefa shook her head. "We took it apart. Verse by verse. Each of us swallowed one piece and burned the maps. I took the word for unmaking. Corvin took the naming-verses. Hespera—" she nodded toward the shadows, "—took the binding of the willing vessel. Twelve singers, twelve fragments, scattered so that no one throat held the whole song. It was the only way. The crown could eat a singer, yes, but it couldn't eat what a singer had already destroyed in herself."

"But it also means," said Corvin quietly, "that none of us can finish it."

"It means," Sefa said, "that the only person alive who can carry the whole unbinding rite is a child who doesn't know she's carrying it. And the woman who ran mid-rite — who fled with an unfinished song in her mouth and built a valley out of it — was Ilset Nakamura. And the girl who walked into our crypt yesterday wearing Ilset's chin and Ilset's stubbornness is asking us for the pieces."

She stood. Her knees cracked like green wood in a fire, and she ignored them.

"So here is the bargain, and I'll put it plain because you've earned plainness. If we give Xanthe our fragments, the crown may find us. Not will — may. But Vey has hunters in every sewer and every stairwell, and a child singing true-words in the deep places rings like a bell. If we hide instead, we are safe, and Aldric stays a puppet, and the city keeps forgetting itself one tithe at a time, and eventually there won't be anything left to be safe *in.*" She let that sit. "Forty-three of us. Forty-three holes. I'm asking which you'd rather be: safe in a grave you dug yourselves, or useful for one loud season before the end."

Nobody answered right away. That was fair. She hadn't expected them to.

* * *

They argued until the tallow burned down to pools. Sefa let them. That was the discipline of the Underchoir: nobody decided in haste; nobody decided alone. Hespera spoke longest, and hardest, and at the end of it she said the thing everyone else was thinking:

"I gave up my fragment to save myself. Giving it back is dying twice."

"Yes," Sefa said simply. "It is."

And that, somehow, was the turning of it — not eloquence, not memory of Ilset, but Hespera standing in front of all of them admitting the cost aloud, and then, after a long moment, holding out her hand palm-up in the old choir-gesture that meant *I yield the floor.*

One by one, the others did the same. Some wept. Old Tam, who had been mute for thirty years, made a sound like a rusted hinge and pressed both hands over his heart. Corvin laughed — a real laugh, no true-words in it, just air and grief and something that might have been relief.

Only Wren hesitated. She looked at the sleeping child and said, "And if she fails? If the crown takes her voice and ours too, and we've handed it the last pieces of the song?"

Sefa thought about Ilset's face, young and terrified, in the lamplight of the rite-chamber forty years ago. She thought about the unfinished song still hanging over a valley like a roof that had never been finished being built.

"Then it takes everything," she said, "and at least it takes it while we're singing toward something instead of crouched in the dark waiting to be found anyway." She paused. "Also, she won't fail. She's Ilset's daughter, and Ilset never once hit a wrong note in her life. Infuriating woman."

Wren gave her fragment. They all did.

* * *

They woke Xanthe gently, the way you wake something wild — no sudden movements, no reaching hands. Sefa knelt before her on the cold stone, close enough to see the burn scar on the child's palm, crown-shaped, older than the girl should have been allowed to be.

"We're going to teach you a song," Sefa said, and then, remembering, made the hand-signs alongside the words: *song. gift. yours.*

Xanthe watched the signs with those unsettling eyes that seemed to count things — counting Sefa's wrinkles, counting the forty-three throats in the room, counting perhaps the years of hiding stacked like sediment above their heads. Then she signed back, slow and careful: *what will it cost?*

Sefa's breath went out of her. Not *what do I get.* Not *will it hurt.* *What will it cost.* A child raised in a nursery of hunted singers had learned the grammar of price before she'd learned her letters.

"Everything we have left," Sefa signed honestly. "And everything you're willing to lose."

Xanthe considered that for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched Sefa's wrapped throat, feather-light, exactly where the missing word lived, and made a sign Sefa hadn't seen since she was a girl in the choir-school: *mended,* or near enough — the gesture the youngest singers used for a broken instrument that could still be played.

Then the teaching began.

It went on for what felt like a night and half a life. One by one, the Underchoir came forward and gave up their fragments — not spoken, for most of them could no longer speak theirs, but signed, hummed through damaged throats, tapped out in rhythm on stone, drawn in candle-smoke on the walls. Corvin gave the naming-verses through trembling hands. Hespera gave the binding of the willing vessel in a voice like a knife dragged over slate, and wept without sound while she did it. Tam, who could not speak at all, drummed his fragment against Sefa's palm until she had it by heart. And last of all, Sefa leaned close to the child and breathed the word for *unmaking* into her ear — the word she had burned out of herself four decades ago, recovered now not from her throat but from forty years of keeping its shape alive in her memory, a stone remembered so faithfully it could be carved again.

Xanthe received each piece the way dry ground receives rain: silently, completely, without waste. And when the last fragment settled into her, she opened her eyes and looked at Sefa with an expression that was not gratitude, exactly. It was recognition. The look of one instrument acknowledging another.

Then, somewhere far above them, muffled through forty feet of palace stone, a bell began to ring.

Not the hour-bell. Not any bell Sefa knew. It rang twelve times, paused, and began again — and in the pause, in the silence between the twelfth stroke and the first, Sefa heard something else underneath, thin and wrong, threading up through the crypt floor like smoke: a humming. Patient. Pleased. Almost a lullaby.

Hespera was suddenly beside her, gripping her arm hard enough to bruise. "Sefa. The moon-count. What's the moon-count?"

Sefa counted backward through the fragments she'd just given away, through the years, through the nights since the child arrived — and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

"One," she said. "One moon left."

Above them, the humming swelled — and this time, unmistakably, it was singing the unbinding rite. Backwards.
