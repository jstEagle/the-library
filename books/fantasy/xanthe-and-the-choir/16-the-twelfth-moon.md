## 16. The Twelfth Moon

The twelfth moon rose over the capital like a coin held up to the light of something larger coming behind it.

Petra felt it in her teeth before she saw it — a pressure, a hum, as though the sky itself had begun counting down. The crypts beneath the palace were far from any window, but Sefa had hung a shard of polished silver from a chain in the Underchoir's main chamber, angled toward a hairline crack in the vaulting where starlight bled through, and every night now the singers gathered beneath it to watch the moon's slow fattening.

Twelve. One left.

Petra sat cross-legged on cold stone with Xanthe beside her and the fragments of the unbinding rite spread out between them on a square of grey cloth: scraps of vellum, strips of bark, a child's slate covered edge to edge in Sefa's cramped hand. The pieces the Underchoir had amputated from themselves lay here like the parts of an instrument taken apart so it could never be played — and yet all of them together still made no whole song.

"It doesn't close," Petra said, for perhaps the fortieth time that week. She traced the shape of the rite with one finger, the way Maren had taught her to trace the shape of a fever — looking not at what was there but at what was missing. "Listen. It opens like a door. It calls the crown by its oldest name. It binds the hunger to a vessel." Her finger stopped at the ragged end of the last fragment. "And then nothing. It just *stops*. Like someone set down the pen mid-word."

"Someone did," Sefa said from across the chamber. She sat apart from the others, as she always did, wrapped in a shawl against a cold the crypts did not actually have. Her voice came out in pieces, gravel and gaps, the sound of a woman who had cut her own true-words out with more courage than skill. "Your mother. Mid-*rite*. Whatever she meant to sing last, she never sang it."

The others had gone quiet around their silver shard. There were nine of them now — nine singers who had once been eleven, though Petra tried not to do that arithmetic, tried not to look at the small shrine they kept in an alcove with two names scratched into the stone and no bodies beneath it. The crown had found them anyway, eventually. It always found them eventually.

Xanthe touched the end of the rite where it stopped. She did it gently, the way she touched everything written — as if words might bruise.

Then she looked up at Petra and shook her head slowly, and made the sign they had built between them over these weeks: two fingers walking, stopping, turning back. *Not stopped. Held back.*

"Held back," Petra repeated. "By whom? By what?"

Xanthe pressed her palm flat over her own heart, then pointed at the fragment, then made the sign again.

*Held back inside someone.* And there was only one person alive who could have been holding a piece of an unfinished rite inside herself for twenty years.

*

Petra did not sleep that night. She climbed instead to the crypt's highest gallery, where the old ossuary shelves gave way to bare rock and the breathing of the stone was audible if you listened long enough — a slow tide-sound, in and out, the palace dreaming above them without knowing what slept below.

Her mother's name was in the ledger three levels down. *Ilse Nakamura, singer of the third voice, fled mid-rite, payment incomplete.* Payment incomplete. As if her mother had run out on a bill. As if she had not been running to save a daughter, to build a valley of forgetting around a child the crown would otherwise have eaten alive.

But Xanthe's signs said something else, and Sefa's silences said something else, and the rite itself said something else, and Petra had learned — from Maren, who had learned it from her own hard decades — that when three separate things say the same thing you had better listen even if it hurts.

She closed her eyes and let herself remember her mother, which she almost never did, because the memories were thin as frost and hurt worse for being thin. A voice humming over a cradle. Hands grinding herbs — no, that was Maren. That was the memory she'd paid Oskar, gone now, a hole in the shape of a kitchen. A woman standing at the boundary stones with her back straight, singing something under her breath while little Petra watched from the grass.

Singing. Always singing. Even the thinnest memory of Ilse Nakamura had song in it, the way even the poorest soil has some seed waiting.

"Oh," Petra said aloud, to the dark, to nobody. "Oh, you clever, terrible woman."

The ward around the valley wasn't just a ward. It was the rite. It was the rite's final movement, sung incomplete, buried in stone and silence, sustained by twenty years of her mother's stolen memories — and it could be *finished*. It could be finished from anywhere, because a song does not care where its singer stands. It needed only the missing part.

And the missing part, according to everything the fragments told her, was simple. Horribly, beautifully simple.

A choir of true-words to call the crown. A vessel of willing memory to hold the hunger when it came loose. Those were the first two requirements, and they were already nearly impossible: the Underchoir could not sing whole, their voices amputated, their grammar mutilated; and Xanthe could not be the vessel, because Xanthe was the choir's last living throat, and every word she spent shortened her life, and a vessel must give *everything* —

No. Petra sat up straight in the dark. Not everything. She went back down the gallery stairs two at a time, her heart going like a hammer, and she was right, she was *right*, the fragments said it plainly if you read them in order instead of in fear: *a vessel of willing memory.* Not a willing body. Not a willing life. Memory. Freely given, freely poured out, a mind opened wide enough to hold a starving god.

The rite didn't need Xanthe to die. It needed someone to *give* — the way her mother had given, incompletely, running out mid-song; the way the valley's dead king had given his memories grain by grain until the crown wore him like a glove; except this time the giving would be chosen, and bounded, and aimed, and the crown would pour itself into a vessel that was not a throne but a single mortal mind —

— and whatever that mind was afterward, emptied, hollowed, the rite's last line said nothing about it. Because her mother had never sung the last line. Because the last line was still locked inside the valley, inside the stones, inside the ward.

Petra reached the main chamber with her breath burning and everyone looking up at her, nine ruined singers and one silent child, and she heard herself say it before she had decided to say it:

"The missing part is me."

*

They argued until the silver shard went grey with approaching dawn.

"You're a hedge-witch's apprentice," Sefa rasped, her broken voice cracking worse than usual, which for Sefa meant she was frightened. "You can't hold a crown-spirit. Your mother was a trained singer of the third voice and it nearly took her anyway —"

"My mother wasn't trying to *finish* it," Petra said. "She was trying to run away with half a song. I'm not running. And I'm not a singer at all, which might be the point." She spread the fragments out again, jabbing at the lines with a grimy finger. "Look at what it asks for. Willing memory. Not a trained throat. The crown eats memory — that's all it is, that's all it's ever been, a hunger shaped like a crown. So we feed it a meal it can't refuse, freely offered, and while it's swallowing, the choir closes the door on it. My mother's unfinished song is the bowl. I'm the food."

"And afterward?" That was Corvin, the eldest of the Underchoir, whose true-words had been burned out of him so thoroughly he could no longer remember his own children's faces. He spoke rarely and never gently. "What's left of the bowl after the feast?"

"I don't know," Petra said.

It was the honest answer and it landed in the room like a dropped knife. Somewhere above them the palace shifted and settled, and through the crack in the vaulting the first grey light of the twelfth day crept down the wall.

Xanthe had not signed anything through the whole argument. She sat with her hands folded in her lap, very still, watching Petra with those dark feral eyes that had learned, over these weeks, to read human faces the way Petra had learned to read roads. Now she rose, crossed the space between them, and took Petra's hands in both of hers.

Her fingers were cold. They were always cold. Petra had stopped asking why.

Slowly, deliberately, so there could be no mistake, Xanthe made the sign they both knew best: one hand flat, the other cutting across it. *No.*

"No?" Petra laughed, and hated how thin the laugh came out. "You get a vote now? You were ready to spend your last true-words on this. You were ready to *die* singing it."

Xanthe's jaw tightened. She pulled her hands free and signed fast, sharp, the grammar of refusal: *My death. My choice. Your death —* and then she stopped, because they had never needed to invent a sign for that, because it had never come up, because Petra had spent the whole journey being the one who decided, who planned, who carried the pack and the map and the weight.

Now the child stood there with her empty hands and her full silence, and the whole room waited, and Petra understood that the decision had stopped being hers somewhere in the last few minutes. That this was what inheritance meant, the real kind, the kind her mother had paid for: not safety handed down, but obligation passed forward, and obligations are not carried alone or they crush the carrier.

"The trap," Petra said quietly, mostly to herself. "Do you see it? This is the trap. It was always the trap." She looked around at their faces — Sefa's ravaged tenderness, Corvin's flinty grief, the others' hollow-eyed hope. "The crown has been eating this kingdom for years and nobody could stop it because nobody would pay. The singers hid instead of singing. My mother ran instead of finishing. Vey surrenders to it and calls it mercy. Everyone found a version of the answer that didn't cost what it cost. And now the rite finally tells us the price, and the price is exactly one person's whole remembered life, freely given — and I'm standing here thinking, *well, it should be me, I'm the least trained, the most expendable, the apprentice, the orphan, the one with holes already in her* —"

She stopped. Her voice had gone strange.

"— and that's not willingness," she said. "That's just despair wearing willingness's coat. My mother taught me that much without ever teaching me anything."

Sefa made a low sound, half laugh, half sob. "Hedge-witch's apprentice," she rasped, "indeed."

*

They spent the twelfth day doing what singers do when they cannot yet sing: preparing.

Corvin drilled the Underchoir on the fragments until the broken voices could carry the shapes if not the substance, harmony without teeth, a choir of ghosts rehearsing to become briefly solid. Sefa wrote out the sequence of the rite on the wall itself, in chalk that would wash away with weeping if it came to that, each line annotated in three hands — hers, Corvin's, and Petra's careful copyist's script. Petra copied the whole thing twice more onto bark and hide, one scroll for the rite itself and one for Xanthe to memorize by eye, because if the worst happened the grammar should survive even if none of them did.

And Xanthe sat by the silver shard and counted moons.

Petra found her there near dusk, knees drawn up, chin on her knees, staring at the sliver of reflected light. Petra lowered herself down beside her. For a while neither of them did anything. That was another thing the road had taught her: sometimes company is just two people agreeing to breathe at the same speed.

"One moon," Petra said finally. "Tomorrow night it'll be full — the thirteenth. The rite has to happen then, doesn't it. When the crown is fat and close."

Xanthe nodded once.

"And you'll sing the calling. The opening. You're the only throat left that can speak the crown's oldest name." Petra watched the girl's face. "How many words does that take?"

Xanthe held up her hand, fingers spread, then slowly, deliberately, curled four of them down until only the thumb remained.

One word. Her whole remaining ration, spent on a single syllable of the old tongue. Enough, maybe, for one true-word — the name itself, or the binding, but not both. Enough for one act of speaking and then a silence that would last the rest of a very short life.

"That's why you signed *no* last night," Petra said. "Not because you're afraid for me. Because if I'm the vessel and you're the caller, then we've spent everything — your words, my memories — and neither of us gets to walk away from this."

Xanthe didn't move. But her eyes were shining, and she reached out and took the hem of Petra's sleeve between finger and thumb, and held it, the way she had held it the first night on the road outside the burning house, when the world had ended in smoke and the only fixed point in a reeling universe had been a stranger's sleeve.

Petra put her hand over the small cold fingers.

"Then we'd better make sure somebody walks away," she said. "Both of us, if we're lucky. One of us, if we're not. And if it comes down to choosing—" she exhaled, long and shaky, "—then I choose the version where the song finishes and the crown cracks and Aldric wakes up and the valley's stones stand open and a certain stubborn mud-child learns to say *bread* and *home* and *Petra* in new words that don't cost her a single hour. I choose that one. I've chosen it since the kitchen, I think. Since valerian all over the floor."

Xanthe's mouth twitched. It was almost a smile. It was, from Xanthe, a speech.

From the gallery above, Sefa's ruined voice called down, cracked but carrying: "Girl. Apprentice. Come see this."

They climbed together. In the crypt archive, by lamplight, Sefa stood before the ledger shelf with a volume open in her hands — not the crown's ledger this time but an older one, water-stained, its spine repaired with wire. She turned it toward Petra and her finger, trembling, rested on a page of musical notation unlike anything else in the crypts: not the crown's harvested notation, not the Underchoir's mutilated fragments, but a full line of the third voice, complete, confident, ending mid-phrase at the bottom of the page as though the singer had simply stood up and walked away.

Beneath it, in a hand Petra recognized from a locked almanac in a valley that felt like another lifetime — no. Not that hand. A different hand, rounder, quicker. A hand she had seen only once before, signing a ledger three levels down.

*Ilse Nakamura,* the page read. *Third voice. If I cannot finish, may my daughter.*

The rest of the sentence was smudged beyond reading, drowned in some old water, some old grief. Only the first four words survived.

*May my daughter —*

Petra stared at it. Around her the crypt seemed to lean in, the ossuary shelves, the chalk rite on the wall, the nine broken singers, the silent child gripping her sleeve. Outside, far above, the twelfth moon was rising to its fullness, dragging the thirteenth behind it like a tide.

"May my daughter what," Petra whispered. "Finish it? Choose it? Pay?"

Sefa shook her head slowly. "There's one way to find out," she rasped. "Ask the valley."

And Petra felt the words land like a stone dropped down a well — because the valley was five days' ride, and the moon was one night from full, and the rite required a place, and the place it required was suddenly, horribly obvious. Not the cathedral. Not the capital. The boundary stones, where an unfinished song had been sleeping for twenty years, waiting for someone to come home and finish it.

"We can't get there in time," she said. "It's too far. We'd need — "

She stopped. Because there was one way to move faster than horses through a kingdom that remembered every road, and it belonged to a tinker with a warded wagon who took payment in memories, and Petra had already paid him once and knew exactly how much a small memory cost and exactly how large the hole was where it used to be.

Xanthe looked at her. Slowly, deliberately, she raised one hand and signed the newest word in their private grammar, the one they'd invented only days ago, the one that meant *again*.

*Again,* the child said, in the language of hands. *We go again.*
