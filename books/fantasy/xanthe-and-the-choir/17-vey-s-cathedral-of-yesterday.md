## 17. Vey's Cathedral of Yesterday

They came for her in the hour before dawn, when the palace corridors were at their quietest and the torches burned low and blue.

Petra had not slept. She had spent the night in the cell Vey called a guest room — it had a real bed, a window of thick glass, even a basin and pitcher, luxuries she hadn't seen since Maren's house went up in smoke — and she had lain on that bed staring at the ceiling, listening to Xanthe breathe in the next room, and waiting for the door to open. It was the waiting, she understood now, that was the point. Chancellor Vey did not need to torture anyone. She only needed to let them sit in a comfortable room long enough, and their own minds would do the work of breaking.

The door opened without a sound. A servant stood there — or what Petra supposed had once been a servant, a woman with kind eyes gone smooth as river stones, who said, "The Chancellor requests your presence," in a voice like a recitation, like words said so many times they had worn down to pebbles.

"And if I refuse?"

The woman smiled. It was a warm smile. That was the worst part. "You won't."

* * *

They took her down, and down, and down again, through staircases Petra lost count of, past guards in grey livery who did not look at her, past doors banded with iron and marked with sigils she didn't recognize. The deeper they went, the older the stone became. The palace above was marble and gilt, all of it no more than two hundred years old by the look of it, but this under-place was rough-hewn granite, ancient, the kind of stone that remembered being a mountain. Her mother had walked these stairs. The thought arrived unbidden and sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Xanthe walked beside her, small hand tucked into Petra's larger one. The child had not spoken since the tower — not truly spoken; she made her small sounds, her gestures, tugged Petra's sleeve when she wanted to point something out — but her grip was fierce, and her eyes moved constantly, cataloguing, reading. Reading the walls, Petra realized. Xanthe read the old carvings in the granite the way Petra read herb-rows: fluently, hungrily. Once she stopped dead and stared at a section of wall where someone had scratched a spiral of marks, and her breath went ragged, and Petra had to pull her gently onward before the guards noticed.

"What is it?" Petra whispered.

Xanthe looked up at her with an expression Petra was coming to know well: grief and wonder braided together, too large for the small face. She pressed her scarred palm flat against her own chest. Then she pointed at the wall. Then she shook her head slowly, twice.

Someone's name. Someone gone. Petra didn't need the grammar to understand that.

At last the stairs ended, and the servant pushed open a pair of doors tall enough to admit a giant, and Petra stepped into the cathedral.

She had expected a throne room, or a prison, or some combination of both. What she saw stopped her feet and stole the air from her lungs.

It was vast — a cavern of a space, ribbed with pillars that rose out of sight into a darkness that glowed faintly, softly, the way the sky glows just after sunset. And filling that darkness, suspended in the air like lanterns at a festival, were memories.

There was no other word for them. They hung in their thousands, tens of thousands, each one a small sphere of shifting light, and each one was *playing*. Petra could see them — couldn't help seeing them — as she walked the central aisle between rows of silent kneeling benches. A farmer's harvest, golden wheat under a gold sky, over and over. A wedding, the bride's face lit with laughter, the moment replaying until the laughter became something eerie, a sound worn smooth. A child's first steps. A deathbed, gentle, a hand held in a hand. A summer storm watched from a doorway. Bread coming out of an oven. Over and over and over, each memory circling its own small orbit of light, perfect, preserved, *eternal*.

"Oh," Petra said. It was all she could manage.

"Beautiful, isn't it."

Vey stood at the far end of the nave, beneath an enormous unfinished arch, and she was not in her grey riding clothes now but in robes of white and silver, and her eyes still reflected the light wrong — catching it and holding it a half-second too long, like a cat's, like glass. She spread her hands.

"I hoped you'd come willingly. Most people have to be dragged down here the first time, and then they never want to leave." She gestured, and the nearest memory drifted closer — the wedding, the laughing bride — and enlarged, until Petra could see every detail: the freckles on the bride's nose, the way her bouquet trembled, a tear on the groom's jaw that he'd deny to his grave. "Do you know what this one is? Harvested forty-one years ago from a laundress who died of fever. She was frightened when we took it. They're always frightened. But look at her now." Vey's voice softened into something almost reverent. "She has been happy for forty-one years. Every day, her wedding day. Every day, the best day of her life, forever. Tell me, apprentice — what does your hedge-witch offer that compares?"

"She offers the truth that days keep moving," Petra said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"Yes. Days keep moving, and people die, and everything you love is taken from you one morning at a time." Vey began to walk, slow, her footsteps silent on the stone, and the memories parted around her like fish around a shark. "I was a healer before I was a chancellor. Did you know that? I sat with the dying for thirty years. I held their hands while they forgot their children's faces, and I watched the terror in their eyes, and I swore — I *swore* — that I would find a way to stop it. The crown gave me the means. The crown takes, yes. It is hungry, yes. But hunger is just appetite without purpose, and I have given it purpose. Everything it takes, I *keep*. Nothing here is lost. Nothing here decays. Nothing here ends." She turned those wrong eyes on Petra. "I am building heaven, girl. The first heaven that has ever actually existed."

"It's a cage," Petra said. "Look at them. They're not living it. They're *stuck* in it."

"They are safe in it." Vey's serenity did not crack, but something behind it shifted, deep, like a current under ice. "You've been outside. You've seen what remembering costs. You walked roads that replay the dead because the dead can't let go of them. Grief is a wound that never closes, and I have closed it. For thousands. For *everyone*, eventually."

* * *

They walked, and Vey talked, and Petra listened because listening was the only weapon she had down here.

The cathedral was bigger than she'd first grasped. Side chapels branched off the nave, each one dedicated to a kind of memory — Vey called them *choirs*, which made Xanthe flinch every time — and in each chapel the harvested light-spheres were arranged in patterns, spirals, constellations. There was a chapel of mothers. A chapel of first loves. A chapel, small and dim, of last words, and Petra could not make herself walk through it, and Vey did not force her.

"You want to know why I brought you here," Vey said at last, "and it isn't for the tour."

They had come to the head of the nave, beneath the great unfinished arch. Here the memories clustered thickest, and among them — Petra saw it and her heart stuttered — hung spheres of a light she recognized without knowing how. Warm amber shot through with green, like sunlight through leaves, like the specific color of late summer in a walled garden.

"These were taken from the ledger of the rite," Vey said quietly. "From your mother."

The floor tilted. Petra felt Xanthe's grip tighten on her hand, anchoring her.

"When your mother fled the unbinding rite, she carried more than herself away," Vey continued. "She carried fragments — memories the rite had already begun to claim, payment already rendered. She hid them inside the ward she built around your valley. Wove them into the stones themselves. That is why the ward holds so strong, girl. It isn't just magic. It's *her*. Every year of her life she gave up, singing itself into the boundary stones." Vey smiled, and it was almost gentle. "And when the ward frays — as it is fraying now — those memories will come loose. They will drift. And I will gather them, as I gather everything."

"No," Petra said.

"I can give them back to you."

The words dropped into the silence like stones into a well, and Petra heard them fall and fall and fall.

"All of them," Vey said. "Every stolen year. Her voice. Her laugh. The way she looked at you when you were born. The lullabies. The reason she ran. The name she whispered over your cradle. Everything the valley ate, everything the rite took, everything you have spent your whole life aching over without knowing what the ache was." Vey stepped closer, and up close her eyes were terrible, bottomless, patient. "I have them nearly assembled already. A few more fragments and the reconstruction will be complete. I can put them back into your mind, girl, whole and warm and *hers*. You could know your mother. You could remember being loved by her. No one else alive can offer you that. No one else alive has ever been able to offer anyone that."

The cathedral hummed around them. Ten thousand memories played their small eternities. And Petra stood beneath the unfinished arch with her hand in a child's hand, and she *ached*.

It wasn't a noble ache. That was what she would remember later, what shamed her even in the moment: it was small and greedy and human. She thought of Maren's almanac, her mother's name inked out. She thought of the road that remembered footprints leading toward the capital and never back. She thought of Oskar's wagon and the hole in her mind where Maren's kitchen used to be, the shape of an absence she'd learned to walk around like furniture in the dark. She had paid one memory already. She knew exactly what a missing memory felt like, the way it caught on everything, the way it made her flinch at ordinary moments — reaching for a jar that wasn't there, turning to say something to someone who wasn't there.

Her mother was a hole the size of her entire childhood.

"I can see it working," Vey murmured. "That's not weakness. That's sanity. Only a fool wouldn't want her mother back."

"If I say yes," Petra said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it, "what happens to Xanthe?"

"She becomes the choir." Vey said it simply, the way you'd say *the rain falls*. "The crown needs a final instrument to complete its work — a voice that can speak the old grammar, to sing the kingdom into permanence. Not death. Never death — I told you, nothing here dies. She will be preserved, like all of them. She will be the most beloved thing in this cathedral. Every voice in it will harmonize around hers. She will be safe, and honored, and eternal, and you—" Vey's hand hovered near Petra's shoulder, not quite touching, "—you will be whole. Your mother's memories returned. Your grief finished. Your debt paid. Everyone gets what they actually want, Petra Nakamura. That is all mercy is."

And God help her, Petra wanted it.

She wanted it with a ferocity that scared her. She looked at the amber-green spheres drifting overhead — her mother, *pieces of her mother*, right there, close enough to touch — and her body leaned toward them the way a cold person leans toward fire. She thought: *I could hear her voice.* She thought: *I could remember her hands.* She thought: *Maren always said I got my stubbornness from somewhere.*

Xanthe's hand was trembling in hers. Small. Fierce. Rationing even her fear.

And Petra thought about the goat. The dying goat in Maren's yard, and Xanthe crouched beside it, and the single true-word she'd spent like a coin she couldn't afford, and the goat standing up on legs gone steady. She had spent a piece of her life — Petra understood that now, understood it fully — to save an animal that belonged to strangers. Because it was hurt. Because she was there. Because that was what the language was *for*.

"Petra." Vey's voice was silk over steel. "You are not responsible for her. You found her. You are not her mother."

"No," Petra said.

Vey waited.

"No," Petra said again, louder, and the word rang off the pillars and came back to her changed. "You're right that I'm not her mother. Her mother died getting her out of your nursery. And I'm not her keeper either. She's not a thing to be traded, Chancellor. Neither am I. And neither—" she lifted her chin toward the ten thousand playing lights, the laundress on her forty-one-thousandth wedding day, the farmer in his eternal wheat, "—are *they*. This isn't heaven. Heaven doesn't need walls. You've built the largest cage in the world and hung lamps in it so no one sees the bars."

Something moved behind Vey's eyes — not anger. Something older and sadder. Pity, maybe, or its twin.

"I offered your mother a choice once, too," Vey said. "Did you know that? Midway through the rite. I told her she could stop. She chose to run instead, and look what her running bought: a valley of forgetting, a daughter raised by a stranger, a ward that's failing anyway. Mercy delayed is mercy denied, Petra. Sooner or later everyone comes to my cathedral. The only choice any of us ever really makes is whether we arrive intact." She turned, robes whispering, and began to walk away down the aisle of playing lights. "Keep the child tonight. Think. I'll return at moonrise with the memories assembled. If you refuse me then, I'll take what I need regardless — but I'd rather not. I have always preferred willing hands."

The great doors closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The cathedral was not empty when Vey left. That was the horror of it — the place stayed lit, stayed *singing*, ten thousand small eternities humming their harmony, and the kneeling benches sat ready, and somewhere in the dark a bell that had no ringer marked hours no one living counted.

Petra sank onto the lowest step of the altar dais and pulled Xanthe into her arms, and for a long time neither of them moved.

Above them, the amber-green spheres drifted. Her mother. Close enough to touch. Close enough, maybe, to *take* — if Petra simply opened the door of herself and let Vey pour them in.

"I almost said yes," she whispered to the child. "I need you to know that. I almost said yes."

Xanthe pulled back far enough to look at her. In the cathedral light her eyes were huge and dark and very old. She raised one small hand and pressed it, palm out, against Petra's sternum — the scarred palm, the crown-shaped burn — and held it there.

Then, deliberately, she shook her head.

*Not yours,* the gesture said. Or maybe: *Don't.* Or maybe, in the grammar Petra was only beginning to learn: *what is freely kept cannot be bought.*

"You'd give it to me, wouldn't you," Petra said suddenly, understanding arriving like cold water. "If I asked. A memory of her. One true-word, sung once, and gone from you forever. That's what you do. That's what you've been doing since the day I met you."

Xanthe didn't nod. She didn't deny it either. She just looked at Petra with those old, old eyes, and slowly folded her scarred hand closed, keeping whatever it held.

And Petra understood, sitting in the enemy's church beneath her mother's stolen light, that the temptation had never really been Vey's to offer. Vey could give her a reconstruction, a copy, a lamp in a cage. But Xanthe could give her the true thing — at a price that would shorten a life Petra had sworn, somewhere between a burning house and a boundary stone, that she would protect.

The bell rang an hour with no ringer. Above them, her mother's memories circled their small orbits, patient as stars.

Moonrise was coming. And Petra had a decision to make that no one could make for her — which was, she was beginning to understand, the only inheritance her mother had ever actually left her.
