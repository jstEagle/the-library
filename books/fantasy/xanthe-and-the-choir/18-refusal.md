## 18. Refusal

The silence after Vey's offer had a texture to it. Petra had never known silence could be heavy — the valley's silences were soft things, wool and herb-smoke — but this one pressed down on her shoulders like snow on a roof, and somewhere in her chest she could hear the creak of rafters.

Her mother's memories hung above them in the cathedral of yesterday.

That was the only way Petra could think of it. Vey had walked her through the nave with something like tenderness, past pews of sleeping courtiers wired into their gentle loops of remembered harvest festivals and dead children's laughter, up to the apse where the light pooled gold as egg-yolk. And there, suspended in columns of preserved luminescence, orbited the archive: *Liora Nakamura, neé Callas, unbinding-singer, defector.* Thousands of motes of stored remembering, each one a day, an hour, a smell. A woman stirring nettle soup. A woman laughing at something Maren had said before Petra was born. A woman holding an infant — herself — in a garden that would later be walled inside stones, singing to it in a language Petra did not know but which Xanthe, standing beside her, had gone very still at hearing.

*She sang like us,* Xanthe had signed, hands quick and small in the gold light. *Before.*

Vey had spread her hands beneath the archive like a priestess at altar, and made the offer simply, without theater, because Vey understood that the cruelest bargains are the ones dressed plainly.

*"Everything she gave,"* she'd said. *"Every memory your mother surrendered to buy your valley its safety. I can give them back to you. Whole. You could know her the way she knew herself. You could hear her voice saying your name. All you have to do is walk away from the child and let me keep her safe in the only way that has ever worked."*

And now the offer sat in the air between the three of them — Petra, Vey, and the small mud-colored girl who was not looking at the archive at all, but at Petra's face.

Petra looked at her mother.

There was no other honest word for what she was doing. She stood in the gold light and she looked, and she let herself want it, because refusing to look felt like another kind of lie, and she had spent this whole long road learning to stop lying to herself. Her mother's face — she had never known her mother's face. Maren had described it once, badly, the way people describe things they are protecting a child from: *tall, dark-haired, laughed too loud for a singer.* But here, in the nearest orbit, was the truth of it, rendered in harvested light: sharp-boned, sun-darkened at the cheeks, a scar splitting one eyebrow that no one had ever thought to mention to Petra in all her seventeen years. Her mother had a scar. Her mother had burned the porridge and hummed off-key when she thought no one listened and had a way of tilting her head that Petra caught in every polished surface of her own reflection and had never once been able to name until now.

*I could have this,* Petra thought. *I could have all of it.*

The hole in her — the mother-shaped absence she had carried so long it had become part of her architecture, load-bearing, structural — gaped open in the gold light like a mouth.

"You're crying," Vey observed, without judgment. That was the terrible thing about Vey; there was never any cruelty in her. Cruelty required believing you were doing harm. "That's not weakness, girl. That's evidence. Grief is just memory with nowhere to go. I can give it somewhere to go."

Behind her, Petra heard Xanthe shift her weight on the cathedral floor.

"I need your answer by moonrise," Vey went on. She turned toward the great rose window, where the twelfth moon already showed pale against the darkening blue, one night from full. "Tomorrow is the thirteenth turn. If you refuse, the crown completes its feeding tomorrow night regardless — it has waited three hundred years, it will not wait longer — and everything in this room goes into it, including your mother, including the child, quietly, painlessly, forever. If you accept—" Vey's serene face did something almost like warmth, "—you will lose nothing that matters. You'll understand that soon. Loss is mostly a rumor told by people who haven't yet found a better arrangement."

*Loss is mostly a rumor.* Petra turned the words over and found them smooth as river stone and hollow as one too.

She thought about Maren's kitchen. Not even the important parts — not the confession, not the grey-faced terror at the door at dusk — but the ordinary morning of it: the valerian jar shattering, the smell of milk and mint, Maren swearing softly at the mess with her sleeves rolled up. The memory Oskar had taken from her at his warded wagon, a small one, a taste, he'd said, and Petra had walked out onto the remembered road carrying a hole shaped like cinnamon smoke and had wept for twenty minutes without knowing why, because the grief remained after the thing grieved for was gone, like the ache of a tooth pulled in someone else's house.

She had paid that toll once. She knew exactly what the coin felt like.

And Vey was offering to pay her — to fill the largest hole in her with light — if only she would make a new hole first, child-shaped, Xanthe-shaped, and stand in it forever pretending it was mercy.

"No," Petra said.

It came out smaller than she meant. She said it again, to the archive, to her mother's scarred eyebrow, to the whole golden machinery of preserved yesterdays.

"No. I won't give her to you."

Vey sighed — genuinely, sadly, the sigh of a woman watching someone choose a hard winter over a warm cage. "Because it's wrong? You've seen what I keep here. Is this wrong?"

"It's not— " Petra stopped. Here was the thing she had been circling since the apse, the thing she could not say honestly while standing in the light of everything she wanted, and she made herself say it anyway, because Xanthe deserved the truth even now. "Maybe it isn't wrong. Maybe you're right about all of it. Maybe a heaven made of yesterday is kinder than a world made of tomorrows. I don't know. I'm seventeen and I've never even left a valley until last month." Her voice shook. She let it. "But I know what my mother did, Vey. She didn't sell her memories to buy safety. She spent them to buy *choice*. Mine. A valley where nothing gets decided for you, where magic can't reach in and take anything — not your children, not your voice, not your mind. It was a small life. It was a *free* one. And you're asking me to trade the only inheritance she ever gave me back for a lamp in a cage."

"Freedom is what people call their suffering once it's over," said Vey gently.

"Then I'll suffer," said Petra, "in the correct direction."

Something moved behind Vey's wrong-reflecting eyes — not anger. Disappointment, maybe, or the loneliness of a missionary whose gospel keeps failing. Her hand drifted, unhurried, toward the bell-pull that would bring guards who did not ask questions.

And that was when Xanthe stepped forward into the gold light.

* * *

Petra saw it happening and understood it too late, the way you understand a wave only when it has already broken over you.

Xanthe walked past her — close enough that Petra caught the woodsmoke-and-warm-stone smell of her, the smell that had replaced, over these weeks of roads and ruin, every notion Petra had ever had of what home was — and planted herself between Petra and the archive. She looked up at the orbiting lights of Liora Nakamura's stolen self for a long moment. Then she raised both hands, and began to sign, and Petra realized with a lurch that she wasn't signing to Petra at all.

She was signing to Vey. Slowly, formally, the way one addresses an audience.

*Tell her what you offered me,* the hands said. *In the tower. Tell her the whole shape of it.*

"She offered me sanctuary," Vey said, and there was puzzlement in her voice now, the first true puzzlement Petra had ever heard there. "Safety under the crown. I don't hide my bargains."

Xanthe shook her head once, sharply, like a bird shaking rain.

*You offered to keep my voice,* the hands said. *To wear it. To sing through me forever. Tell her that part.*

"The crown requires an instrument," Vey said. "It would not hurt her. Nothing under the crown hurts. That is the entire point of everything I have built."

*Yes,* Xanthe signed. And then her hands changed, and Petra felt the air change with them — felt the fine hairs on her arms lift the way they did before a storm, the way they did when Xanthe spoke true-words, the grammar that made things true —

*Watch,* Xanthe signed to Petra. One word. Then she took a breath, opened her mouth, and spent.

What came out was not sound, exactly, though Petra's ears heard it. It was older than sound. Two syllables of the choir-tongue, the binding grammar, the language that does not describe but *enacts* — and the gold light of the cathedral bent toward the child like grass toward sun.

And Petra's mother stepped out of the light.

Not her mother. Petra's reeling mind seized on that immediately, the way it had seized on the difference between Oskar's toll and Vey's archive — a copy given versus a truth bestowed. What stood before her was a memory, worn like a body: Liora Nakamura in the garden, singing to an infant, luminous at the edges, translucent at the hem. She was mid-song. She would always be mid-song. But her eyes were awake, and they found Petra's face the way water finds a drain, and she smiled — startled, delighted, the smile of a woman seeing her daughter for the first time, because in the moment this memory was taken from her, that was exactly what she had been doing.

"Oh," said the memory of Petra's mother, in a voice that cracked on a laugh too loud for a singer. "Oh, look at you. You have my horrible chin."

Petra made a sound that was not a word.

The memory could only hold what had been held in it — one evening, one song, one first sight — but within it, Liora moved freely, and she reached out and touched Petra's cheek with a hand that was warm the way late sunlight is warm, present the way an echo is present. And she kept singing, low, the same rising phrase over and over, and Petra understood suddenly, horribly, beautifully, that it was not a lullaby.

It was the unbinding rite. The first phrase of it. Sung to a baby, the way you teach anything to a baby — not expecting understanding, only planting.

"This is what I paid for you," her mother said, still smiling, still touching her face. "This moment, and ten thousand like it. Do you know what I asked myself, at the boundary stone? Not whether I could bear to lose it all. Whether *you* would be glad of the trade, someday, and never get to tell me." Her thumb traced Petra's cheekbone, memorizing what it was already doomed to forget. "I decided yes. I bet your whole life on yes."

"Mama," Petra said, and the word came out of her like something exiting a wound.

"I know," said the memory. "I know, love. Listen — listen — I only get the one verse." And the light of her was already thinning at the edges, fraying like cloth, because a memory sung cannot be unsung, and Xanthe had told Petra the cost of true-words often enough with her hands. Every spoken word shortened her life. This was two syllables of the oldest grammar there was, spent on this. Spent on *her.*

"Don't," Petra gasped, turning to Xanthe — and Xanthe was swaying, white-lipped, nose bleeding in a bright thread down her chin, but her eyes were clear and fixed on Petra with a ferocity that stopped the plea in her throat. *Don't waste it,* the eyes said. *Don't you dare call this waste.*

"Take care of each other," said Liora's memory, and her voice was going thin now, going distant, a song heard through a closing door. "That's the whole rite, in the end — that's the secret nobody wrote down — it was never about unbinding crowns, it was about—" she laughed, too loud, wonderful, "—about handing things on. Memory isn't a treasure, sweetheart. It's a *current.* You dam it, it goes bad. You pass it, it stays alive. Now — hold still, I'm almost at the good part—"

And she sang the last line directly to her, the phrase completing itself at last across sixteen years and one unfinished song, and the memory folded itself up like a letter being sealed, and was gone — not into Vey's archive, not into the crown's hunger, but into Petra, whole and permanent and *hers*, stitched into her where the hole used to be, and Petra felt it settle into place with an almost physical click, load-bearing, structural, home.

The cathedral was silent. Even the bells had stopped.

Xanthe's legs buckled. Petra caught her before she hit the floor.

For a moment Petra knelt there on the cold stone with the child in her arms, feeling her own heart hammering against Xanthe's shoulder, feeling the new weight of her mother sitting inside her chest like a lit lantern. Xanthe's blood was on her collar. Xanthe's breath came shallow and fast. She had spent perhaps a month of her finite life in the last sixty seconds, and she had spent it buying Petra a gift that could never be taken, taxed, archived, or archived again.

"Why," Petra whispered. "Why would you — you were rationing them. You told me. Each one is — Xanthe, you could have *lived* on that word."

Xanthe lifted one trembling hand. Drew, in the air between them, the sign Petra had come to know best — the one that meant *give*, the open palm turning over, the motion that was also the sign for *teach* and the sign for *inherit* and, Petra realized now, had been the sign for *love* all along.

*Now you know what she sounds like,* the hand said, and fell.

* * *

Vey had not called her guards.

Petra noticed it slowly, in the strange underwater time that followed — the way the cathedral stayed empty around them, the way the Chancellor of the Hollow Court stood at the foot of her own altar with her hands clasped and her face unreadable, watching Petra hold the unconscious child, and doing nothing.

"You should understand what you've seen," Vey said at last. Her voice had lost none of its serenity, but something underneath it had shifted, tectonic, like furniture moving in a house at night. "That word. Two syllables of living grammar, spent on a *feeling.* On making one person whole for one hour. No preservation. No return. Just — given away." She looked up at the archive, at her thousands of caged days, and for the first time since the ridgeline, Petra watched the Chancellor doubt. "My cathedral can replay your mother forever, girl. A thousand years of her, perfect, lossless. And it would not be worth those four seconds. Why is that? Explain it to me. I've built everything and I cannot explain it."

"She wasn't yours to replay," Petra said. She got to her feet, Xanthe in her arms, lighter than she should have been, heavier than anything in the world. "Nothing is, Chancellor. That's the whole answer. Memory is a current. You dammed it. It went bad."

Above them, the rose window had gone fully dark, and through its colored glass the twelfth moon poured down cold and complete, and below the horizon, unseen, the thirteenth was turning toward its rise.

"Tomorrow night," Vey said quietly. It was not a threat. It was almost a request — a scholar asking for the conclusion of an argument she was losing. "The crown feeds whether you sing or not."

"Then tomorrow night," said Petra, "you might want to hear how the song ends."

She carried Xanthe out through the sleeping pews, past the dreaming courtiers wired into their gentle yesterdays, down the nave of the beautiful wrong church, following the pull in her chest that was half grief and half compass: toward the crypts, toward the broken voices of the Underchoir, toward Sefa and the stolen fragments of the rite. Behind her, in the empty gold apse, her mother's archive still turned its patient orbits — but Petra no longer needed it. She had the true thing now. Given, not hoarded. Current, not dam.

And in her arms, half-conscious, Xanthe was signing against Petra's collarbone — faint, feverish, unstoppable — rehearsing, even now, the words she had left.

There were so few of them. Tomorrow they would have to be enough.
