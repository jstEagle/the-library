## 19. The Unbinding Rite

### Petra

The cathedral of yesterday smelled of dust and roses that had never wilted because they had never truly lived.

Petra stood in the nave where Vey's harvested memories played in ribbons of light along the pillars — a wedding replaying in the transept, a funeral looping quietly by the font, a child's birthday flickering gold above the altar like trapped weather. Thirteenth moonlight poured through windows that showed not saints but yesterdays: harvests, first kisses, last words. Everything here was preserved. Nothing here was alive.

She thought of Maren's kitchen, of what remained of it in her head after Oskar's toll and her own deliberate giving — she had spent three memories already on the road, and tonight she would spend the rest.

*All of them,* Sefa had said, broken voice rasping like wind through crypt-grass. *The rite wants willing payment, girl. Not blood. Not pain. Memory given freely, gladly even. That's what your mother couldn't do. That's why her song broke.*

Petra's hands shook as she unrolled the ledger page they'd taken from the crypt archives — her mother's name at the top, the unfinished notation crawling down the vellum in the old grammar's careful curves. Xanthe had traced it for hours, touching each glyph, teaching Petra the shapes by pressing them into her palm, because there was no longer breath enough in the child to speak them aloud.

Thirteen moons hung in the clerestory now, the thirteenth risen fat and silver over the cathedral's broken roof, and below it the Underchoir assembled in the nave like a congregation of ghosts. Twelve singers. Some had no tongues. One had sewn his lips shut decades ago and now sang through a slit cut in his cheek. Sefa stood among them with her throat wrapped in linen, her voice a ruin that had once, Petra now knew, harmonized beside her mother in this very rite — before her mother fled, before everything.

"You don't have to do this," Sefa said. "There may be another way."

"There isn't." Petra heard her own voice come out steady, and was briefly grateful to whatever part of her had learned to lie. "My mother left half a song. I'm the only one who can finish it."

"And the payment—"

"I know what the payment is."

She looked at Xanthe, standing small and grave in the moon-colored light, and felt the shape of what she was about to lose press against the inside of her ribs. Maren scolding her for crushing the feverfew. Maren's hands, cracked and capable, wrapping a poultice. Maren dropping the valerian jar when the feral child spoke at their door — that memory was already gone, paid to Oskar, and its absence was a smooth blank place in her mind, like a tooth socket the tongue keeps finding.

Tonight she would give up every one that remained.

"Maren," she said aloud, practicing the name. It still meant something. In a few hours it might mean only a word.

Xanthe took her hand. The burn scar on the child's palm — crown-shaped, old, terrible — pressed cool against Petra's skin. And into that palm, slowly, deliberately, Xanthe signed: *I will remember her for you. I will hold them while you sing.*

Petra laughed, or sobbed; from the outside they probably sounded the same.

"Then let's not keep the moon waiting."

* * *

### Xanthe

*Xanthe counted her remaining words the way other children counted coins.*

Fourteen. She had entered the palace nursery knowing two hundred, perhaps more — the whole bright inventory of the choir-tongue, each one a small sun that could make a thing true — and she had rationed them across years of hiding, spending one to seal a door, one to blind a tracker hound, one, terribly, to heal a dying goat in a green valley while a brown-haired girl watched with wonder in her face. Fourteen left. The rite would take thirteen.

One would remain. She had decided which one long ago.

The cathedral rose around her, and Xanthe hated it in a quiet, fluent way. It was built of stolen things. Every stone sang faintly with the memories packed inside it — Vey's great archive, the crown's larder — and to Xanthe's ears the building droned with a thousand voices all saying *mine, mine, remember me, mine.* The crown-thing sat above it all in Aldric's empty throne, behind the high altar where Vey had moved it for the ceremony she'd imagined: her own coronation of eternity. But Vey was gone now, hollowed by her own empty heaven, wandering the cathedral's upper galleries muttering offers no one took. The crown waited alone. It had been waiting a long time.

It saw her. She felt it the way a hare feels the hawk's shadow — not sight but pressure, a vast attention turning toward her small self. *Little vessel,* it did not say, because it had no mouth, only hunger shaped like speech. *Come. I will give you back everyone. Every singer. Every word you ever lost.*

Xanthe looked at it and signed, deliberately, in the hand-speech Petra had helped her build from ordinary gestures: *You eat. I make true. We are not the same.*

The pressure tightened, amused. *Your words are finite, little singer. Mine are not.*

*No,* Xanthe agreed, and began to climb the steps to the altar, to the place where the unfinished song waited in Petra's trembling hands. *But mine were given. Yours were taken. That is the whole difference, and tonight it will be enough.*

* * *

### Petra

They began when the thirteenth moon touched the crown of the rose window.

Sefa raised her ruined voice first — a sound like a door opening in an empty house — and the Underchoir answered, twelve broken instruments finding one another in the dark the way fingers find each other in a fire. Their fragments wove together into something that should not have held: a tongue missing most of itself, sung through mutilation and scar, and yet unmistakably the same music that lived in the ledger page. Her mother's music. Petra's inheritance, coming home.

Now, Petra told herself. Now or never.

She opened her mouth and sang her mother's unfinished song.

It came out rough, untutored, her accent wrong in eleven places Xanthe winced at — but the grammar caught anyway, because the song remembered itself, because some things wait in the blood for the voice that finally claims them. The notes rose and the cathedral's stolen memories shivered in their ribbons of light. And the rite asked its price, gently, the way water asks a drowning thing.

*Give.*

Petra gave.

Maren's kitchen first — bread and bitterness and steam on the window, gone, dissolving out of her like warmth leaving a cup. She gasped and kept singing. Maren teaching her the difference between hemlock and cow-parsley, Maren drunk on midsummer mead and laughing until she cried, Maren's grey head bent over the locked almanac — gone, gone, gone, each one a small death she walked through without stopping, because the song required her voice continuous, unbroken, and if she stopped to mourn she would lose the thread and her mother's sacrifice with it.

She was weeping. She discovered this distantly, the way you discover rain. The tears fell on the ledger page and the ink ran and it didn't matter, because by then the song was living in the air instead of the vellum, held up by twelve ruined voices beneath her one whole one.

And in the spaces where her memories had been, the song grew.

That was the horror and the mercy of it: the rite did not simply take. It *used*. Each surrendered memory became a note, a foundation-stone, and Petra felt Maren's kitchen become a wall against the crown's hunger, felt Maren's laughter become a gate that hunger could not pass. Her teacher was becoming the binding. It was the closest thing to keeping her that magic allowed, and Petra clung to that thought as the last memories went — Maren at the boundary stones, Maren saying *hide the child*, Maren staying behind in the smoke —

Gone. All gone.

She sang on with an emptiness inside her shaped exactly like a woman she could no longer picture, and the song reached its final phrase, the phrase her mother had fled rather than finish, and Xanthe stepped forward and opened her mouth.

* * *

### Xanthe

*Thirteen words.*

Xanthe had hoarded them through years of silence, and now she spent them like a woman pouring out the last water of a desert crossing — not carefully anymore, but gloriously, wastefully, the way words were meant to be spent before the choir-tongue taught fear.

The first made the cathedral's stones forget their theft, and the archived memories rose free like birds. The second named the crown for what it was — *parasite, hunger, thief-of-faces* — and naming is binding, and the thing on the throne shrieked without sound. The third through the twelfth she spent in a rising spiral, sealing the rite's architecture, each true-word shortening her life by a season she could feel leaving her body like heat, her hair going grey at the temples, her knees aging under her, twenty years falling away between one syllable and the next. The Underchoir sang louder to carry her, Sefa's ruined voice cracking open into something that was almost beautiful.

And the crown fought. Of course it fought. It poured itself down the walls in a flood of borrowed faces — Aldric young, Aldric old, courtiers, children, thousands of the eaten dead wearing expressions of appeal — *save us, little singer, we are in here, we are in here* — and Xanthe wept as she sang because some of those faces she remembered from the nursery, from before.

The thirteenth word was the keystone. She saved it for the end, gathered it, and set it into the song's crown like the last stone of an arch—

—and the arch held.

The crack began at the throne and ran upward through the moonlight, a seam of white fire splitting the crown-spirit along the fault line every unbinding rite had been written to find, and the cathedral filled with a sound like a kingdom exhaling after centuries of held breath. Memories came loose everywhere, drifting down through the air like luminous snow, returning to no one and everyone, free at last of both keeper and keeper's cage.

Xanthe swayed. She was very old now, or very tired; the difference had blurred. Her inventory stood at one.

One word. The one she had chosen long ago.

She looked at Petra — hollow-eyed, emptied, swaying on her feet with her mother's song finished at last — and Xanthe smiled with a mouth that had grown unfamiliar, and lifted her hand for the final signing.

*For you. So you won't be empty after all.*

She spent her last true-word on Petra Nakamura.

* * *

### Petra

The memory arrived like sunrise.

Her mother — not inked-out, not a name in a ledger, not footprints on a remembering road — her mother, whole and laughing, kneeling in a garden outside the valley stones, planting seeds, looking up at a younger Petra with dirt on her cheeks and saying, clear as bells:

*"Remember me forward, little one. Don't keep me. Pass me on."*

And then it was gone — Xanthe's gift spent in the giving, the child's last true-word consumed so that Petra could hold one single memory of her mother that no toll, no crown, no rite could ever take, because it had been *made true*, and true things, the grammar promised, endure.

Petra stood in the falling snow of freed memories with nothing left to pay and everything she'd paid for arriving all at once. The crown lay split on the throne like a dropped circlet, its hunger bleeding out into the moonlight, and from within the crack something stirred — the starving thing without a face, unbound at last, older than kingdoms, and it was not dying easily.

It lunged — not at the Underchoir, not at Petra, but at Xanthe, at the last vessel, at the final spark of the language it had spent lifetimes devouring —

And King Aldric stepped out of the freed snow of himself, mortal, remembering, his own man for the first time in years, and threw his body into the gap between the hunger and the child.

"No," he said, in a voice raw with everything he'd lost. "Not the girl. You've worn my face long enough. Take the face and choke on it."

The crown took him into itself rather than nothing — a king, remembered, ending the hunger with the weight of a whole and willing soul — and the light went out of the crack, and the thirteenth moon rolled on past the rose window as though nothing had happened at all.

Petra caught Xanthe as the child's aged legs folded. Around them the Underchoir sank to their knees, weeping, singing fragments with no rite left to serve. Somewhere above, in the galleries, Chancellor Vey's murmuring had gone silent.

Petra held the nearly-wordless child and could not remember Maren's face — could not, would ever again — but she found, in the smooth blank places where her teacher had been, something new growing already: the certainty that somewhere beyond the valley, past the open stones, a grey-headed woman might have survived the smoke, and that Petra would walk out of this cathedral and go and learn her all over again, from the beginning, as a stranger.

"Come on," she whispered to Xanthe, to herself, to the kingdom waking raw around them. "We've got new words to invent."

Behind them, in the shattered throne, something stirred that was neither crown nor king — and the freed memories drifting through the cathedral suddenly froze midair, every ribbon of light turning, all at once, to look at the door.
