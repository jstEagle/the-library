## 20. The Thing Without a Face

The crown cracked like ice on a thawing river, and what came out of it was not a king.

Petra had imagined this moment a hundred times since Sefa pressed the rite's fragments into her hands — imagined the unbinding as an ending, clean and bright, the way Maren ended fevers: one long exhale and then quiet. She had not imagined the sound. The crown did not shatter so much as *open*, gold splitting along seams no smith had ever forged, and from those seams poured a cold that had nothing to do with temperature. It was the cold of a cellar where something had been stored too long. It was the cold of a mouth deciding whether to swallow.

The cathedral of yesterday went silent around her.

That was the first horror: silence. For weeks Vey's great vessel had hummed with harvested memory, thousands of replayed yesterdays layered into a drone that lived in Petra's teeth. Now every stolen recollection in the vaults below simply stopped, mid-laugh, mid-grief, mid-song — cut off like a choir whose throats had been taken at once. The Underchoir stood scattered across the marble in their ragged grey, mouths still open around words they had spent. Sefa was on her knees, both hands pressed flat to the floor as if checking for a heartbeat. Xanthe stood at the center of the broken rite-circle with her arms at her sides, and she was breathing, and she was looking at Petra, and her eyes were full of a terror too old for her face.

*It worked,* Petra thought. *We did it. Why does it feel like we broke something open instead?*

Because they had. Because unbinding was not killing. She understood it now, in the way you understand things only after it is far too late to be careful — the rite had not destroyed the thing in the crown. It had removed the crown. And a hunger without its cage is not a dead hunger.

The air above the throne dais began to move.

Not wind. Movement itself — a suggestion of turning, like heat-shimmer over a road in high summer, except it was winter-cold and it was *looking*. Petra felt it pass over her the way a hand passes over bread at market, judging weight, judging freshness. It had no face. That was the worst of it. Every story she'd ever heard about devils gave them horns or teeth or burning eyes, something to aim at, something to hate. This had nothing. It was absence shaped vaguely like attention, and it was older than kingdoms, older than the language Xanthe rationed word by word, older perhaps than remembering. It had been starving for so long that starvation was all it knew how to be.

It turned — there was no other word for it — toward Xanthe.

"No," Petra said, and her voice came out small in all that marble.

Xanthe had one true-word left.

Petra had watched her spend the others through the night of the rite, each one a small death: the word that opened Aldric's sealed will, the word that woke the Underchoir's amputated fragments and made them singable again, the word that had cracked the crown's first seal. Each cost her color, warmth, years Petra couldn't count. Xanthe had gone into the circle carrying a whole dying language and come out holding a single syllable cupped in her chest like the last coal from a burned house. Petra didn't know what it was. Xanthe hadn't told her. Perhaps it was a name. Perhaps it was the word for *enough*. Perhaps it was simply the last piece of the grammar that could make things true, and the hunger knew it — knew that with it, the choir could begin again, could bind it anew, could starve it forever behind new words.

So it lunged.

There was no graceful way to describe what happened next. The absence-shaped-thing *moved*, all that vast patient hunger collapsing into speed, and it came down toward the small mud-brown girl who had once knocked on Maren's door with a scrap of royal purple in her fist, and Petra threw herself forward and knew even as she moved that she would be too late, that flesh is slow and hunger is not —

Aldric got there first.

He had been standing at the edge of the dais all night, freed and mortal, wearing a body that the crown had hollowed for eleven years. He moved like a man learning his own joints again, and he should have been slow, he should have been nothing but a ghost in a gardener's coat, but kings are made of one thing that outlasts crowns, and Aldric had spent eleven years with nothing left to him but that. He stepped between the hunger and the child.

"Take me," he said. "You've worn me long enough. Take what's mine."

The hunger paused.

Petra would think about that pause for the rest of her life. It was the pause of a starving animal offered meat when it expected stone. It did not understand willing payment; everything it had ever eaten had been torn from the unwilling, memories ripped out bleeding. And here was a man offering himself whole — not just his memories but the vessel that carried them, the face the crown had worn, the name it had hidden inside. Not a meal snatched from a table. A table laid down entirely.

"You cannot keep my face forever," Aldric said, and his voice cracked on it, because he was begging, Petra realized, the same way he'd begged her in the garden — not to save him, but to make sure this thing never wore him again. "If you take nothing else, take *this*. Take all of it. Let me end remembered rather than worn."

And then he opened his arms, the way you welcome a returning child, the way you welcome rain after drought.

The hunger took him.

It did not roar. It did not flash. It folded around him like water closing over a dropped stone, and Aldric — King Aldric, the garden-king, the man who had tended moonflowers because gardens need no memory — Aldric smiled. Petra saw it clearly, saw the whole of it: the terror underneath, and beneath the terror something that looked almost like relief, and above both of them a kind of terrible dignity, the dignity of a man who has finally found the one door he can walk through on his own feet. His lips moved. No sound came out. But Petra had learned enough of Xanthe's grammar by hand-sign to read the shape of it, and the shape was:

*Remember me.*

Then he was gone, and the hunger went with him, drawn down into the offering like a wick drinking flame, and the cold in the cathedral broke apart all at once, and warmth came back into the world so suddenly that several of the Underchoir wept at the mere feeling of it on their ruined throats.

* * *

Afterward there was a great deal of noise and very little of it mattered.

Vey's guards, released from the crown's harvest, woke confused in their grey livery and did not know whom to arrest. Courtiers sat on the marble steps cradling their heads as memories returned to them raw and unordered — some sobbed, some laughed horribly, one old woman simply repeated her own name over and over like a prayer. The cathedral's eternal replays guttered out one by one, and in the sudden ordinary silence the building seemed smaller, older, merely a building. Somewhere below, the vaults of preserved yesterdays were going dark, and no one had decided yet whether that was salvation or the largest theft in history.

Sefa found Petra sitting on the dais steps with Xanthe curled against her side, asleep or pretending to be, her small hand gripping Petra's sleeve with the desperation of someone who has been hunted since before she could remember.

"The king," Sefa rasped. Her voice, always broken, was barely there now. "Did it—"

"He chose," Petra said. "That's what matters. Nobody chose anything for him."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment, and then the old singer bowed her head, the closest thing to a coronation anyone would give Aldric now. "Eleven years I hid in the dark while he wore that thing," she whispered. "I told myself there was nothing left of him to save." Her hand found Xanthe's hair, gentle as falling ash. "I was wrong about kings. I was wrong about a lot of things."

They buried him three days later, though Petra suspected the kingdom would spend a generation burying him, each town adding its own grief to the mound. There was no body — there was nothing to bury but his gardening gloves and the crown itself, split and dark and finally, finally empty. Sefa sang over the grave in a voice like wind through a ruin, and the Underchoir lent their fragments, and it was not beautiful, but it was true, which Petra was learning was worth more.

On the last night before they left the capital, Petra stood alone in the royal garden among the moonflowers Aldric had tended, and she let herself grieve for a man she had known for one conversation and one sacrifice. He had asked her to remember him. She found, testing herself, that she could: the glassy eyes, the lucid fragment of the beggar-king, the smile as he opened his arms. The crown had wanted to wear him until the world forgot there had ever been anyone underneath. Instead he was everywhere now — in the returned memories of ten thousand courtiers, in Sefa's broken song, in Petra herself, a stranger carrying a stranger's last request.

*Dying a king, remembered.* It was not the ending he deserved. It was the ending he had chosen, which might be better.

Behind her, small feet on gravel. Xanthe came and stood beside her in the moonflower light, and did not sign anything, and did not need to. They watched the flowers open together, white as surrendered memories, and Petra thought about valleys and boundary stones and a witch who had held a ward with her life, and how far away all of it felt, and how close.

"We're going home," she said aloud, and was surprised to find the word *home* fit in her mouth like a key she'd owned all along without knowing.

Xanthe signed back, slow and careful: *Where is home?*

Petra looked north, toward the mountains, toward the valley whose stones now stood dark and open, toward whatever waited there that was neither hiding nor safety but something new.

"Wherever we plant things," she said.
