## 22. Open Stones

The road home was not the road they had taken.

Petra had braced herself for that — for the way the world outside the valley rearranged itself when you weren't watching, the way a dream reshuffles its furniture. But the road remembered them. It rose to meet their feet with a faint warmth, like an old dog lifting its head at a familiar hand, and where they walked the frost of early winter thinned and fell away behind them. The footprints of the dead still flickered in her peripheral vision — a farmer's heavy tread, a bride's slippers, once a child running with arms out as if flying — but now the living prints were layered over them, fresh and bright: hers, deep-heeled and deliberate, and Xanthe's small bare ones beside them, because Xanthe had refused boots all the way from the capital and no amount of arguing would put her in them.

*Stubborn,* Petra thought, with the particular tenderness of someone who has learned that stubbornness is another word for survival.

They came over the last ridge on a morning so clear it hurt, and Petra stopped walking before she meant to.

The valley lay below them exactly as she had carried it in her memory through every mile of the journey — the river's silver stitchwork, the terraced fields gone gold and fallow, Maren's crooked chimney standing up out of the trees like a finger raised in perpetual argument with the sky. But something was different, and it took her three full breaths to find it, and when she found it her throat closed so hard she could not have spoken if Xanthe had asked her to.

The boundary stones stood open.

She had known, of course. She had felt it from ten miles off, the way you feel a held breath finally released — the ward was finished, sung whole at last through her own mouth on the thirteenth night, and what remained of it was not a wall but a memory, resting quiet in the stones the way a song rests in an instrument nobody is playing. But knowing and seeing were different countries, and she stood on the border between them now, looking down at eleven grey monoliths that no longer leaned inward like conspirators but simply stood, dark and ordinary, wearing lichen and bird-lime and twenty years of being feared for nothing.

No shimmer. No hum. No wall between the world and the world.

"Come on," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and Xanthe looked up at her with those eyes the color of old honey and did not need language to understand. She took Petra's hand. Her palm, scarred with the crown-shaped burn that had started everything, was warm.

They walked down into the valley together.

* * *

Maren was in the garden.

Of course she was. Petra had imagined a hundred reunions on the road — Maren weeping, Maren scolding, Maren struck dumb — but the reality was this: a broad-shouldered woman on her knees in the cold dirt of the herb bed, wrapping the roots of a rosemary cutting in burlap against the frost, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who has done this same work every autumn for forty years and intends to do it every autumn for forty more.

She looked up when their shadows fell across her, and Petra saw the moment the recognition landed — saw it move through Maren's face like weather, shock and relief and grief and fury all chasing each other across the terrain, and then, because Maren was Maren, settling into a scowl.

"You're thin," Maren said.

"I'm—"

"And filthy. Both of you. There's river-mud in your hair, girl, I can see it from here." She hauled herself upright, knees cracking like green wood, and stood there gripping her burlap-wrapped rosemary like a weapon or a talisman, and her eyes were swimming. "The whole kingdom wakes up remembering things it spent twenty years forgetting, half the court in the capital doesn't know who they are until somebody tells them, there are stories coming up the trade road about a cathedral full of yesterday that emptied out overnight — and my apprentice walks back into my garden after four months without so much as a letter."

"There's no one to deliver letters to a valley with no road in," Petra said.

"That," said Maren, "is not an excuse, it is an observation," and then she crossed the garden in three strides and pulled Petra against her chest hard enough to bruise, and the burlap and the rosemary smell and the heartbeat underneath — steady, stubborn, infuriatingly alive — undid Petra entirely. She cried the way she had not let herself cry at any point on the journey, not at the burning house, not at the boundary stones with Maren staying behind, not even at the foot of Aldric's cairn. She cried like a child, ugly and graceless, and Maren held her through it and did not say *there, there*, because Maren had never once in her life said *there, there*, and instead said, into her hair, "I knew you'd finish it. I told the stones. They didn't believe me either."

Then Maren pulled back, held Petra at arm's length, and looked past her to the child standing at the garden gate with her hands folded and her chin up, watching them with the careful attention of someone who has learned that reunions are fragile things and strangers should not touch them.

"And this," Maren said, "is Xanthe."

Xanthe looked at Petra. Petra nodded. And Xanthe stepped forward, took Maren's free hand in both of hers, turned it over, and traced one deliberate finger down the lines of her palm — the greeting she used now instead of speech, the grammar of hands that she and Petra had built together out of necessity and were building still. *I see you,* it meant. *I know what you paid.*

Maren went very still. Then she closed her other hand gently around the small fingers and said, "Aye. I know what you paid too, little one. More than me, I'd wager. Come inside, both of you. There's soup. There's always soup. Four months of worry and the first thing I'll hear about is whether you've been eating, and don't think you can lie to me, I can count ribs through a shirt."

* * *

The house was the same and it was not the same.

The scorch-marked doorframe had been replaced with new wood, pale as bone against the old timber. Someone had rebuilt — Vey's riders had burned the kitchen wing that night on the ridgeline, four months and a lifetime ago, and Maren had evidently stood in the ashes exactly long enough to be furious, and then begun hauling stone. The new walls were thicker. The windows were bigger. Petra understood, running her thumb along the joinery, that Maren had built the house to be seen into — a hedge-witch who had spent her life behind curtains choosing glass.

"The ward's down," Maren said, following her gaze. She ladled soup without looking up. "Has been since that night. When the crown broke, everything it had eaten came back out of it — memories, mostly, pouring back into the kingdom like water finding old channels. And the part it had swallowed of your mother's song came home to the stones." She set a bowl in front of Xanthe, who was already eating with the focused intensity of someone who had learned never to waste food. "Eleven stones holding an unfinished verse, Petra. That's all the wall ever was. Your mother's fear, sung halfway and left hanging for twenty years. When the song finished — when you finished it — there was nothing left for the stones to do but stand there and be rocks."

"It feels strange," Petra said. "Walking in. I kept waiting for the push against my skin. The feeling of crossing."

"You'll feel it for years yet. Walls live longer in the body than in the world." Maren sat down across from her, and for a moment the firelight moved on her face and Petra saw how much the four months had cost her — new lines, a certain careful slowness in the left hand where the ward had burned through her when she held it. "Your mother thought safety was a place you could hide in. I think she was wrong. I think I was wrong, helping her build it." She tapped the table once, twice. "Safety's a thing you carry. Or it's nothing."

Outside the window, dusk came down blue and cold, and somewhere beyond the fields the boundary stones stood in the dark doing nothing at all, and Petra found that she could look at them without flinching for the first time since childhood.

* * *

Three days later, Petra took her mother's seeds outside the stones.

She had carried them the entire journey in a twist of linen inside her shirt, against her skin — fennel and clary sage, feverfew and angelica, and one small paper envelope marked in her mother's cramped, confident hand: *moon-penny, rare, sow facing the open sky.* In the crypt archives beneath the capital, among the crown's ledgers, she had found the rest of her mother's story — the unbinding singer who fled mid-rite, who ran to the far edge of the kingdom and built a wall of forgetting and hid inside it and raised a daughter and died without ever telling her why the valley had no magic in it. A woman who hoarded safety like a miser hoards coin, and paid for it with everything, and passed the debt down instead of the fortune.

Petra had hated her a little, on the road. Then Xanthe had spent her second-to-last true-word giving Petra a single memory of her mother — just one, sung once and gone forever: her mother laughing in a kitchen, younger than Petra was now, flour on her hands, saying *seeds want to be spent, love; that's the whole trick of them* — and after that Petra could not hate her, only grieve her, which was harder and cleaner and would take longer.

The spot she chose was a hundred paces beyond the northernmost stone, on a south-facing slope where the ground was dark and loose and the wind came off the hills carrying the smell of far places. She knelt. The frost-hard earth gave to her trowel in slow reluctant curls.

Xanthe squatted beside her, chin on her fists, supervising.

"It's strange soil," Petra said. "Out here. Everything grows wilder. Magic gets into the roots." She worked the first furrow. "Inside the stones, nothing ever grew that wasn't planted. Nothing happened that hadn't already happened. I used to think that was peace." She pressed a fennel seed into the dark and covered it, gentle as closing an eye. "It was just a very small room."

Xanthe touched her wrist. Then she pointed at the envelope marked *moon-penny*, and made the sign they had settled on for *rare* — fingers drawn slowly inward, toward the heart — and then opened her hand toward the sky.

"I know," Petra said. "Facing the open sky."

She dug the hole deeper for that one. As she worked, she talked, because she had discovered on the road that Xanthe liked being talked to while she watched — that words spoken freely, uselessly, lovingly, were themselves a kind of gift to a child who had spent her whole life rationing them. So Petra talked about her mother, and about Maren's kitchen, and about the taste of Oskar's stolen memory when it had come back to her with everything else the crown released, arriving whole one morning on the road like a bird landing on her shoulder — the cinnamon heat of Maren's stove, the specific scrape of the good knife — so that she had been given back almost everything, and had chosen to give it forward again anyway, teaching it to Xanthe in signs and stories, because inheritance meant passing forward, not hoarding. She talked about Aldric, who gardened now in the king's garden under his own name, mortal and forgetful and free, planting things he would not remember planting and being delighted by every one of them twice. She talked about Sefa, who was teaching the Underchoir to sing broken songs beautifully, and about Vey, who walked the empty cathedral with her wrong-shining eyes and had, last report, begun letting people leave.

"And someday," Petra said, tamping the earth down over the moon-penny seed, "you're going to say a true-word again. Not today. Not soon. But the grammar isn't dead, Xanthe. It's just sleeping in us. I've got two of your words now, the ones you taught me with your hands, and I've invented three more that probably mean nothing yet, but they will." She sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the slope of turned earth, the eleven seeds buried in it, the eleven stones standing dark and open behind them, keeping nothing in and nothing out. "New words. Grown out here, where anything can get into the roots."

Xanthe looked at the seeded ground for a long time. Then she reached out, and with one finger, in the soft fresh dirt beside the moon-penny's grave, she drew a shape Petra had never seen before — a spiral opening outward, like a fern unrolling, like a road leaving a valley — and she looked up at Petra with a question in her honey-colored eyes.

"What's that one?" Petra whispered.

Xanthe tapped the shape. Tapped her own heart. Pointed — north, toward the hills, toward the capital, toward the wide remembering world — and then, slowly, brought her hand back and laid it flat over the seeds.

Petra's breath caught. She did not know the sign. It was not in their grammar yet.

But she understood it anyway, the way you understand a true-word: some things arrive already true. *Something planted here is going to grow outward.* A word for the future, maybe. A word for the two of them. A word that had been waiting in a dying language for someone to need it.

"We'll figure out what to call it," Petra said, and her voice was not quite steady, and she found she did not mind. "We've got time now. Nobody's hunting anybody. The stones are open." She laughed — wet, surprised, real. "We can plant as many words as we want."

Below them, smoke rose from Maren's chimney, straight and silver in the still air. Above them, the sky did what open skies do, which is go on forever. And in the dark warm earth between, in its own time, in its own way, the moon-penny began.
