## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There are two ways to be invisible in the Guild of Impossible Things. The first is to be an apprentice, which I was for six years, during which time I developed a theory that the Guild's real business was not impossible things at all but the manufacture of people nobody looked at. The second way is to simply be me, Isandro Halloran, scribe third-class, debt-bound, keeper of the ledgers nobody reads and all the secrets nobody wants.

I want it noted that I was good at my job. Not *great*—the Guild does not permit greatness among the unlettered ranks of its scriveners—but reliably, dependably good, in the way a chair is good. Nobody thanks a chair. Nobody notices a chair. And when the chair finally breaks, everyone acts betrayed.

My morning began the way every morning began: with ink, cold tea, and the inventory of the impossible.

For those unfamiliar—and I was unfamiliar once, back when I still believed the world had edges—the Guild of Impossible Things maintains custody of everything the world has agreed not to believe in. Bottled storms, forty-three of them, shelved by temperament. Borrowed years, kept in brass reliquaries that tick faintly if you hold them to your ear. A door that opens onto last Tuesday. The left shoe of a giant whose name has been deliberately forgotten, though his footprints across the northern marshes have not. My duty was to record all of it: acquisition date, provenance, condition, and cost.

Cost was the interesting part. Everything in the Guild's keeping had been paid for, because the first law of impossibility is that nothing comes free—not wonder, not power, and certainly not the bottled scream of a storm that spent three centuries learning to hate the sea. The ledgers recorded what each item had cost and who had paid it, and I copied these entries in my neat, forgettable hand, and for eleven years that was the whole of my ambition: copy, file, seal, sleep, repeat.

Invisibility, I have since learned, is a luxury purchased at someone else's expense. But that morning I didn't know it yet. That morning I was happy, insofar as a man can be happy who has mistaken numbness for contentment.

* * *

The royal ledger arrived on the second Tuesday of autumn, as it always did, borne by a courier who never met my eyes and never waited for a receipt. It was bound in white calfskin stamped with the sunburst of House Aldric, and it smelled faintly of hot metal, which I attributed to the palace vaults and thought no more about. I uncapped my pen. I opened to the new entries. And I stopped.

The entry read: *Received, one coronation oath, binding grade. Paid: three memories of the queen, condition unassessed.*

I read it again. Payments made in memory were not unheard of—they appeared in the oldest volumes, from the founding wars, marked always with a black border and a warning in a dead language. But they hadn't been used in two hundred years. They *couldn't* be used anymore; the Guild had burned the formulas. Yet here was one, dated four months past, entered in a hand far finer than any palace clerk's, describing a transaction as casually as a purchase of lamp oil.

I turned the page. *Received, renewal of ward-tithe. Paid: the king's memory of his daughter's birth.*

Turned again. *Renewal, quarterly. Paid: the king's memory of his own name-day, age nine.*

Again. *Again.* Page after page, months of them, each entry smaller and stranger—a memory of a harvest festival, a memory of a childhood dog, a memory of his mother's voice—and each one crossed off against a single recurring line item, written always in the same fine hand:

*Crown maintenance.*

I sat back. The lamplight hummed. Somewhere below, in the Vault of Weathers, Storm Forty-One rattled its bottle in its sleep.

Here is what I should have done: fetched Master Corvane, reported the anomaly, returned to my tea, and remained a chair. Here is what I actually did: I kept reading. Because buried in the margins, in a cramped shorthand only a Guild scribe would recognize, someone—some earlier keeper of this ledger—had annotated the entries. And beside *Crown maintenance*, over and over, in handwriting gone progressively shakier across two hundred years of pages, ran the same marginal note:

*It is not maintenance. It is feeding.*

* * *

I worked until the light went thin and then I worked past that, because copying is safer than thinking. By the time the bell rang for evening meal, I had constructed and demolished four theories, each less comforting than the last. The crown was alive. The crown ate memories. The crown ate something else too, something the ledger called only *tithe*, drawn quarterly from a source listed under a sigil I'd never seen: a coiled line, like a serpent swallowing a star.

I filed the royal ledger in the cabinet of active accounts, sealed it with wax, and told myself the shaking in my hands was the cold.

That was when the lights went out.

Not the lamps—those held. The *other* lights. Through the scriptorium's high windows, the night sky, which an hour before had been strewn with stars in their familiar arrogant constellations, went dark in a single motion, like a hand passing over candle flames. All of them. At once. No dimming, no flicker, just absence, sudden and total, as if the sky had been a curtain and someone had finally pulled it back to reveal nothing behind it at all.

And beneath my feet, deep in the foundations, I felt—or heard, or dreamed I felt—the border wards falling. Every ward-keeper in the realm maintained the great circles that kept out what lived beyond the maps. Eleven years of copying inventory had taught me the wards' accounting: paid annually, funded faithfully, never once late.

Until tonight.

The bells started after that. Not the meal bell, not the fire bell—the long bell, the one that hung in the Guild's deepest tower and was rung, according to the older scribes, only when the world changed shape. Its sound was low and slow and did not stop.

I ran. Chairs do not run, but I ran anyway, down stairs and through corridors hung with bottled weathers, into the master's hall where the entire Guild had already gathered, a hundred faces lit by panic-lamps, all of them looking up at Master Corvane where she stood on the dais with the founding charter open before her like a shield.

"The stars," someone was saying. "The stars are *gone*."

"The wards," said someone else. "Every circle, all at once, as if the payment simply—stopped."

Corvane raised a hand and the hall fell silent enough to hear the long bell tolling on. She looked old in the lamplight, older than I had ever seen her, and her eyes swept the crowd and passed over me as they always did—except that this time they came back. This time they stopped. For the first time in eleven years, Master Corvane looked directly at Isandro Halloran, and there was something in her face I had never been important enough to see before.

Fear.

"Seal the records," she said. "All of them. Every vault, every ledger, every scrap of paper in this building. Nothing leaves. Nothing is spoken." Her gaze held mine a moment longer, and her voice dropped, though I heard it anyway, and I understood—with the particular clarity that arrives only when your life is already coming apart—that she was speaking to me alone.

"Especially the royal account."

Then the lamps guttered, all together, though there was no wind, and in the darkness I stood very still among a hundred strangers and realized that invisibility had never once protected me. It had only kept me from seeing how long I had been watched.

Someone was already asking, quietly, in the crowd behind me: "Where is the scribe who keeps the royal ledger?"
