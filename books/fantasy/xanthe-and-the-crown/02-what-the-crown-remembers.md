## 2. What the Crown Remembers

*Isandro*

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its records in a cellar that smelled of cold iron and old decisions, and I had spent nine years learning to be furniture in it.

That was not a complaint. Furniture was safe. Furniture got fed. The morning after the stars went out, I sat at my desk with the royal ledger open before me and tried very hard to remain furniture while my hands shook badly enough to rattle the inkwell.

I had found the entries three days before the wards fell—found them the way you find anything in this place: by accident, while looking for something duller. A requisition slip for storm-bottles had been misfiled under *C*, and wedged between *Crowns, Ceremonial (Assorted)* and *Crowns, Cursed (Do Not Dust)* sat a slim volume with no label at all.

The entries inside were written in the king's own hand, which was odd enough. The payments were what stopped my heart mid-beat.

*Paid to the Crown: one summer afternoon, aged nine, the smell of my mother's kitchen.*

*Paid to the Crown: the name of my first hound.*

*Paid to the Crown: the reason I wanted to be king.*

Nobody pays in memories. Memories aren't currency. They aren't anything. You can't bottle them like storms or lend them out like years—the Guild had tried both, and the ledgers said so, and the ledgers never lied. That was the one law above all our laws: *the record is true even when no one wants it to be.*

So either the ledger was wrong, which was impossible, or someone had been making impossible payments to an impossible thing, which was worse.

I should have reported it. Instead I copied it, because invisibility has one advantage over honesty: nobody watches the invisible. Then the wards fell, the stars went out like snuffed candles, and Master Corvane ordered every record sealed under triple wax, and I understood with terrible clarity that whatever ate those memories was still hungry.

I took the slim volume home inside my coat, against regulation, against sense, against the small voice that sounded suspiciously like self-preservation.

The knock came at midnight. Three sharp raps, then two soft ones, like someone who knew how doors worked but distrusted them.

I opened it on a stranger: broad-shouldered, travel-worn, with frost in her close-cropped hair despite the season and a ward-keeper's badge hanging from her neck by a fraying cord—the badge scored through with a single deliberate line. Struck off. Disgraced.

"You're Halloran," she said. "Guild scribe."

"I'm furniture," I said. "Furniture doesn't confirm names."

"Your master sealed the ward-records at sundown. I've been trying to read them for two days." She pushed past me into my one room with the confidence of someone who had slept in worse places. "Then I heard the Guild keeps *other* ledgers. Ones that explain why the border stones drank themselves empty in a single night."

"That's not public knowledge."

"Neither is what I know, scribe. So we're even." She turned, and her eyes caught the lamplight—grey, tired, absolutely unbending. "My name is Sefa. I kept the northern wards for eleven years until I asked why they needed feeding so often. Now show me what you stole, because I can smell the paper on you. It's warmer than your fire."

* * *

*Sefa*

Here is what eleven years of ward-keeping teaches you: everything is a debt. Every stone that holds back the dark is paid for, and the payment always comes from somewhere, and nobody who benefits ever asks where.

I asked. That was my whole crime, really. Not incompetence, not treason—just arithmetic. The northern wards consumed three times their rated draw, year over year, and when I filed my findings, the Guild struck my badge and the Crown struck my name from the rolls. I went home to a village that no longer appeared on maps and nursed my grudge like a hearth-fire.

Then the wards died, the stars went out, and my grudge turned out to be the only lamp left lit.

Halloran's room was a scribe's room—ink-stained, narrow, meticulously dull—and Halloran was exactly what I expected: a thin, watchful creature who flinched when looked at directly. But the book he pulled from his coat lining made my breath stop.

It was bound in grey leather, unmarked, and when I opened it the pages *breathed*. Not metaphor. The parchment expanded and contracted like something sleeping.

"The royal ledger," Halloran said. "Unlisted. Payments from King Aldric, dated from coronation night onward."

"To whom?"

"That's rather the question."

I read. I have read ward-grids in four languages and the dead script of the mountain folk, and I have never seen accounts like these. The entries weren't purchases. They were *feedings*. And interleaved among the memory-payments ran a second column in different ink—a deep red that seemed to move when I wasn't looking—recording withdrawals from somewhere called simply *the Source*.

Withdrawals measured in flame.

"Dragon-fire," I said aloud, because saying it made it real and real things could be fought. "There hasn't been a living dragon in the settled lands for sixty years. But there are hoards. Preserved flames. The old texts say dragon-fire doesn't go out unless something *drinks* it."

"And the last recorded hoard," Halloran said quietly, "is under the palace. It's in the Guild inventory. *One flame, legacy item, do not touch.*"

We looked at each other across the breathing book, and I saw him arrive at the same ugly sum I had: the wards fell because the Source ran dry. The Source ran dry because something drank it. Something drank it because—

"—because it's alive," he finished. "The crown. The entries aren't payments to a treasury. Aldric has been paying a *creditor* for twenty years. Buying authority. Buying love, maybe. Buying the right to be king." His laugh came out cracked. "I catalogued cursed crowns for nine years. There's a whole shelf of them. Do Not Dust. I dusted them every Thursday."

"Furniture," I said, "that steals books and reads them by candlelight isn't furniture anymore."

He flinched again, but differently this time. Like the words landed somewhere he'd thought was empty.

"There's one more entry," he said, turning to the final page. "Not in the king's hand. Older. Look at the date—it predates the coronation by a century."

I read it twice to be sure:

*Item on deposit: Princess Veyra Aldricsdottir, exiled by royal decree following inquiry into crown expenditures. Status: inconvenient. Note appended by creditor: the daughter remembers too much. Recommend continued distance.*

"They exiled her for asking about the ledgers," Halloran whispered. "Same as they struck you off for asking about the wards. Same as—" He stopped.

"Same as they made you invisible for noticing," I finished.

Outside, the darkness pressed against the window like a tide. No stars. No moon. Somewhere in the city, a temple bell rang the hour, and even its sound seemed thinner than yesterday, as if the night itself were being eaten from the edges inward.

* * *

*Isandro*

Princess Veyra arrived two days later, which I maintain proves either fate or excellent record-keeping, and given my profession I choose to believe the latter.

She came to the Guild's public counter—because even in a dying world, paperwork continues—and requested copies of her father's coronation expenditures, citing a statute so obscure I'd only ever seen it cited by ghosts. The clerk refused her. She argued. Loudly, precisely, with the specific fury of someone who has rehearsed this argument alone in exile for six years.

And because I am apparently no longer capable of being furniture, I walked up to the counter, set the grey ledger down between them, and said, "Perhaps Her Highness would prefer the unabridged edition."

What followed was forty minutes of the most terrifying conversation of my life, conducted in the Guild's back reading-room, during which a princess, a disgraced ward-keeper, and a debt-bound scribe discovered they had each been holding one corner of the same trap.

Veyra read her father's memory-payments without blinking, though her jaw tightened at *the reason I wanted to be king*. When she reached the note about herself, she read it three times, and then she laughed—one short, sharp sound with no humor in it whatsoever.

"He wrote me off as an *expense*," she said. "Filed under inconvenient. My own father." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "So. The crown is alive. It eats memories and dragon-fire. It hollowed my father into a puppet and starved the wards to fatten itself. And now the world is going dark while it digests the last flame under the palace."

"It offered me something," I said, before I could decide whether to say it. Both women turned. "In the margins of the ledger. Writing that wasn't there when I copied it. It knows I read it. It said—" My voice did something humiliating. "It said it could give me a name that people remembered. A life that counted. All I had to do was put the book back and walk away."

Sefa's hand came down on the table, flat, final. "And you didn't."

"No."

"Why not?" Veyra asked. Genuine curiosity, no judgment. From anyone else it would have been cruel.

"Because it was offering to sell me myself," I said. "And I've seen its prices."

Something shifted in the room then—not warmth exactly, nothing so generous—but three separate silences aligned, the way ward-stones align just before they hold. Sefa spoke first.

"The binding chamber sits below the throne room. I helped design the containment wards as a cadet; they'll recognize my work, which means they might let me in. Veyra knows the palace corridors the guards have forgotten. And you—" she fixed me with those unbending grey eyes, "—you're the only one here who can read a creditor's terms and live. Unbinding a contract requires a party named in it."

"I'm not named in it."

"You are now. It wrote to you." She almost smiled; it was a grim, weathered thing, but a smile. "Congratulations, scribe. You've been promoted from furniture."

Veyra stood, pulling a hooded cloak from beneath her traveling coat—the cloak of a woman who had planned this return for years and never let herself believe it. "The last flame is guttering. My father may already be beyond reach. We leave within the hour, and we do not stop."

"One question," I said, gathering the ledger, my pens, the pathetic tools of a life I was walking away from. "When we unbind it—when the crown loses its debtor—what happens to everything it's owed? Twenty years of a king's memories. A century of interest."

Sefa and Veyra exchanged a look over my head, and neither of them answered fast enough, and the silence told me everything the ledger hadn't yet dared to write:

The debt doesn't die. Debts only ever change names.

And mine was the freshest one in the room.
