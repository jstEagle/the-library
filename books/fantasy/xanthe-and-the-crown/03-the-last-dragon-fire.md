## 3. The Last Dragon-Fire

I had been exiled for asking too many questions, so you would think I'd have learned to stop asking them. Instead I was crouched in a drainage culvert beneath my father's palace, asking one more: *how did I get here?*

"Because you bribed a fishmonger with your signet ring," Sefa said, reading my face the way she read wards—annoyingly well.

"I traded it," I corrected. "There's a difference. A bribe implies guilt."

"You are guilty of something," Isandro said from behind me. They were carrying the ledger under their coat like a stolen child, which, in fairness, it was. "You're guilty of optimism. It's terminal in this family."

We came up through the culvert into the servants' cellars, and the dark was wrong. I want to be precise about that, because I grew up in this palace and I know its darknesses the way you know the moods of an old dog. This dark was not empty. It was *hungry*—a dark that leaned on you, that pressed at the edges of your eyes as if tasting whether you were worth eating. The last dragon-fire was guttering somewhere above us, and every shadow in Vaelmoor knew it.

The torch Sefa carried burned low and blue. Ward-keeper's flame; it fed on ambient magic instead of pitch, which meant it was feeding on almost nothing now.

"How long?" I asked her.

"If we hurry? Enough." She didn't say enough for what. She never did. That was the trouble with disgraced ward-keepers: they kept their honesty in ledgers too.

* * *

We climbed through the palace by ways I had known since childhood—the dumbwaiter shaft behind the kitchens, the gallery with the cracked saints, the stair where my mother used to catch me eavesdropping. Every corridor held a memory wearing my father's voice, and every one of them was a little less him than the last. The portraits had it worst. Aldric the Bold, Aldric the Just, Aldric Crowned Beneath Seven Stars—and in each frame his painted eyes had gone pale as skimmed milk, as if he had been looking at something bright until there was nothing left to see.

"He gave it everything," Isandro murmured at the largest portrait. Their fingers hovered over the gilt frame without touching. Scribes have odd reverence; they touch paper more gently than skin. "The coronation entries. He paid in memories for twenty years of borrowed authority. First the small ones—his mother's lullaby, the taste of rain. Then—" they swallowed— "then the ones with you in them."

I looked at my own painted face beside his, aged eleven, holding a wooden sword. My father's brush-captured smile had been real once. I could no longer confirm it from memory myself; exile does its own kind of ledger work.

"Then we're even," I said. "Neither of us remembers him loving me."

"That," said Isandro, "is exactly why we're going to fix it."

* * *

We found him in the binding chamber, because of course he was there. Kings do not haunt their own palaces, but crowns make exceptions.

The chamber sat at the heart of the old star-fort, a dome of black glass through which the night should have shown seven constellations. Instead there was nothing—a ceiling of perfect, starving dark. And below it, on the iron throne that wasn't really a throne but a *lock*, sat my father.

Or the shape my father had been worn down to. He was not skeletal—he was worse than that, *tidal*, his body moving slightly out of rhythm with itself, like a man remembered by someone who hadn't loved him carefully. His hands were folded in his lap. His eyes were open and milk-pale.

"Aldric," I said. "Father."

His head turned. For one heartbeat something surfaced in him—recognition, or the ghost of it, a man drowning who sees light through water.

"Veyra?" His voice was gravel in an empty bucket. "You shouldn't—you asked too many questions."

"Yes. I'm told it's hereditary." I stepped forward. Sefa caught my sleeve; I shook her off. Some mistakes you have to walk into on your own feet.

"The crown," my father whispered. "Don't let it hear your name."

Too late. The crown heard everything.

It lifted from his brow—or rather, it had never quite been *on* his brow; it hung a finger's width above, suspended in its own gravity, a circlet of black iron set with seven stones that had once held dragon-fire and now held only appetite. When it spoke, it did not use sound. It used certainty. Its voice arrived inside the skull already believed.

***Veyra of House Aldricson. You have come far to inherit what is freely given.***

"It's talking to me," I said stupidly.

"It talks to everyone," Isandro said. "Ignore it. It lies by telling the truth about things that don't matter." They stepped forward, past me, past Sefa, into the center of the chamber, and the crown turned its attention on them like a lighthouse discovering a ship.

***Isandro Halloran. Debtor. Invisible. We have watched you carry other people's accounts for eleven years. We know what you were denied. Your name struck from the guild rolls. Your scholarship refused. Your family's debt purchased with your childhood. Walk away, and all of it returns to you inverted. Wealth. Standing. A name that opens doors. The world will remember you were always worthy.***

I saw it hit them. Of course I saw it. Isandro had spent three weeks being wry at me and Sefa, deflecting every kindness with a joke filed neatly under accounting humor, and now the crown offered them the one thing no joke could reach.

They stood very still. Then they laughed—one short, cracked note, the sound of a coin bitten to test its metal.

"You've read my file," Isandro said to the crown. "Very thorough. But here's an entry you missed." They turned and looked at us—at Sefa, arms crossed and jaw set; at me, probably crying, absolutely refusing to dignify it. "Two people walked into a starving dark place with me. Nobody has ever done that. Not for wealth. Not for standing. You can't sell me back what I only just learned I was owed."

***Sentiment is a currency. It devalues.***

"Then I'll spend it while it's good," Isandro said, and opened the ledger.

* * *

What happened next, I will write down as accurately as I can, though accuracy feels insufficient, the way a map is insufficient to the country.

Sefa went to the four corners of the binding chamber and cut her palms on the ward-stones, speaking the old words of unbinding—the ones she'd been stripped of rank for translating, the heresy that said *no crown may own a king*. Her blood lit the stones blue. My father groaned on his lock-throne, and the shape of him steadied, tide pulling back toward shore.

Isandro climbed the dais and stood before the crown with the ledger open across their forearms like an offering.

"In the name of the Guild of Impossible Things," they read, "all debts recorded herein transfer to the undersigned." A pause. "That's me. I'm the undersigned. It's been a strange year."

***You cannot afford this debt. It will hollow you faster than it hollowed him. He was a king. You are nothing.***

"Yeah," Isandro said softly. "But I'm nothing *with witnesses*. There's a difference. Ask any scribe."

They tore the page free—the coronation entry, twenty years of payments in memories—and held it into the last light of the last dragon-fire, which chose that exact moment to gutter up from the floor grates one final time, gold and furious, a dying animal making itself enormous.

The fire took the page. The crown screamed—not in sound, in *certainty*, and for the first time I disbelieved it. The iron circlet fell. Sefa caught it in a ward-glove and slammed it into the lead box she'd carried since chapter one of all our misfortunes, and the lid closed, and the hungry dark above us shuddered—

And the stars came back.

Not all at once. One, high over the eastern dome, tentative as a struck match. Then another. Through the black glass ceiling I watched the sky remember itself, and below me my father sagged against the throne, breathing, *present*, his eyes clearing from milk to grey to—oh—to recognition, terrible and total, the recognition of a man seeing everything he'd sold.

"Veyra," he croaked. "How much do I owe you?"

But Isandro didn't answer him. Isandro was on their knees at the top of the dais, and the transferred debt was arriving in them visibly—color draining, hair going white at the temples, years being collected all at once by a creditor with excellent penmanship.

"No," I said, and started up the dais. "No, you absolute idiot, you don't get to do this alone—"

Behind me, Sefa was already running, tearing open the lead box again, shouting words I didn't know yet but would soon learn by heart—words from an old guild law about debts shared being debts halved.

Isandro looked up at us, white-haired, smiling, and said, "I told you. Witnesses."
