## 4. A Debt Shared Is Halved

The first star came back while I was counting.

Not counting anything important. Counting, because counting was what my hands did when my mind refused to be in the room. The binding chamber still smelled of scorched dragon-fire and something older underneath it—ozone and parchment, the smell of a debt being transferred. My name sat in the crown's ledger now where Aldric's had been for thirty years. *Isandro Halloran. Debtor.* The ink was still wet in a way that ink shouldn't be, glistening like the whole page was weeping about it.

And above us, through the shattered dome of the binding chamber, one star blinked back into existence over the northern ridge, small and stubborn as a candle someone had relit out of spite.

"There," Sefa said. She was sitting on the floor with her back against a pillar, holding a cloth to the burn along her forearm, looking up through the hole with the expression of a woman who had spent ten years keeping wards alive and had just watched one heal itself. "Told you the sky wasn't dead. Just hungry."

"You told me it was collapsing," I said.

"I tell people lots of things. They rarely listen." She nodded at my hands. "You're shaking."

"Am I?" I looked down. I was shaking. Funny how you don't notice these things until someone says them aloud. "I think that's the debt settling in. Like a house creaking after a storm."

Veyra knelt beside her father. King Aldric—I supposed I should get used to saying *former* King Aldric, though the title hadn't quite finished dying yet—lay on the dais where the crown had kept him standing for three decades, a puppet who'd forgotten he was ever anything else. His eyes were open. That was new. For most of our descent into the palace they'd been open but empty, windows with nobody home, and I had written enough ledger entries to know exactly what had been purchased with whatever was behind them.

Now there was someone behind them again. He looked old. He looked *tired*. He looked, most of all, like a man waking from a very long dream to discover the bill had come due and been paid by strangers.

"You," he rasped, and his gaze found me. "The scribe. You took it."

"Somebody had to," I said. "It was in the ledgers. Everything gets entered eventually. I just moved the entry."

"That's not—" He stopped. Coughed. Veyra held water to his lips without being asked, and something passed between them, some old wound acknowledging another old wound, neither of them ready to stitch it closed yet. "That is not how debts work, boy."

"With respect, Your Majesty," I said, "I've read more ledgers than anyone alive, and I can tell you with authority that no one actually knows how debts work. They just pretend very loudly until everyone believes them."

* * *

We carried him down through the darkened palace, or rather Sefa and Veyra carried him while I walked beside them clutching the crown itself wrapped in three layers of ward-cloth, because apparently I was the only one whose skin didn't crawl when it touched mine. Or maybe my skin crawled too and I'd simply stopped having opinions about my own suffering. Hard to say. The debt sat in my chest like a stone swallowed whole—not painful, exactly, but *present*, the way a bruise is present, the way grief is present.

Outside, the city had noticed.

You have to understand: when the stars went out, the world went quiet in the specific way of things holding their breath. Now the breath was coming back. Stars returned one by one across the black—not all at once, nothing so dramatic, just here a point of light, there another, the sky remembering itself constellation by constellation. And beneath that slow mending, people were coming out of their houses. Torches were lit. Someone was singing, badly, in a tavern on the processional road, and others were joining in, and the song was old and off-key and absolutely perfect.

"They'll want answers," Veyra said. She had ash in her hair and her father's jaw set at an angle that suggested she'd inherited more than his face. "About the wards. About him. About what the crown did."

"The Guild will want to give them *ledgers* instead," I said. "Sealed ones. Summaries. A very polite account of nothing."

"Not anymore." Sefa said it quietly, and both of us turned. She was looking at the palace gates ahead, where the Guild's seal-bearers were already gathering—of course they were, my former masters could smell an unsealed record from three provinces away—and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before. Not the wariness she wore like armor. Something harder and kinder underneath. "Master Corvane sealed those records the night the stars died. Sealing records during a catastrophe of state—that's not prudence. Under the Old Concord, that's *concealment*."

"The Old Concord hasn't been invoked in two hundred years," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It hasn't."

* * *

Here is what happened in the Guild hall, and I'm going to tell it plainly because I've spent my whole life watching other people dress plain things up in ceremony until you couldn't see the bones anymore.

Master Corvane stood before the great vault of ledgers—the true ones, the memory-payments, the borrowed years, every impossible transaction the Guild had ever hidden behind its respectable front—and he said, "These matters are beyond public understanding."

And Sefa, disgraced ward-keeper, stripped of rank, the woman whose warnings about the border wards had been filed and forgotten, stepped forward and recited the Old Concord from memory. Every clause. I checked afterward; she got every clause right. *When the Guild's accounts endanger the realm, the accounts belong to the realm.* Concealment voids the seal. Three witnesses of standing may invoke it.

She had two.

"I'm a princess of the realm," Veyra said. "Exiled for asking questions, which I note the Concord specifically protects."

"And I'm the current holder of the crown's debt," I said, "which makes me, by the Guild's own accounting, its single largest creditor. It seems I finally matter."

Corvane looked at me then—really looked, the first time in eleven years—and I want to record honestly that there was no villainy in his face. Just an accountant's horror at an unbalanced book. "Halloran," he said. "Do you understand what you're doing? Those ledgers opened, every debt made public—half this kingdom will tear itself apart."

"Maybe," I said. "But the other half already lived in the dark, Master. I know. I worked there." I set my hand on the vault. "Open it."

They opened it.

* * *

The abdication happened three days later, in the throne room, before the assembled court and half the city craning through the doors. Aldric did it standing, which cost him visibly, and he did it simply, which cost him more. Kings are not good at simple.

"The crown borrowed my years," he said, voice carrying in the hush, "and I let it, because power was easier than truth. I am done borrowing." He lifted the circlet of state—the ordinary one, silver, gloriously non-sentient—and placed it in Veyra's hands. "My daughter asked too many questions. I am asking her to keep asking them."

Veyra took the crown and, to her eternal credit, did not put it on. She held it at her side like a tool, not a halo, and said, "First act: the Guild's ledgers stay open. All of them. There will be a public reckoning, debt by debt, and restitution where restitution is owed." A pause, and then, wry as any scribe: "Starting with mine. I intend to pay back every year of my exile with interest. The court will find the arithmetic humiliating. Good."

The cheering went on a long time. I stood at the back, as I had always stood at the back, and found—to my genuine surprise—that I wasn't invisible anymore. People knew my name. *Isandro Halloran, who unbound the king.* They said it like it meant something. I kept wanting to correct them, to explain that I'd mostly been terrified and lucky and propped up by two extraordinary women, but Sefa had told me that if I deflected my accomplishments one more time she would enter them in a ledger under my name so I couldn't deny ownership.

Which brings me to the problem. Because there was a problem.

* * *

The debt was killing me. Slowly, politely, in the manner of well-kept books, but certainly. The stone in my chest grew heavier by the day. I started forgetting things—small things first, the taste of bread, my mother's laugh, whether I'd ever been happy—and I recognized the symptoms immediately, because I'd read thirty years of Aldric's payments. The crown's debt was collecting itself. Memory for power; now, apparently, memory for nothing at all, since I hadn't taken the power, only the bill.

I hid it. Of course I hid it. Invisibility is a hard habit to break, even when you've stopped being invisible.

Sefa caught me on the ninth night, when I wrote *ward-anchor, third tier* in my journal instead of *Sefa*, because the debt had eaten the difference between a person and a thing. She read the page upside down—ward-keepers read everything upside down, occupational habit—and went very still.

Then she did not shout. She went and got Veyra, and the two of them came back and sat across from me at my little table in the Guild annex like a tribunal of two, and Veyra said, "Tell us the terms."

"It's not negotiable," I said. "Debts aren't. Believe me, I've read—"

"The Old Concord," Sefa interrupted, "clause nine. *No debt of the Guild shall rest upon a single soul when others willingly claim share of it.*"

I stared at her. "That clause has never—"

"Neither had clause seven," Veyra said. "Until last week. We're establishing precedent at an impressive rate. Clause nine, Halloran. Say the words with us, or I'll have it entered by royal decree and you'll spend eternity being *grateful* in addition to everything else, which I promise is worse."

So we said the words. The three of us, hands on the crown's ledger, in a drafty annex smelling of ink and cold tea, invoking a law older than the kingdom to do the simplest thing anyone had ever done for me: they took part of it. Sefa claimed her third with the flat certainty of a woman who had lost her rank and decided she'd rather have this. Veyra claimed hers with a queen's signature, which made it legally unassailable, which was very like her.

The stone in my chest did not vanish. But it lightened. It became, for the first time, a weight I could stand under—a burden with handles, something shared, something that pulled three directions instead of crushing one. When I breathed that night, the air tasted like bread again. Like my mother's kitchen. Like, possibly, happiness remembered rather than happiness owed.

* * *

Today I began a new ledger.

Not the Guild's. Mine. First page, my own name at the top—*Isandro Halloran, Keeper of Accounts*—and beneath it, the first entry:

*Owed to Sefa: one life, freely given. Repayment: ongoing.*
*Owed to Veyra: one signature's worth of salvation. Repayment: ongoing.*

I left the amounts blank. Some entries, I'm learning, you don't close—you just keep paying into them gladly, and the gladness is the interest.

There's one more entry pending. This morning a courier arrived with a letter sealed in grey wax I didn't recognize, and inside, a single line in a hand I've never seen but somehow feel I ought to:

*The crown had creditors too. One of them is coming to collect.*

Sefa read it over my shoulder. Veyra read it after her. Then the three of us looked at each other across my table, and Veyra smiled the smile of a queen who has decided trouble is simply arithmetic she hasn't finished yet.

"Well," she said. "Shall we see what we owe?"
