## 4. Terms of Debt

The summons came at the hour when the counting house smelled most of tallow and old paper, when the daylight through the high windows had gone the color of weak tea and every scribe's shadow stretched long across the ledgers like something trying to escape its body.

Mara was mid-column — forty-one entries into a reconciliation of memory-debts owed by the dyers' quarter, each line a person's recollection of their mother's face or the taste of a particular summer, sold to the guild in exchange for grain during the famine years — when the runner touched his shoulder.

"Guildmaster wants you. The small room."

The small room. Not the counting floor, not the vault gallery where debts were weighed and sealed, but the narrow chamber behind the guildmaster's office where contracts were renegotiated and, occasionally, where people learned that the number beside their name had grown larger without their consent. Mara set down his pen with the deliberate care of a man who had taught himself never to leave a line unfinished, blotted it, squared the ledger, and followed.

His debt stood at eleven years, four months, and some odd days. He knew it the way other men knew their own weight or the sound of their own breathing. He had inherited nine of those years from his father, who had borrowed against his children's futures to keep the family forge lit through a bad winter, and he had added two of his own — one for the privilege of learning letters as an indentured boy, one for a mistake he did not let himself think about, involving a brother whose name he still wrote carefully, when he had to write it at all, as though the ink might notice his hesitation.

Eleven years. He was thirty-two. The arithmetic of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: he would walk out of this building a free man at forty-three, if his health held, if the guild's ledgers held, if no guildmaster ever looked at his file on a slow afternoon and found a reason to add a line.

In the small room, Guildmaster Vess stood at the window with her back to him, watching the courtyard below. She was a compact woman, gray at the temples, with the kind of stillness that made lesser motion seem like disorder. On the table between them lay a single sheet of paper, face down.

"You found something in the archive last night," she said, without turning.

Mara's stomach performed a slow, practiced rotation. "I audit what I'm assigned to audit, Guildmaster."

"I know exactly what you're assigned to audit. That's rather the point of you." She turned then. Her eyes were the pale brown of watered tea, and they held the particular patience of someone who had watched a thousand debtors try to lie about small things while owing large ones. "You requested access to the sealed collection. Founder's era. Your justification was thin. Your handwriting was nervous. You've been balancing this house's books for fourteen years, Oyelaran; don't imagine I can't read a ledger of a different kind."

He said nothing. Denial was a currency he could not afford to spend here, and she would know its counterfeit weight anyway.

"The courier who came through the gate last night," Vess said. "You saw him arrive."

It was not a question either. Mara thought of the man half-carried through the back gate in the hour before dawn — mud to the thighs, a wound along his ribs that had soaked through three bandages, hands locked around a lead-sealed case the way a drowning man locks around driftwood. He remembered the cold. That was the thing he kept returning to, the detail that had lodged under his skin like a splinter: the case had burned cold. The air around it had frosted in the lantern light, and the two gate-porters who took the courier's arms had flinched from it as though from a brand.

"I saw him," Mara said. "I saw what he carried."

"And?"

"And I entered it in the night log. Weight unrecorded — we have no scale for it. Seal intact. Courier conscious but delirious. He would not release it." He paused. "He has not released it. It is in the infirmary with him now. Under guard."

Something moved at the corner of Vess's mouth — not quite approval, not quite annoyance. "Under guard," she repeated. "Yes. Well. Here is your new assignment."

She turned the paper over.

Mara read it twice before the words stopped sliding around. *Handler's bond. The scribe Mara Oyelaran is hereby attached to the person of the courier known as Xanthe, in the capacity of custodian-interpreter, until such time as the cargo in his keeping is surrendered to the Ember Guild or otherwise disposed of by the Guildmaster's authority. Should the courier defect, abscond, or be lost while under the said scribe's supervision, the said scribe's outstanding obligations shall be extended by seven years.*

Seven years.

The stone in his chest gained weight. Eighteen years, four months, and some days. He would leave this building at fifty. If his eyes lasted. If his hands lasted. If the arithmetic of his body permitted him anything at the end of it but a chair by a window.

"With respect," Mara said, and his voice came out level, which cost him more than shouting would have, "the courier is a stranger. I cannot guarantee the behavior of a stranger."

"No one can. That is why the penalty exists." Vess crossed to the table and set her palm flat on the contract, not quite offering it. "Consider your alternatives, Oyelaran. I could give him to Corvane's people — the enforcers — to hold. But enforcers are blunt instruments, and whatever sits in that case has already cracked one porter's knuckles frost-white through a leather glove. I need someone who listens. Who notices. Who reads." Her gaze flicked up. "Who audits."

"You think there's something to read about him."

"I think," Vess said slowly, "that a courier walks out of the southern marshes carrying a seal I have seen perhaps twice in my life, half-dead, refusing to surrender it even unconscious — his fingers had to be pried loose to treat the wounds, and he woke fighting — and I think a man does not defend a parcel like that out of greed. Greed runs out. Loyalty doesn't. I want to know who he is loyal *to*, and whether that loyalty can be bought, redirected, or survived." She slid the paper toward him. "And I want someone in that room whom I trust to tell me everything. Which is you, God help this house."

Mara looked at the contract. The ink was fresh and black and final. Somewhere below, in the infirmary, a stranger slept with his fists curled around emptiness because they had taken the case away while he dreamed.

He picked up the pen. His hand did not shake, which surprised him. Fourteen years in the counting house taught the hands a terrible calm.

He signed.

* * *

The infirmary occupied the guild's old chapel, deconsecrated so long ago that no one living remembered what had been worshipped there, though the alcove niches along the walls still held the shadows of removed statues. Six cots. Afternoon light fell through windows of glass so old it went green and wavy, turning the whole room into the inside of a bottle at the bottom of a river.

Xanthe was awake. That was the first thing. The second thing was that his hands were empty and he was staring at them as if they belonged to a stranger — as if the absence of the case were a wound worse than the one stitched along his ribs.

A guard stood by the door, and gave Mara a nod of recognition and something close to pity.

"You're the ledger man," Xanthe said. His voice was rough, scraped low by fever and road dust. He was younger than Mara had guessed in the dark of the gate — twenty-five at most, dark-skinned, marsh-country features, with a traveler's weathering laid over what might once have been city softness. His eyes were the remarkable thing: amber-brown, nearly gold in this light, and fixed on Mara with the unnerving directness of someone too exhausted to perform politeness. "They sent a ledger man to watch me."

"They sent a scribe," Mara corrected, and pulled the stool from beneath the cot's footboard, sitting at a careful distance — near enough to converse, far enough that the conversation would not feel like a cage closing. He had learned, over fourteen years, that frightened people talked to distance and went silent before proximity. "To take your statement. The guild needs to record how the cargo came into your possession. Provenance matters. Everything here has provenance."

"Does it." Xanthe shifted against the pillows, winced, and Mara saw him make the calculation — pain against pride — and lose gracefully, settling back. "And what's my provenance worth? They took it off me while I slept. Tell me the guild hasn't opened it."

"It hasn't been opened. It won't be, without—" Mara stopped. Without whose authority? Vess's? And what authority did Vess truly have over a thing that predated her grandmother? "It hasn't been opened," he said again, and heard in his own voice the precise tone of a man choosing his words, and saw Xanthe hear it too.

The courier almost smiled. It was a thin thing, more scar than smile. "You're honest. That's rare enough I should mark it. You're also scared. Also rare — most of the people in this building look at me like I'm inventory." He flexed his empty hands. "Where is it?"

"In the strongroom. Cold storage — there's a vault on the lower level where nothing spoils. It seemed..." Mara searched for the word. "*Comfortable* seemed wrong. Appropriate. The seal hasn't been touched."

"Good." Something in Xanthe's shoulders unlocked by a degree. Then, warily: "Why do you care whether it's been opened? You're guild. The guild owns everything in this building, including, I'm told, you."

The barb landed precisely because it was true. Mara let a silence stand rather than fill it with denial.

"I audit," he said finally. "Which means I spend my life determining what things are worth. Most things in this house are worth exactly what someone desperate agreed to pay for them. A mother's memory of her wedding day: two sacks of barley. A man's right to ever see his daughter: forgiveness of a gambling debt." He kept his voice flat, procedural, though the words tasted like ash — they always did. "I have gotten very good at knowing the difference between a thing's price and a thing's value. Whatever you carried through that gate, the porters wouldn't touch it after sundown. Two of them asked to be transferred off gate duty entirely. That is not the behavior of men guarding inventory." He met Xanthe's gold-flecked eyes. "So yes. I care what's inside it. Professionally. And somewhat more than professionally, if you want the full entry."

Xanthe studied him for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled across the courtyard cobbles; the green light shivered.

"You want to know what I know," Xanthe said at last. "That's what this is. The guildmaster couldn't get it out of me while I was bleeding, so she sends the patient one." He laughed — a short, dry sound with no humor in it, only exhaustion. "Fine. Ledger man. Write this down: I don't know what it is. Not truly. I know what I was told, and I know what it did, and those are two different entries, aren't they? In your terms."

"Two entries," Mara agreed quietly, and took out his notebook — not the official statement form, just his own pocket book, because he suspected the real testimony would come in the margins. "Tell me the first entry."

Xanthe looked at his hands again. When he spoke, his voice had changed — flattened, reciting, the cadence of a story told many times to himself on long roads.

"Three weeks ago I was running the marsh circuit. Low work — letters, small parcels, the kind of carrying that pays in meals. There's a woman lives out past Sallowmere, a hedgewitch named Odile Fen, and she'd hired me twice before to run herbs and charms to the river villages. Good coin for bad ground. This time she met me at her door before dawn, and she had this case wrapped in oilcloth, and she said —" his jaw tightened — "she said, 'You will carry this to the Ember Guild in Vellmarrow, and you will put it in no one's hands but the keeper of the oldest book.' Those were her exact words. I remember them because they made no sense. The guild has hundreds of books."

*The keeper of the oldest book.* Mara's pen stopped moving. In the sealed archive, in the founder's own hand, there was a ledger older than every other record in the guild — the one he had been auditing when he found the date circled twice, the date that was today. He said nothing. He wrote nothing. Some entries you keep off any page at all.

"She paid me half upfront," Xanthe went on. "More than the job was worth. I asked her what it was. She said it didn't matter what it was; it mattered that it got there. I asked her why her. Why me. She said—" and here the courier's voice caught, just slightly, a hairline crack in fired clay — "she said, 'Because you'll do it honestly, and honesty is the only wrapping that holds.'"

"What happened to her?" Mara asked, though he already knew, from the way Xanthe's knuckles whitened against the blanket, from the shape of the wound, from three weeks of hard travel compressed into a body that should have needed six weeks of rest.

"Bandits, the second week out. Or men dressed as bandits — they went straight for the case, never touched my purse. I ran. I'm not proud of it. I ran and they chased and one of them put a blade in me, and I got into the reeds, and I heard—" He stopped. Started again. "I heard her voice behind me. Shouting. Then not shouting. Then nothing." His eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, to a grey morning in the rushes. "I walked after that. Didn't eat much. Didn't sleep much. Every time I set the case down, the ground around it frosted, and I started to think — maybe that's her. Maybe the cold is her, telling me hurry. So I hurried."

The infirmary was very quiet. In the bottle-green light, the young courier looked suddenly very young, and very alone, and Mara felt the familiar sick lurch of recognizing a debt being created in real time — because that was what this was, wasn't it? A dying woman's trust, loaded onto a stranger's back. A dead woman's last instruction, and a living man who could no longer fulfill it except by proxy, because the guild had taken the case into its strongroom and taken the man into its bed, and both were now assets on someone's balance sheet.

*The last honest thing.* No — he hadn't said that yet. That would come later, or in another telling. But the phrase arrived in Mara's mind unbidden anyway, the way certain phrases did, the way certain dates did, circled twice.

"And the second entry?" Mara said. "What it *did*."

Xanthe hesitated. For a moment Mara thought he would refuse. Then the courier turned his head, checked the guard by the door with a glance, and leaned closer, and his voice dropped to almost nothing.

"On the fourth night, I dreamed the case was open. And inside there wasn't fire, like I expected — ember guild, ember cargo, I figured fire. Inside there was a winter. A whole winter, going on forever, and in the middle of it, everyone I have ever failed, standing in the snow, looking at me. Not angry. Just *looking*. Like witnesses." His breath came a little faster. "When I woke, the frost on the floorboards was in the shape of footprints. Leading away from the case. Toward me." He looked up, and for the first time since the gate, Mara saw genuine fear in the gold-flecked eyes — not fear of pain, not fear of the guild, but the older, colder fear of a man who suspects the universe is keeping accounts of its own. "Ledger man. Tell me honestly. What did she send me carrying?"

And Mara — fourteen years a scribe, expert in the difference between a thing's price and its value, a man who had that very morning signed away seven additional years of his life to sit at this bedside — opened his mouth to say *I don't know*, because it was true, because it was safe, because every instinct trained into him by this house said reveal nothing.

What came out instead was: "Before I answer that — did she say which book? Did she describe it? The binding, the lock, anything at all?"

Xanthe blinked. "Why?"

"Because," Mara said slowly, "if it's the book I think it is, then she didn't send you a cargo at all. She sent you a *message*. And I read part of it last night, and it mentions a date." He closed his notebook. "It mentions today."
