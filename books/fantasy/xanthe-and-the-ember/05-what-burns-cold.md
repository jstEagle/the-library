## 5. What Burns Cold

The case had been cold for eleven days.

Not the cold of river water or night air — Xanthe knew those colds, wore them like old friends. This was a cold that came from inside, that pressed outward through lead and leather and the double-wrapped oilcloth he'd bound around it in desperation somewhere past the second ford. It was the cold of a held breath. Of something waiting to be let out, or waiting to be kept in; after eleven days he could no longer tell which, and he had stopped trying, because the not-knowing had become its own kind of company.

He sat on a cot in the guild's infirmary with his boots off and his feet wrapped in wool someone had brought him without speaking, and the case sat on the floor between his ankles where he could feel it against his shins like a sleeping animal. The guild physician had wanted it gone from the room. *Instruments,* she'd said. *Cold interferes with the tonics.* He'd said no. She'd looked at him the way people look at a dog that has bitten once, and he'd said no again, and eventually she'd worked around him, tending the gash along his ribs with the case breathing its strange winter against his leg.

They had taken the case from him at the gate. That was the part that still sat wrong in his chest, worse than the gash, worse than the fever. Guildmaster Vess herself had come down into the yard with her gray cloak beaded with rain, and two enforcers had lifted the case off his shoulder with the reverence of men handling a relic, and she had said, *You did well, courier. Rest now. It's safe.*

Safe. As if safety were a place you could put something down and walk away from.

He'd made it three steps toward the infirmary door before he turned around and said, loud enough for the whole rain-hammered yard: "I want it back when I've slept."

The yard had gone quiet except for the rain. Vess had studied him for a long moment — not angry, he'd remember that later, not angry at all, but *measuring*, the way she might measure a ledger entry that didn't balance — and then she'd smiled and said, "Of course," in a voice that meant nothing of the kind.

So now there was a scribe.

* * *

The scribe arrived at dusk, when the infirmary lamps were lit and the rain had softened to a whisper against the shutters. Xanthe heard him before he saw him — the particular careful step of a man who had learned to move through rooms as if apologizing for occupying them. Then the door opened and a narrow figure stood there holding a slate board and an ink-stained cloth, looking at Xanthe the way accountants look at things they intend to count.

"You're the handler?" Xanthe said.

"I'm Mara." The man set the slate down, pulled a stool to the foot of the cot with the economy of someone who had done it a thousand times, and sat. Up close he was younger than his stoop suggested, with ink worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that it looked less like dirt than like grain in wood. His eyes were tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. "I keep the courier ledgers. I'll be keeping yours."

"I don't have a ledger."

"Everyone has a ledger." He said it without malice, the way you'd say *the sun rises*. "Yours just got longer. The guild extends your debt term by one year for every day the cargo remains undelivered, and by five years if the cargo is lost, damaged, or surrendered to any party other than the guildmaster. Those are the terms you signed at intake." He glanced up. "You did read what you signed?"

Xanthe almost laughed. "I read that I'd be paid. I read that the run was marsh-to-city, sealed case, no questions. The rest was small print and I was hungry."

Something moved behind Mara's eyes — not pity exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing his own story told badly by a stranger. "Then you should know the terms are real," he said quietly. "And you should know that I'm the one who writes them down. If you run, I'm the one who records it. If you die, I'm the one who closes your line. So it's better for both of us if you don't do anything interesting."

"And if I ask questions?"

Mara's pen paused over the slate. "Questions are free," he said. "Answers cost more."

"Then here's mine." Xanthe leaned forward, and the case shifted against his ankle, and even that small movement sent its cold up through the bone of him like a struck bell. "Why does the guild want me watched? I delivered. I'm half-dead, I'm feverish, I couldn't steal my own boots. What is in that case that makes a counting house post guards outside an infirmary?"

Mara didn't answer at once. He wrote something instead — Xanthe caught only the shape of it, a few quick strokes — and then he looked up, and whatever he'd been about to say as a handler, he set aside, because what he asked instead was a person's question, not a guild's:

"What did they give you? When you took the run. Did anyone tell you what you were carrying?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"There wasn't time. There was a woman dying in the marshes and a case going cold and a boat leaving on the tide." Xanthe heard his own voice go flat, the way it went flat when memory pressed too hard. "You want the story? Fine. But I'm telling it once, and then you're writing it down exactly, because I've decided if anyone in this city is going to know what happened out there, it's going to be the man whose whole life is writing things down."

Mara dipped his pen. "Go on."

* * *

"It was the ninth of the Drowning Month," Xanthe said. "Or near enough. The marshes south of Vell don't keep good calendars. Everything out there rots on its own schedule — reeds, boats, promises. I'd taken a short run up from the coast, salt and letters, nothing worth remembering, and I was cutting across the fen road because the long way added two days and two days is food I didn't have.

"She was lying in the shallows under a lean-to of black reeds. Old woman. Marsh-born — you can tell by the way they build, low and crooked, everything angled against the wind. She'd built her fire up on a peat shelf above the waterline, and she was propped against a bundle of sacking with her hands in her lap like a lady at prayer, except her hands were wrong. Burned. Not fresh-burned. Old burns, healed bad, the fingers curled like fern heads. Like she'd held something hot for a very long time and never learned to put it down.

"I thought: bandits, or a forge accident, or marsh-fever. I thought: none of my business. I was already past her when she spoke.

"'Courier,' she said. Not a question. And that stopped me, because I wasn't dressed as a courier — I dress plain on the fen roads, no guild colors, colors get you robbed. But she said it like she'd been waiting for the word all day, the way you wait for rain.

"She said, 'You'll carry it or no one will.'

"The case was beside her under the sacking. Lead-bound, banded in iron, wrapped in oilcloth gone green with damp. When I lifted it I nearly dropped it — not from the weight. The weight was nothing. Forty pounds, maybe less. But it was *cold*, cold like a well in high summer, cold like the underside of a stone that hasn't seen sun in a hundred years, and the cold moved. You know how ice has edges? This didn't. The cold breathed. In and out, slow, patient. Like the case was asleep and dreaming of winter.

"I told her I couldn't take it. No manifest, no seal of transfer, no payment. That's how I was raised in this trade — paper first, cargo second. Paper keeps couriers alive.

"She laughed. It turned into coughing, and the coughing went on so long I crouched down and held water to her mouth, and when she could speak again she said, 'Paper. They'll have paper for it too, boy. Paper's how they lost it.' And then she said the thing I've been turning over for eleven days like a stone in the shoe.

"She put her burned hand flat on the case — flat, deliberate, the way you'd quiet a horse — and she said, 'This is the last honest thing left in the world. Everything else has been counted. This hasn't. Don't let them count it.'

"Then she told me the delivery. Not a name, not an address. She said: 'Take it to the Ember Guild. To the counting house. Give it to whoever balances the books, and tell them the debt comes due tonight.' And she gave me this—" he touched his collarbone, where a cord hung under his shirt, and drew out a token: a disc of dark metal, worn smooth, stamped with a mark like a flame folded closed, petals of fire bent inward around an empty center. "She pressed it into my hand and said, 'Show them the seal. They'll know. They've known for a hundred years.'"

The lamp had burned lower while he talked. Somewhere down the ward another patient coughed in their sleep. Xanthe realized his own hands had gone cold — not from the case now, but from the telling, from hauling the memory up out of the marsh water where he'd tried to sink it.

"She died maybe an hour after I took it. Quietly, between one breath and the next, like she'd only stayed alive long enough to hand it off. I buried her under her own fire-shelf and stacked the stones the way marsh-folk do, and I took the boat, and I ran the rivers day and night because the case got colder every mile I carried it toward the city, and I started to understand that it wasn't cooling with distance. It was cooling with *nearness*. Like it knew where it was going. Like it was glad."

He stopped. Looked at the scribe, who had written nothing for some time.

"You're not taking this down."

Mara's pen hovered above the slate, unmoving. He was staring at the token in Xanthe's palm — at the mark stamped into the dark metal — and his face had done something complicated, the way a ledger does when a column refuses to sum. Wonder, Xanthe would think later. And underneath the wonder, fear, the way a cellar sits underneath a house.

"That mark," Mara said. His voice had gone careful, the voice of a man testing ice. "Folded flame. Closed around an empty center."

"That's what she called it. The closed fire."

"I've seen it." Mara set the pen down with a small, precise click, as though even that sound were too loud. "Two days ago. In the founder's archive — the sealed collection, the ledgers no one audits but me. There's a book down there, a hundred years old, and this mark is pressed into its cover in wax that never hardened. Wax that's still soft. A century, and it's still soft, like it was sealed yesterday." He rubbed his ink-stained thumb against his forefinger, unconsciously, the gesture of a man feeling for residue. "And Xanthe — the date inside that book. The date the prophecy names for the debt coming due. It's circled twice. It's *tonight*."

The case, between Xanthe's ankles, exhaled its cold.

He felt it pass over his skin like a draft from a door opening somewhere far below — felt the breath of it, slow and patient, and for the first time since the marshes he understood that the cold had never been the case's at all. It was what the case kept out. Or kept in. Something on the other side of the lead had noticed them noticing, and it was listening.

Outside, in the corridor, boots rang on stone — more boots than an infirmary needed, moving with purpose. Mara rose, slate clutched to his chest like a shield, and for a moment neither of them breathed.

"They're early," Mara whispered. "Or we're late. With prophecy, I honestly don't know which is worse."

The door handle turned.
