## 6. The Inquisitor's Summons

The door opened on gray wool and candlelight, and Mara understood in a single heartbeat that the world had shifted its weight onto a different foot.

Three figures stood in the corridor of the infirmary wing where no visitors were permitted after dusk. The first was a temple inquisitor — Mara knew the cut of the vestments even before he saw the sigil: a circle bisected by a single vertical line, the Mark of the Unblinking Flame, embroidered in thread so fine it seemed to smolder. The second was a clerk of some kind, thin as a ledger spine, carrying an armload of blank folios and a traveling inkwell chained to his belt like a prisoner. The third was guild security — Hesk, broad and apologetic, wearing the expression of a man who had tried to stop a tide with his hands.

"Master Oyelaran." The inquisitor's voice was pleasant. That was the worst part of it. Inquisitors in the street ballads were always thunderous, all brimstone and accusation, but the real ones — the dangerous ones — spoke the way a moneylender spoke when he already knew your accounts were rotten. "Forgive the hour. I am Inquisitor Sera Vane, of the Third Circle. And you are exactly the man we hoped to find."

Mara's mind performed its oldest trick: it began counting. Boots in the corridor behind them — four more, at least. Lanterns lit where lanterns should not be lit. The infirmary door at his back, and behind it Xanthe, half-conscious, and the case, and the cold.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Mara said, and was proud of how level his voice came out. "I'm only the scribe."

"Yes," said Inquisitor Vane, smiling. "Only."

* * *

They walked him ahead of them through the counting house like a guide through a cathedral, which was, Mara supposed bitterly, what it was. The Ember Guild's halls at night had a different architecture than by day. By day the building was all motion and arithmetic — couriers stamping out with sealed cases, clerks shouting figures across balconies, the great abacus wheels turning in the central rotunda with a sound like distant grinding teeth. By night it became what it actually was: a vault. Every corridor narrowed toward the center. Every lamp burned behind iron grating. Even the air tasted of tallow and old paper and the faint mineral sweetness of ember-ash, the smell of debts being kept warm.

Vane took it all in with the unhurried attention of someone memorizing floor plans. Her clerk walked behind her, scratching notes without looking at the page, which meant either an excellent memory or a shorthand of heresy.

"The guild has been most forthcoming," she said, as they crossed the rotunda. Above them, the abacus wheels hung silent, their beads frozen mid-calculation. "Guildmaster Vess received our letter of inspection three weeks ago, of course. But events appear to have accelerated matters. A courier arrived tonight through the back gate. Carrying something."

"A courier arrives most nights," Mara said.

"Not most nights bleeding from three wounds and refusing to surrender his case." She stopped beneath the great seal of the guild set into the floor — the founder's mark, the flame inside the open book — and looked down at it as though reading it. "You attended him. You've been with him for two hours. Your name is on the infirmary roster, Master Oyelaran, along with the notation *handler assigned*. So the guild has already decided you are the man who touches this. Good. That simplifies things."

"Simplifies what?"

She turned to him then, and in the lamplight her eyes were the pale amber of resin, and very still. "The temple requires access to the Ember Guild's ledgers. All of them. As is our ancient right under the Concordance — but you know this; you're a scribe, you'll have read the founding charters more carefully than anyone alive." She let that sit between them like a coin placed on a table. "Including," she said, "the sealed archive. Including the founder's own books. We arrive tomorrow at midmorning. Everything will be laid open for audit. Everything."

Mara felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs, cold and precise, like a needle slipped between bones.

The sealed archive. The Ledger of Impossible Things. Tonight's date circled twice in ink a century old.

"There are ledgers down there," he heard himself say, "that no one has opened since the founder died. Their seals are wax from her own signet. Breaking them—"

"—is exactly what the Concordance authorizes us to do." Vane smiled again, gently, the way one smiles at a child explaining why the stove is hot. "Unless the guild has something to hide from the god it claims to serve."

Behind her eyes, something moved that was not piety. Mara had spent eleven years learning to read faces the way he read columns of figures — looking for the digit that didn't sum, the entry that didn't balance — and what moved behind Inquisitor Vane's composure was hunger. Not for truth. For possession.

"I'll inform the guildmaster," he said.

"Do," she agreed. "In fact—" and here her voice lightened, almost playfully, which frightened him more than anything else she'd said, "—we'll wait while you do. It's late, but I find honesty keeps poor hours anyway."

* * *

Guildmaster Vess received them in her office above the rotunda, and Mara watched her the way he imagined a gambler watches a rival's hands.

She was not what strangers expected. Small, gray-streaked, with ink stains permanently mapped across her knuckles and spectacles pushed up into her hair like a forgotten crown. She had been a courier herself once — everyone knew the story, the run through the Ashfall Pass, the case carried forty miles with a broken ankle — and she still had the courier's economy of movement, nothing wasted, everything aimed. When the inquisitors entered she did not rise. She finished writing the line she was writing, blotted it, capped her pen, and *then* looked up.

"Inquisitor Vane. The temple honors us."

"The temple instructs us," Vane corrected pleasantly. "We honor whom we choose."

"Of course." Vess folded her hands. "Your letter mentioned an audit for the spring equinox. It is barely autumn."

"Events accelerate. A courier arrived tonight carrying a lead-sealed case older than any contract currently active in your house. The temple would very much like to see it. And while we are here, we will exercise our right of full inspection — every ledger, every archive, including the sealed ones. You'll have until midmorning tomorrow to prepare the records for presentation."

For a moment — a moment so brief Mara might have invented it later — Vess's gaze flicked to him. Not a plea. An instruction. He had seen that look before, from creditors assessing collateral.

"Full inspection," Vess repeated slowly. "You understand what you're asking. Some of those books contain the private debts of families who've served this guild for generations. Debts of memory. Years of life. Favors whose terms would shame their holders if read aloud by strangers."

"That," said Vane softly, "is rather the point."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them the abacus wheels creaked, settling, like the building itself holding its breath.

"You may inspect," Vess said at last. "Tomorrow, midmorning. My clerks will prepare the ledgers tonight."

"They needn't trouble themselves," Vane said. "My clerk will do the preparation. We prefer the originals untouched."

Another flicker of the eyes to Mara. This time he read it clearly, and it dropped through him like ice water finding a crack: *they can't touch the originals.*

"Of course," Vess said. "Then we are finished?"

"We are finished — for now." Vane rose, gathered her clerk with a tilt of her head, and paused at the door. "One more thing, Guildmaster. The courier. Xanthe. He'll remain in the city? Available for questioning?"

"He's under guild protection and guild authority," Vess said evenly. "He goes nowhere."

"Mm," said Vane, in the tone of a woman writing a note she did not intend to share. "Goodnight, Guildmaster. Master Oyelaran." Her resin-pale eyes found him once more in the doorway. "A scribe who reads carefully. What a rare treasure that must be. Do take care of him, Guildmaster. Carefully read men are so often... useful. To everyone."

Then they were gone, boots ringing away down the stone, and the door closed, and Vess sat without moving for the length of ten heartbeats.

* * *

"Sit down, Mara."

He sat. His legs made the decision without consulting him.

"You were in the archive tonight," Vess said. It wasn't a question. "Auditing the sealed collection. Tell me why."

"The quarterly reconciliation flagged a discrepancy in interest accrued against pre-war contracts. The formulas reference founder-era conventions I couldn't verify from the working copies, so I—"

"Mara." She removed her spectacles from her hair and put them on, and behind the lenses her eyes were tired in a way he had never been permitted to see before. "I have known you since you were nineteen years old and too honest to lie about a miscounted column. Don't give me the recitation. Why were you in the archive tonight?"

Because of the date, he did not say. Because tonight's date is written in a hundred-year-old book, circled twice, and a courier walked through the gate six hours later carrying something that burns cold, and I don't believe in coincidence because I've spent my life learning that nothing balances by accident.

"I was following an anomaly," he said.

Something passed over her face — not surprise; recognition, which was worse.

"And did you find it?"

"The books are consistent," he lied. The first deliberate falsehood he had ever spoken to Vess, and it came out smooth as fresh ink, and he hated himself for how easily it came. "Within rounding."

Vess studied him for a long time. Then she nodded, slowly, as if confirming a figure she'd already computed.

"Here is what happens now," she said. "At midmorning, the inquisitors walk into the sealed archive, and whatever they find there, they will use. Understand — the temple does not audit. The temple *harvests*. Every discrepancy becomes leverage. Every irregularity becomes a chain. They will find something, Mara. They always find something. The question is only what we choose to let them find."

His mouth had gone dry. "And what do we choose?"

She drew a folio across the desk toward her — a working copy, blank, good vellum — and set it beside the inkwell, and looked at him over the spectacles with an expression that was almost gentle, which was somehow the cruelest thing she could have done.

"You choose it," she said. "Tonight. You are going to make a clean copy of the sealed archive. Every page. Every entry. Corrected. Consistent. Within rounding. Whatever anomalies exist — dates, annotations, marginalia, anything that doesn't belong in a guild's honest history — will not exist by morning. You will write the archive the way it should have been kept, and at midmorning the inquisitors will audit *your* book, and the originals will stay sealed where they belong."

The room tilted slightly. Mara gripped the arms of the chair.

"You're asking me to falsify the founder's records," he said. "To commit forgery against the guild itself. If the temple compares—"

"They won't compare. The seals break once, in front of witnesses, and what's inside is what's inside." She leaned forward. "And if they *did* compare, Mara — if anyone ever held your copy beside the truth and saw the difference — who do you imagine swings for it? The Guildmaster? Or the scribe whose hand is on every page?"

There it was. Laid out flat between them like a knife on white linen. Complicity, itemized: *your hand, your ink, your gallows.*

"Why me?" he asked quietly. "You have forgers. Every counting house does."

"Because you're the best hand in this building," Vess said. "And because you were already in the archive tonight, which means whatever anomaly exists, you've already seen it — which means you already know exactly what needs correcting. And because—" she paused, and for one unguarded instant she looked less like a guildmaster than like a woman standing in a rising flood, "—because you owe this guild eleven years of your life, Mara, and I am the one who decides whether it becomes twelve, or twenty, or the rest of you. I have never once used that. Hear me: I am using it now. Not because I want to. Because the alternative is letting the temple open books that end this guild tomorrow, and every courier in this house ends with it."

He thought of Xanthe, upstairs, fevered and stubborn, refusing to let go of a case he didn't understand. He thought of the date circled twice. He thought of his brother's face at the debtor's gate, four years ago, and how debt was never just numbers — it was a rope, and someone always held the other end.

"And if I refuse?" His voice sounded far away to himself.

Vess didn't answer immediately. She picked up her pen, uncapped it, and wrote a single line in the margin of the folio — a handler's authorization, his name, effective immediately, extended terms pending completion of duties — and turned it so he could read it upside down.

"Then your debt accrues at the penalty rate," she said, not unkindly. "Effective midnight. You'd be eighty before you walked out of here. If they let scribes walk out at all, these days."

Outside, the rain had begun again, ticking against the high windows like a slow abacus counting down the hours until midmorning.

* * *

The sealed archive at night was a country of the dead.

Mara descended the iron stair with a lamp in one hand and a satchel of fresh vellum in the other, and the dark closed behind him with the particular silence of places where paper sleeps. Shelf upon shelf upon shelf, descending into the guild's foundations — every debt ever contracted, every memory ever pawned, every year of life ever borrowed against tomorrow, all of it filed and boxed and dusted by men like him. Eleven thousand contracts. Forty thousand subsidiary entries. A civilization of owing.

And in the innermost alcove, behind the founder's broken wax seals, the books no honest eye was meant to see.

He set his lamp on the reading table. He sharpened his pen. He opened the clean folio to its first page, and dipped the nib, and sat there for a long moment watching the ink gather at its tip like a small black heart preparing to beat.

*Corrected,* Vess had said. *Consistent. Within rounding.*

But correction required knowing what was wrong. And so, before he wrote a single false line, Mara opened the original — the Ledger of Impossible Things, the prophecy ledger, the founder's own hand — and read it through once more, cover to cover, the way an auditor prepares a fraud: by learning the truth perfectly enough to destroy it convincingly.

Page by page, the lamplight crawled. The founder's script was beautiful, upright, disciplined — a hand that had never trembled in its life, or never admitted to it. Prophecies marched across the pages in her flawless cadence. Dates. Names. The coming of a bearer. The choice between temple and fire. All of it flowing with the terrible confidence of ink that believed it was fate.

Mara copied it all. Faithfully. Line for line, flaw for flaw — except the flaws.

Where the original recorded a debt the guild had quietly repaid itself, he wrote *paid in full.* Where a courier's death was logged as accident, he wrote accident with cleaner grammar. Where the margins whispered in a second hand — corrections, doubts, a single furious annotation that read *she promised it BACK, she PROMISED* — he left clean vellum, smooth and innocent as snowfall.

And when he reached the date — tonight's date, circled twice in century-old ink — his pen hovered above the page for the space of ten breaths, dripping.

If he erased it, and the temple ever saw the original, the erasure itself would be the confession. If he preserved it, the inquisitors would read it aloud in the rotunda and ask the guild, pleasantly, *and what happened today?*

In the end he copied it. Circles and all. Because a forged book that hid nothing looked honest, and because some part of him — the part that balanced impossible columns at three in the morning — wanted the date witnessed. Wanted it *on record*, in a hand that could swear to it, in case the world needed proof afterward that it had all been written down long before any of them were born.

The lamp burned low. His wrist ached. Outside, the rain kept its patient count.

It was nearly dawn when he finished the last page, blotted the final line, and closed the clean copy — the archive as it should have been, a guild without secrets, a founder without sins, a history with all its teeth pulled.

And that was when he saw it.

There, in the margin of the original's final leaf, pressed so lightly he had missed it in every previous reading: a second date, in the founder's flawless hand, smaller than the rest. Not tonight's date at all.

A date thirty years from now.

And beneath it, in letters fine as hair: *if the bearer fails, write the next one.*

Mara sat alone in the dark of the archive, his forgery complete and drying at his elbow, and understood that he had just spent the whole night copying a book that expected to be copied — that had perhaps always expected it — and that somewhere in its pages, waiting in ink a century old, the next date was already circling itself around a name that had not yet been born.

His lamp guttered. Above him, muffled through stone and shelf and forty thousand debts, the guild began to wake — and boots were moving on the iron stair, coming down, though midmorning was still hours away.
