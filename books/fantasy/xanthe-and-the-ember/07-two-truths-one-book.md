## 7. Two Truths, One Book

The counting house at night was a different animal than the counting house by day.

By day it breathed — the rustle of ledgers, the click of abacus beads, the cough of old men hunched over columns of figures that represented other people's lives in increments of years and favors and memories. By day there were voices, footsteps on the stair, the bell that rang when someone came to pay down what they owed. But after the guildmaster's lamp went dark behind her door, after the last clerk shuffled out into the fog with his collar turned up against the cold, the building settled into itself like an old man into a chair, and what remained was the sound of paper. Paper settling in stacks. Paper ticking as it dried. Paper, always, waiting to be written upon.

Mara sat alone at the long table beneath the high window where the moon came through the frost-furred glass in a single pale blade, and he forged.

He had been forging since the ninth hour. His ink horn was half empty, his wrist ached in a way that would still be aching tomorrow, and before him lay two books: the original archive ledger, its leather gone soft and dark as old blood, and beside it his copy, written on fresh stock from the guild's own stores so that even the paper's watermark would pass inspection. The temple wanted clean ledgers. The guildmaster wanted them by morning. And Mara Oyelaran, who had once believed that a scribe's whole virtue was fidelity — that the hand which recorded was sacred precisely because it did not invent — was giving the inquisitors exactly the history they wished to find.

There was a craft to it. He had not known that until tonight; no one taught forgery in the apprentice halls, and yet every rule of honest transcription turned inside out became a rule of dishonest transcription done well. Do not improve the hand. Let the errors breathe. A too-perfect ledger is a confession in itself. He varied his pressure where the original varied. He let one entry sit slightly crooked, because the original sat crooked. He blotted a line where the original had blotted, smeared where it had smeared, and twice he stopped himself from correcting an arithmetic mistake made a century ago by some clerk whose name was lost but whose error now had to be preserved, faithfully, like a fossil.

*You are good at this,* said a voice in his head that might have been his own and might have belonged to the founder whose seal he had broken three nights ago. *Isn't that the worst part?*

It was. It was precisely the worst part. Because the work came easy, and ease was a kind of appetite, and he could feel the appetite growing with every page — the small hot pleasure of making the world say something it had not said. His brother had worn that look once, Mara remembered. Kess, hunched over a dice cup in the alley behind their mother's house, learning the weighted throw, his face lit with the particular joy of a boy discovering that reality could be bent if your fingers were quick enough. Two years later he'd bent it once too often, and the debt collectors had come, and Mara had stood in the doorway of their kitchen with the ledger open between them and chosen — 

No. Not tonight. Tonight there was only the work.

He dipped his pen. The moon-blade had crawled across the table and now lay across the original ledger's open page, illuminating the prophecy in the founder's own crabbed hand — the passage the inquisitors would demand to see, the passage about the ember and the bearer and the date circled twice in ink the color of dried rust. *When the sealed fire returns to the city,* she had written, *the bearer shall stand at the counting house door, and the choice shall be put to him—*

Mara copied the words onto his fresh page. Then he copied them again.

And stopped.

His pen hung above the paper, dripping a slow bead of ink that he caught, just barely, on the blotting cloth. He looked at the line he had just written. He looked at the line above it. He read the original again, slowly, the way he read anything that mattered — aloud, under his breath, letting the syllables catch on each other.

*...and the bearer shall stand at the counting house door.*

But two lines earlier, in the same passage, same hand, the founder had written: *...and the bearer shall come before the temple altar, and the priests shall judge him.*

Both sentences described the same moment. Both could not be true.

He set down the pen very carefully, as if it might go off.

* * *

For a long while he simply sat in the cold and let the discrepancy sit with him, the way you let a stranger sit in your kitchen at an hour when strangers should be asleep.

There were innocent explanations. Of course there were. Scribes corrected themselves; authors revised; a hundred years was time enough for a writer to change her mind about where her imagined bearer would stand. Perhaps she had crossed one version out — but she hadn't. Neither line was struck through. Neither bore the marginal mark, the little looped *c* for *corrigitur*, that guild practice required whenever a scribe amended a text. Both stood in clean confidence, side by side, each claiming to be the future.

Unless they weren't claiming to be the future at all. Unless one of them was claiming to be the past — a past that had been edited.

Mara pulled his copy toward him and studied it with new eyes. When he had begun, hours ago, he had worked mechanically, eye moving from original to page, word to word, a machine for duplication. Now he read for sense, and reading for sense, he began to see what mechanical copying had hidden. The prophecy's early passages — the ones describing the guild's founding, the founder's covenant, the flame entrusted to her keeping — flowed in a single unbroken hand, the ink consistent, the rhythm confident. But midway through, the texture changed. Not the handwriting; she was too careful for that. The *diction* changed. Sentences grew shorter where they had been long. Words appeared that did not sound like her — he had spent three nights in her archive, he knew her taste for hard verbs and bitter qualifiers — words like *ordained* and *sanctified*, soft ceremonial words, the vocabulary of institutions explaining why they deserve to exist.

And here was the thing that made his hands go cold on the pen: those softer passages were the ones that matched the guild's public doctrine. The catechism every apprentice memorized. The verses painted on the counting house ceiling in gold leaf, that debt was holy, that the ember's keeping was a trust from heaven, that the Ledger of Prophecy was scripture. He had learned those verses at eleven years old, standing on cold flagstones with the other apprentices, and he had never once thought to check them against the archive, because the archive was sealed and seals existed, presumably, to protect fragile truth from clumsy hands.

He compared verse to verse now, line by line, his lips moving, his heart going harder and harder. In the archive original, the founding covenant ran four lines and ended on a strange, unresolved phrase: *...and what was carried out of the fire at Ashfall Road she kept, and did not say why.* In the public doctrine, in his own forged copy, in the gilded ceiling above his head every working day of his life, that line had become: *...and what was carried out of the fire at Ashfall Road she kept, as the god commanded, in holiness.*

Someone had added *as the god commanded.* Someone had added *in holiness.* Someone had taken a sentence that admitted ignorance — *did not say why* — and turned it into a sentence that claimed divine authority.

The moon had moved off the table. The candle had burned down to a stub drowning in its own wax. Mara sat in the little island of light his lamp made and understood, with the slow terrible clarity that comes only in the smallest hours, that he was not looking at a scribal error or an author's revision. He was looking at a forgery older than his grandfather — a forgery so successful that it had become the truth everyone else was measured against. Whoever had rewritten these passages had not altered history to match events. They had altered history to manufacture events. To make a date inevitable. To make a courier's arrival into a fulfillment. To make a choice that should belong to a living man into a line item already entered in a book.

And he, Mara Oyelaran, was at this moment performing the same office for the temple that some dead hand had once performed for the guild — producing a clean copy, smoothing the record, deciding by omission what tomorrow's people would be allowed to know.

He looked down at his beautiful forgery. Three hundred pages of faithful lies, each one rendered with the care of a craftsman. It was, he thought, the most honest dishonest work ever done, because it lied only about things that had already been lied about, and it lied in service of keeping the inquisitors from burning the archive wholesale. That was what he told himself. That was the story. Every forger has a story.

The floorboard at the top of the stair creaked.

Mara closed both ledgers in the same motion, one hand apiece, and put his elbows on them, and looked up with the mild bored face of a man working late, which was even true.

Xanthe came down into the lamplight barefoot, which explained the silence of everything except that one telling board. He wore the guild's borrowed blanket around his shoulders like a cloak and his hair was loose and damp and he moved the way he seemed to do everything — quietly, directly, without any evident awareness that large institutions preferred people to announce themselves.

"You're still awake," Xanthe said.

"The guildmaster wanted the copies by dawn."

"I know. I've been listening to your pen for an hour." He came to the table's edge and stood looking down at the closed books, at the ink horn, at Mara's face, with an attention that Mara found more dangerous than any inquisitor's. Couriers, Mara was learning, noticed everything; it was the whole trade. "You stopped," Xanthe said. "An hour ago, maybe more. The pen sounds different when it stops being copying and starts being thinking. I could hear it from the cot."

"Your hearing is wasted on the courier trade. You should audit."

"What did you find?"

The question came flat and fast, and Mara felt it land exactly where Xanthe had aimed it, which was between the ribs. He had known the boy for three days. Three days of assigned proximity, of walking him to meals, of answering his questions about guild procedure with careful half-truths while the guildmaster's shadow lengthened over both of them. Three days, and already Xanthe asked questions the way other people drew knives.

"Nothing," Mara said.

It came out smooth. That was the horror of it — it came out smooth, first try, no catch in the throat, no telltale pause. He heard himself lie with the same fluency he had used all evening on three hundred pages of falsified history, and some part of him stood back in appalled admiration, the way one admires a fire spreading through a beautiful dry field.

Xanthe watched him. The blanket shifted on his shoulders. Somewhere above them the house ticked and settled, and outside, far off, a temple bell rang the hour — the fourth, though it hardly mattered anymore; the hours had all become the same gray labor.

"You're lying," Xanthe said, without heat, almost with curiosity. "That's interesting. Everyone in this building lies. I thought you didn't. You have an honest face. Well — you *had* one."

"It's late," Mara said. "Go back to bed."

"They're going to give me to the temple. Aren't they. That's what the copies are for — you're cleaning the books so that when they hand me over, nothing in the record says I was ever promised anything else." He said it evenly, testing the weight of it, watching Mara the whole time. "You can tell me I'm wrong."

Mara looked at him — at the bruised hollows under his eyes, at the burn scar along his forearm where the lead case had seared him through his sleeve on the road, at the stubborn set of his jaw that reminded Mara, unbearably, of Kess standing in the kitchen doorway refusing to cry — and he opened his mouth to say *you're wrong,* which was the kind thing, the safe thing, the thing that kept the machinery running smoothly until morning.

"You're wrong," he said.

The second lie was easier than the first. That was how it worked, then. He filed the knowledge away with the rest of tonight's terrible inventory.

Xanthe nodded slowly, and Mara could not tell whether the boy believed him or had simply decided the truth wasn't recoverable from him tonight. "All right," Xanthe said. "Goodnight, scribe." At the foot of the stair he paused, one hand on the rail. "Whatever it is you found in those books — you should know the woman in the marshes told me something before she died. She said the thing I'm carrying is the last honest thing left. She was wrong about a lot of stuff. But she wasn't wrong about that, and I think whatever you're reading in there knows it too."

Then he was gone up the stairs, and the house was quiet, and Mara sat alone with two books and one truth.

He waited until he heard the cot above creak and settle. Then he opened the archive ledger again and read the disputed passage once more, and made his decision with the deliberation of a man signing his name to something.

In his forged copy — the clean book, the temple's book, the book that would survive while the original perhaps did not — he would transcribe the prophecy as the doctrine had it. *As the god commanded. In holiness. The bearer before the altar.* He would preserve the official lie, because preserving it was his only leverage, his only shield; a scribe who revealed that he had noticed the discrepancy was a scribe who became dangerous, and dangerous men got audited, and audited men got collected. No. The discrepancy would live in exactly one place: in his memory, folded small, next to his brother's face.

His first deliberate lie he had told to Xanthe's face. His second he was about to commit to paper, in his own hand, in ink he had ground himself.

*Someone rewrote history,* he thought, dipping the pen, *and I am keeping their revision safe so that no one will ever know there was anything to rewrite.*

The pen touched the page. The false words came out clean and sure, in his best archival hand, and Mara wrote on through the last dark hours before dawn, alone with the sound of paper, wondering — as the sky behind the frosted glass went the gray of old ash — whether he had become the first honest forger in the guild's history, or merely its latest.

He did not hear the third floorboard creak, up near the top of the stairs, where someone else had been listening far longer than an hour.
