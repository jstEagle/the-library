## 9. Night Market of Small Debts

The city had two skins, and by day you only ever saw the one that wanted to be seen.

Xanthe had learned the second skin in a dozen towns on a dozen routes — the way every place kept a shadow-market where the ledger of ordinary life was balanced in whispers. Ashfall was no different. It was only bigger, and colder, and its underbelly smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone instead of river mud.

He moved through it now with Mara Oyelaran one careful step behind him, the scribe's soft shoes whispering against cobbles that hadn't seen honest daylight in three generations. Overhead, the vaulted underside of the Guild Bridge arched away into darkness, its stones furred with moss and hung with lamps that burned low and orange, like coals someone was ashamed of.

"Keep your hood up," Xanthe said, not turning around. "And don't look at anyone longer than they look at you."

"I'm a counting-house scribe," Mara said behind him, voice thin with the particular dignity of a man who has just realized how little his dignity is worth down here. "I have never *been* anywhere. I have *audited* places. From a desk."

"Then audit this."

They came out from under the bridge into the market proper, and even Mara's breathing changed.

It wasn't large — maybe forty stalls strung along a sunken lane where an old canal had been roofed over and forgotten — but it was *alive* in a way the Ember Guild's marble halls would never understand. A woman sold years. Not metaphorically; she sat behind a scale with brass pans and a placard reading YEARS BOUGHT, YEARS SOLD, FAIR RATES, and her customers leaned over the pans whispering while she watched them the way a butcher watches a joint of meat. Two stalls down, a boy traded memories out of glass vials, each one glowing faintly with whatever color the remembering had been: a wedding in gold, a funeral in grey-blue, something violent and private in deep red that he'd priced high and hidden at the back.

Debt made flesh. The whole guild economy laid out raw, without the marble and the polite terminology. Xanthe watched Mara take it in — watched the scribe's face do something complicated, grief and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"You knew," Mara said quietly. "You knew this was here."

"I've delivered to places like it." Xanthe kept moving. "Half my runs end in rooms like this. You think the Ember Guild's couriers carry sealed cases to *temples* all day? Most of what moves through this city moves through lanes like this one. Debts, favors, years. Somebody has to carry them."

"And nobody asks what's inside?"

"Somebody always asks. That's not the same as being told."

He said it lightly. He did not feel light. The case was gone now — taken by Vess, locked in the guildmaster's strongroom, and every hour since he'd surrendered it, Xanthe had felt the absence like a pulled tooth. His tongue kept going to the gap. His hands kept curling around air shaped like a handle.

The dying woman in the marshes had put it in his arms and said *the last honest thing*, and he had promised — he had *promised*, kneeling in the reeds with her blood cooling through his sleeves — that he would not let it out of his sight.

Then he'd walked into the Ember Guild's back gate like a good little courier and let a woman with kind eyes and iron ledgers take it upstairs.

*Stubborn loyalty,* some cold internal auditor observed, *meeting institutional arithmetic.* He'd been on the wrong side of that equation before. He remembered exactly how it ended.

So tonight he was fixing it. And he needed the one person in the guild who could read the dead woman's name backwards through a century of records — which was why Mara Oyelaran was currently standing in a den of illegal memory-brokers looking like a lamb that had wandered into a wolf convention wearing a very nice coat.

***

The sister's stall was at the far end of the lane, past the years-seller, past a fortune-teller whose cards were actual debts written on scraps of parchment (Xanthe had once watched her lay out a farmer's future as a sequence of promissory notes, each one due to somebody crueler than the last).

The sign above the last stall read simply: RELICS, HONESTLY SOURCED. Beneath it, in smaller letters: *or at least honestly lied about.*

The woman behind it was broad-shouldered and grey-streaked, mending a lantern chain with hands that didn't stop working when she looked up. Her face did the thing faces did when they recognized a courier — a flicker of calculation, then wariness, then something older and more tired underneath both.

"We're closed," she said.

"We're not buying," Xanthe said. He set his palms flat on her table, the way you did when you wanted a trader to know you had nothing in your sleeves. "I'm looking for Odile Ferran. Courier, southern route. She'd have come through here—"

The mend-hook stopped moving.

For a heartbeat the whole market seemed to hold its breath around them — the memory-boy mid-pour, the years-seller's scale perfectly balanced, the tallow lamps bending their flames toward the silence.

"Odile," the woman repeated. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices went around open wounds. "Who's asking?"

"Xanthe. I carried for her. Three weeks ago, the marsh route, the crossing at Sallow Ford." He made himself say the rest plainly, because she deserved plainness and because anything else would curdle into pity. "She was hurt. Badly. Men on the road behind her. She gave me her case and told me to run, and I ran, and by the time—" His throat closed. He pushed through it. "By the time I got back with help, the reeds were burned and she was gone. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I've been carrying that apology for three weeks and it doesn't get lighter."

The woman set down the hook. She looked at Xanthe for a long moment, and then at Mara, who had the grace to look like a man attending a funeral rather than auditing one.

"I'm her sister," the woman said finally. "Rell. Odile wrote to me every month. Her last letter said she'd found something on the southern run. Something that scared her." She stood, slow, joints cracking like green wood. "Come around back. Not here. Here, everything is inventory, and I don't sell my family."

***

Behind the stall there was a room the size of a coffin, walled in salvaged ship-timber, lit by one lamp and crowded with things that had no price tags: chipped icons, a child's shoes, bundles of letters tied in courier's twine. Rell cleared a stool for Mara and another for Xanthe, then took the letters down from their peg — a thick bundle, decades of them, sorted by month with a precision that would have made the counting house proud.

"She joined the guild at fifteen," Rell said, untying the most recent bundle. "Same as half the fools in this city. Debt-bound, like everyone. But she was *good*, Xanthe. Fastest southern runner they had. Twenty years, and every year her contract got extended instead of paid off. You know how that works?"

Mara's voice came out small. "I know how that works."

"Of course you do." Rell glanced at him, and something in her expression softened by a degree. "A scribe. They sent a scribe with you? Or are you here on your own recognizance?"

"He's here because I dragged him," Xanthe said. "He reads old handwriting better than anyone in the guild, and I need someone who can tell me what Odile actually found. Because the guild took her case off me the moment I walked in the gate, and Master Vess smiled at me and called me brave, and I have been a courier long enough to know that when powerful people smile at you and call you brave, they are measuring you for a grave."

Rell barked a laugh — sudden, sharp, startled out of her — and for a second she looked so much like the woman in the reeds that Xanthe had to grip his own knee to keep steady.

"Odile used to say that exact thing," Rell said. "Word for word." She unfolded the last letter, smoothed it on her knee, and held it out to Mara. "Here. Read the middle part. She coded some of it — old courier cipher, route-talk — but she trusted paper more than people, and if she hid something in plain ink, it'll be in the margins."

Mara took the letter the way he took everything, Xanthe was learning — carefully, reverently, as though paper could feel pain. His eyes moved. His lips parted slightly, the way they did when he was reading fast and forgetting to breathe.

Then his finger stopped on the page and did not move again for a very long time.

"Mara?" Xanthe said.

"The margins." Mara's voice had gone strange — hollowed out, like a bell with the clapper removed. "There are dates in the margins. Route dates, delivery dates, and then..." He turned the letter so the lamplight caught it. "This line here, in cipher — but it's the old cipher, the pre-war one, they still teach it for historical audits. I can read it." He swallowed. "*It is not what they say it is. It never was. Ask the temple what they lost. Ask them why they never went looking.*"

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere out in the market, the years-seller's brass pans chimed against each other.

"What isn't what?" Xanthe asked, though the question was already forming its own answer in the pit of him, cold and inevitable, the way ice formed on a still pond — slowly, and then all at once.

"That's what I don't know yet," Mara said. "But Xanthe — the case you carried. The ember. Every record in the guild archive says the same story. That after the last great war, the temple surrendered the godflame to the Ember Guild for safekeeping. That the guild's magic — the debt-binding, the contracts, the whole architecture — runs on that flame, held in trust, a sacred stewardship. That's the founding myth. It's carved over the doors of the counting house in letters taller than a man."

"Yes," Xanthe said slowly. "That's the story."

"It's also," Mara said, with the terrible gentleness of a man delivering a diagnosis, "a story about the temple giving something away. And Odile's letter says to ask the temple what they *lost*."

Silence filled the coffin-room like water.

Rell broke it first, reaching into a crate beneath her stool. "If we're doing this, do it properly." She brought out a scrap of oilcloth, unwrapped it, and revealed a courier's token — lead, worn smooth, stamped with a seal mark Xanthe knew intimately, because he'd spent three weeks staring at it pressed into the case he'd carried across half a country.

"This came in Odile's last letter too," Rell said. "No note. Just the token, wrapped like something precious. She was telling me where to look if she didn't come home."

"Where?" Xanthe managed.

"The temple archive," Mara whispered. He'd gone pale in the lamplight, pale as fresh paper. "That's a temple archive seal. Pre-war issue. Which means Odile had access to temple records, or stole them, or—" He stopped. Started again. "Xanthe, if the seal on your case matches temple archival marks, then the ember wasn't surrendered to the guild. It wasn't entrusted. It was *taken*. The founding theft — the whole reason for the war — and the guild didn't end the war by making peace. They ended it by stealing the thing both sides were bleeding over, and then writing a hundred years of ledgers that said otherwise."

The floor of the world tilted. Xanthe gripped the edge of the stool and let it tilt, because fighting it would mean being sick.

He thought of the case burning cold against his ribs. Of the way the guildmaster's fingers had lingered on it — not with reverence, but with the proprietary ease of a woman handling something she considered hers. Of Vess saying *you've done your part, courier, rest now*, and meaning *disappear now*.

"They sent me," he said aloud, mostly to hear how it sounded. "They send couriers to collect it. Regular runs. Which means either they didn't know where it was for a hundred years, or—" His mind snagged, turned the thought over. "Or the runs aren't collections. They're *check-ins*. Like checking on a prisoner. Like feeding something."

"Or moving it," Mara said. "Hiding it somewhere new every few years. You don't leave stolen goods in one vault for a century, not when the rightful owner is still looking."

"The temple's still looking," Rell said grimly. "Temple inquisitors came through this market two months back. Buying, they said. Auditing, they said. They bought three memories off the boy with the vials and paid in temple script, which nobody down here takes twice." She folded her arms. "They were asking about southern-route couriers. About Odile."

The cold that went through Xanthe then had nothing to do with the ember.

"They killed her," he said. "The men on the road. Inquisitors, or temple-hired blades. She found proof of what they lost, and they—" He couldn't finish. His hands remembered the weight of her anyway, the way she'd shoved the case into his chest with the last strength she had, and the words *the last honest thing*, and how he'd thought she meant the case itself.

Maybe she had. Maybe the honesty wasn't in the object. Maybe it was in what the object proved.

"I have to go back," he heard himself say. "Tonight. Before Vess moves it, before—"

"No." Mara's hand landed on his forearm — the first time the scribe had touched him unprompted, and his grip was stronger than his frame suggested. "Listen to me. If you walk into the counting house tonight and demand the case, you'll be a disloyal courier asking questions about guild property, and there are rooms in this city where inconvenient couriers become entries in missing-persons ledgers. I've filed those ledgers, Xanthe. I've *balanced* them." His face was grey. "We do this the way the guild does everything. On paper first. I need to see the founder's original charter — the true one, not the public copy. If the theft happened, it's recorded somewhere, because a thief who writes everything down eventually writes down the theft. They always do. Pride. Or guilt. Or just the habit of bookkeeping."

"You want to audit a hundred-year-old crime," Xanthe said.

"I want to *reconcile* it," Mara said, and for the first time since Xanthe had met him, there was fire in the scribe's quiet — banked, controlled, but real, the way embers under ash are real. "Every entry has two sides. Debit and credit. If the guild took the flame, then somewhere in those books there is a credit to the guild and a debit to the temple, and if I find it—" He stopped himself. Looked down at his hands. "If I find it, nothing will ever balance again. Do you understand? Everything I am, everything this city is, sits on top of that lie. My debt. Your contract. The temple's authority. All of it."

Rell snorted. "Welcome to the night market, scribe. We deal in small debts down here. Yours is just bigger than most."

Outside, the tallow lamps guttered in a draft from the canal-grate, and somewhere above them, in the marble city, the Ember Guild slept soundly on its hundred-year lie, and the temple's inquisitors sharpened their questions, and somewhere between the two, in a strongroom behind a door with three locks, a lead-sealed case sat burning cold around the last honest thing in Ashfall.

Xanthe stood, and his knees held, and that surprised him.

"All right," he said. "Paper first. But Mara — if the books move faster than we do, if Vess ships that case out on tomorrow's southern run—"

"Then I'll falsify a routing order," Mara said. "I'm very good at it."

He said it like a confession. He said it like a vow.

Xanthe decided, watching the scribe fold Odile's letter with funeral care and tuck it inside his coat, over his heart, that he believed him — and that believing a man who admitted to forgery should have frightened him more than it did.

Above the canal-grate, far overhead, the first grey suggestion of dawn was already bleeding into the dark.

They had until morning. Maybe less.
