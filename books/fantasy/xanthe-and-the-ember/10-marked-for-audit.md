## 10. Marked for Audit

The inquisitors did not leave after the inspection. That was the first thing Mara noticed, and the first thing he pretended not to notice.

They had arrived three days ago in their ash-grey cloaks with the temple's sunburst stitched in thread so pale it looked like scar tissue. There were four of them — three functionaries who smelled of lamp oil and incense and did nothing but copy, and one who did not copy at all. The functionaries sat in the guild's reading hall from dawn until dusk, transcribing ledger entries into their own books with the patient industry of silkworms. But the fourth — Inquisitor Sera Duvane, though she never introduced herself, and Mara only learned her name by reading it off a wax seal she left on Vess's desk — walked.

She walked through the counting house the way water walks through a cracked foundation. Slowly. Inevitably. Finding every fissure.

Mara watched her from behind his desk on the first day, his pen moving over a column of figures he would later have to do twice, because he had written *seven* where the original said *nine* and could not remember which was the lie and which was the truth. She was tall without being imposing, grey-eyed without being cold, and she smiled at clerks when they brought her tea, and she asked questions that sounded like courtesies. *How long have you served the guild? What a beautiful hand you have. Do the debt-ledgers ever keep you past dark?*

By the second day, Mara understood that every question she asked was a key fitted quietly into a lock, and that she was not turning any of them yet. She was only learning which ones turned.

He should have stayed away from her. Every instinct honed by eleven years in the counting house told him to become furniture — to be a man made of ink-stained cuffs and bent spine whom the eye slid off like rain off oiled canvas. And for two days he managed it. He became a fixture. He balanced the third-quarter remittances. He ate his supper of cold rice and pickled radish at his desk. He slept four hours on the cot in the record room because the guildmaster's orders still stood: *the archive is closed, scribe; what the temple sees is what we give them.*

But on the third night, near midnight, when the reading hall was empty and the lamps burned low, Inquisitor Duvane came down into the counting house proper, alone, and stopped at the foot of the stairs that led to the sealed archive, and looked directly at Mara as though she had known exactly where he would be sitting.

"Scribe Oyelaran," she said. "You're working late."

"Year-end," Mara said. It wasn't even year-end.

"Mm." She descended the last steps and crossed the floor, her boots making no sound on the stone, which was wrong, because everyone's boots made sound on that stone, Mara had spent eleven years listening to boots on that stone, and hers made none, as if the building itself had decided not to hear her coming. "I've been reading your founder's correspondence."

"There's no correspondence," Mara said. "The archive contains ledgers only."

"Of course." She smiled. It was a good smile. It reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "I've been reading the ledgers your founder kept, then. My mistake."

She set something on his desk. A book. Not one of theirs — the binding was temple-grey, the pages rag-paper, and when Mara looked down at it he saw his own handwriting reproduced there in careful facsimile, entry after entry, including the forged remittance columns he had prepared for the inquisitors' inspection, copied flawlessly, annotated in a small precise script in the margins: *altered. see original. see original. altered.*

His mouth went dry. "Those are—"

"Yours," she said. "Yes. Very fine work. You have a gift, Scribe Oyelaran. Most men who forge numbers make them too clean. Numbers that have lived honest lives have dirt under their fingernails. Yours do." She tapped the facsimile of his forged column. "Except here. These figures are too tidy. Whoever balances the guild's public face doesn't understand that money is ugly." Her grey eyes lifted. "You clearly understand it. So why is this page pretty?"

Mara's hands were flat on the desk. He was distantly amazed that they weren't shaking. Eleven years of sitting across from debtors who lied about dead mothers and sold years of their own lives had taught him to keep his face like a closed ledger — but Duvane wasn't asking him about debts. She was asking him about himself, and he found he had no training for that.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Not yet." She drew out the chair opposite his and sat, uninvited, arranging her grey cloak around her with the care of a woman settling in for a long story. "Let me tell you what I mean instead, since you're busy. I mean that I have spent nine days in this building — no. Four days. Forgive me. Time moves strangely in counting houses." She folded her hands. "In four days I have read six hundred ledgers, and I have learned the following. The Ember Guild buys memories. It buys favors. It buys years of human life, weighed against coin, and it holds those years in vaults beneath this floor like grain against famine. It calls this lending. Its contracts are iron. Its interest is ruinous. Its founder is venerated in this city as a saint of honest dealing." She paused. "And its ledgers are full of ghosts."

"Ghosts," Mara repeated.

"Entries with no debtor. Payments with no payer. A hundred years of transactions that balance beautifully and refer to nothing." She leaned forward slightly. "Do you know what I call a ledger full of ghosts, Scribe?"

"A forgery," Mara said.

"A confession," said Duvane. "A very long, very careful confession, written by someone who couldn't stop recording the crime even while burying it." Her voice didn't change at all. It stayed level and warm and reasonable, which was how Mara knew he was in more danger than any enforcer's knife could have put him in. "And I think you've been reading the same confession I have. I think you found something in the sealed archive. Something with tonight's date written in it. And I think that's why your founder's public ledgers suddenly needed a prettier face."

The lamp between them guttered. Somewhere above, in the reading hall, one of the copying functionaries dropped a pen, and the sound was enormous in the silence.

"The archive is closed," Mara said.

"Yes," Duvane said. "To me. Not to you. The guildmaster gave you the keys eleven years ago, the week your brother went down into debtor's holding, because a man with a brother underground will do anything for extended terms. Am I wrong?"

Mara said nothing. His heartbeat sounded loud enough to audit.

"You're not wrong often, are you?" Duvane continued softly. "That's the trouble with people like us. We're right so consistently that we forget rightness is a currency too, and someone always wants to buy it." She reached into her cloak and produced a second object, setting it beside the facsimile-book: a single sheet of temple paper, blank except for a seal of white wax at the bottom, unbroken, and a line of text at the top in formal ecclesiastical script.

Mara read it. Then he read it again, because the words rearranged themselves each time, refusing to be believed.

*Full and unconditional immunity of person, debt, and record, granted to the bearer, in consideration of testimony given freely before the Holy Tribunal concerning the practices of the Ember Guild.*

"It's countersigned already," Duvane said. "By the Provost of Inquiry herself. All it needs is your name." She slid a pen across the desk — his own pen, lifted from his own inkwell, he hadn't even seen her do it — and waited.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then nothing happens tonight." She shrugged, a small economical motion. "The guild falls or it doesn't; that was decided before I arrived. The question the tribunal cares about isn't whether the Ember Guild is guilty. Guilt is a formality. The question is where the ember is."

*The ember.* The word went through Mara like a fishhook finding flesh. Xanthe's name was not in any guild ledger — Vess had seen to that within an hour of the courier's arrival, striking the gate log with her own hand — and yet here was a temple inquisitor saying *where* as though the *what* required no discussion.

"We don't have—" he began.

"Scribe." Duvane's voice remained gentle. "Please don't insult both of us. The guild has it. Your guildmaster took it from a half-dead boy at the back gate and locked it below, and the boy is being held in the east wing under the fiction of a lodging contract, and every hour that passes the temple's patience becomes a shorter document." She tapped the immunity writ once, lightly. "I am offering you the only honest trade anyone has offered you in eleven years. Testify. Name names. Describe the archive, the forged copies, the ghost entries, the founder's sealed prophecy — everything you've been carrying around inside you like a swallowed coal. In exchange, your debt is dissolved. Not deferred. Dissolved. Your record cleansed. Your brother released from holding with his term annulled, retroactive to his intake date. Full restitution of whatever he paid before you bought him out of taking his own place." Her grey eyes held his. "You'd walk out of this building owing nothing. To anyone. Ever."

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if listening.

Mara thought about his brother. He thought about Tobi's face at the intake hearing, fourteen years old and trying to look older, trying to look like a man worth imprisoning in place of his brother because that was the only dignity the law allowed them. He thought about the eleven years since — the quarterly visits, the way Tobi's hands had gone calloused and quiet, the way he'd stopped asking *when* and started asking nothing at all. He thought about the number in the guild's books next to his own name: a figure so large it had stopped being money centuries of arithmetic ago and become simply weather, a climate he lived inside.

All he had to do was say yes. Say yes, and sign, and testify, and walk out into the morning a free man with a free brother, having betrayed the institution that owned him — and what was betrayal, really, when the institution had built itself out of theft? The guild had stolen the ember once, if the marsh-woman's sister was to be believed; the founder had stolen the truth out of history and called it prophecy; Vess had sold couriers before and would sell them again. The temple, whatever else it was, was *lawful*. The tribunal kept records. The temple did not erase dates.

And then he heard himself thinking *the temple does not erase dates*, and understood, with a lurch like a stair missing in the dark, exactly how far down the hook had gone.

Because the temple erased things too. He had seen it. Two nights ago, cross-referencing the forged copy against the founder's original, he had found the discrepancy — the prophecy text that differed between editions, the century-old revision, the hand that had rewritten what was fated until fate agreed with it. He had assumed that hand belonged to the guild. He had kept the knowledge secret, hoarded it like a coin bitten for gold.

But sitting across from Duvane now, watching her wait with the serene patience of a creditor, Mara performed the one operation no ledger could survive: he audited his auditor.

*Why*, he thought, *does the temple want the ember back?*

Not destroyed. Not examined. Not returned to the people whose war it ended. *Back.* As if it had merely been misplaced. As if a hundred years of guild magic — every memory bought, every favor lent, every year of life held in trust beneath this floor — were not the real treasure, but a fence around the true one, a lockbox the temple had lost the key to. As if the war-god's altar needed feeding.

And the altar, Xanthe had told him, wild-eyed, in the market's smoke — the altar was hollow. He'd sworn it. Whatever the temple worshipped, it wasn't what the temple said it worshipped.

"You want it," Mara said aloud. It came out quieter than he intended. "The ember. That's what the immunity is for. Not the guild's crimes. The guild could confess everything tomorrow and the tribunal would file it and forget it, if the ember weren't involved. You need a witness who can tell you where it is, because your own records end a hundred years ago, and the woman who took it wrote the ending herself, and you've spent a century pretending you know how the story goes." He looked up. "You don't want truth, Inquisitor. You want your property."

For the first time since she had entered his counting house, Duvane's smile changed. It didn't vanish. It narrowed, the way light narrows through a closing door, and Mara saw beneath it something old and tired and genuinely dangerous.

"Clever," she said. "Vess said you would be."

"She talked to you about me."

"She talks to everyone about everyone. It's how institutions survive." Duvane rose, smoothing her cloak, and took the facsimile-book back but left the writ where it lay, a white rectangle on the dark wood like a door standing open. "Keep it. Immunities don't expire, Scribe. Neither do my patience or my reach. When the guild does what guilds do — when it sells its own people to save its skin, and it will, it always does — you'll want that paper in your pocket. Sign it then. Or don't, and go into the fire with the rest of the inventory." At the foot of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at him with an expression that was almost kind. "One thing, as a professional courtesy. The marginalia you left in the archive — pencil, in the margin of the founder's third ledger, the notation about the date? Pencil can be erased, Scribe Oyelaran. But erasure leaves a ghost of its own. Graphite remembers pressure. I've already lifted it."

The stairs took her up without a sound.

* * *

Mara sat alone for a long time. The lamps burned low. Outside, the city moved through its midnight errands — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the distant bell of the temple marking an hour that meant nothing to anyone who didn't owe it something.

He picked up the writ. The white wax seal was warm, faintly warm, as if freshly pressed, though Duvand had carried it folded in her cloak all evening. He turned it over. On the reverse, in tiny script along the edge, ran a line of legal boilerplate so standard he almost missed it:

*Witness acknowledges that all matters touching the sacred regalia fall under ecclesiastical jurisdiction and may not be disclosed save to the Tribunal.*

Sacred regalia. Jurisdiction. Disclosure restricted. Even her mercy came with chains — finer links than the guild's, polished brighter, but chains. Sign this, and anything he knew about the flame became temple property, and Xanthe — Xanthe, who had carried a dying stranger's last honest thing through marsh and winter and betrayal — became evidence. Evidence to be recovered. Confiscated. Returned to an altar that was empty, to feed a god who wasn't home.

Mara thought of Tobi. He thought of freedom, real freedom, the lawful kind, the kind with paperwork. It would be so easy. It would be *right*, by every law the city recognized. The guild was guilty. The founder was a thief. Vess was a seller of children. Let the temple have them. Let the machinery grind the guilty, and let him stand clear with his clean hands and his clean record, and tell himself the price was acceptable because other people were paying it.

But the flame had shown him his worst act already — had shown him the courtroom, twelve years ago, the moment he had stepped back and let a fourteen-year-old boy raise his hand and say *I'll take his place*. And Mara understood, with terrible clarity, that the writ in his hand was the same act wearing better clothes. Step back. Let someone else stand in the fire. Sign your name to your own innocence.

He fed the writ to the lamp.

It caught reluctantly, temple paper wanting to burn slowly, ceremonially, and he held it by the corner and watched the seal blister and blacken, and felt nothing like relief. Only the specific loneliness of a man who has just declined the last exit.

When it was ash, he swept it into his inkwell, ground it fine with the muddler, and diluted it into the black until no trace remained. Old scribes' trick. Ink hides many sins.

Then he took up his pen, pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and began to write — not the guild's numbers, not the temple's testimony, but a letter in cipher, in the old accounting shorthand his father had taught him, addressed to a courier locked in the east wing who could not possibly receive it tonight:

*They know about the marginalia. They know about me. The offer was my brother and my debt, and I burned it, and I don't know yet whether that was courage or the stupidest thing I've ever done. What I know is this: the temple doesn't want justice either. It wants its property. Which means we are the only two people in this city who want the truth, and we are running out of time to decide what to do with it.*

He signed it with a symbol, not a name — a small circled figure, the mark of a balanced column.

Below the window, in the counting house yard, a lantern moved where no lantern should be. Mara blew out his lamp and stood very still in the dark, listening.

Boots. Two pairs. Crossing the yard toward the east wing.

Toward Xanthe.
