## 12. What Mara Saw

The flame did not burn.

That was the first cruelty of it — that something called an ember could be cold as a coin left overnight in winter, and yet when Mara looked at it, his eyes watered the way they did before grief, not heat. The case hung open between them on the floor of the abandoned tally-room, lead peeled back like fruit skin, and inside burned a light the color of the moment just before you say something unforgivable.

Xanthe had already had his turn. He sat against the far wall with his knees drawn up, ash-pale, the marks of tears cut clean through the grime on his face. He would not look at the flame. He would not look at Mara either.

"You don't have to," Xanthe said. His voice was rough as unpolished vellum. "It doesn't make you."

But Mara was a scribe. He had spent eleven years reading other people's accounts, entering their debts in careful columns, balancing what they owed against what they could bear to pay. He knew better than anyone alive that a thing unwritten is not a thing undone.

He reached into the case.

* * *

The counting house smelled of tallow and wet wool, the way it always did in the last week of the fiscal year, when the ledgers came home fat with arrears and the clerks worked by candlelight until their fingers went gray with ink. Mara was twenty-three. His brother Tam was nineteen and laughing at something — he was always laughing, even then, even with the debt collectors' letters stacked like kindling on their mother's table — and the two of them stood before the assessor's desk while rain hammered the shutters of the guild hall.

"Two hundred crowns outstanding," the assessor said. He was a soft man, patient the way a scale is patient. "The contract names one debtor. The house will take whoever signs."

Mara remembered thinking, with a clarity that still shamed him, *I am the better hand.* His penmanship was known throughout the counting house. His columns never wavered. If one of them must go down into debtor's prison — three years, perhaps five, in the under-halls where the guild kept the people whose debts outlived their patience — then the family needed the one who could keep writing, keep earning, keep the ledger open so that someday, somehow, the remaining brother might buy the imprisoned one free.

That was the arithmetic. That was the story he had told himself for eleven years, in the voice of a reasonable man doing reasonable sums.

The flame showed him the rest of the page.

It showed him Tam stepping forward — eager, god help him, *eager*, because Tam had always wanted to matter, to be the brave one, to give his careful older brother something back after all those years of being protected. It showed Mara opening his mouth to say *no, wait, let me,* and it showed — with terrible, patient fidelity — the half-second where he didn't. The half-second where some small cold clerk in the back room of his heart checked the figures and found that Tam's signature balanced better than his own. Tam was young; his years were worth more per annum. Tam had no craft to lose. Tam would survive the under-halls the way a weed survives a drought, and Mara —

— and Mara needed to keep writing. Someone had to.

He had said nothing. That was all. He had said nothing for exactly long enough, and the assessor, being a fair man, took silence for consent, and slid the contract across the desk, and Tam signed his name in a scrawl like a bird's footprints, laughing even then, saying *five years, I'll be out before my hair goes gray, don't look so tragic, Mar.*

Tam died in the fourth year. A fever, they said. The under-halls were damp and the guild's mercy was cheaper than its ink. And Mara had gone to the funeral and wept honestly, and gone back to work the next morning, and entered the final settlement of his brother's estate in a fair round hand, and told himself for eleven years that the arithmetic had been sound.

The flame held him there in that half-second of silence. It did not accuse. It simply waited, the way a ledger waits, for him to enter the true figure.

*I chose the numbers over him,* Mara thought, or said, or became. *I did the sum and I let him sign.*

The flame guttered once, satisfied, and released him.

* * *

He came back to himself on the floor of the tally-room with his cheek against cold stone and Xanthe's hand hovering near his shoulder, not quite touching. The abandoned chamber around them was thick with dust and the ghosts of old arithmetic: rusted counting frames, a shattered abacus, tally-sticks scattered like fallen reeds. Above them, somewhere in the living heart of the guild, enforcers were still searching. They had perhaps an hour. Perhaps less.

"I killed him," Mara heard himself say.

"No." Xanthe's voice came from very far away and very close at once. "You didn't. Whatever it showed you —"

"It shows the truth. You told me. Yours was true, wasn't it?" Mara pushed himself upright. His hands were shaking, and he watched them shake with a scribe's detached interest, as though they belonged to someone else's account. "The woman in the marshes. You left her."

Xanthe flinched as if struck. "She was dying. There was nothing — I couldn't have carried her and the case both, the marsh road, the tide coming in, I *calculated* —" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Gods. That's what it does, isn't it? It makes you hear yourself. The way you sound when you're lying to yourself."

"The way we all sound," Mara said quietly. "Every day. In every counting house in this city."

And then — because the flame had opened something in him that could not be closed again, some door that only swung inward — he began to confess.

Not just the brother. Everything. It poured out of him the way ink pours when a bottle tips, unstoppable, staining everything it touches.

* * *

"The night you arrived," he said, "I was auditing the founder's sealed archive. It's part of my duties — no one else has the clearance, no one else wants it, the air down there is bad and the founder's cipher gives clerks headaches. And there's a prophecy ledger. A century old. Sealed with her own mark." He laughed, a thin and awful sound. "I found tonight's date in it, Xanthe. Circled twice. Written before either of us was born. I told myself it was coincidence. Scribes are very good at telling themselves things."

Xanthe had gone very still. "You knew. Before I ever walked through the gate. You knew the date meant something."

"I suspected. Then the guildmaster assigned me to you, and I found the seal mark on your case matched the founder's archive mark, and I — I started asking questions I shouldn't have asked." He drew a breath. "Then the inquisitors came. Two days ago. They demanded inspection of every ledger, including the sealed archive. Vess ordered me to forge a clean copy overnight. So I did."

"You forged —"

"A century of the guild's private accounts. Debts paid in memories, in years of life, in favors no court would enforce. The temple can't see that and survive; the temple would burn us for trafficking in souls, never mind that the temple's own tithe rolls are written in the same currency. So yes. I forged. I'm good at it. It may be the only thing I'm truly good at." He met Xanthe's eyes. "And here is the part I haven't told anyone. Not Vess. Not you. While I was copying the prophecy ledger for the forgery, I compared it line by line against the original. They don't match."

"What do you mean they don't match?"

"I mean someone rewrote the prophecy. Between editions, between copies, somewhere in the last hundred years, the text was altered — the terms changed, the bearer changed, the date itself moved. Prophecies aren't supposed to have revisions, Xanthe. They're not supposed to have *editions.*" His voice dropped. "A prophecy with drafts behind it isn't a prophecy. It's a document. And documents have authors, and authors have reasons."

The flame between them pulsed softly, as if listening. Mara found he was no longer afraid of it. It had already taken the worst he had. There was a strange freedom in that — the freedom of a man whose books have been audited down to the last copper, who has nothing left hidden, and who discovers that the nakedness is lighter than the clothes.

"There's more," he said. "There's one more sin and then you'll know all of me, and then you can decide what I'm worth, because I've decided you deserve to know what you're carrying and who I am before either of us takes another step. An inquisitor found my marginalia in the archive. My notes. My questions. She offered me immunity — full pardon, debt dissolved, my name cleared — if I testified that the guild traffics in forbidden debts. All I had to do was hand over what I'd found and point at your case."

Xanthe stared at him. In the cold light of the ember his face looked carved, all planes and shadows. "And?"

"And I said I'd consider it." Mara's voice cracked and he let it crack. He was done with the fair round hand, done with the steady columns, done with the reasonable arithmetic. "That's the truth. I sat in my room with her offer on the table and I *considered it*, because she promised me the one thing I've wanted for eleven years, which is to owe nothing to anyone. I lay awake doing the sum. Your life against my debt. And the clerk in the back room of my heart — the same one who stayed silent while my brother signed — started checking the figures."

* * *

Silence filled the tally-room. Somewhere above, boots crossed a floor; dust sifted down through a crack in the ceiling like slow snow. The flame breathed its colorless color across the ruins of the counting frames, and in its light Mara saw Xanthe's expression change — not to anger, which he had braced for, but to something worse. Recognition.

"You didn't take it," Xanthe said at last.

"No."

"Why not?"

Because the flame had shown him. Because he had knelt in his own worst act and seen that it was not the silence at the assessor's desk — that was only the wound. The worst act was everything after. Eleven years of keeping the books balanced instead of tearing them open. Eleven years of *reasonable*. The inquisitor's offer had been another assessor's desk, another contract, another signature that balanced better if someone else bled for it.

"Because I've already sold one person to balance my account," Mara said. "His name was Tam. He laughed even when he was afraid, and he signed his name like bird tracks, and he died in the dark believing his brother would buy him out. I won't do it again. Not for a pardon. Not for anything." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand and was surprised to find it wet. "That's everything. The forgery, the altered text, the offer, all of it. You know me now. All the entries. Audited in full."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was hoarse but level. "The woman in the marshes. Before she gave me the case, she said it was the last honest thing in the world. I thought she meant the ember." He looked at the flame, and then at Mara. "I think she meant this. What it does. What it just made you say out loud. Nobody confesses like that unless it's true."

"It's true."

"Then we're even," Xanthe said simply. "You saw mine. I saw yours. Neither of us gets to hold it over the other, because neither of us comes out clean." He almost smiled; it was a battered, beautiful thing. "My mother used to say a debt shared between friends isn't half a debt. It's a different thing entirely."

"We're not friends."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "We're co-signatories."

Despite everything — despite the dead brother and the forged ledgers and the inquisitor waiting somewhere in the lamplit corridors above with her offer of immunity — Mara laughed. It came out broken and real, the first honest sound he had made in eleven years.

Then the laugh died in his throat, because his eye caught on something in the flame's shifting light: the wall behind the ruined counting frames, where centuries of clerks had scratched initials and dates, and beneath the oldest layer of grime, half erased, a single line of cipher. Founder's cipher. The one that gave clerks headaches. The one Mara had spent eleven years learning to read.

He rose slowly and brushed the dust away with his sleeve.

The inscription was short. It was dated the year of the guild's founding. And it was not a prayer, or a motto, or a memorial.

It was a *draft note*. An author's margin. *Amend the third clause before sealing,* it read, in handwriting Mara knew better than his own late mother's, because he had spent a century's distance studying it line by line in the sealed dark. *The date must hold. Let them believe the book, not the woman.*

"Xanthe," Mara said, and his voice had gone very quiet and very cold, the voice he used for entries that would not balance. "The founder wrote the prophecy. I need the archive. Tonight. Before the inquisitors burn it, before Vess seals it, before anyone else decides what we're worth — I need to prove whose hand has been writing our lives."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the searching boots stopped.

Someone had found the tally-room stair.
