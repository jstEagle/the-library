## 18. The Temple's Empty Heart

The temple did not look like a place where a war had been born.

It looked like a place where a war had been *tidied away* — swept into corners, folded under altar cloths, dusted and polished and forgiven. From the processional road it rose in pale terraces of quarried stone, each level narrower than the last, until the summit held the Sanctum of the Ember like a cupped hand holds a coal. Pilgrims climbed those steps by the thousands on high holy days. Tonight there were only beggars sleeping against the balustrades and the slow drip of the fountain that no one had cleaned since before Xanthe was born.

He crouched in the shadow of the fountain's dry lower basin, the lead case strapped to his chest beneath a pilgrim's gray robe, and felt it breathing against his sternum.

That was the word he'd finally settled on, after everything. Not burning. Not humming. Breathing. The flame inside had a rhythm now, slow as sleep, and when he pressed his palm flat over the seal he could feel it expand and contract, patient, like something waiting for a door to open. Like something that had been waiting a very long time.

Beside him, Mara Oyelaran was having what Xanthe had learned to recognize as a scribe's crisis.

"The vigil rotations change at the third bell," Mara whispered. His voice had the flat, clipped quality it took on when he was reciting facts to keep himself from feeling them. "Two guards on the western stair, four in the Reliquary Hall, and the Undercantor makes her rounds of the inner sanctum at the half-hour. I have their schedule memorized from the tithe ledgers. What I do not have memorized is how we're going to get past four guards in a hall with nowhere to hide."

"We don't hide." Xanthe shifted the case higher against his chest. "We walk."

"Walk. Past the guards. Carrying the single most dangerous object in the city. Into the heart of the temple we are robbing."

"We're not robbing it." He kept his eyes on the terraces above, counting windows, measuring shadows. "We're returning something. There's a difference. Robbers run."

Mara made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been taxed out of him years ago. "You've spent three days learning what the guild does to couriers who walk through front doors."

"I've spent three days learning what the guild does to couriers who let other people decide for them." He turned and looked at Mara properly — the ink-stained fingers, the hollows under his eyes, the way he held his shoulders like a man carrying a ledger no one else could see. "Vess sold us. The inquisitors want to bury us. The founder wrote our whole lives in a book a hundred years ago so she'd never have to pay her debts. Every institution in this city has told us what we are." He tapped the case once, gently, the way you'd tap a friend's shoulder. "This is the first thing that ever asked me instead."

*It shows each viewer their own worst act.* He didn't say that part out loud. He hadn't said it since the counting house, since the night the enforcers cornered them and he broke the founder's seal and the flame had opened him up like a letter and read him aloud. *The marshes. Her hand slipping out of yours. The sound your boots made walking away.* Some things you confessed once and then carried quietly, the way you carried a scar.

Mara looked at him for a long moment. In the dark, with the fountain dripping and the temple glowing faintly gold above them, the scribe looked younger than his debts.

"You really believe it belongs here," Mara said. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"I believe someone died in a marsh calling this *the last honest thing*, and everyone who's touched it since has lied about it. The temple says it's theirs. The guild says it's theirs. Nobody's asked what *it* wants." He stood, knees cracking, and pulled the pilgrim's hood up. "So we ask. We put it on the altar where they claim it lived for a thousand years, and we see what happens. Publicly. In daylight, tomorrow, with witnesses. But tonight we find out whether the altar is even real."

"And if it isn't?"

Xanthe had been avoiding that thought for two days. It sat in him now like a swallowed stone.

"Then we'll know why nobody ever opened the Sanctum doors," he said, and started climbing.

* * *

The pilgrim's gate stood open all night for the devout — that much was true, at least; the temple had never stopped wanting people to come. A novice on gate duty glanced at their gray robes, at the ash marks Mara had smeared on their foreheads in imitation of mourning pilgrims, and waved them through with the boneless indifference of someone three hours past caring. Inside, the temple smelled the way Mara had promised it would: cold stone, old incense, and underneath both, faintly, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a locked room.

They climbed the western stair. The two guards Mara had predicted were exactly where he'd said they'd be, and exactly as bored — one picking at his bootlaces, the other asleep sitting up. Xanthe walked past them at a mourner's pace, head bowed, hands folded over the case, and neither guard so much as lifted his eyes. Grief was the best disguise in any city. No one wanted to look at it too closely.

The Reliquary Hall was worse than Mara's fear and better than Xanthe's hope. Four guards, yes — but arranged along the walls like furniture, their attention fixed on the reliquaries themselves: glass cases where the temple displayed its proof. Fragments of the war. A rusted helm fused to a skull. A child's shoe, blackened. A spearhead labeled in gold leaf: *FROM THE FIELD WHERE THE EMBER WAS TAKEN BACK.*

Xanthe stopped in front of that one despite himself.

*Taken back.* As if the ember were a runaway animal, an escaped prisoner, a thing that had wandered off and been lovingly retrieved. As if a woman hadn't crawled out of the marshes with her insides burning, pressed a sealed case into his arms, and said *the last honest thing* with her last breath. He thought about the sister in the Night Market — Yeva, with her brother's courier ring on a cord around her neck — and what she'd told them in a back room that smelled of lamp oil and dried fish: *My brother carried dispatches for the temple before he carried for the guild. Do you know what he found in their deep archive? Requisition orders. A century old. The temple paid for that ember to be taken. They've known where it was the whole time.*

The guards didn't look at him. That was the thing about holy places, Xanthe was beginning to understand. People assumed holiness was watching them, so they watched themselves, and the actual eyes in the building grew lazy.

Beyond the Reliquary Hall, a corridor narrowed, and the light changed.

Not torchlight. Something older. The walls here were not dressed stone but raw rock, veined with mineral seams that caught the glow and threw it back amber, so that walking down the corridor felt like walking down the throat of some enormous patient creature. The temperature dropped. Then — strangest of all — it rose again, warm as a hearth room, warm as skin, and Xanthe realized with a lurch that the warmth was coming from ahead of them, from behind the great doors at the corridor's end, and that the flame in the case against his chest had begun to beat faster.

Like a dog smelling home. Or a prisoner smelling freedom.

"There," Mara breathed. He'd gone pale in the amber light. "The Sanctum doors. I've read the inventories — the tithes that funded them, the craftsmen's contracts. Solid bronze over ironwood, plated with a century of votive offerings. They say the High Cantor opens them twice a year, at the Ember Vigils, to—"

"To show the crowd the flame," Xanthe finished. "I know. Everyone knows. You can see the glow from the plaza on vigil nights." He stepped closer to the doors, close enough that his reflection ghosted across the polished bronze: a thin man in a gray robe, warped and wavering, holding nothing at all.

Because that was the strange part. The doors glowed. The seams around them glowed. On vigil nights the whole city saw the light of the sacred flame pouring out through the Sanctum's windows, proof eternal, the war-god's living heart.

But standing this close, Xanthe could feel the case against his chest, and the case was *quiet*. Not dim — quiet. The way a room goes quiet when two people realize they're both lying.

"Mara," he said slowly. "When the flame showed me my worst act. When it opened for me. How bright was it?"

Mara frowned. "Bright enough to read a ledger by. Why?"

"And tonight? Since we came through the gate?"

The scribe went still. Xanthe watched him do the arithmetic — the same careful, terrible arithmetic he did with numbers, except this time the numbers were light and silence and the absence of heat.

"It's dimmer," Mara whispered. "It's been getting dimmer all night, hasn't it. The closer we get—"

Xanthe set his shoulder against the doors and pushed.

They weren't locked. Of course they weren't locked. Locks are for things someone might steal, and no one steals from a lie.

* * *

The Sanctum of the Ember was a bowl of darkness.

That was the first thing. After the amber throat of the corridor, after the glow that the whole city knew, the room itself was *dark* — vast and round, tiered like an amphitheater, built to hold ten thousand worshippers all facing inward toward the center, toward the dais, toward the altar.

The second thing was the smell. Old smoke, decades of it, soaked into the stone. Smoke without fire. The ghost of combustion, preserved like a flower in wax.

And the third thing —

Xanthe walked down into the bowl alone. He didn't decide to; his legs decided for him, drawn down the concentric tiers the way water is drawn down a drain, and behind him he heard Mara whisper his name and then follow, because of course he followed, because whatever else Mara Oyelaran was, he had never once let Xanthe walk into the dark without him.

The altar stood at the center on a dais of black marble. It was beautiful. That was the cruelty of it. Carved from a single block of some red-veined stone, shaped like cupped hands the size of a wagon, inlaid with gold that caught even this darkness and made it gleam. A century of offerings lay heaped around its base — candle stubs, ribbons, milk teeth, letters, the small desperate currency of the grieving. Ten thousand people had knelt on these steps. Ten thousand wars' worth of mothers.

The altar was empty.

Not vacant. Not unoccupied. *Hollow.* There was a recess in the heart of those carved hands, a socket shaped exactly — Xanthe's stomach turned as he understood — exactly the size and curve of a lead-sealed case. And inside the socket, nothing. Black marble polished smooth by a century of ceremonial handling. A hole where the god should be.

"No," Mara said behind him. Just that. Just *no*, very quietly, in the voice of a man auditing a column of numbers that refuse to balance.

Xanthe reached the dais and put his hand into the hollow.

Cold stone. Nothing else. He ran his palm along the socket's curves, feeling for scorch, for residue, for anything — and found instead, near the rim, worn nearly smooth by touch, a groove. A channel cut into the marble, leading from the hollow down and back, into the altar's body, into the wall.

A drain. Or a duct. Something for smoke to travel through.

His eyes went up. Above the altar, hidden in the darkness of the dome, a network of channels fanned out through the stonework like veins, like roots, branching and branching until they fed — he traced them, heart hammering — until they fed the walls of every tier, the corridor outside, the great doors themselves.

The glow. The eternal glow visible from the plaza on vigil nights. Twice a year the High Cantor opened these doors and the city saw the light of the living flame, and the light was real, it had always been real — but it came from lamps. Oil lamps, banked and tended behind the stone, piped through a century-old ventilation system built by craftsmen whose contracts Mara could probably recite from memory.

"They never had it," Xanthe said. His voice sounded wrong in the huge dark room, too small. "Mara. They never *had* it. The woman in the marshes stole it *from* them a hundred years ago, or they lost it, or they sold it — but they've been guarding an empty bowl ever since. All of it. The vigils. The tithes. The war itself." He turned. "They sent my brother-couriers to die on the roads hunting for something that was already gone."

Mara had sunk onto the lowest step of the dais. In the lamplit gloom his face was terrible — not shocked, Xanthe realized. Worse than shocked. *Recognizing.*

"The founder's ledger," Mara said. "The prophecy. *'And the flame shall come home when the book says so.'*" He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "She wrote it as a threat. A deadline. But she was right in a way she didn't mean. The flame coming home would be the first time it was ever here. This altar was built *around an absence*. The whole religion is a shell around a hole the size of—" He gestured at the case on Xanthe's chest. "The size of that."

Xanthe looked down at his own hands, resting over the seal.

All his life he'd believed in the ember the way everyone believed — distantly, conveniently, the way you believe in weather. The temple guarded the flame; the flame justified the temple; the war had been fought over it and the peace was kept because of it and somewhere in the arithmetic of all that suffering there must be meaning, because otherwise the suffering was just suffering. He'd carried the case for six weeks and hated it and feared it and protected it, and somewhere under all of that he'd believed he was carrying the reason.

It wasn't the reason. It was the *receipt*. Proof of purchase for a hundred years of lies, and the only honest thing left in either building.

Above them, hidden in the dome, a lamp guttered. Just one — a draft, a wick burning low — but the effect raced down the veins of the dome like a shudder, and for a heartbeat the Sanctum's famous glow stuttered, flickered, and the darkness leaned in close, and Xanthe understood with perfect clarity that this was what the temple actually was: a machine for producing certainty out of oil and bronze and fear, running on its last reserves, one gust of wind from going out.

He felt pity then. It arrived unwanted, unwelcome, curdling the awe in his chest into something sour. Pity for the novice at the gate. Pity for the guards picking their bootlaces. Pity for the ten thousand mourners who had left their milk teeth and their letters in a pile around a beautifully carved *nothing*. Even — God help him — pity for Vess, who had known, who had always known, and had chosen to keep the lamps lit rather than let ten thousand people see the dark.

"They'll fight for it anyway," Mara said quietly. He was staring at the hollow. "Tomorrow, on the steps, when you offer it back. They can't let it be seen. An empty altar ends them faster than a stolen one. They'll call it a forgery. They'll call you a heretic. They'll kill us both on holy ground and call it mercy."

"Probably."

"And knowing that, you still want to stand on those steps and hold it up in front of everyone."

Xanthe thought about the dying woman's hand. About the flame opening him like a letter. About his worst act, which he could not undo, and about this one, which he still could.

"Yeah," he said. "I do."

He began working the lead seal loose with his thumbs.

Behind them, at the top of the amphitheater, the Sanctum doors swung open with a groan of bronze, and lamplight spilled down the tiers, and a voice — dry, precise, familiar from the counting-house raid, from every nightmare he'd had this week — said:

"So. The courier found the family jewels."

Inquisitor Sarel-Vane stood silhouetted in the doorway, flanked by six temple guards, and in her gloved hands she held something white that Xanthe needed a moment to recognize: a ledger. Open. Turned toward them like a mirror.

"Now," she said, "let's discuss what you're going to testify."
