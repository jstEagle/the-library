## 21. Audited by No One

The flame died on the temple steps the way a debt dies — not with a scream but with a sigh, a small gray curl of smoke that rose into the morning and was gone before anyone could decide whether to mourn it.

Xanthe felt it go.

For eleven days and most of his life before that, he had carried a weight at the center of his chest that was not quite heat and not quite cold, a presence that pressed against his ribs like a second heart with opinions of its own. He had slept with it against his sternum in ditches and way-stations and once, memorably, inside a hollowed-out cheese wheel in a smuggler's cellar outside Vell. He had talked to it when there was no one else to talk to. He had apologized to it more than once, though he had never been sure it heard.

Now his chest was just a chest. The absence rang in him like a struck bell after the sound has faded — you hear the shape of what is no longer there.

He sat down on the temple steps because his legs told him to, and the crowd around him did not notice, or if they noticed they assumed he was one more penitent come to gawk at the ruin of certainty. The steps were still scorched where the flame had flared when the inquisitors refused it. Three of them stood at the bottom even now, in their gray-and-gold vestments, looking up at their own empty god-house with expressions Xanthe would spend the rest of his life trying to describe. Not rage. Not shame. Something worse: recalculation. He watched the senior inquisitor, a woman named Halveth with a voice like a closing door, turn slowly on her heel and begin issuing orders in a low, urgent stream — seal the archive, secure the relic-vaults, no statements until the Conclave rules.

As if ruling were something the Conclave could still do. As if anything were left to rule over but an altar with nothing in it and a century of hymns sung to a hole.

Beside him, Mara sat too, folding down onto the stone with the graceless care of a man whose body has only just remembered it belongs to him. His hands were ink-stained to the wrist — they always would be now, Xanthe thought; some stains go all the way through — and he held them palm-up in his lap as though checking whether they were still his.

"It's quiet," Mara said.

"I know."

"No. I mean—" He stopped. Started again. "I've had the ledger-noise in my head for nine years. Every entry. Every interest calculation. Every year of my brother's sentence accruing like mold in a wall. And now it's just—" He made a small gesture at the air, at the whole sunlit, indifferent morning. "Quiet."

Xanthe understood. He had carried the case for less time than Mara had carried the books, but the silence where the ember had been was the same country. You crossed into it and found it enormous.

Down at the base of the steps, someone had begun to cry — one of the guild couriers, a broad-shouldered woman Xanthe half-knew from the road, Sefa, who had run the western circuit for six years and never once asked what she carried. She cried the way working people cry, silently, facing away, hands busy with nothing. Then another courier joined her, and then a counting-house clerk, and then a knot of citizens who had come to watch and stayed to grieve for reasons they couldn't have named.

Nobody rioted. That was the strange thing. Xanthe had expected riots. Instead the city did what cities do when a great lie dies: it went about its business in a daze, buying bread, arguing about cart-tariffs, glancing up every few minutes at the temple as if expecting the sky to fall in specifically there.

"Come on," Mara said at last, hauling himself upright. "I want to see the counting house."

"Why?"

"Because I spent nine years wanting to leave it, and I'd like to find out what leaving actually looks like."

* * *

It looked like this: doors open. All of them.

The Ember Guild's counting house, which for a hundred and forty years had kept its ledgers behind iron grilles and its debtors' registry under triple lock, stood with its great bronze doors flung wide to the street, and people walked in and out of it freely, the way they would walk in and out of any shop. Inside, the tellers' cages stood unlocked. Someone had dragged a table into the middle of the floor and set out bread and hard cheese and a jug of thin wine, and clerks — actual clerks, in their ink-bitten aprons — were sitting among their former customers explaining contracts line by line while those customers read them with their own eyes, many for the first time.

A boy of maybe twelve was standing at the old debtors' registry, turning pages with both hands, reading names aloud to his mother. "Grandda," he said suddenly. "Here. Grandda's name." And his mother took the book gently from him and read the entry herself, and her face did something complicated, and Xanthe looked away to give her privacy with it.

The guild wasn't gone. That was the thing nobody had predicted, least of all the guild. Strip away the ember-contracts, void the memory-debts, unmake the years-of-life liens and the favor-economy with its cruel little interest rates — and underneath all of it there remained, embarrassingly, a functioning trading house. Warehouses. Routes. Ledgers full of ordinary numbers attached to ordinary goods. The guild collapsed the way a rotten tooth comes out: horribly, and revealing that the jaw beneath was still sound.

They were calling themselves the Oyelaran House already, Xanthe heard, in the tones people use for jokes that aren't jokes. Because a scribe had written one honest line in the oldest book in the city, and the city needed somewhere to put its gratitude, and naming a trading house after him cost nothing.

Mara turned scarlet when he heard it, and said, "That's absurd," and then stood very still for a long moment, listening to his own name spoken without a debt attached to it, and Xanthe pretended not to see him wipe his eye.

In the corner of the main hall, a clerk had set up what she was calling the Reckoning Desk — a plain table with a bell on it. Anyone who believed the guild owed them something could ring the bell and state their claim, and it would be written down, publicly, in a fresh ledger with blank pages, to be honored or disputed in the open. The queue stretched out the door and down three streets. Some claims were monstrous. Some were petty. One man wanted compensation for a dream the guild had purchased off him in 1189 and never returned. The clerk wrote them all down anyway.

"That's the difference," Mara said quietly, watching. "Before, debts were how they owned you. Now a debt is just... a thing somebody says, out loud, that other people get to weigh."

"You sound like a man describing water to fish."

"I feel like a fish describing land." He exhaled. "I keep waiting for the trap. There's always a clause. There's always fine print. I've read ten thousand contracts, Xanthe. Nothing is free."

"The flame was free."

"The flame showed me my worst act and then burned itself to ash so I couldn't take it back." Mara's mouth twisted. "That wasn't free either. That was the most expensive thing I've ever been given."

Outside, the light had begun to go gold. Somewhere a bell rang — not the guild's bell, not the temple's; just a temple-ward bell marking the hour, doing its modest work. They walked out through the open doors without being stopped, which neither of them mentioned, because mentioning it would have meant admitting how long it had been since either of them had passed a threshold without permission.

* * *

Vess faced the families on the fourth day, in the guild's old receiving hall, with the portraits of the founders taken down and stacked face-to-wall against the far side.

Xanthe went because he thought he should see it, and because part of him — the part that had watched the guildmaster sign his name over to the inquisitors like a line item — wanted her to suffer. He was not proud of that part. It had gotten louder since the flame died. Grief, it turned out, didn't burn clean; it smoldered, and threw off smoke, and the smoke smelled like things you weren't proud of.

There were forty-some families in the hall. Wives and husbands and mothers and one ancient grandfather who had come to ask about a daughter lost on the northern circuit thirty-one years ago. The couriers the guild had traded away over the decades — not just Xanthe, not just last week; there had been others, quietly, across the years, whenever a contract got inconvenient. Vess had signed for some of them herself. She stood at the front without notes, without the guildmaster's chain of office, in a plain gray dress, and she did not ask for forgiveness, which Xanthe noticed first, and respected second.

She read names. She gave back effects — a boot knife, a wedding ring, a lock of hair kept in a locket the way the guild kept collateral. She answered questions as far as the truth went and said *I don't know* when it went further, and she said it plainly, without the administrative softness that officials use to make ignorance sound like discretion.

The grandfather asked his question last. "Did she die owing you?" His voice was dry as old paper. "I need to know if she died owing you. Because if she died owing you, then in your books she was never done, and I can't—"

"She died owing us nothing," Vess said. "I've checked. It's one of the few mercies in the record. Your daughter paid everything. She was free for four years before the marsh fever took her. She just never came home."

The old man nodded, and wept, and it was somehow worse and better than rage.

Afterward, in the corridor, Vess passed Xanthe close enough to touch. She stopped. For a moment neither spoke.

"They'll want a trial," she said. "The families. Or the city will, on their behalf. And I won't contest it. I want you to know that whatever happens—"

"I don't want anything from you."

"I know." Her mouth moved in something adjacent to a smile. "That's what makes it unbearable. Everyone I ever dealt with wanted something. It was the only language I knew. And then you two walk in and want nothing, and it turns out I don't speak anything else." She looked down the corridor toward the hall, where the families were filing out with their returned effects clutched to their chests. "Tell your friend the House honors its ordinary debts. All of them. Even the ones that ruin it. If the Oyelaran name is going to mean something, let it mean that."

Then she went back in to face the next family, alone, and Xanthe stood in the corridor a while longer than he needed to, working through the uncomfortable discovery that he didn't hate her anymore, and that this felt less like mercy than like exhaustion.

* * *

The temple's problem was simpler and stranger: what do you worship when the god turns out to be an empty room?

Xanthe went up there on the sixth evening, alone, because he'd left something — or thought he had — and found the great nave lit by hundreds of candles that the faithful kept lighting anyway. Nobody had ordered them to stop. Nobody had ordered anything; the Conclave was in session and had been for six days, and rumor said the sessions were less theology than accounting, priests going through a hundred years of tithes and relics and asking each other, in increasingly strained voices, what exactly any of it had been *for*.

The altar was still hollow. Of course it was. But someone had laid flowers in the emptiness — a scatter of marigolds and one white lily — and someone else had left a child's drawing pinned to the altar-cloth: a stick figure holding a box, rays coming off it. Underneath, in wobbling letters: *THE FIRE THAT TOLD THE TRUTH.*

Xanthe stood in front of it for a long time.

A young acolyte came down the nave, sweeping — sweeping, in the middle of an institutional apocalypse, because floors don't stop needing it — and saw him, and stopped, and Xanthe braced for reverence or accusation. Instead the boy said, "Were you really the bearer? My mother says you broke it open on the steps and everyone had to look at themselves."

"I opened a case," Xanthe said. "People did their own looking. That was the trick of it. The flame never showed anyone anything they didn't already know."

"Is that why the inquisitors refused it? On the steps?"

"Yes."

The boy considered this with the terrible directness of the young. "My mother says they refused because they already knew their worst acts and liked them."

"Your mother sounds like she should be running the Conclave."

"She says that too." He resumed sweeping. Halfway down the nave he paused. "What will they worship now? Do you think?"

And Xanthe, who had carried the last honest thing across half a country and watched it die on a staircase, heard himself answer honestly: "Maybe just the truth. It doesn't need an altar. It burns fine on its own."

The boy seemed satisfied with this, or bored by it, and swept on, and the candlelight moved on the hollow altar like water over an empty well.

* * *

On the ninth night, Xanthe unpacked.

This requires explanation. Couriers don't unpack. A courier owns one bag, keeps it packed, sleeps with its strap around his wrist or ankle, and measures every room he enters by its exits. Xanthe had lived that way since he was fourteen, since the roads took him in the way roads do, and the case had made it worse — you don't set down a bag containing a war's original sin. For eleven days the case had been the heaviest thing in any room, and then it was ash on the wind, and the bag was just a bag, and on the ninth night, in a rented room above a chandler's shop on Tanner's Row — rented, paid for with coin earned carrying freight for the new House at ordinary rates, the first money he had ever earned that wasn't bonded — he unpinned the strap and lifted out his belongings one at a time and did not put them back.

A spare shirt, worn soft as paper. A whetstone. A tin cup. A coil of good cord. A carved wooden bird, no bigger than his thumb, that a girl in a border town had given him six years ago for carrying a letter that saved her father's farm, and which he had carried ever since for reasons he had never examined until the flame made him examine everything.

He set them all on the shelf above the bed. The room looked different with his things on a shelf instead of in a bag. It looked, he realized, like a room someone lived in.

He sat on the bed afterward and felt the strangeness of it hit him all at once, the way weather hits. Freedom, it turned out, was not a trumpet. It was a shelf. It was waking tomorrow with nowhere you had to be and no one you belonged to and nothing burning cold against your ribs, and discovering that all of that felt less like flying than like standing on the ground for the first time — solid, unglamorous, slightly terrifying, yours.

He could go anywhere. That was the fact of it, plain as bread. The roads were open in every direction. The western passes, the coast road south, the river barges north — he knew them all like old lovers, and every one of them was saying *come back, come back, you were good at this.* He could be a courier again, a real one, freight and letters and nothing heavier than salt.

Instead, on the tenth morning, he went to the counting house and asked the clerk at the Reckoning Desk if the new House needed couriers.

"Everyone needs couriers," she said. "Half our routes are dark. Can you start Thursday?"

"Can I start now?"

She blinked at him, then laughed, and wrote his name in a fresh ledger — his name, spelled right, with no bond number after it, no lien, no term of service, just *Xanthe, courier, Tanner's Row,* and beside it the word *wages* — and Xanthe looked at the entry for longer than a grown man should look at one line of text.

"What are you grinning at?" Mara asked, appearing at his elbow with an armful of contract folios and the harried air of a man discovering that freedom mostly means paperwork.

"Nothing."

"You're grinning at a ledger."

"I'm allowed to."

Mara stopped. Looked at him. Something passed between them there in the noise of the re-made counting house — not gratitude, exactly, and not friendship yet, though it was on its way to becoming both. Nine days ago they had stood on the temple steps and watched a god-sized lie burn itself out together, and you do not come back from that as strangers.

"Dinner," Mara said. "Tonight. There's a place on Chandler's Lane that does eel pie and doesn't ask questions. I owe you—"

He caught himself. Both of them froze. Then Mara smiled, slow and crooked, the first real smile Xanthe had seen on him since the flame died.

"No," Mara corrected himself. "I don't owe you anything. That's rather the point. Dinner anyway."

"Dinner anyway," Xanthe agreed.

They went out together past the tellers' cages and through the bronze doors, which stood open, as they would stay open, and into the loud ordinary evening of a city learning to live without its oldest lie.

Behind them, on the Reckoning Desk, the fresh ledger lay open to its first page — and near the bottom of that page, in the clerk's neat hand, a new entry had just been written that neither of them had seen:

*Auditor unknown. Claim filed by anonymous party: one debt, origin predating the Guild. Payable to whom, unknown. Term: when the book says so.*

The clerk, reading it back, frowned, and reached for a pen to query it — and found, when she looked up to ask the claimant, that the person who had filed it was already gone.
