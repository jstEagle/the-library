## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

The lamps of Vell's night market came on one by one as the sun went down behind the wall, and Xanthe watched them from the mouth of Tanner's Row like a gambler counting cards.

Lamplight meant the day-merchants packed their scales. Lamplight meant the constables changed shift at the Saltgate and got slow about who they searched. Most of all, lamplight meant people who wanted to forget—or wanted to remember—and had coin enough for either. The charm trade ran on the dark hours the way moths did.

Xanthe set out the tray.

It was a lacquered thing, black as a beetle's back, and on it sat forty-one small glass vials in rows, each stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. To anyone walking past, they looked like perfume samples, or medicine, or nothing at all. That was the point. In the five years since the Edict, *memory-monger* had become a word you could hang for, so the vials were labeled in a private shorthand only buyers learned to read: a pressed violet for first loves. A fishhook for summers by the sea. A plain white bead for griefs, because grief was what most people came for, though few would say it aloud.

"Evening, moth." Old Sefa had her fruit stall two down, and she never used Xanthe's real name in public. Nobody did. "You look like something the tide refused."

"And you look like a woman who'll want a taste of spring before this winter's done." Xanthe smiled the merchant smile—quick, warm, entirely load-bearing. "I've got an orchard in August. First bite of a honeycrisp off your grandfather's tree, if I remember the provenance right."

"You always remember better than we do." Sefa laughed and went back to stacking pears, and Xanthe turned to greet the lamplight crowd, and did not let the smile slip until no one was looking.

Because that was the joke of it, wasn't it. Everyone assumed the thief kept nothing. Sold a memory clean, walked away light. But charms weren't cut like cloth. You couldn't slice a wedding day in half and hand over only the good part. You copied it whole—the way a key copies every tooth of the lock it opens—and then you sold the copy and kept living with the original rattling around in your skull like a stone in a boot.

Forty-one vials. Forty-one other lives. Some nights Xanthe woke up grieving a husband they'd never met. Some nights they flinched at a sound—a whip-crack, a door slamming—that belonged to a soldier dead thirty years whose last sunset Xanthe carried in a vial with a poppy seed sealed under the wax.

The Edict said magic was theft. The Edict was right, in its way. It just had the direction wrong: Xanthe stole, yes, but the kingdom had stolen first. Every soul in Vell was walking around with memories that didn't fit them, wearing them like coats cut for someone else. Xanthe just charged for the alterations.

The first customer came before full dark. A widow, sixty if she was a day, dressed in mourning gray gone soft at the elbows. She stood at the edge of the tray a long time, hands folded, the way people did when they'd rehearsed this in front of a mirror.

"The violet," she said finally.

"For a first love?" Xanthe kept their voice low and kind, pitched under the market noise. "That's a gentle one. I can tell you where it's from, if it helps. A garden in Marrowdown. Apple blossoms. There's a boy in it who is going to say something foolish and mean it completely."

Her chin trembled once and steadied. "My husband's been dead eleven years. I find I'm—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I find I've forgotten what it felt like. Being loved first, before everything. Before children and debts and his cough. I have the fact of it. I don't have the feeling."

Xanthe knew exactly what she meant. They had a dozen borrowed facts in their head without the feelings attached, and worse, feelings without facts, orphaned joys that surfaced at odd hours and made no sense—why should the smell of wet slate make them happy? Whose happiness was that?

"That's the commonest ailment in Vell," they said. "And the one nobody's allowed to name."

"How much?"

For the widow, less than usual. Xanthe quoted a number, she paid without haggling—which told Xanthe everything about how long she'd saved—and they moved together into the alcove behind Sefa's stall, out of the lamplight, where the transaction could happen.

The mechanics were simple and never stopped being strange. Xanthe took her hands. Skin to skin, breath slowed, and they opened the vial between their palms so the wax seal warmed and cracked, and the memory rose up through their own chest first—apple blossoms, a garden in Marrowdown, a girl laughing at a boy who had dirt on his knees and love all over his face—and passed from Xanthe into her like water finding a lower level.

They always felt it leave. Like a rib being gently returned.

The widow gasped. Her eyes went bright and far away, and for three heartbeats she was seventeen in a garden that had burned down before Xanthe was born. Then she blinked, and she was sixty again, but something had been rehung inside her, some picture straightened on a crooked wall.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, he *was* foolish."

"They usually are." Xanthe pocketed the empty vial. "Come back if it fades. First visits are often faint."

She pressed their hand, said something about blessings, and went off into the market walking like a woman twenty years lighter. Xanthe watched her go and felt the hollow where the memory had sat, already beginning to fill with the next thing, the way riverbeds fill after a flood. They kept a ledger in their head of everything sold—not prices, provenance. *One first love, Marrowdown, to a widow of the Weavers' Quarter.* Somebody had to remember where these things came from. It seemed wrong for them to vanish twice.

* * *

Business picked up as the moon rose. A dockhand bought a storm at sea—people were strange about what they craved; he liked the terror, said it was the only time he felt awake. A young clerk bought confidence, which wasn't technically a memory but a distillation of one, a moment when someone had stood up to their father and survived it; Xanthe charged double for those and slept badly after selling them, because courage thinned a little each time it was poured out of the same vial.

Then, near the middle hours, the soldier came.

Xanthe saw him before he reached the tray—the way he walked, weight even, eyes doing the market's exits in order—and knew the trade before he spoke. Soldiers wanted two things: sleep or sunset. Sleep was easier to sell. Sunset was the one that hurt.

He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with a border campaign ribbon sewn inside his collar where it wouldn't draw the ward-keepers' notice. He stood over the tray a long moment, and his gaze went straight past the violets and the fishhooks and the beads, to the single vial at the tray's end with a poppy seed beneath the wax.

"That one," he said.

"I should tell you what it is."

"It's a sunset. Kessel Field." His voice was flat as a road. "The last evening before the third assault. I know because I was there, and mine's worn through. Bought a copy off a man in Harrowgate eight years ago, and it's faded to almost nothing. Just colors now. No—" He hunted for the word. "No *company* in it."

Xanthe's chest did the thing it did sometimes, the painful expansion, the sense of holding too many lives in too small a space. Because they knew the memory intimately. They'd harvested it years ago off a dying man's request—a mercy sale, the only kind Xanthe ever initiated rather than accepted—and the man had asked them to keep it alive, to sell it only to soldiers, so that the evening would go on happening somewhere.

*A last sunset,* the dying sergeant had said. *Not mine. Ours. All of ours. Let them have it.*

"Kessel Field," Xanthe agreed quietly. "The ridge above the river. There's a man playing a reed flute badly and everyone pretending not to mind. Someone passes around plum brandy in a dented canteen. And the light comes down through the smoke like the smoke isn't there at all, like the war has agreed to step outside for the duration."

The soldier's jaw worked. "How much?"

For him, nothing close to fair. Xanthe named a figure low enough to be charity without insulting him, and he paid it, and they stepped into the alcove.

This transfer always cost more than the others. Heavy memories did. Xanthe braced the way you brace to lift a fallen beam, and let the evening move through them: the bad flute, the brandy, two hundred men and women watching the sun go down over ground they'd bleed on by morning, and every one of them, in that hour, perfectly, unbearably alive.

The soldier came out of it with tears cutting lines through the dust on his face, and he didn't wipe them, and he didn't apologize. He just nodded at Xanthe—soldier to soldier, though Xanthe had never held anything but a lockpick—and said, "Whoever gave this up. Thank them for me. If you can."

"I will," Xanthe lied, because the giver was ash in a field grave outside Harrowgate, and the thanks had nowhere to land except in Xanthe's chest, where it joined everything else.

He left. Xanthe stood in the alcove breathing, hands flat against the cold brick, letting the residue settle. This was the part no buyer ever saw: the minutes after a heavy sale, when you were a house someone had moved out of, rooms suddenly too big, echoes everywhere.

*You chose this,* they reminded themselves. *Rent doesn't pay itself. The Edict doesn't repeal itself. Get back out there.*

* * *

The rest of the night passed in the small trades. A homesick cook bought a mother's kitchen. Two lovers split the price of a shared festival memory, which was illegal twice over and sweet besides—they held hands during the transfer so they'd receive it simultaneously, and came out of the alcove looking at each other like the world had been remade, and Xanthe had to turn away and busy themselves with the tray because some nights the tenderness of strangers was more than they could carry on top of everything else.

Sefa brought them tea around the dead hours, no charge. "You're pale, moth."

"I'm always pale."

"Paler." The old woman studied them with fruit-seller shrewdness. "Heavy stock tonight?"

"Heavy-ish."

"Mm." Sefa sipped her own tea. "You know what my mother used to say? A person's a purse. Everything goes in, nothing comes out whole." She tapped Xanthe's tray with one knuckle, careful, respectful. "Careful yours doesn't tear at the seams."

When she'd gone back to her stall, Xanthe turned the thought over. Forty-one vials. Minus tonight's sales, thirty-four. Thirty-four lives riding behind their eyes, plus the uncounted residue that soaked in no matter how clean the technique—the splashes, the drips, the things that clung. They remembered a language they'd never learned, a lullaby in it. They remembered drowning, briefly, and loving it, which belonged to a fisherman's daughter somewhere and arrived every time Xanthe crossed the canal bridges.

Five years of this. Five years since the Edict came down and the ward-keepers started burning charm-sellers in the public squares, and Xanthe had gone underground, gone quiet, gone *small*. Five years of selling strangers their own pasts back by the gram.

It was a living. That was the cynical ledger-line, the merchant's arithmetic, and most nights it held.

Most nights.

Near dawn, when the market was thinning and the lamps burning low, a new customer approached—and Xanthe's merchant instincts lit up like a struck match, because this one was wrong in every particular. Fine boots, city-polished. A coat worth more than Xanthe's entire inventory. Gloves, indoors, at a night market, in summer. And eyes that moved over the tray not with longing but with *inventory*, the way Xanthe themselves looked at a rich man's house.

"Good morning," the stranger said. Voice smooth, accent high-town. "I'm told you deal in... rare recollections."

"I deal in what I deal in." Xanthe kept the smile fixed. "Nothing here's rare. Grief's common as rain. So's joy. It's only the owners who think otherwise."

"A philosopher monger." The stranger's smile didn't reach anywhere near the eyes. "What I want isn't on the tray. I want a commission. A harvest, done discreetly, from a particular household in the Hill Quarter. A noble house. There's a memory inside it I'm told is... remarkable. I'll pay triple your best year."

Every instinct Xanthe owned said *walk away.* Rich clients meant ward-keepers, meant traps, meant the gallows they'd spent five years avoiding. And yet: triple a year's take. Rent on the shop. Food through winter. Maybe enough to buy silence, real silence, a season with no sales at all, no more strangers' deaths rattling in their skull.

"One condition," Xanthe heard themself say. Fool. Hopeful fool. "I inspect the memory before delivery. If it's anything political—if it touches the crown, the Edict, the wards—I walk, and you lose your fee."

Something flickered across the stranger's face. Satisfaction, Xanthe would realize later. The satisfaction of a hook seating itself home.

"Agreed," they said. "The house is called Amberly. Come tomorrow night, and wear something plain."

They left a purse on the tray—heavy, gold, obscene—and walked away toward the Hill Quarter as the sky went gray at the edges.

Xanthe stared after them, then down at the gold, then at the thirty-four remaining vials, each one a life, each one a weight. Somewhere beyond the wall, in the dark countryside, something was already listening. Though Xanthe wouldn't know that for days yet.

All they knew, folding the purse away and packing the tray as the market died around them, was the old familiar tiredness, and under it, faint as a hairline crack in glass, the first cold whisper of trouble coming.

They went home through streets going pale with dawn, carrying everyone's yesterdays, unaware that tomorrow night they'd look into a nobleman's memory and see something looking back.
