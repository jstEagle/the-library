## 2. What the Wards Kept Out

The house of Lord Aldous Vey had eleven locks on its memory-vault door, and Xanthe could have picked ten of them blindfolded.

The eleventh was the interesting one—a ward-seal stamped in wax the color of dried blood, pressed with the sigil of the Crown itself. Which was absurd. Nobles didn't get crown seals on their private vaults unless they were hiding something worth more than their estates, and Aldous Vey hid nothing well except his second wife's debts and his first wife's grave.

Xanthe crouched in the manor's linen closet for three hours before the household settled into its evening rhythms. They'd learned patience the hard way, back when they were young enough to think speed was a virtue. Speed got your hand caught in the trap. Patience let you watch the trap being set and walk around it.

Through the slats of the closet door, Xanthe watched the steward lock up the study, watched the last lamp gutter out, watched the house exhale into sleep. Then they waited another hour, because the best thieves were also the most boring people alive.

The vault was behind a false panel in the study's north wall. The ten mundane locks surrendered in nine minutes. The crown seal took longer—not because it was difficult, but because Xanthe stopped to look at it properly, which was a mistake. You never looked too long at anything with the king's face on it. In Vell, looking was halfway to confessing.

The seal wasn't royal work. Xanthe had seen genuine crown-warding once, five years ago, before the ban—had felt its hum through their fingertips like a struck bell. This thing didn't hum. It *breathed*, faintly, a rhythm too slow to be machinery and too regular to be alive.

They peeled it anyway. Curiosity had always been the knife edge under their ribs, the thing that kept them moving when caution said sit still.

Inside: a single memory-charm, unmarked, wrapped in silk gone yellow with age.

Xanthe turned it over twice before understanding what it was. Charms came off the street by the hundred—grief pressed into glass, joy bottled for lonely widows, the whole gray economy of forgetting that Vell pretended not to see. But this charm was old craft. Pre-ban. The glass had been blown by someone who loved the work; you could see it in the swirl at the base, like a fingerprint left in honey.

Someone had paid a fortune to keep this one memory, then hidden it behind ten locks and a false king's seal.

Xanthe should have put it back. That was the rule: steal what you can carry, sell what you can explain, touch nothing you can't name. But their thumb found the seam of the charm the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth, and the harvest came easy—too easy, the way water comes easy when you're already drowning.

They pressed the glass to their temple and slipped inside a dead man's afternoon.

* * *

The memory opened onto sunlight.

Not Vell sunlight, which came down filtered and apologetic through smelter haze. Real sun, high and white, pouring over a courtyard of pale stone. A procession. Banners. The crowd pressing close and laughing, because it was a coronation day or a festival or some other occasion when the city pretended the walls were celebration rather than siege.

And there, at the center of it all, the king.

Not the current king—the old one, the father, the one whose portrait still hung in taverns where the inquisitors didn't look. He was younger than his coins made him seem. He waved. He smiled. The crowd roared its practiced roar.

Xanthe drifted along the edges of the memory, cataloguing as they went. Harvest-work was mostly observation: find the emotional core, the moment the memory curved toward feeling, and cut there. Everything else was packaging. This one's core would be the crowd's joy, probably, the collective lift of a city in love with itself—

The king turned.

He looked directly out of the memory, directly at the point where Xanthe floated, and for one impossible instant Xanthe forgot that memories couldn't see you. That was the first wrongness: the sense of being *regarded*. The second wrongness was the eyes themselves.

Behind the king's eyes, deep in the wet dark where no light should live, something green flickered.

Not reflected green—not banner-light, not the flash of a standard. This glow came from *inside*, from somewhere behind the pupils, patient and vegetal and slow, like lanterns hung far down a garden path at midnight. Like something growing in the dark and liking the dark.

The smile didn't change. That was the worst part. The king went on smiling at the crowd with that green light swimming behind his face, and the crowd roared, and nobody saw, and the memory—

The memory *leaned toward them.*

Xanthe had harvested hundreds of charms. Memories were inert things, recordings, amber with a fly in it. They did not lean. They did not notice. But this one gathered itself around Xanthe like a tide pulling back to show what lay underneath, and beneath the coronation-day surface, Xanthe glimpsed roots. Vast ones. Threaded through the memory's foundations, through the stones of the courtyard, through the bodies of the cheering crowd, a lattice of pale filaments all running inward, all converging on the throne, all feeding into that slow green burn behind the king's eyes.

Then a voice that was not a voice said—or rather grew, the way moss grows, so gradually that Xanthe only understood it had spoken after it finished:

*You are early.*

Xanthe tore free.

They came back to their body kneeling on the study floor with blood in their mouth—they'd bitten their tongue—and the charm shattered in their grip, hairline cracks spidering through glass that should have held for a century more. Sap-smell filled the room, green and sweet, the smell of a greenhouse at night.

Outside the window, Vell glittered its ordinary glitter. Somewhere a drunk sang. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing.

Xanthe sat very still until their hands stopped shaking, which took longer than they wanted to admit even to themselves.

Then they did the professional thing. They swept up every shard of glass, wrapped the fragments in the yellowed silk, tucked the bundle into their coat, checked the locks, closed the panel, and left the house exactly as they'd found it—except emptier by one secret, and heavier by about a thousand questions.

* * *

The night market was winding down when Xanthe got back to the Tallow District, stalls folding like tired flowers, lamplighters working their rounds with the weary choreography of men who'd done it ten thousand times. Xanthe's own stall was a fold-down table in an alley mouth, a strip of velvet, and a locked case with room for twenty charms. Tonight it held six. Business was thin. Fear was thick. When people are afraid, they hold their memories tighter, like coins in a fist.

"Late night," said the pie-seller across the alley, who was also the district's best listener.

"Good night," Xanthe lied, and set out the velvet.

The first customer came within the hour: a dockhand wanting to sell the memory of his brother's funeral. Grief-work, clean and quick. Xanthe gave him fair coin and a cup of the cheap wine they kept for the freshly hollowed, and watched him walk away lighter and slightly less himself, already forgetting why his chest felt so spacious.

That was the trade. Everyone hated it and everyone used it. Five years since the kingdom outlawed memory-craft, and the trade hadn't died—it had just gone dark, gone small, gone into alley mouths like this one. The ban hadn't stopped people from needing to forget. It had just made forgetting expensive.

Xanthe was weighing a widow's charm—a wedding day, forty years old, worn smooth from handling—when they noticed the boots.

Ward-keeper boots. Grey leather, government issue, standing at the mouth of the alley in a formation of four.

"Proprietor." The lead keeper had a voice like a ledger closing. "City business."

Xanthe kept their hands visible and their face bored, the twin disciplines of survival. "Market's closing, officer."

"We're not shopping." He produced a paper, though he didn't offer it. "By order of the Wardens' Circle, all practitioners of unsanctioned mnemonic arts are summoned for questioning. Voluntary cooperation is noted favorably."

"And involuntary?"

"Involuntary cooperation is also noted." He smiled without warmth. "There's been an incident at the border wards. We're asking everyone with... talents... whether they've sensed anything unusual."

*Anything unusual.* Xanthe thought of green light behind a king's eyes, of roots threading a crowd, of a voice that grew rather than spoke. Their heartbeat stayed level through long practice, but something cold slid down their spine and pooled there.

"What kind of unusual?" they asked, mild as milk.

"That's what we're asking you." The keeper's eyes moved over the stall—the velvet, the case, the six charms glinting like dull stars. "You'll come to the Wardenhouse tomorrow, midbell. Or we'll come to you tonight. Your choice."

"I'll come tomorrow."

"Wise." He tapped the stall once, a gesture that managed to be both casual and proprietary. "Interesting trade you have here. Memory-mongering. Dangerous business, since the ban. You know what happens to convicted charm-sellers."

"They fine you and confiscate your stock," Xanthe said. "I've read the statute."

"The statute changed last month." He let that sit. Then, turning to go: "One more question, proprietor. Purely informal. Have you ever handled a charm older than the ban? Something from the palace era?"

The cold pool at Xanthe's spine turned to ice.

"No," they said. "Old charms don't circulate. They're all destroyed or hoarded."

"Mm." He glanced back, and for a half-second his ledger voice cracked open and something else showed through—something that looked, absurdly, like fear. "If one crosses your table, bring it to us. Don't harvest it. Don't sell it. Bring it." He paused. "There are things in the old stock that aren't memories. Or weren't, originally."

Then the grey boots were gone, all four pairs, swallowed by the market's closing noise.

Xanthe stood alone in the alley with their heart hammering against their ribs like something trying to escape. Slowly, deliberately, they touched the bundle in their coat where the shattered charm's fragments slept in their silk wrapping. Through the fabric, faintly, they could feel it.

Breathing.

That slow, patient, vegetal rhythm, keeping time with nothing, waiting for nothing, growing.

Across the alley, the pie-seller called something cheerful about closing up. Xanthe answered on autopilot, packed the stall with hands that remembered how, and walked home the long way, checking reflections in darkened windows, counting the footsteps behind them twice because the first count came out wrong.

Tomorrow they were supposed to appear at the Wardenhouse and answer questions. Tomorrow, if they were smart, they'd burn the fragments in the forge and swear they'd never touched old stock in their life.

But lying awake in their rented room above the dyer's shop, listening to the city settle, Xanthe kept seeing it: the king's smile, unchanged, while something green swam behind his eyes and knew their name without being told it.

*You are early,* it had said.

Which meant it had been expecting someone.

And meant—this was the thought that finally dragged Xanthe out of bed at the dead hour of the morning, to stand at the window with the shards wrapped safe against their chest—it meant that whatever lived behind the crown's eyes had plans, and schedules, and patience measured in decades.

It meant the game had started years ago, and Xanthe had only just now heard the opening move.

Down in the street below, a figure in grey stood beneath the lamp at the corner, looking up at the dyer's shop windows, one by one, unhurried, as if taking inventory.

It moved on. Eventually.

Xanthe didn't sleep again.
