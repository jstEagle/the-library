## 3. Ilsa of the Broken Circle

The wards failed the way old men die: slowly, then all at once, and always in the middle of the night.

Ilsa Vane had been walking the border circuit since dusk, lantern swinging from her left hand, a tuning fork of cold iron in her right. Every hundred paces she stopped, struck the fork against her thumb, and held it up to the dark. The fork was supposed to hum. A healthy ward answered iron the way a struck bell answers its twin—a clean rising note that you felt in your teeth before you heard it with your ears.

Tonight the note came back wrong. Flat. Muffled. Like shouting down a well.

She marked the pace-stone with chalk—*third flat reading at mile forty*—and kept walking.

This was not her circuit anymore. That was the joke of it, the bitter little punchline she swallowed every evening when she buckled on her boots. Ilsa Vane had designed this circuit. Had walked it for eleven years as Warden-Prime of the Western Ring, had known every stone and sigil along it the way a mother knows the sound of her own child crying in another room. Then the inquiry had come, and the tribunal, and the word *negligent* spoken by men who could not have drawn a ward-lattice if their lives depended on it, and now she walked the same ground as a hired contractor on a maintenance contract, checking other people's work for coin paid through an intermediary who never met her eyes.

Her replacement, Warden-Captain Doss, was twenty-six years old and believed everything the manuals said. The manuals said the border wards were sound. The manuals had been written ninety years ago, and no one had updated them because no one had needed to, because the wards *were* sound, because they had been built by the last generation of true ward-masters and maintained ever since by people like her—

Except they weren't sound. They hadn't been sound for two years. And she had tried to say so, once, loudly, in front of the wrong people, and it had cost her everything except the work itself, which was the one thing she couldn't stop doing even now.

She struck the fork again at mile forty-one. The answer was flatter still.

Ilsa stopped. Set the lantern down on the pace-stone. Breathed the cold air in through her nose and let it out slow, the way she did when her hands wanted to shake, because a warden's hands did not shake, not ever, not even a disgraced one, not especially a disgraced one whose entire remaining argument with the world was the quality of her craft.

Then she knelt and pressed her bare palm flat against the boundary stone.

Ward-work was mostly patience and arithmetic, but underneath the arithmetic there was listening, and listening required skin. Through her palm she felt the lattice: the deep hum of the anchor-stones, the interlocking harmonics of the ring-wards running north toward the mountains and south toward the marshes, all of it steady, all of it familiar, all of it—

All of it *thinner* than it should be.

Not broken. Not cracked, not yet, not anywhere she could put a finger or a fork. But thin, the way cloth goes thin before it wears through, the way ice goes thin over a river in late winter while still looking perfectly white and solid from the bank. Something was drawing on the wards. Drinking from them. Slowly, patiently, in amounts so small that any single night's loss would read as normal fluctuation to anyone who wasn't looking for it.

Anyone who wasn't her.

"Two years," she said aloud, to the stone, to the dark fields beyond it. "Two years I've been telling them."

The stone said nothing. Stones were good like that.

She got up, retrieved her lantern, and made a decision that would have gotten her arrested five years ago and might still get her arrested tonight: she stepped off the maintenance path and walked out into the unlit grass toward the ward-line proper.

* * *

There is a difference between patrolling a border and touching one, and the difference is the difference between reading about drowning and being underwater.

From the maintenance path, the border wards were invisible—that was the point of them, that was the elegance of the old masters' work. You walked your circuit, you struck your fork, you logged your readings, and the great woven thing above and around and beneath you went about its business of standing between the kingdom and whatever stood outside the kingdom, unseen and unseeing.

But walk ten paces past the last pace-stone, into the strip of land where the wards anchored into the world instead of merely passing over it, and you entered the working. The air changed texture. Sound went slightly strange, flattened at the edges, as though the whole landscape were holding its breath. And if you had the knack—if you'd been born with whatever it was the old books called *hearing the wall*—you didn't just feel the lattice through your palm anymore.

You heard it. All of it. A chord sustained without pause for ninety years, played by stones and sigils and the buried bones of the first ward-trees.

Ilsa stood inside it and listened, and what she heard made her cold all the way down to the place where cold doesn't come back from.

The chord was sour. Not everywhere—no, that was the terrible part, the part that would have let her sleep if she were a worse warden or a better liar. Most of the chord was still true. Nine parts in ten of the great ring still sang exactly as designed. But threaded through the harmony, here and there and *growing*, were notes that did not belong. Low notes. Green notes. She had no better word for them and hated herself for needing one: a sound like sap moving under bark, like roots prying apart masonry, like a seed cracking open in soil too dark to see.

Vegetal harmonics. In a ward-lattice. In *her* lattice.

Magic didn't do this. Magic had rules, and the rules were the reason she'd given thirty years of her life to them: iron answered iron, fire answered fire, and nothing living answered the dead geometry of a ward-wall, because wards were built of will and stone and the refusal of things to change, and green things were change itself, growing, splitting, climbing—

A hairline crack sang under her right boot.

She froze. Looked down. The grass here grew in a seam between two anchor-stones, ordinary winter-brown grass, and she almost moved on—but the sound came again, faint as a plucked thread, and Ilsa Vane had spent thirty years learning the difference between a ward's voice and everything else, and that was a ward's voice. Or what was left of one.

She set the lantern down and parted the grass with both hands.

Between the anchor-stones, running diagonally across the face of the western stone like a scratch from a cat's claw, was a crack. Hairline. Barely visible. Any inspector in the kingdom would have called it frost damage and passed on.

But frost damage didn't sing. And frost damage didn't *move*.

She watched it happen—watched the crack breathe. Widened a hair, narrowed a hair, widened again, in a rhythm slow and patient and horribly organic, like the gills of something underwater. And from the crack, so faint she had to press her ear nearly to the stone to catch it, rose the green note. Unmistakable now. Alive.

Ilsa sat back on her heels. Her breath fogged the air. Somewhere behind her, miles east, the lights of Vell glowed against the low clouds, warm and certain and asleep.

"Hello," she said quietly, to the crack, because thirty years of ward-craft taught you that you addressed anomalies directly; naming them was half of binding them. "What are you?"

The crack pulsed. Once. Twice. And then—this she would swear to, later, under questioning, under anything—the green note resolved. Stopped being noise. Became, for three heartbeats, something with shape and intent and *direction*, a sound leaning toward her the way a person leans toward a door they mean to open.

Then it stopped. And in the silence after it, Ilsa became aware of a second sound, very near, that had been there the whole time: a whisper of air through a small hole. Wind through a keyhole. Except there was no wind, and no keyhole, and the whisper was coming from the crack itself—from *through* the crack—from the other side of a wall that was supposed to have no other side.

Something outside the wards was breathing inward.

* * *

She worked for two hours by lantern light, and the work was the only prayer she had ever believed in.

First she mapped the crack—its length, its depth-sound, its breathing rhythm—recording everything in the shorthand of her old notebooks, the ones the tribunal had confiscated and returned with the pages about the thinning cut out. Then she tested the surrounding anchors. Both were sound. Whatever had opened the crack had done it from outside, patiently, the way water opens stone—not by force but by finding the one flaw and worrying it, night after night, year after year.

Two years. The thinning had started two years ago. She'd reported it and been told the instruments needed recalibration. She'd recalibrated the instruments herself and been told her methods were outdated. She'd asked to inspect the outer faces of the anchor-stones and been informed that exterior inspection required a writ signed by the Warden-General, and when the writ finally came through, eleven months later, it specified a different section of the border entirely.

She had thought, then, that it was bureaucratic spite. Petty punishment of the woman who had embarrassed her superiors. It had never occurred to her—it genuinely had never occurred to her—that someone might *want* the wards thin.

She took out her kit: chalk, iron filings, a spool of silver wire, and the small hammer-faced chisel no warden was supposed to carry off the training grounds. Repairing a ward-crack without authorization was a misdemeanor. Repairing one *badly* was a catastrophe. She knew the risks the way you know the location of your own heart, and she did the work anyway, kneeling in frozen grass at two in the morning, because the alternative was walking away and she had discovered, somewhere in the five years since the tribunal, that she was constitutionally incapable of walking away.

Silver wire first, laid into the crack's length, humming as it touched the stone—she felt the lattice grab at it, hungry, the way a starving body grabs at food. Then iron filings tamped and packed, then chalk sigils in the old style, the pre-manual style, the style the tribunal had called *idiosyncratic* and *insufficiently standardized*. Let them standardize this. The old style worked because it was a conversation rather than a command: you asked the stone to hold, you offered it your hand to hold with, and the stone, which had been holding for ninety years and was tired, accepted.

The crack fought her.

That was the thing she would remember most clearly afterward, sitting later over cold tea, hands still faintly trembling: the crack *fought her*. Not the stone—the stone was grateful, she could feel it drinking the repair like rain on drought-ground. The crack itself, the thin green line of not-this-world wedged between two honest anchors, resisted the wire with a pressure she could only describe as *opinion*. As though it resented her. As though it had plans.

She finished anyway. She always finished. The sigils closed over the crack like a bandage, the green note dropped away beneath the restored harmony, and the whisper of breathing-through-a-keyhole faded until she could almost pretend she'd imagined it.

Almost.

It was as she was packing her kit, wiping chalk from her fingers, that she saw the message.

* * *

It was carved into the inner face of the southern anchor-stone, low, near the ground, in a place where grass would hide it from anyone standing and shadow would hide it from anyone kneeling unless they turned the lantern just so—which she did, reaching back to shift the light, and the stone's rough surface threw up a scatter of fine lines that were not weathering.

Ilsa went still.

She knew the script. Every warden of her generation had been taught to recognize it and forbidden to use it: the old royal cipher, the hand of the last true king's own ward-wrights, the men and women who had raised the great rings ninety years ago. The cipher wasn't secret anymore, exactly—it was in the archives, it was in her old textbooks, it was carved into half the foundation stones of the capital. But it had fallen out of use the moment the crown stopped trusting anyone outside the palace walls, and no one had written anything new in it for longer than Ilsa had been alive.

Which meant either some scholar had come out here, in the dead of night, to carve antiquarian graffiti on a border anchor-stone—

Or the message was old. Older than the tribunal. Older than her career. Carved into this stone when it was laid, or shortly after, by someone who had known that someday a warden with the knack and the stubbornness to kneel in the grass at the exact wrong spot would find it.

The cipher was elegant, compact, and cruelly difficult—designed to defeat casual reading even by those who knew the letterforms, because each character's value shifted according to the harmonic context of the stone it sat on. You couldn't just translate it. You had to *listen* to it. She pressed her palm to the carving, closed her eyes, and read it the old way, letting the stone speak each character's true value through her skin, feeling her way along like a blind woman tracing letters cut deep enough to hurt.

It took her the better part of an hour. The cold worked into her knees and her back and she ignored it. Twice the green note flickered somewhere far off in the lattice, testing the edges of her repair, and she ignored that too, head down, lips moving, translating.

When she finished, she sat back and looked at the words she had traced in her notebook in plain modern letters, and the night seemed to lean closer around her lantern.

The message read:

*TO THE KEEPER WHO FINDS THIS: THE WALL WAS BUILT AGAINST NOTHING THAT WALKS. WHAT COMES FOR THE WALL GROWS. IT IS ALREADY INSIDE. IT WEARS THE CROWN AND CALLS ITSELF THE GARDEN. COUNT THE THIN PLACES. COUNT THE YEARS. WHEN THE STARS GO OUT, GO TO VELL AND FIND THE ONES WHO REMEMBER WHAT THE KING FORGOT.*

Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought, in a shakier hand:

*I am sorry. We had no time and less choice. Do not trust the palace. Trust the thieves. —A.W.*

Ilsa read it four times. The fourth time was not for comprehension. It was because her eyes kept snagging on the same phrase, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*Trust the thieves.*

She laughed once, short and disbelieving, and the laugh steamed in the cold air and died. Of all the sentences in all the languages she knew, that was the one the old wright had chosen. Trust the thieves. The city's memory-mongers, charm-hawkers, the bottom-feeders of the night market whom her own order had spent decades suppressing, licensing, squeezing—the people she had helped arrest, back when she'd had a badge and the authority to use it.

And beneath the absurdity of it, cold and clear, the rest of it arranged itself into a shape she recognized from her worst professional nightmares: a pattern. The thinning, two years of it, denied and deflected at every level above her. A parasite that wore the crown. An order that had buried a warning carved by its own founders. *Do not trust the palace.* She thought of the writ that had taken eleven months and pointed the wrong way. She thought of the tribunal, and the word *negligent*, and how quickly, how smoothly, how *gratefully* the hierarchy had seized on a pretext to remove the one warden who kept reporting that the numbers didn't add up.

They hadn't punished her for negligence. They had removed her for *noticing*.

Far to the east, the glow of Vell lay soft against the clouds. Somewhere in that city, in the markets and the cisterns and the crooked streets below the respectable ones, were the people the message named. Thieves. Rememberers. People who dealt in the one currency the palace had spent a century quietly hoarding.

Ilsa Vane folded her notebook closed, tucked it inside her coat next to her heart, and looked west, past the repaired crack, past the invisible wall, out into the darkness beyond the border where something patient and green was waiting for its next thin place.

Above her, high and faint, the stars were burning exactly as they had burned every night of her life.

She gave them one long look—the kind of look you give something you are about to lose—and picked up her lantern, and started walking toward Vell.
