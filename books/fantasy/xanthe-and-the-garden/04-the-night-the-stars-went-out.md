## 4. The Night the Stars Went Out

*Xanthe*

The last charm of the night sold itself.

That was how Xanthe thought of it, anyway—the good ones practically leapt from the tray. A ferryman's daughter with ink-stained fingers held the vial up to the lamplight and watched the memory inside swirl like smoke caught in glass: a soldier's last sunset, all amber and salt wind, the kind of evening that made you believe the world had been built for exactly this.

"How much?" she asked.

"Twelve coppers." Xanthe kept their voice bored, merchant-flat, while inside they did the arithmetic of what it would cost them to carry another stranger's death around for a week. "It fades in three days if you don't wear it against the skin."

She counted out the coins one by one, savoring the transaction, and Xanthe let her. That was part of the product too—the ceremony of buying a life secondhand. In Vell's night market, under strings of paper lanterns and the smell of fried onions and tallow, people didn't want magic. They wanted permission to feel something they hadn't earned.

The lanterns guttered.

All of them. Not one after another, not a draft snaking through the market stalls, but every flame on the street dipping at once, like the whole city had taken a breath and forgotten to let it out.

Xanthe's hand went to the tray before their mind caught up, covering the remaining charms—three vials, each holding someone's stolen hour—as if the darkness might shoplift them.

Then the light came back, and Xanthe looked up, and the sky was wrong.

There had been clouds earlier, thin high ones, the kind that smudge the stars without erasing them. Those were gone. The sky above Vell was black and clean as fresh ink, and there was nothing in it. No stars. Not dimmed, not hidden—*absent*, the way a pulled tooth is absent. The eye kept sliding to where the constellations should have been, the Shepherd's Yoke, the Weeping Sisters, and finding only smooth dark, like a page scraped blank.

Around Xanthe, the market went quiet in stages. First the haggling stopped. Then the babies stopped crying, which was worse. Then a man near the onion cart said, very reasonably, as though reporting a lost dog, "Where'd they go?"

And then everyone started talking at once, and no one said anything true.

Xanthe stood behind their little table with its velvet cloth and felt the charms shiver against their palm.

Not metaphorically. The glass was trembling, faint and constant, like something inside the vials had woken up and was pressing at the walls. Three memories—a widow's wedding day, a baker's first snowfall, the soldier's sunset, minus the piece now living in the ferryman's daughter's pocket—were doing something memories were not supposed to do.

They were whispering.

Xanthe had carried fragments of other lives for five years. They knew the texture of them the way a seamstress knows thread: the widow's day was warm and gold-edged and smelled of lilies; the baker's snowfall crackled; the sunset ached. They sat in the back of Xanthe's mind like lodgers, quiet, mostly, occasionally murmuring when Xanthe slept.

Now they weren't murmuring. Now they were *talking to each other*.

*Did you see it,* said the wedding day, in the widow's voice, except the widow hadn't seen anything, because the widow's memory predated the sky going dark by forty years. *Did you see it go?*

*It didn't go,* said the snowfall, in a child's bright gasp. *It was taken.*

*It was called home,* said the sunset, and that one hurt to hear, because the soldier had died believing the sky loved him back.

"Hey." The ferryman's daughter was still standing there, coins clutched, face tilted up. "Is this—is this a thing that happens? Do the lights do this?"

"No," Xanthe said. Their voice came out steady, which took work. "No, it is very much not a thing that happens."

Somewhere down the market row, a temple bell began to ring. Not the hour bell—the alarm, the cracked old thing that hadn't sounded since the plague year, dragged by rope by some priest who'd decided the end times deserved notice. Its clang rolled over the rooftops, and the crowd's murmur sharpened into motion, stalls collapsing, coins vanishing, everyone suddenly needing to be somewhere else, preferably indoors, preferably drunk.

Xanthe packed the tray in eight seconds flat, an old reflex, and pressed the three trembling vials into a padded box, and then made the mistake of looking at the sky again.

Because the stars hadn't just gone out. As Xanthe watched, a single point of light rekindled—far north, low on the horizon, green as a beetle's back—and hung there for three heartbeats before it, too, went dark.

Like a lamp being carried away down a long corridor.

*Come and find us,* whispered the memories, all together now, in unison, in a voice that belonged to none of the people they'd been harvested from. *We remember the way.*

Xanthe slammed the box shut and went home through streets full of shouting, and did not sleep.

*Ilsa*

Ilsa Vane was on the wall when the stars went out, which meant she was the first person in Vell to understand what it meant, and possibly the last person anyone would believe.

She'd felt the wards dying for weeks. That was the shame she lived inside: she could feel them the way you feel a rotting tooth, a low sweet ache along the jaw of the world. The border wards ran in a ring two hundred miles around the city and its sister towns—Ilsa had helped lay the northern arc herself, eleven years ago, kneeling in frozen mud with her hands full of silvered stone and her heart full of certainty. Ward-keeping was supposed to be permanent work. You built the circle, you sang it into the ground, and then you spent your career listening to it hum.

For the past year, the hum had been souring.

Tonight she'd climbed to her old post on the Wallwatch tower because the ache had turned to screaming, and because nobody else would come. She'd been dismissed from the Order four years ago—for drunkenness, officially, though the truth was more complicated and less flattering—and disgraced ward-keepers did not get to file reports. They got to drink cheap gin on cold towers and listen to the world's teeth rot.

At the stroke of midnight, the hum stopped.

Ilsa had spent eleven years inside that sound. It was the sound of safety, of the kingdom's whole architecture of *inside* and *outside*, and when it cut off, the silence hit her like water hitting a burn. She gripped the parapet. Below her, Vell glittered, oblivious. Above her—

Above her, the sky peeled open.

That was the only word for it. The stars didn't flicker or fade; they were erased, constellation by constellation, as though something enormous and patient were working down a list. Ilsa heard herself make a sound, a small involuntary syllable, and was grateful no one was there to hear it. Then the last star went, and the darkness above wasn't empty the way night is empty. It was occupied. It was the darkness of a closed eyelid, of soil, of a mouth.

And beneath her feet, the ward-line sang one broken note and shattered.

She felt it go the way you feel a bone snap—not in the ear but in the body, a sickening displacement, the border between *here* and *not-here* simply ceasing to hold any meaning. Eleven years of silvered stone and careful song, undone in a heartbeat. And threaded through the breaking, unmistakable, was a smell.

Green things. Crushed leaves. Sap.

Ilsa had tended the wards for a decade. Wards smelled of ozone and hot metal, of lightning kept politely outside. They did not smell of gardens.

She stayed on the tower until dawn refused to arrive properly—the east stayed bruised and gray, the sun coming up like something reluctant, like a candle burning behind cloth. By then the city below had worked itself into a fever of rumor: stars stolen, stars never real, the gods blinking, the king dead, the king triumphant. She heard three separate strangers explain the phenomenon with total confidence, and all three explanations required a universe smaller than the one she'd watched break.

What she couldn't shake was the message.

Because there *was* a message. An hour before midnight, walking the wall's inner circuit, Ilsa had found it—or rather, found it again; she'd walked past it twice already without seeing, which was itself impossible, because ward-keepers were trained to see marks. Carved into the underside of the parapet stone, in old royal cipher, the cipher that hadn't been used since before the Regency: a short line of characters, and beneath them, a drawing.

A crown. With roots growing down out of it, into whatever lay beneath the stone.

The characters read: *IT WAS NEVER THE KING'S GARDEN.*

She had copied it onto her cuff in chalk, hands shaking, and told herself she'd turn it in to the Order in the morning, and known even then that she wouldn't. The Order had buried her. Let them dig this up themselves.

Except the Order's tower, when she finally descended toward it at gray dawn, had its doors barred, and men in the usurper's gray-and-green were posted outside with the flat, patient faces of people who have been ordered to wait a long time.

Ilsa turned around and walked back into the waking, panicking city, with a cipher on her sleeve and a dead hum in her bones, and started doing the one thing she still knew how to do.

Listening to the ground.

*Xanthe*

By midmorning, Vell had divided itself into three kinds of people: those praying, those looting, and those selling.

Xanthe was professionally obliged to admire the third group. Within hours of the sky going dark, the night market's daytime cousin had sprouted along the Tanners' Row—hawkers offering star-charms, anti-darkness candles, bottled pieces of yesterday's sky at scandalous prices. One enterprising boy was selling jars of ordinary river water labeled STARBLOOD, and doing brisk trade.

Xanthe set up nothing. Xanthe sat in their room above the dye-shop with the door barred and the box of charms on the table, watching it.

The whispering hadn't stopped. If anything, daylight had emboldened it. The three memories had stopped speaking in their borrowed voices sometime before dawn and settled into a single low chorus underneath Xanthe's hearing, the way you can hear blood in your ears if the room is quiet enough. When Xanthe lifted a vial, the chorus leaned toward it. When Xanthe set it down, the chorus receded, sulking.

Memories wanted to return to their owners. That was natural—that was why the trade worked, why buyers felt so briefly and beautifully whole. But this wasn't homesickness. This was orientation. North, the chorus insisted. Up. Through the walls. *Home* had changed its address overnight, and the new address was somewhere past the border, past everything, wherever the green light had gone.

"You are objects," Xanthe told them, aloud, because five years of solitary crime makes you talk to your inventory. "You are merchandise. You do not get opinions."

The widow's wedding day pulsed once in its vial, warm and reproachful.

Fine. Fine. There was a protocol for restless stock: ground them in the owner's context, remind them whose they were. Xanthe uncorked the widow's vial and let a thread of the memory rise—just a thread, just enough to look at—and braced for lilies and gold light.

What rose instead was the wedding, yes, but wrong. The bride's face had gone smooth and slightly green-tinged, like waxwork, and behind her, where the chapel window should have shown sky, there was only that same deep occupied black. And the groom turned his head—slowly, slowly, in the way of nightmares—and he had no eyes at all, just two points of soft green light, and he smiled with enormous kindness, and said:

*You've been keeping them safe for us. Thank you, little thief.*

Xanthe dropped the vial. It rolled, unbroken, blessedly unbroken, and the memory sank back down like something diving.

Their hands were shaking badly enough that they had to press them flat against the table. Five years. Five years of harvesting strangers' joy and grief, five years of never once touching anything that touched back, and now their own merchandise was *answering*. Worse—it knew them. It had called them by name, by trade, by the exact shape of their guilt. Little thief. Something on the far side of a dead sky had reached through a widow's wedding day to say thank you.

Outside, a scream started up two streets over, then another, then a whole chorus of them, and the bells began again—all the bells, every temple and clocktower in Vell, ringing in no pattern at all.

Xanthe went to the window.

The crowd was running south, toward the River Gate, which was the wrong direction for a fire and the wrong direction for a riot. Xanthe craned, caught scraps: *"—the gate—" "*—it's open, the wards are down, the gate's just standing open—" "*—something's coming up the road, something *growing*—"

Growing. On the north road. Where, as far as Xanthe knew, there was nothing but the border march and two hundred miles of winter fields.

The chorus in the box rose to meet the bells, and for one clear moment it wasn't whispering at all. It was singing, three stolen voices in perfect harmony, a song Xanthe had never learned and somehow knew every note of—a planting song, a sowing song, old as seed catalogs and graveyards.

And underneath it, fainter, threaded through it like wire through wool, came a fourth voice. Not from the box. From inside Xanthe's own chest, from the hollow places where five years ago something had been cut out and sold, though Xanthe had never known what, never questioned the gaps, the fog where a past should have been—

The fourth voice said, gently, in a voice Xanthe almost recognized:

*Xanthe. Come home.*

The box burst open. All three vials stood upright on the table, unbroken, glowing faintly green, pointing north like compass needles.

Xanthe grabbed their coat, their coin purse, and the box, and did the smartest thing they had ever done in their life, which was run—not north, not toward the voice, but down, down toward the oldest part of the city, because if the rules of the world were breaking, there was exactly one person in Vell who collected broken rules and filed them alphabetically.

The cisterns under Tanner's Hill were flooded, illegal, and haunted by a rumor with ink-stained fingers.

Xanthe went to find out if the rumor could read.
