## 5. Refugees and Rumors

The dead stars had stopped being news by morning. What replaced them was worse.

I was at the charm stall before dawn—habit older than sense—and I watched Vell's East Gate open to let in a river of people who should not have existed. The border wards had fallen two nights ago. Whatever they'd been holding back was now walking down the King's Road on foot, and it was coming here.

They came in farm carts and on lame horses and carrying children who were too quiet. They came with the particular silence of people who have seen something and cannot decide whether telling it will make it more or less true. And every one of them, as they filed past my stall into the city, was looking over their shoulder at the horizon behind them like it owed them money.

"You selling?" asked a woman with mud to her knees and a shawl that had once been fine.

"Charms," I said. "Memories. Good ones."

She stared at me for a long moment, and then she laughed—a cracked, terrible sound. "Memories." She said it like the word itself had betrayed her. "Do you know what I'd give to forget what I saw?"

*That,* I thought, "is literally my business model."

But I didn't say it, because her hands were shaking, and because behind her the line of refugees stretched back past the granary, past the tanner's row, all the way out of sight, and because the sky above them was still black where stars used to be.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a memory-thief in a walled city: you become an archivist of other people's disasters. Five years of harvesting charms means five years of carrying fragments that aren't mine—a fisherman's drowning brother, a bride's mother's face, a soldier's last sunset over a field he'd never see again. I kept them organized. Labeled. Priced.

And some of them, lately, had started talking to each other. Which is not something memories are supposed to do.

So when the rumors started moving through the refugee crowd faster than the refugees themselves, I listened harder than I wanted to.

"It bloomed overnight," a man told the water-seller near the gate. "The whole valley. Flowers everywhere, right through the frost. You could watch them open."

"There was no fire," a girl said to no one, to everyone. "There was no burning. The village just—" she made a gesture like something unfurling, petals from a fist. "It went green."

"The church bell was ringing when we left," an old man insisted, "but there was nobody in the tower. There were vines in the tower. The vines were ringing the bell."

I heard forty versions before noon. In most of them the details contradicted each other—the color of the flowers, the direction of the spreading, whether the hedges moved before or after the screaming. That's how panic works; it grinds truth into rumor-grit.

But one detail showed up in every single telling, and it was the detail that made me stop pricing charms and start doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

*Flowers where villages stood.*

Because I had heard that before. Exactly that, in those words, five years ago, in a memory I never sold.

* * *

It was in my third year of the trade. A harvest job, routine: an old woman in Salt Row dying slow, willing to sell her best decade to leave her grandchildren something better than debts. Her name was Maren and her mind was a house with all the doors open. You're not supposed to take everything from a dying seller—it's considered poor form—but I was hungry that winter and she offered, so I took more than I should have, the way thieves do.

Most of it was ordinary gold. First love. A wedding. A son's face.

But underneath, at the bottom of her like a stone in a well, there was a memory she hadn't lived.

I know the difference. That's the whole craft—you learn the grain of a memory, whether it belongs to the head you're taking it from. Hers didn't. It sat in her the way a splinter sits in flesh: grown around, never healed. And it was vivid in a way real memories never are. Real memories blur at the edges. This one was sharp as a woodcut print.

In it, I stood—I stood, in the memory, in someone else's eyes—on a hill above a village at dusk in high summer. Except it wasn't high summer. Frost silvered the fence posts. Every rooftop wore rime. And below, where the village stood, there were flowers. Not scattered blooms. A tide of them, red and white and a yellow so deep it looked bruised, filling doorways, climbing chimneys, pouring from windows like the houses had drowned in blossom. The bell tower was green with ivy, and the ivy was moving, gently, the way a sleeper moves.

And in the middle of the square, where a market cross should have been, something tall stood among the flowers. I can't tell you what it looked like. That's the part of the memory that was worn away—not blurred, *removed*, cleanly, like a name scratched off a ledger. Just an impression of height, and patience, and a smell like honey and turned earth.

Whoever had owned this memory first had looked at that thing, and looked away, and run, and eventually carried the splinter of it into Maren's dying mind, and from her into mine.

I never sold it. Some instincts are louder than hunger. I buried it deep in my stock and told myself it was a madwoman's bad dream, and for five years it stayed quiet, the way things stay quiet right up until they don't.

Now half the countryside was describing it back to me word for word.

* * *

By late afternoon the city guard had closed the gate behind the last cart and the refugees had pooled in the market district like floodwater, and that's when the whispers found a new current: *the ward-keepers knew.* Someone said the Keeper's Circle had sealed its hall. Someone said inquisitors were already asking questions about how the wards fell, and who profited, and—this was the part that made my hands go cold inside my gloves—who in Vell traded in stolen memories.

Because of course. When the world breaks, the law needs somebody small enough to arrest for it. Memory-mongers fit nicely. We always have.

I packed the stall early. Kept my head down. Took the long way home through Coopers' Lane, which was a mistake, because Coopers' Lane ran past the fountain square where the refugees had been given bread and a place to sleep, and that was where I heard the woman singing.

She was sitting against the fountain with a blanket over her shoulders, middle-aged, hollow-eyed, and she was singing a lullaby in a language I didn't recognize—and I recognized the lullaby anyway. Because it was playing in my head. Had been since noon. One of the stranger's fragments in my stock, a scrap of melody attached to nothing, a woman's voice humming it while rain fell somewhere. I'd carried that tune for three years without knowing whose it was or why it mattered.

This woman knew whose it was. She sang it through twice and then stopped and said, to the blanket, "I can't remember who taught it to me."

And I understood, with the particular sick clarity that has followed me around like a stray dog my whole life, exactly what she meant. Something had taken the memory from her but left the song, the way you skin a rabbit and the shape stays. She was walking around wearing the outside of a life with the inside scooped out.

The fragments in my head shifted. Whispered. The lullaby hummed against the soldier's sunset and the widow's wedding day, and underneath all of them, patient as roots under a sidewalk, the old splinter-memory stirred—the hill, the frost, the flowers swallowing the village, the tall thing standing in the square.

*I know you,* it seemed to say, in the tone of a debt collector finding his mark's new address. *We have business, you and I. We settled it once already. Now I've come about the interest.*

* * *

You want to know the worst part? I'll tell you the worst part, since we're being honest and there's no one else in this room.

When I got back to my rooms above the dyer's shop, I did what I always do when the stock gets loud: I laid it out. Charms on the table, candle low, thief's focus in my hand—a sliver of ward-glass, illegal twice over. I touched each charm in turn, reading the labels written in the wax, taking inventory the way a shopkeeper does after a break-in.

Widow's wedding day. Soldier's sunset. Fisherman's brother. Maren's decades. All present. All accounted for.

Except.

At the bottom of the box, wrapped in the black cloth I use for the things I'll never sell, there were three charms I had no record of making. No label in my hand. No memory of harvesting them. Their wax was worked in a style I didn't recognize—too fine, almost ceremonial—and when I passed my focus over them they didn't whisper like the others.

They sang.

And what they sang, faintly, sweetly, in a young woman's voice, was the lullaby. The same one. The fountain-square lullaby, the one humming in my skull for three years, the one the hollowed woman couldn't remember learning.

I don't make charms I don't remember making. My hands are my livelihood; I'd know if they'd been working nights without me. Which meant someone else had made these, and put them in my box, and that someone had known I'd keep them—that I couldn't sell them and couldn't throw them away, because whatever was in them, my thief's instincts had claimed them the way a magpie claims glass.

Three unmarked charms. A lullaby. A memory of a flowering ruin that had been waiting five years for the rest of the world to catch up to it.

Someone had been keeping a ledger on me longer than I'd been keeping one on myself.

* * *

I slept badly, which is to say I sat up with the candle until the grey hours, listening to my own stock murmur like a room full of sleeping strangers. Around the fourth bell I gave up and went to the window, and that was when I saw the light.

Not starlight. There was no such thing anymore. This came from beyond the walls—eastward, out where the villages had been—a glow along the horizon the color of the deep yellow flowers from the splinter-memory. Bruised gold. Honey and turned earth.

It pulsed once, slowly, like something breathing.

Nobody else on the street seemed to notice, or maybe they'd all decided not to. The refugees slept in their blankets by the fountain. The guard stamped his rounds. Vell went about pretending the sky wasn't broken and the fields weren't blooming and the world still had rules.

But I had spent five years learning to read the grain of other people's minds, and I knew what I was looking at now. This wasn't a disaster arriving. Disasters arrive fast. This was something that had happened a long time ago finally coming due—the past showing up at the door with paperwork in order, polite as anything, saying: *you took something that belonged to me. Or I took something that belonged to you. Either way, little thief, we're going to sort out the accounts.*

The splinter-memory sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. *Flowers where villages stood.* Maren's borrowed eyes on a frozen summer hillside, five years gone.

Five years. Almost to the week.

Almost to the night, if I was honest, that I'd woken on my floor with blood on my lip and three days missing from my own head—gaps I'd told myself were drink, or a bad harvest, or the ordinary toll of carrying strangers' lives. Gaps I had never, in five years of meticulous inventory, been able to account for.

The glow on the horizon pulsed again.

I dressed, packed the box—all of it, even the unsellables, especially the unsellables—and put on the plain coat with the hidden seams. If the inquisitors were hunting memory-mongers, my stall was burned by morning. If half the countryside was turning into a garden out of a dead woman's nightmare, then the answer wasn't going to come from the ward-keepers, who either didn't know or had known all along.

But there was a person in this city who collected exactly the kind of history that gets erased. Who dealt in banned records and uncomfortable truths and knew more about what the kingdom had buried than anyone sane would want to. Juniper Sable, the archivists called her, when they called her anything at all—down in the wet dark under the city, where the cisterns held older water than the wells.

I didn't know how to find her. But I knew a fence who knew a smuggler who owed me for a charm of his mother's voice, and that's how every road in Vell starts.

One last look at the window first. At the gold pulse breathing on the edge of the world, patient as interest accruing.

"All right," I told it. "I'm coming to settle up."

Then I took the stairs down into the sleeping city, three unmarked charms singing softly against my ribs the whole way.
