## 6. The Archivist in the Cistern

The cistern under Tanner's Row had been dry for sixty years, ever since the city diverted the aqueduct to feed the tanneries' appetite. That was the story, anyway. The truth, according to the three people in Vell who knew it, was that the water hadn't been diverted at all. It had been *hidden*—piped through a dozen false channels so that anyone consulting the city's blueprints would conclude there was nothing down there worth flooding.

I was not one of the three people who knew. I was one of the maybe thirty who suspected, and I got my suspicion from a charm.

Two winters back, a dying lamplighter sold me his memory of a funeral—not for coin, but because he wanted someone to carry it after him. In it, I'd seen a woman with ink-stained fingers standing at the edge of a dry reservoir, holding a lantern, saying: *When you need the history they burned, come where the water would have been.* Then she'd turned and walked into a wall that wasn't a wall.

Lamplighters see everything. It's the trade. They walk every street at every hour, and nobody looks up at them because looking up means admitting the dark is bigger than your lamp. So when the stars went out and refugees started describing flowers growing out of the eye sockets of their villages, I went where the water would have been.

* * *

Getting into Tanner's Row after curfew was simple. Getting into it *unnoticed* required craft.

The ward-keepers' patrols had doubled since the night the sky emptied. Every corner now had its brazier of green-tinted flame—the usurper's new standard—and every patrol walked in threes, which told me they were scared. Soldiers walk in twos when they're confident and fours when they're stupid. Threes is fear doing arithmetic.

I went over the rooftops instead, along the spine of the tannery district where the chimneys vented something between rot and religion, and came down through the gap behind the glue-works where even the inquisitors didn't like to stand for long. The hatch to the old cistern access was exactly where the dead lamplighter's memory said it would be: under a grate disguised as a drain cover, sealed with wax that had been renewed recently enough to smell of beeswax rather than dust.

Someone was maintaining this place. Recently. Carefully.

I knocked the pattern from the memory—two slow, three fast, one slow—and waited in the stink of the glue-works with my hand on my knife, feeling the particular foolishness of a thief knocking politely on the door of a stranger's hiding place.

A slot slid open at knee height. One eye, brown, unimpressed.

"You're not the bread delivery," said a voice.

"No."

"The bread comes at moonrise. You're two hours late and wearing a knife."

"I'm never the bread," I said. "A lamplighter named Corvin sent me. He died two winters ago. Before he did, he gave me a memory, and in it a woman said—" I stopped, because reciting her words felt like spending them. But the eye was watching me decide whether to spend them, and I understood that the deciding was the test. "*Come where the water would have been.*"

Silence. Long enough that I counted my own heartbeats and got to eleven before the slot closed.

Then the hatch opened, all the way, and a hand reached out—ink-stained, as promised, though the ink was mostly under the nails now, ground in past washing—and pulled me down into the dark.

* * *

Stairs. Forty-one of them, spiraling, each worn in the center by feet older than the stairs deserved. At the bottom, lantern light opened into a space so large my breath caught.

The cistern wasn't empty. It was *full.*

Shelves. Thousands of them, cut into the stone walls in tiers, climbing up beyond the reach of the lamps, and every shelf packed with books, folios, scrolls, loose pages pressed flat under glass, bundles of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth. The dry air of Vell, usually the enemy of paper, had been beaten here by careful engineering—I could feel the faint coolness of water running somewhere behind the walls, hidden channels keeping the temperature honest. A hundred years of banned history, shelved like wine.

"You're staring," said the woman.

"I'm appraising," I said. "Habit."

"Appraise this, then." She held up a slim volume bound in grey leather. "Last royal census before the Purges. Do you know what's missing from it?"

"People who'd been harvested?"

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "Most thieves who find this place ask about the value first."

"Most thieves who find this place don't get found by it. Corvin chose me. I'm still deciding whether that flatters me." I looked around at the shelves again, slower. "Xanthe. Charm-seller, Row Nine, night market. And you're Juniper Sable, unless the ink is lying."

"Juniper Sable." She set the census down with the care people reserve for things that can't defend themselves. She was younger than I expected—thirty, maybe, with the kind of tired face that comes from reading bad news by choice for years. "Corvin's dead, then."

"Two winters back. His lungs. He asked me to carry a funeral he couldn't afford to keep."

"That sounds like him. He carried other people's things his whole life." She studied me with the frank assessment of someone cataloguing an acquisition. "You've been selling charms in the night market for what, five years? Since the outlawry."

"Six months before it. I was legal when I started."

"And since then?"

"Since then I've been very careful." Which was true, and also a lie by omission, and she knew it, and let it go—which told me she'd done her own arithmetic about what survival cost.

"You came because of the sky," she said. Not a question.

"The sky, and the flowers. Refugees are coming in from the east road describing bloom where there should be ash. Whole villages gone green. And five years ago I bought a memory off a dying farmer—a stolen one, secondhand, the seller couldn't say where it originated—and in it there was a valley just like what the refugees describe. Flowers out of season. Hedgerows that moved when no wind moved them. A green light in the eyes of a man the farmer called 'the king's man.'" I paused. "That memory predates the border wards failing by five years, Sable. Whatever's happening out there started long before the stars went out."

Something changed in her face. Not surprise—confirmation. The look of a scholar whose worst footnote has stood up and introduced itself.

"Sit down," she said. "This will take longer than you want it to, and shorter than it deserves."

* * *

She led me deeper into the stacks, past shelves organized in a system I couldn't parse—by date? By danger? By some third thing?—to a reading table lit by three lanterns and covered in papers weighted down with river stones. On it lay a half-finished transcription, ink still wet at the margins.

"This is a copy," she said, tapping it. "The original was burned eighty years ago in the Second Censorship, along with everything else from what we call the Interregnum—the hundred-year stretch before the current dynasty that official history says doesn't exist. Officially, King Aldous the Founder simply *began*, divinely appointed, no predecessors needed. Unofficially—" she flipped the transcription toward me "—there was a king before Aldous. His name was Maerin. And Maerin had a problem."

I sat. "Which was?"

"A garden." She said it flatly, letting the word do its work. "Royal records from the period—not many survive, but enough—describe a botanical obsession in the palace. New species appearing overnight. Hedges that grew in patterns no gardener could explain. And a corresponding disappearance of people. Not killed. *Diminished.* Survivors described neighbors who smiled too easily, who forgot their children's names, who gave away their memories willingly—or thought they did—to 'the crown's collectors.' The collectors paid well. Nobody asked where the memories went."

"To the garden," I said.

"To whatever wore the king's face and called itself a garden. There's a fragment here—" she turned to a page copied in cramped, furious handwriting "—from a court physician's journal. Listen. *'His Majesty sleeps and wakes refreshed, yet I swear upon my oath the man who wakes is taller than the man who slept. The garden grows a foot each night. His Majesty laughs more easily than any king I have served, and cannot tell me why.'*" She looked up. "Does that sound familiar?"

It did. It sounded like the nobleman's memory I'd glimpsed inside—that impossible flash of green light behind royal eyes, the one that had put ward-keepers on every street asking questions about thieves. I kept my voice level. "Go on."

"Aldous ended it. That much is true in the sanctioned histories, if nothing else is. He burned the gardens, executed the court, and built the border wards—the same wards, incidentally, that failed last week. Ward-keepers have maintained them ever since without knowing what they were really keeping out. Aldous wrote one letter about it, near the end of his life, to his successor. It survives only here." She touched a glass case almost tenderly. "In it, he says the thing he destroyed was never fully alive, and therefore could never fully die. He says it fed on *consent freely given*—that's why the collectors paid, why they made the giving feel like generosity. And he says—" her voice tightened "—he says he could not kill it because killing it would mean returning everything it had eaten, and there was no one left who remembered being eaten."

The cistern was very quiet. Somewhere behind the walls, the hidden water moved.

"So it waited," I said.

"For a century. Growing patient the way roots are patient. And then someone in the current palace either woke it or invited it back. Either way, the wards fell from the inside. Ilsa Vane's order has been guarding a door while the thing they feared was already sitting in the parlor."

"Ilsa Vane," I repeated. "The disgraced ward-keeper."

"You know her?"

"I know *of* her. Everyone does. The woman who broke the Circle."

"She didn't break anything," Juniper said sharply. "She reported the cracks in the wards and her superiors buried the reports. I have copies of those too. She was right, and she was punished for being right, which makes her either your enemy or your best ally, depending on how much pride she has left." She gathered her papers, squaring their edges with small precise movements. "Here's what matters tonight. The pattern repeats. A century ago, the harvest began quietly—collectors, coins, willing sellers—and ended with a kingdom that couldn't remember itself. The free cities think they're safe because they're outside the wards. They're not outside anything. The garden doesn't respect borders; it respects *appetite.*"

"And you want me for what, exactly? I sell trinkets. Widow's wedding days. Soldier's sunsets."

"You sell the only currency that thing eats." She met my eyes. "Every charm you carry is a seed it hasn't planted yet. And every charm you sell puts a piece of someone's life into circulation where the collectors can find it. You're not neutral, Xanthe. None of us carrying memories are. We're all couriers in a war most people don't know is happening."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue, and what came out instead was the truth, quiet as confession: "The memories whisper. Have done for days. Since the stars went out. Strangers' lives in my head, talking to each other like old friends at a wake. I thought I was going mad."

"You're not mad. You're *listening.*" She pressed the transcription into my hands—her copy, hours of work, given away like it weighed nothing. "Take this. Learn it. And come back in three nights with an answer to one question."

"Which is?"

"When the collectors come—and they will come, because a century of habit doesn't die with one censorship—whose side will you be standing on?" She smiled, and it was a tired, real smile, the first I'd seen from her. "The Archive doesn't recruit. It only opens the door. What walks through is up to the walker."

I climbed the forty-one stairs with the transcription against my chest and the weight of a hundred years of erased dead pressing through the paper.

Aboveground, the false stars of the usurper's braziers burned green at every corner. Below, somewhere under Tanner's Row, hidden water moved through hidden channels, keeping a hundred years of forbidden truth cool and alive.

Three nights. One question.

I had until then to decide which side of my own ledger I was standing on—and somewhere across the city, I knew without knowing how, the first collector was already counting.
