## 7. Inquisitor Hale

The river barge smelled of tar and wet wool, and Hale had spent nine days on it, and he had not complained once.

He stood at the rail as Vell resolved out of the morning haze—white walls first, then the green smear of gardens beyond them, then the cranes of the river quarter lifting their arms like patient herons. A city built by merchants who had never wanted a king and never quite stopped pretending they'd accept one. He knew its reputation. Everyone did. Vell was where the law went to be negotiated.

"Nine days," said Sergeant Osk behind him. "You could've flown the sigil-route from Corven's Rest in two."

"The sigil-route is for messages," Hale said. "I'm carrying a warrant."

Osk was twenty-six and thought this was funny. Hale let him think it. There would be fewer things to laugh about before this assignment ended.

The warrant in his breast pocket weighed nothing at all—that was the trouble with paper—and it named no one, because the crime had no name yet. The old statutes called it *soul-theft*. The street called it charm-selling. The Crown's new writs, drafted in the year of his own appointment, called it *unlicensed extraction of mnemonic substance*, which was the kind of language lawyers used when they wanted a thing buried under so many syllables it couldn't scream.

Hale preferred the old word. Soul-theft. It told you what you were looking at.

He had seen it done badly, once, in the border provinces—a hedge-witch with cracked knuckles and a kettle of stolen summers, selling a farmer his own dead wife's voice back to him by the hour. The farmer had wept with gratitude. That was the horror of it, Hale had written in his report. *The victims thank you.* They queued up. They paid. And every coin bought another inch of hollow space inside them where something of theirs used to live, and they walked out lighter and called it relief.

Law existed because people could not be trusted to know what was good for them. Not out of contempt—Hale had never felt contempt for ordinary people—but out of the same tenderness that made a father lock a medicine cabinet. Chaos had taught him that. Chaos had taught everyone that, in the years after the Succession, when the roads filled with the dispossessed and every village had its own idea of justice and none of them matched. He had been nineteen when the free companies burned his hometown's records hall, and he had watched three generations of land-titles and marriage lines and court judgments go up in smoke, and he had watched what the town became afterward without them. Without memory, without proof, without law.

He had spent his whole life since building walls against that night.

Vell's harbor master met them at the customs pier with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. Hale presented the writ. The harbor master read it twice, looked up at Hale's plain gray coat—the coat of an office, not a man—and said, carefully, "The city charter doesn't provide for Crown inquisitors within the walls, lordship. We have our own magistrates."

"I've read your charter," Hale said. "It provides for cooperation in matters affecting the realm's security. The fall of the border wards affects the realm's security." He let that sit. "I'm not here for your magistrates' jurisdiction. I'm here because whatever broke your wards didn't stop at your walls, and my office believes the same hands are involved."

"What hands?"

"That's what I've come to find out."

* * *

They gave him a room in the Ward-Keepers' Hall, which surprised him until he understood it as an insult. The ward-keepers were disgraced—half their order dissolved after the Succession, the rest reduced to maintaining a wall of spells nobody believed in anymore. Giving the Crown's inquisitor their empty guest quarters was Vell's way of saying: *you are welcome among failures.*

Hale unpacked anyway. He hung his coat. He set his father's steel penknife on the desk beside the inkstand, blade worn thin from thirty years of sharpening quills, and he opened the case files he'd brought north.

Three dossiers. Three names.

The first was a ward-keeper: Ilsa Vane, cashiered eight years ago for insubordination during the last full audit of the border network. Her audit notes had flagged anomalies in the western anchor-stones—drainage, she'd called it, power flowing out of the wards into something she couldn't identify. Her superiors had ruled the findings inconclusive. She had refused to sign the final certification. They had taken her badge for it. Hale had read her file four times on the barge, and each time he came away with the same unsettled feeling: this was not a woman who invented problems. This was a woman who found them and was punished for the finding.

She would bear watching. Not arresting. Watching.

The second dossier was thinner. Juniper Sable, listed as a scrivener, formerly of the Royal Archive, dismissed in the general purges. Intelligence suggested Sable had remained in Vell and dealt in banned texts. Banned texts alone wouldn't have warranted the journey. But Sable's name appeared, twice, in correspondence between the palace chancellery and the ward-keepers' high command dated eleven years ago—and both times, the correspondence was about *destruction orders*, and both times, Sable's dismissal followed within a month. Someone had wanted that scrivener gone from the archives very specifically, very quietly, and Hale had learned long ago that quiet was where the truth kept house.

The third dossier was almost blank. An informant's note, a district, a description: a charm-seller working the night markets, small build, sells clean, no known fixed address, goes by various names. Attached was a single evidence sheet—a confiscated charm, sent to the capital two years ago by a customs officer with more diligence than sense.

Hale turned the little glass bead over in his palm now, at the desk, lamplight moving through it like weather. Inside, faint as breath on a window, a swirl of color that wasn't color. He had tested it against every assay method his office possessed. It was genuine extracted memory, human origin, uncorrupted—which was itself remarkable, because everything else about the trade corrupted fast. Whoever made this was good. Better than good.

And there was one other thing, which he had told no one, not even Osk.

When he held the charm too long, he began to remember something. Just a fragment. A kitchen. Rain on a window. A woman humming, and the specific ache of being somewhere safe while the world outside was loud. He had no such kitchen in his life. He had grown up in a garrison barracks, in rooms that smelled of boot oil and discipline. The memory could not be his.

Which meant that holding the object, he was experiencing exactly what its buyer would pay for. Exactly what its seller had once carried around inside their skull.

Hale set the bead down and wiped his hand on his coat, and hated himself a little for the gesture. Sentiment. He was forty-one years old; he had sat with plague carts and execution warrants; a glass bead should not unsettle him.

But it did. Because the bead raised the question the whole investigation turned on, the question he'd been circling since Corven's Rest:

If a stranger's happiness could sit in glass and wait to be sold—then what was a person? Where did the self end? The law said the self was property of the self, inviolable, and Hale had built his life on that sentence. But the law also assumed the self stayed put. The law had not imagined a world where memory could be ladled out like soup.

The stars going out had changed the terms. Nine nights now without stars over half the realm, and the wards down along six hundred miles of border, and refugees already trickling toward Vell with stories Hale's analysts refused to credit. His superiors in the capital wanted someone competent on the ground before panic metastasized. They wanted order maintained. They wanted, above all, for this not to be the Crown's fault.

That last instruction had arrived in a private letter, unsigned. Hale had burned it. But burning a letter doesn't burn its meaning, and the meaning was: find a culprit who isn't us.

Hale intended to find the truth instead. It was the only kind of finding he trusted.

* * *

The night market on Tallow Street operated in the gap between the lamplighters and the dawn watch, and it was exactly as ugly as its reputation. Hale went himself—not Osk, not the local informants, himself—in a dyer's smock and a laborer's boots, because he had learned in fifteen years of this work that rank was a smell and people fled from it.

He bought fried eel he didn't eat and stood at the edge of the crowd and watched.

The market was grief-shaped. He saw that within a quarter hour. Half the stalls sold cloth and knives and smuggled spice like any market anywhere, but threaded through them, quieter, half-hidden, were the others: men and women with trays of small glass and bone charms, and around those trays hovered customers who moved differently. Carefully. Like people approaching altars.

He watched a woman buy a charm from a stooped seller near the well. She held it a long time before paying. When she closed her fist around it, her face did something complicated—relief and guilt arriving together, twins. Then she pressed coins into the seller's palm and hurried away, glancing back once.

Hare followed her—not far, just enough. She stopped in a doorway two streets off, and he saw her raise the charm to her lips, and he saw what happened to her face when it took hold. Ten seconds of borrowed joy. Then the emptiness came back in behind it, worse than before, the way a lamp makes the dark darker at its edge.

She stood in the doorway and cried without noise, and Hale stood in the shadows and felt the old cold anger rise in him, the useful kind, the kind that kept his hands steady.

This was what the trade did. It didn't feed hunger. It *taught* hunger. Every charm sold was an advertisement for the next one, and every buyer walked away a little less able to bear their own unassisted life. He had watched entire districts slide into it, in the southern towns—whole neighborhoods renting themselves out piece by piece, sellers harvesting the desperate's good hours and reselling them to the same desperate at markup. A perfect engine. Nobody starved, nobody bled, and everybody vanished a little.

And the worst part—the part that kept him up on the barge, the part he'd never put in a report—was that he understood it. Standing in Tallow Street in a dyer's smock, he understood the sellers too. Most of them weren't monsters. They were survivors of the same chaos he'd sworn against, people who'd found the one commodity that never ran out as long as people hurt: other people's pasts. If he'd been born poorer, hungrier, if the garrison had turned him out at fourteen instead of taking him in—

No. He shut that door. Understanding was how corruption started; he'd seen honest magistrates excuse a briber because they *understood his circumstances*. You could understand a thing all the way to the gallows and it would still be theft.

A bell rang somewhere in the city—one stroke, wrong hour, wrong tone. The market rippled. Heads turned east, toward the walls. Hale turned too.

Above the eastern quarter, where the Ward-Keepers' Hall stood, a light was rising. Green. Not firelight, not lantern-glass, not any green he had a name for—it moved like water thinks, slow and total, and even from this distance Hale felt it against his skin, a pressure, a hum in the fillings of his teeth.

It lasted four heartbeats. Then it was gone, and the market went on, and most people laughed it off or blamed a ward-keeper's exercise or a god's indigestion. But Hale had spent his career reading crowds, and the crowd's laughter had a crack in it tonight.

He made his way back toward the Hall through streets gone suddenly thoughtful, and at the corner of the Wardens' Bridge he passed a figure coming the other way—small, hooded, walking with the particular economy of someone who has practiced not being noticed. Their eyes met his for half a second.

Later—much later—he would understand what he'd seen in that half-second. But that night he only noted it the way he noted everything: small build, quick eyes, the calluses of a lockworker on the right hand, and a smell he couldn't place until he was ten steps past and then placed all at once, with a lurch.

Glass and rainwater. The exact scent of the charm in his evidence kit.

Hale stopped on the bridge. The river moved below him, black and indifferent. Somewhere across the city the green light had come and gone, and somewhere in the crowd behind him walked the best charm-maker in Vell, and somewhere in his breast pocket lay a warrant with no name on it yet.

He took out his notebook, wrote three words, and underlined one of them.

*Tallow Street. Tonight.*

Then, beneath it, smaller, the word he would spend the rest of this story learning to regret:

*Soon.*

By dawn, the first refugee boats would be tying up at the customs pier, carrying the first of the bloom-touched out of the drowned countryside—and Vell's careful, negotiable peace would begin to crack along lines no magistrate had drawn. Hale stood on the bridge a while longer, watching the water carry the city's reflections apart and put them back together, and told himself the thing he always told himself at the start of a hunt:

The law would reach wherever this led. Even here. Especially here.

He almost believed it.
