## 8. A Charm Gone Wrong

The refugee line outside the Drowned Lantern started before dawn and by midmorning it wrapped twice around the fountain square, a gray river of people who smelled of ash and road dust and fear. Xanthe had never seen Vell like this. Vell was a city that looked away from things—poverty, funerals, its own reflection in the canal—but it could not look away from ten thousand country folk standing in its squares with nothing but the clothes they'd slept in.

Neither, apparently, could Xanthe's customers.

"Charms," they called from their usual corner beneath the Lantern's leaning awning, voice pitched to carry over the murmur. "Fresh charms. First sunset of summer. A wedding on a cliff road. Your mother's kitchen if your mother is dead and you'd give a tooth to stand in it again."

It was obscene, selling memories to people who had just lost everything real. Xanthe knew that. They also knew that obscene paid better than noble, and noble hadn't fed them once in five years.

What they told themselves instead: these people needed it more than anyone. When your village is gone—actually gone, swallowed, hedgerowed, renamed—you don't want soup. You want somewhere to put your grief down for an hour. A charm was a room you could visit. Xanthe had been renting rooms in other people's hearts since they were seventeen. They liked to think they were good at it.

The morning's stock lay in the lined wooden case on their folding table: twelve charms in glass-and-wire settings, each one a bead of silvered memory no bigger than a currant, each one harvested from a paying donor or lifted from a sleeping noble in the old days, before lifting got risky. Twelve little rooms. A widow's wedding day, worn soft as an old coin. A soldier's last sunset over the Marrow valley, gold light on a rifle barrel. A child's first snowfall. Ordinary treasures, all of them. Xanthe knew every one by touch, the way a blind woman knows her spoons.

Business was brutal and brisk.

A tanner with burn scars bought the first sunset. A grandmother bought the wedding and wept into her shawl with something that might have been gratitude. Two sisters pooled coins for the kitchen-memory and stood in the corner of the square holding hands around it, mouths moving, clearly walking through a door only they could see. Xanthe watched them go and felt the familiar ache behind the sternum—the residue, always, of handling other lives. Every sale left a smear. Nothing you could name. Just the sense of having lived an hour you never earned.

By noon the case held four charms and a heavy purse, and Xanthe was thinking about dumplings, and that was when the girl came.

She was maybe nine, alone, which should have stopped him. In a crowd of refugees a lone child meant a dead family or a worse story, and either way the answer was not *sell her a memory*. But there was a quality to her stillness—he'd seen it in soldiers, in widows, in himself at seventeen—and she counted out her coins on the table with terrible care, six coppers and a button, and said, "Something green."

Xanthe blinked. "Green?"

"Flowers," she said. "Everyone keeps saying the flowers. I want to see them before—" She stopped. Recalculated, the way children do when they've learned which adults flinch. "I have this button. It's bone."

The button was bone, carved with a hare. It was worth nothing and everything, and Xanthe's hand was already moving toward the case because cynicism was a coat they wore and coats come off sometimes without permission.

"The first snowfall," they heard themselves say. "Or a garden party, summer, before the war. There's roses in it. Roses count as flowers."

The girl shook her head slowly. "Not those flowers. The new ones."

There are moments in a thief's life when the body understands danger before the mind consents to. Xanthe's hands went cold on the case lid. The new ones. Everyone keeps saying the flowers. The blooming roads, the flowering villages, the gardens where towns used to be—three weeks of rumors and every refugee carried a version, and here was a child asking to see the thing that had eaten her home because she wanted to look at it one more time before—

"I don't have that," Xanthe said quietly.

"You do." Her eyes were very dark and very certain. "You're carrying it. You've been carrying it all day. It's the heaviest one in the box."

* * *

Later—much later, after everything—Xanthe would try to remember exactly which charm they'd sold her. Whether the case had held eleven beads or ten. Whether their fingers had found the wrong room by accident or whether some part of them, tired and softened by a bone hare button, had simply reached for what the child asked for. Memory is a thief's trade and Xanthe was good at it, and the honest answer was that they could not swear to the moment. Only to this: the girl walked away clutching a silvered bead in her fist, smiling for the first time, and Xanthe sold two more charms, ate dumplings at a stall whose proprietor had doubled his prices overnight, and did not think about any of it again until the screaming started.

It started small, the way disasters do. A woman near the fountain said the girl was asleep on her feet. Then someone said she was *walking*. Then the crowd did what crowds do in Vell now, which was part and make way, and Xanthe pushed through the gap and saw her going down the Processional Road toward the North Gate with her eyes closed and her smile fixed and her little fists swinging in time like a metronome, and every hair on Xanthe's arms stood up.

Because they recognized the walk.

Five years of selling charms taught you the difference between a customer savoring a memory and a customer lost in one. Savoring was slow, tactile, eyes half-open; people touched walls, tasted air. Lost was different. Lost was when the boundary thinned—when a strong memory reached up and pulled the buyer under, and they began to *go somewhere*, truly go, the body following the mind's conviction. It happened maybe one time in a thousand. Xanthe had seen a man walk into the harbor chasing a beach memory. You learned to watch for it.

But the man in the harbor had been chasing *his own* remembered sea.

The girl was walking toward the North Gate like a woman answering a summons, and when Xanthe caught her shoulders and turned her, her eyes were open now—open and green. Not reflecting green. Green the way a lampshade is green: lit from within, a soft vegetal glow behind the iris, leaves turning where pupils should be.

"Let go," she said pleasantly. "It's almost time to be planted."

Every sound in the square dropped away except Xanthe's own pulse. Because they knew that green. They had seen it once before, three weeks ago, inside a dying nobleman's stolen memory—a flash behind the king's portrait-eyes, a light that had looked back at them through the memory like a fish looking up through ice. They had told themselves it was corruption, artifact, bad harvest. They had nearly made themselves believe it.

Now it was standing in front of them wearing a nine-year-old girl.

"Sweetheart." Xanthe kept their voice level, kept their grip gentle. Around them the crowd was backing off in a widening ring; someone was shouting for ward-keepers; the word *possessed* moved through the square like wind through wheat. "Where are you going? Who planted you?"

"It's not who." She smiled wider. Too wide. The smile of someone reciting. "It's the garden. The garden gives and the garden keeps. He showed me the rows and rows and rows, all the pretty lanterns hanging in the dark, all the memories ripening, and he said come along, little seed, we're planting in Vell next—" Her head tilted. The green light pulsed. "He says you know his work. He says to tell you the lanterns are lovely from inside."

The world went very far away and very quiet.

*Lanterns.* Xanthe had never spoken aloud about the strange vivid memories in their private stock—the life they'd never lived, the lover named Wren, the house above a cold sea. Had never shown them to anyone. And yet this thing knew. Knew what Xanthe carried. Knew how they carried it. Knew the word Xanthe used, privately, at night, for the glowing beads in their own collection.

"What did you sell her?" someone screamed. "Memory-monger! What did you put in her?"

Hands grabbed Xanthe from behind—refugees, frightened mothers, a butcher with a cleaver still in his belt—and Xanthe let them, because the girl had turned back toward the gate and begun to walk again, serene, unhurried, and the crowd parted for her like water, and nobody else seemed to understand that the North Gate led to the hedgerows, to the moving green, to whatever waited beyond Vell's failing walls being patient and polite about it.

They broke free. They ran. They caught the girl at the gate plaza with the wall looming gray overhead and ward-keepers shouting from the battlements, and this time when they gripped her shoulders they did the thing they had done perhaps fifty times in their life and sworn each time never to do carelessly: they opened the charm from the receiving end.

Harvesting is theft. Receiving is drowning. Xanthe plunged.

*Rows. Rows and rows and rows of them, hanging in a darkness that smells of turned earth and honey, thousands of glass globes strung on vines between trees that grow in no geometry any orchard ever grew in, and inside each globe a light, and the lights are memories, whole memories, villages' worth, harvests and weddings and griefs and the taste of bread, ripening, and something moves down the row tending them, vast and green-crowned and patient, and it turns—*

Xanthe tore out gasping, on their knees in the gate plaza, blood in their mouth from biting their tongue. The girl swayed. The green in her eyes guttered like a lamp running dry—and went out. She blinked, ordinary brown, nine years old, and looked at the wall and the crowd and Xanthe kneeling before her, and said, in a small cracked voice:

"I wanted to see the flowers. Where am I? Whose button is this?"

* * *

The ward-keepers took her name and her brother's address from a sobbing aunt and released her, because whatever had ridden her was gone as cleanly as it came, and there is no charge in Vell for being nine. Nobody arrested Xanthe either, though the crowd's mood had curdled and someone spat near their boots and the word *monger* followed them home through three streets like a stray dog.

Back in the rented room above the chandler's shop, Xanthe locked the door, shutters, shutters again, and laid the remaining charms out on the table in a careful row. Seven beads, seven small lights. Their livelihood. Five years of craft and caution, of rules—never harvest from the desperate, never sell to the sleepless, never, ever open a charm you didn't make.

They picked up the widow's wedding day. Held it to the lamp.

And saw it.

Faint, threaded through the silvered memory like a root through soil: a filament of green. So fine you'd never notice unless you were looking—unless you had just walked through a vaulted dark full of hanging lights and knew, now, exactly what that color meant. The wedding day itself seemed untouched: bride, cliff road, salt wind, joy. But the green ran through it the way a vein runs through a leaf. Present. Patient. *In* it.

Xanthe set the wedding charm down with hands that would not quite obey, and checked the next. The soldier's last sunset: green thread, faint as spider silk. The child's first snowfall: green. All seven. All seven infected, and if all seven, then how many sold before today? How many across five years, in how many cities, sitting in how many pockets and lockets and keepsake boxes, waiting—no. No, not waiting. That was the horror of it, the true horror, the kind that sat down in your chest and made itself comfortable: the threads weren't waiting for anything. They weren't spells. They were *seeds*. Passive, dormant, patient as winter. Waiting for nothing. Needing nothing. Except, perhaps, a buyer weak enough, or grieving enough, or young enough to fall all the way into a memory and stay.

One in a thousand, Xanthe had thought. A rare misfortune.

Or a harvest schedule.

They thought of every charm they had ever sold. Hundreds. Thousands. Thought of the two sisters in the fountain square, walking hand in hand through a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but in a silvered bead and Xanthe's own guilty inventory. Were they home now? Asleep? Facing north?

The candle guttered. Down in the street a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, the *curfew* bell, rung early, rung wrong, and boots were passing beneath the window in numbers Vell's streets did not usually hold. Martial order arriving like weather. Through the shutter slats, torchlight moved in ranks.

Xanthe sat in the dark with seven poisoned beads and understood, with the clarity that comes only after the worst has already happened, three things at once.

First: the Verdant King was not coming for Vell. He was already inside it, and had been for years, and he had used Xanthe—used their hunger, their craft, their decent-enough excuses—as a planting row.

Second: the inquisitors would come for memory-mongers now, if they weren't already on the roads, and Xanthe's face was known in every market from the harbor district to the tanners' quarter, and a mob had spat at them tonight, and tomorrow that mob would have inquisitors to organize it.

Third—and this was the one that made them reach, finally, for the loose floorboard under the cot—for the private stock, the beads they never sold, the life they'd never lived: if the infection ran through everything they carried, then it ran through Wren too. Through the house by the sea, through the laugh they could almost hear, through the one memory in all the world that felt, against all reason, like *home*.

The floorboard came up. The hidden case sat where it always sat.

And every bead inside it was glowing green.
