## 9. Juniper's Ledger

The cistern had three lamps and Juniper allowed herself one.

That was the arithmetic of hiding beneath Vell for eleven years: light was a debt, noise was a debt, even thought, if you weren't careful, could become a debt—because thinking hard made you hum, and humming carried up through the water channels into the ears of people who had no business hearing anything but dripping stone. So Juniper Sable sat in the dark with her one lamp turned low, its flame shielded behind a shard of green glass she'd stolen from a temple that no longer existed, and she worked.

The page lay flat under her hands.

It had cost her four days to get it out of the sealed annex beneath the Hall of Records—a vault so old the archivists who maintained it had forgotten why it was sealed, only that their masters' masters' masters had said never. She had gone in as a clerk renewing a catalog license, which she was technically entitled to do; she had come out with a sheet of vellum folded against her ribs and the particular lightness of a woman who has just committed the only crime that matters.

Not theft. The city was full of thieves and the city survived them. This was *remembering*, and remembering was treason.

The cipher on the page was royal work—old royal work, from before the Concord, when kings still wrote things down themselves instead of letting ministers launder their intentions through committee. Juniper had spent twenty years of her life learning dead hands. Court shorthand. Treasury marks. The little private alphabets queens used for letters to lovers. She knew the shape of secrets the way a midwife knows the shape of a skull: by feel, in the dark, before the crying starts.

But this one fought her.

Three nights now she'd broken it open a finger's width at a time, transcribing fragments onto her own paper in her own plain hand, because the original felt wrong to touch too long. Not physically. Physically it was vellum like any other vellum, though heavier than it should be, the way wet rope is heavier than dry. It was the *content* that made her skin crawl—the sense, growing stronger as the cipher yielded, that whoever wrote this had not wanted it read quickly. Had maybe not wanted it read at all. Had written it anyway, because some truths rot if you keep them.

*Day 41 of the Regency of the Green,* the page began, and Juniper had laughed out loud alone in the dark, one sharp bark that echoed off the cistern walls and cost her a full minute of listening afterward to make sure nothing above had heard.

The Regency of the Green. A century ago, at least. She'd found references to the phrase twice before—in a marginal note in a tax ledger, in a hymnal with three verses scraped out—and both times she'd assumed it was allegory. Some faction. Some reform movement. History was mostly factions wearing costumes.

It was not a faction.

* * *

By the fourth night she had enough to stop being a scholar and start being afraid.

She made herself finish the transcription first. That was discipline; that was the whole of what kept her alive when better-funded rebels got caught—you finished the work before you let the work finish you. Her hand moved through the last columns of cipher while the lamp burned low and the water dripped its idiot metronome, and when she set the pen down her fingers were shaking, and she looked at the plain-hand pages spread across her table, and she understood what was growing under the kingdom.

The document was a steward's report. That was the horror of it—it wasn't a confession or a prophecy or a madman's screed. It was an *administrative record*. Line items. Deliveries. Someone, a hundred years ago, had been doing the accounts for a god.

The Verdant King. The name appeared eleven times, always in the same careful court hand, always capitalized, always preceded by a small ritual mark Juniper recognized from funerary inscriptions—the sign scribes put beside names of the dead so readers wouldn't mistake them for the living.

Except the steward's entries made it brutally clear that the Verdant King was not dead, and not exactly alive either, and not exactly a king.

*Harvest, autumn quarter: 4,100 units. Quality acceptable. The crown's hunger is steady.*

Units. Four thousand units. Juniper had spent enough years reading between the lines of ledgers to translate: a unit was a memory. A harvested, pressed, stored human memory—one person's wedding day, one soldier's sunset, one mother's face—reduced to inventory, delivered quarterly, and *fed* to something wearing the monarchy.

She read the mechanism three times to make sure she wasn't inventing it. The parasite had been bound to the crown itself—not the man, the *crown*, the office, the line, the whole architecture of lawful authority. Whoever wore it, fed it. And it fed not on blood or taxes or labor, all the old currencies kingdoms were honest about, but on remembering. Every coronation was a new root system. Every royal decree that touched a life—a conscription, a land seizure, a pardon—gave the thing a channel into the minds it governed, and through those channels, quietly, without anyone feeling more than a passing fog, a missing afternoon, a softened grief, it drank.

A century of it. The report covered forty-one days and recorded over four thousand memories taken, and the steward noted with satisfaction that the harvest had been scaled up from previous years and that *the subjects experience no loss they can name.*

Juniper pushed back from the table.

Her chair scraped and the sound went up through the dark like a scream and she froze, hand over her mouth, counting the drips until her heart agreed to continue. But it wasn't fear of discovery that had driven her back. It was arithmetic.

Four thousand units per quarter, a hundred years ago, and *scaling up*. The kingdom had grown since then. The machinery of law had grown. If the rate had merely kept pace—if it had done nothing more ambitious than grow with the population—

She did the sum twice because the first answer seemed insane.

Tens of millions of memories. An entire nation, thinned. Not emptied—never emptied, that would be noticed—but *thinned*, the way you thin a row of seedlings, taking every fifth sprout so the rest grow stronger and none complain. A country where everyone was slightly less than they'd been. Where griefs healed too fast because the sharpest edges had been drunk away. Where wars ended strangely, where rebellions lost their fury somewhere between planning and action, where people couldn't quite say why they'd stopped being angry.

Where the stars could go out and half the population would stand in the street and feel, beneath their terror, a strange and terrible *relief*.

Juniper sat very still.

She had assumed the ward-fall and the starless sky were attacks. Everyone assumed that—that something had struck the wards from outside, some enemy, some weapon, the free cities bracing for invasion. But the cipher told a different story, and once she saw it she couldn't unsee it: the wards hadn't been broken. They'd been *eaten*. The border wards were woven from anchored memory—Ilsa Vane's order built them from consecrated recollection, that was the whole craft—and the Verdant King had simply extended its feeding to the largest stockpile of stored memory in the world. The wards were a meal. The stars going out—

She flipped to the final section of the transcription. The steward's closing entry. Day 41, the last day anyone had written this down before the whole record was buried.

*The crown has asked whether the garden might extend beyond the realm's borders. His Majesty's appetite is patient but not idle. The free cities hold memories we do not govern, and ungoverned memory is the only soil the garden cannot yet reach. In time. The roots are patient. We have a hundred years.*

We have a hundred years.

Juniper counted backward from now and the number landed like a dropped blade.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Sleep was another debt and she'd stopped paying it around midnight anyway, when she caught herself reading the same line four times and understanding that her mind was refusing it rather than failing to grasp it.

Near dawn—or what passed for dawn in a world with no stars, the gray hour when the lamplighters above walked their rounds in silence because nobody had slept well since the sky broke—she heard the knock.

Two soft taps, then one, then two again. Xanthe's pattern. The thief had left three nights ago to sell charms in the refugee crowds above and had promised, in that merchant's-cynicism voice that fooled no one who'd watched them give a widow back her wedding day for free, to return before the gray hour.

Juniper opened the hidden panel and Xanthe slipped through sideways, the way they always did, as if the doorway might change its mind. They were thinner than five days ago. Everyone in Vell was thinner. Their eyes did a full circuit of the cistern before they'd even finished entering—lamp, exits, Juniper's face—and Juniper had learned to read that circuit like a ledger. Tonight it came back troubled.

"You look," Xanthe said, "like someone I robbed."

"Sit down."

"I've been sitting for a week. My spine's filed a complaint." But they sat, folding onto the crate across the table, and their merchant's mask slipped the moment they stopped moving, and underneath it Juniper saw the thing she'd seen in refugees: fear being managed, badly, by someone with no manager.

"What happened?" Juniper asked.

"Selling's gotten strange." Xanthe rubbed their thumb along their fingers—the tell Juniper had catalogued early, the thief touching the ghost of coins. "Half the city wants charms to remember what the sky looked like. Can't sell them that; nobody owns that memory, it belonged to everybody, there's no single mind to harvest it from. And the other half—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "The other half don't want memories anymore. They want to forget. I've had six people ask me today to take the stars going out out of their heads. Paying anything. One woman offered her coat, and it's cold, and she offered her coat."

"That's the feeding," Juniper said. "Starting inside the walls."

Xanthe looked at her. "You've found something."

Juniper gathered her plain-hand pages, hesitated—eleven years of instinct screaming about showing anyone anything—and then laid them out on the table between them anyway, because the instinct had been written for a world where the enemy was a ministry with limited patience, and that world had ended the night the sky did.

Xanthe read. Juniper watched them read, and watched the color leave their face in stages, like a tide going out over sand. When they reached the word *units* their hand flattened against the table. When they reached *the subjects experience no loss they can name* they made a sound low in their throat that Juniper recognized because she'd made the same one at the same line.

"The noble's memory," Xanthe said. "Weeks ago. I stole from a lord's head and there was green light behind the king's eyes, in his memory of a court audience, and I thought it was poison, or madness, or—" They laughed, short and ugly. "I thought it was a *story*. Something to sell."

"It's neither. It's the oldest thing in the kingdom." Juniper tapped the steward's final entry. "This was buried deliberately. Sealed, scrubbed, the annex forgotten on purpose—three generations of archivists instructed to lose it. Which means the crown knew. Not the Verdant King—the *crown*. The human apparatus. Ministers, stewards, the Ward-Keepers' founding charters, all of it. Somebody decided a century ago that a god eating the country's memory was preferable to whatever chaos would follow telling the truth."

"And now?"

"Now it's done waiting." Juniper pulled the last page forward. She'd copied the steward's projection in full, the growth curve, the patient arithmetic, and beneath it the sentence that had kept her awake. "'The free cities hold memories we do not govern.' Xanthe—the wards fell because the wards were food. Vell's own memory-stock will be next. The city keeps records, registries, consecrated archives—we're the richest larder left inside the old borders. And after Vell—"

She didn't finish it. She turned the page instead so Xanthe could read the closing lines themselves, because some sentences should be witnessed rather than reported:

*In time. The roots are patient. We have a hundred years.*

"A hundred years ago," Xanthe said slowly. "They gave themselves a hundred years."

"And the hundred years are up." Juniper sat back. The lamp guttered, and she let it, because suddenly the dark seemed honest in a way the light wasn't. "Whatever restraint held this thing back—whatever part of the crown was still human enough to ration the feeding—it's gone. The wards fall, the stars go out, the refugees say villages are blooming where they shouldn't. It's not expanding anymore. It's *harvesting*. The difference matters. Expansion takes generations. Harvest takes seasons."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. Above them, faintly, came the sound of the city: boots, bells rung out of rhythm, someone weeping two streets over in the particular exhausted way that had become the city's second anthem. Then the thief asked the question Juniper had been dreading, because it was the right question, and right questions were the most expensive things in the world.

"How do we kill it?"

"We don't." Juniper had already turned back through her transcription, to a fragment near the middle she'd marked with a charcoal cross, the one passage the cipher resisted longest and finally surrendered at dawn. "Listen. 'The binding is the crown and the crown is the binding; what was bound may be unbound, but only by the rite at the root, and only by a freely given memory of love from one whom the king himself loved. No substitute. No coercion. The parasite cannot counterfeit it; that is why it harvests.'" She looked up. "There's a rite. Buried deeper than this page, probably in the palace itself, probably guarded by everything we just read about. And it needs something the parasite has spent a hundred years making sure doesn't exist: someone the real king loved, still loving him."

Xanthe stared at her. "Does anyone even know if the real king is alive?"

"The cipher says bound, not killed. Bound things endure. That's rather the point of binding." Juniper began stacking her pages, squaring their edges, the small compulsive tidying that meant her mind was running ahead of her mouth. "Which means somewhere under the capital there's a man who has been dreaming for a hundred years while something wears his crown. And somewhere above us, an inquisitor's office is hunting memory-mongers—which means the usurper's regime knows about the harvest and is clearing the field of anyone who might notice it happening. Hale arrived Tuesday. My contact in the Hall of Records says he's requested the annex seal history."

"He'll trace it here."

"He'll trace it here."

Xanthe stood, restless, and walked the length of the cistern and back—their thinking walk, Juniper had learned. When they returned, their face had settled into something worse than fear: resolve. "Then we move tonight. Whatever you need copied, carried, saved—you pack it now. Because if Hale finds this archive, he doesn't arrest you, does he? People who know this much don't get arrested. They get disappeared into whatever passes for the palace these days."

"They get harvested," Juniper said. "I imagine my memory of this table would feed it nicely."

* * *

They worked through the gray hour and into the false morning, packing eleven years of forbidden history into oilcloth and lead-lined boxes sized to pass through sewer grates. Juniper transcribed the cipher summary three times and hid the copies in three places, and taught Xanthe two of the locations, and hated teaching them, because every person who knew a location was a person whose capture could end the knowledge—and then hated herself for the arithmetic, because she'd spent her whole life fighting people who treated humans as inventory, and here she was doing sums on her friends.

It was nearly full morning when Xanthe paused at the hidden door, boxes stacked, and looked back.

"The king," they said. "Someone he loved. Freely given. You believe that's real? The rite? Or is it a story some dead steward told himself so the ledger could end on hope?"

Juniper considered lying, considered hedging, considered the thousand scholarly hedges she'd built her career on.

"I think," she said, "that a parasite which feeds on memory and cannot counterfeit love has told us, in its own hunger, exactly how it dies. That's not hope. That's intelligence. Now go—and take the east channels, not the west. Hale posted men at the west outflows yesterday evening."

Xanthe went pale. "You knew that and waited until now to mention it?"

"I mentioned it the moment it became relevant." Juniper closed the panel behind them, listened to the splash-and-silence of the thief's retreat, and then turned back to her table—to the one page she hadn't shown anyone, the fragment she'd translated last and locked in the bottom of her writing box, the lines that had made her decide, sometime around the coldest hour of the night, that she was done being an archive and needed to become an army.

She smoothed it flat under the lamp.

*Note appended in different hand, later date unknown:* **The rite requires the root-vault beneath the throne. Access is by the gardener's gate. The gate answers only to blood of the old court. If any of the blood survives the purges, find them. Find them before the crown does.**

Somewhere in Vell, Juniper knew, there were lists. Purge lists, five years old, the kind that got people's names erased from registries and their houses reassigned—and somewhere on one of those lists, if any of the old blood had lived, was a descendant of the court that had bound the Verdant King in the first place.

She blew out the lamp, all three of them at once, debt be damned, and sat in the dark composing in her head the most dangerous catalog entry of her life.

*Item: one bloodline, location unknown. Status: hunted.*
