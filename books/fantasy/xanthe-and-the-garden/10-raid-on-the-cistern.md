## 10. Raid on the Cistern

The ledger weighed less than a dead sparrow and more than a city.

Xanthe felt it against their ribs through the oilcloth wrapping as they climbed down into the cistern for the third night running, hand over hand along the wet rope, boots finding the old footholds Juniper had drilled into the stone a decade before anyone thought to need them. Above them Vell was still screaming about the stars. Below, in the dark under the city's feet, Juniper Sable was reading by lamplight like the end of the world was a footnote.

"Page forty," she said without looking up when Xanthe dropped onto the walkway. "You'll want to see page forty."

"You said that about page twelve."

"And you didn't read page twelve, so now you're going to read page forty." She turned the ledger toward them, her finger steady on a column of cipher even though her hands had been shaking since the stars went out. Everyone's hands were shaking. Xanthe's were too; they'd just gotten good at hiding it behind a merchant's stillness. "This is a requisition record. Dated ninety-one years ago. It's a transfer order—memories, bulk volume, from six villages in the Marchlands to the palace. Signed by a ward-keeper."

"Ward-keepers don't harvest memories."

"This one did." Juniper's lamplight caught the hollows under her eyes. She hadn't slept either. Nobody in Vell had slept properly since the sky went blank; sleep had become a country everyone was afraid of crossing alone. "And look at the counter-signature."

Xanthe looked. The second signature was a royal seal pressed into wax that had gone amber with age—a crown over a tree.

They stared at it for a long moment. The cistern dripped somewhere far off, patient as a clock.

"That's the Verdant King's mark," Xanthe said finally. "From the memory I saw. In Lord Cassel's head. The green light behind the king's eyes—it wore that same shape."

"I know." Juniper closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the way she handled everything she was afraid of. "I've known for two days. I've been trying to decide what frightens me more: that this happened before, or that someone made very sure we'd never learn it happened before."

* * *

They worked through the night, because working was better than thinking. That was the arrangement they'd fallen into: Xanthe brought food from the market above—bread, hard cheese, a jar of pickled things nobody would miss—and Juniper translated, reading aloud in her flat archivist's voice while Xanthe copied the important pages into their own hand, in thief's shorthand, on slips of paper thin enough to swallow if it came to that.

It was Juniper who insisted on the copies. "If they take me," she'd said, on the first night, matter-of-fact as a woman discussing weather, "the archive dies with me unless it lives somewhere else too. You're the somewhere else."

"They won't take you."

"Don't be sentimental. Sentiment is how archives burn." She'd tapped the ledger. "Copy."

So Xanthe copied. And somewhere between the requisition orders and the fragmentary field reports—the villages that bloomed out of season, the ward-keepers who filed complaints and then vanished from every subsequent record—Xanthe stopped being able to pretend this was somebody else's history.

Because there it was, in a report dated eighty-eight years back, in a clerk's cramped hand: *subjects report loss of affect following contact with flowering hedge. Recommend quarantine. Recommend further study.* And beneath it, in different ink, added later: *study concluded. no further action required.*

No further action required. A hundred years of quiet theft, signed off in bureaucratic ink, and then erased so thoroughly that not even the ward-keepers' own order remembered doing it.

"It's been feeding the whole time," Xanthe said. They were lying on their back on the walkway, staring up at the vaulted dark where the ceiling should have been but wasn't visible, which was its own kind of mercy. "A century. Slowly. Village by village. And when the free cities got too big, too warded—" they gestured vaguely upward, at the whole weight of Vell sitting on its foundations above them, "—it pulled the border wards down instead. Stopped being patient."

"People are usually patient right up until they're hungry enough not to be." Juniper blew out a long breath. "There's something else. Page sixty-one. The cipher shifts halfway through—older hand, older grammar. I think it's the original binding rite. Or part of one."

"Can you read it?"

"Some of it. Enough to know it needs things we don't have." She hesitated, and Xanthe had learned enough of Juniper Sable in three nights to know that hesitation meant the worst sentence of the evening was coming. "Enough to know that whoever bound it used memories as anchors. Living ones. Freely given."

"Freely given," Xanthe repeated. "Who gives away a memory freely?"

"Someone who loves more than they want to keep." Juniper said it lightly, like a joke, and neither of them laughed.

That was when Xanthe heard the sound.

At first they took it for water—the cistern was full of water-sounds, echoes that traveled strange distances through flooded channels. But this was footsteps. Many of them. Boots on stone, moving with the particular discipline of people who had trained together, spreading out rather than clustering, covering ground.

Juniper heard it a half-breath later. Her lamp went out between one heartbeat and the next. In the sudden black, her hand found Xanthe's wrist and squeezed once: *quiet.*

The footsteps came closer. And with them came light—not lamplight, which wandered and guttered, but a cold white glare that slid along the walls like a blade feeling for bone. Ward-light. The kind the inquisitors carried, the kind that showed truth whether truth wanted showing or not.

"There," said a voice above them, from the direction of the access shaft. Calm. Almost gentle. "See? Someone's been living down here. Mind the books—I want the books intact."

Hale.

Xanthe had never met Inquisitor Hale, but the whole night market knew his name by now, passed around in whispers the way plague news used to travel. He'd arrived four days ago with a warrant and a reputation: the man who cleaned out Corvess in a single season, who never raised his voice, who offered every suspect the same fair trial before the same certain verdict. Memory-mongers, he'd come for. Charm-sellers. Thieves of the interior.

He'd come for exactly this.

"Side channel," Juniper breathed, so close to Xanthe's ear it was barely sound. "Thirty paces east. It floods, but—"

The ward-light swept the walkway.

There was no time for thirty paces. There was time for one decision, and Xanthe made it the way they made all their decisions: fast, selfish on paper, and then not at all what the paper said.

They shoved the ledger into Juniper's arms.

"Go," they whispered.

Her grip tightened on their wrist. In the dark he couldn't see her face, only feel the argument in her fingers—*no, you, you're faster, you're the one they can't afford to lose*—and Xanthe pried her off, gently, the way you'd take something fragile from a child's hands.

"They're here for the archive. If we both run, they chase us both. If one of us runs, they have to choose." A lie, maybe. Probably. But it was the kind of lie that kept people moving. "Take the west stairs. Don't stop for anything. Don't come back for me."

"Xanthe—"

"Sentiment is how archives burn," Xanthe said. "Go."

The ward-light found them both a moment later, and everything after that happened quickly, the way bad things do.

* * *

There were eight of them. Xanthe counted between heartbeats, an old habit, a thief's arithmetic: eight inquisitors in grey, ward-lights held high, wands of cold iron capped with focus-crystals. And at their center, a tall man with a scholar's stoop and a soldier's stillness, whose eyes went first to the ledger in Juniper's arms and only afterward to the two people holding it.

"Inquisitor Hale," Juniper said. Of course she knew him. Archivists knew everyone dangerous; it was a professional courtesy. "You're trespassing on municipal waterworks."

"I have a writ." He produced it without flourish, held it where the light could confirm it. "Signed by the Crown's justice, authorizing search and seizure of contraband histories. You're Juniper Sable. Unlicensed keeper of restricted texts. I've been looking forward to meeting you."

"Funny. I've read your file. All two lines of it. Very mysterious man."

"Two lines is generous." His gaze moved to Xanthe, and something flickered there—not recognition exactly, but the particular attention of a man cataloguing. "And your companion?"

"A customer," Xanthe said. "I sell charms. Nothing illegal about remembering things."

"No," Hale agreed mildly. "Nothing illegal about remembering. Only about selling it." He gestured, and two inquisitors moved toward Juniper with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this many times. "The ledger, please. And any copies. And then we'll discuss your arrangements upstairs, where it's warmer."

Xanthe's mind ran the exits and found them all shut. East channel: flooded, and two inquisitors already posted at its mouth. West stairs: Juniper's route, and Juniper was already gone up it, which meant it was watched or soon would be. The main shaft: eight armed professionals and a writ.

Which left the thing Xanthe never used. The thing they'd sworn off five years ago, when the law changed and using it became a hanging offense instead of a living.

Their own magic. Not charm-craft, not the careful siphoning of a stranger's wedding day into a glass bead. The raw version. The version that had gotten them fed as a child by a grandmother who said *you were born with open hands, little one, the trick is learning when to close them.*

The inquisitors reached Juniper. One took her arm. She didn't resist—she was smarter than resistance—but her eyes cut sideways to Xanthe, once, quick, and in them was no fear at all, only instruction: *don't do anything stupid.*

Sorry, Juniper. Sentiment.

Xanthe opened their hands.

Not literally—though the gesture helped, some old somatic habit, palms up, fingers spread, the way their grandmother had taught them to *listen*. Because that was what the deep craft was, underneath everything: listening. Every person was full of memories, and memories were loud, and most people spent their whole lives shouting inside themselves without knowing it. A charm-thief eavesdropped. A memory-worker could turn up the volume.

Xanthe seized the nearest inquisitor's surface thoughts and *shouted.*

It wasn't subtle. Subtlety was for markets. This was a bell rung inside a skull: Xanthe grabbed hold of whatever memory floated closest to the man's surface—a kitchen, a woman laughing, flour on someone's nose, something warm and ordinary and devastatingly human—and hurled it outward, amplified, unshielded, into every mind within reach.

The effect was like dropping a lamp into a room full of sleeping people.

All eight inquisitors staggered. Two went down entirely, knees cracking on wet stone, hands clapped over their ears though there was nothing to hear—the memory wasn't sound, it was *presence*, a stranger's most private moment suddenly occupying the inside of your head at full volume. The one who'd been holding Juniper's arm let go and sat down hard, weeping without knowing why, saying *ma, ma, ma* to no one.

Even Hale rocked back a step. Only a step. His ward-light wavered and steadied. But his composure cracked visibly, just for a moment, and what showed underneath wasn't anger.

It was grief. Old, banked, carefully maintained grief—and Xanthe, still elbow-deep in other people's heads, caught the edge of why before he slammed his mental doors: a name. *Tobin.* A face gone. A promise made to ashes.

Then the doors shut, and Hale was only Hale again, pale and upright and pointing at Xanthe with iron focus.

"Take them," he said. "Carefully. They're stronger than they look."

*Stronger than they look.* Xanthe was already moving, because the burst had bought exactly what bursts buy: seconds. They went over the walkway rail into the side channel, into water thigh-deep and freezing, and the shock of it nearly folded them in half. Behind them, splashes—two inquisitors recovering fast, too fast, the burst wearing off quicker than it should have, these were trained minds, warded minds—

Juniper.

The thought arrived whole and terrible. Xanthe looked back once, from the mouth of the channel, water streaming off their hair into their eyes.

Juniper stood between two staggering inquisitors with the ledger hugged to her chest, and she wasn't running. She was buying Xanthe the seconds they were spending, standing in plain sight, drawing every eye, and as the ward-light steadied on her face she did something extraordinary: she smiled at Hale, lifted the ledger slightly, and said, in a carrying voice designed to echo through the whole cistern:

"The crown has been eating its own people for a hundred years, Inquisitor. Read it before you burn it. That's all I ask. *Read it.*"

Then they took her arms, and she went, and the last Xanthe saw of Juniper Sable was the top of her head disappearing up the access shaft, upright, unbowed, an archivist to the end—being filed away.

Xanthe turned and swam.

* * *

The channel let out through a storm grate into the canal district, and Xanthe came up out of the black water gasping, shaking, alive. They hauled themselves onto the moss-slick stones beneath a bridge where the watch never bothered to look, and lay there dripping while their heart tried to climb out through their teeth.

They had the copies. That was the arithmetic of survival, the cold sum: eight slips of thief's shorthand tucked in an oilskin pouch against their skin, the essential pages, the requisitions, the seal, the fragments of the rite. Juniper was in chains somewhere, and the archive was in inquisitor hands, and the knowledge lived on in one soaked thief lying under a bridge smelling of canal.

*One of us runs,* Xanthe had said. *They have to choose.*

They hadn't chosen. They'd taken both the ledger and the archivist, and they'd have taken Xanthe too if water and a stolen kitchen memory hadn't intervened. So much for the lie. So much for the clean arithmetic. Juniper had stood in that light and paid for Xanthe's escape with her freedom, and she'd done it smiling, and she'd done it *on purpose*, and there was no version of this where Xanthe got to feel clever about survival.

Guilt came first, then fury, and fury was easier, so Xanthe fed it. Fury at Hale with his writ and his two-line file and his locked door hiding Tobin-whatever-he'd-lost. Fury at the crown that had signed requisitions for villages' worth of souls and stamped them with a crown-over-tree. Fury at a world where the stars went out and the law responded by arresting librarians.

But underneath the fury, quieter and worse, was the memory-burst itself. Eight strangers' minds, and Xanthe had torn through them like a pickpocket through a coat. Somewhere in that squad was a man who'd been thinking about flour on his mother's nose, and now he'd carry the violation of that forever alongside the memory itself. Xanthe had done to him what the Verdant King did to villages. Smaller scale. Same shape.

*The cost of survival,* their grandmother used to say, *is always paid in the same coin you're trying to save.*

Xanthe sat up. Water ran out of their clothes and pooled on the stone. Across the canal, Vell glittered under a starless sky, ten thousand windows lit by frightened people, and somewhere in the palace district a thing wearing a king's body was growing a garden out of everything those people had ever been.

Juniper had said *read it.* Fine. Xanthe would make sure it got read. They'd get the copies to someone who could finish the translation, someone who understood the rite, someone with wards and authority and a grudge—

The answer arrived before the question finished forming, and Xanthe laughed, once, bitterly, at the shape of it.

Ilsa Vane. Disgraced ward-keeper. The one person in Vell who'd know what a broken border ward looked like from the inside—and who owed the truth an apology, if half of what Xanthe had read in the ledger was right about who built those wards and who signed off on the plans.

Getting to Ilsa Vane meant crossing a city under martial law with inquisitor squads on every corner, carrying contraband cipher, with Xanthe's own name presumably on a list by now. *Memory-monger, wanted for questioning.* Maybe worse than questioning, after tonight.

Xanthe wrung out their sleeves, checked the oilskin pouch one more time—eight slips, dry, intact—and stood up under the starless sky.

"Hold on, Juniper," they told the dark. "I'm coming back with better company."

Somewhere across the city, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bells. The *curfew* bells, earlier than usual, the city pulling its blankets over its head. Xanthe pulled up their hood and started walking.
