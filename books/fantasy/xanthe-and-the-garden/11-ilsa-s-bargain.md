## 11. Ilsa's Bargain

Ilsa Vane had not slept in three days, and the city knew it the way a body knows weather.

She stood on the roof of the Saltgate watchhouse with her collar up against a wind that smelled wrong—green, wet, growing, when it should have smelled of chimney smoke and cold iron—and she watched Vell strangle itself. Curfew lanterns made islands of the streets. Between them, the dark had teeth now. Inquisitor patrols moved in pairs, then fours, their white hoods pale as bone under the absent stars. Somewhere south, beyond walls that no longer kept anything out or in, the countryside was blooming.

She'd read Juniper Sable's name on the raid manifest an hour ago. *Archivist. Sedition. Resisting inquiry.* And one line that had made her go back and read it twice: *Recovered materials forwarded to Inquisitor Hale, priority seal.*

Priority seal meant the palace wanted it before anyone else saw it. Priority seal meant it mattered.

Ilsa had spent eleven years learning to read manifests the way other people read faces, and every instinct she had was screaming at her tonight. Her order—the Wardens of the Circle, what was left of them—had gone silent since the wards fell. No dispatches. No orders. Just a single message burned into her private slate in the old cipher, four words long: *Stand down. Await garden.*

Await garden. She'd told herself it was code she didn't have clearance for. She'd told herself a lot of things in eleven years.

The hairline crack she'd found in the border wards last week still sang to her when she let herself listen—a thin green note underneath the failing hum of wardstone, vegetal and patient, like something breathing under floorboards. She'd reported it. The report had come back stamped *noted* by an office that should have sent three senior keepers and a survey team inside a day.

Noted. Nothing more.

So now she watched the streets, because watching was all she had left, and because she'd posted herself where the routes from the Cistern district would funnel any survivor of tonight's raid. If Juniper Sable had run, someone would be running with them. Someone carrying whatever the palace wanted badly enough to burn a hundred-year archive over.

She didn't expect the thief to come to *her*. But she'd learned the shape of desperation, and desperation ran downhill toward the gates, and the Saltgate was the only gate still answering bells.

* * *

Xanthe felt the ambush before they saw it—not magic, nothing so useful, just the old animal arithmetic of a life spent in doorways. The alley mouth ahead was too empty. A cart sat at the far end with its mule unhitched. Nobody unhitches a mule at curfew.

They stopped. Considered the rooftops—too slick with dew, and their arms were lead after hauling Juniper's ledger through half of Vell. Considered going back. Going back meant circling toward the Cistern district, toward the smoke and the white hoods, toward the place where Juniper had looked at them through a wall of inquisitor staves and said, clear as a bell: *Run, you idiot. It's worth ten of me.*

"Come out slowly," Xanthe said to the darkness behind them. "I've got a knife and a very bad attitude."

"You've got a knife," said a woman's voice, level as a mason's line, "and I've got a ward-cord that will drop you before your arm finishes moving. Drop the pouch."

Ilsa Vane stepped out of the shadow of a drainpipe with a lantern held low and a coil of braided silver wire in her off hand. Xanthe knew her by reputation the way everyone in Vell's underworld did: the keeper who'd built the eastern anchor points, the one who'd been stripped of rank after the Ember Street failure five years ago. Five years ago. Everyone's disasters came from five years ago lately.

"Warden Vane." Xanthe set the pouch down with exaggerated care. "Heard you were dead."

"Heard you were smart." Her eyes went to the oilskin bundle on the cobbles. "What is it?"

"It's nothing. It's laundry."

"It's leaking," Ilsa said, and it was true—Juniper's ledger bled a faint light at its seams, the cipher-glyphs glowing through oiled cloth like veins under skin. "That's archive-light. That's *palace-seal* work, if my eyes haven't rotted. Pick it up. Slowly."

Xanthe picked it up. Weighed the odds. Found them bad. "If you're taking me to Hale, I want it noted I cooperated beautifully."

"I'm not taking you anywhere yet." Ilsa hadn't moved the ward-cord. Her face in the lantern light was all planes and exhaustion, forty-some years of it, and her eyes had the particular flatness Xanthe had seen in soldiers after a battle goes wrong—not cruelty, just the end of pretending. "One question first. Answer it true and you walk. Lie, and you'll spend the night in a ward-circle that makes your worst hangover look like a lullaby."

"Ask."

"The wards fell because something ate them. I've seen the residue. I need to hear you say what's in that ledger, because if it says what I think it says, my whole order has been—" Her jaw worked. "What's in the ledger, thief?"

And there it was. Not *give me the contraband.* Not *you're under arrest.* A question asked like a prayer by someone who already suspected the answer and needed it spoken aloud to make it real.

Xanthe had spent five years lying professionally. It was, in fact, the entire business model. But there are moments when a lie would be a kind of theft worse than anything they'd ever done—stealing from someone the last clean thing they owned.

"There's a thing wearing the king," Xanthe said quietly. "It's been eating memories for a hundred years. Your order built the wards that kept everyone from noticing. And your order knew."

The silence afterward had weight. The lantern hissed. Somewhere two streets over, an inquisitor's whistle rose and fell.

"I found a crack in the border line," Ilsa said finally, and her voice had changed—smaller, scraped raw. "Last week. Before everything. It sang. Green magic, old growth. I reported it through proper channels." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "*Noted.* That's what came back. One word. Eleven years of service, and the answer to the end of the world is *noted.*"

"Because they already knew," Xanthe said. "Open it. Read it yourself. I'm not going anywhere—I couldn't get across this city without tripping over a white hood, and we both know it."

Ilsa hesitated. Then she holstered the ward-cord—an act of trust or surrender, hard to tell which—and took the ledger in both hands like something that might bite.

* * *

She read by the lantern, standing, turning pages with a warden's care while the thief watched her face come apart by degrees.

Juniper's transcription laid it out in the old royal cipher, cross-referenced with fragments of the burned originals: the founding of the Wardens of the Circle, one hundred years ago. Not founded to protect the realm from outside threats. Founded *by the crown* to maintain a perimeter that would conceal the Verdant King's early spread—to keep farmers from seeing the fields that bloomed where villages had been, to keep travelers on roads that curved politely around the garden's edge. The wards were never a shield pointed outward. They were a blindfold, worn by an entire kingdom, buckled on from within.

And there, in a marginal hand older than the others, a line Juniper had translated twice to be sure: *The harvest continues by consent of the Crown. The Warden-Keepers shall be told only what is needful. What is needful is little.*

"My mother served twenty-two years," Ilsa said. Her voice came out steady, which somehow made it worse. "My mother died a Keeper of the Second Circle with honors. She thought she was holding the line against the dark." She closed the ledger. Opened it again, as if the words might have rearranged themselves into something survivable. "We were the blindfold. We were proud of it."

Xanthe said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either cruelty or comfort, and both would have been theft.

"The Ember Street failure." Ilsa laughed again, quieter. "Five years ago. They blamed me for a ward-anchor collapse. Stripped my rank, put me on night patrol, told the order I'd grown careless." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake. "It wasn't a collapse. Was it. Something was testing the perimeter. Testing *me.* And when I noticed—when I actually did my job and *noticed*—they buried me instead of the truth."

"That's why they left you alive," Xanthe said. "A disgraced keeper nobody listens to. You can scream the truth from the Saltgate and it's just the madwoman who failed at Ember Street."

The whistle sounded again, closer. Ilsa moved without seeming to decide to—snuffing the lantern with her palm, pulling Xanthe back against the wall into the deep shadow beside the drainpipe, her body angled between the thief and the street. Boots passed. White hoods. Four of them, walking a grid pattern, professional and unhurried.

They waited until the sound faded. In the dark, Ilsa spoke barely above breath:

"Hale will have this city locked down by morning. Every gate, every sewer grate, every rooftop route your kind loves. He's thorough. It's his best quality and it's going to get us both killed." A pause. "You want to reach the capital."

It wasn't a question. Xanthe decided, in the space of a heartbeat, that lying to this woman was finished. "The free cities first. Then the capital. There's a rite. There might be a way to unbind the king without killing him—if he's even alive under there. Juniper paid for this knowledge with their freedom. I'm not wasting it."

"And you can't do it alone, and the only person in Vell who knows the old ward-lines well enough to move unseen knows she's got nothing left to lose." Isla exhaled slowly. "Gods. This is what it comes to. Me and a memory-thief."

"We could always part ways bitter enemies," Xanthe offered. "Very traditional. Lots of precedent."

Something twitched at the corner of Ilsa's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first in what looked like years. "Here's my bargain, thief, and it's the only one on the table. You carry that ledger, you guard it with your life, and you tell me everything, no edits, from here to the capital. In exchange, I get you out of Vell tonight through lines only a keeper knows, and I don't put these chains on you that I've been carrying since sundown hoping I'd catch you."

"You were hoping to arrest me?"

"I was hoping arresting you would make the world simple again." She pulled a coiled length of chain from beneath her coat and let it fall to the cobbles, deliberately, the sound bright and final in the dark. "It doesn't. Simple ended when the stars did."

Xanthe studied her. The flat exhausted eyes. The dead mother. The eleven wasted years. Everything about Ilsa Vane screamed *bad idea,* and everything about tonight said *only idea.*

"One condition," Xanthe said. "When this is done—whatever's left of done—you help me break Juniper out. Whatever prison, whatever hole they're in. That's the price of passage."

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. "Inquisitors don't release prisoners."

"Inquisitors don't. Keepers might remember how."

Another silence. Then Ilsa bent, picked up the chains, and threw them into the drainage channel, where they landed with a splash that sounded almost like laughter.

"Sewers first," she said. "There's a maintenance gate under Saltgate that predates the order. Predates the *city*, probably. Stay close, touch nothing that glows, and if you see flowers growing somewhere flowers shouldn't be—you tell me before you touch them. Understood?"

"Loud and clear."

They moved out together into the starless dark, the disgraced keeper leading and the thief following, the stolen ledger warm between them like a coal neither could put down.

Behind them, at the mouth of the alley, the shadows thickened—and out of the cleft between two buildings, pale in the moonless night, a single white flower opened its petals and turned, slowly, to follow the sound of their footsteps.

Neither of them looked back to see it bloom.
