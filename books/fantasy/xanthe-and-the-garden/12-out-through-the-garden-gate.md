## 12. Out Through the Garden Gate

The bells started at dusk, and they did not stop.

Xanthe had lived in Vell long enough to know its languages of sound. The market bells, bright and greedy. The temple bells, slow as honey. The alarm bell in the Wardhouse tower, rung once a year on Remembrance Day, so ceremonial it barely counted. This was none of those. This was all of them at once, overlapping, ragged, as if every bell-ringer in the city had been told to ring until their arms gave out and no one would tell them when.

"Curfew," Ilsa said, from the doorway of the cistern stair. She'd wedged a rusted iron bar across it an hour ago and now stood listening with her head tilted like a woman reading weather. "Full martial law. Listen—boots between the bells. They're sweeping district by district."

Through the grate above them, boot-heel rhythm echoed off wet stone. Somewhere close, a man shouted *open in the name of the crown* and something heavy went through something wooden.

"They'll find Juniper's dead-drop list within the hour," Ilsa went on. "Names, safehouses, the lot. Which means by morning there will be nowhere in Vell with a door we can trust."

Xanthe sat on the last step with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, feeling it breathe. Not literally. But ever since the raid, since Juniper's voice cracking across the cistern—*run, you idiot, don't you dare look back*—the book had felt less like paper than like a held breath. Every time Xanthe touched it, the stolen memories in their own stock stirred, whispering among themselves in voices that were not quite theirs.

"We can't outrun a sweep on foot," Xanthe said. "Not with half the city's gates sealed."

"I'm not planning to use the gates."

Ilsa pulled back the tarp she'd laid over the cistern's lowest channel. Black water moved down there, sluggish, smelling of everything a city wants to forget. A maintenance walkway ran along the wall, barely wider than a coffin laid sideways, vanishing into dark.

"The sewer runs northwest under Tanner's Row, past the old tannery outfall, and comes up outside the walls in the reed marsh," she said. "It was built three hundred years ago for exactly this. Ward-keepers called it the Garden Gate." Her mouth twisted. "Ward humor. Everything important gets a pretty name."

"You've used it?"

"Once." She didn't elaborate. Ilsa Vane didn't elaborate; she issued statements like a clerk issuing fines. "Take the ledger strap over your shoulder, both hands free. If I say duck, you duck. If I say swim, you swim. If I say nothing—" she looked at Xanthe, and in the lantern light her face was older than her years, "—then keep moving anyway."

Xanthe thought about arguing. About the fact that they'd known this woman for less than a day, that she'd started the night intending arrest, that trust between a disgraced ward-keeper and a wanted thief was a coin neither could afford to spend. Then the bells crashed again, nearer, and somewhere overhead a woman began screaming—not fear, Xanthe realized, but grief, the raw kind, the kind you only make when soldiers take someone from your house.

They climbed down into the dark.

* * *

The Garden Gate was a country unto itself.

Xanthe had imagined sewers as filth and nothing else, but this was older than filth—a vaulted artery of fitted stone, dry-walked along its upper shelf, built by masons who'd carved running vine-patterns into the walls and then, apparently, felt embarrassed about it, because someone had chiseled the vines off in patches. Ward-marks remained in the gaps: small sigils of silver-inlaid tin, most of them dead, a few still faintly warm, humming against Xanthe's magic-sense like distant bees.

"You can feel those?" Ilsa asked, without turning. She walked with her lantern low, hood up, moving like water finding its level.

"A little. They're starving. Whatever fed them is gone."

"Everything's gone. The border wards died the same night your stars did." She said it flatly, but Xanthe heard the flinch underneath—the same night the wards died, Ilsa's whole life's work died with them. "We're walking inside a corpse, thief. Try not to wake anything."

They walked. Hours, maybe. The channel bent and branched; Ilsa chose branches without hesitation, counting junctions under her breath. Once they passed a side gallery where pale fungus grew in perfect spirals, each ring a hand-span wide, and Ilsa stopped, held up a fist, studied it for a long ten-count before moving on. She didn't explain, and Xanthe didn't ask. There was a grammar to silence, and they were both fluent.

Twice they heard the sweep above them—muffled thunder of doors, orders barked, a dog whining—and twice they pressed into alcoves behind curtains of hanging root and let the city hunt itself.

The third time was different.

Lantern light ahead. Not reflected, not filtered—direct, swinging, coming toward them around the next bend. Ilsa killed her lamp and pulled Xanthe into a drainage mouth barely deep enough for one. They ended up chest to chest in the dark, Xanthe's back against cold stone, Ilsa's palm flat over Xanthe's mouth, her other hand holding a knife whose existence Xanthe had somehow failed to notice all day.

Boots on the walkway. Two men, maybe three. Inquisitor grey, from the smell of the lamp-oil they favored—something resinous, expensive, meant to announce itself.

"—swept Cistern Row already. Nothing."

"Hale says the archivist had help. Someone who knew the tunnels." A pause; the boots slowed. "You hear that?"

Xanthe's heart was loud enough to be treason. Against their chest, through two layers of wool, they could feel Ilsa's heartbeat too—slow, infuriatingly slow, a metronome set to *patience*. Her thumb pressed once against Xanthe's jaw. *Still.*

"There's nothing down here," the first man said finally. "Whole ward-line's dead. You want to go poking around a dead ward-line in the dark, be my guest."

The boots receded. The light shrank to a coin, then a thread, then nothing.

Ilsa kept her hand where it was another full minute. In the absolute black, with her breath steady against Xanthe's ear, she murmured, so quietly the words were more vibration than sound:

"Inquisitor Hale's men. He's good. He'll have the Garden Gate on his list by dawn, which means we have until dawn."

She stepped back. Relit the lantern. And Xanthe, alone in the dark for that one unguarded second, put a hand over their own sternum where her palm had been, and hated themselves a little for what they found there—not desire exactly, but the animal gratitude of being held while the world tried to kill you. It was a dangerous thing to feel about a woman with a knife and a cause.

They kept moving.

* * *

The tannery outfall was a mouth of corroded iron bars, and beyond it—air. Real air, cold and green-smelling, threading through the grating like fingers.

Ilsa worked the lock mechanism with a strip of tin, muttering ward-phrases under her breath, and the old gate groaned open onto a night that made Xanthe stop breathing.

Stars. There were stars.

Not many—maybe a dozen, scattered and trembling, like embers refusing to admit the fire was out—but after five nights of absolute blank sky, after the whole city had stood in the streets staring upward like mourners at an empty grave, a single star was a miracle. Xanthe gripped the iron frame and stared, and felt something crack open in their chest that had been sealed since the night the sky went dark.

"They came back," they whispered.

"Some did." Isla shouldered past them into the reeds, businesslike, but she looked up too, just for a second. Just long enough. "The scholars will argue about why for a hundred years. Move."

The marsh lay flat and silver under the partial starlight, reeds hissing, water standing in black mirrors between tussocks. Behind them, Vell's wall rose against the sky, and along its top burned watchfires—too many, spaced wrong, a crown of flame. Drums carried across the water. Even from here, the city sounded like a held-down animal.

They waded north through the reeds for an hour, following a path only Ilsa could see, until the ground firmed into meadow, and the meadow became orchard.

That was when Xanthe understood that the world had not merely broken. It had *changed sides*.

Apples hung in the trees. Ripe ones—blushing red, heavy, some split and fermenting sweet on the branch, wasps drunk among them. It was the dead of winter. Frost silvered the grass between the rows, and the trees wore frost too, ice feathering the bark—and fruit. Fruit and frost together, abundance and killing cold holding hands, which was not how seasons worked, which was not how anything worked.

"Ilsa." Xanthe's voice came out thin. "Look."

"I see it." She'd stopped. Lantern raised. Her face, when she turned, held something Xanthe hadn't seen in her yet—not anger, not guilt. Fear. Clean, undecorated fear. "This is the Marrow orchard. It burned four years ago. Blight took the whole east row." She touched a trunk. Under the frost, the bark was warm. Genuinely warm, like skin. "And it's *fruiting*."

A memory surfaced in Xanthe—not their own, one of the stock, a farmer's memory bought cheap off a dying man years ago: standing in this exact orchard, watching the burn crews, weeping into his sleeve. The memory recognized the place. The memory was *happy* about it.

Xanthe pushed it down and told Ilsa, quickly, what they'd glimpsed behind the nobleman's eyes weeks ago—the green light, the king's smile wearing something else's patience—and what Juniper's ledger claimed. That the garden wasn't spreading like fire spreads. It was spreading like *memory* spreads. Like something remembering itself outward.

"Then every mile it covers," Ilsa said slowly, "is a mile it remembers. Or a mile it's eaten the remembering of." She looked down the orchard rows, into darkness that seemed, tonight, to have texture. "My order's oldest records called the wards 'the fence around forgetting.' We thought that was poetry." Her laugh had no floor under it. "It was a job description."

They cut west, away from the orchard, looking for the road.

* * *

They found the road because they tripped over it.

One moment: open meadow, frost, the half-repaired stars. The next: a wall of hedge, chest-high, dense as woven iron, running to both horizons. Except hedges didn't run to horizons. Hedges belonged to lanes and estates, tidy and bounded. This one had *ambition*.

Ilsa put her lantern close. The hedge was hawthorn—or had been. Its twigs had fused into lattices too regular for growth, patterns repeating with mathematical insistence, and threaded through the black wood ran veins of the same green light Xanthe had seen behind the king's eyes. When Xanthe reached out—against every instinct, hands moving before sense caught up—the light *leaned toward them*. The whole hedge shifted, a slow tidal lean, like grass bending toward sun.

"Don't touch it." Ilsa hauled them back by the collar. "Thief, I swear on every ward I ever raised—"

"It knows me." Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "Ilsa. It's not reaching for you. It's reaching for *this*." They patted the ledger through the oilcloth. "Or for what's in me. The memories. It can smell them."

For a long moment Ilsa studied them, lantern-light jumping in her eyes. Xanthe watched her decide something.

"Then we don't go through it," she said. "We go around."

Around took the rest of the night. The hedge kept pace with them, or they kept pace with it—it was impossible to tell direction anymore, with the stars few and unfamiliar and the land subtly reshaped, hills where maps promised flats. Twice the hedge threw out runners, exploratory tendrils that crossed the meadow at walking speed, patient as bureaucracy, forcing them to detour. Once they crossed a farmstead where the farmhouse stood intact but the fields around it had gone to bloom—winter roses, acres of them, open and scentless—and no one answered Ilsa's hail. Doors stood open. A kettle sat on a cold hearth. A child's shoe lay in the yard, pointing at the hedge.

Neither of them said the obvious thing. Neither had to.

Dawn found them on a rise overlooking what remained of the King's Road—and the road was losing.

Hedgerow had swallowed it in sections, great green fists closing over cobblestone, and where the road still showed, it showed *wrong*: stones heaved up and reordered into spirals, milestones repurposed as trellises for climbing flowers that opened, as the light strengthened, with a sound like a sigh. An apple tree had grown straight through a waymarker shrine, splitting the little roofed niche in half, and the shrine's carved blessing—TRAVELER, THE KING KEEPS YOUR WAY—now framed a knot of white blossoms instead of the king's face.

"It's beautiful," Xanthe said, before they could stop themselves. And it was. That was the horror of it. Every instinct of craft in them—the eye that knew beauty when it saw it, that had spent years bottling beauty for people who'd lost theirs—recognized this as beauty, real beauty, the kind that makes you forget what things cost. "That's the trap, isn't it? It's gorgeous, and you want to stay, and staying is how it eats you."

"Yes." Ilsa's jaw was set. "My grandmother tended a hedgerow maze at the summer fair. Told me: never follow a hedge that follows you back." She scanned the swallowed road, then the meadows flanking it, calculating. "There's a drover's track south of here. Drovers are stubborn people; they'll have cut it clear recently, or died trying. We follow it to Thornhollow, resupply, and pick up the pilgrim road to the capital."

"Thornhollow." Xanthe tested the name against the ledger's map, drawn from Juniper's memory of century-old documents. "There was a market town there. Four thousand people."

"Was."

They came down off the rise as the sun cleared the hedgerows—sunrise arriving late and strange, filtered green through leaves that shouldn't exist—and followed a smell of smoke that might have been hearths or might have been burning.

* * *

The drover's track held. Barely. For two hours they walked a corridor of trampled mud between living walls, hedge pressing closer year by year—no, month by month, Ilsa corrected grimly, judging by the growth rings she snapped off and examined like a coroner. The track narrowed. Their shoulders brushed thorns. And all the while, Xanthe's stolen memories grew louder.

That was new. Since the raid, since the cistern, the fragments had been whispering. Now they were *organizing*. The widow's wedding day hummed in harmony with the soldier's sunset. The farmer's burned orchard chimed against the nobleman's glimpse of green-eyed king. Strangers' lives, stitched together in Xanthe's chest for five years, were discovering they had something in common.

They were all afraid of the garden. And they all, in some buried way, missed it.

*Xanthe*, whispered the widow's voice, from inside Xanthe's own skull. *Come see. Come home.*

"Stop that," Xanthe muttered aloud.

Isla glanced over. "Talking to yourself is either madness or strategy. Which?"

"Neither. Housekeeping." Xanthe flexed their hands, the way they did when a charm fought the transfer. "The memories are pulling toward the hedge. Like iron filings. Whatever's growing out there, it's calling its own home."

"Can you quiet them?"

"Maybe. Ask me when it matters."

It mattered an hour later, when the track crested a low hill and Thornhollow appeared below—and Thornhollow was *blooming*.

Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys, ordinary and domestic. Market stalls lined the square. People moved through the streets doing the small errands of any morning. And over everything—roofs, doorways, the well, the church spire—grew flowers. Climbing roses in colors Xanthe had no names for, soft as skin, opening in the winter air. The whole town looked dipped in blossom, preserved like a bride, like something arranged rather than abandoned.

From the distance, it was lovely.

Then a woman in the square laughed, high and delighted at nothing, and the laugh repeated—same pitch, same timing, note for note—thirty seconds later. From a different throat.

Ilsa's hand found Xanthe's arm. "Down."

They dropped behind the hill's brow. Below, life went on in Thornhollow, looping. A man swept his step, reached the end of the flags, turned, swept again. Children skipped rope to a rhyme that never changed verses. A baker set loaves in a window, took them out, set them in again. Everyone smiling. Everyone moving. Nobody *arriving anywhere*.

"Juniper's accounts," Xanthe breathed. "The villages from a hundred years ago. 'Alive, and hollow.'" Their stomach had become a stone. "They're not dead. They're *stuck*. Like charms. Like—gods, like the ones I sell." Memories worn smooth by repetition until nothing new could get in. Whole people, looped. "The Verdant King isn't killing them. It's *keeping* them. Perfect days, forever."

"And feeding on whatever it siphons off the top," Ilsa finished quietly. She crouched, eyes on the town, and Xanthe watched her rebuild herself piece by visible piece—fear folded away, guilt converted to fuel, the clerk of catastrophe issuing herself new orders. "We need food, rope, and information, and there's a town full of smiling people who may or may not notice us. We go in quiet, we take nothing from anyone who's still in there, and we leave before the loops change."

"Loops change?"

"Everything changes eventually. That's usually when the eating happens."

They went down through an orchard edge where frost and fruit shared the branches, past a shrine where the king's carved face had been gently, almost tenderly, overgrown—green light pulsing beneath the bark like a sleeping pulse—and entered Thornhollow by its western lane, quiet as thieves.

Which was, Xanthe supposed, appropriate.

The town smelled of bread and roses. The loops went on around them: sweep, turn, sweep. Skip, chant, skip. Set out, take in, set out. Faces turned toward them as they passed—smiling faces, warm faces, faces that tracked them for exactly as long as the loop allowed and then slid away like water finding level. One old man at a gate looked directly into Xanthe's eyes and said, with enormous kindness, "Lovely morning," and thirty seconds later, passing him again on their backtrack, he said it again, identical, and Xanthe had to bite down hard on the inside of their cheek to keep from answering the second time.

At the square's edge stood an inn with a sign of a gilded pear. Its door stood open. Inside: supplies, surely. Rope, oil, bread, a map of the pilgrim road if they were lucky.

Xanthe stepped in first.

The common room was full of people sitting perfectly still, all facing the hearth, where a fire burned green.

Every head turned at once. Every face smiled. And in the green light, softly, in a chorus of voices that included the widow's, the soldier's, the farmer's—all of them, every stranger whose memory Xanthe had ever bottled, speaking from inside Xanthe's own ribs—

*Welcome home,* said Thornhollow. *We saved you a chair.*
