## 13. The Village That Forgot Itself

The road to Amberlow smelled wrong.

Xanthe noticed it before anything else—the way you learn to, in their trade. Smell was memory's front door. Woodsmoke meant hearth, hearth meant home, home meant a hundred small attachments you could pry loose and sell by the dram. But this was woodsmoke without a hearth behind it, smoke with nothing burning, drifting over hedgerows that had no business being green in the dead of a starless winter.

"Smell that?" Ilsa said. She'd gone quiet an hour back, the way she did when the wards under her skin started itching. Xanthe had learned to read her the way you read weather. "Someone's baking bread."

"At dawn. In a village we can't see yet."

"Someone's baking bread," Ilsa repeated, "and there's no flour within forty miles. The granaries burned in the first wave. I saw the ash on the road."

Xanthe breathed in again. Bread, and under it something sweeter—cut grass, apple blossom, the green smell of a garden in high summer, all of it stacked wrong, like a song played in two keys at once. Their stolen memories stirred in the back of their skull, the way they always did near green things now. The widow's wedding day rustled like a page turning. The soldier's last sunset went gold at the edges.

*Hush,* Xanthe told them. *Not now.*

The memories didn't listen. They never listened anymore.

* * *

They came over the last rise at full light—or what passed for light now, a gray wash with no sun behind it, the sky the color of unpolished pewter—and stopped.

Amberlow sat in a bowl of fields, and the fields were in bloom.

Not wild. Not choked, the way the hedgerows on the south road had been, swallowing fences and milestones whole. This was *tended*. Rows of flowers ran where plow lines should have been, marigold and nightshade and a blue flower Xanthe didn't know, arranged with a precision that made their skin crawl. Between the rows, winter wheat stood golden and heavy-headed, ready for a harvest that had happened four months ago and couldn't happen again. An orchard at the village's edge hung with fruit—apples and cherries on the same branches, blossoms and rot in the same tree, everything ripe at once and nothing falling.

And the people were in the fields.

That was the first thing. Not hiding, not fleeing, not starving. Working. Bent over the flower rows with sickles, cutting blooms and laying them in baskets, moving with the unhurried contentment of people who had done this every morning of their lives.

"Are they harvesting flowers," Ilsa said flatly, "in winter. To feed whom?"

"Nobody," Xanthe said. Their mouth had gone dry. "It's not about the flowers. It's about the *motion*. Look at their faces."

Ilsa had a ward-keeper's eyes; she'd spent twenty years reading crowds for the first flicker of panic. She read this one and went pale. "They're smiling."

All of them. Every soul in the field, bent and cutting and smiling, the same mild, settled smile, as if joy were a uniform issued at the gate.

"Come on," Xanthe said, and hated how steady their voice came out. "Let's go meet the neighbors."

* * *

The first man they met was stacking cut flowers by a well. He was perhaps sixty, hands like tree roots, and when he saw them his smile widened by exactly the right amount to be welcoming.

"Travelers! Lovely morning."

"Morning," Xanthe said carefully. "We're looking for food. Shelter, if you've it. We can pay."

"Pay!" The man laughed, warm as fresh bread. "No need. There's plenty. There's always plenty now." He gestured at the fields with his sickle, an expansive, generous sweep. "Take what you like."

Ilsa, behind Xanthe, had gone very still. Xanthe knew why. Nobody said *take what you like* in a famine winter. Nobody said it with a straight face while the granaries burned.

"Thank you," Xanthe said. "Can I ask your name?"

"Of course." The man smiled.

He kept smiling.

He kept smiling, and the silence stretched out between them like a rope, and Xanthe watched him reach for the answer and find—nothing. Not confusion, exactly. Confusion would have been better. This was a smooth, pleasant blankness, a drawer that slid open to show polished empty wood, and the man looked into it with perfect serenity and said, "It's a lovely morning, isn't it?"

"Your name," Ilsa said, stepping forward, ward-craft crackling faint and desperate at her fingertips. "What do they call you? What did your mother call you?"

The man's smile didn't waver. But something behind his eyes shifted, the way light shifts on water when something large passes underneath. "She called me—" He stopped. He looked at his own hands. He looked at the flowers. "It's a lovely morning," he said again, and there was the faintest crack in it now, a hairline in the porcelain, and Xanthe's whole body went cold because they knew that crack. They'd heard it in their own voice, some nights, when a stolen memory pressed too hard against the walls of them and they couldn't remember which life was the borrowed one.

"Where's your family?" Xanthe asked softly. "Your wife? Your children?"

"Down the lane." He pointed with the sickle, happy again, relieved to have an answer. "The blue house with the roses."

They found the blue house. It had no roses. It had morning glories growing through the window frame and out the chimney, and inside it there was bread baking in an oven with no fuel, and a woman setting the table for six.

* * *

The woman's name was Marta. They knew because it was carved on the door lintel—*MARTA & OLLY, 1187*—and because a child's slate hung by the hearth with *MAMA MARTA* written on it in a wobbly hand, and because a loom in the corner had *MARTA'S* burned into the frame.

She didn't know it. She set six places at the table, smiling, and when Ilsa asked her who the six were, she looked at the plates with that same pleasant blankness and said, "Company. There's always company."

"Your children," Xanthe said. They'd moved into the house without being asked, drawn by something they couldn't name, some thief's instinct for a lock that had already been picked. "How many do you have?"

"Children." Marta said the word the way you'd repeat a word in a foreign language, tasting it, finding it pleasant but strange. "Yes. Children." She touched the slate by the hearth, lightly, the way you'd touch a wall to check if it was warm. Her fingers knew the slate. Her fingers knew everything—the weight of the loom shuttle, the seam of the doorframe worn smooth, the exact spot on the table where the sixth plate went—and none of it reached her face. Her body remembered her life entire. Her self had been cut out of it like the middle of a book, pages gone, covers intact.

"Mama Marta," Xanthe said, and pointed at the slate. "That's you. Who wrote this?"

Marta looked at the slate for a long time. The smile held. Underneath the smile, in the muscles around her eyes, something was straining—Xanthe could see it, a fish under ice, a shape moving beneath a surface that wouldn't break.

"I don't know," Marta said, and her voice was still pleasant, still mild, and it was the most horrifying thing Xanthe had ever heard, because she *didn't know*, and she wasn't even afraid. "Isn't that funny? I don't know, and it doesn't seem to matter. Nothing seems to matter. Isn't that nice?"

"No," Xanthe said. "No, it isn't."

"It's lovely," Marta said. "Everything is lovely now. You'll see. The garden takes such good care of us."

The words went through Xanthe like a blade finding the gap in armor. *The garden.* Ilsa's hand closed on their shoulder, hard, a warning: don't ask, don't push, not in front of her. But Xanthe was already somewhere else—five years back, a noble's memory, a green light behind the king's eyes, and a voice like roots growing through stone: *everything that blooms is mine.*

"Where is the garden?" Xanthe asked.

"Everywhere," Marta said, smiling. "It's so *big* now."

* * *

They walked the village. Xanthe made themselves do it, street by street, house by house, because some part of them—the merchant part, the part that counted costs—insisted on an accurate inventory of the damage.

Amberlow held perhaps two hundred souls. Every one of them was alive. Every one of them was fed, warm, unafraid, and *empty*. A blacksmith stood in his forge, smiling, unable to say what he made. A girl of maybe eight sat on a fence, smiling, and when Ilsa knelt and asked her name, the girl said, "I'm me," in a bright, pleased voice, as if that were the whole of it, as if *me* were a complete answer, and Ilsa had to walk away fast and stand with her back to the lane for a long moment, shoulders rigid, breathing like a woman hauling a rope.

"They're not dead," Ilsa said finally. "That's what I keep—people would call this a mercy, some people, the ones who've never—"

"They're not dead," Xanthe agreed. "They're *shelved*. Like stock." They heard their own voice come out flat and hard and were grateful for it. "Something took everything they were and left the bodies running. It's not a mercy, Ilsa. It's a harvest. The worst kind. The kind where the field keeps growing."

"The flowers," Ilsa said. "They're cutting flowers and stacking them and the stacks just sit there. It's not work, it's—"

"Motion," Xanthe said. "It's keeping the bodies busy so nobody notices the houses are empty." They stopped in the lane. Ahead of them, the church—small, whitewashed, ordinary—had ivy growing over it in patterns too regular to be ivy's own choosing, and through the open door came a smell of green and growing that made every stolen memory in Xanthe's head turn toward it at once like iron filings to a lodestone.

*No,* Xanthe thought at them. *We are not going in there.*

The memories subsided. Mostly.

* * *

It was in the churchyard that Xanthe found the graves, and the graves were where the thesis of the whole rotten thing stood written out plain.

New stones. Dozens of them. And carved on each one, in a fine, flowing hand that no village mason had cut:

*GIVEN WILLINGLY. REMEMBERED ALWAYS.*

"Nobody's buried here," Ilsa said. She'd knelt, pressed her palm to the earth. "No digging. These were set, not dug. It's theater. It's a *receipt*."

"It's a trophy wall," Xanthe said. They crouched by the nearest stone and read it again and felt the cold go all the way through. *Given willingly.* That was the lie at the center of it, the signature move. The Verdant King didn't just take. It made you *glad*. It made the taking feel like blooming. Marta smiling over her empty table—somewhere inside that hollowed-out woman was the moment she'd said yes, or thought she'd said yes, or been made to feel that she had, and that moment had been kept and the rest of her thrown away.

Xanthe stood up. Their hands were shaking, and they looked at them with a kind of clinical interest, because the shaking wasn't fear. They knew fear. Fear was familiar, fear was *stock in trade*.

This was rage.

"Ilsa," they said. "My satchel. Give me my satchel."

* * *

Here is what Xanthe had never told anyone, not Juniper, not Ilsa, not the night market full of clients who thought they knew what they were buying: a memory-charm, properly made, holds the whole of what was taken. The seller keeps a copy—that's the cost of the craft, the fragments that stay, the strangers' lives rattling around in your skull—but the charm holds it *entire*, every sense, every weight. A widow's wedding day, sold in Vell for three silvers, contained a whole morning: rain on the awning, her mother's hands pinning the veil, the specific terror and certainty of the moment before the door opened.

Xanthe had two hundred and eleven charms in their satchel. They had told themselves, for five years, that they were a merchant. That the stock was stock.

Standing in the churchyard of Amberlow, they stopped lying to themselves.

"Marta," they called, across the lane. "Marta! Can you come here a moment?"

She came, smiling, sickle still in her hand, mild as milk. Ilsa watched Xanthe dig through the satchel with an expression caught between alarm and understanding.

"Xanthe. What are you—"

"Pick one," Xanthe said, not looking up. "Any one. It doesn't matter which. It matters that it's *hers*—no. It doesn't even matter that it's hers." They pulled a charm out, a small glass bead with a thread of gold light coiled in it like a sleeping ember. The widow's wedding day. The first one they'd ever sold, or nearly. "It matters that it's *real*. That's the thing it can't fake. That's the whole thing. It can grow anything, it can make anything bloom, but it can't make something that *happened*."

"Xanthe," Ilsa said quietly, "you don't know what returning a memory to a stranger does. Nobody knows. The craft's been illegal for five years. The whole body of knowledge—"

"Then we learn today." Xanthe turned to Marta, who stood smiling among the false graves with her empty hands and her full heart's worth of absence. "Marta. I'm going to give you something. It might be frightening. It might hurt. I need you to trust me for one minute, and then you can go back to your flowers, and I'll never trouble you again. Can you do that? Can you trust me for one minute?"

Marta smiled. And then—just for a heartbeat—the fish under the ice surged upward, and something looked out of her eyes that was *her*, the whole of her, drowning and reaching, and her mouth said, in a voice nothing like the pleasant one:

"*Please.*"

Xanthe pressed the charm to Marta's brow and broke it.

* * *

They had given memories back before—small ones, to clients who'd sold in haste and regretted it, a refund here and there in the early years. They had never given one to a hollowed person. They had never stood at the seam between an empty life and its contents and watched it pour back in.

Marta screamed.

She went down to her knees in the churchyard grass, among the false graves, and screamed like a woman giving birth, and Ilsa moved to catch her and Xanthe caught Ilsa's arm. "No. Don't. You don't stop a birth."

It took four minutes. Xanthe counted them, because counting was the only thing their hands could do that wasn't shaking. Marta wept, and laughed, and wept, and curled around herself in the grass, and her face did in four minutes what faces are supposed to do across a whole life—every weather, every season—and then, slowly, she went still.

She knelt in the grass. She breathed. She looked at her hands.

"The rain," she said. Her voice was wrecked, raw, *hers*. "It rained on my wedding day. Everyone said it was bad luck and my mother said, no—my mother said rain at a wedding means the marriage will weather anything. She pinned my veil and her hands were shaking worse than mine and she said, *there. Now you're armored.*" Marta looked up, and her eyes were red and blazing and alive, and Xanthe felt something in their chest crack open along a seam they hadn't known was there. "I have a daughter. Tam. Tam and Joss and the baby, Rill—Rill was born in spring, she'll be three, where are my *children*—"

"Alive," Ilsa said quickly. "The children were taken to the relief camp at Ford's Crossing—we passed it, there were Vell wagons, they're alive, Marta, they're alive and they'll know you."

"Know me." Marta laughed, and it broke in the middle. She pressed her hands to her face. "I didn't know me. An hour ago I was standing in my kitchen setting a table for people I couldn't name and it felt *nice*, it felt like being wrapped in wool, and I couldn't—there was a door in my head and I couldn't find the handle, I *knew* there was a door—" She grabbed Xanthe's wrist, grip like a vise, and her eyes were terrible and magnificent. "You put the handle back. You put the handle back on the door. What did it cost you? Don't lie to me, charm-seller, I was a merchant's wife for thirty years, *what did it cost?*"

"Nothing I can't carry," Xanthe said.

It wasn't entirely a lie. The fragment that stayed with them—the widow's wedding day, now twice-widowed, rain on the awning, a mother's shaking hands—settled into its old place among the rattling crowd of strangers in Xanthe's skull, and it was heavier now, or Xanthe was, and either way it was bearable. It was always bearable. That was the whole trick of them, the thing that had kept them alive and solvent through five outlawed years: they could carry anything that wasn't theirs.

"Then do it again," Marta said.

* * *

They did it again. And again. Through the morning and into the gray false noon, Xanthe moved door to door and field to field with the satchel open, breaking charms against brows, and Ilsa worked beside them—she learned fast, ward-keeper's hands, steady as a surgeon's—and the village of Amberlow came apart and came back together, one person at a time, screaming and weeping and clutching their own names like rescued children.

Not everyone. That was the arithmetic Xanthe carried out of Amberlow, and it was the heaviest thing they'd carried yet. Two hundred and eleven charms against two hundred hollowed souls, and the charms didn't match the people—this widow's wedding day for that blacksmith, a soldier's last sunset for a girl of eight—and some memories, returned to the wrong skull, simply didn't take, sliding off like water off wax. And some took and shouldn't have. A man who received a stranger's dying sunset wept for a death that had never been his and was, afterward, somehow more himself for it, and Xanthe had no theory for that and no time to build one.

By late afternoon, forty-one people in Amberlow knew their own names. The rest still smiled in the fields, cutting flowers that fed no one, and Marta stood at the village gate with her daughter's name carved into a stick she'd whittled with shaking hands—*TAM, TAM, TAM*—and told Xanthe: "You come back. When you've more. You come back, and if you don't, I'll find you, and I'm told I was a frightening woman before all this."

"You're still frightening," Xanthe said.

"Good," said Marta, and smiled, and this time the smile was hers, crooked and worn and real, and Xanthe turned away fast because their eyes were doing something they didn't have stock to cover.

* * *

They made three miles before dark, and camped in the ruin of a waystation with a fire Ilsa built low and shielded. Xanthe sat with the satchel in their lap, counting what was left. Sixty-one charms. Sixty-one lives against two hundred hollowed people in one village, in one valley, in one country filling up with valleys.

"We can't fix it this way," Ilsa said. It wasn't cruelty. It was the ward-keeper in her, doing sums. "Charm by charm. Person by person. It's a bucket against a flood."

"I know."

"What happened in there—the rite, whatever it was, the *handle*—it needs to be a well, not a bucket. It needs to be the wards themselves, the way they were meant to work, giving and holding, consent on both sides—" She stopped. She was staring at the fire. "Xanthe. The wards I built. The original design. It was a *circulation*. Memory in, protection out, freely given, freely held. That's what the old cipher meant. That's what the king's covenant *was*. And someone turned the circulation into a siphon."

"Juniper's ledger," Xanthe said. "The rite. *A memory freely given by someone the king loved.*" They'd turned it over every night since the cistern. "We still don't know what he loved. A hundred years of harvest, and nobody wrote down what the man *loved*."

"Then that's the next lock," Ilsa said. "And the capital's four days out, and the army's two days behind us, and—"

She stopped. Her head came up, sharp, and Xanthe heard it a half-second later: hooves. Many. Moving fast on the north road, toward Amberlow, toward the village of forty-one people who now remembered enough to be afraid.

Ilsa snuffed the fire with a sweep of her hand, ward-craft eating the light, and in the sudden dark she said, "Inquisitor gray. That's Hale's column—I'd know that marching order anywhere. He's not chasing us."

"No," Xanthe said. The cold came back, all the way through, and this time it was fear, plain and familiar, and almost a relief. "He's going where we went. He's going to find forty-one people who remember everything—and a woman at the gate whittling her daughter's name, waiting for us to come back."

Marta's voice in their head: *if you don't, I'll find you.*

Xanthe stood up in the dark and slung the satchel. "We go back. *Now.*"
