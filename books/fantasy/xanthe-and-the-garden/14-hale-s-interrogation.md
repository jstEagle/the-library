## 14. Hale's Interrogation

The cell beneath the Vell garrison had been a wine cellar once, back when the garrison belonged to the city and not to the crown. Hale could tell by the iron rings still bolted into the walls, meant for hanging hams, repurposed for hanging prisoners by their wrists. Someone had scratched a tally into the stone near the door—forty marks, then a forty-first half-finished.

He had the tally sanded out before he brought her down. It was the kind of detail that festered.

Juniper Sable came down the stairs with her hands bound in front of her and her spine very straight, which was either courage or theater, and Hale had interrogated enough rebels to know it was usually both. She was smaller than he'd expected. The report described an archivist, dangerous, keeper of banned histories; the woman in front of him looked like someone's retired schoolmistress, gray at the temples, ink stains on three fingers that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully lift.

"Sit," he said.

"I'd rather stand."

"It wasn't a request."

"Nothing ever is." But she sat, on the stool he'd placed in the center of the room, because she was practical as well as proud, and he noted both. He sat opposite her, close enough to speak without raising his voice. That was his method. No shouting, no hood, no manacles tightened past comfort. Shouting told a prisoner where your fear was. Hale had never once shouted in nineteen years of service, and he intended to keep the record clean.

"You are Juniper Sable," he said. "Formerly under-archivist of the Royal Library, dismissed eleven years ago for cause. Since then, unlicensed collection of restricted documents. Unlicensed copying. Distribution of sedition."

"If you already know all that, why am I here?"

"Because knowing what a person did is different from knowing why."

She smiled at that, thin and dry. "An inquisitor who wants motives. They must hate you at court."

"They find me slow," Hale agreed. "I prefer thorough."

* * *

He began the way he always began: with the small things. Where she slept. How long she'd held the cistern. Who else knew the way in. She gave him nothing, but she gave nothing in a particular style—not stonewalling, not weeping, not defiance for its own sake. She answered every question with a question of her own, each one slightly wrong-shaped, like a key cut to open a different lock entirely.

"How many people work this garrison, Inquisitor?"

"That's not relevant."

"How many of them were born in Vell?"

"Relevant to nothing you need to know."

"When the wards fell—" and here her voice changed, dropped its archness, went flat and careful, "—how many of your own soldiers stood at the wall looking out at the dark, and asked you what was on the other side? And how many times did you answer?"

Hale kept his face still. It was a good question, which annoyed him. Most rebel questions were bad questions dressed up as traps. This one was a trap dressed up as something honest.

"The stars are not my jurisdiction," he said.

"No," Sable said. "They're everyone's jurisdiction now. That's rather the point."

* * *

He broke for the second hour and took tea in his office above, standing at the window, watching the column drill in the yard below. His aide, Pell, brought the tray and lingered, which meant Pell wanted permission to speak.

"Say it."

"She's too calm, sir. The ones who've been hiding ten years come down those stairs shaking or spitting. She came down like she'd been invited."

"She has been invited. By me."

"You know what I mean."

Hale did know what I mean, and he turned it over while the tea went cold. There were two kinds of calm prisoners: the kind who believed rescue was coming, and the kind who wanted something from the interrogation itself. He had assumed, descending again, that Sable was the first kind—that some network in Vell would move for her before dawn.

By the third hour he understood she was the second kind, and by the fourth hour he understood why, and understanding it was like stepping off a stair that wasn't there.

* * *

"Show me the ledger," she said, when he returned.

"There is no ledger."

"There is a ledger. Your men inventoried the cistern. Every document catalogued, sealed, and shipped to the capital under seal of the Inquisition. I watched them carry my life's work up those stairs in oilcloth." She leaned forward, hands still bound, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "So somewhere in this building is a copy of a page written ninety-seven years ago, in old royal cipher, bearing the seal of King Aldous the Second. And somewhere above us sits an officer of the Inquisition who has read it—or hasn't—and I want to know which, because if you haven't read it, Inquisitor, then you are the only honest man in your entire chain of command, and I think you should have the chance to stay that way."

Hale said nothing. The lamp between them hissed.

"You read it," she said. "You read it the moment it came off the cart, because you're thorough, and thorough men can't leave a sealed thing sealed when it might contain the answers they're hunting. So you've read it. So tell me, Inquisitor Hale, in your own words, what does the crown of Vell feed on?"

"That's sedition."

"That's a question."

"That's a trap."

"Yes," Sable said, almost kindly. "It is. The best kind—the kind you spring yourself. You've spent four hours trying to find the shape of my conspiracy, and I'm going to spend four minutes finding the shape of yours, and mine is bigger than yours, and it has a crown on top."

* * *

He should have ended the session there. Procedure said so; instinct said otherwise. Nineteen years of procedure had made him the most feared questioner in the free cities' border provinces, and nineteen years of instinct had never yet been wrong about a person, and everything in him said that Juniper Sable was not lying about the ledger, because liars wanted things from him and she wanted nothing except that he look.

So he sent Pell away, locked the door himself, and put the ledger page on the table between them.

"Tell me what I'm looking at," he said.

"A confession," she said. "Ninety-seven years old. Written by the king's own hand."

He read it aloud, slowly, because reading aloud was how he caught forgeries—his tongue tripped where his eyes slid past. But nothing tripped. The cipher resolved into plain words under his mouth, and the words were these: that the king had found a thing growing in the palace gardens that wore memory the way ivy wears brick; that it had offered him long reign and quiet borders in exchange for the memories of criminals first, then dissidents, then anyone whose absence would not be missed; that the border wards were not built to keep enemies out but to keep witnesses in; and that the king had signed, and fed it, and slept well.

Below the signature, in a different hand, a countersignature. Hale recognized the name. Every officer of the Inquisition recognized the name. It was on the founding charter of his own order.

"This is a forgery," he said, and heard his own voice do the thing voices do when they're negotiating with themselves.

"Check the watermark. Check the ink oxidation. Check the cipher errors—a forger gets the cipher perfect, Inquisitor; only a real hand makes mistakes, and this hand made three, all in words a forger wouldn't stumble over." She recited them from memory. Of course she recited them from memory. She had probably been born able to do that. "Your order authenticated documents with less provenance than this to hang people I knew. So authenticate it. Right now. Tell me it's false, using the methods you'd use on any prisoner's evidence, and I'll sit here quietly until morning."

Hale picked up the page. Put it down. Picked it up again.

The ink oxidation was right. The watermark was right. He ran the authentication the way he'd run it on two hundred confessions, and the document passed every test his order possessed, and at the end of it he was sitting in a wine cellar with a dead king's handwriting in his hands and his whole architecture of certainty humming a single wrong note.

"The order was founded after this date," he said finally. "If the charter signatory countersigned this—"

"Then the Inquisition was not founded to hunt memory-mongers," Sable said. "It was founded to hunt the people who noticed them."

* * *

She let him sit with it. That was her cruelest technique, and he catalogued it even as it worked on him: she never pushed. She laid a truth down between them like a card face-up and waited for him to pick it up himself, because a truth you pick up is yours forever and a truth that's handed to you can be handed back.

He thought about the purge five years ago. He had signed seventeen arrest orders in the purge. He had been proud of the paperwork—clean warrants, proper chains of custody, everything done by the book, because the book was the only thing standing between the free cities and chaos, and he had said so, publicly, to younger officers who needed saying it to.

One of the seventeen had been a boy of twenty with no record except unlicensed charm-selling. Hale had reviewed the warrant personally and found the evidence thin, and he had flagged it, and his superior had returned it approved with a note in the margin: *proceed.* He had proceeded. He had never learned what happened to the boy. He had told himself, for five years, that the system worked, because the alternative to that sentence was unthinkable, and now the alternative was sitting across from him in a wine cellar with ink-stained fingers and bound wrists, telling it to him in old royal cipher.

"The boy," he heard himself say. "Five years ago. Xanthe. What happened to—"

He stopped. Sable's expression changed, minutely, the way ice changes before it cracks.

"You know the name," he said.

"I know several names," Sable said carefully. Too carefully. He was a thorough man; he heard the seam. "I'm an archivist. Names are my trade."

But she had gone still in a new way, and Hale had spent nineteen years learning the difference between a person deciding whether to trust him and a person deciding how much to fear for someone else. He filed it. He did not press it. Later, he would understand that this was the moment he crossed over—when he chose not to press a question that would have served his mission, because pressing it might have served the crown's.

The crown's. Not the kingdom's. He noticed, with a sensation like a bone setting, that he had begun separating the two words.

* * *

"What happens to me now?" Sable asked, near dawn.

"That depends on what I write in my report."

"And what will you write?"

Hale looked at the page between them for a long time. He could burn it. He could send it to the capital with his endorsement and be promoted for it, and sleep, perhaps, eventually. Or he could do the thing the law actually required of him—because the law required it, that was the whole point of law, it bound you precisely when binding hurt—and open a formal inquiry into a document implicating the crown.

Both paths led through the Verdant King. Only one of them led through the truth.

"You'll be transferred," he said. "Convoy, south road, day after tomorrow. To the provincial seat, pending review of evidence in your case. It will be a slow convoy. Poorly guarded. Roads are dangerous lately." He stood, gathered the page, and paused at the door with his hand on the lock. "Sable. The people who escaped your cistern. The thief and the ward-keeper. If they're listening to rumors out of the countryside—if they hear about a prison convoy on the south road—"

"Then they'd be fools to attack an Inquisition escort," Sable said, and for the first time since the stairs, she smiled like a young woman.

Hale unlocked the door, then stopped, one last thing surfacing from the night's wreckage, something small and human that he hadn't planned to say.

"The tally on the wall. Forty-one marks. Who was the forty-first?"

Sable looked at the sanded stone for a moment. "A girl who counted her days until we got the archive hidden. She stopped counting the night we finished." A pause. "Her name was Wren."

The name landed in him strangely—he didn't know why, only that the air in the cellar shifted, the way a room shifts when a door opens somewhere far off. He wrote it down anyway. Thorough men write everything down.

Upstairs, he sat at his desk until the windows went gray, a blank report in front of him and a dead king's confession in his lap, and outside, far to the south, the roads were already whispering with green.
