## 15. Memories That Aren't Mine

The fire was small because big fires got you killed. That was the first lesson of the roads outside Vell, learned in the hour after we climbed out of the sewer and into a world gone wrong: keep your light low, keep your noise lower, and never sleep deeper than half.

Ilsa Vane built it anyway with a soldier's economy—a ring of stones, dry hedge-twigs she tested for movement before touching, like they might bite. Some of them probably would have. We'd watched a hedgerow swallow an ox-cart an hour past dusk, slow as syrup, patient as tax collectors.

We ate hardtack and dried apples from her pack. The stars were still missing. Without them the sky was a lid, a black ceiling pressed close enough to touch, and the darkness felt less like night than like something leaning down to look at us.

I turned my charm-stock over in my hands for the hundredth time since the village.

* * *

You should understand what my stock looked like by then. Forty-one charms, each one a sliver of someone's life, harvested with a thief's touch and sold with a merchant's smile. A widow's wedding day. A soldier's last sunset before a border he'd never cross back over. A baker's memory of his daughter's first steps, sold three times because he kept forgetting he'd sold it at all—that one still made me feel like a grave-robber when I thought about it, which was often.

Except now the stock whispered.

Since the night the wards fell, since the stars went out like snuffed candles, the memories had started talking to each other in the dark hours. Faint. Fragmentary. A soldier humming where a widow should be laughing. The baker's daughter walking across somebody else's kitchen. Like forty-one strangers pressed into one room, learning each other's voices, getting confused about whose life was whose.

And underneath them, louder every night, there was the other thing. The thing I hadn't told Ilsa. The thing I hadn't told anyone, ever, because saying it out loud would make it real, and because the only other person who might have understood it was dead or captured in a cistern under Vell, and thinking about Juniper's face when the inquisitors took them was a wound I kept bandaged with motion.

But we had a long night ahead, and a fire too small to hide behind, and Ilsa Vane watched people the way ward-keepers watch wards—looking for the hairline crack that sings.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"Neither have you."

"I don't need much." She fed a twig to the flames. "You're doing the thing where you count your charms. You've done it eleven times tonight. I counted."

Of course she had. "It's how I take inventory."

"You're not taking inventory. Inventory has an order. You're looking for something." Her eyes came up, grey and level. "Or away from something."

I could have lied to her. Lying was my native language; I'd spoken it fluently since before I could write. But Juniper was in chains because I'd run instead of fought, and the village-that-forgot-itself was thirty miles behind us with its smiling hollow people, and the sky was empty, and I was so tired of carrying things alone.

"There are memories in my stock," I said, "that aren't mine. And aren't anyone's."

Ilsa went very still, the way she did when a ward cracked under her hands. "Explain."

* * *

So I explained. And once I started, I couldn't stop, because five years is a long time to hold a secret the size of a sea.

"Every harvester knows their own stock," I said. "You have to. When you pull a memory out of someone, it doesn't come clean—it comes with edges, with texture. Their fear smells different from their joy. Their childhood feels different from their grief. After a while you can sort a hundred charms blind, just by touch. This one belongs to a man who misses his brother. This one belongs to a woman who's ashamed of something she did in the famine year. It's the whole craft, really. Not the pulling. The knowing."

"And?"

"And I have seven charms I can't place." My hands weren't steady. I put them flat on my knees, which helped, which didn't help. "They've been in my collection for as long as I can remember having a collection. They feel like mine. That's what's wrong with them. Every other memory I carry, I can feel the seam where it entered me—the little scar tissue of the theft. These have no seams. They fit like they grew there."

"What's in them?"

I made myself say it. Made myself see it, because it was always there, always playing behind my eyelids when I let my guard down.

"A house," I said. "A house by the sea. Grey stone, blue shutters, a garden going riot because neither of us believed in weeding. There's a storm door that sticks and a kitchen with a crooked shelf and—" My voice did something embarrassing. I pushed through it. "There's a lover in them. Wren. Dark-haired, quick-mouthed, laughs like they're getting away with something. I know how their hand fits against mine. I know the exact sound the floor makes outside the bedroom at night, third board from the left. I know what they look like asleep. I have *years*, Ilsa. Whole seasons. Salt on the windows in winter. Arguing about whether to plant lavender. Them teaching me a song in a language I don't speak, and me getting it wrong on purpose to make them laugh."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, something rustled that wasn't wind.

"I have never been to that house," I said. "I have never lived by any sea. I grew up in the tanner's quarter of Vell, in a room that smelled like death and lime, and I have never loved anyone named Wren. Except apparently I have. Down to the bone. Because when I hold those charms, I don't feel like a thief holding stolen goods. I feel homesick."

Ilsa was quiet for a long time. She was good at quiet. Most people fill silence like water fills a hole, rushing to cover everything. She let it sit between us until it had weight.

"When you say you can't remember harvesting them—"

"I can remember harvesting every other charm. Where I was standing. What the seller's face did. What they bought with the money, usually. Drink, mostly, or passage." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "These seven, there's nothing. Just—there one day, like they'd always been there. Which is impossible. Nobody's memory just *appears*."

"Unless," Ilsa said slowly, and I watched her mind move the way it moved through old cipher text, testing each turn, "it was never yours to begin with. Someone could have planted them. Slipped them into you while you slept, or during a harvest gone sideways, or—"

"Planted seven years of a fake life? Planted a whole *person*?" I shook my head. "You can't fake that much detail. Believe me, I've tried to find the fakes. Forgers try it sometimes—synthetic memories, stitched together from scraps, sold to lonely rich people. They're always wrong. Too smooth. Real memory has grit in it. Boredom. The parts nobody would choose to include." My throat tightened. "My Wren-charms have boredom in them. Hours of it. Watching rain. Waiting for bread to rise. Nobody forges waiting-for-bread-to-rise."

"Then the other option."

I stared into the fire. "Then the other option."

"Say it."

"You say it. I've been trying not to say it for five years."

Ilsa leaned forward, and the firelight moved on her scarred hands, the hands that had held the border wards together for twenty years until the kingdom decided she was worth more broken. "Either someone planted a life in your head," she said, "or those memories are real, and they're *yours*, and something took them from you and left copies behind. Either you're haunted, Xanthe—or you've been robbed by better thieves than you'll ever be."

* * *

That was the shape of it, laid out plain, and hearing it said aloud was worse than carrying it folded small.

Because here was the thing I hadn't confessed yet, the thing underneath the thing. If the memories were planted, then someone had spent enormous effort—craft beyond anything I'd encountered—to make a stranger believe they'd been loved. That was cruelty of a kind I couldn't name. And if the memories were real—if somewhere there was a grey stone house and a dark-haired person who had actually known me, known me down to the third board from the left—then at some point, someone had reached into my life and taken it, and left me standing in the tanner's quarter with no idea what I'd lost.

Five years. The purge was five years ago. The kingdom outlawed memory-magic, and harvesters ran, and harvesters died, and I ran faster than most. What if running hadn't been my idea? What if the gaps in my own past—the years I'd always assumed were just the blur everyone's early twenties are made of—what if they weren't blur at all? What if they were excisions?

I'd built a whole self on top of a foundation I'd never inspected. Cynical merchant. Careful thief. Survivor. And under all of it, possibly, a person who planted lavender and sang songs in a language I couldn't name and was loved so thoroughly that even the echo of it ached like a pulled tooth.

"Do you know what's worst?" I said. "Not knowing which is worse. If they're fake, I'm a victim of some monster's experiment. If they're real, I'm a victim of ordinary theft—my own theft, turned around on me. And either way I can't grieve properly, because you can't mourn something you can't prove existed." I looked at my hands. Thief's hands. Good hands, once you got past what they were good at. "Juniper would know what to make of it. Juniper knows everything. And I stood in a doorway and watched them get dragged away and I *ran*."

"That's not what this is about."

"It's a little what this is about."

"Xanthe." Ilsa said my name the way she said everything—flat, precise, and somehow, underneath, careful. "You escaped so the ledger would escape. Juniper made that choice with both eyes open. I read enough of their notes to know they'd have done it twice." She paused. "Guilt you can spend later. Right now I want to know the practical question. Your infected charms put a sleepwalker on the road to the gates. Your strange charms—these Wren-charms—are they whispering too?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. I closed my eyes and sank into the stock the way I did every night now, reluctantly, like checking a trapline.

Forty-one voices. The widow, the soldier, the baker's daughter, all murmuring, bleeding into each other at the edges, sick with whatever the Verdant King's garden had breathed into the world the night the stars went out.

And the seven.

The seven were silent.

No—not silent. Separate. While the stolen memories of strangers tangled and frayed like wet rope, the seven sat apart from the infection, cool and clean and whole, like they were warded. Like they were *protected*.

"They're not whispering," I said slowly. "They're... apart. Quarantined. Everything else is sick and these seven are—" I stopped, because the word arrived with a cold drop straight down my spine. "Preserved. Someone preserved them. You preserve things you intend to come back for."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Or things that were meant to be returned."

"To whom?" I spread my hands. "To me? I'm right here. If someone wanted me to have my own life back, why leave me a copy instead of the original? Why leave me homesick for a door I can't find?"

"Maybe the return isn't finished." Ilsa poked the fire, and sparks rose toward the starless lid of the sky and vanished, swallowed. "Maybe whoever did this meant to give them back and something interrupted them. A war. A purge." Her voice went dry. "The kingdom has been excellent at interrupting things."

Five years ago. The purge. Everyone connected to memory-magic had scattered or burned, and if someone had been mid-working on me—mid-restoration, mid-return—they'd have fled too, leaving seven charms stranded in a thief's chest like letters that never got delivered.

Or.

Or the Verdant King had gotten there first. Had taken the original and left me the copy, the way it left the village its smiles without their children's names. A parasite doesn't eat everything. A good parasite leaves the host alive. Maybe love was the part it couldn't digest, so it scraped out the substance and left the shape.

I didn't know. That was the honest truth, sitting there in the firelight with a woman I'd known four days and trusted more than I'd trusted anyone in five years: I did not know what had been done to me, or by whom, or why.

But I knew one thing I hadn't known an hour ago.

"The charms point somewhere," I said. "They always have. When I hold them, there's a... lean. Like a compass needle. South and east. I always figured it was nothing, an artifact, the memory reaching for its setting." I met Ilsa's eyes. "It's not nothing. It's stronger every night. Whatever that house is—or was—it's south and east of here."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Then she reached into her pack and drew out the map she'd taken from the ward-house, the one marked with the cracks in the border wards, and spread it across her knees. Her finger traced south and east, past the villages, past the moving hedgerows, past the places blooming out of season.

It stopped on a waystation town on the old coast road. One of the last stops before the capital.

"Harrow's Rest," she read. Then she frowned, and I watched something shift in her face, some ward-keeper's instinct catching a frequency it didn't like.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just—" She tapped the mark. "This station was flagged in the palace dispatches we found. Flagged for inspection six years ago. Before the fall of the wards. Before any of this." Her grey eyes lifted to mine. "Somebody important passed through there, Xanthe. Somebody the crown cared about. Six years ago—which would be right around when your seven seamless memories would have had to enter your stock."

The fire burned low between us. Out in the dark, far off, something that might have been a bird sang three notes of a song I almost recognized, in a language I almost spoke.

South and east. Toward Harrow's Rest. Toward the capital. Toward whatever had been done to me, and whoever had done it.

I looked at my hands, and for the first time in five years, they weren't shaking.

"Then we're going the right way after all," I said.
