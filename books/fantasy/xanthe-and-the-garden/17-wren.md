## 17. Wren

The waystation was a shrine once, before the stars went out and shrines stopped meaning anything. Now it was a fortified barn with a bell tower, squatting at the crossroads where the Salt Road met whatever remained of the King's Way. Ilsa called it a relay point. The resistance, apparently, called it nothing out loud, in case the hedgerows were listening.

Xanthe didn't like it from the moment they saw it. Not because of the defenses—though the arrow slits had been cut recently, and someone had salted the ground around the door in a pattern Xanthe recognized as old ward-craft, pre-Verdant, the kind Ilsa's order used to teach apprentices before the order decided memory-mongering was a crime. No. What Xanthe didn't like was the feeling of the place. A tugging behind their ribs, like a fishhook set years ago that had finally caught on something.

"You're doing the face," Wren—no, not Wren, *Ilsa*—said beside them. "The merchant face. The one that means you think you're being cheated."

"I don't have faces," Xanthe said.

"You have eleven. I've counted."

They'd been traveling together nine days now, long enough for Ilsa to develop opinions about everything: Xanthe's sleeping habits, their charm-stock, their habit of talking to horses. Nine days since the cistern raid. Since Juniper went into chains so Xanthe could go through a wall with a ledger full of dead kings' secrets under their coat. Xanthe carried the guilt like a second satchel, heavier than the first.

The waystation door opened before they knocked.

A woman stood in it—or not a woman, exactly; someone built like a drawn bow, all lean tension and watchfulness, with a scarf wound around her throat against the cold. She had a scar through one eyebrow and ink-stained fingers and eyes that went from Ilsa to Xanthe and then did something Xanthe had never seen eyes do before.

They broke.

Not visibly. Nothing so crude as tears. But something behind them gave way, quietly, catastrophically, the way a bridge gives way—not all at once, but in the moment after the last cart has crossed and the river has won.

"Vane," she said, and her voice came out level by what must have been tremendous effort. "You're supposed to be dead."

"I get that a lot lately." Ilsa pushed past her into the warmth. "We need relay to the Free Cities council. Fast horses, faster letters. And food, if any of it isn't growing out of things it shouldn't."

"And him?" The woman's gaze hadn't left Xanthe. "Him or them or—" she caught herself, jaw tightening, correcting course like a sailor in a squall. "Them. Who are they?"

"They?" Xanthe said.

"The pronouns are on the ledger," Ilsa said, unshouldering her pack. "Xanthe. Charm-seller out of Vell. Memory-thief, technically unlicensed, currently conscripted. They know more about the Verdant King than anyone alive who isn't us."

Something flickered across the woman's face at the word *charm-seller*. Something that wasn't surprise.

*She knew,* Xanthe thought, and the fishhook behind their ribs pulled harder. *She knew before we walked in.*

"I'm Wren Sable," the woman said. "Quartermaster for this stretch of road. Come in before something blooming finds you."

*Sable.* Like Juniper. Cousins, maybe. Or worse—another archivist, another person whose family tree was made of banned paper. Xanthe followed her inside because there was nowhere else to go, and because some part of them—the part that carried strangers' memories like loose change—was already whispering that this room mattered, that it had always mattered, though they couldn't have said why if you'd held a knife to their throat.

* * *

The waystation's main hall smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and, faintly, of roses. Roses, in deep winter, with every star blacked out of the sky. Xanthe noticed and said nothing, but filed it away with the other wrongnesses accumulating in their head like unpaid debts.

Wren fed them. That was the first strange thing—she served Xanthe personally, not the table generally, setting down a bowl of barley stew in front of them with a care that bordered on ceremony. The second strange thing was that she knew how Xanthe took it. No pepper, extra onion, bread torn not cut.

"I didn't tell you that," Xanthe said.

"Tell me what?"

"How I eat."

Wren went very still. It lasted half a heartbeat, then she smiled, and it was the worst smile Xanthe had ever seen, because it was real. "Lucky guess."

"Lucky," Ilsa repeated, from across the table, in the tone of a woman filing evidence.

Over the meal Wren talked business—relay schedules, which roads were still passable, how the hedges had taken the eastern ford ("they don't move fast, they move *patiently*, that's the horror of them"). She was good at it. Efficient, dry-humored, precise. Every few minutes her eyes found Xanthe's face and stayed a beat too long, the way you look at a word you've forgotten the meaning of.

It was on the fourth such look that Xanthe's stock whispered.

That was the only word for it. Their stolen memories—the widow's wedding, the soldier's sunset, the dozens of others they carried like coins in a purse—had been murmuring to each other ever since the night the stars went out. Usually nonsense. Fragments calling across the dark. But now, in this room, in the rose-smelling warmth, three fragments rose together and spoke in something like harmony:

*A hand with ink-stained fingers tracing letters on bare skin.*

*Someone laughing, breathless, saying* you absolute disaster *with such tenderness it could have been a blessing.*

*The smell of roses and lamp oil, and a voice saying* stay, *just* stay—

Xanthe put down their spoon.

"What is it?" Wren asked, too quickly, too carefully.

"I don't know." Xanthe pressed their knuckles against their sternum, where the ache lived. "There's something in my stock that knows this room."

"Your stock." Wren's voice had gone thin. "Of stolen memories."

"That's the trade."

"Yes." She stood abruptly, gathering bowls that didn't need gathering. "Yes, I imagine it is."

Ilsa watched her leave the room, then looked at Xanthe with an expression Xanthe had learned to read as *you are standing on a cliff and I am not going to warn you, because you need to see the edge yourself.*

* * *

Xanthe found her in the bell tower an hour later, because of course they did. Because their feet knew the way up spiral stairs in the dark without being told, and that fact frightened Xanthe more than anything the Verdant King had grown so far.

Wren stood at the open arch, wrapped in her scarf, looking east toward the capital. Toward the green glow on the horizon that no fire could account for.

"You should sleep," she said, without turning.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep much these days." A pause. "Five years of not sleeping much, actually."

"Since the purge," Xanthe said. Everyone measured time that way now. Five years since the crown outlawed memory-work and burned the archives and put the practitioners to the sword. Five years since Xanthe had gone underground and started selling charms in Vell's night market.

"Since the purge," Wren agreed. Then she turned, and in the starless dark her face was just pale shapes and two bright points of attention. "Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not asking since you walked through my door. You're a thief, Xanthe. Thieves notice. You've noticed my hands, my voice, the way I knew your stew. You've felt your own memories pulling toward me like compass needles." Her breath fogged. "So ask."

The wind moved through the arch. Somewhere below, a sentry coughed. Xanthe stood very still and let the thief do its work—cataloguing, correlating, the craft that had kept them alive in Vell's gutters. Ink-stained fingers. A laugh they almost recognized. The way *stay* echoed somewhere in their chest like a word written in a language they'd lost.

"Did I know you?" they asked. "Before?"

Wren laughed—one short, cracked note, nothing like a laugh at all. "Know me." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Gods. Five years I imagined this conversation. In most versions I was angrier. In some of them you remembered." She dropped her hands. "In none of them did you look at me the way you're looking at me now. Like I'm a locked door."

"Wren—"

"You knew me," she said. "For four years. We lived together in the archive quarter of Vell, above a dye-shop, in rooms that flooded every spring. You hated the flooding. You loved the rain on the roof. You stole my coat twice and claimed you'd won it gambling. You—" her voice snagged; she smoothed it flat. "You were going to ask me to marry you. You'd been carrying the question around for months like a coin you couldn't spend. You told me that once, drunk, that you'd practiced it in mirrors."

The world tilted. Not the floor—the floor was fine—it was Xanthe, tilting inside their own skin, because every word landed on empty ground and yet the ground *ached*, the way an amputated limb aches.

"I don't remember," Xanthe said. "Any of it. There's no—you're describing someone else's life. I'd remember a whole life. Four years doesn't just—"

"It does," Wren said, "if you sell it."

The words dropped between them like a stone down a well. Xanthe listened for the splash and never heard it.

"What?"

"Five years ago," Wren said, and her voice had gone steady now, the terrible steadiness of someone who had rehearsed devastation until it obeyed, "when the purge came to Vell, they had names. My name was on the list—I was an archivist's apprentice, my cousin's network, all of it. There was a man who could arrange an escape. Smuggling papers, a cart, a route south. He didn't want coin." She looked at Xanthe, and her eyes were dry and awful. "He wanted stock. Rare stock. And there was one seller in the city with something rare enough to be worth a life."

"No," Xanthe said.

"He wanted everything you had of yourself. Your own memories. Your childhood, your—us, all of it, four years of it, every memory you had of me and of the person you were when you were mine. Sold in a single lot. And you sat across the table from him and you bargained, because you were always bargaining, even then—and you got him to throw in the cart and the papers and safe passage for me past the southern wards. And then you drank the draught he gave you, to loosen the roots, and you sold yourself."

"Stop."

"You woke up the next morning with gaps you couldn't name and a purse full of coin and no idea why you flinched at the smell of roses. You built a new self out of strangers' leftovers and called it survival, and it *was* survival, and it worked, and I got on the cart." Her breath shuddered once and steadied. "And I have spent five years wondering if you chose it. If you looked at our life and thought, *this is what it's worth, this is the price, fine.* Or if you were so afraid of losing me that you cut me out of yourself rather than carry the grief."

"Stop," Xanthe said again, and heard their own voice crack down the middle.

"I can't. Five years, Xanthe. I've been carrying all of it alone—the whole life, every day of it, the flooding rooms and the rain and the question you never got to ask me. Do you know what that is? Being the only witness? Being the sole survivor of something that isn't even dead?" She stepped closer, and Xanthe should have stepped back and didn't. "You asked me once what I'd do if the purge came. I said I'd run. You said you'd run with me. And then it came, and you ran me out of the city and stayed behind in an empty house with nothing in your head where I used to be."

Xanthe's hand had risen without permission and pressed flat against their sternum, over the place where the ache lived, the fishhook place, the place that had been aching for five years without a name. Gaps. Gods, there had been gaps—everyone who harvested memory had gaps, the work ate the worker, everyone said so—but theirs had never felt like erosion. They had felt like absence with edges. Rooms in a house with the furniture gone but the shadows of the furniture still on the walls.

"The buyer," Xanthe said. Their voice came out scraped. "The man who bought—where are they? What did he do with them? Can they be—"

"Gone." Wren said it gently, which was worse. "Consumed. Whoever wore your childhood is dust, or worse, one of the hollow ones walking the garden roads smiling at their children. That's what the Verdant King's brokers did with the best stock, back when the harvest was quiet. Fed it to the root." She paused. "Except."

Xanthe looked up.

"Except he kept one thing back," Wren said. "Or you hid one thing. I've never known which. When you woke, you still had one fragment that wasn't yours—a stranger's memory, buried deep. A garden. Green light. A king with leaves behind his eyes."

The night went cold all through Xanthe's body, colder than the wind off the Salt Road.

"That memory," Xanthe whispered. "I've carried it for years. I thought I stole it. I thought—" They thought of the noble's mind, the impossible glimpse, chapter upon chapter of dread arriving late. Thought of Juniper's ledger, of the pattern repeating across a century. Thought of a thief who had sold everything and woken with one seed left behind, planted in the ruin, growing in secret for five years.

"You didn't steal it," Wren said softly. "I think it was the receipt."

Below them, in the yard, a sentry shouted—not alarm, just relief, the changing of the watch. The green glow on the horizon pulsed once, slow, patient, like a heart the size of the sky.

Wren reached out—stopped herself, hand hovering in the air between them, trembling with the effort of not touching. "I'm not telling you this to hurt you. I'm telling you because tomorrow we ride for the free cities, and you'll stand in front of the council, and they will ask what you're willing to pay. And I needed you to know that you've already paid more than any of them. You paid it blind, for a stranger's life, and you never even learned her name."

The wind gusted through the arch, smelling of roses and lamp oil.

"But here's the thing," Wren said, and now her voice did break, just once, and she let it. "I got my life back. Every year of it. And I've decided I'm done being the only witness. So you don't have to remember me, Xanthe. You don't have to feel it. But you are going to *know* it—every story, every stupid fight, every morning—because I refuse, I absolutely refuse, to be a stranger to you twice."

Xanthe stood in the starless dark with a hole in their chest shaped like four years, and found—to their astonishment—that the hole had a shape. That it had always had a shape. That grief, it turned out, could survive its own forgetting, waiting patiently in the body like a seed in winter soil.

"I don't remember you," Xanthe said hoarsely. "But I think I was coming home to you. All this time. Every road."

Wren's hand closed around theirs, warm and ink-stained and shaking.

"Then let's go save the world," she said. "You owe me a question, and I intend to collect."

Far to the east, the green light flared—and in its flare, brief as a struck match, Xanthe saw the horizon bloom with shapes like towers, like thorns, like a crown.
