## 18. What I Bought You With

The waystation smelled of lamp oil and old cedar, and Wren would not stop looking at me.

I'd noticed it the way you notice weather—gradually, then all at once. The tilt of their head when I spoke. The way their hand hovered near mine on the table, close enough to touch, never landing. They were watching me like a person watches a house fire from across the street: unable to look away, unable to help.

"You keep doing that," I said finally. "Looking."

"Sorry." They weren't sorry. Their eyes went back to my face like water finding its level. "It's just—I spent five years remembering your face, and now I'm looking at it, and you're looking back at me like I'm a stranger who owes you money."

"You are a stranger who owes me money," I said, because the alternative was to say something true.

Wren laughed. It was a terrible laugh, cracked down the middle, and it made something in my chest ache in a way I couldn't account for. Like a scar itching in cold weather—a body remembering an injury the mind had been spared.

Ilsa, who had been pretending very hard to read her ward-manual by the fire, cleared her throat. "I'm going to check the perimeter."

"It's a waystation," I said. "It's four walls and a roof."

"I'm going to check it *thoroughly*." She left with her boots half-laced, which told me everything about how long she intended to be gone.

And so it was just the two of us and the fire, and Wren Sable—no relation to Juniper, they'd established that within minutes of meeting, some old joke I wasn't party to—and me, and the story between us like a debt laid out on the table.

"Tell me," I said. "All of it. If you're not going to be a stranger, then don't be one halfway."

Wren set down their cup. Their hands were steady. I remember noticing that—the steadiness—as if they'd rehearsed this. As if they'd had five years of nights to practice telling a lover they no longer existed to.

"All right," they said. "But you should eat something first. You'll want to throw things, later, and you shouldn't do it hungry."

* * *

Here is what I knew before that night:

I knew I was twenty-three years old when the purge came to Vell. I knew I had been a charm-seller for two years by then, small-time, working the night market under three different names because a memory-monger's first rule is *never be findable*. I knew the king's men came through the market districts in the spring of the Year of Quiet Bells, dragging memory-workers into the square, and I knew that afterward there was a hole in my life shaped like five years of nothing in particular—no faces, no names, just a gray stretch where a life should have been, papered over with the fragments of strangers I carried instead.

I had always assumed the gap was trauma. Shock does that; everyone said so. The mind protects itself.

Here is what I learned that night, sitting across from someone who loved me and whom I could not love back, because the loving had been sold:

There was no gap. There was a transaction.

* * *

"The purge didn't come to Vell all at once," Wren said. The fire popped. Outside, somewhere on the dark road, a hedge shifted with a sound like a slow breath, and we both flinched, and that flinch—that shared, ordinary flinch—was the most intimate thing that had passed between us yet. "It came in waves. First they took the registered workers. Then anyone whose name appeared in another worker's ledger. Then anyone the informers named."

"Were you a worker?"

"No." A ghost of a smile. "You always asked me that too. No, I was a clerk in the Hall of Records. Boring as bread. But I was also—" they hesitated, and I watched them decide to trust me with it, watched the decision cost them something, "—I was forging travel papers for memory-families. People trying to get over the border before the wards sealed. Someone talked. They came for me on a Tuesday."

"And I bought you out." I said it flatly, testing the shape of it. It fit my mouth wrong, like a shoe made for someone else's foot.

"You did more than buy me out." Wren's voice stayed even, but their hands had finally started to shake, and they let them. "Xanthe. Do you understand what your memories were worth? A working charm-seller's own stock—lived experience, raw, uncut? In the last week of the purge, when every smuggler and fixer in Vell was charging blood prices? You didn't pay a bribe. You paid a *fortune*. And the only fortune you had was yourself."

"How much?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"All of it. Everything you had of us." They said *us* without flinching, and I envied them that. "The broker—you remember the broker, no, of course you don't—his name was Corvane, worked out of the tannery district. He told me once, years later, drunk, that it was the largest single sale he'd ever handled. That he'd had to bring in three other brokers just to price it. That some of what you sold him he couldn't even catalogue."

"Couldn't catalogue."

"Because it wasn't just scenes. It was—" Wren pressed the heel of their hand against their eye. "He said you sold him whole years. Not moments. Years. Continuous. He said in thirty years of the trade he'd never seen anyone give up continuity. Everyone keeps the thread, Xanthe. Even the desperate ones. You gave him the thread."

I looked at my hands. Thief's hands, charm-seller's hands, clever and quick and suddenly obscene to me, sitting there in the firelight holding a cup of tea like nothing had ever passed through them but warmth.

"Why," I said. "Why all of it? Why not enough to get you over the border and keep—keep something. Keep a season. Keep a song."

Wren was quiet for a long moment.

"Because I wouldn't go," they said finally. "That's the part I've hated myself for, five years running. They were going to take me on the Tuesday. Word reached you Monday night. And you came to the records hall—we'd arranged, if anything ever happened, a signal, a chalk mark—and you told me you could buy my passage, and I told you no."

"No."

"I told you I wouldn't run while the families I'd promised papers to were still being rounded up. I told you I'd rather hang useful than live safe. I was very noble and very stupid and I meant every word." Their laugh again, that broken thing. "So you went to Corvane the next morning and sold everything, and you didn't tell me until the cart was already moving, and by the time I understood what you'd done you were standing in the doorway of the wagon and you didn't know who I was anymore."

The fire crackled. Somewhere in my chest, that scar-ache deepened into something with teeth.

"I stood in the doorway," I repeated.

"You looked at me like a customer appraising goods," Wren said. "Polite. Careful. You thanked me for the ride." Their voice finally broke, just at the edges, just enough. "Five years, Xanthe. Five years I have carried both sides of our whole life. Every argument, every morning, every—everything. Carrying it alone. And you've been walking around with strangers' weddings in your pockets because yours were too expensive to keep."

* * *

After that, I couldn't sit still. Ilsa came back in from her thorough perimeter inspection, took one look at my face, and announced she'd make tea, though the tea was already made. Juniper's absence sat in all of us—inquisitor's cells held her somewhere south of the city—but tonight it was Wren's history that filled the room, and it kept filling it, hour after hour, because I kept asking.

Piece by piece, others' accounts stitched together the person I had been.

From Wren: that I used to hum when I worked, tunelessly, songs I invented, and that they'd teased me for it until I hummed louder. That I was afraid of deep water and swam anyway every summer because I refused to be ruled by it. That I cried at other people's weddings—not the ceremonies, but the moment after, when the families embraced, because, I had once told them, *that's the part people forget to sell.*

From Ilsa, reluctantly, over her cup: that in the early years after the purge, a charm-seller matching my description had worked the border towns, and the ward-keepers' reports noted one who returned stolen memories to refugees free of charge—slipped into their pockets like charity, untraceable, against every law of the trade. *"We thought it was sentiment,"* Ilsa said. *"Now I think you were paying something back. Or paying something forward. I think you were trying to be the kind of person whose memories were worth what you'd paid for them."*

From a refugee at the waystation door, briefly, before Wren waved them off to bed: that the memory-thief Xanthe had a reputation east of the river. That they never haggled. That they looked at buyers too long, like they were searching every stranger's face for something.

For me, apparently. For the person I'd been. Searching crowds for a self I'd pawned.

I sat with all of it the way you sit with a verdict. Not arguing. Just absorbing the weight.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Wren said, near dawn, when the fire had burned down to patient orange.

"There's a worst part? Wren, I sold my entire life and forgot I did it. What's worse."

"You didn't forget you did it." They leaned forward, elbows on knees, and their eyes caught the embers. "You chose not to know. Corvane told me—he was drunker than usual—you insisted on one term. You said: *don't tell me what I sold. Don't tell me his name, don't tell me what we were. Because if I know, I'll spend the rest of my life grieving someone I can't remember, and I can't work, can't survive, carrying that.*" Wren's jaw tightened. "You grieved in advance, Xanthe. You pre-paid the heartbreak along with everything else. And then you built this—" they gestured at me, at my cynic's slouch, at the merchant's mask I wore like a second face, "—this armor, out of the wreckage, and you called it survival."

"Wasn't it?"

"It was," Wren admitted. "That's the hell of it. It worked. You're alive. I'm alive. We're both here." They looked at me across the dead fire, and their voice dropped to almost nothing. "But I won't do it again. I won't be a stranger who knows too much. I refuse. So here's what happens now: I stay. Near you. However long it takes. You don't have to feel anything—you can't, I understand that, gods, I understand that better than anyone breathing—but you will *know* me. Day by day. Until either the memory comes home or a new one grows in its place. I'm not asking you to love me. I'm telling you I'm not leaving."

"That's not fair," I said. "To you. Loving someone who looks at you like a locked door."

"Loving someone was never the fair part," Wren said. "It's just the part worth doing."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I walked the waystation's narrow gallery while the sky did its starless black, and I tried to hold the shape of what I'd lost the way you try to hold water.

Here is the truth I arrived at, somewhere in the coldest hour: I could not feel it. That was the obscene genius of what I'd done. Grief requires an object, and my object had been excised with surgical completeness. I mourned the mourning and could not reach the thing beneath it. It was like standing outside a burned house where only the foundation remained—you could measure the rooms by their absence, pace out where the kitchen had been, where the bedroom, where the bed—but there was nothing left to touch.

Except.

Except my hands remembered things. When Wren handed me my cup that morning, our fingers brushed, and my hands went still—completely, unnaturally still, the way they never went, the thief's hands that were always moving—and for one full second I could not make them work. Wren saw it. Wren said nothing. Wren just smiled, small and fierce and patient as a planted seed, and took the cup back, and poured me another.

And except the humming. Later, packing the satchels for the road, I caught myself doing it—tuneless, invented, a song with no source—and stopped so hard I nearly dropped the strap. I hadn't known I did that. *I* didn't do that. The person who hummed had been sold to a broker in the tannery district along with everything else.

But my throat didn't know that. My throat hummed anyway.

Ilsa found me standing in the yard with my hand pressed over my own mouth like a criminal.

"We move in an hour," she said gently. She had guessed, I think, or she had simply decided to offer mercy without asking its price. "There's word on the road. Refugee column says the usurper's army is marching west—fast. Faster than the council expected. If they take the river crossings before we reach the free cities—"

"Then it doesn't matter what Wren and I were or weren't," I finished. "No future to argue about."

Ilsa nodded once. Behind her, Wren emerged into the gray light with their pack already shouldered, and fell into step beside me without asking, without ceremony, as if five years of distance were a puddle they'd simply decided to walk through.

They were not going to be a stranger. They had made that perfectly clear.

And ahead of us, past the hedgerows and the hollow villages and the army swallowing the horizon, the capital waited—with a parasite wearing a crown, a king buried alive inside his own peace, and a rite that needed a loving memory no one living could name.

Or so we thought, that morning.

I glanced sideways at the person who loved me, walking willingly into the garden's shadow, and felt the first cold edge of a thought I would spend chapters refusing to finish:

*Everyone keeps saying the rite needs a memory of love.*

*And the only one left in the world was walking beside me.*
