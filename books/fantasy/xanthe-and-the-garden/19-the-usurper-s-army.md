## 19. The Usurper's Army

Hale had marched with armies before, but never one that smelled like this.

He'd expected the usual stench of a campaign in motion—horse dung and woodsmoke and the sour sweat of ten thousand men who hadn't bathed since the border fell. What he got instead was sweetness. Heavy, cloying, everywhere. The columns moved through a corridor of blossom that hadn't existed three weeks ago, hawthorn and something he couldn't name flowering out of season on both sides of the king's road, and the air was so thick with perfume that the men had stopped complaining about it, which worried him more than the smell itself.

An army should complain. An army that went quiet was an army thinking thoughts its officers hadn't approved.

"Report," he said, without turning.

Corporal Vess fell into step beside him, notebook already open. She was twenty-three, sharp as a paper cut, and the only member of his staff who still wrote things down instead of simply *remembering* them, which Hale had come to understand was a distinction that mattered.

"Third column reached Marrow's Ford at dawn," she said. "No resistance. Town surrendered before the heralds even arrived."

"No resistance." Hale watched a supply wagon roll past, its wheels silent on a road carpeted with fallen petals. "Or no one left capable of it?"

Vess's pen hesitated. That was the thing about Vess—she noticed what she noticed, and she didn't pretend otherwise. "The scouts say the townsfolk came out to meet them. Smiling. Brought bread."

"Bread."

"And flowers. They put flowers in the soldiers' hands." She closed the notebook. "Sir, I've been through four conquered towns now. I haven't seen a single crying child."

Hale said nothing for a long moment. The column wound ahead of them toward the horizon, banners limp in the windless morning, and above it all the sky sat empty and black where the stars should have been—not dark, exactly, but *vacant*, like a room after the furniture's been repossessed. He had grown up under those stars. He had taught his daughter their names before she could write her own. Their absence was not grief; it was theft, and Hale had built his entire life on the principle that theft must be answered by law.

He had believed, five weeks ago, that the law was what he served.

"Inquisitor." A courier had appeared at his elbow, one of the palace men, gray-cloaked and faceless behind his riding mask. "His Excellency requests your presence at the front. The heralds are conducting a harvest at Thornfield, and His Excellency wishes the Office of Inquiry to observe protocol."

"Protocol," Hale repeated.

"The new protocols." The courier extended a sealed folio, wax stamped with the crown—and beneath the crown, Hale noticed for the first time, a small device he'd been seeing more and more of lately: a sprig of leaves, worked into the wax so finely you'd miss it if you weren't looking for it. He was looking for it now. He saw it everywhere. On the army's new standards. On the requisition forms. On the collar of the provost-marshal who had stopped asking prisoners questions and started *listening* to them instead, hours at a time, in tents that smelled like honey.

He took the folio. The courier was gone before he'd finished breaking the seal.

* * *

Thornfield had been a market town of perhaps two thousand souls. It sat in a shallow valley, and from the ridge above it, Hale looked down at what conquest looked like when the Verdant King conducted it, and felt the first clean cold thread of understanding go through him.

There were no fires. There were no corpses. There was no line of refugees stumbling west with bundles on their backs—the sight he had spent thirty years of service preparing himself to prevent.

Instead there was a garden.

It had swallowed the town the way water swallows a dropped stone. Vines sheeted the rooftops. Hedgerows stood where fences had been, twelve feet high and dense as masonry, herding the streets into green corridors. And in the square at the center—Hale borrowed Vess's spyglass and pressed it to his eye—the people of Thornfield stood in rows.

They stood very quietly. They were smiling. Down each row walked figures in robes woven from living leaves, the King's heralds, and each herald carried a staff crowned with a bud the size of a fist, and as they passed along the lines the buds would open—slowly, tenderly, the way a hand opens to receive something—and lean toward a villager's brow, and drink.

Hale watched a farmer's face as the bud touched him. Watched the smile stay fixed while something behind the eyes went out like a lamp in a far window. When the herald moved on, the farmer remained standing, serene, vacant, tended. A moment later a vine curled up around his ankles almost affectionately, and guided him—gently, gently—toward a doorway that had been walled over with flowering thorn.

"They're not killing them," Vess whispered. She had the glass now, and her voice had gone thin. "Sir, they're not even hurting them. Why would anyone fight this?"

"That," Hale said, "is the question."

Because that was the horror of it, laid bare on a hillside in the morning light: resistance required a reason, and the Verdant King was in the business of removing reasons. You could not rally a town against an enemy that took nothing visible. No plunder, no blood, no burned granary to point at and say *there, there is your grievance.* Just a bud against the forehead and a lifetime folded away into someone else's keeping. The townsfolk of Thornfield would wake tomorrow in bodies that remembered how to work and mouths that remembered how to smile, and they would never know what had been taken, because knowing was precisely what had been taken.

Hale had spent his career hunting memory-mongers. Charm-sellers, mind-thieves, the gutter-witches of Vell's night markets who sold other people's weddings by the vial. He had believed them parasites. He had believed the law existed to protect the interior country of the self, the one possession no magistrate could enter.

And here was the crown itself, running the largest harvest in history, and calling it liberation.

"Count the heralds," he said quietly. "Count the staffs. Count the intervals between harvests. I want numbers, Vess. Rates. Yield per town."

She lowered the glass and stared at him. "Yield, sir?"

"What would you call it?" He heard his own voice go flat and hard, the voice he used in courtrooms. "I've read the new protocols. They don't call these people casualties or subjects. They call them *sources*. If we're going to write reports, we'll use honest words."

* * *

He wrote the report that night in his command tent, by lamplight, in the careful legal hand that had convicted forty-one memory-mongers and never once been overturned on appeal.

*To the Office of Inquiry, Vell. Observations at Thornfield, day nineteen of the advance. Harvest conducted openly, without pretense of consent. Population unharmed in body; memories extracted en masse via agency of the heralds' instruments. Extracted material conveyed under guard toward the capital in sealed wagons. Inquirer notes: the statutes of the realm define exactly this practice, when performed by private parties, as grand larceny of the person, punishable by forfeiture and the wall. Inquirer further notes: no statute exempts the crown. Inquirer therefore concludes—*

He stopped. The pen hovered. Somewhere outside, the camp was singing—not the rough marching songs armies always sang, but something slower, sweeter, hundreds of voices in unison on a melody he didn't recognize, and the sweetness of it crawled up the back of his neck.

*Inquirer therefore concludes that the authority ordering these harvests acts against the law of the realm, and recommends formal inquiry into the chain of command extending to—*

To the crown. To the throne. To whatever wore it.

His pen scratched the words out anyway. That was the whole of his creed, the thing he had told Juniper Sable in her cell, the thing he had clung to through every ugly order of the past years: the law was not the king's property. The law was the wall between the strong and the weak, and if the king broke the wall, then the king stood accused like any man. He signed it. He sealed it with plain wax—no leaf-sprig, he made sure of that—and gave it to Vess with orders to place it in the dispatch case bound for the Office of Inquiry, and he slept, badly, listening to the singing.

In the morning the dispatch case was gone.

Not lost. Gone. And in its place, on his field desk, lay his own report, unopened seal intact, and atop it a single hawthorn blossom, fresh, dew still on it, though there were no hawthorn trees within a mile of the camp.

There was no note. There didn't need to be. The message was legible enough: *We read what you write. We are patient. Look how beautiful we are.*

Hale stood holding the flower for a long time. Then he did the only lawful thing left to him—he began a second copy, hidden, written in cipher, to be carried by hand.

* * *

He went looking for Vess at midday and found her tent empty, bed made, kit squared away with the neatness she'd learned in her first year under him. Her notebook was gone too. So was the spare cipher key he'd issued only to her.

"Corporal Vess was reassigned," the sentry told him, when asked. "Palace detail. Left before dawn."

"By whose order?"

The sentry's eyes flicked sideways—a small motion, quickly mastered, but Hale had spent thirty years reading witnesses and he read it fine. "Orders came down, sir. I couldn't say whose."

"Then say what the orders looked like."

The boy's throat moved. "They smelled like flowers, sir," he said, very quietly, and then straightened and stared ahead and would say nothing more, and Hale understood that he was looking at a man who had decided survival meant not-knowing, and who was already practicing.

He filed the report on her disappearance. Of course he did—it was procedure, and procedure was the last dry ground he owned. The report traveled up the chain and came back down in four days stamped *RECEIVED AND NOTED*, which was the bureaucratic equivalent of a headstone. No inquiry. No search. A twenty-three-year-old corporal with a mother in Vell and a habit of writing things down had stepped off the face of the earth, and the machine that Hale had served his whole life absorbed her without so much as a change in gear.

That night he took out the second copy of his report—the hidden one, the cipher one—and read it through, and added a final paragraph in a hand shakier than he liked:

*I have served the law for thirty years on the belief that it was a wall. I now have evidence that it has been, for some time, a garden gate. The law does not serve the crown. The crown serves the thing in the garden. Every statute I have enforced, every thief I have condemned, every memory I have confiscated and destroyed in the name of order—I ask myself tonight whose hunger all of it fed. I ask whether the charm-sellers of Vell were ever the crime, or merely the small version of it. I do not yet have the courage to write the answer. I am working on the courage.*

He sealed it and hid it inside the binding of his old law primer, the one book no palace servant would think to search, because everyone knew Inquisitor Hale kept the law like scripture and scripture was safe.

Then he sat awake until dawn, listening to the camp sing its sweet songless song, and thought about a woman named Juniper Sable sitting in a cell somewhere, weaponizing truth against anyone who came near her, and thought: *she showed me records of my superiors' complicity, and I called it sedition, and I was wrong, and being wrong is not the sin—staying wrong is.*

He thought about the free cities to the west, and the road to them, and how many days' ride.

He thought about Vess, and whether she was smiling somewhere, tended, hollow, and whether she had thought of him at all in the last hour before whatever hour had been her last.

And when dawn finally came—gray and starless and sweet-smelling—Inquisitor Hale rose, dressed, buckled on the insignia of an office he no longer believed in, and walked out to review the troops with his face arranged into obedience, because a man planning treason must first become very good at seeming loyal.

Three columns east, the heralds were packing their instruments. The army would march on the free cities within the week. Whatever Hale was going to do—defect, testify, burn the whole rotten edifice down with the truth as his torch—he would have to do it fast, and alone, and soon.

He touched the law primer in his satchel, once, like a man touching a talisman.

Then he smiled at the soldiers, and gave the order to break camp, and began, carefully, to plan.
