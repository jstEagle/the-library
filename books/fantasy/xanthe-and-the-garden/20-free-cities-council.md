## 20. Free Cities Council

### Ilsa

The Council of Free Cities met in the Salt Hall at Corvane, a chamber built for argument the way fortresses are built for siege. Seven tiers of benches ringed a floor of pale stone worn concave by two centuries of pacing delegates, and the acoustics were famous: every whisper carried to every seat, every evasion echoed back at its speaker until it died of shame.

Ilsa Vane stood on the lowest tier with her ward-keeper's badge torn from her coat and the hole it left still visible in the wool, and she listened to six cities negotiate their own surrender one clause at a time.

"Terms," said Delegate Maro of Sallow Ford, a soft man with a ledger's voice. "We open our gates, we disband our militias, we submit to census. In exchange, the crown guarantees—" he consulted his notes, though everyone knew them by heart— "guarantees non-conscription and continued market autonomy."

"The crown guarantees nothing," Ilsa said.

Maro didn't even look up. "Delegate Vane is not a member of this council."

"I'm the only person in this room who has seen what happens to towns that submit." She let that sit. The acoustics did their work; three hundred heads turned. "I walked through Hallowmere last month. You won't find Hallowmere on any map now, because there's no one left to ask where it was. The people are alive. They smile. They cannot tell you their children's names. That is what 'census' means when the Verdant King takes it. He counts you the way a farmer counts stock."

Murmuring, the sound of a hundred private fears given public permission. Maro's jaw tightened. "Hysteria and rumor—"

"Then let me show you something that isn't either."

She turned to the doors, and Xanthe came in.

They looked, Ilsa thought, exactly like what they were: a thief asked to walk into the most official room in the free world, wearing their best coat over the fear. Their eyes swept the tiers the way a pickpocket reads a crowd—not hostile, just cataloguing exits. Then they found Wren's face in the visitors' gallery, held there a half-second too long, and looked away with the particular blankness of someone touching a scar they can't remember earning.

"Delegates," Ilsa said. "This is Xanthe of Vell. A memory-charmer. What they're about to do is illegal under crown law and, as of yesterday, under ours. I'm asking your indulgence before I ask your attention."

Xanthe stepped onto the concave floor, alone at the center of seven tiers of stares, and Ilsa watched them decide—visibly, the shoulders dropping an inch—to stop performing calm and simply be afraid out loud instead. It was, she had learned on the road, the more convincing of the two.

"My trade was stolen memories," Xanthe said. Their voice carried perfectly; the hall had been built for confessions too. "A widow's wedding day. A soldier's last sunset. Five years of other people's lives, sold in little glass charms so I could eat." They drew a chain from beneath their collar. On it hung perhaps thirty small vials, each glowing faintly with the color of its contents. "Three weeks ago, some of them started doing this."

They lifted one vial high. Inside, the memory-light—the soft gold of ordinary recollection—was threaded through with green. Not reflected green. Grown green, like moss through mortar, like roots through a coffin.

"This was a baker's memory of his daughter's naming day. Watch."

Xanthe unstoppered the vial and tipped a single drop onto the pale stone.

The hall leaned forward. For a moment nothing happened, and Ilsa felt the council's skepticism reassemble itself—

—and then the drop *bloomed*.

A flower opened on the bare rock, white-petaled, perfect, obscene. And rising from it came a sound: a child laughing, a woman singing a naming-song in a dialect none of the Corvane delegates spoke but every one of them recognized as *someone's mother*. The memory played itself into the air, and woven through the sweetness ran the green note, the garden note, and the flower grew as it fed—a second bloom, a third, tendrils creeping toward the nearest bench with the slow confidence of something that has never once been told no.

A delegate shrieked. Someone else knocked over a chair getting away from a flower.

"That is my merchandise," Xanthe said over the noise. "That is a *child's birthday*, and the Verdant King has gotten his roots into it, and when I sell a charm like this now, the buyer starts sleepwalking toward the nearest gate within a week. His garden grows through memories the way fire grows through a hayrick. Every mind it touches becomes soil." They crouched and, with a thief's steadiness, pinched the first bloom off at the stem. The rest wilted instantly, orphaned. "He doesn't want your gates open, Delegate Maro. Gates are for armies. He wants your *heads* open. Surrender isn't a term he'll honor. It's a harvest method."

Silence, of the heavy kind. Ilsa counted it a victory anyway. Fear without a plan curdles; fear with a plan is fuel.

"Assuming," Maro said at last, with the delicacy of a man stepping over a corpse, "that this display is genuine and not stagecraft between a disgraced ward-keeper and an admitted criminal—what precisely would the council be authorizing? Resistance how? Our combined levies are four thousand men with pikes. The usurper fields forty thousand. Arithmetic does not care about flowers."

"Arithmetic doesn't care about kings, either," said a voice from the third tier, dry as old paper. "Which is why we should establish whether there still is one."

Juniper Sable rose from among the archivists' benches, ledger under one arm, prison-still thin, moving like someone who had learned to make their body cost as little as possible. The hall murmured—they knew the name; the archives had been officially purged of Juniper Sable twice.

"You've heard Delegate Vane's testimony and this charming demonstration," Juniper said. "Both true, both incomplete. The question underneath every question today is simple: who wears the crown? If the thing in the palace is merely a sorcerer wearing King Aldous's body, then he is a tyrant, and tyrants can be killed, and cities have survived tyrants for as long as there have been cities. But if the king himself is alive in there—buried, captive, the binding anchored to his living blood—then killing him kills the anchor's host and frees the parasite into whatever soil it likes next. Everything depends on the answer. And we have no answer, because the parasite has spent a century erasing the records that would give us one." Juniper tapped the ledger. "*Except* the ones I pulled out of the fire. And those say: the Verdant King is bound to the monarchy itself. Fed by harvested memory. Kept sleeping inside its host unless woken. Which means somewhere beneath all that beautiful horror, there is an old man who may not even know he's a king anymore."

"And if he is?" someone called. "What then?"

"Then he can be *unbound* rather than killed, and the parasite starves. I have the rite. Partially. Its final requirement is—" Juniper paused, and Ilsa saw them choose honesty over tidiness— "unconfirmed. We go to the palace to finish the scholarship as much as anything else."

"So go to the palace," Maro said. "With whose army?"

"With no army at all," Ilsa said, and felt the sentence leave her like something she'd been carrying since Hallowmere. "An army can't get within ten miles of the capital; his heralds would taste it coming—an army is nothing *but* shared memory and intention, a feast walking in formation. What's needed is small. Quiet. People who know wards, records, locks, and lies." She looked around the tier, at the delegates and their fine terror. "You want proof the king lives. Fine. Proof requires going to look. And looking requires volunteers, because I will not order anyone into that place, and neither should you."

The silence stretched. Delegates examined their hands. Somewhere above, a pigeon resettled in the rafters with a sound like a page turning.

It was Wren who moved first—Ilsa heard the gallery rail creak—but Xanthe got there ahead of them.

"I'll go," Xanthe said, from the center of the floor where everyone could watch them volunteer, which Ilsa understood was deliberate. Thieves knew about witnesses. "I've spent five years learning to steal past wards built to stop exactly this kind of infiltration. Also, apparently I'm already infected, so I've got the least to lose and the best reasons to be furious." A ripple of uneasy laughter; Xanthe let it live for exactly one breath. "But I want it minuted that I'm not doing this for the free cities. I'm doing it because that thing is growing inside my own head, using my trade as a root-cutting, and I intend to pull it out by the crown."

"I'll go," Wren said, standing in the gallery, and the hall turned. "My grandmother served the old court. There are doors in that palace that answer to our blood and no one living knows it but me."

"I am obviously going," Juniper said. "You'd lose the rite without me. Also I refuse to be rescued a second time; it's undignified."

Ilsa stepped down onto the floor beside Xanthe. Her heart was doing something complicated that she filed under *later*. "And me. I built the border wards. I know what the palace's defenses used to look like, which means I know what they've become."

Maro stared down at the four of them—at the thief, the exile, the archivist, the ghost—with an expression Ilsa had seen on men watching a boat put out into weather no sane sailor would cross in. "You understand," he said slowly, "that if you fail, this council will deny ever having heard of you. Officially, you are private citizens acting without sanction."

"Officially," Xanthe agreed pleasantly, "we've been private citizens all along. Ask anyone I've robbed."

The vote, when it finally came near midnight, was not a vote for war—the arithmetic delegates saw to that. It was a vote for *inquiry*: supplies, safe houses along the river road, letters of passage under false names, and a single line, fought over for an hour, granting the expedition "such further assistance as circumstances may warrant." Bureaucratic cowardice, Ilsa thought, watching the tally board. And also, somehow, the first time in a century that any governing body had put its seal to opposition against the crown.

Circumstances, she intended, would warrant a great deal.

### Hale

Inquisitor Edmund Hale arrived at Corvane two days behind his report and one day ahead of his arrest.

He knew it was coming. You did not accuse the Lord Protector's household of treason in a formal dispatch and expect a pension. So he had done the arithmetic the way he did everything—precisely, unhurriedly—and concluded that the free city of Corvane, currently hosting a panicky council and a great deal of unsupervised archive traffic, was the correct place for his next professional act.

He presented himself at the Salt Hall's security desk in full inquisitorial black, warrants displayed, and requested entry to observe proceedings relevant to crown security.

The clerk read his credentials twice, went pale, and sent for someone senior.

While he waited, Hale reviewed his position with the satisfaction of a man whose accounts balance. He had testimony—sworn, witnessed, dated—of heralds harvesting civilian memories under military protection. He had a confiscated report proving the army command knew. He had a missing aide whose disappearance he had also documented, because if he was going to be destroyed, the record of his destruction would at least be accurate. And he had the law. Not the crown's convenience of the law—the law itself, the primer his father had pressed into his hands at fifteen: *authority derives from charge and answer; a power that cannot be questioned is not authority but force.*

For eleven years he had believed he served the former. The evidence now indicated the latter. This was not, he reminded himself for the fourth time that morning, a crisis of faith. It was a finding of fact. Feelings were irrelevant. Procedure was everything.

The someone-senior turned out to be a harried woman with a marshal's sash and ink on her fingers, who took one look at him and said, "Inquisitor Hale. Yes. We've been expecting you."

"Then you have the advantage of me."

"We have a message that came through the river safe houses two days ago. Unsigned. It said an inquisitor of the third rank would defect to this council carrying evidence against the Lord Protector's household, and that he'd present it in proper legal form, because he wouldn't know any other way to do it." She studied him with frank curiosity. "Was it right?"

"It was accurate," Hale said. "I haven't decided yet what it is."

"Good. Honest. Come with me." She was already walking. "There are four people you need to meet, and I warn you, one of them is a thief, one is a traitor to your service, one is a dead archivist, and one is the reason half this council hasn't slept in a week. They're leaving for the capital tomorrow night, and the council is about to vote on whether you go with them—as witness. Your evidence, formally entered. Your oath, transferred." She glanced back. "You should know the vote is close. Half the delegates think you're a plant."

"They're right to think it. I would send a plant."

The marshal laughed despite herself. "Saints. You *are* going to be popular."

The Salt Hall again. The same concave floor, the same acoustics that made whispers public. From the doorway Hale watched the tail of the session: the vote passing by a margin of four, the delegation of the four volunteers standing at the center—ward-keeper, thief, archivist, and a quiet man with court-blood in his face whom Hale marked automatically as *significant, unknown*.

Then the marshal announced him.

The hall's reaction taught Hale something no intelligence briefing had: fear travels faster than reputation. Three hundred delegates flinched at the word *inquisitor* as one animal. On the floor, the thief—Xanthe—went very still in a way Hale recognized from interrogation rooms, the stillness of prey calculating distance. The disgraced ward-keeper moved half a step to stand between them. Interesting. Interesting *all around*.

"Inquisitor Edmund Hale," the marshal read, "third rank, presents evidence against the household of the Lord Protector, and requests transfer of oath to this council, per the Provision on Defection, subsection—"

"*Subsection nine,*" Hale finished. "If the chamber pleases."

He crossed to the center of the floor, unrolled his testimony, and began to read in the flat, merciless cadence of sworn record: dates, units, quantities of memory harvested, names of officers who signed the requisitions. The acoustics gave every name to every seat. He watched the council hear its fears confirmed in bureaucratic prose and understood, with a strange calm, that this was the most useful thing he had ever done: not heroism, not conversion—just procedure, aimed like a weapon at the men who had mistaken force for authority.

When he finished, the thief spoke up from beside him, without raising a hand, in the tone of someone adding a postscript to a will.

"For the record. I've seen what the heralds' harvests feed. If the council wants proof of where all this goes—it goes under the palace, into a vault of hanging lights, and one of those lights is mine." Xanthe touched their collarbone, where no vial hung, an unconscious gesture toward something gone. "Your inquisitor's paperwork and my nightmares agree. That's usually when a fact stops being deniable."

Hale looked sideways at them—the memory-thief, the criminal his writs had hunted for five years—and found, to his considerable discomfort, a colleague.

"The vote," said the marshal.

It passed by six. Closer than his evidence deserved, Hale judged, and sufficient regardless; majorities were for ceremonies. As the chamber emptied, the ward-keeper approached him, arms folded, assessing him the way she'd assess a suspect wall.

"Hale. I've read your file. You burned a school of hedge-witches at Thornmarch."

"I supervised arrests," Hale said. "Four died resisting. I have never burned anything." He considered, then added the truth, because the truth was the entire currency he had left. "I believed then that I was enforcing lawful order. I believe now that I was enforcing a fiction maintained by a parasite. Both beliefs were sincere. Only one of them is supported by evidence. I try to keep my beliefs current."

Vane's eyes narrowed—and then, unexpectedly, she almost smiled. "You're going to be unbearable on the road."

"Noted."

That night, packing his satchel in the safe house above a chandler's shop, Hale laid out his effects with a quartermaster's economy: the law primer, the testimony copies, a change of clothes, and—after a long moment—the black badge of his rank. He did not throw it away. He wrapped it in cloth and stowed it at the bottom, because evidence of what he had been belonged in the record too, and he was, whatever else had changed, still a man who kept records.

From the next room, low voices: the thief and the court-blooded one, Wren, talking in the careful overlapping way of people circling a wound neither could name. Hale noted it, classified it *not my operational concern*, and failed entirely to stop listening.

Tomorrow night, the capital. Tomorrow night, the root of it.

He closed the satchel, and slept, for the first time in a month, without dreaming of trials.
