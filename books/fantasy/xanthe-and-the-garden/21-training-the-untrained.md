## 21. Training the Untrained

The mill had been abandoned for thirty years before the garden got it.

Ilsa Vane stood in its doorway at dawn and took inventory the way she'd once taken inventory of wards: what was broken, what was load-bearing, what would kill you if you touched it wrong. The great waterwheel had rotted to a skeletal half-circle. Ivy—ordinary ivy, thank every patient god—climbed one wall. The millpond beyond was still water, still cold, and nothing in it moved that shouldn't.

Behind her, the strike team was waking up badly.

Hale had slept in his boots again, sitting upright against the wall like a man who expected arrest in his dreams. Wren had not slept at all; Ilsa could tell by the way they held their cup of chicory, both hands, knuckles white. Juniper's absence sat in the middle of the room like a fifth chair no one used. And Xanthe—

Xanthe was already outside, barefoot in the frost, drawing shapes in the mud with a stick.

"Your thief," Hale said quietly, coming to stand beside her, "has been out there since before light."

"I know."

"They're practicing returning memories. On themself." His jaw worked. "That's not how the craft is taught. That's not how anything is taught. You don't practice surgery on your own—"

"No," Ilsa said. "You don't. Which tells you everything about where Xanthe learned it." She stepped down into the frost, boots crunching, and raised her voice. "Everyone up. We have maybe three days before the council's scouts confirm the army's route, and after that we move whether you're ready or not. So let's make sure you're ready."

Wren groaned into their cup. Hale straightened like a soldier called to inspection, which, Ilsa supposed, he was. Three days to teach two civilians and one lawman how to survive the Verdant King's garden.

She'd trained ward-keeper cadets for eleven years. She'd never once had to teach them to fight a hedge.

* * *

"The first thing you need to understand," Ilsa said, pacing the mill floor an hour later, "is that the garden doesn't want to kill you. Killing you is wasteful. It wants to *keep* you."

She had laid out her materials on a trestle table: iron filings in a wooden bowl, a bundle of dried rowan, a spool of copper wire, six smooth river stones, and a jar of honey that had cost Wren two hours of haggling in the last village with a pulse.

"Everything the Verdant King grows is a trap with a flower on it. The hedgerows on the capital road—they look like walls, yes? Obstacles. They are not obstacles. They are mouths that move slowly. You walk along a hedge long enough, brushing it with your sleeve, and it learns the shape of you. Your stride. Your scent. By the time it closes, it isn't closing on a stranger. It's closing on something it has been cultivating." She let that sit. "So rule one: never touch the green. Not with skin, not with cloth, not with a walking stick you intend to keep."

"And if we must touch it?" Hale asked.

"You don't. But if you must—" she picked up the bowl of iron filings "—iron interrupts the graft. A handful thrown against a hedge will make it flinch back for perhaps ten seconds. Ten seconds is enough to run if you were already running. It is not enough to hesitate in." She poured a thin line of filings across the threshold of the door. "Rule two: thresholds matter to it more than they matter to us. A doorway, a gate, a bridge—it can't easily grow through a boundary that was made with intent. This mill's doorsill was cut by a mason who meant it as a doorsill, and that meaning holds. So when you move through the garden, you move from threshold to threshold. Never sleep under open sky if stone or timber with a purpose is available."

Wren raised a hand, half-sarcastic, wholly serious. "What about the flowers themselves? The ones that smell like—"

"Like your grandmother's kitchen. Like the summer you turned nine. Yes." Ilsa set the rowan bundle down. "Those are the worst thing in the garden, and everyone always asks about the hedgerows instead. The flowers are memory-bait. Breathe them in and you'll remember something so perfectly, so completely, that standing still to feel it will seem like the only reasonable act left in the world. People kneel in those flowerbeds and smile until the roots take them." She looked at each of them. "If you smell something impossibly sweet, you breathe through your mouth, you count backward from a hundred, and you grab the arm of the person next to you. That's an order. Grabbing arms is now doctrine."

"It saved my life twice on the hedgerow road," Xanthe said from the doorway. They'd come in silently—Ilsa was learning that Xanthe did most things silently—and frost still clung to their hair. "Once for me. Once because Ilsa grabbed me."

"You bit me," Ilsa said.

"You grabbed me without warning."

"I'm adding warnings to doctrine too, then. Rule three: warn people before you grab them, but only if there's time, which there won't be."

Hale almost smiled. Almost. It was progress; Ilsa noted it and moved on.

* * *

Afternoon belonged to Xanthe.

Ilsa stayed for the first lesson partly to supervise and partly because she was, though she'd have denied it under oath, curious. Memory-craft had been outlawed before she was born. Everything she knew about it came from confiscated manuals and burned teachers. Watching it done was like watching someone speak a language she'd only ever seen written.

Xanthe had cleared the mill's center floor and placed Wren in a chair facing them. Six river stones ringed the chair.

"What I do is theft," Xanthe began, in the flat merchant's voice Ilsa recognized from the night market—the voice that pretended feeling was inventory. "What you're going to learn is not theft. It's carrying. Think of yourselves as couriers, not thieves. A courier takes a package, keeps it whole, and delivers it intact. If you damage what you carry, you haven't helped anyone—you've just robbed them slower."

They held out their hand. In the palm lay a charm—one of the old ones, a twist of silver wire around a bead of glass. Ilsa saw the light inside it turn slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

"This is a stranger's memory. A fisherman's, actually. The morning he taught his daughter to gut a mackerel. Small. Harmless. Perfect for practice." Xanthe crouched so their eyes were level with Wren's. "When I give this to you, you'll live it. Not watch it—*live* it. Your hands will be his hands. Here's the discipline: it isn't yours. When it ends, you put it down. All of it. The love you feel for the daughter in that memory is not your love. Grief, joy, fear—borrowed, all borrowed. If you carry someone's grief home and keep it, you've become a hoarder, and hoarders end up like the village that forgot itself. Empty houses full of other people's furniture."

"How do you put it down?" Wren asked. Their voice had gone quiet. "Truly?"

"That's what the stones are for." Xanthe picked one up. "Grounding. Before you take a memory, you name five things that are true about *you*, here, now. Your name. Where you are. What your body feels. What you choose. Then you take the memory, and when it ends, you say them again. If any of them feel false—if you can't remember your own street but you can remember a dock you've never seen—that means you didn't put it all down. And then you come to me, and I pull the rest out, and it hurts, and you'll forgive me eventually."

"Eventually," Wren repeated.

"I'm told I'm very forgivable." Xanthe pressed the charm into Wren's palm and closed their fingers over it. "Five truths first. Go."

"My name is Wren. I'm in a ruined mill east of Vell. My hands are cold. I'm afraid." A pause. "I choose to be here."

"Good. Now open."

Ilsa watched the change come over Wren's face and felt her stomach tighten. One moment they were a tired resistance courier in a cold room; the next, their expression softened into something older, rougher-handed, sunlit. They laughed—a laugh that wasn't theirs—at something no one said. Their right hand moved in a small practiced motion, filleting air.

Then it ended, and Wren sat blinking, and Xanthe said, "Truths."

"My name is Wren. Mill. Cold hands. Afraid. Choose to be here." Their voice steadied on each word. But at the last one they stopped, and looked at their right hand, and said, very softly: "There was flour on the dock boards. Why do I know there was flour on the dock boards?"

"Because he knew it, and it stuck." Xanthe took Wren's hand and, with a motion so quick Ilsa barely caught it, drew something out—a thread of pale light, wound onto a fresh charm. "See, this is why we practice on small ones. The residue. Every carry leaves a trace. One trace is nothing. Ten traces and you start having dreams that aren't yours. Fifty—" they shrugged "—fifty is how the King's harvesters work, actually. They never take a whole person at once. They take traces until there's nothing original left to resist with."

The room had gone cold in a way that wasn't temperature.

"So we drill," Xanthe said briskly, and the merchant's cynicism slid back over them like a coat. "Every day, until it's boring. Boring is safe. Everyone take a stone."

* * *

By the third evening, they had a rhythm, and the rhythm had, without anyone announcing it, become a household.

Hale cooked. Nobody had assigned it; he had simply started doing it on the second day, producing from nowhere a stew of barley and salt pork that tasted like an army mess hall and, in that ruinous place, tasted like civilization. He ate last, after everyone else, and washed the pot himself, and Ilsa caught Wren watching him do it with an expression that had traveled a long way from suspicion.

Wren kept watch and told stories on watch, loudly, so the rest of them could hear through the walls. Half the stories were true. They refused to say which half.

And Ilsa drilled them, morning and night, until the countermeasures lived in their hands: iron thrown low, breath through the mouth, thresholds marked, arms grabbed without warning. On the third night she ran them through the darkened millyard blindfolded, calling hazards, and Wren tripped over a root, and Hale caught them by the collar without breaking his count, and afterward they all stood in the cold laughing in the ragged, disbelieving way of people who had forgotten laughter was allowed.

Ilsa stood apart, as she usually did, and let herself feel it for exactly as long as it took to notice she was feeling it.

She had built the border wards. She had believed, for twenty years, that safety was a thing you constructed between yourself and the world—a wall, a procedure, a clean division of inside from out. She had lost her circle, her rank, her name in the order, because she'd insisted on reporting a crack nobody wanted acknowledged. And here was the lesson the garden had been trying to teach her since the stars went out, delivered finally by a barley stew and a blindfold drill: safety was never a wall. It was these four people knowing each other's counts.

"Vane." Hale appeared at her shoulder, two cups in hand. Chicory. It was always chicory. "A moment."

"If it's a confession, save it for a priest."

"It's strategy." He handed her a cup anyway. "The convoy schedule. I've been thinking about it."

Juniper Sable was somewhere on the north road, in a prison wagon bound for the capital, and every hour of training was an hour stolen from rescue. They all knew it. None of them had said it aloud until now.

"The escort is twelve men," Hale said. "Regulars, plus two of the King's garden-priests. I wrote the transfer orders myself before I—" he paused, choosing honesty, "—before I understood what I was serving. Which means I know the route. There's a ford at Marrow Bridge where the road crosses the Ellow, and the wagons slow to a walk."

"You're proposing an ambush."

"I'm proposing that we stop pretending we have time for training." He stared into his cup. "Inquisitors' convoys don't wait for us to be ready, Vane. The army reaches the free cities in nine days. Juniper knows things we need—the rite, the cipher, the king's name, everything—and every day in that wagon is a day the priests spend picking through their mind. You've seen what they do wholesale. Imagine what they do carefully, to one archivist, with time."

Isla looked across the yard. Xanthe and Wren were at the fire, heads bent together over a charm, Xanthe's hands guiding Wren's through some delicate turning. They weren't touching otherwise. They didn't need to be. Whatever had been taken between them, the shape of it was still there, the way a doorsill holds its meaning.

"We're not ready," she said.

"No."

"Wren has managed exactly one clean return without residue. Hale, you've never carried anything. I've never carried anything. And Xanthe—" she lowered her voice "—Xanthe hasn't slept properly in four days, because every time they close their eyes, the infected charms whisper."

"Correct on all counts," Hale said. "And the convoy passes Marrow Bridge tomorrow at midday."

The fire cracked. Somewhere past the tree line, faint as a thought, something green was growing toward them through the winter dark.

Ilsa drained her chicory. "Wake them up," she said. "Doctrine's over. Tomorrow we steal an archivist."
