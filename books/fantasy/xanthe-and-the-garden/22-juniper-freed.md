## 22. Juniper Freed

### Xanthe

The convoy left Greywater Prison an hour past dawn, and by noon Wren had already found three ways it could go wrong.

"Four," they said now, crouched beside Xanthe in the gorse at the top of the ridge, breath fogging in the cold. "I forgot the outriders."

Xanthe counted them without meaning to—old habit, a thief's arithmetic. Six soldiers on horseback flanking a black wagon whose wheels were banded in iron. Two more on the box seat. A driver who sat too straight to be a driver.

"Twelve armed, one prisoner," Xanthe murmured. "And us?"

"Nine." Wren's mouth twitched. "If you count Ilsa, which she insists you do twice."

Behind them, half-buried in the ditch where the old road dipped toward the ford, Ilsa Vane was doing something patient and terrible with copper wire and river stones. Ward-craft, but field-made—the kind that used to be taught in the border academies before the academies were burned. She'd spent the morning laying it like a snare, humming under her breath, and when Xanthe had asked what the song was she'd said, *a counting rhyme for dead ward-keepers*, and hadn't elaborated.

Hale stood apart from all of them, arms crossed, watching the road with the flat attention of a man reading a legal document he already knew was forged. Three days since he'd unlocked their cells. He still moved like someone waiting to be arrested.

"You're sure she's alive?" Xanthe asked.

Wren didn't answer right away, and that silence told Xanthe everything about how sure they were. But then Wren said, quietly, "The Verdant King doesn't kill archivists. It archives them."

*Charming.*

The plan was simple, which meant it would probably work, which meant Xanthe distrusted it. The ward-trap would take the lead horses. Hale would walk into the open road wearing his inquisitor's grey and his lawful face and order the convoy to halt in the name of statutes so old most soldiers had never heard them cited aloud—and men hesitated for Hale, because hesitation was trained into anyone who'd ever been on the wrong side of a tribunal. In those seconds of hesitation, Wren and the resistance fighters would come off the ridge, and Xanthe—

Xanthe would do what Xanthe did best. Get inside. Take what needed taking.

In this case: Juniper Sable.

They waited for the wagon. The road ran white and empty under a sky that still had no stars in it, even at noon—even though the stars had supposedly come back two days ago over the free cities. Here, closer to the capital, the sky stayed blank as scraped vellum. Whatever the Verdant King was doing to hold its ground, it held the heavens too.

"There," Wren breathed.

The convoy crested the far hill, and Xanthe felt something twist behind their ribs—not fear exactly. Recognition. Somewhere in that black wagon was the person who'd opened the first door for them, who'd shown them that the histories in their head were contraband worth more than coin. Who'd been taken because Xanthe escaped the cistern raid with the ledger and Juniper hadn't.

Debt collector, meet debtor.

"Hold," Wren whispered, hand raised. "Hold... hold..."

The lead horses reached the ford. Reached the stones Ilsa had dressed with wire.

"Hold—"

The world went green-white and silent.

Not thunder—ward-work didn't thunder, it *unmade*, and for one heartbeat the six lead horses simply weren't touching anything, hooves skidding on air that had briefly forgotten to be ground. They came down screaming. Two went into the river. The rest bolted back up the road and plowed straight through the outrider line, and suddenly there were four fewer soldiers standing, and Hale stepped out onto the road in his immaculate grey coat with his hands visible and his voice pitched to carry:

"In the name of the Concord of Nine Cities, article twelve, this conveyance is seized! Any man who resists commits treason against the law itself!"

It shouldn't have worked. It absolutely should not have worked. Twelve soldiers against nine irregulars with one mad ex-ward-keeper and one disgraced inquisitor—but soldiers were trained to obey the voice of the law before they were trained to think about whether the law still lived, and for three full heartbeats the entire rear guard stood frozen while Wren's people poured off the ridge.

Then a sergeant started shouting orders, and the heartbeats ran out.

Xanthe was already moving.

### Wren

Later, Wren would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail, because battle did not arrange itself into narrative—it happened all at once or not at all, and mostly it happened in the corners of your eyes.

What they remembered clearly: the sergeant going down under Tam's shield-boss. The driver drawing a blade and Ilsa stepping out of the ditch like something rising from a grave, one hand splayed, and the driver's blade *wilting*—rusting, softening, dropping from fingers that had gone suddenly numb. What they remembered less clearly but more painfully: a crossbow bolt that took Denna of Millbrook through the throat, and the way she folded, and the way there was no time to fold with her.

And what they remembered best, the part they knew they'd carry: Xanthe reaching the wagon door, pressing both palms flat against the iron banding, and the banding *opening*—because Xanthe had spent five years learning locks from the inside, and the Verdant King's garden-iron was just another lock if you understood what it wanted. Everything the King made wanted to grow. Xanthe gave it somewhere to grow. The bands split along seams no smith had cut and peeled back like petals.

The smell that came out of the wagon made Wren gag even at forty feet.

Juniper Sable lay in the corner of a cell that had been built for growing things. Moss on the walls. Roots through the floorboards, pale and hair-fine, and—Wren's stomach turned—*flowering*. Small white blooms opening along the roots in slow pulses, like breathing.

And Juniper, curled among them, was reciting.

Wren got the cell door open and dropped to their knees and heard it: a cracked whisper, barely a voice anymore, running through catalog numbers. Shelf designations. Cross-references. The liturgy of a person holding themselves together by reciting what they loved.

"—cross-ref Sable 4471 to Vell municipal ordinance 9-through-14, see also the burning of the Aldermoor library, see also—" The eyes that found Wren's took a moment to arrive. Then Juniper smiled, and the smile was worse than crying would have been. "Oh. You're real. I'd begun to suspect I invented all of you."

"Juniper—"

"They planted things in me." Flat. Clinical. An archivist describing damage to a collection. "Every time I slept. Little roots. They wanted the ledger locations." A pause, and something flickered behind the wrecked face—pride, ferocious and absurd. "They got nothing. I put the ledger *somewhere they couldn't reach*, and then I ate my own map to it."

"Eat—"

"I memorized it and burned the memory-shape out of my waking mind. There's a technique." Juniper coughed, and the cough had roots in it somehow; Wren didn't want to know how. "Crude. Painful. I don't recommend it. Help me up. And bring the page."

"What page?"

Juniper's hand emerged from the folds of their ruined robe clutching paper gone soft as cloth. "Stole it off the warden's desk my third week. He reads it every night before bed, the fool. Thinks it's scripture." Their grip closed on Wren's wrist with sudden shocking strength. "It's the rite, Wren. The unbinding rite. Read it when we're not lying in a flowerbed."

* * *

### Xanthe

The fight ended the way these things always ended—suddenly, lopsidedly, with the survivors of the escort throwing down weapons once they understood that the grey-coated inquisitor commanding the other side was real, and ranked, and very much prepared to hang his own former comrades for continuing to serve a crown under formal charge of treason. Hale read them the charge list while they knelt in the mud. Two of them wept. One asked, in a small voice, whether the stories about the heralds harvesting towns were true, and Hale looked at him for a long moment and said, "Worse. Now get up. You're conscripted."

Which was how their force of nine became eleven, minus Denna, who got wrapped in a blanket and carried, because Ilsa refused to leave her and nobody argued with Ilsa.

By dusk they'd put eight miles between themselves and the ford, holed up in a shepherd's ruin whose roof had half fallen and whose remaining half was strangled in ivy. Xanthe caught themselves eyeing the ivy with new hatred and made themselves stop. Ordinary ivy. Probably. They built the fire anyway, well back from the walls.

Juniper sat closest to the flames, wrapped in every spare cloak they owned, hands shaking around a tin cup of broth they kept forgetting to drink. But their eyes were clear now—clearer, Xanthe thought, than they'd ever seen them. Something had been burned out of Juniper in that wagon, and whatever grew in the space was harder than what it replaced.

"Well," Juniper said. "Read it to me again. Slowly. I want to hear it in a voice that isn't mine."

Wren unfolded the page. The firelight made the ink swim. Old royal cipher, transcribed into plain script by whoever had written the original—a century ago, maybe more, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places.

"The unbinding requires four workings," Wren read. "The ward-key, to open the root-vault. The witness of law, to name the binding theft and make it legible. The blood of the old court, to call the sleeper by his true name." A breath. "And fourth: *a memory freely given, of being loved by one whom the king himself loved.*"

Silence around the fire.

"That's it?" Hale said at last. "That's the whole requirement? Someone has to give him a memory of loving him back?"

"Not loving him back." Juniper's voice was thin but precise. "Read the grammar. *Loved by one whom the king loved.* The memory has to be freely given—that's the hinge, that's why the parasite could never do it, the thing can only take—and it has to be a memory of the king *being loved*, held by someone the king cared for. Reciprocity. Proof from outside the binding that he was a person, not a crown."

"So we find someone the king loved," Ilsa said. "A hundred years ago. Or however long."

"And ask them to hand over the dearest thing they own," Wren said quietly, "to a stranger."

Nobody answered that.

Xanthe stared into the fire and felt the shape of the problem close like a trap. "Who even remembers the real king? The records are ash. Juniper said it themselves—erased. The parasite's worn his face for so long that the man underneath is a rumor. We don't know what he loved. We don't know who loved him. His wife, his children, his friends—they'd be dust by now, or so old they're legends, and even if some ancient retainer is alive in some village somewhere, how do we find them before the armies reach the free cities?"

"We ask the king," Juniper said.

Everyone turned.

"He's still in there. Buried, dreaming, but *there*—the rite presumes it, the whole architecture assumes a mind to wake. He'll remember who loved him. He's the last archive of his own life." Juniper finally drank the broth, grimaced at the cold. "Of course, to ask him, we have to reach him through a century of parasite-root and a garden that eats memories, and we have to do it fast enough that asking isn't moot."

"Wonderful," Ilsa said. "So the plan is: fight our way into the throne room of a god-shaped weed, interview the hostage, hope he remembers his own heart, locate a person who may be a legend, persuade them to surrender their happiest memory, and complete a rite no one living has performed—all before the Verdant King's army finishes eating the free cities." She fed a stick to the fire. "I've served under worse plans. Barely."

"It's not a plan," Juniper agreed. "It's a bibliography. But bibliographies become books. That's how it's always done."

Xanthe laughed—short, surprised out of them—and saw Juniper's mouth curve in answer, and felt something in their chest loosen for the first time since the cistern raid. Juniper was back. Broken and re-set, but back.

Then Wren shifted beside them, and the page rustled, and Wren said, "There's a postscript. I almost missed it. Smaller hand—different ink."

"Read it," Juniper said.

"*To whoever finds this: the fourth working cannot be counterfeited. The parasite tried, in the early years. It harvested a thousand loving memories and stitched them into a garment and wore it to the binding, and the binding laughed at it. Love given under compulsion, love bought, love stolen—none of it holds. Only what is offered. Remember this. Remember that it will offer you everything to avoid this. —R.*"

The fire popped. Out in the dark, wind moved through the ivy on the ruin's broken wall, and for a moment—in the way the fronds swayed, in the rhythm of it—Xanthe could almost swear it sounded like breathing.

*R*, Xanthe thought. Whoever you were, you knew it would come to this. You wrote us a map out of hell and signed it with one letter, and we're going to follow it anyway, because there's nothing else.

"One letter," Wren murmured, staring at the mark. "A hundred years, and all we get is one letter."

"Archivists have rebuilt empires from less," Juniper said. Then their gaze drifted to the fire, and their voice dropped into something quieter and more terrible than any catalogue. "But understand what you're carrying into that palace. The rite needs a gift. The King will offer you a thousand gifts to stop you giving one. And someone—" the clear, hard eyes moved around the circle, resting on each of them in turn, and Xanthe felt it like a physical touch, "—someone in this company is going to have to decide what their best memory is worth. Before we ever reach the root. Decide it now. Tonight. Because the garden won't give you time to decide later."

No one spoke.

Xanthe looked across the fire at Wren—at the stranger who kept choosing to stay, whose face did something complicated whenever their eyes met, who had appeared out of nowhere five years after Xanthe's own past vanished and claimed to be the center of it. Xanthe couldn't remember loving them. Couldn't remember being loved. But watching Wren listen to Juniper's warning, watching their jaw set, Xanthe thought: *whatever memory you'd give, I already know which one it would be.*

And they didn't know why they knew. That was the worst part.

Above the broken roof, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

Eighty miles to the capital.
