## 23. The Palace Gardens

The palace had eaten its own walls.

That was the first thing Xanthe understood, crouched in the shadow of a hedgerow taller than the gatehouse it had swallowed, watching green tendrils thread through stone the way fingers thread through hair. The outer fortifications of Corvale—the seat of kings for nine hundred years—were still there. You could see their bones: a buttress here, a crenellation there, the arched suggestion of a barbican. But everything between the bones was leaf.

"It's worse than the ledger said," Ilsa breathed beside them. She lay flat in the dead grass at the treeline, her ward-keeper's glass raised to her eye. Even now, even here, she wore the grey half-cloak of her order like an accusation she'd decided to stop apologizing for. "The wards I built on the border were designed to fail outward. Push anything hostile *away* from the kingdom." Her jaw worked. "This grows inward."

"Everything about this place does," Wren murmured on Xanthe's other side. They'd been walking since before dawn, and Wren's voice had gone quiet in that particular way it did when they were frightened and refusing to spend words on it. "Juniper's maps put the servants' postern on the north face. If it still exists."

*If.* The whole plan was built on *if*. Juniper, recovering in the free cities from months in an inquisitor's cell, had pressed a hand-drawn map into Xanthe's hands and made them recite the route twice. Hale had argued for waiting—for his tribunal, his split army, his slow machinery of lawful resistance—and the council had overruled him with arithmetic: the Verdant King's heralds were three days' march from the free cities' granaries. Tonight or never.

So: tonight.

Xanthe checked their charm-bag out of habit, though they knew exactly what it held now. After the village-that-forgot, after Wren, after everything, they carried almost nothing they hadn't earned the right to carry. A lockpick charm Juniper had taught them to make. A burst of a blacksmith's forge-fire, hot enough to blind. And one memory they refused to name, kept wrapped in three layers of intention, that they told themselves was for emergencies.

It wasn't. It was just too heavy to set down.

"Movement," Ilsa said.

They watched a patrol come around the hedge-line—not soldiers. That was the thing that made Xanthe's skin crawl, more than the hedges, more than the dark where stars should have been. The patrols of the palace gardens were gardeners. Men and women in unblemished aprons, moving with pruning shears and lanterns, tending the growth with the tenderness of people who genuinely loved what they were doing. One of them paused by a rosebush the size of a wagon, cupped a bloom in both hands, and *inhaled*, eyes closed, smiling.

Then he turned his head toward the treeline—toward them—and smiled wider, and walked on.

"He saw us," Wren whispered.

"No," Ilsa said. Her knuckles had gone white around the glass. "He saw something. He doesn't know what he saw. That's worse."

* * *

They went in through the roots.

It had been Xanthe's idea, because Xanthe thought like a thief, and a thief knows that every fortress has two doors: the one it shows you and the one it grows. The palace's drainage system predated the Verdant King by four centuries, cut through limestone that the parasite's magic found hard to grip—or so they hoped. They waded the last hundred yards in water to their thighs, cold enough to make breathing a decision, following Ilsa's ward-glass which glowed a sickly amber whenever the walls around them began to green.

Twice, roots came down through the ceiling of the tunnel like the fingers of something feeling for them in the dark. Twice, Ilsa pressed a counter-sigil to the stone—a ward-keeper's trick, old craft, permission-shaped rather than force-shaped, *we belong here, we are stone's guests*—and the roots hesitated and withdrew, the way a hand withdraws from a door it has no right to open.

"They're not attacking," Xanthe said the second time. Their voice echoed strangely; the tunnel had begun to smell of loam. "Why aren't they attacking?"

"Because we're not worth it yet." Ilsa didn't look back. "Or because it already knows we're coming, and it wants us to arrive. Stop asking questions the answer to which you won't enjoy."

Wren laughed once, short and sharp, and took Xanthe's hand in the dark without either of them deciding to. Xanthe felt the callus on Wren's palm from a sword they'd learned to hold after Xanthe forgot them, and felt nothing—no flood of recognition, no warmth of return, only the fact of the hand itself, solid and present and *chosen*, and somehow that was worse and better than memory both.

They surfaced in the root-vaults beneath the east wing, in air thick as wool, and climbed.

* * *

The gardens proper began where the palace ended, and the distinction had stopped meaning much.

Corvale's famous terraces—Xanthe had sold memories of them, once; a duchess's recollection of her presentation ball, all fountains and string-light—were still terraced. But the geometry had gone soft, the way a face goes soft. Hedges had swollen into architecture of their own, arching over gravel walks in tunnels of green, and everywhere, everywhere, there were flowers blooming out of season and out of turn: winter roses beside spring crocuses beside autumn marigolds, a whole year happening at once, impatiently.

And the topiary.

The old royal gardeners had been masters. Peacocks, stags, heraldic lions, a famous yew unicorn that children rode for luck—all of it had been lovingly clipped into being across generations. Now the shapes remained, but they had grown wrong. Deeper. The stag's flanks bulged with new wood. The lion's mane had become a thicket with small pale flowers nested in it like teeth.

As they crossed the third terrace, keeping to the shadows of the hedge-tunnels, Xanthe heard the unicorn scream.

There was no other word for it. It was soft—that was the horror of it, that it was *soft*—a high, thin sound like wind through a keyhole, like a child singing far away in another house. It rose and fell. It did not stop. When Xanthe slowed, frozen, staring up at the yew shape against the starless sky, Ilsa caught their arm.

"Don't look at it long."

"What *is* it?"

"Memory." Ilsa's voice was flat, the voice she used for things she'd already grieved. "The wards sang when they held. These—" she nodded at the topiary, at the screaming unicorn, at the lion with flower-teeth, "—these are holding something too. Or someone. The King harvests memories, thief. Where do you think they go?"

"The root-vaults," Xanthe said. "Juniper said—"

"Some go to the vaults. The vaults store." Ilsa's eyes moved along the hedge-line, counting shapes, and Xanthe watched her count and saw her stop counting. "But some memories are *used*. Shaped. Grown into form. A garden needs ornament, and the King has a century of ornament to work with." She paused. "That unicorn might be somebody's wedding day. Somebody's mother's voice. Clipped into a shape and made to stand outside forever, screaming softly, so the garden looks loved."

Xanthe thought of their charm-bag. Of the widow's wedding day they'd sold in the night market five years ago, and the soldier's last sunset, and all the small bright stolen hours that had passed through their hands like coin. They had told themselves for years that selling memories was gentler than killing, that the people they took from mostly didn't miss what they couldn't remember missing.

Standing under the unicorn, listening to it sing its one note of grief into the dark, Xanthe finally stopped telling themselves that.

"We keep moving," Wren said quietly. "We get proof. We get out."

They moved.

* * *

The throne room, according to every map Juniper owned, opened onto the great west loggia, and the loggia looked down across the sunken garden called the Queen's Bowl—a hollow of lawn and roses where, in the old days, courtiers had strolled after supper and kings had conducted the business too delicate for council chambers.

The business, tonight, was being conducted in the Bowl.

They reached the loggia through a gallery hung with tapestries that had gone to seed—literally; ivy had taken the woven hunts and forests and grown them real, so that woven hounds chased woven stags that now trembled in three dimensions, soft screams threading out of the fabric like smoke. Xanthe led, because Xanthe's feet knew galleries the way honest people's feet knew roads: where the boards creaked, where the shadows pooled, where a guard would stand if there were guards. There were no guards. There was no need. The building itself was loyal.

At the gallery's end, behind a screen of flowering jasmine that had eaten a marble balustrade, they looked down into the Queen's Bowl, and Xanthe stopped breathing.

The court was assembled.

Courtiers stood in concentric rings on the lawn, dressed in clothes that had been magnificent a season ago and were now magnificent again, in the way that flowers are magnificent—petals growing from cuffs and collars, green shoots braiding through hair. They stood perfectly still. Not rigid, not afraid. *Content.* Their faces wore the mild, settled happiness of people deep in a pleasant dream, and when the breeze moved them, they swayed slightly, all together, like a field.

In the center, on a chair that had been a throne and was now mostly a tree, sat the king.

Xanthe had seen portraits. Every soul in Vell had seen portraits: King Aldous IV, a heavy-jawed, tired-eyed man in his fifties, painted a dozen times holding scepter and orb and the weight of a nation. The figure in the Bowl had the king's face. It also had the king's body, or what the king's body had become—upright, graceful, *blossoming*, crowned not with gold but with a circlet of living branches whose leaves turned, slowly, all of them together, tracking something Xanthe could not see.

The Verdant King was holding court.

"—and the western road is mine to the river," it said. Its voice was beautiful. That was the first unbearable thing. It was a warm voice, a patient voice, the voice of a grandfather telling a story everyone already loved. It came from the king's mouth, and it used the king's face to smile while it spoke, and the smile was kind the way a well is kind—deep, and dark, and full of your own reflection. "The free cities will send their petition within the month. They always do, when the harvest gets close enough to smell. And I will receive them graciously, because I am gracious, and I will ask them what they remember, and they will tell me, and then they will not need to anymore."

A murmur moved through the rings of courtiers—a sigh, really, a tide-sound of contentment.

One of them stepped forward. An old woman, in a gown of grey silk gone green at the hem. She curtsied with perfect grace and said, in a clear and pleasant voice:

"I had a daughter, majesty. I can't recall her name, but I know I loved her. Would majesty like my love? It seems a shame to waste it on a name."

The Verdant King inclined its borrowed head. Around its crown, the leaves turned faster.

"Keep the love, dear heart. Love is the vintage; names are only the barrel. Give me the day you named her, though. Such a fine day. Rain on the window, and her fists so small."

"Oh," said the old woman, radiant, "yes. Yes, it *was* fine—"

She closed her eyes. Something left her. Xanthe saw it go—it looked like heat-shimmer, like the ghost of breath on glass—and it streamed upward and sank into the crown, and the leaves shivered with pleasure, and somewhere on a terrace above them, faintly, a topiary nightingale began to sing.

Xanthe's hands had curled into fists so tight their nails drew blood. Beside them, Wren had gone very still. Below, the court sighed its tide-sound, and the Verdant King settled back among its branches, patient as weather, and said to its garden of dreaming faces:

"And tomorrow, we'll talk of the granaries."

Ilsa's hand closed on Xanthe's shoulder. Not pulling. Just resting there, steady as a keel. *Not yet,* it said. *Look. Learn. Live.*

Xanthe looked.

They looked until looking was a wound, memorizing everything a thief knows how to memorize: the layout of the rings, the number of gardener-patrols visible past the hedges, the way the crown-leaves stuttered, just slightly, each time it took a memory—a hitch, a blink, a momentary *hunger satisfied then immediately hungry again*. It could be distracted. It could be fed. It could, maybe, be *slowed*.

And beneath all of it—beneath the beauty and the patience and the terrible kindness—Xanthe felt the thing Juniper's cipher had promised and Xanthe had not quite believed: the king. Not the parasite wearing him. The man. Somewhere inside that blossoming body, buried deeper than the roots, an old tired mind sat dreaming in a garden chair, convinced that this was peace.

*Hold on,* Xanthe thought at it, absurdly, uselessly, the way you think at a stranger through a lit window. *We're coming.*

The Verdant King's head turned.

Not quickly. Nothing about it was quick. It turned with the unhurried grace of a sunflower finding the sun, and the king's handsome ruined face lifted toward the jasmine-screened loggia, toward the exact patch of shadow where three intruders stood not breathing, and the leaves of the crown went still, all together, like held breath.

For a moment, the whole garden was silent. Even the unicorn had stopped screaming.

Then the Verdant King smiled—the well-smile, the reflection-smile—and said, in its warm grandfather's voice, pitched perfectly to carry up through the jasmine:

"Do come down, little thieves. The garden has been expecting you."

The courtiers turned their heads in unison, all those contented dreamer-faces tilting up toward the balcony, and began, softly, to walk forward.
