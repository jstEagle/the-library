## 24. Audience with the Crown

They did not drag me. That was the first insult.

Two guards in bark-woven armor flanked me down the corridor, and though their hands never touched me, the walls themselves seemed to lean in, hedgerow pressing close from both sides until the passage narrowed to the width of a coffin. I walked because walking was the only choice left that was still mine. I counted my steps the way I counted coins—out of habit, out of spite.

Forty-one steps before the corridor opened, and I stopped counting.

The throne room of Vell's kings had been marble once. I'd seen it in a stolen memory—a pickpocket who'd climbed a drainpipe just to watch a coronation through a window, and I had sold that view a hundred times over, sunset light on white stone, trumpets, a young king waving at crowds he would never remember individually. Now there was no marble. There was no stone at all. The entire hall had been swallowed and replaced: living wood arched overhead in ribs like the inside of some enormous patient animal, and every surface bloomed. Flowers I had no names for opened along the pillars as we passed, turning—not toward the light, because there wasn't any light except them—but toward *me*.

They were smelling me. My memories. The whole stolen stock of my trade, all those fragments of strangers' lives packed behind my ribs like contraband, and the garden could smell every one of them the way a dog smells fear.

"Keep your eyes forward," one guard murmured. His voice had a rustle in it, leaves moving under words. "It is kinder that way."

Kinder. The Verdant King's servants spoke of kindness the way moneylenders spoke of charity.

The hall ended at a dais that had not been built but *grown*, tiers of root and blossom rising like a wedding cake left too long in the sun. And on it, beneath a crown of woven green that had fused with the hair beneath it—

Him.

I had seen the true king once, in that same pickpocket's memory. A slight man, thirty years old, laughing at something his sister said while priests fussed around him. What sat on the dais now wore that man's face the way my charms wore other people's joy: faithfully, hollowly, with nothing behind it. He was older. He was beautiful in the way overgrown ruins are beautiful. Vines threaded through his robes, through his skin at the wrists, up the side of his neck where they pulsed faintly, slow as breathing. His eyes were open and fixed on me from across the length of the hall, and they were the exact green I had glimpsed five chapters of my life ago behind a nobleman's memory—the green of light through leaves, of poison bottle glass, of spring.

Behind him, arranged in the shadows of the root-arches, stood his court. Heralds. Gardeners. Things that might have been people once, standing very still among the flowers, smiling the way the villagers of Hollowmere had smiled—with nothing behind it.

I made myself walk the last fifty feet alone. They let me. Of course they let me. Everything here was allowed; that was the horror of it.

When I reached the foot of the dais, the king descended. Not walked—*poured*, unhurried, vines rearranging themselves around his ankles like courtiers making way. Up close he smelled of loam and honey and something underneath it, older and wronger, the smell of a cellar where things grow in the dark.

"Xanthe," he said.

My name in his mouth sounded like it had always belonged to the garden. Like it had grown there.

"You know me," I managed.

"I have known you for five years." He smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was a *kind* smile, warm and rueful, the smile of someone greeting an old friend slightly late to dinner. "You have carried pieces of me since the night you took Lord Cassel's memory of the hunt. Did you think I would not notice? Every thief who steals from my garden leaves fingerprints. Yours were—" he tilted his head, considering, "—lovely. Careless in places. Lovely overall."

Lord Cassel. Chapter two of my ruin. The routine theft, the noble's memory, the green light behind the king's eyes that had started all of this. Five years ago. Before the wards fell, before the stars went out, before Wren—

Before Wren.

Something must have moved in my face, because the king's smile deepened by one degree, the way frost spreads across a window.

"Ah," he said softly. "There it is. You're wondering about them already. Good. That will make this easier for both of us."

"I'm not here to bargain."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You're here because you lost. But losing and bargaining are not so different, in my experience. Come." He turned, vines trailing, and gestured toward the dais. "Walk with me. I want to show you something, and then I am going to make you an offer, and you are going to hate how much you want it. I find honesty shortens these conversations considerably."

Against every instinct I owned, I followed him up.

* * *

What he showed me was a lantern field.

Beyond the throne, the hall opened into the root-vault—I didn't know its name yet, wouldn't learn it until later, but I knew what I was seeing immediately, the way a locksmith knows a lock. Hundreds of lights hung in the vaulted dark, suspended from roots like fruit. Each one glowed with a soft interior warmth, gold and rose and pale green, and each one *moved*, gently, the way a sleeper moves. As we walked among them, whispers drifted down—not voices exactly, more like the memory of voices, half-heard laughter, a snatch of a lullaby, someone saying *come home, come home, dinner's getting cold.*

Memories. Harvested. A century of them, maybe more, hanging here in the dark like lanterns in a festival that no one had ever attended.

"This one," the king said, stopping beneath a small amber light, "is a baker in the city of Sorrel. He remembers his mother teaching him to knead dough. It is the only thing he remembers of her now—he gave it to a charm-seller during the famine year, for bread. The seller sold it on. It passed through eleven hands before it came to mine." He looked up at the little light with something that looked, horribly, like tenderness. "He does not miss it. You cannot miss what you do not know you had. But *I* keep it. I keep all of them. Nothing loved is ever lost in my garden, Xanthe. That is the difference between me and your city. Your city lets people forget on purpose. For coin."

"And you take it without asking," I said. "For a crown."

"For everything," he corrected gently. "But yes. Also for a crown."

We walked on. The whispers thickened. And then, near the center of the vault, I saw it—a cluster of lights bound together with silver-green filament, brighter than the rest, humming almost audibly. Seven or eight of them, tangled close, and even before the king stopped, even before he turned that leaf-green gaze on me with unbearable gentleness, I knew.

My knees nearly went. I locked them. A thief learns to keep her face still above a full purse.

"You recognize them," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I don't know what—"

"Xanthe." He said my name again, patiently, the way you'd speak to a child insisting the stove isn't hot. "I am the oldest thing in this kingdom. Do not lie to me for your pride's sake. It costs you and buys me nothing."

I looked up at the cluster anyway. I couldn't not.

A house by the sea. Salt wind through a broken shutter. A kitchen table scarred with knife-marks, two cups, one chipped. A laugh I could almost hear and couldn't place, which was the cruelest part—it fit somewhere in my chest like a key fits a lock whose door has been bricked over. Wren. These were mine. These were the memories I had sold to buy Wren's escape, five years ago, the ones I'd been told about secondhand, the life I'd lived and couldn't feel. They hung here in the parasite's pantry, glowing, waiting.

"They came back to me," the king said, "when the charm they were stored in broke. Three months past. The woman who wore it died in the flowering of Ashford, and her possessions flowed downhill to my roots, as everything does." He reached up, and one vine extended, delicate as a finger, to touch the nearest light. It brightened at his touch. Leaned into it. "I could have shown them to you in the cell where you woke. I chose not to. Do you know why?"

"Because you wanted me to want them first."

"Because wanting," he said, "is the honest part. Everything else—pride, duty, rebellion—that is story. Wanting is just the body telling the truth." He lowered his hand. "So. Here is the truth of your situation, plainly told, and then my offer, and then you may say whatever you like."

He began to walk again, slower now, and I walked beside him because the alternative was standing alone in a room full of whispering ghosts.

"The unbinding rite your friends carry—the archivist's page, the ward-keeper's craft, the soldier-lawyer's authority, the bloodline girl's courage. Yes, I know. My roots drink deep, Xanthe; your council chamber leaks like a basket." He smiled at my expression. "It might even work. I am not immortal, merely patient, and patience has limits like anything. So I have prepared alternatives, as one does. If the rite fails, I continue. If the rite succeeds—" he shrugged, and vines rippled down his back like a shawl settling, "—then the kingdom needs a keeper. Someone to tend what remains. Someone who understands that memory is currency, and consent is the mint, and that a garden left unattended becomes a wilderness. I had hoped for the archivist, but she is made entirely of grievance. Grievance sours soil." His eyes slid to me. "You, though. You have spent your whole life handling what doesn't belong to you, and doing it *carefully*. Returning things, even, when no one paid you to. I have watched you give back a dead soldier's last sunset to his mother for nothing. Don't look surprised. Who do you think taught the flowers to listen?"

The floor seemed to tilt. All those nights, all those quiet kindnesses I'd thought were secret, hidden, mine—

"You've been watching me for five years."

"I've been *gardening* you for five years," he said, without shame. "And now you are ready to bloom, or to be pruned. Those are the options. Here is the shape of the first."

He stopped beneath the cluster of my own stolen life and turned to face me fully, and when he spoke, his voice changed—it dropped its courtliness, its ancient patience, and became simply, terribly, sincere.

"You walk out of here tonight. Hale's warrant dies unread; the inquisition forgets your name. Your friends go free—all of them, Juniper from her cell, Wren from their war, Ilsa from her guilt. The free cities get their winter, their councils, their slow clumsy peace; I will withdraw to the old borders and keep my bargains, because I always keep my bargains. And you—" he reached up, and with two fingers, as gently as lifting a moth, he detached one light from the cluster and held it out to me between us.

It was warm. Through its skin I could see movement: a kitchen, sea-light, hands—my hands, younger, flour-dusted—reaching for a cup that wasn't theirs.

"Wren's face," the king said. "Wren's laugh. The house, the years, the reason you wake up glad. All of it. Every memory you sold, returned whole, planted back where it grew. You will remember being loved. You will remember loving. You will lie next to them tomorrow morning and know exactly who they are and why you burned your life down to save them—and they will finally be known back, after five years of loving a stranger. Two people made whole with one gift." His voice softened further. "That is what I hold, Xanthe. Not threats. *This.*"

The lantern trembled between us. Inside it, the flour-dusted hands lifted the cup. Somewhere beyond its glow, faintly, someone laughed.

I could not breathe. I want to be honest, since the king valued honesty: in that moment I could not have told you my own name if you'd offered me a kingdom for it. Every lonely night since Wren found me in the waystation and said *you knew me, you knew me*—every time they looked at me with five years of love and I had nothing to return but politeness—it had been a wound I'd learned to walk on. And here was the cure. Held out. Freely offered. By the monster, yes, but the monster was right about one thing: wanting is the honest part, and God, I wanted. I wanted so hard my vision blurred.

"What does it cost," I whispered. Because there is always a cost, and thieves know it best of anyone.

"To be my keeper." He said it lightly, like naming a price in a market. "Tend the lanterns. Learn the roots. When I sleep—I do sleep, eventually, everything does—you keep the garden. In a century, perhaps less, perhaps more, you will stop noticing the difference between keeping a garden and being one. Most of my predecessors felt it was a fair trade. The baker's memory agrees, if you ask it."

"That's not a cost, that's a sentence."

"It's a *purpose*," he said, and for one flickering moment the courtliness cracked and something vast and tired looked out of the king's borrowed face, something that had been alone in the dark with a hundred thousand other people's happiest moments for a hundred years. "Do you know what it is, Xanthe, to hold everything everyone ever loved and have none of it be yours? To be the vault? I was made to keep things. I would like, before the end, to hand the keeping to someone who chose it." The crack sealed. The smile returned, smooth as bark. "But sentiment aside: yes. You would belong to the garden, as I belong to it. Forever is the term. I won't dress it as anything smaller."

The lantern swayed between us. The flour-dusted hands. The sea-light. Wren's laugh, almost audible, almost *mine*.

Somewhere above us, muffled by a hundred feet of root and stone, the palace shook—distant, rhythmic, deliberate. Ilsa's countermeasures, maybe. Hale charging the crown with treason in front of assembled officers, if he'd gone through with it. The clock ticking that Juniper had warned about. My friends were up there spending themselves to reach me, and here I stood, being handed everything I'd sold my soul for, priced at only my soul.

"Take your time," the king said graciously. "Though not too much. The rite begins at moonrise, and moonrise is soon."

I looked at the lantern. I looked at his green, green eyes.

And I heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized—steady, market-day flat, the merchant's voice I wore over the tender one:

"Show me the rest of them first. All seven. If I'm going to consider selling my forever, I want to see the full stock."

Because a thief knows: never grab the purse on the first display. Make them show you everything. Buy time in inches.

The king's smile widened, and for just a moment—half a heartbeat—I saw something flicker behind the green. Surprise. Respect. Maybe, God help me, hope.

"Of course," said the Verdant King. "Let me show you what you were."

And he raised his hands, and the whole cluster began to come down.
