## 25. Refusal

The Verdant King's offer hung in the air of the throne room like pollen, sweet and suffocating.

*Everything back,* it had said, through the mouth of a man who might once have been beloved. *Every memory you sold. Every year you carved out of yourself to survive. And more—the ones you never had to sell. Wren's face when they laughed. The house by the sea. Five years of mornings you gave away like a fool gives away gold.*

Xanthe stood in the center of the overgrown court, thorned vines curling at their ankles—not grasping, not yet. Just waiting. Courteous as a debt collector with good manners.

Behind the king's eyes, that green light pulsed patient as a heartbeat.

"You could be whole," the king said, and its voice was the rustle of leaves, the creak of orchards heavy with out-of-season fruit. "You could remember what it is to be loved."

And gods, Xanthe wanted it. That was the obscene part. They wanted it so badly their hands shook, wanted it the way a drowning woman wants air—instinctive, total, without shame. Somewhere in the hollow where five years of their life used to be, something leaned toward the king like a plant toward light.

They thought of the village.

Three days' ride behind them now, but vivid as yesterday: the smiling faces, the woman who couldn't name her own daughter. Xanthe had given her back one memory—a small one, a market day, a spilled basket of plums—and watched her weep herself whole in the street, clutching fruit-sticky hands like they were made of treasure.

That was one memory. One. The Verdant King was offering a hundredfold return—but the payment would be the same coin everyone else had paid. Their self. Their edges. The particular stubborn grain of who Xanthe was, dissolved into the garden's green patience until there was nothing left to lose because there was no *left* at all, no separate thing standing outside the bloom.

"No," Xanthe said.

The word came out rough. Unimpressive. The smallest word in any language, and the hardest one they'd ever said.

The court of flowering things went still. Somewhere in the rafters of the ruined throne room—or what had been a throne room, before the hedges came through the windows and took it—something with too many petals turned slowly to look.

"No?" The king smiled with borrowed lips. It wore the monarch's body well; that was the horror of it. Tall and gray-bearded and dignified, dressed in living ivy threaded with white blossoms, it looked like every painting of a wise king Xanthe had ever seen in Juniper's banned histories. "Child, I have worn this crown for longer than your city has had walls. I have heard ten thousand refusals. Do you know what they all had in common?"

"They were wrong?" Xanthe kept their voice flat. Merchant's cynicism. The mask that had kept them alive since they were fourteen years old selling pieces of strangers' lives in Vell's night market.

"They were afraid," the king said gently. "You are not afraid. You are *grieving*. And grief, little thief, is just love with nowhere to go. I am offering it somewhere to go."

"I've seen your somewhere." Xanthe lifted their chin. "A village full of people who smile at their children like strangers. An old man watering flowers on the grave of his own name. You don't give memories somewhere to go. You eat them. You ate a king and wore him to court."

Something moved behind the green light. Not anger. Something older and colder—*recalculation*.

"You would rather be broken," the king said. It wasn't a question anymore.

"I'd rather be *mine*," Xanthe said. "Even the broken parts. Even the empty parts. Especially the empty parts—they're proof I paid my own debts instead of making someone else pay them for me."

The silence stretched. A petal fell from the ceiling and landed without sound.

"Then keep your emptiness," the king said at last, and the kindness drained out of its voice like sap from a cut stem. "Keep it somewhere it can't do harm."

The vines moved.

They didn't strike—that would have been mercy, quickness. They *gathered*, rising around Xanthe like a tide deciding, and the last thing they saw before the green closed over their vision was the king turning away already, bored, returning its attention to the court of blooming horrors—as if Xanthe had been a minor negotiation, a footnote, a thief caught stealing bread and fined accordingly.

Then the thorns took them down into the dark.

* * *

The cell was beautiful, which was the worst thing about it.

It was a sphere of woven branches, or maybe grown was the better word—grown in an hour, grown around them while they were unconscious, walls of living wood studded with buds that opened as Xanthe woke, one by one, releasing a scent like honey and cut grass and something underneath that made their teeth ache with longing. Through the gaps between branches they could see other cells, or the shapes of other cells, hanging in the vast root-vault like lanterns in a festival hall. Some of them glowed faintly. Some of them *swayed*.

Some of them whispered.

"—remember the rain on the roof, we were children, we were children—"

"—her name, her name was—no. No. It was—"

"—sold for two coppers, two coppers, and I'd pay it again—"

Memories. Hanging in cells like theirs, memories harvested from a century of quiet theft, waiting to be ripened and eaten. Xanthe pressed their back against the thorned wall—carefully; even the wall had a kind of gentleness to it, thorns blunted, almost soft, the garden wanting no harm to come to its stores—and breathed.

Panic first. That was honest. Let it move through: the tight chest, the sweat, the animal certainty that this time the trap had finally closed for good. Inquisitors, ward-keepers, city guards—those Xanthe had outrun for fifteen years. But this was different. This was a thing that owned the country above them, that wore the law like a coat, that had eaten a king and planted a kingdom.

Let the panic finish. Then—work.

Because here was what fifteen years of theft taught you, the lesson under all the other lessons: *every lock is a habit.* Doors lock the way their makers think. Cells hold what their builders fear losing. And everything—everything—wants something.

The Verdant King wanted Xanthe's consent. That was why the cell was comfortable. That was why the thorns were soft and the air smelled like forgiveness. The parasite fed on freely given memory—Juniper's ledger had been clear about that, clear as scripture. Stolen memories soured in its roots. So it couldn't just take what it wanted from Xanthe.

It had to wait for yes.

"Fine," Xanthe muttered to the listening dark. "We'll both wait."

They inventoried themselves the way they'd inventory a mark's house. Hands: bound? No—the garden didn't need rope. Pockets: the guards hadn't bothered searching them, which meant either arrogance or that the garden considered pockets part of what it already owned. Either way—

Their fingers found it in the second pocket. Small, cool, shaped like a beetle of black glass.

Juniper's charm.

Made in the cistern archive, weeks ago now, pressed into Xanthe's palm with the archivist's dry humor: *"For the day some palace inevitably locks you up. Archivists plan for history repeating."* A lockpick charm—one of the old craft, pre-ban, keyed not to metal tumblers but to the *intention* of enclosure. Juniper had spent three months teaching it to recognize the shape of a locked thing and unmake the idea of it.

The problem: it worked by touch. And the lock here was a living sphere of thorned wood wrapped around their entire body, and the charm worked best on things built by human hands, and this cell was built by something that hadn't been human in a very long time.

But. Juniper again, in the cistern, ink-stained fingers tapping the ledger: *"Nothing in the rite is purely the King's work. It grew through a kingdom. It grew through people. There are human joints in everything it builds—there have to be. Even a parasite needs a host's bones."*

Human joints. Human habits. Somewhere in this cell's living weave, there was a place where a person's hand had shaped the growth—some gardener-servant, some thrall, some memory-hollowed villager set to tending the king's stores. And where human hands had worked, human habit lived, and where habit lived, the charm could find a seam.

Xanthe closed their eyes and did the hardest thing they'd done in weeks: they thought about Wren.

Not to grieve. To *aim*. The charm needed intention the way a blade needed force, and there was only one intention in Xanthe's whole hollowed heart strong enough to crack a king's prison. Not memory—they didn't have those anymore, not of Wren, not really. But they had the shape the memory had left. The way their chest answered when Wren walked into a room, that answering-before-understanding, the thing that survived being forgotten. The certainty that somewhere out there was a person who refused to be a stranger to them.

*I'm coming back,* they told that shape. *I don't remember you. I'm coming back anyway.*

They pressed the beetle-charm to the innermost branch and whispered the word Juniper had taught them—"open"—and poured everything else into it.

For a long moment, nothing. The charm warmed against their palm like a sleeping animal. The buds along the ceiling turned toward them, curious, drinking in the flavor of their longing—let them drink, let them choke on it—

Then, with a sound like a knuckle cracking in a cathedral, a single branch split.

Not wide. A finger's width. A seam in the world, letting in cold air that smelled of stone and old earth and—faintly, impossibly—of the sea. Beyond the gap, the root-vault spread: the hanging cells, the whispering lanterns of stolen lives, and far below, the pale tangled architecture of roots descending past sight.

And between Xanthe's cell and the next—drifting slow as dust—a single glowing thread, thin as spider silk, humming with familiar magic.

A ward-line. Ilsa's craft. Which meant either Ilsa was close, or the king had woven her stolen patterns into its own defenses—which meant the patterns had seams too. Everything grown through human hands had seams.

Xanthe worked the charm into the crack and began, patiently, methodically, with a thief's tenderness, to widen it.

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of root and stone, the free cities' council was running out of time. Somewhere in the palace, Hale was charging a crown with treason or dying for the attempt. Somewhere in the dark, Wren was carrying a secret heavy enough to break the world open.

And somewhere in this vault of stolen centuries hung the memories of a king who had loved someone once—who had been loved, and woken, and wept.

*Xanthe didn't know yet how they knew that.*

They only knew they had a seam, a charm, and a debt to pay. They put their shoulder to the crack in the world, and pushed.
