## 27. Escape Through Blooming Halls

The thorn cell opened like a mouth deciding, at the last moment, not to bite.

Hale stood in the corridor outside with his officer's baton still in his hand—the same baton he'd used to break the lock—and behind him three of his own soldiers lay groaning among the fallen petals of a hedge that had tried to stop them. His face was pale and set, the face of a man who had just committed treason in the name of the law and could not yet decide which of those words frightened him more.

"Up," he said to Xanthe. "Now. The king's guard knows I've gone wrong. We have perhaps a quarter hour before they decide we're a problem worth solving."

Xanthe got their feet under them. Their cell had been a cage of flowering blackthorn, and the flowers were already closing over the space where they'd sat, stitching themselves shut, erasing the evidence that anyone had ever been held there. They took one last look at it.

"Juniper," they said.

* * *

They found Juniper two cells down, and Juniper was already free—had been free, Xanthe would learn later, for most of an hour, working a stolen hairpin against a lock that should have taken a ward-key, because Juniper Sable did not believe in waiting for rescue when there was scholarship to be done. They came out of their cell dusty and blinking, clutching something flat wrapped in oilcloth to their chest like an infant.

"You're alive," Xanthe said stupidly.

"I'm *furious*," Juniper said, "which is adjacent." Then they saw Hale, and stopped, and the fury on their face rearranged itself into something colder and more careful. "Inquisitor."

"Hale. Formerly." He said it flat, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. "I've read your ledger, archivist. All of it. I charged the crown with treason an hour ago in front of forty officers, and half of them believed me, which is why I'm about to be hunted through this palace alongside a thief, a heretic, and—" he glanced at Ilsa Vane, who had appeared from the shadowed end of the corridor with her ward-satchel slung across her back and her jaw set like masonry, "—whatever she is."

"Ward-keeper," Ilsa said.

"Disgraced one," Juniper said.

"Currently employed," Ilsa said, and looked at Xanthe. "Which way?"

"The rite needs the root-vault," Xanthe said. "Beneath the throne. But the palace has changed since I was brought in. The halls—" They hesitated, because saying it aloud made it real. "The halls are growing."

* * *

It was worse than growing.

The Grand Processional, where Xanthe had been marched in chains past ranks of bowing courtiers, was now a tunnel of green. Ivy had swallowed the pillars whole; roses the size of cabbages bloomed from the cornices, and their scent was thick enough to taste, sweet and rotten at once, like fruit left too long in the sun. The marble floor still showed in patches, but between the patches, moss grew in patterns—not random, Xanthe realized with a lurch. In *rows*. Cultivated. Tended.

And everywhere, softly, the sound of screaming.

Not loud. That was the horror of it. The topiary along the walls—hedges cut into the shapes of courtiers, of guards, once, horribly, of a child—made a sound like wind through a keyhole, rising and falling, and when you listened too long you began to hear words in it. Names, maybe. Or pleas. Xanthe made the mistake of stopping beside the child-shaped hedge, and the shape turned what might have been a face toward them, and the keening rose into something almost articulate—

"Don't listen." Ilsa's hand closed hard on their shoulder. "It's bait. It wants you standing still. Everything in this palace wants you standing still."

"It's people," Xanthe said. Their voice came out thin. "Ilsa, some of these are *people*. Memories grown into shapes. Like my stock, but—" rooted. Alive. "Like lanterns, but planted."

"Then we'll dig them up later." Ilsa's voice didn't shake. Her hands, Xanthe noticed, were white-knuckled on her satchel strap. "Right now we walk."

They walked. Hale led, because he alone still knew the palace's true geometry—he'd served here for eleven years, and he navigated by counting doors that no longer existed, turning at intervals that seemed arbitrary until you understood he was walking a ghost map of the building as it had been, refusing to let the garden's new geography overwrite the old one. It was, Xanthe thought, very much like him. The law was also a ghost map. You kept faith with it even when the world grew over it.

Behind them, far off, horns began to sound.

"They've decided we're a problem," Hale said, without breaking stride. "Faster."

* * *

The first fight happened at the junction of the Processional and the Gallery of Kings, where the corridor forked around a fountain that had become a pond choked with lilies, and out of the lilies rose the gardeners.

They had been guards once—you could see it in the way they moved, the drilled economy of soldiers—but the Verdant King had repurposed them the way it repurposed everything. Their armor was bark now, their eyes were seed-pearls, and where their mouths had been, flowers opened and closed, breathing pollen into the air. There were eight of them. They drew no weapons. They simply walked forward, unhurried, certain, the way weather is certain, and the pollen ahead of them hung in the air like a golden curtain, and Xanthe knew—if it touched you, you'd stand still. You'd smile. You'd let yourself be planted.

"Cover your faces!" Ilsa shouted, tearing a strip from her sleeve and soaking it from her flask. She'd brewed something bitter-smelling; Xanthe caught a whiff of it and their eyes streamed. "Wormwood and salt! Breathe through the cloth!"

Then Ilsa Vane stepped forward alone.

Later, Xanthe would try to describe what they saw, and never quite manage it. Ward-craft was supposed to be a defensive discipline—wards held, wards sealed, wards maintained. But Ilsa had spent fifteen years learning how borders failed, and she had learned, in the process, everything about how they *worked*, and a border drawn sharply enough is a weapon. She pulled chalk and iron filings from her satchel and drew a line across the corridor floor in one long stroke, speaking as she drew—a ward-keeper's cant, old words, sharp ones—and the line *caught fire without burning*. A wall of pale light stood up out of the floor, humming.

The first gardener reached it and came apart. Not burned—unraveled. Its borrowed shape lost its argument with reality and it collapsed into a drift of dead leaves and a scatter of seed-pearls that rolled and spun on the marble like dropped jewels.

The second and third came together, and the wall flickered, and Ilsa went down on one knee with blood running from her nose, holding the line with both hands pressed to the floor.

"It's feeding on me to hold," she said through her teeth. "Go. *Go.*"

"We're not leaving you," Wren said—they'd all forgotten, briefly, that Wren was there, quiet as ever at the rear—and then the fourth gardener came over the top, through a gap above the light-wall where the ivy had grown to the ceiling, dropping silently toward Ilsa's back.

Xanthe didn't decide anything. Their hands simply moved, thief's hands, faster than thought, and the memory-charm they'd carried since Vell—the widow's wedding day, the one they'd never been able to sell, the sweetest thing in their stock—went spinning through the air, and they caught the gardener's flowering mouth with it, pressed it home like sealing a letter.

For one heartbeat the creature stood utterly still.

Then it wept. Golden pollen fell from it like rain, and inside the pollen-haze its soldier's face surfaced for a moment—just a man's face, confused, mortal—and it stepped backward off the line, off the edge of the gallery, and was gone into the green below, taking the wedding day with it, giving it back somewhere, maybe, to whatever remained of itself beneath.

"Ilsa," Xanthe said. "*Move.*"

"I can't hold it and move."

"Then don't hold it. Drop it on my mark. Everyone through the left fork—NOW—"

They ran. Ilsa snatched her hand off the floor and the light-wall died mid-flicker, and the remaining gardeners surged forward into the space where it had been just as the last of them cleared the fork, and then Ilsa was running too, bleeding freely from the nose, laughing in a cracked wild way that Xanthe had never heard from her and never wanted to again.

"Left fork goes to the servants' stair," Hale panted. "It's narrow. They can't come six abreast. It buys us time."

"How much time?"

"That depends entirely," Hale said, "on how fast you climb."

* * *

They climbed. The servants' stair was a stone throat barely wide enough for two, spiraling up through the heart of the palace, and somewhere below them the sound of pursuit changed—from footsteps to a rustling, a growing, a green whisper of things climbing the walls faster than any man could run.

Halfway up, the stair began to bloom.

Small flowers first, pushing through the mortar joints, white and innocent. Then thicker. By the third landing the steps themselves had gone soft with moss, and the handrail was a living branch, and the air was warm and humid as a greenhouse, and Xanthe understood with cold clarity: the palace wasn't chasing them. The palace was *digesting the route behind them*. Every stair they'd already climbed was sealing itself with new growth. Retreat was being deleted.

"There," Juniper gasped from the middle of the line. They had fallen behind, and now they stopped entirely, leaning against the branch-handrail, and Xanthe saw why: the oilcloth bundle clutched to their chest had torn. Green shoots were moving in the folds of it, patient, hungry, winding toward the pages within.

Juniper's archive-copy of the unbinding rite. The only copy. Juniper had transcribed it by lamplight in a prison cell from memory and hidden pages, and now the garden had found it, and the garden wanted it dead.

"Juniper." Xanthe reached for them. "Give it to me, I'll carry it—"

"No time." Juniper's voice was terribly calm. Scholar's calm. They had already unwrapped the bundle, and the pages were browning at the edges where the shoots had touched them, and the shoots were multiplying. "It's in the paper now. If I pass it to you, it passes to you, and then it's in your coat, and then it's in your lungs while you sleep. This is how it works, Xanthe. This is how it always worked. It doesn't burn knowledge. It *grows over* it."

"Then we save the pages—"

"The pages are lost." Juniper looked up, and their eyes behind the smudged lenses were bright and absolutely steady. "But I am not."

Xanthe understood one second before it happened, and the second was not long enough.

Juniper Sable pressed the ruined manuscript flat against the wall of the stair, laid both palms over it the way a ward-keeper lays hands on a failing border, closed their eyes, and *took it in*. Not reading—Xanthe would swear afterward it was not reading—it was a theft performed in reverse, a harvest performed upon oneself. Juniper had spent thirty years memorizing banned histories in case the books burned; this was only the largest book they had ever burned into themselves. Line by line, cipher by cipher, the rite went into the archivist's mind, and the archivist's body paid the toll: their breath went ragged, their knees buckled, blood threaded from both nostrils, and the shoots on the wall, finding nothing left to grow over, shriveled and curled in on themselves like questions no one would answer.

Then Juniper tore the empty pages apart, scattered the scraps into the humid dark, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"Well. Now I'm the archive. Try to keep me alive."

* * *

They came up out of the stair onto the high gallery that ringed the throne room's upper reaches, and stopped, all five of them at once, because below—visible through a colonnade of living columns that had been marble that morning—was the throne room floor, and on the throne room floor, the Verdant King was holding court.

The monarch's body sat straight and beautiful amid a sea of flowers, and the courtiers knelt in rows, smiling, still, planted to the waist in beds of soft loam where the audience chamber's tiles used to be. The king's head turned slowly, surveying its garden, and even at this distance, even through the green haze, Xanthe felt the attention of it pass over them like a searchlight. They ducked behind a column. The others followed.

"It knows we're loose," Wren breathed.

"It's known since the cells opened," Ilsa said. She had stopped the nosebleed with moss and salt; her face was gray but her hands were steady. "It's choosing not to care yet. It thinks we're already caught. Everything here is a trap with patience."

"Then we disappoint it quickly," Hale said. He had gone very quiet, in the way Xanthe was beginning to recognize as his version of fear managed and filed. "The root-vault entrance is below the dais. Which means we need to cross that room. All of it. Past the king."

Silence, except the soft keening of the topiary, except the wet green breathing of the palace.

"Not past," Xanthe said slowly. An idea was forming, and it was a thief's idea, ugly and practical. "Through the *walls* of it. Those colonnades—the columns are hollow, look, the vines grew around the old marble and left channels. I've crawled tighter spaces than those for less reason."

"And if something's in the channels?" Wren asked.

"Then something's in the channels." Xanthe tested the nearest column, found the gap where vine met stone, and began to pull themselves up. Below them, the Verdant King raised one graceful hand, and every planted courtier turned its smiling face upward, in unison, toward the gallery.

"But hurry," Xanthe said. "I think court is about to be in session."

* * *

Inside the columns, the world was a green throat.

The channels were exactly wide enough for a person who had spent years in other people's houses and attics and strongrooms, and not wide enough for comfort, and they smelled of sap and old sweetness, and they were not empty. Things moved in the walls—slow, blind, rooting things, and once something brushed Xanthe's ankle with what felt unmistakably like fingers. They didn't scream. Screaming uses breath, and breath was needed for crawling.

They went by feel and by Hale's ghost map, whispered from column to column: *left at the third joint, down where the vine forks, follow the cold stone.* The cold stone was the truth of the palace underneath, the marble the garden hadn't warmed yet, and they followed it down and down, the keening fading above them, replaced by a deeper sound, a pulse, slow and vast, like a heart made of sap beating somewhere beneath the floor of the world.

The root-vault. They were close. Xanthe could feel their own stolen memories answering it, tugging in their chest like dogs hearing a whistle.

They reached the column's base in a chamber of tangled roots older than the dynasty, and Ilsa dropped down last, landed badly, and swore with a fluency that would have gotten her expelled from the order twice over.

"Door?" Juniper rasped. They were gray-faced, swaying, thirty years of archive crammed behind their eyes, and still their mind was already working. "There has to be a door. Vault doors are made by people, and people build doors—"

"Here," Hale said.

It was small, human-sized, iron-banded oak, half-swallowed by root but not yet pierced—as if the garden were saving it, or savoring it. Across its face, in old royal cipher, the same warning Ilsa had found carved into the border wards five years ago:

*WHAT IS GIVEN CANNOT BE GROWN OVER.*

Isla put her hand flat against the wood. Beneath her palm, faint but unmistakable, the door was warm.

And from the other side—muffled, distant, impossibly patient—came the sound of a hundred voices whispering in the dark, and among them, threading through them like a single bright wire pulled from a knot of rope, a voice Xanthe knew better than their own name.

Their own.

Below the roots, in the vault where a century of stolen memories hung like lanterns, something that belonged to Xanthe was calling them home—and the door was opening from the inside.
