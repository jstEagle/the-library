## 28. The King Beneath

The root-vault had a door made of forgetting.

That was the only way Xanthe could describe it afterward, though at the time they simply knew: the archway of woven yew at the bottom of the garden stairs was not locked, not guarded, not warded. It was *absent*. Anyone who reached it would look at it and think of something else — a debt unpaid, a lover waiting, a door back home that suddenly seemed very important to check. The Verdant King didn't need sentries. He had a century of stolen longings to whisper in your ear until you turned around.

Xanthe stood before it with their palms pressed flat against the living wood and thought of nothing at all.

It was harder than picking any lock they'd ever touched. Harder than emptying their face for a con, harder than going still in an alley while Hale's lantern swept past. Every skill they had was a skill of misdirection, and here misdirection was the trap. So they did the thing that had always terrified them most: they opened themselves completely, let every stolen fragment in their chest sit visible and unhidden, the widow's wedding day, the soldier's sunset, Wren's laugh that wasn't theirs, and offered all of it up like a thief turning out their pockets.

*I have nothing you want,* Xanthe thought at the door. *I'm already full of you.*

The yew shivered. And because the garden's whole nature was appetite — and because Xanthe, five years a charm-seller, knew appetite down to its grammar — the door recognized a fellow merchant and let them pass.

Beyond it, the world changed scale.

They had expected a chamber, a throne room inverted, something architectural. Instead there was only green light, sourceless and warm as late afternoon in childhood, and paths of soft moss winding between hedges grown so tall they blotted everything above them. Lanterns hung in the branches — hundreds of them, thousands — and it took Xanthe three steps to understand what the lanterns were.

Memories. A century of harvested memories, each one sealed in a bud of glassy resin, each one glowing faintly with the color of the life inside it. Gold ones and rust-colored ones, one that pulsed a deep bruised violet, a cluster near the path's first bend that flickered in rhythm, two children's worth of summer days strung together like festival lights.

Xanthe stopped walking.

Because four lanterns along, hanging at exactly the height of a person reaching up, were memories they knew from the inside. Their gold was unmistakable — they'd carried that warmth in their chest every day for five years without knowing why. Their own past, sold off in a market stall by someone who loved them enough to do it, harvested after, hung here like fruit.

"Hello, old friend," Xanthe whispered to the nearest one, and their voice cracked on it. "You're heavier than I remembered."

The hedge path pulled gently onward, the way currents pull. Somewhere ahead, deeper than sound, something vast was breathing slow and vegetal and content. The king's mind. Or what the king's mind had become: a garden with a man buried under it like a seed under snow.

*Find him,* Ilsa had said, gripping Xanthe's shoulder hard enough to bruise. *Not the parasite. The parasite is everywhere; you can't stab weather. Find the man.*

*And if there isn't a man left?* Juniper had asked, practical to the last.

*Then we're all going to die in this palace,* Ilsa had said, *so I'd find him anyway.*

* *

The lanterns thinned as Xanthe went deeper. That frightened them more than anything else so far — the realization that even the hoard had geography, that some memories were kept close and some pushed far away, and what lay farthest from the root-vault's entrance was whatever the Verdant King wanted least examined.

Or whatever *he* — the real one — had hidden best.

The green light dimmed to something like dusk. The moss underfoot gave way to flagstones, old ones, cracked and heaved apart by roots but unmistakably human work. A sundial, its gnomon snapped, furred with lichen. A stone bench with a cushion gone to mold. And then, through a gap in the hedge shaped suspiciously like a keyhole—

A garden chair.

Wrought iron, white paint flaking, the kind that cost almost nothing and lasted forever and appeared on every modest terrace in every modest town in the kingdom. Sitting in it was an old man.

He was small. That was the first shock — Xanthe had braced for majesty, for the terrible beauty they'd seen holding court upstairs through the monarch's stolen body, and instead here was a thin old fellow in a cardigan the color of weak tea, slippers, hands folded over his stomach. His crown sat on the arm of the chair like a hat someone had taken off because it gave them a headache. His eyes were closed. His breathing matched the great slow breath of the garden around him, in and out, in and out, and Xanthe understood with a lurch that it wasn't the garden matching him. It never had been. Everything above — the hedgerows that walked, the orchards fruiting wrong, the villages smiling themselves hollow — all of it grew from this man's sleeping exhale.

"Your Majesty," Xanthe said.

The old man smiled without opening his eyes. "You're standing in my marigolds."

Xanthe looked down. There were no marigolds. There was bare moss, faintly glowing.

"I—" They stepped sideways, obliging. "Forgive me."

"That's all right." He settled deeper into the chair, contentment radiating off him like heat off a hearthstone. "Everyone stands in them eventually. You get used to company." He opened his eyes then, and they were pale grey, kind, and utterly untroubled, and looking into them was like looking into a pond so still you could see the sky perfectly and none of the mud beneath. "Are you one of mine?"

"One of yours?"

"The gardeners." He gestured vaguely upward, toward the canopy, toward the palace, toward everything. "They come and go. Sweet people. Very dedicated. Always telling me how well everything is growing." His brow creased, the smallest wrinkle of doubt, then smoothed again like water closing over a dropped stone. "How *is* everything growing?"

"It's not," Xanthe said carefully. "People are being hurt. Villages are being emptied. Memories are being taken."

"Mm," the old man said, comfortable, unmoved, and Xanthe felt horror rise in their throat, cold and clean, because this was worse than resistance. This was peace. This was captivity so complete it had been renamed. Somewhere in the roots of this place, the Verdant King fed, and the man in the chair dreamed his imprisonment as a retirement, and both of them were satisfied.

"You're dreaming," Xanthe tried.

"Am I?" He chuckled. "If I am, it's a good one. Warm sun. No meetings." He patted the chair beside him. "Sit. There's another seat. There's always another seat."

Xanthe didn't sit. Thief's instinct: don't accept gifts in a mark's territory. But they came closer, close enough to see the details the green dusk hid — the tremor in his folded hands, the way his cardigan sleeve had worn thin at the elbow, a faded ink stain on one knuckle. A writing hand. An ordinary hand.

"What's your name?" Xanthe asked.

He considered that for a long moment. The pond-sky eyes went somewhere else.

"Aldous," he said at last, wonderingly, like a word recovered from deep water. "Yes. Aldous. Nobody's called me that in—" He frowned. "In some time. The gardeners call me Father. The voice calls me Beloved."

"The voice," Xanthe said. "The one that tells you everything is well."

"It doesn't tell me." Aldous smiled, indulgent, correcting a child. "It *asks* me. Would I like the borders quiet? Yes. Would I like the quarrels ended? Yes. Would I like to rest? Oh, yes." He sighed, bone-deep, and the sigh moved through the hedges around them like wind. "It has given me such rest."

"And what does it ask in return?"

The question landed somewhere. Xanthe watched it land — watched the old man's comfort stutter, watched his hands tighten on each other, watched a hairline crack open across the pond-smooth surface of his peace.

"I don't remember," he whispered.

"There it is," Xanthe said softly. "There's the price."

"No." He shook his head, small at first, then harder. "No, it wouldn't — it promised me peace, it swore, it said only what I didn't need—"

"What didn't you need?"

"I don't—"

"*What didn't you need, Aldous?*"

The name broke something. The old man looked up at Xanthe, and for one instant the grey eyes cleared — really cleared, terror swimming up from the mud at the bottom of the pond — and he grabbed Xanthe's wrist with shocking strength and said, fast and desperate, all in one breath:

"It takes them when I sleep, it takes the ones that hurt, and then it takes the ones that healed the hurt, and then it takes whatever held them together, and now there's nothing left but the chair and the sun and I cannot remember my wife's face, I cannot remember her *face*, young one, I have been trying for sixty years—"

And then the crack sealed. Xanthe watched it happen — the terror draining, the smile reassembling itself feature by feature like a hedge growing over a ruined wall — and Aldous released their wrist and folded his hands again and said, mildly:

"You should go before dark. The garden gets hungry at dark."

"No," Xanthe said. Their heart was slamming. Their eyes were hot. Five years of carrying strangers' griefs had taught them to hold other people's pain at arm's length, and they could not do it now, would not. "No, I'm not leaving you in this chair. Listen to me. You are under an enchantment. Your kingdom believes you dead or worse. There are people upstairs right now risking their lives to reach this spot, and I did not crawl through a century of stolen lives to watch you nap through your own rescue."

Aldous tilted his head, considering them with mild, distant curiosity. Then:

"How do you know it's a rescue," he asked, "and not theft?"

The question stopped Xanthe cold.

"Because you're a thief, aren't you." Not accusing. Almost gentle. "I can tell. You have the hands for it. All those lights in the lanes outside — you've carried other people's things your whole life." He nodded at the memory-lanterns glimmering far off through the hedge. "So tell me truly. When you take a memory back to someone — the way you apparently do — how do you know it isn't theft? How do you know you're not just moving their life into a shape you prefer?"

Xanthe thought about the woman in the village that forgot itself. About watching her weep herself whole, one returned memory at a time, her son's name coming back to her mouth like blood returning to a numb limb.

"Consent," Xanthe said. "It's theft when it's taken. It's a gift when it's given. Same object. Different universe."

The old man was quiet for a long time.

"Yes," he said finally. "Yes, that's exactly it. That's what she—" The crack flashed open again, briefly, and sealed again. He exhaled. "You remind me of someone," he said. "Isn't that strange. Someone who used to argue with me in this garden. Before the chair." His eyes drifted half-shut. "She never once let me win."

"Your wife."

"Mm." A smile, soft and lost. "I've been told I loved her very much. Isn't that funny? Told. Like a rumor about a stranger." He turned his face up toward the light that wasn't sunlight. "The voice offers to give her back to me sometimes. In exchange for staying. It says it kept her, all of her, safe in the roots. It says I could have her whole, every evening, forever, if I just keep resting."

Xanthe's skin crawled. Upstairs, hours ago, the Verdant King had worn the monarch's body and made them the same offer in different words — everything returned, love restored, all it would cost was becoming the garden. It had only one song, this thing. It sang temptation in every key it owned.

"And you said no," Xanthe said.

"I say no every night." Aldous sounded puzzled at himself. "I don't know why. I want her more than anything. But something in me knows that a wife handed to you by the thing that ate her wouldn't be her." His grip tightened on his own hands. "Love that's administered isn't love. Something remembers that, even now. Some little cellar of me keeps the door shut."

Xanthe went very still.

Because Juniper had found the rite in the burned archive, had decoded it line by line in a prison convoy ambush and a stolen page: *the binding may be loosened only by a memory freely given — freely given, of love, to the bound.* And they had come all this way and freed Juniper and fought through blooming halls and descended into the root, and the requirement had hung over all of them like a blade, because nobody alive knew what the true king loved anymore. The parasite had seen to that. It had eaten his loves first, precisely, thoroughly, the way you'd clear a foundation before building.

But it hadn't eaten them *all*, had it. Because the old man in the chair had just refused the counterfeit six thousand times, on instinct, in his sleep, in the deepest cellar of himself — which meant the knowledge of love still lived in him somewhere. He couldn't produce the memory. But perhaps he didn't need to.

Perhaps he needed to *receive* one.

"Aldous," Xanthe said quietly. "The rite that binds you — I've read it. It can be undone. But it needs a specific key. A memory of being loved. Given freely, by someone who meant it, straight into your own hands." They crouched beside the chair so their eyes were level with his. "Nobody knows what you loved. The thing wearing your crown made sure of that. So I need you to tell me anything — a name, a smell, a song, the shape of a hand — anything at all that survived it."

The old man was silent for so long that Xanthe thought the crack had sealed for good, that the garden had reasserted itself, that they'd have to go back up and confess failure to people who'd bled for this.

Then Aldous began, slowly, to hum.

It was a small tune, wandering, half-remembered, the kind of thing a person hums while doing dishes. His foot tapped. His face had gone soft and young.

"She used to sing that," he murmured. "Off-key. On purpose, I think, to make our daughter laugh. Our—" His humming stopped. His hand rose, trembling, and pointed—not at Xanthe, past them, out through the keyhole gap in the hedge, deep into the dusk of the buried garden where the lanterns hung fewest and oldest. "There," he breathed. "Whatever she was. Whatever's left. It's there. I hid it there myself, at the very start, before I forgot I was hiding anything. The one memory the voice couldn't ask me for, because I buried it too deep to know I had it."

Xanthe turned. Far off in the failing green light, one lantern hung alone in a blackthorn tree, burning a color Xanthe had never seen in a hundred thousand stolen memories — warm rose-gold, steady, patient, like a lamp left in a window for someone coming home.

"The rite needs it given freely," Aldous said. His voice had changed — still small, but awake now, awake in the way that mattered, a man speaking from the cellar door instead of the chair. "By someone who meant it. She can't give it. She's been a lantern for a hundred years."

"Then we find her blood," Xanthe said, hope rising like fire. "Her descendants, her family, anyone—"

"She had a brother," Aldous said softly. "Who died young. Whose line everyone thinks died with him." His grey eyes fixed on Xanthe, clear and terribly tired. "It didn't."

And behind them, all through the buried garden, every hedge at once began to move.

* * *

The blackthorn was forty yards away and the paths between here and there were rearranging themselves like a fist closing. Xanthe ran — thief-fast, low, reading the hedge the way they read alleys — while behind them Aldous rose from his chair for the first time in a century, swaying, gripping the armrests, shouting in a voice that shook the leaves: *"RUN, YOUNG ONE! IT KNOWS WHAT YOU'VE FOUND!"*

Forty yards. Twenty. The rose-gold lantern swelled in Xanthe's vision, close enough now to see what was written in careful scratched letters on its resin base — a name, worn nearly smooth, and beneath it, smaller, in a different, newer hand, five words that stopped Xanthe's heart mid-stride:

*For my brother's line. Keep it.*

Xanthe's hands closed around the lantern as the hedges came down like a green avalanche—

—and from somewhere impossibly far above, muffled through a hundred feet of soil and root and throne, they heard Wren's voice calling their name.
