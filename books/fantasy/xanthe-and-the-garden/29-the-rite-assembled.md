## 29. The Rite Assembled

Juniper Sable had spent eleven years cataloguing the dead, and had learned this much: every catastrophe leaves a paper trail. Kings believe themselves immortal, and immortals do not burn their correspondence. They file it.

So while Ilsa drilled hedge-countermeasures in the courtyard above and Xanthe taught frightened volunteers how to hold a memory without dropping it, Juniper sat in the lamplit cellar of the free cities' council hall with four hundred years of confiscated archives and did what archivists do best—refused to be impressed by urgency.

The rite was real. That much she had confirmed three times over, cross-checking the prison page against fragments in the sealed ward-keepers' annals and a merchant's ledger from a century back whose margins were annotated in the same old royal cipher. *Unbinding of the Crown-Bound*, one source called it. *The Gardener's Rite* said another, which was either a scribe's joke or an early warning nobody heeded. The mechanics were consistent across all three: five components, five hands, no substitutions.

She laid them out on the table now, one by one, the way she'd once laid out evidence for tribunals that never listened.

First: the ward-craft. A living boundary, drawn and held by someone who understood wards not as walls but as sentences—words that meant what they said. She wrote *Ilsa Vane* beside it and underlined it twice. The woman had built the border wards from the palace's own plans; she knew their grammar, including the lies buried in it. Whatever bound the king was ward-work at its root. To unbind him you needed someone who spoke the language fluently enough to argue with it.

Second: legal authority as witness. This was the part the sources were maddeningly vague about, but Juniper had pieced it together from a single line in the annals—*the rite is void without an officer of the realm to see it done*. Not to perform it. To witness it. Because the binding had been done under color of law, a century ago, signed and sealed by chancellors who told themselves they were preserving stability. It could only be undone the same way: law watching its own crime get reversed. She wrote *Inquisitor Hale* and paused, then added, smaller: *if his conversion holds.* Hale had unlocked cells and charged the crown with treason before assembled officers, and half the army's loyalty had cracked along the fault line he'd opened—but conversions made under pressure could unmake just as fast. Still. He was the only lawful officer left standing on their side of the wall, and the law, whatever else you said about it, kept excellent records.

Third: scholarship. Herself. Someone had to read the cipher aloud correctly, in the right order, in the original tongue—and the rite's text explicitly warned that a mistranslated word would bind rather than loose. *A century of harvested memory hangs on your diction,* she wrote wryly in her own margin, and felt no humor at all when she read it back.

Fourth: bloodline. Wren.

Juniper set down her pen for that one. Wren's tie to the old court wasn't rumor; it was in the genealogical rolls she'd smuggled out of the capital's burned annex years ago—a cadet line, thought extinguished in the purges, surviving under a name nobody thought to check because nobody checks the names of servants and dockworkers. The rite required a hand that carried the crown's own inheritance, someone the binding would recognize as kin to the man in the garden chair. Wren had argued about it exactly once, quietly, and then stopped arguing, which in Juniper's experience meant the person had already decided and was simply waiting for everyone else to catch up.

Four components. Four hands. She looked at the fifth line on her page and found, as she had found every time before, that her pen stalled there like a cart in mud.

*A memory, freely given, of being loved by one whom the king loved.*

That was the requirement. And it was impossible, and she knew precisely why, and knowing why made it worse.

The king had been captive for a hundred years. Everyone who had loved him was dead—the queen whose portrait hung cracked in the annex, the daughter whose name survived only as a footnote, the friends and fools and gardeners whose lives the Verdant King had eaten along with everything else. Memory could be stolen, sold, archived. But it could not be conjured from paper. Juniper had spent her whole life believing that the truth, once written down, was indestructible—and here was the one truth that could not be written down after the fact, only lived. You could not forge being loved. The cipher was explicit: *given freely, known truly, or the rite turns in the giver's hand.*

Turns in the giver's hand. She'd translated that phrase four times hoping she'd gotten it wrong. She hadn't. A false offering wouldn't merely fail. It would feed the parasite.

She heard footsteps on the cellar stairs—Ilsa's uneven tread, the left knee that had never forgiven her for thirty years of ward-patrols—and closed her ledger out of habit, though there was no longer anyone to confiscate it.

Ilsa came down carrying two cups of something hot and terrible and set one beside Juniper's elbow without asking. "You've been down here since before dawn."

"I've been down here since the concept of dawn became aspirational." Juniper slid the page across. "Read the fifth component."

Ilsa read. Her jaw did the thing it did when she was keeping her voice level. "Someone who loved him."

"Someone he loved, who loved him back, freely, truly. A hundred years dead, Ilsa. Every candidate is ash. We have witnesses, we have ward-craft, we have blood and scholarship, and the keystone of the entire rite is a feeling, and feelings don't survive in ledgers." Juniper pressed her palms flat on the table. "I have spent my life insisting nothing true is ever lost if someone writes it down. I am looking at the exception. I hate it personally."

"Then we find another way in. There's always another way in."

"There isn't. I've checked the ways in. All nine of them. The parasite grew through the cracks of the original binding; the binding can only be unwound from inside, by the king himself waking, and he only wakes—" she tapped the fifth line, "—here. It has to be given to *him*. Not performed near him. Given."

Ilsa sat down slowly, which she only did when her knee had won an argument. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere overhead the courtyard drill went on—Xanthe's voice, patient and low, teaching a farm boy how to receive a stranger's grief without flinching.

"You know," Ilsa said at last, "when I was young, ward-keepers used to say the oldest wards weren't built on power. They were built on promise. Two people swearing something to each other, and the ward holding the shape of the oath." She turned her cup. "I always thought that was superstition dressed up as tradition. Now I'm wondering if it's the other way around. Maybe all magic is memory wearing armor. Maybe love is the only thing heavy enough to anchor a rite like this because it's the only thing people actually refuse to sell."

"People sell it constantly."

"They sell the *idea* of it. Cheaply." Ilsa looked up. "But the real thing—you saw the village that forgot itself. You saw what that woman paid to be whole again. Nobody sells the real thing. That's why the parasite farms it instead. It can't make more. It can only take."

Juniper stared at the fifth line until the letters blurred. "Then we're short one miracle, and the army arrives in—"

"Two days. Hale bought us thirty hours with the treason charge, maybe forty before the loyalists regroup." Ilsa stood, wincing. "Get your components ready. All five lines filled by tomorrow night, or we go anyway and fail properly instead of failing at home."

"That's not comfort, Ilsa."

"It's not meant to be. It's scheduling."

* * *

Wren found her at dusk, which surprised Juniper—she'd expected Xanthe first, since Xanthe had appointed themselves unofficial auditor of the rite and came by twice daily to ask questions Juniper mostly answered honestly. But it was Wren who climbed down the cellar stairs, moving carefully, carrying a wrapped bundle and an expression Juniper recognized from the archive: the look of someone about to donate something irreplaceable and already grieving it.

"You wanted bloodline documentation," Wren said. "Proof of the tie, for the rite."

"Yes. Descent, ideally written, though the cipher accepts spoken attestation under oath if the lineage is contested."

"It's not contested." Wren set the bundle down and unwrapped it: a ring, old gold, worn thin at the band, and a scrap of embroidery gone brown with age. "My grandmother's. Her mother served in the palace gardens—not the parasite's garden. Before. The real ones. My family kept the tokens and the names and never told anyone, because telling anyone got the last branch killed." Wren recited the descent without stumbling, six generations back to a gardener who had married into the court's lowest rank, and Juniper wrote it all down with steady hands and a rising sense of something she couldn't name yet, some discrepancy forming at the edge of the page like weather.

"Good," she said. "This satisfies the fourth component completely."

"Good." Wren didn't move to leave. Stood there with the lamplight catching the hollows under their eyes, and asked, lightly, far too lightly: "And the fifth? Have you found it yet?"

Juniper should have lied. She was good at lying; she'd kept an archive alive under an inquisition for a decade. But exhaustion makes honest people of us all, and besides, she believed in Wren—believed in the whole ragged lot of them—and so she said, "No. It's the one piece we don't have. Everyone who loved the king is dead. We need a living memory of loving him, freely given, and there is no such thing left in the world. Unless," and she laughed, short and bitter, "someone out there has been hoarding devotion in a jar for a hundred years."

Wren was quiet for a moment too long.

"No," they said finally. "Nothing like that. I'll keep asking around. Someone might know something."

They gathered the bundle, thanked her, climbed the stairs. And Juniper sat alone with her ledger and the discrepancy that had finally taken shape, and hated herself for seeing it, because seeing it changed nothing unless she acted, and acting might destroy everything.

Here was what she had noticed: Wren had asked about the fifth component the way a person asks about a wound they're already covering. Had stood in front of the only living archive of the old court—the last person alive who might conceivably carry a genuine, living memory touching that vanished world—and said *nothing like that* in a voice with a hairline crack running straight through it, the same hairline crack Ilsa had once found singing in the border wards.

Juniper looked down at her notes. At the genealogy she'd copied from the burned annex, six generations, ending in a name she had verified twice.

At the fifth line, still empty.

An archivist's job was to notice. What she did next was her own decision, hers alone, and she picked up her pen and wrote, in cipher, where no casual eye would read it:

*If Wren knows something and won't say it, I will not force it. Consent is the whole point of the rite. But I will watch. And if the missing piece walks into the rite on its own legs tomorrow night, wearing a face I recognize, then I will understand what was planned, and I will let it happen, and I will hate myself efficiently later, on schedule.*

She blew out the lamp.

Above her, faintly, through the floorboards, she could hear Xanthe still teaching—*hold it gently, it's not yours, you're only carrying it home*—and Juniper Sable, who had spent eleven years believing the truth was safest on paper, sat in the dark and prayed for the first time since childhood that for once, just once, the most important document in the world would stay safely unwritten.
