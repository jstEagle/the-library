## 30. Wren's Offer

Wren had learned, over five years of resistance work, that the worst nights were the quiet ones. Battles had a rhythm to them—fear, noise, then either grief or relief, and either way it moved. It was the still hours that got inside you. The ones where there was nothing to do but sit with what you knew.

Tonight they sat alone in the waystation's back room with Juniper's stolen page spread flat on the table, weighted at the corners with four brass buttons because Wren didn't trust themselves not to touch it.

The rite was written in old royal cipher, but Juniper had annotated it in her cramped, slanted hand, translating as she went, and Wren had read the annotations so many times they could recite them in the dark. *The binding requires a century of memory to sever. The severing requires a freely given proof of love. The proof must be given whole. What is given is spent.*

*What is given is spent.*

Four words. They'd underlined them until the ink had gone soft and gray.

They understood now—Ilsa had explained it twice, patiently, while Xanthe was out on watch—what *spent* meant. Not borrowed. Not lent. The rite didn't take a copy. Memory wasn't like coin, no matter how much Vell's charm-sellers pretended otherwise. When you gave a memory to the unbinding, you gave it the way you'd give blood to a dying man. It left you. It did its work in someone else. And you woke up lighter by exactly that much, and nothing in the world could weigh it back into you.

And there was exactly one living memory in all the world that would serve. One proof that the king had once been loved—not the parasite wearing his crown, not the garden wearing his kingdom, but the man underneath, the old dreamer in his chair. The rite needed to show him what devotion looked like from the inside. It needed a love so specific, so stubbornly particular, that no parasite could counterfeit it.

Juniper's scholarship said the king's own memories might have served, once—but a century of the Verdant King's feeding had hollowed him past even that. He couldn't remember being loved. That was the horror of it. That was why he slept so peacefully in his garden chair. You could keep a man captive forever if you took everything that would have made captivity unbearable.

So the rite needed a memory from outside. A living one. Freely given.

Wren looked at their hands on the table, turned them over, looked at the palms as if the answer were written there in lines they'd never learned to read.

There was one memory in the world of loving Xanthe Zhou from the inside. One. Because Xanthe had sold every other one—theirs and Wren's both, the whole shared country of those five years—to buy Wren's life out of the purge. Xanthe carried fragments of strangers' weddings and soldiers' sunsets, but of their own love, nothing. Not a thread.

Wren had it all. Every hour of it. The first meeting in the rain behind the tannery, when Xanthe had picked their pocket and then given everything back plus a flower, embarrassed, caught. The house by the sea with the blue door that stuck. The morning Wren had woken to find Xanthe gone and a ledger entry where a person used to be, and the years after, learning to hate them for it before learning the truth.

All of it lived in Wren and nowhere else.

And the rite would eat it.

Wren laughed—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it—and pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. Outside, someone was laughing too, really laughing, one of Ilsa's hedge-trainees who'd just survived their first countermeasure drill. The sound came through the wall like it belonged to another species.

Because here was the arithmetic, laid out plain as a merchant's scale:

Give the memory, wake the king, end the Verdant King, save the free cities—and forget Xanthe. Forget the tannery rain. Forget the blue door. Wake up tomorrow looking at the person you had crossed a kingdom for and feel nothing but polite curiosity. Become, in the space of one rite, exactly what Xanthe already was: a stranger holding a shape where a love used to be.

Or refuse. Keep it. Live. And let the king sleep, let the garden spread, let Hale's charge split an army that would be overrun within the month anyway. Let some other proof of love be found, if one existed, which Juniper's notes said it didn't, not anymore, not anywhere else on the earth.

There was no third option. Wren had looked for one for three days. They'd drunk through two pots of tea and interrogated Juniper until the archivist had thrown up her hands and said, *unless you want to try giving it secondhand through a charm, and I'd sooner pour soup into a wound,* and then she'd softened and said the thing nobody wanted said: *I'm sorry, Wren. It has to be whole, and it has to be yours.*

It has to be yours.

That was the part that kept catching, like a nail in cloth. Not *it has to be a loving memory.* Not *a strong one, a deep one.* It had to be Wren's specifically, because the king needed to see love as it actually happened—one particular person, one particular thief with quick fingers and tired eyes—not love in the abstract. Juniper had found precedent: the original binding a century ago had been anchored by a queen's memory of her husband, freely surrendered, and the parasite had fed on that anchor ever since, twisting it. To break an anchor made of love, you needed an anchor made of love. Like called to like. The symmetry was elegant. Juniper had called it elegant. Scholars, Wren decided, should not be allowed to name things.

They thought about telling Xanthe.

They'd gotten as far as the door twice. Both times they'd stood there with their hand on the latch and imagined it: walking out into the yard, finding Xanthe at the fire or on watch, saying *I have to tell you something about tomorrow.* Imagining the look on that face—the face Wren knew better than any map they'd ever memorized, better than the palace corridors Ilsa was sketching for the infiltration. Xanthe would understand immediately. That was the problem. Xanthe understood prices; they'd been paying them their whole life. They would do the same arithmetic in three seconds and arrive at the same answer, and then they would refuse to allow it, and they would mean to stop it, and they would spend their last night before the root-vault trying to find a way around it instead of resting, instead of eating, instead of—

Instead of anything. Instead of the last night they'd ever share.

Because that was the truth underneath all of it, the truth Wren kept circling and refusing to land on: this was the last night. Tomorrow Xanthe went down into the root-vault, and whether the rite worked or failed, the person who walked out of it—if anyone walked out of it—would not be walking out with Wren. If the rite failed, everyone died, and the question was moot. If it succeeded, Wren would stand in the ruins of the garden having saved the world and lost the only thing in it they'd ever wanted to keep.

Unless—

No. Wren shut that door hard. There was a version of this where they told Xanthe and Xanthe found some thief's workaround, some charm-trick to duplicate what couldn't be duplicated, and it half-worked, and the rite got a diluted memory, and the king half-woke, and the parasite kept enough of its anchor to survive in some seed, some cutting, waiting. Half-measures against the Verdant King weren't mercy. They were agriculture. You'd just be planting it again somewhere new, in softer soil.

It had to be whole. It had to be theirs.

Wren got up and paced the small room, four steps and turn, four steps and turn, the way they'd paced prison cells and safehouses and one memorable week on a river barge. The floorboards creaked their familiar complaint. Somewhere below, Juniper was still awake too—they could hear the scratch of her pen through the floor, cataloguing, always cataloguing, building the Archive of Given Things on paper before it existed anywhere else. Given things. As if she'd known.

What do you do with your last day of loving someone?

You don't waste it telling them goodbye. That was the answer Wren kept arriving at. You don't make them carry it. Xanthe had spent five years carrying the cost of Wren's life without knowing what they'd paid. Wren could carry this one night the same way. It was almost fair. Almost symmetrical. Juniper would probably have called it elegant.

So Wren dried their face, and straightened the page on the table, and put the brass buttons back in their tin, and went downstairs to spend the evening like it was any evening.

Xanthe was in the kitchen, teaching two of Ilsa's trainees to wrap a memory-charm without bruising it. Their hands moved with that economy Wren had fallen for before they'd fallen for anything else—quick, precise, gentle in a way Xanthe would deny under torture. The trainees were hopeless. Xanthe was patient anyway.

"Your knots are sentimental," Xanthe was saying. "A charm isn't a gift box. Wrap it tight or don't wrap it."

"It feels wrong," one of the trainees said. "Squeezing something that matters."

"Everything that matters gets squeezed eventually," Xanthe said. "Better it happens on purpose."

Wren stood in the doorway and let the words go through them like cold water. Then they smiled, and Xanthe saw them, and that happened—that thing that always happened, the small shift in Xanthe's whole posture, shoulders dropping half an inch, like coming indoors. Five years of absence and it still worked instantly. It had worked from across the waystation yard this afternoon, from thirty feet, and Wren had thought: *one more day. I get one more day of being seen like that.*

"Wren." Xanthe's voice did the thing it did. "Come sit. These two are murdering my charms."

"We're improving," the trainee said.

"You're embalming them."

Wren sat. They took the clumsy half-wrapped charm the trainee pushed at them and unwound it and started over, slow, letting Xanthe correct their grip, their tension, their angle. It was absurd. It was a lesson neither of them needed, performed for an audience of two nervous volunteers, and it was the best hour of Wren's life, and they paid attention to every second of it the way you drink water in a desert—deliberately, knowing the supply.

Xanthe's knee rested against theirs under the table. Neither of them acknowledged it. Both of them leaned in slightly.

Later there was stew, and Ilsa arguing ward-theory with Juniper across the table while Hale, newly arrived from his mutiny-by-paperwork at the front, ate silently and watched the room like a man taking inventory of everything he was about to risk. Later still, the fire burned low and people drifted to bed, and Xanthe lingered, and Wren knew—they knew the way you know weather coming—that Xanthe was working up to say something. Some speech about tomorrow. Some thief's blessing, some promise about what they'd do after, some list of things they'd show each other when the stars stayed lit and the roads were roads again.

"Don't," Wren said gently.

Xanthe blinked. "Don't what?"

"Whatever speech you're loading. Save it. Tell me after."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face—hurt, quickly filed away, then something else, something that looked almost like relief. Maybe they'd been dreading the speech too. Maybe they were afraid of promising things the garden could hear. "After," Xanthe agreed.

"After," Wren said, and held out their hand, and Xanthe took it, and they sat together in the ember-light without talking, which had always been the truest conversation they had.

Wren memorized it. Deliberately, greedily, the way they'd once watched Xanthe memorize a lock. The weight of the hand in theirs. The smell of woodsmoke and the green smell that clung to Xanthe now, faintly, since the palace. The exact temperature of the silence. The scar on Xanthe's knuckle from a lockpick slipped ten years ago, which Wren had kissed once, in a house with a blue door, and Xanthe had laughed and said *you're going to make me vain.*

*I'm going to make you vain.* Wren held onto that. They built a little room in their mind, Juniper-style, and shelved everything in it carefully: the rain behind the tannery. The blue door. The laugh. The knee against theirs, right now, warm through rough cloth. If you can't keep it, know it perfectly. Know it so well that whatever wakes up tomorrow without it will still, in some way it can't name, lean toward the place where it used to live.

Near midnight, Xanthe's head tipped sideways onto Wren's shoulder. Asleep sitting up, the way thieves slept, ready to run.

Wren sat very still and did not cry. They breathed. They counted Xanthe's breaths, sixty of them, a full minute of borrowed time. Then, moving with a thief's own patience learned by osmosis over five missing years, they eased out from under the sleeping weight, settled Xanthe against the wall with a folded blanket, and stood looking down at them for a long moment.

"After," Wren whispered. It was a lie. It was also, in a way they couldn't explain and didn't try to, not entirely one.

They climbed the stairs to the back room where Juniper's page waited under its brass buttons. Ilsa was there, checking the infiltration route one last time, and she looked up and read Wren's face in a single pass, because Ilsa read faces the way Juniper read cipher.

"You've decided," Ilsa said.

"I've decided."

"You're not going to tell them."

"No."

Ilsa set down her charcoal. For a moment she looked older than her years, older than the wards she'd built, older than the border. "There's a version of me that orders you to tell them," she said. "Consent cuts both ways. That's the whole point of everything we're fighting for."

"I know."

"And there's a version of me that understands why you won't." She rubbed her eyes. "Tell me which one wins."

"The second one," Wren said. "Barely. Ask me again tomorrow and it might be the first."

"And if they hate you, after? If they find out?"

Wren thought about it honestly. "They'll never know I chose it. That's the design. Tomorrow will look like duty to everyone—to you, to Juniper, to the king. Stepping forward because someone had to." They paused. "Let it look like that. Please."

Ilsa was quiet a long time. Then she nodded once, the way she nodded at a ward holding under strain. "Then rest. Tomorrow we go to the root."

Wren lay down on the cot and did not rest. They lay in the dark doing the work they'd assigned themselves, walking the little room in their mind, touching each shelf: the rain, the door, the laugh, the hand. Committing it deeper. Saying goodnight to it item by item, the way you'd walk a house you were leaving, putting your palm flat against each wall.

Outside, far off toward the capital, the sky over the enemy's country glowed a faint, wrong green—the garden, growing in the dark, feeding, waiting. Tomorrow they would go down under the throne into the lantern-hung dark where a hundred years of stolen lives hung in rows, and Wren would open their hands and give away the truest thing that had ever been theirs, and the world would be saved, and they would look at Xanthe across the ashes of the Verdant King and feel the polite curiosity of a stranger.

But tonight—tonight the memory was still alive in them. Tonight the blue door still stuck. Tonight the rain still fell behind the tannery, and a pickpocket with a guilty face was offering back a stolen flower, and Wren was young and the whole road was ahead.

Tonight, loving Xanthe was still the easiest thing in the world.

Wren closed their eyes and kept it, all of it, one last hour—every shelf, every room—while beyond the window the green light grew, and the clock ran down, and dawn came on steady and merciless toward the root-vault, carrying all of them with it, and none of them, least of all Xanthe, knew what Wren was about to spend.
