## 32. Into the Root

The passage beneath the throne was not built. That was the first thing Xanthe understood about it, standing at its mouth while Ilsa's lamp threw their shadows long across the walls.

Built things have seams. Mortar lines, chisel marks, the honest geometry of human intention. These walls had none. They were root—vast, pale roots grown together into a corridor, polished smooth as river stone, and faintly warm under the palm when Xanthe touched them without meaning to.

"Don't," Ilsa said quietly.

Xanthe pulled their hand back. "I didn't—"

"You were going to. Everyone does. It's warm, it's smooth, it wants to be touched." Ilsa's face was a mask of ward-keeper discipline, but her voice had a frayed edge Xanthe hadn't heard since the border. "It's how it feeds the small things first. Rats come down here and don't come out. Then cats following rats. Then—" she stopped herself.

"Then thieves following cats," Xanthe said.

"Then thieves."

Behind them, Hale stood with his back to the corridor they'd come by, sword drawn, watching the dark for pursuit. Juniper was three days free of the convoy and still moved like someone expecting chains to close around her wrists at any moment; she held the rite-pages against her chest the way a mother holds a child in a crowd. Wren—

Wren was looking at Xanthe. Not saying anything. Just looking, the way they had looked every day since the waystation, patient as tide, refusing to be a stranger no matter how many times Xanthe's empty hands came up between them.

"We should go," Wren said softly. "Before it notices us."

"It already knows we're here," Juniper said. "It's known since the gate."

* * *

The descent was longer than the palace above could possibly contain.

Xanthe counted steps out of habit—a thief's discipline, mapping space in the body—and lost count somewhere past six hundred, past the point where any cellar or crypt or foundation stone should have given way to open earth. The roots narrowed, widened, spiraled. The air grew thick and green-smelling, like a hothouse, like cut stems, like the inside of something alive. And always, always, the warmth. A breath of heat rising from below, slow and rhythmic, so that after a while Xanthe realized with a lurch that they were hearing it too: the whole passage breathing. In. Out. Patient.

"Talk," Ilsa said. "All of you. Keep talking. Silence is where it gets in."

So they talked. Juniper recited archive catalog numbers from memory, a century of banned histories in dry inventory order. Hale muttered legal citations like prayers. Wren sang—low, half-under their breath, an old Vell harbor song about a girl who waited on the quay—and Xanthe felt the song land somewhere in their chest before their mind could object, felt their lips shaping words they didn't know they knew.

They didn't say anything about that. There wasn't time, and there wasn't language yet, and some part of Xanthe had begun to understand that everything they needed to feel about Wren was going to arrive all at once or not at all, and this was neither the place nor the hour.

The stairs ended at a door of woven thorn, and the thorn parted for them like water.

* * *

The root-vault opened beneath the throne the way a heart opens inside a ribcage: vast, hidden, essential.

Xanthe stepped through the thorn and forgot how to breathe.

Lanterns. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

They hung from the vaulted dark on filaments of root as fine as spider silk, descending in tiers and terraces until the eye gave up trying to find the floor—or the bottom, or the end of it. Each one glowed with its own light: gold, blue, rose, the deep amber of lamplight through wine. They swayed slightly in the vault's slow breath, and where their light overlapped, colors married and made new ones, and the whole cavern turned slowly, gently, like the inside of a kaleidoscope, like the night sky if the night sky had been farmed.

"They're memories," Xanthe said. Their voice came out wrong. Small. "Juniper. Tell me I'm wrong."

Juniper had gone gray. She walked forward until one filament hung level with her face and studied what swung at its end, and whatever she saw made her step back as if struck.

"Each light is a life," she whispered. "Or the best parts of one. Harvested. Curated." She swallowed. "A century of them. Maybe more. This is the treasury. This is what the crown has been *collecting*."

"The wards," Ilsa said. Her knuckles had gone white on her lamp. "The border wards were never keeping anything out. They were a fence. Keeping the harvest *in*." She laughed once, and it was the worst sound Xanthe had ever heard her make. "I built a fence. For twenty years I built a fence."

"Ilsa—"

"I built the *fence*, Xanthe."

Hale moved to stand beside her. He didn't touch her—he was not a man who touched—but he put himself at her shoulder like a wall, and said nothing, which from Hale was its own kind of oath.

Wren drifted forward into the tiers of light, and Xanthe followed because Wren was moving and Xanthe's body had apparently decided, without consulting them, that where Wren went it would go.

Up close, the lanterns resolved.

They were not lamps. They were memories wearing light the way a flame wears a wick—and Xanthe knew that shape, knew it in their marrow, because they had spent ten years pulling memories loose from minds and spinning them into charms. They knew the weight of a wedding day, the color of a soldier's last sunset, the specific gold of a widow's remembered morning. Every charm they had ever sold had been a lantern like these, only small. Only borrowed.

These had been taken.

And some of them—

Some of them Xanthe recognized.

It started as a prickle, the way a stolen thing recognizes its sibling. Then a lantern swinging near Wren's shoulder flared as Xanthe passed, leaning toward them like a flower toward sun, and Xanthe looked into it and saw—

A kitchen. Rain on the window. Someone humming the harbor song while two cups steeped on the sill, and the person humming had Xanthe's own hands, Xanthe's crooked left little finger, and the laugh that answered from off-frame was low and warm and *Wren's*—

"No," Xanthe said.

Another lantern, further in. A beach. Gray water, gulls, a house with salt-eaten shutters, and Xanthe's own feet bare in the sand, and Wren's hand in theirs, easy as a ring worn for years—

"No, no, those are *mine*, those are—" They grabbed for it and Ilsa caught their wrist, hard.

"Don't touch. Don't take it back here, not here, do you hear me? Whatever you pull loose in this place belongs to it the moment it leaves the filament. You'd be handing it your name."

Xanthe stood shaking in the green-breathing dark, surrounded by ten thousand stolen lives, four of which—their own—hung among them like fruit.

"That's what it did," Wren said quietly. "Five years ago. When you sold everything. It didn't buy your memories to keep them locked away. It kept the ones it wanted and hung them here. In its collection." Wren's voice cracked and steadied. "It's been wearing our love like a piece of jewelry."

* * *

They found the center by following the brightest light.

It took hours. Or minutes. Time ran strangely under the vault's slow breath, and Xanthe suspected that was deliberate—the garden had no need of hurry, had never had need of hurry, had been growing for a hundred years at the pace of trees. But eventually the tiers of lanterns thickened toward a core, and at the core, suspended on a single root as thick as a cathedral pillar, hung the eldest light.

It was white. Not the white of blankness—the white of *everything*, every color at once, so bright the others seemed dim around it, and when Xanthe came within ten paces they felt it the way you feel a great fire across a room: heat without touching, presence without sound.

Inside it, moving like figures behind frosted glass, was a man. Old. Sitting in a garden chair that existed nowhere but inside the light. His eyes were closed. He was smiling.

"The king," Juniper breathed. "He's alive. He's *alive*, he's been alive in there the whole—"

"He isn't imprisoned," Xanthe said slowly. Thief-sight, the craft of seeing what people hide, was working on the lantern whether Xanthe wanted it or not. "Look at him. He thinks this is peace. He thinks he's *retired*. It let him keep a dream of his garden and he's been sitting in it for fifty years while it wears his face upstairs."

"So we wake him," Hale said. "Simple. We break the lantern."

"And pour a century of harvested lives back into a man who can't hold them?" Ilsa shook her head. "You'd shatter him like a dropped cup. No. The rite. Juniper's rite—it unbinds gently. One thread at a time. It needs—"

"A memory freely given by someone the king loved," Juniper recited. "Yes. And we don't know what he loved. His whole life is *here*—" she gestured at the burning tiers, the ten thousand stolen lights, "—and none of it is ours to give."

The white lantern pulsed.

Once. Slowly.

And in the silence after it, from everywhere at once—from the roots, from the walls, from the warm breathing dark—from a voice like honey poured over broken glass, gentle as a gardener kneeling at a bed:

"*You've come a long way to see my collection.*"

The lanterns all leaned toward them at once, ten thousand lights bending like grass in wind.

"*Let me show you what you're worth.*"

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of root, the throne room doors boomed open, and the Verdant King began, patiently, to come downstairs.
