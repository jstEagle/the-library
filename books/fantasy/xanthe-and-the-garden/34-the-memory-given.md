## 34. The Memory Given

Wren had always known this moment would come. They had simply imagined it differently — imagined themselves brave in a clean way, imagined the giving would feel like setting down a heavy pack after a long road. Instead, standing at the edge of the root-vault with the rite half-assembled around them, Wren felt like someone standing at the shore of a country they were about to be deported from.

Below, the lanterns burned.

A century of harvested memories hung in the dark beneath the throne room, thousands of them, each one a soft gold-green light in a cage of root and glass. Xanthe's whole stock was down there somewhere. The king's stolen century was down there. And at the center of it all, coiled around the deepest root, the Verdant King waited — patient as ivy, hungry as winter.

Ilsa was drawing the last ward-line in chalk and salt and something older than either. Juniper stood at the vault's edge with the rite recited from memory, eyes closed, lips moving. Hale had gone back up to hold the corridor, because that was what Hale did now — he had discovered, late and at great cost, that he was a man who held corridors.

And Xanthe was at the vault's mouth, sleeves rolled, thief's hands already working, looking down into the dark like it owed them money.

Wren watched them and did the arithmetic one more time. It always came out the same.

The rite needed a memory freely given by someone the king loved. That was the flaw in Juniper's stolen page, the impossible requirement, the hinge on which everything turned. No one alive knew what the real king had loved — the parasite had eaten it, root and branch, a hundred years ago. The king himself, dreaming in his buried garden, could not remember.

But the rite did not actually need the king's love. Juniper had found it in a marginal note, in a hand two scholars dead: *the binding feeds on taken things; it can only be unbound by a given thing of the same weight.* The same weight. A century of theft. What balanced that scale was not a king's love for a garden or a crown.

It was a memory of loving someone so completely that giving it away would hollow the giver to the same depth the parasite had hollowed a kingdom.

Wren was the only person alive who carried one.

They had known it since the cistern, since Juniper first read the requirement aloud and everyone had gone quiet and looked at the floor. They had known it, and they had said nothing, and they had spent every night since lying awake next to a fire, listening to Xanthe breathe, memorizing the sound. Because that was the cruelty of it: Wren would remember giving the memory right up until the moment they gave it. After that, they would be a stranger at a stranger's fire, and they would not even know what they had lost.

You don't get to keep the receipt for the thing you give away. That was the whole point of *given*.

"Wren." Ilsa's voice, low. She had finished the ward-line and was looking at them with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing. "You don't have to—"

"I do, though." Wren kept their voice light. It was a skill; they'd had five years of practice, smiling at Xanthe across market stalls, across campfires, across the aching ordinary distance between a person and the person they loved who did not love them back, could not, the ledger of their heart having been sold to buy a life. "It's the only coin that fits the slot. You know it. Juniper knows it."

Juniper opened their eyes. "There may be another way. There's always another way, in the texts, there's always—"

"There isn't." Wren said it gently. "You've read everything. You know there isn't."

The silence that followed had weight, the way the silence in the vault had weight, a hundred years of it pressing down.

Xanthe turned from the vault's mouth. They looked tired in a way Wren had never seen before the stars went out — tired down to the bone, tired in the place where a person keeps the things they can't afford to look at. Five years ago Xanthe had sold every memory of Wren to buy their escape from the purge, and Wren had spent those five years carrying all of it alone: the house by the sea, the winter they'd nearly frozen laughing, the way Xanthe hummed when they counted coin, the way they said *stay* like a question they were afraid to ask.

Wren had tried, once, early on — had found Xanthe in Vell and told them the truth. Xanthe had looked at them with polite, merchant's blankness, the look you give a stranger selling something you can't afford, and Wren had understood that the truth was a door with no handle on that side. So they had moved to the resistance waystation and waited for Xanthe to come down the road, because Wren had always been better at waiting than at leaving.

"I'll do it," Wren said. "But I want to do it now, before I can talk myself into a clever alternative. Cleverness is how the Verdant King got everything it has. Somebody was clever about someone else's memories a hundred years ago and here we are."

Juniper laughed — a cracked, wet sound, grief wearing humor's coat. "That is the first accurate history of the century I've heard."

* * *

The rite was simple, which was the worst thing about it. Juniper had explained it on the road: the great bindings were always simple, because complexity was a human vanity and the old magics had no vanity. A circle of ward-craft to hold the parasite still. A scholar's voice to speak the terms. A freely given memory, placed in the giver's own hands, offered — not thrown, not spent, *offered* — to the sleeping king.

And then the unbinding would pull. It would pull everything the parasite had taken, a century of it, out through the wound the given memory made. It would pull Xanthe's stock, the lanterns in the vault. And it would pull, Juniper suspected, the price back along the same channel: whatever the giver gave, they gave wholly, and the unbinding would not return it. The memory would live in the king. Wren would go on without it.

"You could tell them," Ilsa said quietly, as they made their final preparations. She was checking the ward-line a third time, though Wren knew it was perfect. Ward-keepers checked perfect things when their hands needed work. "Xanthe. You could tell them what you're about to give."

"And they'd what? Refuse to let me?" Wren smiled. "Xanthe would stand in front of the rite like a door. They'd find some other way, some thief's way, some clever way, and the clever ways are how we got here. Besides." They looked down at their own hands. "If I tell them, they'll spend the last hour I have as myself trying to save me from myself. I'd rather have the hour."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Then: "My order taught me that a ward is a promise made in stone. That the keeper's whole life is holding the promise when it would be easier to walk away." She met Wren's eyes. "I broke mine once. I'm not going to tell you yours is wrong. But I'll witness it. Someone should witness it. That matters, in the old law. A given thing should be witnessed."

"Thank you," Wren said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much the meaning hurt.

* * *

The descent into the root-vault took eleven steps. Wren counted them, because counting was a thing you could carry with you even out of yourself, maybe. Eleven steps down into a century of stolen light.

The lanterns hung around them like fruit. Some of them whispered. Wren had learned on the road not to listen too closely — the memories that weren't yours had a way of making room for themselves inside you, a way of saying *you could be me, you could have been me, you were almost me.* Xanthe had it worse; Xanthe's own memories hung down here, five years of them, and Wren had watched Xanthe flinch each time one whispered their name.

At the center of the vault, the root. It came down through the throne room floor like a dark vein, and where it met the earth it had grown a chair — of course it had, the parasite had a taste for chairs, for thrones, for the furniture of power — and in the chair the old king sat sleeping.

He was smaller than his statues. That was the first thing. A narrow old man in a rotted crown, hands folded, face slack with a peace so complete it looked like drowning. Green light moved behind his closed eyelids. Around his wrists, delicate as jewelry, the roots had grown rings.

"He's in there," Xanthe said. They had come down too, against every argument, because no one could stop Xanthe from anything, a fact the whole world was about to be reminded of. Their voice was strange — flat, careful, a merchant's voice over a wound. "I went in last night. While you slept. I had to see."

Wren went cold. "Xanthe—"

"He's dreaming about a garden." Xanthe didn't look at them. "A real one. Small. Tomatoes. He's arguing with a gardener about roses, and he's losing, and he's *happy*. The parasite let him keep that much because it costs nothing. It wears him like a coat and lets him dream about tomatoes." Their jaw worked. "I've seen a lot of theft, Wren. I've committed most of it. This is the first time I've wanted to burn the whole profession down."

"Then let's do it," Wren said.

Xanthe finally looked at them. And Wren saw — the way you see a thing only when it's nearly too late to unsee it — that Xanthe knew. Not the details. But Xanthe had spent five years reading strangers' hearts for a living, taking them apart and selling the pieces, and they had spent the last weeks relearning Wren's face, and something in them had done the arithmetic too.

"Wren," Xanthe said slowly. "What are you about to do?"

"Stand in a circle," Wren said. "It's all I'm good for. You're the one who breaks into places. I'm the one who holds them."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." And Wren smiled, and watched the smile land wrong, watched Xanthe's eyes narrow, watched the thief in them reach for the shape of the thing behind the words — and then Ilsa called from the vault's mouth that the circle was ready, and the moment folded itself away like a letter into a pocket, to be read later by no one.

* * *

They took their places. Ilsa at the north of the ward-line, hands flat on the chalk, holding the parasite's attention the way you hold a wolf's — with perfect stillness and total commitment. Juniper at the east, the rite open in their memory, the words worn smooth from a week of silent rehearsal. Hale, who had come down for this and would go back up after, stood at the south with his inquisitor's seal in his hand, because a given thing should be witnessed, and who better than the law itself, repented. Xanthe stood at the west, and Wren did not look at them, because if they looked they would stop.

The Verdant King felt the circle close and woke the way ivy wakes — all along its length at once, a shiver through the roots, the lantern-light bending toward it like grass toward wind. The old king's body did not move. But the voice came, from everywhere the roots were, which was everywhere.

*Little thieves,* it said, almost fondly. *Little witnesses. You have brought me a gift?*

"You've taken enough gifts," Juniper said, and began the rite.

The words were old and plain. Wren understood maybe one in three, and understood the shape of all of them: *this thing was bound by taking; let it be loosed by giving; let the scale be balanced; let what was stolen go home.* As Juniper spoke, the lanterns began to tremble in their cages, and the whispering rose, a hundred years of stolen lives all murmuring at once, and the green light in the vault deepened, and deepened, and the parasite understood — Wren felt it understand, a slow cold attention turning through the dark like a searchlight.

It found Wren.

*Ah,* it said. *Ah, I see. The lover. You carry the last of it, don't you? The thing I never managed to harvest, because it was never his to take and never yours to sell. A memory of loving. Do you know how rare that is? I have a kingdom's worth of being loved. I have almost nothing of loving. It's the one crop that won't grow in my soil.* The voice curled, gentle, terrible. *Give it to me and I will keep it safe forever. Nothing given to the garden is ever lost. You could visit it. You could visit him — the thief, the one with the careful hands. I would let you stand in the garden together, forever, and neither of you would ever have to lose the other again. Isn't that what you want? You've been waiting five years at the side of a road for someone who doesn't know your name.*

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Wren would have carried to their grave if they'd had a grave to carry it to: it was working. For one whole breath, it worked. Wren stood in the circle with the parasite's offer wrapped around them like warm water, and every cold night at the waystation, every market stall where Xanthe had sold them a charm with a stranger's smile, every *you look familiar, have we met* — all of it rose up and said *yes, yes, take the deal, an eternity of Xanthe is worth more than a lifetime of remembering Xanthe.*

Then Wren thought about the village that had forgotten its children.

They had walked through it together, weeks ago, Xanthe and Wren and Ilsa. The smiling people. The woman who could not name her daughter, and the daughter standing right in front of her, and Xanthe giving back one small memory and the woman weeping herself whole in the street. Wren had understood something in that street that they had never fully understood in five years of loving a person who couldn't love them back: that love held in a cage isn't love. That the Verdant King's garden was full of flowers and not one of them was loved, because nothing in the garden had ever been given anything. Everything there was taken. A paradise of theft, forever, and theft was exactly what Wren would be committing if they took the offer — stealing from the old king the one thing that could free him, stealing from Xanthe the truth of what they'd paid, stealing from themselves the right to have chosen.

"No," Wren said.

The word came out small. It came out anyway.

"I'm not giving it to *you*," Wren said, and their voice found its feet. "I'm giving it to *him*. The man in the chair. The one dreaming about tomatoes. You've been wearing him for a hundred years and you never once asked him what he loved, because you don't care what anything loves, you only care what it *has*. Well. Here's what he has. Here's the last unharvested field in the kingdom."

They opened their hands.

* * *

The memory rose in their palms like a coal.

Wren had chosen it on the road, in the long sleepless nights — chosen it the way you choose which book to burn, which letter to leave unread, which single thing to carry out of a burning house. There had been so many. The first morning in the house by the sea, salt light on the ceiling, Xanthe asleep and unguarded and humming in their sleep. The winter they'd nearly frozen laughing. The night of the purge, the last night, when Xanthe had kissed them and lied to them — *I'll find you in the spring* — already knowing they were going to sell every memory of Wren to buy the escape, and had kissed them anyway, had made the lie as beautiful as they could, because that was the kind of thief Xanthe was: they stole beautifully, and they gave away everything.

Wren had chosen a small one. That was the trick of it, the thing they'd understood that the parasite never would. Not the grand moments — the grand moments were the parasite's currency, the dearest memories it weaponized, the nostalgia it fought with. It could counterfeit grandeur. It could not counterfeit a Tuesday.

So Wren gave it a Tuesday.

An afternoon in the house by the sea, years ago, rain on the windows, nothing happening. Xanthe mending a coat badly, thread in their teeth, swearing at the needle with real feeling. Wren reading aloud from a bad novel and doing the voices. A pot of something on the stove that they'd both forgotten about until it burned, and neither of them getting up to save it, because the rain was good and the voices were good and the coat was finally, finally taking a stitch. Xanthe looking up, thread still in their teeth, and saying — not *I love you*, they'd both been too clever for that, too poor, too proud — but saying, *stay*, like a question they were afraid to ask. And Wren saying, *I'm not going anywhere*, like an answer they weren't afraid of at all.

That was the memory. That was the whole of it. Rain, a burned pot, a badly mended coat, and two people choosing, in the smallest words available, to remain.

Wren held it out in their open hands and gave it — not threw it, not spent it, *offered* it — to the old king.

The king took it.

* * *

The green light went out.

Not dimmed — *went out*, all at once, through the whole vault, a hundred years of parasitic glow snuffed like a candle in a draft, and in the sudden dark the lanterns blazed up gold, every stolen memory at once crying out like birds at dawn. The root around the chair blackened and split. The rings at the old king's wrists crumbled into mulch.

And the old man opened his eyes.

He wept immediately. That was the thing Wren had not expected, the thing no one had expected: he did not wake confused, or afraid, or roaring for his crown. He woke weeping, both hands pressed over his mouth, and the sound he made was not a king's sound at all. It was the sound of someone remembering, after a hundred years in a cage of peace, what it felt like to be devoted to. To be the *stay* in someone's *I'm not going anywhere*.

"I had a garden," the old king said, and his voice was rust and rain. "A real one. Small. There was a woman — Marta — she grew the roses, I only argued about them—" He looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, which they did, which they had for a century. "What did you give me?"

"Nothing important," Wren said. "A Tuesday."

The old king looked at them — really looked, the first honest look anyone had given anyone in this vault in a hundred years — and Wren watched the memory settle into him, watched him take it in the way you take in rain after drought, and watched his face do what the woman's face had done in the village street. Come home to itself.

"Someone stayed," the king said.

"Someone stayed," Wren agreed, and found they were crying too, and could not for the life of them have said what about.

Then the unbinding pulled.

Juniper had warned them, but warning was nothing. The rite turned over like a tide and the whole vault exhaled — a hundred years of stolen memory tearing free of the roots all at once, the lanterns rising from their cages like a reversed rain, thousands of lights streaming up and out through the broken throne room floor, going home, going home, going home. Wren felt it pass through them, the great returning, felt it tug at the place where the memory had been and find, as promised, nothing left to take. The scale was balanced. The debt was paid. The garden—

The garden screamed.

It was not a sound. It was a *withdrawing*, a hundred years of appetite suddenly starving, the parasite losing in one tide everything it had ever eaten, and the roots writhed, and the walls of the vault shivered with a green light that was dying rather than burning, and somewhere above them the throne room began, softly, to come apart into ordinary ivy.

"Wren." Xanthe's hand closed on their arm — Xanthe had crossed the circle without breaking it, because of course they had, rules were for other people — and their grip was shaking. "Wren, what did you give? What did you *give*?"

And Wren looked at the person they had crossed a kingdom to find, the person they had been sold for, the person standing in front of them with tears on their face and no idea why, and opened their mouth to answer.

Nothing came.

There was a shape where the answer should have been. A Tuesday-shaped room, swept clean. Rain on a window somewhere, and no window. Wren knew — intellectually, distantly, the way you know the plot of a book you read as a child — that they had loved this person. That they had given the proof of it away, freely, to a stranger king, and that the giving had been the whole point: love exists outside the parasite's economy, love is the one thing that survives being taken, because it was never a possession to begin with.

But the knowing was all they had. The *having* was gone.

"I don't remember," Wren said, and heard their own voice crack down the middle. "Xanthe, I don't— I know I'm supposed to remember. I know it mattered. I know *you* mattered. I can feel the shape of it and it's the biggest shape I've ever—" They pressed a hand flat against their own chest, as if to hold the shape in. "I gave it to him. I gave it to him and I'd do it again, I know I would, that's the one thing I'm sure of—"

Xanthe caught them as their knees went. Held them up in the dark of the ruined vault while the last of the lanterns rose past them into the sky, going home. And Xanthe — who did not remember either, who had sold it all five years ago, who had no more of Wren in them than Wren now had of Xanthe — held on like the answer to a question neither of them could ask.

"I don't know why I know you," Xanthe said into their hair, fierce and broken. "I don't know why. But I know you. Whatever you gave away, whoever you were to me — I know you. Do you hear me? That's not nothing. That's not *nothing*."

Above them, through the splitting floor, the first true starlight in months came down into the vault like rain.

And far away, at the edge of the kingdom, the Verdant King's armies stopped mid-march, and stood in their thousands among the hedgerows, and did not know, any of them, why they had come.
