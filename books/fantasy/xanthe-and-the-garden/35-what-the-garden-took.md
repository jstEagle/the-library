## 35. What the Garden Took

The rite did not end so much as exhale.

Xanthe felt it as a change in pressure—the whole root-vault breathing out at once, a hundred years of held breath released through the splitting stone. Above them the floor had opened in a slow spiral of cracking granite, and starlight poured down through the gap like something poured from a jug, and every memory-lantern hanging in the dark began to rise.

They went gently. That was the thing Xanthe would remember—no, that was the thing they *wouldn't* remember, and would have to be told later, and would have to believe on faith. The lanterns rose gently. Each one a stolen life, a harvested hour, a grief or wedding day or last sunset peeled out of someone's skull by the parasite's patient garden—and now they lifted off their hooks of root and drifted upward through the broken floor, streaming toward the sky like a river running backward, like all the world's rivers deciding to return to their sources.

Wren was still in Xanthe's arms. Wren was lighter than they should have been. Xanthe could feel that already—the way you can feel a fire going out in the next room, some subtle wrongness in the air.

"No," Xanthe said. Not a question. Just the word, said twice, three times, into Wren's hair. "No. No. You don't get to—"

"I knew what it would cost." Wren's voice was steady, which was somehow worse than weeping. They pulled back far enough to look at Xanthe, and their eyes were already going distant at the edges, like windows fogging over. "I did the arithmetic, love. One memory against a kingdom. It's not even close."

"It's not yours to spend."

"It's mine," Wren said. "It's the only thing that's ever been entirely mine."

The lanterns kept rising. And with them, Xanthe understood, rising too—drawn up the same channel, caught in the same current—came everything else.

* * *

The old king was awake.

He stood among them in the ruined vault, an old man in a gardener's coat, his crown nowhere in sight, his hands shaking. He looked smaller than any monarch had a right to look. He looked like someone's grandfather who'd wandered away from a funeral.

The Verdant King came apart above him.

That was the closest word for it. Unmade. The great presence that had worn a crown and a country both for a century was unraveling in the starlight, its green fire dimming to ordinary chlorophyll, its vast attention narrowing and narrowing until it was simply—a plant. Ivy. The walls of the vault, which had been crawling with living hedge and flowering thorn and shapes that screamed softly when you touched them, were suddenly just walls again, damp and old, hung with ivy that was only ivy: dumb, green, harmless, growing at the speed ivy grows.

Juniper Sable sat down hard on the wet stone and started laughing—a cracked, disbelieving sound that turned halfway through into sobbing. Ilsa Vane stood with her ward-craft still burning along her forearms in pale blue lines, watching the ivy like a soldier watches a defeated enemy for treachery. Hale was on his knees, saying something under his breath that might have been a legal formula or might have been a prayer; with Hale it had always been hard to tell the difference.

And the king—the true king, the buried man, the dreamer in the garden chair—was weeping without any shame at all.

"Someone loved somebody," he said. His voice was rusty, a hinge unused for decades. He looked around the vault as if seeing it for the first time, because he was. "I felt it. All this time, under all of it—I couldn't remember what devotion looked like. I'd forgotten there was such a thing. And then—" He pressed a hand flat over his heart. "Someone gave me theirs. A stranger. Someone gave me the shape of loving, just so I'd know it was real."

Xanthe wasn't listening. Xanthe was watching Wren's hands.

The fog was moving inward from the edges of them. Fingertips first, then the backs of hands, then wrists—not visibly, nothing visible, but Xanthe's thief-sense had always been able to see memories the way other people saw light, and they could see the memory draining out of Wren like color from a photograph left in the sun. The memory of loving Xanthe. Five years of it. Every stolen afternoon in a house by the sea, every argument, every night Wren had spent memorizing the way Xanthe slept, every moment of the purge and the price and the choice—it was all of it climbing now, gentle as everything else, gentle as theft always was in this garden, up toward the sky where it would be poured into a dying king's chest like medicine.

"Wren." Xanthe grabbed those fading hands. "Look at me. Fight it. You can give him the shape of it—you don't have to give him all of it, there has to be a way to keep—"

"There isn't." Wren smiled. Their smile was the same. That was the cruelest part, that the smile was exactly the same while everything behind it emptied out. "I asked Juniper. There's never been a partial version of a freely given memory. It's all or nothing. It's the whole point of freely given." They turned their hands over so they could hold Xanthe's instead of being held. "You taught me that. Did you know that? Five years ago, in the market, you told me that a charm taken without consent is theft but one given is a gift, and the gift is worth more because the giver feels the whole weight of it. I'm feeling the whole weight of it right now."

"Stop talking like a eulogy."

"I'm talking like a love letter. There's a difference." Wren's thumb moved across Xanthe's knuckles, once, the way it had moved ten thousand times before and would never move again. "Listen to me. We have minutes. Maybe less. So I'm going to say the things I'd say if we had years, quickly, and you're going to hate that, and I'm going to do it anyway."

"Don't."

"I don't regret it." Their voice didn't waver. "Not one hour. Not the worst ones. I got five years of you, Xanthe. Some people get none. And whatever happens next—whatever's taken—I want you to know that somewhere it's still true. Somewhere outside your head, outside mine, in the world itself, it happened. We happened. Memory is just the record. The record burns. The thing recorded—"

"Don't you dare philosophize at me while you're dying—"

"I'm not dying." And here Wren laughed, soft and terrible. "I'm worse than dying. I'm staying. I'll be at your door tomorrow, and the day after, and I won't know why I keep coming back, and neither will you, and we'll drink tea like polite strangers, and it will be unbearable, and I will bear it. Because you told me once—do you remember? No. Of course you don't. But you told me once that love outlasts memory. You said it about a widow's charm. You believed it enough to sell it. So I'm calling in the belief. Prove yourself right."

Above them, the last of the lanterns cleared the broken ceiling and the sky took them.

Xanthe felt the current turn toward themself.

It was not violent. Nothing in this place was violent anymore; the parasite's gentleness lingered in its ruins like perfume in a dead woman's house. It was simply a tide coming in, and Xanthe standing in it, and the tide full of everything they had recovered—everything they'd bought back at the palace, every piece of themselves the bargain had promised and the refusal had forfeited and the rite had, in its vast indifferent arithmetic, reclaimed.

The house by the sea went first.

Xanthe felt it lift out of them the way you feel a splinter leave: relief and loss in the same instant, indistinguishable. Blue shutters. Salt on the wind. Wren painting the kitchen table and getting it in their hair. Gone—not forgotten, not yet, but *leaving*, and Xanthe could feel the leaving, could track each memory's departure the way you track a bird you raised from the egg flying south.

"No—" Xanthe said, to the sky, to the rite, to whatever accounting was happening in the starlight. "Those are mine. Those are *mine*, they were stolen from me first, you can't steal from a thief and call it justice—"

But of course it could. It was the same logic that had run the whole century: that some harvests are necessary, that some losses serve a greater keeping, that the ledger balances if you stand far enough away from it. Xanthe had spent five years telling buyers' widows and soldiers that memory was identity, that no one had the right to take it. Now the universe had taken up the argument on the other side.

The purge went. The night Wren fled through the marshes with soldiers' torches behind them. The deal in the archive, the price named, the signing away. Xanthe watched it go and reached for it and their hands closed on nothing.

Then the smaller things, the terrible small things. Wren's laugh, low, mostly nose. The scar on Wren's left palm from a fishhook, shaped like a crooked Y. The particular weight of Wren asleep against their shoulder on the ferry to Vell. The fight they'd had about leaving, the door slammed, the door opened again, the apology that was just Wren standing in the doorway holding two cups of tea like a white flag.

Gone. Gone. Gone.

"Xanthe." Wren's voice. Already thinner. Already a stranger's voice wearing a familiar face. "Hey. Look at me. Last thing. Last thing, okay?"

Xanthe looked. It cost everything and they looked anyway, because if these were the last seconds of being known—if this was the final moment anyone alive would hold the complete shape of what the two of them had been—then Xanthe intended to be present for it, wide awake, eyes open, a witness to their own amputation.

"I loved you," Wren said.

The words went in deep, deeper than memory, down past where the rite could reach, down into whatever bedrock a person stands on when everything else has been excavated. Because that was the thing. The rite took the *memory* of loving. But Wren was saying it *now*, in the present tense, with the memory already half-gone, and the words were still true. The words were true without the memory holding them up. Love outlasting memory, demonstrated live, in front of witnesses, in a collapsing vault under a dead god's throne.

"I loved you," Wren said again, slower, like pressing a seal into wax. "Whatever I am in an hour—stranger, friend, ghost—I loved you. Somebody in the world knows it even if nobody remembers it. The world knows. Let that be enough. Let it be—"

And then Wren blinked, and straightened slightly, and looked at Xanthe with the mild puzzled courtesy of someone encountering a stranger on a stair.

"Oh," Wren said. "Hello. Are you—are we waiting for someone? This place looks dangerous. Should we be under a roof?"

The tide finished going out.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, and starlight, and ivy.

Xanthe stood very still in the middle of the vault while the others moved around them—Hale lifting the dazed old king to his feet, Juniper already scrambling through the wreckage of a century's records, Ilsa checking the walls for anything that still moved. Nobody spoke to Xanthe. Perhaps they thought Xanthe needed space. Perhaps Ilsa, who knew exactly what had been paid and by whom, understood that space was the last thing that would help and the only thing she could offer.

The Verdant King was a pile of ordinary ivy on the steps of the dais. Truly ordinary. Xanthe made themselves walk over and touch it—somebody had to, somebody had to confirm it, that was the job, that had always been the job, touching the thing everyone else was afraid of. The leaves were cool and dumb and slightly dusty. When Xanthe crushed one between their fingers it smelled like every garden in the world, green and simple and asking nothing.

Somewhere in that smell was the ghost of everything the parasite had eaten, and everything the rite had taken back, and everything Xanthe had surrendered to balance the books.

Their stock was gone too. They realized it distantly, cataloguing like a merchant: the widow's wedding day, the soldier's last sunset, the whole inventory of five years' trade—all of it swept up in the returning tide, gone home to whoever it belonged to. Xanthe was, for the first time since childhood, empty. A clean slate. A stranger carrying nothing but their own thin thread of self, and holes in it now, holes shaped like a person.

"Xanthe." Ilsa's hand on their shoulder. Steady. Ward-keeper's hands, built for holding failing things together. "Can you walk?"

"Yes."

"That's all I need to know. Can you climb?"

"The spiral's intact on the north side," Juniper called, hoarse, ink-stained, alive, hugging a salvaged armful of pages like a lifeboat. "We go up through the old orangery. And someone help me carry the king, he keeps stopping to look at plants."

"I *like* plants," the old king said, wounded. "I appear to have missed them enormously."

Hale laughed—one short bark, surprised out of him—and then looked appalled at himself for laughing, and then laughed again, and Xanthe thought: *there's a man who will spend the rest of his life learning what to do with his hands now they're not holding chains.*

Xanthe climbed.

Halfway up the spiral, Wren fell into step beside them. Not deliberately—there was no deliberation left in it, no memory steering those feet—but the body remembered routes the mind had lost, and Wren's body had apparently decided that where Xanthe went, it went.

"This is strange," Wren said conversationally, after several turns of the stair. "I keep almost knowing you. Is that odd? You probably think that's odd. People must tell you that all the time, though. You seem like someone people almost-know."

"You have no idea," Xanthe said.

"Did we meet? Before? You're looking at me like—" Wren stopped on the stair. Frowned. Pressed their palm flat against their own sternum, the way a person does when something knocks there from the inside. "There's something. Like a song stuck in my chest that I don't know the words to. Do you know it?"

"No," Xanthe lied, and their voice held, and it was the hardest magic they had ever done.

Because the truth was that Xanthe knew the song. Knew every note of it. Would know every note of it forever, because that was the joke, the exquisite cruelty of it—the rite had taken Xanthe's *recovered* memories, the bought-back years, the restored stock. But the fragments from Chapter One of their life, the stray pieces carried since the beginning, the residue of a thousand charms sold in the dark—those had woven themselves into Xanthe too deeply to be separated out. The rite had taken the whole cloth and left the embroidery. Xanthe remembered Wren in pieces. A laugh. A scar. The weight of a sleeping shoulder. A song hummed in a kitchen with paint in its hair.

They would remember everything except the story the pieces belonged to. A stranger's face assembled from beloved details, forever.

"Yeah," Wren said softly, still frowning at their own hand on their chest. "Me too. Whatever it is, I've got it too." Then they smiled—Wren's smile, the exact one, surviving in muscle and bone where memory couldn't reach—and climbed on ahead toward the light.

Xanthe stood on the stairs and let themselves break, silently, completely, for exactly as long as it took the others to round the next bend. Then they wiped their face with the heel of their hand, the way they used to after closing the stall on bad nights, and followed.

Above, through the shattered roof of the orangery, the stars were all out at once—the good kind, the ordinary kind, thousands of them, indifferent and ancient and free. Dawn was a gray rumor in the east. Somewhere beyond the walls, an army was standing in the hedgerows trying to remember why it existed, and finding it couldn't.

The kingdom was saved. Xanthe climbed toward it, empty-handed, carrying a song they couldn't name and a stranger they'd loved, and behind them on the dais steps the ivy grew one inch in the night—just ivy, just green, just a plant doing the only honest work left to it:

reaching, patiently, toward the light.
