## 37. A Stranger at the Door

The shop had been a pie seller's stall, then a coffin-maker's workshop, then nothing at all for three years—just another wound in Vell's crooked ribs. Now it had a door that stuck in damp weather, one window with glass so old it turned the street green, and a hand-painted sign above the lintel that read:

**XANTHE'S. NOTHING SOLD. EVERYTHING HEARD.**

Xanthe had argued with themselves about the sign for two full days. *Nothing sold* felt like an epitaph. It was also the truest sentence they knew how to write.

Five years of selling memories had taught them everything about price and nothing about worth. The new shop was meant to be the correction. People came in off the street carrying their lives like overloaded pack-mules—refugees from the garden-towns, veterans of Hale's prosecutions, neighbors who'd woken from the Verdant King's harvest missing years and finding the gaps unbearable—and Xanthe listened while they talked. That was all. They didn't take anything. They didn't give anything back, either; that was Juniper's work now, over at the Archive of Given Things, where the recovered century hung in its vaults waiting to be matched to its owners like lost luggage.

Here there was only tea, two chairs, and the strange modern miracle of being heard.

It paid worse than thieving, which was to say it paid nothing, which was to say Ilsa slipped coins into the till when she thought Xanthe wasn't looking and Xanthe pretended not to find them.

* * *

On a grey Tuesday in the second month of the shop's existence, a woman came in who could not stop apologizing.

"I'm sorry," she said, sitting down in the customer's chair as if it might bite her. "I'm sorry, I don't even know why I'm here. My sister said—she said you don't sell charms anymore, you just... sit with people. Which is mad. That's mad, isn't it? I'm sorry."

"Tea?" Xanthe asked.

She took the cup with both hands. She was maybe fifty, with the wind-scoured face of someone who'd walked a long way recently, and she held the tea like it was the first warm thing anyone had handed her in months.

"They say my village chose to forget," she said abruptly. "That's what the archivists told me. The Verdant King came through and everyone ran, but some people—" Her hands tightened on the cup. "Some people *chose*. They went into the flowers on purpose. My husband may have been one of them. I don't know. I can't remember his face clearly enough to know whether he looked like a man who wanted to be done."

Xanthe said nothing. This was the discipline now: saying nothing. It went against every instinct they owned. For five years their trade had been interruption—the swift surgical cut, the memory lifted before the moment closed. Listening without taking was like being asked to stand in a river with open hands and let every fish swim past.

But they were learning.

"My sister remembers him," the woman went on. "She tells me stories about him and I nod along like they're about a stranger, because they are. And last week she got angry at me for it. She said, *you must miss him, you loved him*, and I said—" The woman's voice cracked. "I said, *how would I know?*"

"There's no rule," Xanthe said quietly, "that says grief has to have proof."

She stared at them. "Then what is this feeling? Because it feels exactly like grief. It has grief's whole shape. It just doesn't know what it's grieving."

"That's the shape," Xanthe said. "I know it well."

And they did. Gods, they did. They sat with the woman while her tea went cold, and when she finally rose to leave, she paused at the door.

"What happened to you?" she asked. "If you don't mind my asking. Everyone says you used to sell charms. Everyone says the war cost you something."

"The war cost everyone something," Xanthe said, which was true, and also a dodge, and the woman saw it was a dodge, and mercifully let it be one.

After she left, Xanthe washed both cups and stood at the window watching the street, where workmen were re-shingling the cobbler's roof across the lane and a child was drawing a sun in chalk on a wall that still bore the scorch-mark of an inquisitor's pyre. Vell was rebuilding itself with the stubbornness of a city that had survived worse and intended to survive this too. Stars overhead again, real ones, and wards around the border growing under Ilsa's new design like trees instead of walls—grown, not built, permission-rooted, the whole architecture of protection rewritten.

Xanthe's own losses had no such design. There was just a country inside them with the borders drawn wrong: they knew their own name but not the sound of their mother's voice. They knew they'd been a thief but couldn't remember the first thing they'd stolen. They knew—because everyone told them, because Wren's whole face told them—that they had once been loved so fiercely they'd traded everything they were to protect it.

They could not feel it. That was the wound. Not the absence of the memory. The absence of the *weight*. A love that heavy should leave a dent. All Xanthe had was smooth floor.

* * *

Wren arrived at dusk, as they always did, knocking on the sticking door even though Xanthe had told them a hundred times just to come in.

"It's your shop," Wren said, easing inside with the cold still clinging to their coat. "Knocking seemed polite."

"You've never once been polite."

"Growth." Wren hung their coat on the peg—a peg they had installed themselves, third week after the unbinding, without asking, and Xanthe had watched them do it and felt something turn over in their chest like soil. "Any customers today?"

"A woman whose husband might have chosen the flowers."

Wren winced. "Ah."

"She'll be back Thursday. Or she won't. Both are fine."

"And between now and Thursday, are you going to eat actual food, or am I going to find out you've been living on tea and dried plums again?"

"Dried plums are a food group."

"Dried plums are a *punishment*." Wren was already moving toward the little stove at the back of the shop, the one behind the curtain, in the corner where Xanthe slept. They moved through the space like it was familiar to them—which it was, more familiar to them than to Xanthe, which was the whole strange arithmetic of their situation. Wren knew where everything was. Wren had helped choose half of it. Wren knew that the second chair by the window wobbled unless you wedged a folded slip of paper under the short leg, because Wren had put the paper there himself, four months ago, on an evening Xanthe had no memory of at all.

Xanthe sat and watched them cook. It was the part of the day they would have called their favorite, if they'd allowed themselves to call anything a favorite. Wren chopped onions with the efficiency of someone who had cooked in field camps for five years, humming under their breath, occasionally swearing at the stove, and every so often glancing over at Xanthe with an expression Xanthe had spent months trying to learn how to read.

It wasn't longing, exactly. Longing would have been simpler. It was more like the way you look at a house you grew up in after it's burned—not reaching for it, because reaching hurts, but unable to look away either. Cataloguing. Grieving forward.

"You're staring," Wren said without turning around.

"You're burning the onions."

"I am *caramelizing* the onions, there's a difference, I learned it from—" Wren stopped. The sentence ended like a rope cut. They both heard where it had been going. *I learned it from you.*

The silence lasted three seconds, which was long enough.

"Parsley," Wren said briskly. "Where do we keep—"

"Cupboard. Left of the sink. You know that."

"I know that." A breath of a laugh. "Muscle memory."

They ate at the counter because the table was buried under Juniper's ledgers, which Xanthe kept meaning to return and never did, partly from busyness and partly because the ledgers were full of other people's restored histories and reading them was the closest thing Xanthe had to a mirror. Wren talked about their day with the resistance liaison office—supply routes, the last of the heralds being run down in the eastern passes, the tedious machinery of peace. Xanthe talked about the shop. Between them the food disappeared and the candles burned down and neither of them mentioned, as neither of them ever mentioned, the single obvious fact that organized their entire acquaintance:

Every night, Wren stayed until it was time to walk home. Every night they stood in the doorway a moment too long. Every morning, they came back.

A kind stranger, keeping a promise they hadn't explained to the person it was made to.

* * *

It happened, that night, the way it had started happening lately: a fragment surfaced.

Not a memory. Xanthe had given up hoping for those; the rite had taken clean, and what was given freely did not seep back. But sometimes, at the edge of sleep, something rose that wasn't quite thought and wasn't quite feeling—a residue, a watermark. That night it was the smell of salt and woodsmoke, very specific, tied to a laugh they could hear the shape of but not the sound. Their body reacted before their mind could name anything: throat tight, hands curling on the blanket, an ache in the sternum like a bruise on a bone.

*House by the sea,* they thought. *There was supposed to be a house by the sea.*

Everyone said so. Wren had mentioned it exactly once, drunk on Ilsa's plum brandy at the victory celebration in the spring: *We had plans, you know. A house. You drew it on a napkin. You put the kitchen facing east because you said I'd never get up before noon otherwise and you wanted me to smell the sea anyway.* Then Wren had gone white, set down the cup, apologized for the whole evening, and never spoken of it again.

Xanthe lay in the dark above the shop and let the ache crest and pass, crest and pass, like weather. This was the practice now, too. Grief without proof. Mourning a stranger whose absence had the exact shape of their own life.

Somewhere below, the door clicked.

They went still. Footsteps—they'd know those footsteps anywhere, light and quick, pausing at the bottom of the stairs. A long hesitation. Then, softly, the sound of someone sitting down on the lowest step, settling in, staying.

Xanthe crept to the top of the stairs and looked down through the dark.

"Wren?"

Silence. Then, rueful: "Caught. Sorry. I forgot something. I was just—it's nothing. Go back to bed."

"At midnight?"

"I'm nocturnal. Occupational habit. Five years of resistance work, you don't lose it." A pause. "Go to sleep, Xanthe."

They didn't. They sat down on the top step instead, and after a while Wren laughed, very quietly, defeated, and leaned back against the door, and for a long time neither of them said anything at all. The shop ticked and settled around them. Somewhere outside, a night-watchman called the hour, and stars they had all nearly died for burned over the rooftops of Vell.

"Why do you come here?" Xanthe asked, finally. "Every day. You have your own lodging. Your own work. You could visit once a week like a normal person."

The silence from the bottom step went on so long that Xanthe almost spoke again.

"Because the soup's better here," Wren said at last, which was a lie, since Xanthe made the soup. "Because you'd forget to eat otherwise. Because—" Another silence, longer. When Wren continued, their voice had changed, gone careful, like someone walking on ice they knew the thickness of. "When I was young, my grandmother used to tell me about her brother, who went away to the wars and came back with a fever that took his memory. Whole years, gone. He didn't know her. He didn't know the farm. And she told me that she went to see him every single day anyway, and I asked her why, if he didn't remember her, and she said—" Wren's voice caught, steadied. "She said, *because he's still him. Memory is only the story we tell about who we are. But the person underneath the story stays.*"

Xanthe's chest was doing the thing again. The bruise on the bone.

"So you come every day," they said slowly, "to check whether I'm still me."

"No," Wren said. "I come every day because I already know you are."

The words landed somewhere deep, in a place with no memory attached, and struck true anyway. Xanthe sat in the dark with their arms wrapped around their knees and understood—with no evidence, no recollection, nothing but the ache and the watermark and the way their whole body leaned toward the foot of the stairs like a plant toward light—that this person had loved them. Loved them enormously. Was loving them still, daily, unpaid, unnamed, choosing it over and over with no hope or perhaps with quiet unreasonable hope, and that Xanthe had once, in a life they couldn't reach, chosen exactly the same thing back.

"Why don't you tell me?" they whispered. "What we were. You must know I'd listen. You must know I want—"

"Because it's yours to find," Wren said. "Or not find. I won't hand you a story and make you live in it. You did enough living in other people's stories for one lifetime." The stair creaked; Wren was standing. "Besides."

"Besides?"

"Grandmother's brother," Wren said. "He never got his memory back. Not one year of it. And you know what he said to her, near the end? He said, *I don't remember you, but you're my favorite person in the world. Isn't that funny?*" A soft breath, almost a laugh. "Goodnight, Xanthe."

Footsteps up, past, pausing at eye level on the stairs—close, closer than they'd stood in months—and then, gently, briefly, fingers brushing Xanthe's hair back from their forehead the way you'd touch something precious and sleeping. Gone before Xanthe could move. Down the stairs. The door opened and shut on the cold.

* * *

Morning came grey and clean. Xanthe opened the shop early, swept the step, lit the stove, set out two cups instead of one without thinking about it. Across the lane the child was back, chalking a new drawing over yesterday's rain-washed sun: this time a garden, flowers in rows, and a small stick figure standing among them with its arms raised like branches.

Xanthe watched it for a while. Then, humming something they didn't know—a tune that lived in their hands rather than their head, in a minor key, like a lullaby from a country with no map—they fetched the watering can and went out back to the little plot behind the shop, where things were coming up green in patterns they had not planted on purpose.

The bell over the door rang before they reached it.

Through the window they could see Wren on the doorstep, coat dusted with frost, holding a paper bag of morning buns and wearing the expression—the cataloguing one, the burned-house one—but softer today. Brighter. Like someone who had made a decision overnight and found it good.

"You're early," Xanthe said, opening the door.

"You're humming," Wren said.

Xanthe stopped. "Am I?"

"That song." Wren's voice had gone very careful again, and very warm, and their eyes were wet at the edges in a way they were clearly pretending they weren't. "You hum it all the time. You probably don't notice."

"I don't know it."

"I know," Wren said. "You will."

And they stepped inside, out of the cold, closing the door behind them—home, Xanthe thought, from nowhere, the word arriving unbidden and fitting anyway—and held out the bag of buns like an offering, like a promise, like the first line of a story that wasn't finished yet.

"Eat," Wren said. "Then tell me about your Thursday customer. I want to hear everything."

And Xanthe, not knowing why their eyes stung, not knowing why their chest felt too large for the room, took the bag and said: "There's time. Sit down. I'll tell you all of it."
