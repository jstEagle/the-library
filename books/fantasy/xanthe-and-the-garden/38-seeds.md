## 38. Seeds

The Archive of Given Things began with three shelves and a leak.

Juniper had found the cistern beneath Vell flooded after the war—war, they were calling it that now, though it had lasted less than a season and most of its casualties had been memories rather than bodies—and the water had ruined everything she'd hidden there over nine years of quiet rebellion. The banned histories she'd copied by lamplight. The ledgers of the old royal cipher. The single, precious manuscript of the unbinding rite, which she'd burned into her own memory in the palace archive and then spent two weeks afterward unable to remember why she kept flinching at the smell of roses.

So when the council offered her a stipend and a building, she chose the driest cellar in the city: an old granary on Tallow Row, thick-walled, windowless, with a door that locked from either side. She put three shelves against one wall and labeled them herself, in ink she mixed from soot and tallow because proper ink was still scarce.

*Given Freely. Returned Unopened. Refused.*

That was the whole taxonomy, in the beginning. Three shelves for every memory the Verdant King's root-vault had surrendered, every lantern of stolen light that Xanthe and Wren and Ilsa and Hale had carried out of the dark beneath the throne.

There had been eleven thousand four hundred and six of them.

Juniper knew the number because she had counted them twice, once by hand and once by charm, and the discrepancy between the two counts—one—had kept her awake for three nights until she found the missing lantern tucked behind a shelf support, humming faintly, containing a memory of a dog. Just a dog. A brown dog rolling in grass. Someone had loved that moment enough to steal it, or been robbed of loving it, and for three nights it had sat unaccounted for while a person somewhere in Vell went on not knowing what they'd lost.

She held the lantern up to the lamp and watched the light move inside it like something alive. It probably *was* something alive. Everything about memory-craft suggested that a stored recollection didn't sit inert the way a book did; it breathed. It wanted to be lived again. The Verdant King had understood that better than anyone—it had built an entire garden out of wanting.

"Cataloguing?" said Wren from the doorway.

Juniper didn't startle. She'd stopped startling at Wren months ago, which was itself strange, because before the war Wren had been a rumor to her—a name in a resistance cell, a voice on the other side of a wall. Now Wren came by the granary nearly every day, carrying bread or questions, and Juniper had gotten used to being known.

"Number two thousand and thirty-one," Juniper said. "Dog, brown, rolling. Provenance unknown."

"Anyone claim it yet?"

"Not yet." She set the lantern down gently among its neighbors. "We posted the description last week. Brown dog, long grass, summer. You'd think half the city would come running."

Wren leaned against the doorframe. "Maybe the right half hasn't heard."

"Maybe," Juniper said, and didn't say the other thing, which was that maybe whoever had lost this memory had lost other things too, bigger things, and a brown dog couldn't compete with a vanished marriage or a dead child or five years of your own face looking back at you from a stranger's eyes. She thought of Xanthe often these days—the thief who had sold their own past without knowing it, who now walked through Vell like a person reading a map of a country they'd once ruled. Wren visited them daily. Everyone knew that. Nobody talked about whether it helped.

"How is the shop?" Juniper asked, because asking was kinder than not asking, even if neither of them wanted the answer.

"Sells nothing," Wren said. "Listens. Serves tea." A pause. "They hum sometimes. Songs they don't know. They don't know they're mine."

Juniper looked at the lanterns. Eleven thousand of them, give or take, each one a wound someone else had survived. "You could put yours in here," she said quietly. "The Archive takes gifts. That's rather the point of it."

"I know what the point of it is." Wren smiled, and it was a good smile, and it cost them something visible. "I'm keeping mine. Not to give back. To keep choosing."

*To keep choosing.* Juniper wrote the phrase down later, in the margin of her ledger, because it was the thesis of the whole enterprise and Wren had delivered it standing in a doorway holding bread.

* * *

The work had rules, and Juniper had written all of them down, because rules were how you honored consent instead of merely gesturing at it.

Rule One: no memory leaves the Archive except into the hands of the person who lost it, proven however they can prove it—by testimony, by kinship, by the way their hands shake when the lantern hums near them. Rule Two: no one is told what a memory contains before they choose to receive it, only when it was taken and from whom. Rule Three—and this was the rule people argued about, in letters to the council and once, memorably, in person, a red-faced merchant who shouted at Juniper for an hour—Rule Three: if a person declines to reclaim what was theirs, the Archive destroys nothing but returns nothing either. It keeps the memory sealed, forever, unowned and unowned-by, a light in a jar that no one will ever live again.

"You're hoarders," the merchant had said. "Same as the King, just politer."

"No," Juniper had said, tired. "The King took. We keep the door open. Those aren't the same crime, and if you can't tell the difference, you've never had anything taken."

He hadn't come back. But his daughter had, three weeks later, to reclaim a memory of her mother teaching her to whistle. She'd wept in the reading room for forty minutes, and then she'd asked Juniper the question that Juniper now heard almost weekly, the question the whole city was learning to ask:

"What if I take it back and it ruins me?"

And Juniper had answered honestly, because honesty was the only currency the Archive dealt in: "Then you bring it back, and we seal it again, and no one will ever know but me. Taking it back isn't permanent. Nothing here is permanent except your right to choose."

That was Rule Four, actually. She should have led with it.

* * *

Not everyone chose reclamation. This was the thing Juniper had not predicted, in her years of imagining what a free archive might look like: the refusals outnumbered the reclamations, nearly three to one.

The soldier who came in on a gray Tuesday stood at the counter for a long time before speaking. He was maybe thirty, with a burn scar down one side of his face that the war hadn't given him—he'd had it since before, everyone in his village confirmed it—and he'd identified his lantern by date and district. Taken during the second harvest, when the King's heralds had swept through the eastern towns collecting memories wholesale, the way tax collectors collect coin.

"It's my wedding," he said. "I know that much. My mother told me. She says I was happy." He turned the lantern over in his big scarred hands as though checking it for defects. "I don't remember her. My wife. The heralds took her that same day—not her memory, *her*. She was a keeper. They rooted her in the hedge-line outside Corren's Ford."

Juniper waited.

"If I take it back," he said, "I get one day of her. One. And then I spend the rest of my life knowing exactly what's gone." He set the lantern down with terrible care. "My mother thinks I'm broken. Maybe I am. But I'd rather be whole and hollow than full and bleeding."

He left without it. Juniper logged the refusal—*declined, informed of rights, offered counsel, declined counsel also*—and sat for a while afterward doing nothing, watching the lantern glow faintly on its shelf under the label REFUSED.

A century ago, she knew from her own recovered histories, there would have been no shelf for that choice. Under the old monarchy, a harvested memory was crown property; under the Verdant King, it was fertilizer. There had never been, in all of Vell's recorded law, a category for *mine, and I decline to want it back.*

She added a note to the ledger: *The wound and the remembering of the wound are not the same injury. Some people survive by carrying only one.*

* * *

Ilsa Vane came by on the first of Thawmonth, smelling of sap and woodsmoke, with dirt under her fingernails that Juniper suspected was now permanent. The ward-trees were going in along the new border—one a week, planted with permission from every town whose land they touched, each sapling grown from cuttings the towns themselves donated. Ilsa called it wardcraft. Juniper privately called it the most radical act of governance she'd ever witnessed: a border built out of yeses.

"You look terrible," Ilsa said fondly, dropping onto the stool across the counter.

"I look like a woman who has catalogued eight thousand four hundred memories in five months."

"That's because you are one." Ilsa produced a wrapped parcel from her coat and set it on the counter. "From the Ford. The council there voted. They're donating their harvest-records—the ones the heralds kept, names and dates. Thought your shelves could use some company."

Juniper unwrapped it: a bundle of papers, water-stained, real. Primary sources. Her fingers itched.

"There's a second thing," Ilsa said, and now she wasn't smiling. "The king's abdication ceremony is next month. He wants the Archive represented. Formally. He asked for you by name."

"The king," Juniper said flatly, "who I helped unbind, wants me to stand on a dais in front of the entire capital."

"He's an old man who spent a century dreaming in a chair, and the first lucid year of his reign ended in the loss of his crown and most of his kingdom. I think he wants to apologize to somebody, and you're the somebody with a list of everybody he failed." Ilsa shrugged. "Also Hale will be there. He's prosecuting the third ring of complicit ministers. He says the trial needs an archival witness."

"Hale hates me."

"Hale respects you. With Hale it looks the same."

Juniper looked at the parcel, the shelves, the lanterns glowing in their rows like a city seen from very far away at night. Eight thousand four hundred catalogued. Three thousand more to go. Somewhere in those remaining thousands, statistically, there had to be a memory belonging to a thief on Tallow Row—a life by the sea, a lover named Wren, everything surrendered in the rite to buy a stranger's freedom. Juniper had searched for them already, early on, and found nothing, and had decided not to search again unless asked, because Rule One applied even to people who didn't know what they'd lost.

But lately she'd caught herself wondering. Lately she'd caught herself listening, when Wren described the shop, for any flicker in the account that might mean—

No. Not hers to want. Not hers to give.

"I'll come to the ceremony," she said. "Tell the king he can apologize in writing. I'll file it under Given Freely."

* * *

On the last evening of Thawmonth, a girl came into the Archive whom Juniper had been expecting for months without knowing it.

She was perhaps sixteen, dressed in the gray of the orphan houses, and she stood in the doorway a long time before crossing the room, the way people did when they weren't sure the place was for them. Juniper had learned to let them take their time. The door stayed open. The tea stayed warm.

"My brother's in your books," the girl said finally. "Denny Ferrow. Taken at Corren's Ford, second harvest. Mother reclaimed her own memory in autumn—she's got it back, she's—" Her jaw worked. "She's different. Better. But Denny died in the fighting at the gates, before the unbinding, and the council says his lantern passes to next of kin, and next of kin is me."

Juniper pulled the record. Denny Ferrow, age twelve at harvest, memory taken: one item. Date: midsummer, five years prior.

"One memory," she said. "Do you know what it—"

"Mother won't say. She says that's the rule." The girl's hands were fists at her sides. "I've been standing outside this door for six days, archivist. Six days. Because here's what I keep thinking: if I take it, I get one piece of my brother back, and it's the piece the monsters thought was worth stealing, which means it was the best piece, and I'll have it forever, and it will hurt every single day. And if I don't take it, then the last light of him sits on a shelf in a cellar until I die, and then what? It goes in the ground with me?"

"Yes," Juniper said simply. "Unless you will it elsewhere. It's yours to decide, and after you, yours to bequeath. That's the law now. Your law. The councils wrote it in autumn—all of it built on the principle that nobody gets to reach into another person's head and rearrange what love means." She paused. "Including me. Including your mother. Including Denny, if he were alive to have an opinion."

The girl stared at her. "That's not an answer."

"No," Juniper agreed. "It's the absence of one. That's the whole invention. The King had answers for everyone—what to remember, what to feel, what to grow. All we've got is the door." She slid the lantern across the counter, unlit, cool to the touch. "Take it home. Keep it a week. Open it or don't. If you open it and it breaks you, bring it back and I'll reseal it and we'll never speak of it. If you keep it sealed your whole life, I'll bury it with you myself and say something decent at the funeral."

The girl picked up the lantern. Held it the way the soldier had held his, checking for defects, finding none, finding only weight.

"Why do you do this?" she asked suddenly. "The shelves and the rules and the tea. What's it for?"

Juniper considered the honest answers—that it was penance for nine years of hiding knowledge instead of using it; that Juniper Sable had spent her whole life believing information was freedom only to learn, in the root-vault beneath the throne, that information without consent was just theft with better manners; that she had watched Xanthe give away everything and Wren refuse to stop loving and Ilsa plant trees on battlefields, and had concluded that the war's true weapon hadn't been any rite at all but the stubborn, ungovernable act of letting people choose their own wounds.

"For the same reason gardens grow from seeds," she said. "Because small things, kept safe and given time, become large ones. A hundred years ago someone stole a memory and fed a kingdom-eating monster. Today someone chooses, freely, whether to grieve. That's the difference. That's everything. Consent is the antidote, girl. Everything we're building is just the antidote, poured out slow."

The girl carried the lantern to the door. Stopped there, silhouetted against the evening.

"I'll come back Thursday," she said, and was gone.

Juniper entered it in the ledger—*Ferrow, D.; lantern released to kin pending decision*—and blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark among the shelves, listening to the faint collective hum of eleven thousand lives waiting to be wanted again.

Three thousand remained uncatalogued. Tomorrow she would start on the crates from the western vaults, and tomorrow after that, and someday—not soon, but someday—she would reach the bottom shelf of the last crate and find whatever was there, and decide, under Rule One, whether anyone had the right to ask for it back.

Outside, above the granary roof, the stars were out.

All of them. Every night now. As if the sky, too, remembered what it had lost and refused to lose it twice.
