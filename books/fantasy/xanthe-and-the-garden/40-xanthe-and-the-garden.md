## 40. Xanthe and the Garden

The plot behind the shop was supposed to be nothing. A patch of dirt the width of a cart, hemmed in by Vell's old sewer brick on two sides and a leaning fence that Ilsa kept promising to fix and never did. When Xanthe first turned the soil—six months after the stars came back, on a morning so ordinary it felt like getting away with something—they told themselves it was practical. Herbs for tea. Onions, maybe. The kind of thing any shopkeeper with a spare afternoon might do.

They had not planted onions.

They knelt in the dark loam now, sleeves pushed up, dirt under their nails, and looked at what they'd made instead: three spirals of flowers arranged around a flat stone, blue at the outer edge fading to white at the center, with a single line of yellow running through them like thread through cloth. It was beautiful. It was deliberate. And Xanthe had no idea why they'd done it.

That was the shape of most mornings now.

"Morning-glories," Wren had said once, passing through the yard with an armful of firewood, and stopped so suddenly the wood clattered. They'd stared at the spirals for a long time, and when they finally spoke again their voice had been carefully, expensively light. "Those were my mother's favorite. Morning-glories. She grew them in spirals."

"I didn't know that," Xanthe said.

"I know," Wren said. "I know you didn't."

And that was the whole conversation, because that was how those conversations went. Wren offered a fact like a coin across a counter, Xanthe took it without being able to give change, and both of them pretended the transaction hadn't cost anything. It was the only economy left between them, and neither one liked it, and both of them kept paying anyway.

Xanthe pressed a seedling into the earth with two fingers and found themselves humming.

It took a moment to notice—they were always humming lately, some low wandering tune that lived in their chest the way a splinter lives under skin—but when they did notice, they also noticed it wasn't theirs. Not that any song could strictly be said to be theirs anymore; half of what moved through Xanthe's head these days was salvage, flotsam from a life sold off five years ago in a room above the night market. But this melody had a particular quality. It rose at the end of each phrase the way a question does. It sounded, Xanthe thought, like someone singing to make another person laugh.

They hummed it through twice. Their hands, meanwhile, kept working, and kept working *wrong*—or right, depending—and by the time the sun cleared the fence line there was a fourth spiral sketched out in turned soil, waiting for seeds, positioned precisely where a person sitting on the back step would see it first thing in the morning.

Xanthe sat back on their heels and wiped their hands on their thighs and let the wrongness settle over them like weather.

Here is what nobody tells you about forgetting: it isn't empty. That was the first lesson of the trade, back when Xanthe had been a thief of memories rather than a giver of them—that a removed memory leaves a hole shaped exactly like itself, and holes ache, and people will pay almost anything for the ache to stop. Xanthe had built a career on that ache. Had harvested it, bottled it, sold it by candlelight to widows and soldiers and nobles who wanted to feel something other than the shape of what they'd lost.

Now they were the hole. And the ache had a name they couldn't feel anymore, only know.

*Wren.*

The name fit in the mouth fine. The name was easy. It was everything downstream of the name that had been taken—the five years of it, the small hours and the shared meals and whatever private ridiculous language the two of them had built between themselves, all of it poured into the rite beneath the palace, given to wake a king who had forgotten he was loved. A fair price. Xanthe would say so under oath, if anyone asked. They had seen the village that forgot itself; they had watched a woman weep herself whole around a returned memory, and they knew what the alternative was. You did not haggle with a debt like that. You paid it standing up.

Some mornings, though—most mornings, honestly, in the first grey hour before the shop opened—standing up was the hardest thing in the world.

The bell over the shop door chimed. Customers weren't due for another hour, but Xanthe already knew who it would be, the way they knew to leave the kettle filled and the second cup out, the way they knew the exact weight of footsteps on their stairs. Knowing-without-remembering. It was its own sense now, a sense with no honest name. Juniper would probably invent one eventually; Juniper had invented six words already for the new Archive of Given Things and defended each one like a border.

Wren came around the corner of the shop carrying two paper-wrapped buns from the stall on Lantern Row, and stopped when they saw the garden.

"You changed it," they said.

"Changed what?"

Wren set the buns down on the step very carefully, as though the ground might be listening. "The spirals used to face east." They pointed. "You've turned them. Toward the door."

Had they? Xanthe looked at the bed and felt the truth of it land somewhere below thought. "Huh," they said, which was about as much as they ever managed in these moments, and then, because honesty had become easier than pride somewhere along the road to the capital: "I don't remember doing it. I was going to plant onions."

Something crossed Wren's face—not pain, exactly, or not only pain. Recognition. The look of someone watching a familiar animal perform a familiar trick. "Onions," they repeated softly.

"There's still time for onions."

"There's still time for onions," Wren agreed, and sat down on the step, and handed over a bun, and for a while they just ate together in the early light while the morning-glories—planted by accident, tended on purpose—opened along their spirals one bloom at a time.

* * *

The shop itself had changed trades, though the sign hadn't. THE CHARM SELLER'S HOURS, the old placard still read, because Ilsa refused to take it down—"People should remember what this place was," she said, in her ward-keeper's voice, the one that brooked no argument about history—and so the sign lied cheerfully above a door that opened onto something else entirely.

There were no charms for sale. There never would be again; Xanthe had sworn it over Juniper's ledger with Hale as witness, in language formal enough that even Hale had nodded. What the shop sold now was harder to inventory. People came in off Lantern Row and sat in the two chairs by the window, and talked, and Xanthe listened. That was the entire service. Some of them were the hollowed ones, the ones the Verdant King's harvesters had emptied on the long roads before the fall—Vell had taken in four hundred of them, and the councils were still arguing about whose responsibility they were, and meanwhile they came to the little shop behind the night market because word had gotten around that the proprietor understood what it was to be missing pieces. Some of them were the opposite: people who remembered too much, who carried wounds they'd been offered the chance to release and had chosen, deliberately, to keep. Those ones Xanthe liked best. They sat with their griefs like old friends and said things like *it's mine, though,* and *I earned it,* and Xanthe would nod and pour the tea and think: yes. That. That's the antidote. Consent. Nobody had ever asked the king's century of victims whether they wished to forget, and so the parasite had grown fat, and so a kingdom had gone green and screaming. Ask first. It was such a small law. It was the whole law.

This morning's first visitor was a mason named Odell who had lost his brother in the hedgerow fighting and could not stop dreaming of vines. He didn't want the dream gone. He wanted, he said, twisting his cap in his big hands, to know whether wanting to keep a bad thing was sane.

"It's the sanest thing there is," Xanthe told him. "The right to forget only matters if the right to remember comes first."

Odell blinked at that, then laughed—a wet, surprised laugh—and stayed for a second cup, and left lighter, and Xanthe watched him go and marveled, not for the first time, that this was the work. Five years of taking. Now this. The ledger balanced differently than they'd ever imagined.

Between visitors, they caught themselves humming again. The rising-question song. Third time today.

Juniper would have catalogued it. In fact, Juniper *had* catalogued it—or tried to. Two months ago they'd come to the shop with a slate and that archivist's squint and said, "The recovered-memory returns are showing anomalies. Residue. Fragments that lodged outside the rite's channel. Songs, mostly. Gestures. One man in Saltrow can't stop saluting with the wrong hand. Do you experience—"

"Yes," Xanthe had said.

"—residual patterns. Of course you do." Juniper had written for a while, then looked up. "Do you find it unbearable?"

And Xanthe had considered the question honestly, the way they tried to consider everything now, having learned what it cost to consider nothing. The fragments were strange. The spirals were strange. Waking at dawn with their hand curled around empty air on the far side of the bed was stranger than all of it. But unbearable—

"No," they'd said. "It's like... echoes. You know how the cisterns echo? The words are gone but the shape of them stays in the stone a while." They'd shrugged. "My stone remembers the shape."

Juniper had written that down too. *Stone remembers the shape.* It was in the Archive now, filed under principles, which meant that somewhere in Vell a rebel archivist had formally recorded that love behaves like an echo, and Xanthe had signed as witness, and neither of them had remarked on how far they'd both traveled from the cistern where it started.

* * *

Midday brought Ilsa, because midday usually did.

She arrived the way she arrived everywhere—announcing herself by the sound of boots and the smell of cut pine, since she was forever hauling lumber for the new border works. The ward-trees were her obsession now, her penance and her masterpiece both: a living boundary grown from saplings that had volunteered, in Juniper's careful terminology, rather than been conscripted. She'd walked the old battlefield south of the city for a week before planting began, they said, and had come back quieter, and had said only one thing about it: "The ivy's just ivy now. Somebody ought to tell it that it's allowed."

She dumped a bundle of stakes inside the gate and stood looking at the flowerbeds with her arms folded, in the posture of a woman conducting an inspection she already knew the results of.

"The spirals again," she said.

"You've seen them."

"I've seen every garden in four districts sprout spirals." She said it without accusation, which from Ilsa was a kind of tenderness. "Bakers' windowsills. Barracks courtyards. A tavern in Lowbridge has one chalked behind the bar. Nobody who draws them knows why. Juniper's calling it a cultural residue pattern." She snorted. "I'm calling it what it looks like. It looks like a signature."

Xanthe turned a morning-glory leaf over in their fingers. "A signature of what?"

"That's not the interesting question." Ilsa crouched, joint by joint, and studied the bed with a warden's eye—she still looked at everything as if checking it for cracks. "The interesting question is what the pattern does. Wards are just patterns that do something. I spent twenty years learning that. So." She tapped the flat stone at the center of the largest spiral, once, gently, like knocking on a friend's door. "Whatever this is, it's holding something. Or keeping something out. Or—" and here she glanced sideways at Xanthe with terrible mildness, "—inviting something in."

After she'd gone, Xanthe sat on the step a long while, the tea going cold, looking at their own garden as if it belonged to a stranger. Inviting something in. The spirals faced the door now. They'd turned them without knowing. Toward the door Wren came through every day.

Their chest did the thing it did sometimes—a slow warm pressure, like a hand pressing flat against the inside of the ribs—and they let it, because fighting it had proven pointless and because, privately, in the ledger they kept where no archivist could read it, they suspected the pressure was the truest thing left in them.

* * *

Evening. Shop closed. Lamplit.

Wren stayed late these nights more often than not, ostensibly to mend things—the fence, the shutter, a chair that had been perfectly fine until Wren decided it wobbled. Tonight they sat on the back step in the last of the light, mending nothing, watching Xanthe water the spirals.

"Sing it again," Wren said.

Xanthe's watering hand paused. "Sing what?"

"The song. You're always humming it. I never said—" Wren's jaw worked. "I never told you this, but it's a real song. It's not one you made up. My mother sang it. The rising part at the end of each line? That's where she'd change the words to be about whoever was in the room. She sang it to me. And then—" a breath, held and released with visible care, "—and then later, someone else sang it to me. Different words. Same rise."

Xanthe set down the watering can. The evening had gone very quiet, the particular quiet of a moment deciding what it wants to become.

"Wren."

"Don't." Wren raised a hand, not sharp, just steady. "Don't apologize. You apologize every time we get near it and I need you to hear me for once instead." They turned on the step to face the garden, facing away, which made the words easier for both of them and they both knew it. "I spent the first months furious. Then I spent months sad. And somewhere in there I figured out the thing I actually believe, which is this: you gave that memory away to save a king, and saving the king saved everyone, including me, including you. I'm not angry at the math. I'm angry at the universe for making the math necessary. Those are different angers and only one of them has anywhere to go."

"And where does it go?"

"Nowhere. It sits. Like a guest who won't leave." Wren huffed, almost a laugh. "But here's the other thing. Here's the thing I figured out watching you turn your flowerbeds toward doors and hum my mother's song and leave the second cup out every single morning without knowing why you do it." They turned back around, then, and their eyes in the lamplight were wet and absolutely level. "The rite took the memory. Fine. The memory's gone; I accept it; I've grieved it. But Xanthe—you *do* all of it anyway. Every day. Unprompted. Unremembered. Whatever part of you loved me, it didn't live in the part that got given away."

The pressure in Xanthe's chest rose until it hurt.

"I can't feel it," Xanthe whispered. "I want to be fair to you. I want to be fair to what you gave. And I can't—I reach for it and there's just the hole, the shape of it, and—"

"I know. I know the shape." Wren's voice cracked and steadied. "I live next to the shape too. But watch." They reached out and took Xanthe's hand—dirt-stained, cold from the watering can, unremarkable—and laced their fingers through, the way you'd done ten thousand times if you were the person Xanthe couldn't remember being to them.

And something answered.

Not a memory. Nothing so neat, nothing that could be bottled or sold or entered into a ledger. Just the body's old arithmetic: warmth meeting warmth, grip answering grip, a settling in the sternum like a key finding a lock it had cut itself. Before thought. Before recall. The chest-knowledge arriving ahead of the mind's, the way dawn arrives ahead of the sun.

"Oh," Xanthe said. It came out very small.

"Yeah," Wren said. "That."

They sat like that as the stars came out—all of them, every night, and Xanthe still caught themself counting, still marveled, probably always would—and the morning-glories closed their blooms in their patient spirals, and somewhere across the city Juniper was lighting lamps over the Archive of Given Things, and Ilsa's young ward-trees were breathing their slow permission along the borders, and a mason was sleeping vine-free for the first time in weeks.

"Stay for dinner?" Xanthe asked. "I'll cook. Fair warning: I keep making something with lemon in it. No idea why. It's apparently non-negotiable."

"Lemon," Wren said, and their smile was a ruin and a repair all at once. "Yeah. You always—"

They stopped. Corrected course, gently, the way they'd learned to. "Sounds good," they said. "Lemon sounds good."

They went inside together, hand still in hand, and behind them the garden held its spirals open toward the door, keeping whatever it kept, inviting whatever it invited, tending itself the way love tends: without memory, without reason, without end.

And if the pattern was a signature—if some deep root of the world had signed its work in morning-glories and rising songs and second cups set out at dawn—then let it sign. Xanthe had been a thief of memories and a giver of them, and had learned at last the only law worth keeping: ask first, hold gently, pay standing up.

Everything else, the chest already knew.

It had known all along.
