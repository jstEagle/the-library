## 3. The Book That Writes Itself

The restricted stacks of the Vell Municipal Archive lived in the basement, which was less an architectural decision than a theological one. The city that hated magic kept its oldest books below ground, below the water table, below even the sewers, as if depth itself were a kind of forgetting. Wren had worked down there for six years and had never entirely shaken the feeling that he was a gravedigger who tended graves that refused to stay full.

He descended at half past nine, after closing, with a lamp and a ring of keys and the particular restlessness that had followed him home from dinner.

Dinner. He turned the word over as he took the stairs two at a time, his hand trailing the cold iron rail. Three weeks of dinners now, three weeks of a man named Coral Sandoval who sold luck-knots from a stall on Amber Row and laughed like someone who had practiced laughing quietly for a very long time. Wren knew what that practice looked like. He'd spent most of his own life learning to be unremarkable on purpose — the archivist's trick, the gray cardigan of professions. Two careful people, orbiting each other at a polite distance, both pretending they weren't starving.

But tonight Coral had said something strange. Over the last of the noodles, chopsticks idle, he had looked at Wren with an expression Wren couldn't file anywhere — not fondness exactly, not fear, but some alloy of the two — and said, *Do you ever get the feeling you've done something before? Not déjà vu. Something older than that.*

And then he'd paid the bill too quickly and walked Wren to the archive steps and kissed him once, briefly, like a man setting something down before it could be taken from him.

Wren reached the bottom of the stairs. The stacks breathed out their familiar smell: vellum and dust and the faint mineral tang of old iron gall ink, which was blood-adjacent if you thought about it too long, so he tried not to. He hung his coat on the hook by the ledger desk, signed himself into the night register — *Hollis, W., restricted access, cataloguing* — and stood for a moment in the lamplight listening to the building settle above him.

The manuscript was called, provisionally, *Codex Vellum 114*, because whoever had bound it a century ago had neglected to give it a name, or perhaps had given it one and the name had been scraped away. It had arrived in the archive eleven days ago in a crate of salvaged material from a flooded private collection — drowned-paper work, Wren's specialty, though the irony of that phrase sat differently with him tonight. The codex was a mess. Water-stained, rebound twice, its quires out of order, and stranger than all of that: the ink was still moving.

Not moving-moving. He'd checked. Under magnification, under raking light, under every instrument the conservation lab possessed, the text sat perfectly still. But page by page, over the course of eleven days, new lines appeared where no lines had been. Faint at first, like watermarks surfacing. Then darkening to proper ink. The senior conservator had blamed humidity. The junior conservator had blamed the senior conservator. Wren had privately begun keeping a log, because logging things was what he did instead of panicking, and the log showed a pattern he did not like: the book was writing forward. One day ahead. Never more.

Tonight was the eleventh day. Tonight's pages should have surfaced this afternoon.

He found the codex where he'd left it, cradled in a foam cradle inside a locked case, and carried it to the reading table beneath the lamp. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was one of the few things about himself he'd never had cause to doubt.

He opened to the marked folio.

The new lines had come up dark and sure, in a hand that matched the rest of the manuscript — a cramped, slanted secretary script, the kind used by working scribes, not courtiers. Wren read the first line and felt the temperature of the room change.

*On the eleventh night, the keeper of dead words shall descend beneath the city of denial, and by lamplight he shall read what waits for him.*

His name was in the next line. Not *a keeper of dead words*, not *an archivist*. His name. *Wren Hollis.* In a book bound before his grandfather was born.

He sat down. He hadn't decided to sit; his legs had simply filed the request and approved it without him. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere overhead, a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, and Wren became aware that he was holding his breath, and let it out, and read on.

*He shall carry with him the taste of broth and the memory of a kiss given too quickly, and he shall believe these things are his own.*

The taste of broth. The noodle shop on Tanner's Cut, the one with the steam-fogged window and the owner who always gave them extra scallions because she'd decided they were a couple and would not be argued with. The kiss on the archive steps, given too quickly — he had thought that exact phrase, or near enough, standing there in the cold while Coral walked away with his collar up. *Given too quickly.* Who said that about their own kiss except afterward, in the privacy of their own head?

Wren turned the page with both hands, the way the training manuals insisted, as if procedure could protect him from what procedure could not even name.

*Beside him sits the storm-caller wearing a borrowed name, selling small charms in increments too small to hang him, loving at half-volume because full volume is how his mother found him the first time.*

Coral.

The lamp flickered. Or Wren blinked. He honestly couldn't have sworn to which, and that frightened him more than the sentence did, because Wren Hollis was a man who could swear to things. He had testified in three provenance disputes. He could distinguish four inks by smell alone. Certainty was his native country, and he was suddenly very far from its borders.

*Storm-caller.* He mouthed the word without voicing it. Magic was illegal in Vell — not discouraged, not taxed, *illegal*, struck from the civic code in the same reform generation that had torn down the old temples and paved over the river-shrines. Everyone knew the word the way everyone knew the word for plague. You didn't expect to meet it across a table, holding your hand over the noodle-shop steam, telling you about the weather with genuine tenderness because you'd mentioned your knees ached.

But it wasn't the accusation that made his chest go tight. Accusations could be wrong. Manuscripts could be wrong; forgery was practically a genre. It was the next line that undid him:

*The keeper will not run. This is the part history gets wrong. He will take the storm-caller's hand in the dark of the stacks and say: show me.*

Wren looked at his own hand, resting on the edge of the folio, and understood that he was being read. Not watched — read, the way he read manuscripts, closely, line by line, with attention to what lay between the lines. A hundred years ago someone had sat at some other table with some other lamp and written him down before he existed, and the writing had been *kind*. That was the worst of it. Whoever the scribe was, they had liked him. They had predicted him the way you predict someone you love: accurately, and with hope.

He should photograph the page. Of course he should photograph the page. Documentation was the whole architecture of his life; when the world tilted, you recorded the tilt. He got up on legs that felt borrowed, retrieved the camera from the lock drawer — the archive issued them for condition reports, and he had signed one out that morning for the codex survey, a fact he now clung to like a railing — and came back to the table.

His hands were shaking.

He noticed it the way you notice weather: not immediately, not all at once, but as a fact of the atmosphere that has been true for some time. The camera chattered against the folio's edge, a fine rapid tremor, and he pressed his palm flat on the table until it eased, and it did not ease, and he photographed the page anyway. The shutter clicked. Once, twice, a third time for redundancy, because redundancy was prayer for people like him.

The flash whitewashed the ink, and in the second after the flash died, Wren saw — or thought he saw, and would spend many nights deciding whether he'd seen — a new line surfacing beneath the ones already legible, still pale, still wet-looking, rising through the paper like a body coming up through lake-water:

*Tomorrow he shows the keeper the truth, and the keeper must choose what he already chose.*

He set the camera down very carefully, the way you set down something when you are afraid of what your hands might do otherwise.

Choose what he already chose. He turned the phrase over and found it load-bearing. Because here was the thing he could not stop noticing, the splinter in the whole construction: the prophecy described tonight's dinner verbatim. The broth. The scallions. The kiss given too quickly. Which meant the future in this book was not a forecast. It was a record. Someone had written down what happened, and what happened was happening, and the only difference between the page and the world was that the page was a hundred years early.

So either the book compelled them — in which case every warm thing he felt for Coral Sandoval was transcription, and his love was a copyist's error faithfully reproduced —

Or the book merely knew. In which case it knew everything else, too. It knew what he would do tomorrow. It knew what he would do when Coral told him whatever truth was waiting behind that strange alloyed expression, that look of a man setting something down before it could be taken. And the book said he would not run. The book said he would say *show me.*

Did he want that to be true because it was written, or was it written because it was true? Was there any longer a seam between those two sentences? Wren pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and saw the ghost-image of the page burned there in negative, white letters floating in black.

Above him, the building settled again. The pipe knocked. And beneath the knock, fainter, rhythmic, almost patient, he heard — or invented, and would also spend nights deciding — a sound like rain beginning against a roof very far above, though the sky had been dry for a month and the drought was the talk of every market stall on Amber Row, including one particular stall, including one particular man who sold luck and weathered smiles and, apparently, storms.

Wren lowered his hands. He looked at the codex, at the newest line still darkening toward certainty, and he made the only decision available to a man whose future had already been typeset: he logged it. Date, time, folio, condition, observation. His handwriting came out steadier than his hands deserved. At the end of the entry, after the standard notation, after the accession number, he wrote one line that belonged to no protocol the archive had ever taught him:

*It knows me, and it is gentle about it. I don't know which frightens me more.*

Then he closed the case, killed the lamp, and climbed the stairs toward the dry night air of a city that believed in nothing it couldn't pave over — carrying a photograph of tomorrow in his pocket, and the growing, vertiginous understanding that in three weeks of careful, half-volume happiness, he had fallen in love with a man whom history had already finished writing.

There was only one person alive who could tell him whether the rest of the pages were true.

And Coral had said, over the noodles, in that voice like a man confessing to a crime he hadn't committed yet: *Come by the stall tomorrow. There's something I should probably show you.*
