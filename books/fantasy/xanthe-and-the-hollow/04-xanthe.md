## 4. Xanthe

Coral had spent the whole night deciding what to destroy.

That was how he thought of it, lying awake in the narrow room above his stall with the charm-scraps sorted into little paper envelopes on the table beside him — not *what to tell Wren*, but *what to burn*. Because telling was burning. Every secret given away was a small arson committed against the life he'd built, and you could never un-burn a thing, only stand in its ashes afterward and pretend you'd meant the warmth.

He'd laid the possibilities out like inventory. Option one: say nothing, let Wren come by, sell him a luck-knot, watch him leave with his canvas satchel and his ink-stained fingers and go back to being the beautiful stranger who took his noodles with extra vinegar. Safe. Half-volume. Ache forever. Option two: show him nothing and everything — the stall's false bottom, the letters from a dead country he kept in a tin that had once held throat lozenges, the name. Just the name. Let Wren do with it whatever archivists did with dangerous words.

Option three, which he did not allow himself to think about in any structured way but which arrived anyway at 3 a.m. wearing the face of every dream he'd had since he was nineteen: tell him all of it. The kingdom. The sinking. The mother. The running.

By dawn he had settled, characteristically, on a fourth option he hadn't planned: panic mildly, open the stall late, and reorganize his entire inventory three times while pretending this was a normal Tuesday.

Wren came at half past nine, which was early for him — Coral had learned his rhythms over three weeks of stolen lunches and one long dinner, and Wren was an eleven o'clock man, someone who moved through mornings like a man wading upstream. At half past nine he should have been still asleep or still reading. Instead he stood at the mouth of Copperlane Alley with his satchel clutched to his chest like a shield and his hair doing something uncharacteristically unsupervised, and Coral knew, before a word passed between them, that last night in the archive had not been a fever or a forgery or a joke.

"You found it," Coral said. Not a question. His voice came out flat, which was the voice he used for prices and other things he couldn't afford to feel about.

"I need to sit down," Wren said.

Coral flipped the sign on the stall to CLOSED, which he had never once done during trading hours in four years of trade, and pulled out the stool he kept for himself behind the counter. Wren sat on it the way people sat in waiting rooms. His hands were not steady. Coral watched them try to be and fail, and something in his chest went tight and warm and terrible all at once, because he realized he wanted to reach out and hold those hands still, and wanting that openly felt more dangerous than anything the Ministry had ever done to him.

"Tea?" Coral offered, because he was a coward, and because his hands needed a job.

"No." Wren laughed — a single startled note, nothing like humor. "No, if I stop moving I'll talk myself out of this. I've been talking myself out of it since midnight. I photographed the page. I told myself it was a trick of the light, then a hoax, then — do you know how old that manuscript is? The binding alone predates the Vellish Printing Compact by forty years. You can't hoax vellum, Coral. The ink has *oxidized*. Whoever wrote it wrote it a hundred years ago, and whoever wrote it—" He stopped. Breathed. Reached into his satchel with the care of a man defusing something.

Coral made tea anyway. It gave him something to do with his traitorous hands while Wren laid the photograph on the counter between them, face up, weighted at the corners with two brass charms Coral sold as *decorative* and were, in fact, minor wards against eavesdropping. He hadn't decided to place them there. His body had decided for him. Ten years of exile lived in the reflexes.

The photograph showed a page. Old script, cramped and certain, the kind of handwriting that belonged to someone who believed absolutely in what they were copying down. And in the middle of it, boxed by a scribe's careful rule:

*And the storm-called boy will sit across from the keeper-of-records among the noodle sellers of the low city, on the third night of the waning moon, and will speak of vinegar and small lucks, neither knowing the book has already eaten their evening.*

Coral read it twice. The tea went cold in his hands.

"That's us," Wren said quietly. "That's — we talked about the vinegar. You said the shopkeeper watered it. I said *all shopkeepers water it, that's the business model.* Those exact—"

"I remember." Coral set the cup down. His hands were steady now, which surprised him; steadiness always arrived at the worst moments, the way courage arrives too late for the battle and just in time for the surrender. "What else does it say?"

Wren hesitated. And Coral watched him hesitate — saw the archivist war with the lover, saw the man who believed information wanted to belong to everyone lose, for one visible moment, to the man who believed some truths should be handled with gloves. Then Wren turned the photograph over. There was a second image beneath it, taken from the following page, and Coral had known — had known since 3 a.m., had known since the noodles, had known in some bone-deep way since the day ten years ago when he'd walked out of the surf on the Vellish coast with salt in his lungs and nothing in his pockets — exactly what it would say.

But knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals. Knowing is a cage; seeing is the animal loose in the room.

*Xanthe Sandoval,* the page named him. *Last heir of the Hollow.*

The world went very quiet, the way it did before weather. Coral heard his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like something turning over in deep water. He heard the alley outside: a cart wheel, a gull, the ordinary indifferent noise of a city that had outlawed his existence without ever learning his name — his real name — because no record of it survived anywhere on earth except in the memory of one exiled man and the mind of one drowned queen.

And now, apparently, in a hundred-year-old book that wrote itself.

"Say something," Wren whispered. "Please. You've gone somewhere I can't follow."

"My mother burned that name out of the world."

It came out of him levelly, almost gently, which frightened him more than shouting would have. He looked at the photograph instead of at Wren. Looking at Wren would have been unbearable; looking at the photograph was merely like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt. It did.

"She didn't just change her own records. She changed *everyone's*. That was her gift — not storms, not the fire she tended, but erasure. She could take a word out of the air and out of the ink and out of the remembering, all at once. When I was born she unmade the name from every register in the Hollow. My birth was announced as *the heir*, capital letters, no name, because a name is a handle, she said, and handles are for doors, and she would not give the sea a door into her son." His mouth curved, and it wasn't a smile, or it was the ruins of one. "She was right about the sea, in the end. She was wrong about everything else."

"Coral—"

"Xanthe." He said it aloud for the first time in ten years, and the syllables tasted like seawater and like a nursery he couldn't quite picture anymore, like a hand smoothing his hair, like the smell of hot glass. "Xanthe Sandoval. She only used it at home, where nothing written could hear. And then the Hollow sank, and she stayed, and I ran, and I picked a name off a fishmonger's sign in Vell harbor because it was plain and common and no one had ever been hunted for being called Coral." He finally looked up. "You're holding the only document in existence that says my name. Do you understand what that means? Someone wrote that a century ago. Before I was born. Before the Hollow fell. Which means either your archive owns a miracle, or—"

"Or the book doesn't write about the past," Wren finished softly. "It writes about whatever it wants. Whenever it wants."

*The book that keeps writing itself.* He'd said it so lightly, over the noodles, the way you mention a strange cat that visits your window. Coral had felt the old storm stir under his ribs at the words and had pressed it down with the practiced weight of a decade's discipline, and now he understood that the storm hadn't stirred because of danger. It had stirred because it recognized home. Weather knew weather. Whatever force moved through that manuscript, it was kin to the thing coiled in his chest, and both of them had been waiting for him without his permission.

He came around the counter. It was only three steps. It felt like crossing a border.

"Wren. Before I tell you the rest — and I'm going to tell you the rest, all of it, I decided at three this morning and then spent four hours pretending I hadn't — you need to know what you're standing next to. Not the prophecy. Me. The prophecy is ink. I'm the actual crime scene."

Wren's mouth twitched despite everything. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Don't joke yet. Save your jokes for after, if there's an after." Coral took a breath, and let the walls down one at a time, like opening a house he'd shut up for winter. "I am — was — the crown prince of the Hollow. Not a metaphor. An actual kingdom, actual throne, actual crown, heavy as sin, I wore it once at my investiture and cried, I was nine. We lived on an island nation three weeks' sail southeast, past the Shattered Shoals. You won't find it on any map in this archive. Not because it's hidden. Because it isn't there anymore. Ten years ago the sea came for it — not a wave, not a quake, something older and patient that my mother said had been owed to us since before the first king — and the Hollow went under in a single night. Eleven thousand people. My mother put me on the last boat with her own hands and stayed behind to hold the door."

"What door?"

"The only one that matters." He was speaking faster now, ten years of silence coming up all at once like floodwater finding a crack, and he couldn't stop it and discovered, to his horror and relief, that he didn't want to. "There's a door out of a country that's died. Out of anywhere that's died, maybe. It opens only while the last dragon-fire burns, and ours was the last dragon-fire left in the world, and my mother stayed in the sunken palace keeping it lit so that the dead would have somewhere to go. I was nineteen and I hated her for not getting on the boat. I still hate her. I still — " His voice cracked, clean through, and he stood there in his own stall among his paper luck-knots and his brass wards, an exile in a city of pavement, and let it crack. "I still light a candle for her every year on the anniversary and tell it to go out. It never does. I think that's the truest magic I've ever done."

Silence. The gull again. Somewhere a neighbor's radio playing the shipping forecast, absurd and mundane, the sound of the invisible life continuing around the crater of him.

Then Wren stood up off the stool, crossed the three steps in his direction, and took Coral's face in both ink-stained hands.

"Okay," Wren said. "Okay. Say the rest. I'm not going anywhere. I want you to know that before the rest, so the rest knows it too."

And that — that was the thing that undid him. Not the prophecy, not the name, not even the touch, though the touch went through him like the first warm rain after drought. It was the *before*. Everyone who had ever learned what he was had learned it as accusation: the Ministry's agents, the border clerks, the smugglers who'd priced his passage in fear. Wren learned it as a fact about someone he'd already chosen, and held the choice out in front like a lantern so they could both see the road.

So Coral told him the rest. The mother, Queen Maren of the House of Salt and Glass, who could erase a word from the world and had erased her son's to save him and had never once, in nineteen years, told him she loved him in any medium more durable than a smoothed blanket. The flight. The Vellish coast. Four years of dock work, two years of learning charms small enough to pass for craft — knots and buttons and blessings measured in grams, magic broken into increments too fine for the law's coarse sieve. The stall. The rules he lived by: never same client twice in a month, never accept thanks aloud, never sleep in the same bed twice in one week until Wren, until three weeks ago, until the ache of half-volume got louder than the fear of full.

"And now the Ministry has a book that names me," he finished, "and a photograph of that page in your pocket, and I don't know which frightens me more — them having it, or me being glad you do."

"Why glad?"

Because it means it's real, he thought. Because a thing witnessed is a thing that happened. Because for ten years I have been a rumor I started about myself, and you are the first person to look straight at me and not look away, and being seen turns out to feel like drowning and breathing in the same breath, and I don't know how to want anything that much safely.

"Because I'm tired," is what he said. His eyes were wet and he let them be, here behind his own counter, in the one place on earth that was his. "Ten years at half-volume, Wren. I sold a bride a luck-knot yesterday and lied to her face about what it was, and she'll have mild good fortune and never know she was touched by the last heir of a drowned kingdom, and I thought that was safety. I thought smallness was armor. And then you sat down across from me with vinegar on your tongue and I caught myself thinking, *I wish he could see all of me,* and wishing to be seen is the single most illegal act in Vell."

Wren huffed a laugh, wet-eyed himself. "We're a pair. I catalog forbidden things for a living and I photograph them with shaking hands."

"Is that guilt I hear, archivist?"

"It's terror wearing guilt's coat. Coral — if the Ministry learns that book exists, they won't come for it with subpoenas. They'll come with *unmaking*. They'll erase the manuscript, the case, the shelf, probably the whole restricted stack, and if they're thorough, the archivist who touched it." He paused, and Coral watched him arrive at the next thought the way you watch someone step toward a ledge. "And the heir the book names."

The word settled over them both. *Unmaking.* Coral had seen its work once, at the border, on a family of hedge-witches whose papers had failed: not killed, not imprisoned — *smoothed*, as though a thumb had rubbed them out of the day. People remembered loving them and could not remember why.

"So we have a choice," Coral said slowly. "Run now, separately, while it's just suspicion. Or assume the book has already told them everything it tells, and decide what we are before they decide for us."

"Or," Wren said, and his grip tightened on Coral's hands, "we read the rest of it. All of it. Tonight. If history has already eaten our dinner, I want to see the menu."

Outside, high above Copperlane Alley, the sky that had been clear all morning developed a single cloud. Small, gray, precise, parked directly overhead like a sentry. Coral felt it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck — not his weather, not called by him, and not natural either.

The storm in his chest rose to meet it, unbidden, and for the first time in ten years he didn't press it down.

"Wren," he said quietly, watching the cloud watch them, "how fast can you get back to that archive?"
