## 6. What the Mother Left

The archive at night was a different country.

Wren had known this for eleven years—since the day Head Curator Vess handed him a lamp with a cracked glass chimney and said, *The stacks don't sleep, so neither do we*—but tonight it felt less like a metaphor and more like a fact. The restricted wing breathed. Its pipes ticked as they cooled. Somewhere above him, a shutter swayed in a wind that shouldn't have reached the third floor of a building with no open windows, and Wren chose, deliberately, not to think about what kind of wind that might be.

He had photographed the page three times already. The images sat on his desk in a tidy row, each one showing the same impossible thing: his own handwriting—not his handwriting, but a description of his own gestures, down to the way he'd tucked his napkin into his collar like an embarrassed child—transcribed a century before he was born by a scribe whose ink had gone the color of dried blood.

*And the archivist will lift the noodle from the broth and hesitate, for he is afraid of burning his mouth, and the storm-son will laugh because he has never once been afraid of anything that could not drown him.*

Wren hadn't told Coral about the hesitation part. Some humiliations were private.

But the dinner wasn't the point anymore. The dinner was page one of something much longer, and Wren had spent two days and one stolen night pulling the rest of it apart like a knot, and what he'd found made his hands shake worse than the photographing had.

The manuscript had no title. The catalog card called it *Fragment, prophetic, provenance unknown, ca. 112 years*, which was the archival equivalent of shrugging. But fragments didn't keep writing themselves. Fragments didn't add verses while sitting in a locked case under a ward-seal that required three signatures to break. Wren had checked the seal log twice. Nobody had opened the case since 1961.

And yet the book grew. Every time he looked, there was more of it—new pages bound in where no binder's hand had ever been, the stitching fresh, the parchment still smelling faintly of the sea.

Tonight he had come back for the middle sections. The ones about the mother.

* * *

He worked the way he always worked: aloud, quietly, the way he'd been taught by a professor who believed that a text read silently kept its secrets. The archive stacks swallowed his voice the moment it left his lips, which suited him. He didn't want anyone else to hear what he was reading, least of all himself.

*"The last dragon burns in the hall of black glass,"* he murmured, tracing the line with one finger, careful not to touch the ink directly. Old rule. Superstitious rule. He followed it anyway; superstition was just scholarship with better survival instincts. *"In the palace beneath the water, where the queen holds the flame against the dark."*

Queen. Not *a* queen. *The* queen. And below that, in a different hand—the book changed hands the way rivers change banks, sometimes mid-sentence, as though its authors were passing it between them like a bucket brigade—

*She has held it nine years. She will hold it ten. She will hold it until her son comes home or until the fire forgets her face, whichever comes first.*

Wren set the lamp down because his hands needed to be empty for a moment.

Coral had told him almost nothing, in the end. That was the strange arithmetic of confession: you expected it to pour out, and instead it came in thimbles. *I'm exiled royalty,* Coral had said, in Wren's apartment, at two in the morning, holding a cup of tea he never drank. *The Hollow. You've heard of the Hollow?* Everyone had heard of the Hollow—it was in schoolbooks, a kingdom that sank in a single night ten years ago, drowned so completely that cartographers had argued for years about whether to keep printing it. *My mother was its queen. I ran. That's the whole story.*

That's the whole story, Wren thought now, staring at the page. Except the story apparently continued underwater, in a hall of black glass, where a woman was holding a fire lit with her bare hands, waiting.

*Waiting for me,* Wren translated, because *her son* was standing right there on the page in the next verse, named—not Coral, never Coral, but *Xanthe, heir of the salt throne, the boy who ran.* Waiting for a man who went by a false name in a city that hated magic, selling luck-knots to nervous brides for coins too small to notice.

Wren had spent three weeks falling in love with a half-volume version of a person. Now the full orchestration was arriving, movement by movement, and it sounded like a funeral march with a coronation hidden inside it somewhere.

He turned the page.

* * *

The middle verses were harder. The language shifted—older, denser, the syntax coiling like rope—and twice Wren had to fetch his reference grammar of Old Hollowic from the restricted cabinet, signing himself in and out with the guilty efficiency of a man doing something mildly criminal. Technically, removing restricted materials without a second curator present was a firing offense. Technically, so was photographing a self-writing prophecy and sending the photographs to your own personal email, which Wren had also done, at three in the morning two nights ago, telling himself it was backup, telling himself it was prudence, hearing underneath both excuses the small true voice that said: *you want proof. Proof that survives if the book changes its mind.*

He'd deal with that later. Everything got dealt with later, in Wren's life; it was how a shy man survived in a loud city.

Here was what he could decipher, working line by line, cross-referencing the older hand against a liturgical fragment from the sunken court that some diver had sold the archive in disgraceful condition forty years ago:

There was a door. Of course there was a door—prophecies loved doors—but this one had terms, and the terms were written with the precision of a contract, which unsettled Wren more than any thunderclap imagery could have.

*"A door stands out of the dead country,"* he read, translating aloud, stumbling only twice. *"It does not stand in walls. It is not made by hands. It opens once in an age, and only—"*

He stopped. Read it again. Checked the grammar against the liturgical fragment, checking the tense, checking the conditional particle that Old Hollowic used for things that were true only within a window of time.

Only while the last dragon-fire gutters.

Not while it burned. While it *guttered*. There was a difference, and the difference was a knife: a fire that burned steadily kept its door shut. A fire in its final failing—a flame already sinking toward its own dark, down to its last breaths—that was when the door stood open. The prophecy did not promise escape. It promised escape *at the exact moment everything was ending*. You could leave the dead country only in the act of losing it.

Wren sat back in his chair and listened to the archive breathe.

It was, he realized, a beautifully cruel piece of drafting. Whoever wrote it—or whatever wrote it, because Wren was increasingly unwilling to grant authorship to any living hand—had built a trap out of hope itself. Any heir who came looking for rescue would find the door, and the door would be open, and the price of walking through it would be watching the light go out behind them. You couldn't save the kingdom and leave it. You couldn't leave it and save it. The two verbs refused to hold hands.

And the fire—the last dragon-fire, the phrase repeated through the verses like a heartbeat—was not in some convenient shrine. It was in the ruins of the palace. Underwater. In the hall of black glass. Where the queen held it lit.

Where the queen had, by every account Wren could find afterward in the disaster archives, died.

Except the book disagreed with the accounts. The book, in its newer hand, insisted she was still holding it. Nine years. Ten. *Until her son comes home or until the fire forgets her face.*

Wren thought about what it meant to hold a fire for ten years. He thought about arms. About patience. About love shaped like a duty so heavy it became indistinguishable from punishment, and whether the queen had chosen it or simply never been offered anything else, and whether there was a difference.

Then he thought about Coral, and the ache arrived sideways, the way real grief always does—not at the fact but at the texture of it. A boy who ran from a drowning kingdom. A mother who stayed in it, keeping one small flame alive against the entire weight of the sea, and the message implicit in her staying, the message she may never have spoken aloud because queens don't speak such things: *you left, and I am still here, and every year I hold this longer is another year you owe me.*

Duty arrives wearing love's face. Wren wrote that in his notebook, then crossed it out, then wrote it again smaller. It was the truest sentence he'd produced in months and it wasn't even his—it belonged to whoever was feeding the book its verses, some ghost-hand or future-hand or the sea itself taking dictation.

* * *

He lost track of time, which in the archive was easy and, Wren suspected, occasionally deliberate—the stacks hoarded hours the way misers hoarded coins. When he finally surfaced, the lamp had burned low and his neck had stiffened into a question mark and the notebook held four pages of translation, diagrams, and one marginal note in furious capitals: **THE DOOR IS OPEN NOW OR NEVER — THE FIRE IS DYING *NOW***.

Because that was the other thing. The verses had a tense, and the tense was urgent. Older prophecies lounged in their futures like cats; this one leaned forward. *Gutters. Fails. Last breaths. Comes home.* The window wasn't someday. Reading the draft of the newest pages—which had grown overnight, stitching fresh, smelling of brine—Wren understood that the door was not merely theoretically open. It was open *on a countdown*. Days, maybe. The fire had burned ten years in a drowned hall; nothing about that arithmetic suggested it had many more.

Which meant Coral had perhaps a week to decide whether to walk back into the country that had broken him, confront the mother whose silence had been louder than any scolding, and pay the toll the prophecy demanded: the death of everything he'd been promised he'd inherit.

No wonder he sold luck-knots, Wren thought. No wonder he lived at half-volume. When the full score sounds like this, who wouldn't choose the quiet?

He gathered the photographs, the translations, the borrowed grammar, and then—after a long argument with himself that he lost—he copied the key verses out by hand onto loose paper and folded them into his coat pocket. Backup. Prudence. The small true voice didn't bother lying to him anymore.

On his way out, past the ward-sealed case, he paused. The book lay closed inside, patient as a cat that has heard the can opener. Through the glass, the newest page was visible, and Wren read the first line upside down before he could stop himself:

*The archivist understands the terms. The archivist will tell him anyway, because someone must carry the truth, and truth needs a coward's courage to survive the road.*

"Coward," Wren said to the empty stack room, without heat. "Thanks."

The pipes ticked. The wind touched the shutter again, high above, and this time Wren let himself think it: *that's not weather. That's attention.* Something knew he'd finished reading. Something was pleased, or hungry, or both.

* * *

He found Coral at dawn, asleep on the floor of his own stall, curled among unsold charms like a man sheltering in the wreckage of his livelihood. The Ministry raid had been two days ago; the stall had been "inspected," its inventory scattered, its license flagged for review. Coral had rebuilt it overnight with the stubbornness of a man arranging deck chairs, but he hadn't slept properly since—Wren could see it in the bruised skin under his eyes even before he woke.

Wren crouched beside him and put his hand on his shoulder, and Coral came awake the way soldiers do—all at once, no transition, one hand closing on Wren's wrist hard enough to hurt before recognition softened it.

"You're shaking," Coral said, voice rough with sleep. "You never shake. You alphabetize when you're scared. What did the book say?"

So much for easing into it. Wren sat down on the floorboards beside him, in the gray light, among the little knotted charms that were each technically a crime, and told him all of it. The hall of black glass. The queen and her ten-year vigil. The door with its obscene terms—open only while the last light failed, passable only by surrendering the country it led out of. The countdown in the tense.

"The fire is guttering," Wren finished. "Now. The book says the door won't wait for you to feel ready. It says—" and here his voice finally betrayed him, cracking on the simplest word— "it says your mother is still holding it."

For a long moment Coral said nothing. The market around them began its morning noises—shutters banging open, carts rattling, the first vendors calling prices—and none of it touched the small sealed space where the two of them sat.

"She told me the fire was ceremonial," Coral said at last, very quietly. "When I was young. A lamp we kept lit for tradition. She said—" His jaw worked. "She said it would outlive us all. She let me believe it was safe to run. Or she let me believe running was safe. I've never been able to untangle which."

"Maybe she didn't know herself," Wren said gently. "Maybe holding a fire for ten years teaches you things you can't say to a boy who's already halfway out the door."

"She's dead, Wren. The histories all say—"

"The histories are wrong." Wren took out the folded pages, the copies in his own hand, and pressed them into Coral's palms. "Or they're early. I can't tell anymore. But the book isn't, and you know it isn't—you felt it write itself across our table. She's down there, Coral. In the dark, in the water, keeping one flame alive, and the only door out of everything she lost opens for a handful of days and then closes forever."

Coral looked down at the pages. His thumb moved over the words *hall of black glass* the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"And if I go," he said, "and if I walk through that door, I have to let it die. All of it. Her, the throne, the name—everything. That's the toll. That's what your beautiful terrible book says."

"That's what it says."

"So either I stay here and live small and safe while the last light of my country goes out without me—" His voice caught, steadied. "—or I go home and attend its funeral. Those are my options. Those are the options my own inheritance left me."

Wren wanted, desperately, to offer him comfort, and found he had none to give that wouldn't be a lie. So he offered the only honest thing: he took Coral's hand, the one not clutching the pages, and held it.

"I'm coming with you," he said. "Before you argue. I've read every word of that prophecy, I'm the only person alive who can translate the rest of it, and I have already decided, so any breath you spend arguing is a breath wasted."

Coral laughed—a short, wrecked sound, half sob. "You hate boats."

"I hate boats," Wren agreed. "I'll hate this boat specifically."

Outside the stall, the morning crowd thickened, ordinary people buying bread and complaining about the drought, and somewhere among them—Wren felt it rather than saw it—something attentive had stopped to watch. He didn't turn his head. He squeezed Coral's hand once and said the thing that would send them both into the deep water ahead:

"We need a smuggler. Do you know anyone crazy enough to sail to the Hollow?"
