## 7. Smuggler's Terms

The harbor of Vell smelled the way all secrets eventually did: like fish left too long in the sun, and beneath that, like tar, and beneath *that*, faintly but unmistakably, like salt water that had passed through too many hands to still belong to the sea.

Coral pulled his collar up against the wind coming off the Graywater. The docks at low tide were a country unto themselves—ropewalks and net-menders' stalls, the crusted hulls of trawlers, and past them, where the pilings went rotten and the lamplight gave up, the territory of people who did not want to be found by anyone with a ledger.

He had dressed down for it. His good coat stayed home; this one had been bought secondhand and aged deliberately, rubbed with ash at the elbows until it looked like ten years of honest poverty instead of three weeks of careful performance. Wren walked beside him with his satchel clutched to his chest like a shield, which, Coral supposed, in a way it was. Inside it: the photographed pages, a hand-drawn chart of the drowned coast copied from an atlas forty years out of date, and every coin they had between them, which was not enough.

"You're sure about this person?" Wren murmured. His voice had the archivist's habit of dropping even lower when he was nervous, as though the harbor itself might shush him.

"Ossa Ferrow," Coral said. "One arm, one boat, no loyalties she'll admit to. She ran refugees out of the Hollow during the sinking. Eleven crossings before the Ministry caught her at it."

"What happened?"

"They took her arm." He said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "Unmaking doesn't leave scars. It leaves absences. She was lucky it was only the arm."

Wren was quiet for three steps. Then: "And she'll take us anyway? To the place that cost her?"

Coral watched a gull hang motionless over the black water, riding a wind that didn't exist anywhere else on the dock. "People who've already lost something to the Ministry aren't afraid of it anymore. They're just angry. Anger can be paid."

"Is that what you believe, or what you're hoping?"

"It's what I'm paying for," Coral said. "Hope I keep separate."

* * *

They found her boat at the end of a rotting pier that groaned like an old man asked to remember his youth. She was called the *Kindly Tern*, though the paint on her stern had faded until the name read more like a question than a claim. Ossa herself sat on a coil of rope mending a net one-handed, the stump of her right arm moving with the rest of her in a rhythm so practiced it seemed less like an absence than a third limb folded away somewhere.

She was smaller than Coral remembered, or perhaps memory had made her larger, the way it does with people who once stood between you and drowning. Grey hair cut short as a hedge. A face weathered into topography. When she looked up at their approach, her eyes went through Coral's disguise the way a hook goes through bait—finding the shape underneath without comment.

"Well," she said, in a voice like gravel poured slow. "Ten years, and you come back looking like a beggar. Sit down before the pilings do it for you."

"You know me," Coral said. Not a question.

"I ferried eleven boatloads of screaming children out of a kingdom while it went under the waves. You think I'd forget the crown prince's face because he grew a beard and learned to slouch?" She tied off her knot and set the net aside. "Sit."

They sat. The pier complained. Somewhere behind them, a drunk was singing something patriotic and getting the words wrong.

"I need passage to the drowned coast," Coral said. "The old capital. And I need it quiet, and I need it soon."

Ossa laughed—one dry bark, like a seal objecting. "Quiet. To the Hollow. That's rich. Boy, that coast isn't quiet, it's *silent*. There's a difference. Quiet means nothing's making noise. Silent means everything's holding its breath." She leaned forward. "Why?"

Coral glanced at Wren. This was the part he hadn't rehearsed, because there was no rehearsing it. "Because my mother died there keeping something lit, and I have reason to believe it's still burning. And because there's a door that only opens while it does."

"A door to where?"

"That," Coral said, "I don't fully know yet. Out. Anywhere out."

Ossa studied him for a long moment, the way sailors study weather. Then she looked at Wren, taking in the satchel, the ink-stained fingers, the particular terror of a man who has spent his life among books suddenly standing at the edge of one.

"And you. Archivist. You're the one who found the book."

Wren started. "How—"

"Ministry criers have been on the docks since yesterday morning. Asking after 'two persons of interest connected to restricted materials.' They're offering fifty crowns for information." She smiled without warmth. "I could retire on fifty crowns. Instead I'm sitting here listening to you, which tells you something about how much I love the Ministry. But it also tells you your time's run out. If they're offering rewards, they're close. If they're close, they've already got someone watching the charter captains. So it can't be a charter. It has to be me, and it has to be tonight, and it is going to cost you more than coins."

"We have thirty-one crowns," Wren said quietly.

"Thirty-one crowns buys you a rowboat and a prayer." Ossa spat neatly over the edge of the pier. "What I'm selling costs something else. Stand up."

Cordial confusion, then compliance. She circled them slowly, and Coral realized with a cold drop in his stomach that she wasn't inspecting them—she was inspecting *him*, specifically, the way he stood, the set of his shoulders, the way the gulls had gone quiet overhead.

"When we ran the crossings," she said, "there was always one passenger who calmed the water. Didn't matter how bad the swells were. Boats around us capsized; ours didn't. First few times I thought it was luck." She stopped in front of him. "Then I noticed it only happened when you were scared. Luck doesn't get better when you're frightened, boy. Magic does. You've been strangling that thing for ten years, haven't you? Holding it down so small nobody feels it."

"Yes," Coral said. There was no point lying to a woman who had watched children die and kept rowing.

"Good. Because here's my price." She held up her single hand, fingers spread. "Not money. Not favors. I want you to stop strangling it. One time, openly, if it comes to that—if the sea turns on us, or the Ministry finds us—you call what you are and you don't apologize for it. I've spent ten years watching people hide what they are until hiding killed them slower than any Unmaker would. My price is that you promise me you won't die of smallness on my deck."

The wind came off the water and moved nothing in Coral at all, because everything in him had gone still. Ten years of half-volume living, of charms sold in increments too small to notice, of storms swallowed like stones—and this one-armed woman wanted, as her fee, simply that he *exist*.

"That's not a price," he managed. "That's a kindness."

"Don't flatter yourself. It's a practicality. A storm-caller at quarter strength gets us all killed. Full strength might get us all killed too, but at least it'll be interesting." Her eyes glittered. "Besides, I have a professional curiosity. I want to see what the water does when its prince stops pretending."

"He's not—" Wren began.

"He's exactly," Ossa said, "and we both know it. Now. Payment besides the promise. I hear you used to make real charms, back before. Before Vell. Before the hiding."

Coral's hand went, without his permission, to his shirt collar, where a thin cord lay hidden against his skin. On it hung the last true thing he had ever made: a knot of grey storm-silk braided through with a thread of his own hair, bound with salt and sealed under a drop of glass. A luck-knot, but not like the ones he sold brides and fishermen—those were sugar-water, a whisper of intent, deniable. This one was the real craft. It had taken him a month to make, in his first year in Vell, back when his hands shook too badly for fine work and he'd made it anyway, because a man needs one proof that he hasn't entirely unmade himself.

It had kept him alive through things he didn't talk about. Three years of it hanging against his heart, turning misfortune aside in increments large enough to notice if you knew how to look.

"There's a charm," Ossa said, watching his hand. "I can smell it from here. Old magic, worked deep. I want it."

"No," Coral said. The word came out faster than thought.

"Then no crossing."

"It's the last real thing I've made. It's the last real thing I *have* that I made."

"I know," Ossa said, and for the first time her voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "That's why it's worth anything. Everything else you own is a costume, boy. That knot is the last piece of the man you were before you decided to disappear. And that's exactly why I'm asking for it—because the journey you're taking, you can't take as a man in a costume. The sea won't be fooled, and neither will what's waiting on that coast. You want to walk into your mother's kingdom? Walk in carrying nothing false."

The tide sighed against the pilings. Far off across the Graywater, a Ministry patrol boat ran its lamp along the far shore, unhurried, methodical, a finger dragging across a map.

Wren touched Coral's elbow. "You don't have to," he said softly. "We can find another way. Another captain, another port—"

"There isn't time for another anything, and you know it." Coral closed his eyes. Under his fingers, through the fabric, he could feel the knot: warm, faintly, the way it always was, the little stored patience of it, the three years of small salvations.

When he opened his eyes, he untied the cord and drew it over his head.

The absence was immediate and physical, like stepping out of a warm house into winter—not pain, exactly, but the sudden knowledge of exposure. Ten years of luck lifted off him and left him standing in the raw air of probability, undefended, ordinary, *visible*.

Ossa took it with both her hand and her stump cradling it, the way one holds a bird. She turned it over once in the lamplight. Whatever she saw in it—or felt—made her go quiet for a moment.

"Fine work," she said finally. "Better than fine. Your mother's court had charm-smiths; none of them made anything this honest." She tucked it away inside her coat, over her heart, mirroring where it had lived on him. "Paid in full. We sail within the hour, on the turn of the tide. Go home, pack nothing you'd mourn losing, tell no one, and meet me at the third piling past the customs house. If either of you is followed, I leave without you and I don't refund promises."

* * *

They walked back through the dock district in a silence that had texture to it, layers of it—the fear, yes, but underneath that something stranger. Relief, maybe. Or the specific lightness of a man who has just handed away his armor and discovered that underneath the armor was not softness but *self*, unfamiliar and sore as new skin.

At the mouth of the ropewalk, Wren stopped.

"Coral." He never used the false name aloud in public—another of their careful grammars—but here, in the shadow of the nets, he risked it. "That promise you made her. To stop hiding. Did you mean it?"

Coral looked out at the water. The patrol lamp had moved on. Somewhere below the gray surface, a kingdom lay holding its breath, and somewhere above it a woman with one arm was coiling rope and wearing his luck against her heart.

"My whole life," he said, "people have told me what my magic was for. My mother said it was for reigning. The Ministry says it's for erasing. I've spent ten years deciding it was for nothing—for staying small enough to survive." He flexed his bare hands, feeling the wind move across knuckles that suddenly seemed to belong to someone else. "She's the first person who ever asked me what *I* would use it for. I meant it. Gods help me, Wren, I meant it, and I'm terrified."

"Good," Wren said, and when Coral looked at him, the archivist was smiling—that small, crooked, cataloguing smile that had undone Coral from the first week. "Terrified is honest. I've read enough prophecy to know the difference."

They parted at the corner of Salt Lane—separately, always separately now, two threads that couldn't afford to be seen braiding—and Coral walked home through streets that felt different without the knot's quiet guardianship. Every shadow had teeth again. Every stranger might be a ledger with legs. This was what unprotected life felt like; he had forgotten; he had *chosen* to forget.

And yet his hands, as he packed the flat—nothing he'd mourn losing, which took four minutes—were steady.

* * *

The third piling past the customs house, midnight, the tide on the turn. The *Kindly Tern* rode low and dark, no lamps lit, and Ossa stood at her rail like a figurehead that had opinions.

Coral arrived first. Wren came six minutes later, having doubled twice through the fish market, breathing hard, eyes bright with the specific exhilaration of a law-abiding man discovering crime.

"Below," Ossa said. "Both of you. We clear the breakwater before anyone thinks to look at us, and then, my friend, we have a conversation about what exactly is burning in your mother's palace, because I've carried a lot of cargo in my day, and I have never once agreed to carry a fire."

The boat slipped its mooring without sound. Vell fell away behind them, lamplit and lawful and full of people who had never known Coral Sandoval existed—which, he understood suddenly, looking back at it, had been the whole point of the last ten years, and the whole failure of them too.

The harbor bar passed beneath the keel. The open sea took them, gray-black and enormous, and Coral stood at the stern watching his invisible life shrink to a scatter of lights.

Ossa came to stand beside him, one-handed at the tiller, the stump easy at her side. For a long while she said nothing, letting the water talk. Then:

"You should know something before we're too far out to turn back." Her voice was low, pitched under the wind. "In all those crossings, eleven boats of refugees, I learned one thing about the sea around your kingdom. The rest of the ocean is just water. But that stretch—" she nodded ahead, into the dark, toward a horizon with nothing on it at all, "—that stretch *remembers*. The sea remembers its kings, boy. Every wave over that coast knows your family's name. And when we cross onto it tomorrow night, the water is going to know you're there."

Coral looked at the darkness where the Hollow lay drowned, ten years silent, ten years holding its breath.

"Let it," he said.

Far off, where nothing should have been, lightning flickered once beneath the surface of the sea—a pulse of pale green light, deep down, like something vast turning over in its sleep.
