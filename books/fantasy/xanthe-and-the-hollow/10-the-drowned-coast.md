## 10. The Drowned Coast

Coral felt the land before he saw it.

It came up through the hull of Ossa's boat like a voice through a wall—muffled, insistent, familiar in the way that a song is familiar when you haven't heard it since childhood and your body remembers the words before your mind does. He was standing at the bow with his hands on the rail when it happened, watching the gray water slide by, and suddenly his chest tightened around something that had no name except *home*, and he had to grip the wood until his knuckles went white.

"Landfall soon," Ossa called from the tiller, her one arm working the wheel with an ease that made the absence of the other seem like a rumor. "You'll want to see this part yourself, I think."

"I've seen charts," Coral said.

Ossa laughed, short and salt-cracked. "Charts." She said the word the way another woman might say *children's drawings of the moon*.

Wren came up beside him, and Coral made room without thinking about it—the way they'd been making room for each other for weeks now, a choreography neither had rehearsed. The archivist looked terrible in the way that only honest people look terrible after three days at sea: hair flattened by spray, lips chapped, eyes red-rimmed but steady. He still clutched the satchel with the copied prophecy pages against his chest like a breastplate. They had not entirely forgiven each other for what those pages might cost them. But they had stopped flinching when the other moved.

"How are you feeling?" Wren asked.

Coral considered lying. It was his oldest habit, older than exile, older even than the false name—he had been lying about what he felt since before he could write. But three days of open water had worn something smooth out of him.

"Like I'm being pulled," he said. "Not toward something. Backward. Like the sea is reeling in a line it tied to me ten years ago and I've only just now noticed the hook."

Wren didn't offer comfort, which Coral loved him for. Comfort would have been a lie; there was nothing comfortable ahead of them. Instead Wren just put his hand over Coral's white-knuckled one on the rail, and left it there, warm and slightly damp, and together they watched the coast come up out of the morning haze.

* *

The Hollow had not sunk all at once. That was the thing no one in Vell understood, wrapped in their comfortable hatred of magic—they imagined a catastrophe, a single screaming night, a kingdom swallowed whole like a coin dropped down a well. The truth had been slower and stranger. When the last king's line broke its covenant with the deep currents—and Coral had never learned the full shape of that breaking, only fragments shouted through doors during the final months—the sea had not taken the country. It had simply stopped holding it up.

The capital had gone first, over nine days, so gently that half the population refused to leave. Streets flooded ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then to the second-story windows, and still the lamplighters made their rounds in little boats, and still the bakers fired their ovens until the water reached the coals, and the people stood on their roofs waving at each other across what had become canals, insisting it was temporary, insisting the sea would give back what it had borrowed.

Then the sea kept it.

Ten years later, what remained above water was a coastline scoured white. Not beach—*bone*. The salt had eaten everything green and then everything brown and then everything that had ever held a color at all, leaving a shore like the inside of a shell, blinding under the pale sun. Behind the strand, hills rose in terraces that might once have been vineyards, now striped bare as ribs. And everywhere the water lay shallow and glass-clear over the shapes of streets.

Coral saw the first ghost-light while Ossa was still furling the sail.

It began as a smudge in the shallows—a warmth of gold where the water should have been cold green—and then it gathered itself, the way breath gathers on a winter window, into the suggestion of a lantern. Then two lanterns. Then a dozen, drifting in procession along a submerged avenue that Coral recognized with a lurch so violent he nearly sat down in the boat.

That was Lanternmakers' Row. Those were the festival lights of the Rains-Home feast, the one his mother had always hated because it meant speeches, and the one Coral had loved because it meant every lamp in the city burned amber instead of white, and the whole capital looked like it had been dipped in honey.

"They're not real," Ossa said quietly behind him. She had moored the boat in the lee of a sea-stack and was watching his face with a sailor's careful neutrality. "I ferried survivors off this coast for a month after the sinking. Saw them every dusk. Ghost-lights, echo-lights, whatever the priests call them. The place remembers itself. That's all."

"That's not all," Coral said.

He stepped over the gunwale into knee-deep water and did not feel the cold. That was wrong too—he should have felt the cold; the sea here ran dark and deep-fed even in the shallows—but the water received him like a bath drawn for a guest, and somewhere far below, he would have sworn, something exhaled.

"Coral." Wren's voice, tight. He'd followed, satchel held high, trousers soaked to the thigh, looking furious and frightened and unwilling to be either. "The Ministry cutter could have followed us through the storm. We shouldn't linger on open ground."

"There's no open ground," Coral said. "There's no ground at all anymore. Don't you see? That's the whole point of this place. It doesn't exist." He turned, and the light of the ghost-lanterns caught his face, and Wren went quiet, because Coral knew what his face must look like—he could feel it, the cracking open of it. "We're standing in a country that ended, and it's saying good evening to us."

* *

They made camp above the tideline, in the lee of a fallen wall whose carved frieze had survived just enough to break the heart: a line of dancing figures, all of them headless now, still holding their stone instruments, still leaning toward one another as if the music were merely resting between verses.

Ossa built a driftwood fire with her single hand and the economy of long practice. "I'll take first watch," she said. "You two should sleep. Tomorrow we go down to the palace gate, and after that—" she shrugged, a motion that traveled all the way down her missing side, "—after that, none of my terms cover what happens. Smuggler's terms end at the door. What's past the door is yours."

Coral ate the hard biscuit she gave him and tasted nothing. His body was in the camp, but the rest of him had already waded back out into the shallows, where the ghost-lights were thickening as true dusk fell.

He told himself he wouldn't go. He told Wren he wouldn't go. Wren, wisely, did not believe him, and when Coral rose an hour after full dark and walked down the white sand with his boots in his hand, Wren rose too and followed, and neither of them spoke about it.

The procession was waiting.

Up close, the ghost-lights resolved into something worse and better than lanterns. They were *people*—or the memory of people, worn thin as gauze. Figures drifted along the drowned avenue thirty feet below the surface and also somehow just beneath it, both at once, the way a reflection is both on the water and in it. Coral saw a lamplighter on his ladder. He saw a woman hanging washing that had rotted away decades ago, the clotheslines of light swaying in a current that touched nothing real. He saw children playing a game with a ball of woven rush, and he knew the game, knew its counting-song, and the song rose in his throat unbidden and came out as a broken hum.

"Coral." Wren's hand found his elbow. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They're—" Wren hesitated. "Amber. Like the lamps."

Coral closed his eyes. Opened them. Let it be true. "The city knows me," he said. His voice came out strange, doubled, as if the water were repeating it a half-second late. "Or it knows what I was."

"You're more than what you were."

"Am I?" He laughed, and hated the sound. "I spent ten years being less. Less loudly every year. Maybe there's nothing left over."

"Don't," Wren said, sharp enough that Coral actually turned to look at him. In the ghost-light, the archivist's face was fierce and wet-eyed at once. "Don't do that. Don't perform your own erasure for me. I catalogued your life for a week, Coral Sandoval. I read the census of a dead kingdom down to the grain levies. You are not less. You are *documented*."

And despite everything—the drowned city, the mother-shaped dread rising in him like a tide—Coral laughed, really laughed, and the sound skittered out over the water, and some of the ghost-figures turned toward it, slowly, like flowers finding a sun that had moved.

That was when he saw her.

* *

She stood apart from the procession, on a stretch of submerged paving that had been the palace forecourt, and she wore the storm-gray robes of the regnant, and she wore her hair in the coronet braid he had watched her fasten ten thousand mornings of his childhood, and she was looking directly at him with an expression he knew better than any language: the expression that said *you are late, Xanthe.*

"No," Coral said aloud.

The figure did not move. The others did—the lamplighter climbing his endless ladder, the washerwoman, the children mid-game—but she stood fixed, a portrait hung in a gallery of moving pictures, and the distance between them seemed suddenly very small and very vast.

"Coral." Wren again, urgent. "Coral, who is that?"

"You know who that is. You photographed her funeral record."

"The ghost-court tested outsiders before," Wren said, reciting what the prophecy's margins had taught him, what Ossa's rumors had confirmed. "But the texts say the court appears as a *collective*. A chorus. Not—not individuals with faces. Not—"

"Not mothers," Coral finished.

The ghost raised one hand. Not beckoning. Worse: *waiting.* The gesture of a woman who has waited at many doors and windows and harbor walls and has never once doubted that what she waits for will come.

Every year of exile rose in Coral at once, and it did not come as anger, which would have been survivable. It came as homesickness—for the smell of the palace kitchens on feast days, for the specific creak of the third stair in the west wing, for the weight of a crown he'd worn exactly once at fourteen and had secretly practiced wearing in secret a hundred times, for the sound of his mother's voice reading shipping manifests aloud because she thought it would lull him to sleep and it did, it always did, right up until the day her voice said *go and don't come back* and meant it as love, and it had been love, that was the unforgivable part, it had *been* love, sealed and signed and delivered like a sentence.

"I miss you," he said to the ghost, and his voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "I hate that I miss you. You sent me away and I miss you every single day and I don't know how to hold both of those things in one chest."

The ghost tilted its head. And though it did not speak—though its mouth never moved—Coral heard it anyway, or heard the memory of it, or heard the sea wearing the memory like a mask:

*Almost there, Xanthe.*

Behind him, Wren drew a sharp breath. "The flyleaf," he whispered. "Coral, that's the same words. From the notebook. Someone—or something—is writing ahead of us."

"Or behind us," Coral said. "Writing behind us, and we're walking forward into ink that dried years ago."

The ghost-light of his mother lifted her chin—the old command posture, the one that had launched fleets—and the entire procession stilled. Every lantern, every ladder, every ball mid-arc between the ghost-children's hands: frozen. The water itself seemed to stop moving, and the silence that came down over the drowned coast was so complete that Coral could hear his own heartbeat and, faintly, underneath it, another rhythm—deep, slow, patient, rising from the ruins of the palace.

A fire, guttering. Waiting to be tended. Or waiting to die.

His mother's ghost extended her waiting hand a fraction further, and all around her, in the frozen procession, the ghost-lanterns flared—one great silent gasp of amber light—and in that light Coral saw, far out beyond the shallows where the water darkened toward the deep, the black silhouette of the palace towers breaking the surface like the knuckles of a drowned giant.

And on the highest tower, small and stubborn as a candle in a window, a point of light that was not amber at all.

Red-gold. Dragon-fire.

"She's showing us the way," Wren breathed.

"No," Coral said softly. He had not let himself understand it until this moment, standing in his homeland's grave with its dead queen reaching for him, but understanding arrived whole, the way storms arrive, and he was almost calm inside it. "She's showing us what she died protecting. There's a difference." He looked at the waiting ghost, at the hand that had commanded kingdoms and finally, at the end, commanded only *stay away, live, don't come back*—and now commanded the opposite, because dead women get to change their minds.

"Tomorrow," Coral said to her—to all of them, to the whole frozen city of light—"tomorrow we go down to the gate. And whatever you want from me, Mother, you'd better pray it isn't obedience. Because the boy who ran learned things out there. And one of them was how to say no."

The ghost did not lower her hand.

But the tide, all along the bone-white shore, turned early—turning *toward* the palace, drawing them in—and somewhere below the towers, in a brazier of black glass, the last dragon-fire in the world flickered, once, twice, patient.

As if it, too, had received a message.

And was answering.
