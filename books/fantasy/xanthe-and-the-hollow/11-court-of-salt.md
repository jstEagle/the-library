## 11. Court of Salt

The dead did not sleep, and they did not rest. They waited.

Wren understood this now, standing ankle-deep in water that should have been cold and was instead the exact temperature of a bath someone had abandoned mid-soak. The shallows of the drowned capital stretched before him in every direction—streets that had been streets once, their cobbles furred with pale weed, their gutters still running downhill toward nothing. Above the waterline the sky held its last bruised light. Below it, the city went on being a city, lit from within by a soft green-gold glow that came from everywhere and nowhere, and in that glow the court of salt had assembled to look at him.

They stood on the flooded plaza in ranks, perhaps two hundred of them, arranged with the precision of people who had practiced ceremony for centuries and death for ten years and found no difference between the disciplines. Their clothes were court dress of a fashion Wren had only ever seen in plates: high-collared robes stitched with wave-patterns, mantles of grey silk gone translucent at the edges so you could see straight through them to whatever stood behind. Their faces were the worst part. Not skull-faces, not horrors—just faces, human ones, wearing expressions of mild and patient attention, as if Wren were a petitioner who had arrived at an inconvenient hour and would simply have to wait his turn.

Coral knelt in the water three paces ahead of him, and the ghosts were not looking at Coral.

* * *

It had begun the moment they waded past the boundary stones—granite markers still legible after ten years underwater, carved with the Hollow's crest: a door standing open above waves. Ossa's boat sat anchored beyond the shoals; she'd refused to come closer, muttering something about the sea remembering its kings and not wanting to be remembered back. So it was just the two of them, walking into a drowned country like pilgrims into a cathedral, and Wren had felt the change happen the way you feel weather change—a pressure drop, a hush, the hair on his arms rising.

"They know we're here," Coral had said quietly.

"Can they hurt us?"

"I don't know. I've never—" He'd stopped. Started again. "I left when I was fifteen. I've never met them like this."

Then the lights had risen out of the deep streets. Hundreds of them, drifting up through the water like lanterns released from a sunken festival, and where each light settled, a figure condensed around it—silk, skin, a face. The court of the Hollow, assembling itself out of ten years of patient dark, and Wren had watched Coral go white to the lips because among them, here and there, were faces he knew. A tutor. A guard captain. A woman whose resemblance to him stopped Wren's breath until he realized she was too young to be the queen—an aunt, maybe, or an older cousin.

The ranks had parted. And a voice—not loud, but present everywhere the way damp is present, in the stone and the air and the inside of your ears—had spoken:

*The king returns. The king may pass.*

Then the attention of all those dead eyes had slid, deliberate as a searchlight, onto Wren.

*The other does not return,* the voice said. *The other comes to take.*

* * *

Now Wren stood in the bath-warm water with two hundred ghosts watching him and tried to think like an archivist, which was difficult, because what he actually wanted to do was run.

He catalogued his fear first. That was the training: when a collection overwhelmed you, you didn't panic, you accessioned. Item one: terror, acute, onset recent. Item two: the voice, which had no single source. Item three: the fact that Coral hadn't moved to defend him, hadn't spoken—and when Wren glanced at the back of his bowed head, he saw why. Coral wasn't choosing silence. He couldn't speak. His jaw worked; nothing came out. Whatever law governed this court had taken his voice as the price of admission, and Wren understood with a lurch that he was alone in this. Whatever the test was, Coral could not take it for him. No one could.

"The other comes to take," Wren repeated aloud. His voice sounded absurdly small over the water. "That's true. I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

A ripple went through the ranks. Not anger. Something more like interest—the collective lean-in of an audience that had expected flattery and gotten inventory instead.

An old man detached himself from the front rank. He wore the mantle of a chancellor—heavy chain of office, links of black glass, each link engraved with characters Wren itched to examine—and he crossed the flooded plaza without disturbing the water at all, his feet passing through its surface while Wren's broke it. He stopped close enough that Wren could see the fine detail of him: the ghost-light didn't blur him but sharpened him, every wrinkle rendered in luminous thread, his eyes the pale washed-out blue of sea glass.

"You are the archivist." The chancellor's voice was dry as pressed seaweed. "The book wrote you in."

"It did."

"Do you know why our books write themselves?"

Wren considered lying. Considered comforting. Discarded both. "Because a kingdom that ends unfinished doesn't end. It waits for someone to finish the record."

Something moved across the chancellor's face—not a smile, exactly. The memory of where smiles used to go. "The boy who ran has brought us a clerk."

"He brought you an archivist," Wren said. "There's a difference. A clerk writes down what he's told. An archivist asks what happened next."

The pale eyes fixed on him. Behind the chancellor, the whole court had gone very still, the way a room goes still when the answer matters more than anyone will admit.

"Then ask," the chancellor said.

* * *

They gave him three questions to survive, though no one told him that was the shape of it until afterward. What they told him was this: the court would test the outsider, and the outsider would answer, and the answers would be weighed against the truth, and the truth of the Hollow was a heavy thing, heavier than water, and most things floated away from it.

The first riddle came from a young woman near the front, a lady-in-waiting by her dress, her face so vividly bored-beneath-the-formality that Wren thought: she must have been nineteen when it happened. She might still be nineteen. That thought nearly undid him.

She spoke, and her voice was the voice of the whole court speaking through her, layered, choral: *"A door stands open over the water. Who closed the country?"*

Wren knew the trap immediately. Every instinct of courtesy, of diplomacy, of the careful social arithmetic he'd spent twenty-nine years learning, told him to say: the sea took it. The storm took it. Fate. No one. Because the person standing knee-deep behind him was the son of the queen who had held the palace against the flood, and you did not hand a grieving court a name.

But the court had already heard him tell the chancellor there was a difference between a clerk and an archivist. They would weigh his answer against the record. And Wren had spent three weeks falling in love with a man who flinched every time a storm built on the horizon, and he knew—in the way you know things about people you have decided to love—that Coral believed, had always believed, that the drowning was his fault. That the storm that sank the Hollow had answered his adolescent rage, his runaway grief, his fifteen-year-old hands that didn't yet know their own strength.

"No one person closed it," Wren said.

The lady-in-waiting tilted her head, waiting for the retreat, the softening.

"But the country closed itself," Wren went on, "the way a hand closes around something it can't bear to hold open anymore. Your queen chose to stay. The court chose to stay. The magic chose to sink rather than scatter. And a boy ran, because running was the only door left open to him." He made himself hold the ghost's gaze. "You weren't taken. You were kept. There's a difference, and I think you've had ten years to know it."

Silence. The water lapped. Somewhere far below, in the drowned deeps, something vast shifted its weight.

Then the lady-in-waiting smiled—the real thing, sudden and crooked and nineteen years old—and stepped back into the ranks, and Wren exhaled a breath he'd been holding since the boundary stones.

* * *

The second question came from the chancellor again, and it was worse, because it was aimed.

*"The archivist reads. Tell me: what does the prophecy promise your king?"*

"A door out of a dead country," Wren said. "Open while the dragon-fire burns."

"And what does it demand?"

"That's the thing," Wren said slowly. "I've read it eleven times. Photographed it, transcribed it, cross-referenced the dialect against three linguistic periods of Hollow court-speech." He was babbling; he made himself stop. "It demands nothing plainly. But every verse bends toward restoration. Crown, throne, kingdom reborn. If you read it the ordinary way, it promises your king will bring you back."

"Is that the ordinary way?" the chancellor asked softly.

And here was the moment Wren understood what kind of test this was. Because the court wanted to hear yes. He could feel it—the whole gathered weight of two hundred dead souls leaning toward hope, ten years of drowned patience reaching for the word *restored*. Say yes, and he passed. Say yes, and he walked out of this plaza with Coral's hand in his and the blessing of the dead, and everyone got the story they had starved for.

Say yes, and they might all drown twice.

"I don't know yet," Wren said. "That's my honest answer. Prophecies lie by emphasis—they put the crown in verse four and leave the fine print for the end, because nobody reads to the end while the crown's still shining." He looked at the chancellor steadily. "I haven't read to the end. When I do, I'll tell you the truth even if it costs me the passage. That's the only term I can offer you. Truth over comfort. Even yours. Especially yours."

For a long moment the chancellor said nothing. The green-gold light pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

"The last archivist who stood here," he said at last, "was our own. She drowned with the records in her arms, trying to carry them up. We have missed having someone whose first loyalty is to what happened." He turned his head slightly, addressing the ranks without turning his body. "Two trials stand. Then passage, or the deep."

* * *

The third question did not come from the chancellor. It came from everywhere—from the water itself, rising up around Wren's knees in threads of luminescence that braided into words:

*"Why are you here?"*

Not *why have you come.* Why *are* you here. Present tense. Existential.

And Wren, who had spent his whole adult life in restricted stacks answering reference questions with citations, found that he could not reach for a single safe scholarly response, because the water was asking him the question he'd been avoiding since a quiet archivist named Wren had found his own name in a hundred-year-old book and photographed the page with shaking hands.

He thought about the noodle shop. About Coral across the table, laughing at something Wren had said with his whole face, unguarded for exactly four seconds before the dread came back—and how Wren had thought, in those four seconds: *I want to be the reason the dread stays away.* He thought about the stall, the luck-knots, the life lived at half-volume, and how Coral had once said, not quite joking, that invisibility was just loneliness with good discipline.

"I'm here because he let me see him," Wren said. "Not Xanthe. Not the heir. Him—the version that sells luck-knots to nervous brides and pretends his tea isn't over-steeped so he won't embarrass the vendor." His voice cracked and he let it. "I'm here because loving someone visible is a choice you make daily, and I keep making it. And I'm here"—he turned, finally, and looked at Coral kneeling silent in the water, looking back at him with an expression Wren would carry for the rest of his life—"because a kingdom is a thing you're born to, but this—"

He gestured between the two of them, at the small soaked space they occupied in the middle of a drowned nation.

"This is a thing you choose. I'm not taking your king. I'm staying with your boy. And if the door opens, he walks through it as whoever he decides to be—not as who any of us need him to be. Including you. Including the prophecy. Including me."

The water went still. The entire court, two hundred strong, stood motionless in the green-gold light, and Wren braced for judgment, for the deep, for whatever the Hollow did with outsiders who declined to worship.

* * *

What happened instead was this: the court knelt.

Not to him. He realized that almost immediately, with relief that made his knees unreliable. They knelt facing outward, ranks folding down like wheat, and the gesture wasn't homage—it was clearance. The opening of a road. The chancellor remained standing, and he inclined his head to Wren with the precise, economical respect of one professional acknowledging another across an uncrossable gulf.

"Passage is granted," he said. "On the terms stated. Truth over comfort. We will hold you to it, archivist. Before this is done, you will read to the end, and you will tell him what it says, and it will cost you." The pale eyes flicked toward Coral, and something ancient and tired moved through them. "We have waited ten years for someone willing to finish the record. We had begun to fear the country would wait forever."

"What happens now?" Wren asked. His voice came out rough.

"Now the road opens. The palace still stands beneath the eastern channel—what remains of it. The firekeeper keeps the last flame." The chancellor began to fade at the edges even as he spoke, dissolving upward into motes of green-gold light. "Tell the boy who ran: we are not angry that he left. We are angry at how long he has believed we were."

The court rose as one and poured away into the deep streets like water finding its level, and the plaza dimmed, and the bath-warm water cooled by degrees toward honest cold.

Coral found his voice somewhere in the middle distance—it came back to him in a broken gasp, like a swimmer surfacing—and the first thing he said was Wren's name, and the second thing was "you absolute madman, you told the court of the Hollow the truth," and the third thing he didn't say at all, because he crossed the space between them and held on, both of them shivering in the cooling shallows while the last of the ghost-lights drifted home beneath the waves.

"You passed," Coral said into his shoulder. "How did you—you told them the prophecy might not mean what they hoped. To their faces."

"They deserved better than a comfortable lie." Wren pulled back enough to look at him. "So do you. Which is why, when we get to the palace, I'm reading the final verses first. All of them. Before either of us takes another step toward that door."

Coral went still. "You think there's something in them."

"I think," Wren said, "that the chancellor called you *the boy who ran*, and the firekeeper is waiting for *the boy who ran*, and nobody in ten years has called you *king*." He turned toward the eastern channel, where the drowned ruins waited in the gathering dark, a faint red glow pulsing deep beneath the water like a heart deciding whether to keep beating. "And I think prophecies that hide their endings are hiding them for a reason."

The red glow pulsed again—fainter now, guttering—and far off across the black water, Ossa's anchor lamp blinked twice: *trouble.*
