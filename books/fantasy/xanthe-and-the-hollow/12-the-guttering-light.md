## 12. The Guttering Light

The palace had been beautiful once. Coral knew this the way he knew his own hands—not from memory, exactly, but from some deeper sediment of himself, laid down before he was old enough to choose what he remembered.

They came down through a drowned colonnade where the pillars had gone soft with salt-fur, furred white and green like the antlers of something gentle. Wren swam at his shoulder with the ember-lantern held out ahead of them, its borrowed glow trembling across the water in long gold ropes. Above them, far above, the surface of the sea moved like the skin of a sleeping animal, and the light that filtered down came apart into coins, into threads, into nothing.

*The Hall of Ten Thousand Lanterns,* his mother had called it, on the one occasion she had brought him here as a child. He had counted forty-one lanterns and then been scolded for counting aloud. Now there were no lanterns. There were only their brackets, ten thousand empty iron mouths along the walls, each one crusted with barnacle and shadow, each one waiting with the patience of things made to hold light.

Coral's magic kept the water from his lungs without being asked. It was the oldest trick he knew, the first thing his mother had taught him—*the sea is not your enemy, Xanthe, it is your inheritance; breathe it like a guest breathes your house*—and it worked now with an ease that shamed him. Ten years of pretending to be small, and the power had only grown, patient as roots under pavement.

Wren touched his arm. He couldn't speak underwater—their charm of shared breath was crude, bought from Ossa's stores, good for an hour at most—and so he pointed.

Ahead, past the collapsed mouth of what had been the throne gallery, something burned.

Not the ghost-light of the court they'd left behind in the shallows—that pale blue wandering, that homesick flicker. This was warmer. Older. A color that did not belong to the sea at all: amber, deep as resin, steady as a heartbeat.

The brazier of black glass stood in the center of what had been the inner court, and it was exactly as Coral remembered it, which was impossible, because nothing else was. The court around it lay in ruin—roof fallen, walls slumped like tired men, a fountain choked with sand—but the brazier stood pristine on its plinth of white coral-stone, unmarked by a decade of drowning, and in it burned a flame the size of a candle.

Small. That was the thing that undid him. The last dragon-fire in the world, the fire his mother had died beside, and it was *small*, a coin of light no bigger than a thumbnail flame, bending gently in the current as if the water were merely a breeze it found agreeable.

And kneeling beside it was a woman.

She was old—old in the way stone is old, old in the way the sea floor is old—with white hair drifting around her head like weed, and her eyes were closed. She wore the grey robes of a firekeeper, rotted to ribbons at the hem, and her hands were folded in her lap as though she had simply paused between tasks a moment ago. Her face was turned toward the flame. She did not turn it toward them when their lantern-light reached her, though Coral saw the small motion of her breathing change. She had heard them. Or felt them. Or perhaps she had known for years that they were coming.

She spoke, and the water carried her voice to them whole, the way the Hollow's waters always had—another inheritance, another thing he'd tried to forget and failed.

"You're late," she said.

---

Coral released the shared-breath charm and let the silence of the drowned court settle around them. His heart was doing something complicated and painful against his ribs.

"You know me," he said.

"I have known you since you were the height of my knee," the firekeeper said. "You used to hide behind that pillar—" she lifted one hand without looking, gesturing vaguely toward a broken column to their left, "—and believe yourself invisible because you could not see yourself. You were wrong on both counts. Your mother always saw you. So did I."

Wren drifted closer to him, close enough that their shoulders touched. Coral was grateful. He was afraid his legs would stop working otherwise.

"Who are you?" he asked, though he already knew. He had known her too. He had simply spent ten years folding her away with everything else, pressing the Hollow flat inside himself until it fit in the space of a scar.

"Sereth," she said. "Keeper of the Last Flame, by an appointment I no longer remember receiving and have never thought to resign." Her blind eyes stayed fixed on the fire—or on the place where the fire would be, if eyes could be trusted to find such a thing. "And you are Xanthe Sandoval, heir to the Hollow, who ran."

The words went through him like cold water through cloth.

He opened his mouth—to deny it, to correct her, to say *my name is Coral, I live in Vell, I sell luck-knots to nervous brides*—and found that every defense he owned sounded small in this room. Here were ten thousand empty lantern brackets. Here was his mother's throne somewhere under the rubble. Here was a woman who had knelt beside a dying fire for ten years because a boy couldn't bear to watch it die slowly and had chosen instead to never see it again.

"I didn't run," he said, and heard even as he said it how young the words sounded. "I was exiled. My mother put me on a boat herself."

"Yes," Sereth said mildly. "I loaded your trunk onto that boat. Two books, three shirts, and a jar of candied ginger you'd stolen from the kitchens and believed no one had noticed." She tilted her head. "I have had ten years, little king, to think about the difference between running and being sent. It is not the difference people think it is. But you will want to argue about it, and arguing is a dry-land habit. Come and look at the fire first. It has waited longer than either of us has patience for your pride."

---

They settled on the plinth's edge, the three of them arranged around the brazier like figures on an old coin. The dragon-fire burned in its black glass bowl and gave off heat—actual heat, Coral realized with a start, warmth moving through the water in slow visible waves, a thing the ocean should have swallowed instantly and hadn't, because the fire did not recognize the sea's jurisdiction.

Up close it was smaller still, and stranger. It wasn't shaped like a flame. It was shaped like a *curling*, like a question mark written in light, and it pulsed—not quickly, not desperately, but with the unhurried rhythm of something very old conserving itself. Around its base, the black glass of the brazier had begun to cloud. Ash, Coral understood suddenly. The fire was burning its own container. When the brazier finished becoming ash, the fire would have nothing to stand on.

"How long?" Wren asked quietly. He'd learned enough of the old tongue in three weeks of archive work to ask it haltingly, and Sereth answered in the trade-tongue anyway, out of courtesy or habit.

"A year," she said. "Perhaps less. Perhaps a season. Fire does not keep calendars. It keeps only what it is given." Her ruined hands unfolded at last, and she cupped them near the glass, warming them, though Coral suspected the gesture was older than any need. "When your mother came down—when the water took her—she fed it everything she had. Every story. Every name. Ten years of a kingdom's remembering, poured into a flame the size of a thumb, so that it would burn until her son arrived." She smiled then, and it was a terrible and tender smile. "I told her you would come. She did not believe me. Mothers rarely do, about the things that matter most."

"She told me never to come back," Coral said. His voice came out rough. "That was the condition. Exile meant exile. If I returned, she said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She said she would unmake me herself before the Ministry could."

"And yet here you are," Sereth said, "drawn home by a book that writes itself and a boy who reads it." Her blind gaze slid to Wren, and Wren straightened as though physically touched. "Yes, archivist. I know why you've come. The fire told me you were coming before the fire told me *he* was. It burns prophecy the way other fires burn wood. It has been whispering your dinner conversation to me for months. You ordered the noodles without cilantro. He pretended not to notice that you hate it, because he is polite in ways his mother never managed."

Wren went red. Underwater, somehow, he went red. Coral loved him so much in that moment that it frightened him more than anything the sea had shown them yet.

"The door," Coral said. He had to say it before the tenderness drowned him. "The prophecy says the way out of the dead country opens while the fire still burns. We came for the door. Not—" He gestured helplessly at the ruined court, the empty brackets, the small stubborn flame. "Not for this. Not to sit a throne that's under forty feet of seawater. I need you to understand that."

"Little king." Sereth's voice was very gentle. "I have knelt beside this fire for ten years. Do you imagine I don't know what it wants? It doesn't want a throne. Fire wants only two things: to burn, and to be *given*. Your mother gave it a kingdom. One day soon, someone must give it permission to stop." She folded her hands again. "But that is not today's lesson, and I am an old woman who has earned the right to make guests wait. First you will tell me what you have done with your hair. It was better when you were nine."

---

Something loosened in him after that, or broke loose—he couldn't tell which, and suspected the difference wouldn't matter.

They sat with the fire while Sereth talked, or rather while she asked questions and let him answer, and it was worse and better than any interrogation the Ministry had ever attempted. She asked about the luck-knots. She asked whether he still bit his nails (he did). She asked whether the candied ginger had survived the voyage to Vell, and he laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and said he'd eaten it all in the first week out of spite, and she nodded as though this confirmed some long-held theory.

And underneath the talk, the shame he had carried for ten years began, very slowly, to change shape.

It was not that she forgave him. She never once said anything so clean. It was that she treated his exile not as a wound she needed to salve but as a fact she had already made peace with, the way one makes peace with weather. *You were sent away. I packed your trunk. Both things were true and neither was the whole truth.* And when he finally said it—the thing he had never said to anyone, not to Wren, not to Ossa, not to the dark ceiling of his rented room above the stall—

"I watched it sink." His voice cracked on the water like a struck bell. "From the boat. I was fifteen, and I watched the towers go down, and I did nothing. I could feel the storm building in my chest, the biggest storm I'd ever—I could have fought the wave. Maybe. I'll never know, because I didn't try. I just held on to the rail and let my country drown, and I told myself my mother ordered me to survive, and I have lived ten years telling myself that was obedience and not cowardice."

The fire pulsed. Once, twice. Slow.

Sereth reached out and took his hand. Her fingers were cool and strong and ridged with old burns, and she held his hand the way she must have held the hands of a hundred frightened children during the sinking, guiding them to boats, to high ground, to whatever mercy the day allowed.

"Child," she said, "I stood in this court when the wave came down the mountain pass. I could hear it coming for six minutes. Six minutes is a long time to listen to a wall of water. Do you know what I did?"

He shook his head.

"I knelt here," she said, "and I tended the fire. Because that was my work. Not because it was brave. Because it was the only thing my hands knew how to do, and when the world ends, you do the thing your hands know." She squeezed his fingers. "Your hands knew how to hold a rail. That is all. You were fifteen, and you did the thing your hands knew, and you survived, and survival was the gift your mother actually wanted, whatever she said in her fury and her grief. I have had ten years to learn what she learned in her last hour: that we ask our children to be heirs, and they can only ever be themselves, and the gap between those two things is where all our sorrow lives."

The fire crackled. In water. It crackled, softly, like a thing agreeing.

"So you're not angry," Coral said. "That I ran."

"I am furious," Sereth said placidly. "I have been furious for ten years. I am also glad you are alive. Both of these live in me comfortably, the way the fire lives in the water. You will learn to hold contradictions, little king. It is the primary skill of anyone who survives a country." She released his hand and turned her blind face back to the flame. "Now. Your archivist has been vibrating with questions for the last quarter hour, and if you don't give him permission to ask them, he will burst, and I have cleaned this court enough times that I resent mopping up visitors."

---

Wren's questions tumbled out—about the fire's feeding, about the prophecy's verses, about the door and its terms—and Sereth answered some, deflected others, and listened to all of them with the particular attention of someone who has spent a decade alone with a single conversational partner the size of a thumbnail.

Coral half-listened, watching the fire.

Because he had noticed something, in the last few minutes, that tightened his chest with a new and different dread. The flame was guttering. Not steadily—erratically. Little stutter-steps in its pulse, gaps where there should have been warmth, moments when the curling light bent nearly double as though winded. Each time it recovered. But the recoveries were getting slower.

"It knows you're here," Sereth said suddenly, breaking off mid-answer. She had gone very still. "It has been waiting to finish its work until you arrived. Now that you've come, it doesn't have to ration anymore. It can spend what's left." She looked at Coral, and for the first time her calm cracked, just slightly, just at the edges. "Which means we have less time than I told you. Days, perhaps. Perhaps hours. Whatever the door requires, whatever the prophecy requires—you will need to decide soon what you are willing to let burn."

And that was when Wren grabbed Coral's arm hard enough to bruise, and pointed upward through the broken vault of the inner court, toward the distant silver skin of the sea.

Far above—so far above it might have been a star, except stars did not move like that, deliberate and descending, in a line—lights were coming down. Three of them. Four. Cold white lights, mechanical, unwavering, cutting the water in perfect beams.

Ministry lights.

"They followed us," Wren breathed. "Ossa's decoy wake—they saw through it, or they never lost us at all—"

Sereth rose from her knees. It took her a long time, joints protesting, but she rose like something being built rather than lifted, and when she stood she planted herself between the descending lights and the brazier with her ruined arms spread wide, an old woman making a wall of herself.

"No," Coral said. He stepped up beside her. The storm was already stirring in his chest, vast and patient and finally, finally *useful*. "No. This is mine. She waited ten years for the boy who ran." He felt the water begin to move around him, a slow turning pressure, the sea recognizing its king at last. "She's going to meet him instead."

Above them, the white lights grew brighter, and the last dragon-fire guttered, and flared, and held.
