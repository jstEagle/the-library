## 13. Ministry Descent

Ossa had held worse gates with less.

She'd held the customs lock at Greyharbour for six hours once, with nothing but a crowbar and a lie about plague. She'd held the stern of a burning ferry with her remaining hand while forty Hollow refugees climbed over her back into the dark. She'd held her own screaming when the surgeon took the arm at the elbow without enough poppy to matter. So no—she was not frightened of a flooded corridor in a drowned palace, not as such.

What frightened her was arithmetic.

One gate. One arm. One crate of salvaged festival fireworks that some dead pyrotechnician of the Hollow's court had stacked in a storeroom off the processional hall like he'd meant to come back after the coronation. And somewhere above—or below, depending on how you counted in a building that had drowned—the Ministry's divers, coming down through the breach she'd watched them open on the surface an hour before dawn: three black shapes dropping through green water, lanterns on their belts, lines paying out behind them neat as thread from a spool.

The Ministry did not send three divers for a rumor.

Ossa spat into the water lapping at her boots and got back to work.

* * *

The flooded gate was a grand thing gone modest. It had been the Sea Gate of the Hollow's palace, once—she could still see the ambition of it, twin doors of bronze taller than a ship's mast, worked over with dolphins and crowned heads, all of it furred now with white anemones that opened and closed like slow breathing. The doors stood half-open, wedged by rubble and ten years of tide, and beyond them stretched the long gallery that led down into the heart of the ruin, toward the throne room, toward whatever the boy Sandoval and his archivist were doing down there in the dark with the last dragon-fire in the world.

Ossa had told them she'd hold the gate. They'd looked at her—the way people always looked, that flicker of pity doing its quick arithmetic of her missing arm—and she'd laughed at them until they stopped.

"I've got one arm," she'd said, "which means every blow I land is a decision. You two go make your decisions about kingdoms. I'll make mine about who gets past this door."

Now she made them.

First: the fireworks. She'd spent two hours dragging the crate up out of the storeroom, floating it piece by piece through chest-deep water, and laying it out along the gate like a housewife setting a table. Rocket cones. Star-shells. A coil of festival fuse as fat as her thumb, still supple, wax-sealed against the damp by someone who had expected it to wait years in storage and been right. The Hollow had loved its festivals; Ossa remembered the last one, the night before the sea came in, rockets blooming gold over the harbor while she loaded refugees in the shadow of the breakwater and pretended not to watch. There was something right about ending the country's story with its own fireworks. Something almost funny, if you had her kind of humor.

Second: position. She chose a shelf of collapsed masonry to the left of the gate, hip-high, dry-topped, with a clear view down the gallery and cover at her back where the wall had folded in on itself. From there she could reach the fuse-line she'd run—a main line branching three ways, each branch ending in a cluster of star-shells packed loose among the rubble so they'd throw fire wide instead of straight. Not precision work. But then, she wasn't hunting divers. She was teaching them that this hallway had opinions.

Third: the boat. This was the part she did not say out loud, because saying it made it a promise, and promises were how men died stupid. *Kestrel's Mercy* lay anchored in the lee of the old customs tower, topside, with her dinghy tethered astern and her escape line rigged to a float-buoy that ran down through a crack in the palace roof to within thirty feet of where Ossa sat. If everything went to hell—if the Ministry cut her off, if the water rose, if the dragon-fire guttered out early and the whole ruin decided to finish dying—she would grab the buoy line, pull herself up ninety feet of flooded shaft, and be gone before anyone could ask her to die for a cause.

Because here was the thing the poets never put in the songs, and Ossa had heard enough songs about herself to know: she did not intend to be a monument. She intended to be alive tomorrow, drinking someone else's rum, telling this story badly on purpose. Holding a gate was a job. Dying at one was just bad bookkeeping.

She checked the fuse. Checked it again. Then she sat down on her shelf of stone, laid the knife across her thigh where her hand could find it without looking, and waited.

* * *

The water told her first.

It always did. Ten years of smuggling had taught her that the sea gossips—that a pressure change, a shiver in the current, a school of silverfish scattering through the gallery windows like flung coins, all of it spoke plain if you listened. The fish scattered. The anemones along the gate drew in tight as fists. And far up the flooded gallery, where the water went from grey-green to black, a light bloomed: cold, blue-white, steady, wrong. Lantern-light. The Ministry used lamps burned in airless glass that gave no warmth, because the Ministry believed warmth was inefficiency.

Three lights. Two moving together, one hanging back—the way trained pairs moved, covering each other, with their officer floating behind to watch and report. Ossa felt her mouth pull sideways. Good. An officer meant paperwork, and paperwork meant they'd hesitate before shooting, and hesitation was the whole currency of her profession.

They came on slowly, kicking through the drowned gallery with that unhurried competence she hated, fins stirring up ghost-silt from the mosaic floor. Even drowned, the floor was beautiful—she could see bits of it between the silt clouds: waves, ships, a crowned figure standing on the water itself. The Hollow had liked to remind you whose floor you stood on. She wondered if the Ministry divers noticed, or if they saw only terrain.

She let them come. Fifty feet. Forty. Close enough now that she could see the pale ovals of their faces inside the hoods, the rebreathers bubbling little silver prayers. Close enough to see the stenciled sigil on the lead diver's chest: the closed eye of the Unmaking, the lid drawn shut over the iris.

Ossa had ferried the Hollow's survivors out of the drowning city. She knew what the closed eye meant to people like Coral Sandoval. It meant your name struck from registers. Your street renamed. Your mother's grave unmarked—not destroyed, which would leave something to mourn, but *corrected*, smoothed over, made to have never been. The Ministry didn't hate magic. Hate required interest. What the Ministry practiced was erasure as civic hygiene, tidy as sweeping.

"Come on, then," she said to the closed eyes, under her breath. "Let's tidy."

She thumbed the striker down the festival fuse.

The first branch caught with a hiss like a snake waking, ran its twenty feet of waxed cord, and touched off the star-shell cluster buried in the rubble at the gate's near side. The explosion underwater was a strange, blunt thing—not the crack of open air but a deep *whump* Ossa felt in her teeth and ribs, followed by a bloom of fire that should have died instantly in the flood but didn't, because these were Hollow-made stars, salt-wrapped and greedy, and they burned on anyway, gold and furious, spinning through the water like a handful of thrown suns.

The lead diver took the worst of it. He spun away trailing sparks, thrashing for the cover of a doorway, and his partner hauled him in, and both their lantern-lights swung wild across the ceiling, painting the drowned frescoes in strobing gold—angels, or whales, or kings, hard to tell at this distance and this decade.

Ossa was already moving to the second branch.

The divers were better than she'd hoped, which was worse than she'd planned. They didn't retreat. The pair split—one high, along the collapsed balcony rail; one low, along the mosaic floor—coming at her gate from two depths at once, and the officer behind them unslung something long and deliberate from his harness. A cutting tool. No—a harpoon launcher, Ministry pattern, the kind that pinned a man to a door and let the paperwork start with the body already in place.

"Cheeky," Ossa said, and lit the second branch.

This time she'd packed the shells tighter, and the gallery filled with light. Stars ricocheted off stone and water alike, and in the strobing she saw the low diver flinch off his line, saw the high one take shelter behind a fallen statue of somebody important, and she used the blind seconds to slide down off her shelf and reposition—ten feet left, behind the gate's own hinge-post, bronze thick as a coffin lid, older than the Ministry and harder than their harpoons.

The first harpoon came through the space she'd vacated and buried itself in the masonry with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The second hit the hinge-post a hand's width from her ear and stuck there, shivering, close enough that she could read the maker's stamp on the shaft. Vellish steel. Of course. The city that hated magic sold weapons to the men who erased it, same as it bought luck-knots off boys like Coral in increments too small to notice. Everything in this world ran on hypocrisy and good bookkeeping. Ossa respected both, in their place.

Her place was the third branch, and she saved it, because the third branch was the trick.

* * *

The trick was this: fireworks were loud, and loud was only useful if the enemy believed the noise was all there was. Three exchanges in, the divers had learned her rhythm—fuse-hiss, whump, stars—and learned it well enough to push through the gaps. The low one was in the gate's shadow now, working at the rubble choke with a pry-bar, widening the gap the doors couldn't close, and the high one was laying covering fire, harpoons cracking past her post in disciplined intervals. Behind them the officer hung in the black water with his lamp raised, watching, writing her death warrant in advance with his stillness.

Ossa gave them thirty more seconds of exactly what they expected.

Then she lit the third branch backward.

She'd run it the other way around—from the shell-cluster nearest *her*, back through the rubble, to the biggest cache of all: four rocket cones, court-grade, the kind built to climb three hundred feet over a harbor and burst into a chrysanthemum you could read a letter by. Packed under the gallery's weakened ceiling vault, fifty feet behind the divers' line, directly beneath the crack where her escape buoy ran.

The fuse went the long way. She counted. At seven, she pulled the buoy line free of its cleat and coiled it twice around her forearm—her whole arm, scarred to the shoulder, wrapped in rope like a pledge—and at nine the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. She wasn't a butcher. But four court-grade rockets going off under a vault already bent by a decade of drowning lifted a hundred tons of stone a foot into the air and dropped it where the divers' line of advance had been, and the shockwave through the flooded gallery picked Ossa up bodily and slammed her against the gate's bronze flank, and for a moment the whole world was white noise and gold afterimage and the taste of blood where she'd bitten her cheek.

When the water cleared enough to see, the gallery was a new place. The vault had folded down into a rampart of rubble, sealing the far passage. Dust-clouded water rolled in slow brown billows. One lantern-light still glowed through the murk, distant, retreating—two of them, actually, converging, hauling a third shape between them. The officer's light swept the ruin once, twice, methodical even now, cataloguing.

Then, faintly, through the water, she heard it: a whistle. Two short, one long. Ministry recall. And the blue-white lights withdrew upward into the black, unhurried even in retreat, because the Ministry never hurried—it only rescheduled.

Ossa leaned against the bronze door and laughed until her ribs complained.

* * *

She gave herself sixty seconds to shake, because shaking was honest and honesty kept you alive longer than bravado. Her left arm—her real one, her only one—was scraped raw from wrist to shoulder where the rope had bitten. Her ears rang like a struck bell. Blood diluted fast in seawater; she checked the cloud around her, judged it acceptable, and got back to bookkeeping.

The gate held. Better than held—the collapse had turned it into a wall. Nothing short of another demolition charge was getting through that rampart tonight, and the Ministry didn't carry charges on a reconnaissance descent. They'd come back heavier, yes. Tomorrow, probably. Which meant the boy and his archivist had tonight, and maybe the morning, and not a minute more.

Sixty seconds done. She stopped shaking.

Then the water did the thing it did sometimes—the thing she never mentioned in port, because smugglers who talked about ghosts paid for extra crew who wouldn't come. Down the sealed gallery, through the settling silt, the drowned mosaics began to glow. Faintly. Green-gold, like lamplight under silk. Figures in the old tiles lifting their heads. And from everywhere and nowhere, a sound like a court whispering behind a fan.

Ossa looked at it for a long moment, jaw working.

"I held your bloody door," she told the glow. "Least you can do is keep it."

The whispering paused—she'd have sworn it paused—then resumed at what she chose to interpret as a respectful volume.

* * *

She climbed.

The buoy shaft was ninety feet of flooded dark, cracked plaster, and drifting debris, and she went up it hand over hand with her knife between her teeth for the first thirty feet until her arm stopped screaming and settled into its old working rhythm. Halfway up she passed a window slit and saw the surface far above, grey with false dawn, the hull of *Kestrel's Mercy* rocking at anchor like a patient horse. Below her, through the water, the palace glowed faintly green-gold from end to end now, every mosaic lit, every ghost awake. Something was happening down in the throne room. Something final, by the feel of it—the way the water pressed in around her, attentive, like a room full of people leaning forward.

At the top she broke the surface gasping, hauled herself over the buoy-frame onto the crusted roof-stone, and lay there dripping, one-armed and grinning at the sky.

Below her, the sea remembered its kings. Above her, the Ministry would return by noon with charges and a full company. Between the two, down in the dark, a boy who called storms was deciding what a dead country got to be.

Ossa rolled to her feet, spat salt, and started limping for the dinghy. There was a signal lamp aboard, and a code, and a man named Coral who needed to hear the only report that mattered:

*Gate held. Time bought. Now finish it.*
