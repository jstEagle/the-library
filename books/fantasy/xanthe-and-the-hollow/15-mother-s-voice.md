## 15. Mother's Voice

The throne room of the Hollow had not drowned so much as it had learned to breathe underwater.

Coral stood in the doorway where the sea had peeled the doors from their hinges a decade ago and left them hanging like shed skin—bronze doors worked with leaping fish, now furred with weed, swaying gently in a current that had no business reaching this far into the palace's heart. Beyond them stretched the hall he had been carried through as a child on his mother's shoulders, when it had been bright with dragon-light and loud with courtiers. Now it was a cathedral of blue gloom. Silt lay over everything like snow that had stopped pretending to be temporary. The pillars still stood, though they wore their coral growths the way old courtiers had worn their ceremonial collars—ornamentally, stubbornly, as if dignity could be grown rather than granted.

And at the far end, on a dais of black glass, sat the throne.

He had known it would be there. He had walked three days through the drowned coast with Wren beside him and the ghost-court watching from the shallows like lantern-fish, and every step of it had been aimed here, the way water is always aimed downhill. But knowing did nothing to soften the sight of it. The throne was smaller than memory insisted. That was the cruelty of returning to childhood places: they shrank to fit the child you no longer were.

"You don't have to sit in it," Wren said quietly.

Wren stood a pace behind him, close enough that Coral could feel the warmth of him even through the cold push of the deep current—a small impossible fire, the way Wren was always a small impossible fire. He had the ember wrapped now in oilcloth and salt-glass, cradled against his chest with both arms, because he had refused to let anyone else carry it. The light of it bled faintly through the wrappings, turning his hands amber, and in the blue dark of the throne room that little glow made him look like something out of one of his own manuscripts: an archivist holding the last page of a dying country.

"I know," Coral said.

"But you're going to walk toward it anyway."

"Yes."

"Because?"

Coral watched the silt drift. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty fathoms and the whole weight of the sea, Ossa was holding the flooded gate against whatever the Ministry sent down, and the thought of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. There was no time for the long answer. There was never time for the long answer; that was the shape his life had taken.

"Because she'll come whether I sit or stand," he said. "She always comes. I'd rather meet her on my feet."

They crossed the hall together, though Coral knew—and suspected Wren knew—that the distance between the doorway and the dais was not measured in yards. It was measured in years. Each step lifted silt in slow silver clouds. Fish scattered from the pillars like torn pages. And the ghost-lights of the old court drifted alongside them the whole way, keeping a respectful distance, the way courtiers keep distance from a king they are afraid of.

*Afraid of.* The thought arrived and lodged, uncomfortable as a splinter. They were afraid of him. Not of what he might do—of what he *was*. Of the name he had been born under. He could feel their attention the way you feel weather change: a pressure, a hush, the sense that something enormous was deciding what to become.

The dais steps rose before him. Seven of them, as tradition demanded—one for each river that had fed the Hollow before the Hollow had fed itself to the sea. His mother had explained them once, kneeling on these very steps with his small hands in hers, her voice warm and absolute: *One day you will climb these alone, Xanthe, and when you do, the kingdom will climb with you.*

He climbed them alone. The kingdom stayed where it was, drowned and dark and waiting.

The throne was carved from a single piece of storm-glass, fused lightning, smooth as river stone. As a boy he had been forbidden to touch it. He touched it now, laid his palm flat against its cold seat, and felt—nothing. No hum of sovereignty. No answering tremor in the storms that slept in his blood. Just glass, and cold, and ten years of salt.

It was, he realized, just a chair. It had only ever been a chair that people agreed to be frightened of.

Behind him, the hall changed.

It was not a sound. It was more like the moment before sound—the held breath, the flattened air, the feeling that the world has drawn itself up to say something it cannot take back. The ghost-lights along the walls flared and steadied. The silt stopped drifting. Even the current seemed to pause mid-thought, and Coral understood, with a lurch of recognition deeper than memory, who had entered the room.

She came down the aisle of her own drowned hall the way she had done every dawn of his childhood: unhurried, luminous, certain that the room existed for her passage. Queen Ysolde of the Hollow had worn white silk shot with grey thread, so that when she moved she looked like weather deciding. The ghost wore the same, but the silk was translucent now, and through it Coral could see the water, and through the water he could see the ruin of her, and through the ruin of her—this was the worst part—he could see that she had not changed at all. Death had not softened her. Death had simply preserved her the way the sea preserves things: perfectly, coldly, forever.

Her eyes found him across the hall. They had been green once, the green of shallow water over sand. They were green still.

"Xanthe," she said.

His name. His other name. The one she had burned out of every record in Vell with bribes and threats and one very expensive fire, the one he had spent ten years not hearing, the one that had been waiting for him in a century-old prophecy all along. Hearing it now was like being struck by something soft. It didn't break anything. It just knocked the breath out of him.

"Mother." His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

"You climbed the seven steps." She glided closer, and the ghost-lights bowed as she passed, dimming and rising like grass under wind. "I watched you from the shallows. I have watched you since you were small enough to carry. You climb beautifully. You always did. Your father climbed badly—hesitated on the fourth step, always the fourth—but you, my son, you climb like the throne is already yours."

"It isn't."

"Of course it is." She said it the way she had said everything: as a fact about the structure of the universe, unbothered by objection. "Blood does not drown. The kingdom sank, but kingdoms are not their stones. A kingdom is its heir. You are here. Therefore the Hollow endures." She reached the foot of the dais and looked up at him, and her face did the thing it had always done—the thing that had governed his entire childhood—softening with love and hardening with certainty in the same motion, so that he could never tell where the love ended and the command began. "Sit, Xanthe. Take up the crown. The firekeeper has kept your flame. The court has kept your place. Ten years I have held this hall together with will and grief, waiting for my son to come home, and he *has come home*, and now he stands there arguing with me about furniture."

Behind him, Wren made a small sound—not quite a laugh, quickly smothered, but Coral heard it, and it steadied him like a hand on his back.

"The kingdom is dead, Mother," Coral said.

"The kingdom is *waiting*."

"It's been waiting for ten years. Nothing that waits ten years underwater is alive. It's—" He searched for the word and found it and hated how much it cost. "It's preserved. Like you. Beautiful, and perfect, and unable to change. Unable to *end*."

"End." Her voice dropped, and the temperature of the hall seemed to drop with it. "You would speak to me of ending. You ran. Do you know what it cost to hold this court after you fled? Do you know what the drowning took? I stood in this room while the water came through the windows like a siege, and I commanded the court to sing so they would not hear their own city filling, and I sang too, Xanthe, I sang until the water reached my chin, because a queen does not stop singing while her people can still hear her. And when they were gone—all of them, everyone I had ever sung to—I came back here and I sat in the dark and I asked myself one question." She rose, then, off the silt, up the seven steps without touching them, until her face was level with his and near enough that he could see that the green of her eyes was the green of the sea now, all the way through. "Was it worth it? Was saving one boy worth losing everything else?"

"And what did you decide?" His throat was tight. The old reflexes were rising in him—the duck of the head, the apology forming before the crime, the whole trained architecture of being her son. He could feel the storm stirring under his breastbone, answering her voice the way it had answered it all his life, gathering to defend or destroy, he had never known which.

"I decided," she said, "that you owe me."

There it was. Plain at last, after ten years, after prophecies and exiles and a decade of hiding his hands so no one would see the static crawling under his skin. *You owe me.* The debt she had been carrying since the night she put him on a smuggler's boat and burned his name out of the world. Every year of his hidden life had been a payment against it. Every small charm sold in increments too small to notice. Every dinner at half-volume. Every day he had been Coral instead of Xanthe, he had been paying interest.

And some buried part of him—the part that was eight years old and standing on the seventh step while the water came—wanted desperately to agree. To sit down on the cold glass throne and let the debt be paid in the only coin she had ever accepted, which was himself.

He looked past her shoulder instead. Past her translucent crown, past the ghost-lights bowing in their rows, to where Wren stood at the foot of the dais with the ember glowing against his chest—small, stubborn, warm, *his*, choosing to be here, owing Coral nothing, having paid for his passage with honesty and a copied manuscript and the terrible courage of a man who tells the truth badly and completely.

That was the difference. He saw it suddenly, whole, the way you see a coastline from the water. Everything his mother loved, she loved as a possession of the crown. Everything Wren loved—including him—he loved as a free man gives things: because he wants to, because it costs him, because love that owes nothing is the only kind that holds.

"No," Coral said.

The word fell into the drowned hall and sank, and everything heard it.

His mother went very still. Ghosts, he thought absurdly, should be better prepared for refusal; she had been dead a decade and yet she looked genuinely astonished, a queen confronted with a subject who had stopped existing in her grammar.

"What did you say?"

"No." Louder now, and the storm in his chest rose with it—not in anger, he noticed, not this time, but in something cleaner. Intention. "No, Mother. I don't owe you my reign. You gave me my life—that was love, and I will honor it until I die, and I *have* honored it, I survived, I hid, I lived small and quiet for ten years because it kept me safe and it kept your sacrifice meaningful. But a gift isn't a debt. You taught me that yourself. You said a queen gives because the giving is the crown. Well." He spread his hands. "I am not taking the crown. So I'm not giving you what it costs."

"Xanthe—"

"That's not my name."

The hall flinched. The ghost-lights guttered. Somewhere in the deep water beyond the broken windows, something enormous turned over in its sleep.

"My name is Coral," he said. "I chose it. I chose it in a border town with salt-cracked lips and no money, the first morning of my real life, and I have answered to it for ten years, and every person who has ever loved me has loved *Coral*—not the heir, not the exile, not the instrument of your grief. Him. The man who sells luck-knots to nervous brides. Who eats noodles with an archivist on Thursdays. Who is terrified of the Ministry and bad at lying and—" his voice caught, and he let it catch, let her see it, "—and happy. I was happy, Mother. Small, but happy. At half-volume, yes. But it was *my* volume. Mine to turn up. Mine to silence. Not yours. It was never yours."

"You are the last of the line," she whispered. "If you refuse, the Hollow ends. Is that what you want? To be the king who killed his own country?"

"No," said Coral, and here it came, the truth he had carried up from the fine print of the prophecy, the truth Wren had read to him by dragon-light while the halls collapsed around them. "The country doesn't need killing. It died ten years ago, and it has been waiting ever since for someone brave enough to bury it. That's what the door is, Mother. That's what the prophecy meant. Not restoration. Release. Someone has to tell the court it may rest. Someone has to blow out the candle." He stepped forward, one step, closing the distance between them, close enough now that if ghosts could weep, he thought he saw the beginning of it in her sea-green eyes. "Someone has to let *you* rest. And it was always going to be me. Because I am the last of the line—which means I am the last one who can forgive it."

For a long moment, neither of them moved, and the hall held its breath around them like a shell held to an ear.

Then Queen Ysolde of the Hollow, who had sung while her city drowned, who had burned her son's name to save him and haunted his footsteps to claim him, closed her eyes.

"When you were six," she said, very quietly, "you asked me why the rivers had to reach the sea. You wouldn't sleep until I answered. Do you remember what I told you?"

Coral's chest ached. "That everything runs toward letting go of itself."

"That everything runs toward becoming part of something larger." Her form had begun to thin at the edges, the way mist thins when the sun finds it—not violently, not unwillingly, but inevitably, the way of all honest things. "I told you that so you would understand the tides, little one. I should have remembered to understand it myself." She opened her eyes, and for the first time in his life—or his death—they were only a mother's eyes, tired and proud and unbearably sad and unbearably fond. "Say it again. Your name. I want to leave hearing it."

"Coral," he said. His voice broke on it, and he said it again anyway, louder, to the whole drowned hall and the sleeping sea and the seven silent steps. "My name is Coral. Coral Sandoval. Son of Ysolde, who gave me life twice—once when I was born, and once when she put me on that boat. I'm not the heir of the Hollow. I'm its rememberer. And I'm going to open the door, and I'm going to carry what's warm, and the rest—" he looked at the throne, the black glass, the beautiful cold chair that had only ever been a chair, "—the rest we give back to the water."

The ghost-lights of the old court rose, all together, like birds startled from a field. And they did not scatter. They gathered—drifting inward, spiraling slowly around the dais, hundreds of them, the whole dead court of the Hollow coming to witness, and Coral felt their attention settle over him not as pressure now but as blessing, cool and vast, a tide changing its mind.

His mother smiled. It was a real smile, crooked on one side, the smile she had never let the court see.

"There you are," she said. "I wondered where you'd been hiding."

And she came apart into light—not into nothing, not into grief, but into a brightness that spread outward through the water like dawn through a window, passing through the pillars and the silt and the swaying bronze doors, passing through Wren, who gasped, passing through Coral himself, who felt it go through him like a wave through open fingers, taking nothing, leaving warmth.

When his vision cleared, the throne was empty. The hall was empty. The ghost-lights were gone, all of them, released, and for the first time since the drowning the palace of the Hollow was merely a ruin—quiet, honest, finished.

Coral sat down on the seventh step and put his face in his hands, and Wren came up and knelt beside him and said nothing, which was the exactly right thing, and held the ember where its small heat touched his shoulder like a hand.

He didn't know how long they stayed that way. Long enough for the silt to resettle. Long enough for his breathing to remember its ordinary rhythm. Long enough, he thought, for a kingdom to finish closing its eyes.

It was Wren who broke the silence—and not with comfort.

"Coral." His voice had gone tight and strange. "Listen."

Coral lifted his head. And beneath them—up through the seven steps, up through the black glass, up through forty fathoms of patient, indifferent sea—came a sound he knew from nightmares: a rhythmic, grinding pulse, mechanical and deliberate, like a great heart made of iron learning to beat.

Sonar. A Ministry descent engine, cutting through the deep, and getting closer.

* * *

"They've breached the upper gate," Wren breathed, scrambling up, clutching the ember. "Ossa—"

"Held them at the gate," Coral said, rising, and felt the storm wake fully in his chest at last—not with rage, not with fear, but with a clarity he had never felt in ten years of suppression. His mother was resting. The court was released. There was nothing left in him left to hide behind, and nothing left to hide.

Something was coming down through the dark to unmake the last proof that the Hollow had ever lived.

Coral turned to face the flooded doors, and the water ahead of him began, very softly, to shine.
