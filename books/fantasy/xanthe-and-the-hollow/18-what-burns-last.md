## 18. What Burns Last

Wren had never carried anything so heavy that weighed so little.

The ember sat in a brazier of black glass no larger than his two cupped hands, and it weighed almost nothing at all—a curl of light, a breath of heat, the last ten years of a country's stubborn heart. But when he lifted it from its stand in the drowned throne room, his arms shook as though he'd taken up an anvil. The firekeeper, blind and smiling, had pressed it toward him with both hands and said only: *Warm things keep. Cold things forget.* Wren understood then that he was not carrying fire. He was carrying memory, and memory has no weight except what it asks of you.

The water around them was receding—not draining, nothing so violent, but folding back the way a tide folds back from a shore it has decided, for one hour, to spare. Coral stood at the center of the great hall with his arms raised and his palms open, and the sea obeyed him the way an old dog obeys a whistle it half-remembers: reluctantly, lovingly, with its ears back. A corridor of air ran from the broken dome above them down through the flooded nave of the palace, and at the far end of it—impossibly far, impossibly near—hung the door.

It did not look like a door. It looked like a seam of dawn stitched into black water, a vertical line of gold-pink light no wider than a hand's breadth, glowing against the deep like the first crack of morning under a closed eyelid. It hummed. Wren felt the hum in his teeth, in the hollows behind his eyes, in the scar tissue of his left palm where he'd burned himself as a boy on his grandmother's kettle. Everything in him leaned toward it.

*The door out of a country that no longer exists,* the prophecy had said, *open only while the last dragon-fire still burns.*

And the last dragon-fire was guttering. Wren could see it now, even through the glass brazier—the flame had shrunk since they'd arrived, since the firekeeper had died sitting upright against her wall with her hands still warm, since Coral had knelt beside her for a long time without saying anything at all. The flame that had been small as a candle was now small as a match head. It would not outlast their hesitation. Nothing about it suggested urgency; everything about it demanded it.

"Coral." His voice came out strange in the compressed air of the air-corridor, flattened, close. "Coral, we have to go."

"I know." Coral didn't turn. He was looking up at the dome, at the mosaic there—Wren followed his gaze and saw it: thousands of tiles forming a map of the Hollow as it had been, all its harbors and terraced hills and white-walled cities, every tile intact, every city bright. Underwater for ten years and not one tile lost. The sea had kept it the way you keep a letter you can't bring yourself to reread. "I know we do."

Wren stepped closer, the brazier held out before him like an offering, like a lantern, like the smallest and most important thing in any world. Behind them, down the long dark throat of the hall, he could hear the distant percussion of Ossa's fireworks still going off at intervals—crack, crack, boom—her signal that the gate still held, that she still held it, that somewhere in the flooded dark she was fighting with one arm and salvaged gunpowder and the purest stubbornness Wren had ever witnessed. Each explosion shivered through the water and made the ember flicker.

"Ossa," Wren said.

"She knows the way out," Coral said. "She told me. She always knows the way out." A pause. "She'll meet us at the boat or she won't. She made me promise not to wait."

"Did you promise?"

"I promised I'd think about it."

Despite everything—the drowning country, the dying fire, the Ministry divers who had breached the lower gates twenty minutes ago and were, at this moment, somewhere below them in the dark with their unmaking-tools and their gray uniforms—despite all of it, Wren laughed. It came out of him cracked and wet, half sob, and Coral turned at the sound of it, and for a moment they just looked at each other across the length of a dead kingdom's throne room, two men holding a candle between them while an empire collapsed.

"We're really doing this," Wren said. "We're really ending it."

"You read the fine print." Coral's voice was steady, but Wren had learned to hear the tremor underneath it, the one Coral thought no one could hear. "You tell me. Did we ever have another ending?"

Wren thought of the page in the self-writing book. *And the heir shall open the door, and the door shall open only for the hand that lets go.* He had read those words by dragon-light four hours ago and felt the whole architecture of destiny shift around him like a corridor rearranging itself. The prophecy had never promised restoration. It had never promised a kingdom reborn, a throne reclaimed, a mother avenged. It had promised a door. Only a door. And doors, Wren knew—he was an archivist, doors were practically his profession—doors do not care what you carry through them. They only care that you pass.

"No," Wren said. "We never did."

* * *

They walked.

The air-corridor narrowed as they went, the sea pressing in on either side like a crowd at a funeral, curious and grieving and unable to look away. And the sea *was* grieving—Wren could feel it now, could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your knees. All around them, in the green-black press of the water, the ghost-lights drifted. The old court. He had seen them first on the drowned coast, had mistaken them for bioluminescence until one of them had turned and worn a face. Now they came out of the ruins in numbers, dozens, hundreds, pale figures in the shapes of courtiers and soldiers and fishwives and children, drifting alongside the corridor of air, keeping pace with the two living men and the one small fire.

They did not speak. Wren had learned, in the Court of Salt, that they spoke only to test. But they watched. Gods, how they watched. A hundred years of watching folded into every face, and all of it aimed at the ember in Wren's hands.

One of them drew close to the boundary of the air—a woman in the tattered grandeur of a court gown, her edges fraying into foam—and looked down at the brazier. Her face was young. Her face was also very, very old. She looked at Wren the way you look at a stranger carrying your child.

"Will it hurt?" she asked. Her voice came through the water like a bell rung underwater, round and slow.

Wren stopped walking. Coral stopped too, sensing it, turning back.

"No," Wren said. And then, because he had earned something in the Court of Salt—because he had answered their riddles with truth instead of flattery and they had let him pass—he gave her more than the question asked for. "It will be quiet. That's not the same as painless. But it will be quiet, and it will be yours, and no one will take it from you. Not the Ministry. Not the sea. Not even him." He nodded back toward Coral. "He's not here to keep you. He's here to let you rest."

The ghost studied him for a long moment. Then she did something Wren had not seen any of them do: she bowed. Not the deep ceremonial bow of the Court of Salt's testing. A small bow. The bow of one tired person to another.

She drifted back into the dark, and as she went, the other lights began to move—not away, but *with* them, falling in behind the two men and the fire like a procession, like a funeral cortege, like the whole drowned population of a country coming out to see the last of it carried home.

"They're following us," Wren whispered.

"They're seeing us off," Coral said. His voice was rough. "There's a difference. My mother never understood the difference."

* * *

The seam of dawn grew as they approached, widening from a crack to a doorway to a standing rectangle of morning light suspended in the deep, and through it Wren could see—absurdly, wonderfully—sky. Gray Vell sky, overcast, ordinary, the sky he had catalogued a thousand sunsets under without once thinking of it as a miracle. Beyond the light-shape, blurred by distance and water, he could make out the dark hull of Ossa's boat riding at anchor, and the rain beginning to fall on it, soft and straight.

The ember chose that moment to sputter.

It was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. The flame simply dipped, the way a candle dips when someone walks past it, and did not fully rise again. The warmth against Wren's palms faltered. In the brazier of black glass, the fire lay down flat along its bed of coals like a tired animal, and its color shifted—from gold to orange to a red so deep it was nearly the color of the dark itself.

"No," Wren heard himself say. "No, no—not yet—"

"Wren." Coral's hands closed over his, over the brazier, warm and shaking. "Look at me. Look at me. It's not dying *wrong*. It's dying *done*. There's a difference. The firekeeper waited ten years to hand it off. It's been waiting longer than that to finish."

"But if it goes out before we—"

"Then we walk through in the dark together, and the door closes behind us, and the kingdom ends unwitnessed." Coral's eyes were bright and terrible and calm. "But it isn't going to do that. Are you? No. You're going to hold on exactly as long as one stubborn archivist needs you to, because you're dragon-fire, and dragon-fire does not leave a job half-finished."

The flame flickered. And then—it was impossible, Wren would swear to this for the rest of his life—it *leaned*. It bent toward the door the way a plant bends toward a window. Toward the morning. Toward Vell, and rain, and gardens that needed warming, and a forge that did not yet know it was waiting.

"It wants to go," Wren said, wonder cracking his voice open. "Coral. It wants to go."

"Everything alive does," Coral said. "That was the part my mother never believed. Come on."

* * *

The threshold was cold.

Wren had expected—something else. Fire on the far side of fire, or at least the clean neutrality of ordinary air. Instead, crossing the seam of dawn felt like stepping through the surface tension of a drop of water: a moment of resistance, a shiver across the whole skin, and then the ordinary world, closing around him like a coat.

Rain hit his face. Real rain, cool and mineral-smelling, falling out of a real gray sky onto the deck of a real smuggler's boat. His legs nearly buckled; Coral caught his elbow. The brazier sat in Wren's hands, and inside it the ember glowed—a coal now, truly, no longer a flame, pulsing slow and deep like a sleeping heart—and against the vast gray dimness of the evening it burned brighter than it ever had in the drowned hall. Small things burn brightest against the dark. Wren had read that somewhere. He had never understood it until now.

Behind them, the seam of dawn hung on the surface of the sea, narrowing.

And the ghosts came through.

Not all of them. Not most of them. But some—one in ten, one in fifty, Wren couldn't count them, they moved like reflections moving on water—slipped through the shrinking door in the wake of the ember, drawn after its warmth like moths, like pilgrims, like the last few citizens of a country unwilling to be entirely dissolved. They did not solidify. They did not stay. They rose off the deck like smoke off a doused candle and thinned into the rain, into the wind, into whatever waits for the dead of a finished kingdom—and as each one went, the ember pulsed once, brighter, as if it were being fed.

"That's—" Wren started.

"The choice," Coral said quietly. He stood at the rail with the water-light washing his face gold and then gray and then gold again. "The prophecy's fine print. The door opens for the hand that lets go—but it doesn't say *what* gets to go through. It just says the door opens. Anyone who chooses. Anything." His jaw worked. "My mother is still in there. She's still commanding a court that's already leaving. She could walk through. She won't. She'd rather reign over ash than be free in the rain."

The seam was a hand's width wide now. A finger's width.

"Coral," Wren said. "It's closing."

"I know."

"Your mother—"

"My mother made her choice ten years ago, when she sank us rather than surrender." Coral's voice did not break, but it bent, the way a mast bends. "I'm making mine now. And mine is to stand here and watch it end, because it deserves someone watching. It deserves a witness who loved it." He turned his head, and his eyes found Wren's, and they were wet, and he was smiling. "You're my witness. Remember all of it. You're good at remembering. It's the only thing I've ever asked of anyone."

Wren set the brazier down on the deck between them, gently, the way you set down a sleeping child, and took Coral's hand.

The seam of dawn closed like an eye.

For one heartbeat, the sea where the door had been glowed from within—a lantern shape fading under the waves, the silhouette of a drowned palace lit briefly from nowhere, gold light bleeding up through ten fathoms of water like a sunset happening in reverse, underground, underwater, final. Wren saw towers in the glow. Terraces. White walls. The whole bright map from the dome-mosaic, burning once through the skin of the ocean, saying goodbye in the only language it had left.

Then the light went out.

The sea settled. The rain continued, indifferent and gentle. Somewhere behind them, out in the dark, a last firework cracked—Ossa, still fighting, still alive, still stubborn—and the sound rolled across the water like a door shutting in another room.

The Hollow was gone. Not destroyed—*finished*. There was a difference, and Wren, cataloguer of ten thousand fragile things, held the difference in his hands in a brazier of black glass, where a single coal of dragon-fire pulsed in the rain and refused, with the whole stubborn patience of a dead country's love, to go out.

"It's still burning," Wren whispered.

Coral looked down at it. Something moved through his face—grief and relief and a third thing too large and new to name—and then he laughed, low and disbelieving, and pulled Wren against him, and they stood together at the rail of a smuggler's boat in the rain, two men and an ember, the entire surviving population of the Kingdom of the Hollow.

"Not everything burns last," Coral said into Wren's hair. "Some things burn *onward*."

Wren was still holding him, still laughing or crying or both, when the lookout point on the foredeck went dark—and Ossa's voice cut across the deck, sharp as a knife laid flat:

"Get down. Both of you. We've got company on the water—and it isn't mine."
