## 20. A Book Rewritten

The restricted stacks of the Vell Grand Archive smelled the same as they always had—dust and lamp oil and the faint green tang of paper slowly surrendering to time—and Wren had missed it with a ferocity that embarrassed him. Three weeks. It had been three weeks since he had last stood at his own desk, since he had last run a cloth along the shelves of the sealed cases, three weeks of salt water and smugglers' holds and drowned palaces, and now here he was, cataloguing.

It felt absurd. It felt like coming home from a war and finding the kettle still on the stove.

The book lay on the reading table before him, chained at its corners—not by the Archive's restraints, though Wren had considered them, but by four lead weights of Ossa's devising, because for the past hundred years this book had refused to stay shut, and Wren no longer trusted it to behave just because its prophecies had finally stopped coming true.

*Codex Vellus 114*, the old catalogue card said. *Anonymous. Prophetic verse. Incomplete.*

Wren had already decided the card was wrong on every count except the first.

He looked at it the way he imagined a physician looks at a patient who has survived something no one survives. The cover was sea-damaged now, swollen and wrinkled where the flooded galleries of the Hollow's palace had soaked it through, and yet the pages inside were impossibly dry, impossibly white, as if the book understood itself to be an instrument rather than an object, and instruments must be kept in working order until the work is done.

The work, Wren thought, was done.

He knew this the way you know rain has stopped: not by seeing it, but by listening and finding the silence where the sound should be. For weeks the codex had written itself—the pen-strokes appearing on the page like insects forming from nowhere, ink blooming out of blank vellum in that spidery, patient hand. It had described a noodle shop on Cordwainer Street, two men sharing a bowl, one of them laughing at something small. It had named Wren Ashfield beside a man called Xanthe Sandoval. It had followed them across the sea in verses that arrived sometimes hours before the events they described and sometimes, horribly, after—so that Wren could never be sure whether he was reading the future or being read by it.

And then, in the ruined throne room under the weight of the ocean, while Coral spoke words Wren did not yet fully understand and the last dragon-fire guttered down to a single stubborn ember, the writing had stopped.

Not trailed off. Not faded. Stopped, mid-verse, the way a clock stops.

Wren had carried the book back through the door himself, pressed to his chest inside an oilcloth wrap, because it seemed suddenly less like a prophecy and more like a wound, and you do not leave wounds behind in places that are dying.

Now he opened it to the final page.

* * *

The last entry was not verse.

That was the first thing he noticed, and the thing that made his hands go still on the edge of the table. A hundred years of prophecy—all of it in the same rolling, oracular cadence, all of it obsessed with doors and fires and heirs and endings—and then the final page broke form entirely and became something plainer. Something humbler.

It read like minutes from a meeting. Like a ship's log. Like, Wren realized with a jolt that went through him warmer than he expected, like a catalogue entry.

*Item: the Kingdom of the Hollow, also called the Drowned Court, also called Home by those who loved it poorly.*

*Duration: six hundred and eleven years.*

*Manner of ending: chosen.*

Wren read the rest of the page twice, then a third time, because he wanted to be certain, and because some part of him—a part he recognized as the archivist, which was to say the truest part of himself—wanted simply to be present for it.

The page recorded everything. It recorded the sinking, ten years ago, in language that refused both grandeur and pity: *the sea came in; the court would not leave; the king-mother stayed by the fire.* It recorded the ghost-court that had haunted the shallows, and their dispersal—not as a haunting ended but as *a council adjourned.* It recorded Ossa holding the flooded gate with salvaged fireworks, and it recorded her cursing with such vigor that the ink seemed to smudge itself out of respect. It recorded Wren's own heresy—that he had copied the pages and sent them ahead, that his honesty had nearly killed them all—and it recorded it without judgment, in the flat tone of fact, which somehow absolved him more thoroughly than forgiveness would have.

And it recorded Coral. Not Xanthe. The book, which had spent a century insisting on *Xanthe*, heir and doorkeeper and ender-of-things, used his name the way Wren used it in his own heart, quietly, familiarly, as if the book too had finally met the man underneath the title.

*Coral Sandoval, storm-caller, son of the Hollow,* the page said. *Refused the throne. Opened the door. Chose to return.*

*Herein ends the foretelling. What follows is unwritten.*

Below those words, the rest of the page was blank. Below the blankness, every remaining leaf in the codex was blank as well—he had checked, all three hundred and eleven of them, the night before, alone in the stacks with a lamp turned low, running his fingers through the empty pages like a man counting the ribs of something sleeping.

The book had become exactly what it appeared to be: paper, bound, silent. Waiting, in the way all blank books wait, for someone to pick up a pen.

Wren sat with that for a long time.

Around him the Archive breathed its evening breaths—the tick of the cooling lamps, the far-off door of the day-stacks closing, someone's cart wheels on marble three floors up. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world that had gone on turning while he was underwater learning that destiny is mostly a test of whether you can let go. He thought about how, three months ago, his greatest fear had been that his life was too small: the same shelves, the same ledgers, the same careful, catalogued days. He had fallen in love partly because Coral looked at his quiet life as if it were made of gold leaf. He understood now that Coral had been looking at his own lost kingdom through him.

The future was unwritten again. That sentence kept unfolding in him like paper in water. For a hundred years this book had told the world what was coming, and everyone inside its verses—Coral most of all—had lived like figures in a composition, moving because they must. And now the hand that held them had lifted away, and the page was empty, and empty pages frightened people only when they forgot what they were for.

Wren took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his sleeve, which he did when he needed his hands to be busy while his heart caught up.

* * *

There were practical matters. There were always practical matters; it was the archivist's consolation and his shield.

The codex required a new case. Sea-water damage, even magical sea-water damage, demanded climate control, and the restricted stacks ran dry as a bone in winter. He would need to petition Underwarden Pell—which meant explaining, in language the Ministry's censors would tolerate, how a restricted manuscript had left the building, traveled across the sea, and returned damp. He had drafted the letter four times in his head. The truth was unspeakable; the near-truth was actionable. He was composing a fifth version, involving a private collector and an unfortunate ferry mishap, when he heard footsteps on the stairs.

Only one person walked the archive stairs like that—unhurried, slightly heel-heavy on the left, pausing at the third step from the top the way she always did, because the third step creaked and she liked to give it warning.

"Wren Ashfield." Underwarden Pell's voice preceded her around the corner, dry as the dust she'd breathed for forty years. She was a small woman built like a filing drawer, and she was looking at him with an expression he had learned to dread, which was the expression of a woman who has already read your letter, located your errors, and sharpened her pencil. "You've been reinstated nine days and you've already requisitioned a preservation case from stores without filing the intake form."

"I filed it this morning," Wren said. "Retroactively."

"Retroactive filing." She said the words the way other people said *heresy.* Then her gaze slid past him to the reading table, to the swollen covers and the lead weights, and her face changed—not softened, exactly. Shifted, the way a lock shifts. "So that's it," she said. "The book that writes itself. I catalogued its grandmother, you know, sixty years ago. Also prophetic. Also stopped, eventually, mid-sentence." She came closer, hands clasped behind her back, and looked down at the open final page for a long moment. Wren watched her lips move faintly as she read, and he saw the exact line where her reading slowed: *the king-mother stayed by the fire.*

"Ministry's been asking after this one," Pell said at last, mildly. Too mildly. "Twice this week. A woman with gray eyes and a very expensive coat. I told her the restricted stacks are closed for dehumidification."

Wren's mouth went dry. "Gray eyes."

"And a habit of standing too close." Pell turned her head and regarded him with those flinty, unfoolable eyes. "I am seventy-three years old, Ashfield. I have outlasted four Ministers of Unmaking and one actual fire. I know what it means when the Ministry takes an interest in a book, and I know what it means when a junior archivist comes back from 'medical leave' smelling of salt and looking at blank paper like it owes him money." She paused. "I also know that this Archive exists to keep records, and that a record which predicted a hundred years and then wrote its own conclusion is the single most important object in this building. Possibly in this city."

"Underwarden—"

"You will file the intake form properly," she said, "under a heading that doesn't exist yet, because our taxonomy has no category for what this thing is now. And you will do it tonight, because I am old and I do not sleep well, and I would rather help you than think about you worrying." She unfolded her hands. "Well? You're the taxonomist. What do we call it?"

Wren looked at the book.

He thought about all the headings the Archive had for things like it: *Prophetic Verse, Apocrypha, Materials Pending Verification, Sealed Pending Ministry Review.* Every one of those categories treated the future as the book's subject. None of them fit anymore, because the future had gotten off the page and walked out the door and was currently, at this very moment, probably arguing with a flower seller about the price of rain-lilies somewhere across town, planning to meet him for dinner, alive and free and gloriously unrecorded.

The book didn't predict anything anymore. It remembered. That was all it did now, and it was enough—it was everything. Memory was the Archive's whole reason for existing, and Wren felt the shape of the right word arrive in him the way correct answers always did, quietly, like something returning home.

"History," he said.

Pell's eyebrows rose a fraction. Then she nodded once, slow, like a verdict. "History," she repeated, tasting it. "Yes. That'll annoy the Ministry beautifully. They prefer *evidence.*" She was already moving toward the stairs, trailing lamp-oil smell and authority. "Form 12-C for the case, Form 9 for recategorization, and Ashfield—" she paused at the third step, giving it warning, "—when you write the summary card, spell the names correctly. All of them. Archives forgive everything except bad cataloguing."

Her footsteps descended and faded, and Wren was alone again with the book and the lamplight and the enormous, ordinary silence.

* * *

He worked for another hour. New card, cut clean and squared. New heading, in his best formal hand: *HISTORY — THE HOLLOW (ENDED). SUB-CATEGORY: ACCOUNTS CONTEMPORANEOUS.* Summary line, spelled correctly: *Record of the ending of the Kingdom of the Hollow, witnessed by C. Sandoval and W. Ashfield. Foretelling concluded. Future: pending.*

He liked *pending.* It was the most honest word in the catalogue.

When the card was done he slotted it into the drawer, and there was a small ceremony in that—there always was, the soft click of a thing finding its permanent place—and then he wrapped the codex in oilcloth, lifted it with both arms, and carried it to the case Pell had requisitioned, the one with the brass vents and the silica drawers and the little glass window so scholars could look without touching. He set the book inside. Closed the door. Turned the key.

Through the window the final page was visible, or at least its blankness was, pale and patient under the case's dim interior light.

*What follows is unwritten.*

Wren stood there a moment longer with his palm flat against the cool brass, saying goodbye to something. Not to the book—the book would be here long after him, safe and silent, doing its new job. He was saying goodbye to the version of himself who had found his own name in these pages and felt the floor drop out of his life. That man had believed the future was a place you could fall into. This man knew better. The future was a room with no furniture yet. You brought your own chairs.

Outside the archive windows, Vell was lighting its evening lamps, and above the rooftops the sky had that bruised, heavy stillness that the whole city had learned, in recent weeks, to read differently. Not menace. Promise. Somewhere over the eastern gardens, clouds were gathering with unmistakable intention, gathering gently, the way Coral gathered everything now—deliberately, tenderly, asking permission of the air.

Wren smiled, shrugged his coat on, and locked the stacks behind him.

By the time he reached the archive steps, the first drops were falling—soft, warm, deliberate, falling exactly where they were most needed, on the drought-cracked beds of the public gardens—and people were stopping on the paths to turn their faces up into it. Some of them were laughing.

And some of them, Wren noticed as he descended into the fragrant dark of the rainy street, were not. At the garden gate, motionless while the crowd moved around her like water around a piling, stood a tall figure in a very expensive coat, gray-eyed, unhurried, watching the rain fall with the expression of someone auditing it.

She turned her head, and looked directly at him.

Wren's hand found the strap of his satchel—empty now of prophecy, full only of forms—and he started walking, quickly, toward the lighted windows of the noodle shop on Cordwainer Street, where a storm-caller was waiting for him with two bowls and an unwritten future, and where Wren was going to have to decide, in the next four minutes, exactly how much of tonight's cataloguing he needed to confess.
