## 22. Xanthe and the Hollow

The forge on Ashgrove Road had once been a bakery, and it still smelled faintly of bread when the weather turned warm, as if the walls remembered kindness in a different form. Coral had never corrected the children who came to the door and asked if the dragon-fire was what baked things. He liked the idea. It was nearly true.

It was a grey-gold afternoon in late autumn, eleven years after the sea had closed over the Hollow for good, and six children sat cross-legged on the warm flagstones around the brazier where the ember lived. The ember was small — it had always been small; that was its whole lesson — no bigger than a fistful of coals, glowing a deep patient orange, like something asleep rather than something burning. It gave off heat without consuming anything. The firekeeper in the drowned palace had tended it with prayers; Coral tended it with nothing more than attention, which he had come to believe was the same thing wearing plainer clothes.

"Tell the one about the ghost court," said Pip, who was seven and had a gap where her front tooth should be and used it ruthlessly.

"You always want the ghost court."

"Because it's the best one."

"It's the scariest one," said Tomasz, who was nine and pretended to be too old for stories and leaned in closer than anyone when Coral began.

"All the best ones are," Coral said. He settled onto the baker's old stool, the one Wren had re-caned twice now, and let the familiar hush fall — the hush that was half the craft of storytelling and half the craft of storm-calling, though he only ever admitted this to himself. "You have to understand first what kind of country the Hollow was. Not how big. Not how rich. What *kind*."

The children waited. They were good at waiting, these Ashgrove Road children; they had learned it from the ember itself, perhaps, or from him.

"It was a country that loved its king too much," Coral said. "That was its whole tragedy, if you want to know it plainly. It loved its kings so much it couldn't imagine the world without them. And so when the sea came — and the sea always comes, eventually, to every coast, to every country, to every house — the Hollow couldn't imagine letting go. It held on until holding on was all that was left."

"Was it your country?" asked little Maren, who was five and had not yet learned that some questions weren't asked.

Coral smiled at her. "Yes," he said, simply, because it had taken him ten years to be able to say it simply. "It was mine."

* * *

He had signed his name that morning at the registry office on Lantern Street, the way he did every year on the anniversary of the door — not because anyone required it anymore, but because he had decided some vows should be renewed like gardens, deliberately, in daylight.

*Coral Xanthe Sandoval.*

Both names. One hand. The clerk — a young woman with ink-stained cuffs who had been a child herself during the drought, during the gentle rain he had called down over Vell's starving gardens — had looked at the signature and said, "That's a long name."

"It's two lives' worth," he'd told her, and she'd nodded as if that were an ordinary thing to say, and maybe by now it was.

There had been a time when writing Xanthe anywhere felt like pressing a brand into his own skin. His mother had burned the name out of every record in the kingdom before the sinking — burned it out of ledgers and monuments and the mouths of tutors, leaving only *the heir*, only *the boy*, a shape where a person should have been. She had thought she was protecting him. He understood that now, most days. She had loved him in the only grammar she knew, which was the grammar of erasure: make him invisible so the world couldn't take him.

And then he had gone and made himself invisible anyway, all on his own, in a city that hated magic — selling luck-knots too small to notice, living at half-volume, dating an archivist without telling him the first true thing about himself. Erasure upon erasure, inheritance passed faithfully down the generations of one frightened family.

Wren had broken the chain. That was the truth Coral had arrived at slowly, the way you arrive at the far shore of a lake you've been swimming across without noticing the distance. Wren, with his shaking hands and his photographs of impossible pages, had looked at a name history had tried to bury and refused to look away. *You are written here,* he'd said, in the noodle shop, in the archive, in a hundred quiet moments since. *You are written. I checked twice.*

* * *

"—and then the blind firekeeper," Pip prompted, wriggling. "You skipped to the ghost part but you skipped the firekeeper."

"I didn't skip her. I'm building to her. Stories are like storms; you don't start with the thunder."

"Why not?" asked Maren.

"Because nobody would listen to anything else afterward." Coral held his hands out toward the ember's warmth, palms open, the way he did when he wanted the children to see there was nothing hidden in them anymore. "So. The firekeeper. Ten years she waited in the dark of the sunken palace, tending a flame the size of a candle, because a prophecy said the door out of the dead country would stay open only while the last dragon-fire still burned. Ten years. And do you know what she said when the boy finally came home?"

"What?"

"She said, *I wondered when you'd stop running from the wrong thing.*"

Tomasz frowned, working through it. "Running from the wrong thing? Wasn't he running from the Ministry?"

"He thought he was." Coral watched the ember breathe — it seemed to breathe; Wren insisted it didn't, and Coral enjoyed their small standing disagreement about it. "But he was really running from being seen. From being known. The Ministry wanted to unmake him, yes. But he'd already unmade himself, bit by bit, year by year, until there wasn't much left to erase. The firekeeper saw that in about four seconds. Blind people often see fastest."

"That doesn't make sense," said Tomasz.

"No," Coral agreed cheerfully. "Most true things don't, at first."

* * *

Afterward, when the children had been collected by mothers and grandmothers and one very patient older brother, Coral banked the room and found Wren at the long table under the window, where the finished book lay open.

Eleven years, and Coral still felt the small electric awe of seeing it. *A Book Rewritten*, the archive had catalogued it at last — not prophecy, not history even, at first, but a strange third thing: a record that had written itself forward through time until the night the door closed, and then stopped, mid-sentence almost, as if the future had finally run out of instructions and handed the pen back.

The final page described this room. It described the ember. It described, in the old formal script that had once named dinners before they happened, a man telling children the story of the country that chose to end — and beside it, in Wren's newer, rounder hand, the archival note: *Witnessed. True. Complete.*

"The book's done," Wren said, not looking up. "Really done. I've checked the margins for three weeks. Nothing. Not so much as a stray word trying to be born."

"And how does the archivist feel about unemployment?"

"The archivist feels," Wren said, "that the future is unwritten again, and that this is either terrifying or the best news of the century, and he hasn't decided which."

"Both," Coral said. "It's both. That's the whole curriculum, love."

Wren looked up then, and the lamplight caught the silver coming in at his temples — earned silver, Coral called it, though Wren claimed it was archival dust — and the expression on his face was the one Coral had spent eleven years learning to read without flinching: the look of a man seeing someone fully, entirely, at full volume, and staying anyway.

"Sit," Wren said. "There's a thing I want to show you before I shelve it."

Coral sat. The book lay open to the last page, and beneath the old prophecy-script and Wren's archival note, there was fresh ink. A new line, added today, in the margin:

*Here ends the account of the Hollow. Here begins whatever we make.*

"When did—" Coral started.

"This morning. While you were signing your name at the registry. I know you sign it every year; I've known since the second anniversary; I'm an archivist, I notice patterns." Wren's voice was steady but his ears had gone pink, which after eleven years remained his one involuntary honesty. "I wanted the record to end the way it actually ended. Not with a door closing. With a name."

Coral looked at the page a long moment. Then he took the pen from behind Wren's ear — Wren made a noise of protest, purely ceremonial — and beneath the marginal line, in careful letters, he wrote:

*Coral Xanthe Sandoval, keeper of the ember, husband of the archivist, son of a drowned kingdom, citizen of Vell. Present and accounted for.*

"There," he said quietly. "Let the Ministry chew on that."

"They won't." Wren closed the book gently, his hand resting flat on the cover the way he rested it on everything he meant to keep. "Chief Unmaker Vasquez sent her annual letter last week, did I tell you? Third paragraph. *The Hollow is concluded. The file remains open only as courtesy.* Courtesy. From Vasquez. The world has changed."

"The world," Coral said, "had a very good teacher."

Outside, the autumn light was going amber and thin, and somewhere down Ashgrove Road a cart rattled past, and the ember in its brazier breathed its slow, unconsuming breath — warming the forge, warming the street above it through pipes Ossa had installed and refused payment for (*consider it shipping fees refunded*, she'd said, one-armed and grinning, on her fourth visit, which had become her fortieth), warming, in its small stubborn way, the whole idea of the city.

Coral crossed the room and stood beside Wren at the window, and together they watched the light go gold on the rooftops of Vell — the city that had hated magic and then, slowly, one gentle rain at a time, had learned to say *thank you* instead.

"Do you ever miss it?" Wren asked. "The kingdom. The name. Any of it."

Coral considered the question honestly, because he owed it that. "I miss my mother," he said at last. "Not the queen. The mother is harder to find in all of it, but she's there — in the firekeeper's grace, maybe, or in the fact that I can tell children scary stories and mean them kindly. And I miss the coast at dawn, sometimes. But the kingdom?" He shook his head. "The kingdom chose to end, Wren. I just had the honor of saying it out loud. You don't miss a thing you set free. You carry it."

Wren laced his fingers through Coral's. His hand was warm from the ember-side of the room.

"Carried," he agreed. "Both names. All of it."

And below them, in the brazier of black glass that had once held the last light of a drowned country, the dragon-fire glowed on — small as a promise, patient as a story, burning not for a throne, not for a prophecy, not for a mother's command, but simply because a boy who ran had finally come home, and stayed, and signed his name.
