## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The border of Ashmoor smelled of iron and cold pine, and Ulyana Zhou had spent eleven years learning that smell the way other men learned their mothers' voices. He knew it in rain and in frost. He knew it at dusk, when the wardstones along the ridge woke with a low hum that traveled up through his boot soles and settled somewhere behind his teeth, a reminder — constant, comforting, faintly annoying — that the valley was safe because men like him walked its edge.

Tonight the stones were quiet.

He noticed it first as an absence, the way you notice a clock stopping in a room you thought was silent. He stopped on the switchback trail above Hollowmere Ford, one hand resting on the pommel of his knife, and listened. Wind through the pines. Water over stone. His own breath fogging in the dark. But underneath it all, where the hum should have been — nothing.

"Vess," he said.

His partner was twenty paces back down the trail, checking the third wardstone of their circuit with a brass gauge and the bored competence of a man who had done this four hundred times. "What?"

"Do you hear anything?"

Vess lifted her lantern. She was older than him, gray at the temples, with a scar that split one eyebrow and made her look permanently skeptical. She cocked her head. Listened. Ulyana watched her face change — watched the boredom drain out of it like water out of a cracked cup.

"That's not right," she said.

They reached the third stone together. It stood taller than a man, black basalt veined with silver script that had been carved before anyone alive was born. The script was supposed to glow faintly at night, a pale moonlight-colored shimmer that said, *the wards are awake, the wards are holding.* The veins were dark. And when Ulyana put his bare palm against the stone, the cold that came off it was wrong — not the ordinary chill of night rock but something deeper, something like standing next to a door left open onto winter.

He pulled his hand back. His god-mark prickled.

Every sworn mercenary of Veth carried the mark: a small gray sigil burned into the inside of the left wrist, shaped like a closed eye. It was the sign of the covenant — your strength lent to the wards, the wards lending silence back to you, the god's quiet presence folded into every patrol, every watch, every prayer spoken without words. In eleven years, Ulyana's mark had warmed exactly once, the day he swore. Since then it had been nothing. A scar. A fact of his body, like the knot of bone where he'd broken his collarbone at fifteen.

Now it was burning. And the burn was *cold* — a spreading frost-ache up his forearm, sharp enough that he hissed and clutched his wrist.

"Ulyana?" Vess's voice had gone flat and careful. "Your arm."

"It's the mark." He pushed back his sleeve so she could see. In the lantern light, the closed eye seemed to have opened a hair — the lines of the sigil gone from gray to white, glowing faintly, like the wardstone script should have been glowing and wasn't.

Vess looked at it for a long moment. Then, quietly, in the tone she used for reporting deaths: "We should turn back."

"We haven't finished the circuit."

"*Ulyana.*"

He might have argued. He never got the chance. Because that was when the stars went out.

It didn't happen all at once. It happened like ink dropped in water. First the star directly overhead guttered and vanished, and Ulyana thought — absurdly — *cloud*. Then three more. Then ten. Then whole constellations he'd known since childhood, the Fisher, the Weeping Queen, the Seven Sisters of the ford, blinking out one by one while the sky between them stayed clear and black, blacker than sky, black as the underside of the world. Within the space of twenty heartbeats there was nothing above them at all. No stars. No moon, though the moon had been three-quarters risen when they set out. Just darkness pressing down like a lid.

Vess's lantern flame shrank to a blue bead and barely held.

Ulyana discovered he was praying without deciding to — the wordless, shapeless reaching-toward that Veth's sworn did instead of speech, and for the first time in years it felt less like habit and more like drowning. *Something is coming through. Something is already here.*

Then the wardstone screamed.

There was no better word for it. The basalt shrieked like a kettle, like a living thing, and the silver veins lit all at once — blinding, frantic — and then cracked. A fissure ran up the stone with a sound like ice calving off a glacier, and beyond it, up and down the ridge as far as Ulyana could see in the failing light, the other wardstones began to do the same. One after another after another. A line of dying fires receding into the dark north.

The hum came back all at once, but wrong — a howl, a feedback wail that Ulyana felt in his fillings and his bones — and then it cut off, and the silence afterward was the worst sound he had ever heard.

Eleven years. Eleven years of walking this border, believing the wards were as permanent as the mountains. And they were gone in under a minute.

"What," Vess whispered, "was that."

Ulyana didn't answer, because he was looking at the gap the dead wardstone had opened. Beyond the perimeter, past the old boundary cairns, the Gray Country stretched away under the starless dark — moorland and ruin and wild magic, the place Ashmoor's founders had walled themselves off from three centuries ago. Nothing moved in it. Nothing needed to. The open border itself was movement enough, a wound in the world, and the cold air pouring through it carried a smell Ulyana had never encountered but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a story: *old power. Unwalled. Coming home.*

And someone was running through it.

At first he took the figure for a deer — small, fast, weaving between the cairns. Then it stumbled, rose, kept running, and the lantern-light caught pale hair and a face turned back over one shoulder toward something pursuing in the dark. Toward the breach. Toward *them*.

"Contact," Ulyana said, and drew his knife, and stepped across the boundary of everything he had ever believed in.

* * *

She went down six feet from the broken wardstone.

One moment she was sprinting; the next her legs simply quit, and she hit the frozen turf hard, rolled once, and lay still. Ulyana reached her in seconds, knife out, and put himself between her and the Gray Country — because whatever she'd been running from hadn't crossed the line yet. Whatever it was, it had stopped at the breach. He felt it there, a pressure on the air, a vast attention, and then — slowly, reluctantly, the way a tide withdraws — it receded north into the dark, taking its cold with it. Not repelled. *Choosing.* That was somehow worse.

The girl was young — sixteen, seventeen. Pale hair matted with blood. A wound low on her side, soaked through her jacket, and her hands clenched around something at her throat hard enough that her knuckles had gone bloodless. A cord. A charm of some kind. Bone, by the look of it, small and yellowed and shaped like nothing natural.

"Don't," she rasped, when he knelt and reached for her. Her eyes were strange — too bright, the irises ringed with a color he had no name for. "Don't take it. Please. They'll kill me for it, but if *you* take it they'll kill you too, so just — don't."

"Who's 'they'?"

Her laugh was wet and awful. "You wouldn't believe me. You're one of *his*, aren't you? I can see the mark." Her gaze fixed on his wrist, the still-glowing closed eye, and something in her face cracked open — relief or grief, impossible to tell which. "Oh. Oh, thank the dark. You're one of *his*."

"I'm sworn to Veth," Ulyana said. "As is every soldier on this wall. Now talk. What chased you? What broke the stones?"

"The stones broke because I crossed them carrying what I carry." She tried to push herself up, failed, and sagged against a boundary cairn. Blood ticked steadily onto the frost. "The wards keep magic out, right? That's what they told you? That's not all they do. They keep one thing *in*, too. Kept it buried under the cathedral ruins for three hundred years. And I—" she lifted the bone charm a fraction, and even in the dying lantern light it seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it, "—I'm carrying the piece they stole. The piece they locked on *your* side of the wall so no one would ever find it."

"Girl," Vess said sharply, from behind him, "you're bleeding out on my border and talking nonsense."

"Am I?" Xanthe — though he didn't learn her name until later — bared bloody teeth at both of them. "Look up. Tell me about nonsense. Tell me again how safe the valley is."

Ulyana looked up. The sky stayed empty. The cold in his wrist spread another inch toward his elbow, and the closed eye of his god-mark burned and burned, and for the first time in eleven years of faithful service, Ulyana Zhou understood that everything he had patrolled, everything he had protected, everything he had *been* —

— had been a wall around a lie.

He sheathed his knife, stripped off his cloak, and got it around her shoulders.

"Can you walk if I carry half your weight?"

She stared at him. "I just told you your whole life is a lie."

"You did." He hauled her arm across his shoulders and stood, lifting, feeling her cry out and clamp her teeth against it. Behind them the breach gaped open on the starless dark. Ahead, down the valley, the lights of Ashmoor's watchfires looked suddenly very small, very far away, like embers on the floor of a well. "So we'll want to be moving fast."

They had made perhaps two hundred yards along the ridge road when the first horn sounded from the southern towers — three long notes, repeated, the alarm signal every child in Ashmoor learned before they learned their letters. And beneath it, thin and rising on the wind from the direction of the town, came bells. Not the ward-bells. Not any bell Ulyana knew.

These bells rang in a rhythm he'd only ever heard described in scripture: the tolling reserved for the death of a god.

Xanthe heard it too. Against his shoulder, she went rigid.

"They know I'm here," she breathed. "The ones who stole it. They've been hunting me for nine days." Her fingers found his sleeve and gripped, and her voice dropped to something fierce and shaking and young. "Mercenary. Sworn one. Listen to me — when they come, and they will come — don't let them take the charm. Whatever they promise. Whatever oath they make you swear. *Don't.*"

Ulyana said nothing. He tightened his grip on her, put his head down against the wind, and carried her down toward the valley that had raised him — toward the bells, and the dark, and whatever waited there — while behind him the last wardstone on the northern ridge split from crown to root and fell silent forever.
