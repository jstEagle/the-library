## 2. What the Valley Kept Out

The charm woke before she did.

Xanthe came up out of a dream of falling with her hand already closed around the bone piece at her throat, its surface humming like a struck tuning fork. For three heartbeats she didn't know where she was — and then the smells arrived: wet stone, tallow smoke, and underneath it all the faint mineral breath of wards, the smell of Ashmoor, of safety, of a cage.

*Undercity*, she remembered. The mercenary's name was Ulyana Zhou. He had carried her through a collapsing border and put her in a room with no windows and one door, which he had locked from the outside. The lock had cost him some pride; she'd heard him hesitate on the other side of it, heard the small scrape of metal and the longer silence after.

She lay still and listened to the charm.

It had been quiet for six days on the road north, quiet as river gravel, quiet enough that she'd almost let herself believe the stories wrong. Now it pulsed against her sternum like a second heart, slow and patient, and when she pressed it flat between her palms she felt something on the other side of it lean back.

*I know you're awake,* she thought at it. *Don't.*

Footsteps in the corridor. She dropped the charm beneath her collar and arranged herself against the wall, one knee drawn up, arms loose — the posture of someone bored rather than afraid. It was mostly true. Fear was a currency and she had learned long ago not to spend it where it could be seen.

The door opened and Ulyana Zhou filled most of it.

He looked worse than he had at the border, if that were possible. Someone — an undercity healer, by the smell of poultice — had bound the gash along his forearm, and there were new lines around his eyes that hadn't been carved there two nights ago. He set a bowl of barley soup on the floor between them with the exaggerated care of a man handling something that might be a weapon, and then he sat down across from her, cross-legged, sword across his knees, and did nothing at all for a full minute.

Xanthe ate the soup. It seemed rude to let a hostage situation go to waste.

"Name," he said finally.

"Tamsin."

"Tamsin what?"

"Just Tamsin." She scraped the bowl. "Like rain. Like Tuesday."

"You crossed the Ashmoor border on the night the wards died, carrying a wound that should have killed you, and you don't have a surname."

"I crossed the border because your collapsing perimeter tried to eat me," she said. "That wasn't a plan. I was running from something worse and your valley got in the way."

Something moved behind his eyes at that. Not anger. Hunger. "From what?"

"Bandits," said Xanthe.

"In the Gray Country." His voice was very even. "Where there is nothing to steal from anyone for forty miles."

"Optimistic bandits."

He didn't smile. She'd known men who smiled at bad jokes out of politeness, and men who didn't because they were doing sums behind their faces, and Ulyana Zhou was doing sums. She wished he'd stop. Sums were dangerous. Sums found the remainder.

"The elders want to send for the Oathbound," he said.

The word went through her like cold water through cloth. She kept chewing. Swallowed. "What's an Oathbound?"

"They're the order that maintains the wards." He watched her hands when he said it, not her face. A professional habit. "They've kept the valley sealed for two hundred years. Whatever broke through the other night — whatever you're running from — they'll want to know about it."

"Well." Xanthe set the bowl down with a click. "Sounds efficient. Send for them."

"You're lying."

"About which part?"

"All of it. Every word since you opened your mouth." He leaned forward slightly, and the lamplight caught the mark on his inner wrist — a small gray sigil like a closed eye, inked or branded into the skin. She had noticed it at the border, glowing faintly cold in the starless dark. It was dark now. Dormant. "I've spent my whole life guarding that border, Tamsin-just-Tamsin. I know what smugglers look like when they lie. They lie small. You lie like someone holding up a curtain and praying nobody looks behind it."

"And what do mercenaries look like," she asked, "when they're asking questions they've been told to ask?"

His jaw tightened. Good. She'd scored somewhere true.

"I'm not Oathbound," he said. "Not anymore, maybe. I swore to Veth before I ever swore to them. The god's gone silent, everyone knows it, but my mark—" He stopped. Flexed his wrist. "It burned last night. Cold. First time in nine years. Right when you came through."

*It burned,* she thought, *because the thing you swore to is sitting under my collar wondering what you taste like.* She kept her face still and her heartbeat down, and hated the charm for the way it made lying feel like blasphemy.

"So either you brought the silence with you," Ulyana finished quietly, "or you brought the cure. And until I know which, you're staying exactly where I can see you."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in a month," said Xanthe. "It's unsettling. Do it again sometime."

* * *

They let her out of the cell on the third day, which told her two things: that Ashmoor was more frightened than organized, and that Ulyana Zhou had argued for her and won by a margin thin enough to see light through.

The undercity was older than the town above it. She saw it in the bones of the place — arches cut by no mason's hand, stairways that spiraled the wrong way, doors bricked shut so long ago the mortar had gone the color of old teeth. Ashmoor had been built inside something, she realized. Around something. The wards weren't just a fence at the border; they were a lid, and the whole valley sat beneath it like soup under skin.

The elders held their argument in a round cellar lit by ward-lamps, and they didn't bother lowering their voices just because the prisoner was in the room.

"—two hundred years the Order has answered our petitions within a season," said a woman with a smith's burn-scarred forearms. "Send a bird. Let them come, let them look at her, let them tell us what tore a hole in our sky."

"And give them a fugitive to interrogate?" That was a thin man with ink-stained fingers, a records-keeper by his sleeves. "Serah Ives doesn't interrogate, Maren. She flays. Ask the last village that harbored a god-touched."

"She isn't god-touched—"

"Then what is she? Look at her!"

They all looked at her. Xanthe, who had spent years being looked at by people deciding whether to kill her, gave them her best village-idiot blink and scratched her ear.

Doss, the ward-smith — Maren — came to stand in front of her instead. Close enough that Xanthe could see the fine cracks in the woman's knuckles, silvered like kintsugi, the marks of a lifetime handling wardstone. "Show me your hands," Maren said.

Xanthe showed her hands. Calloused, road-burned, ordinary.

"Now take off the necklace."

The cellar went quiet. Under her collar, the charm lay warm and utterly still, playing dead with an enthusiasm that would have been funny if it hadn't been so obscene. *You owe me,* she thought at it.

"It's bone," Xanthe said. "My mother's. I don't take it off."

"Your mother's," repeated the records-keeper softly, and began, terribly, to write something down.

"Enough." Ulyana's voice, low, from the shadows by the stair. He hadn't spoken the whole session; she'd nearly forgotten he was there, which she suspected was his favorite way to exist. "The girl crossed a dead ward-line and lived. The Order will ask how. If we send for them, we send for questions none of us can answer. Give me a week. I'll find out what she is myself."

"With what method?" Maren asked dryly. "Soup?"

"Barley builds trust," said Xanthe, and several of the elders almost laughed before remembering to be afraid.

In the end they voted — seven to four, she counted — to delay. One week. No birds. And the records-keeper wrote that down too, in a hand like spider legs, and Xanthe memorized his face.

* * *

That night he brought her up to the surface wall, which she understood immediately for what it was: a test, a threat, and an apology, all wearing the same coat.

Ashmoor at night, without stars, was like standing inside a held breath. Above the valley the sky was a smooth black absence — not clouded, not overcast, simply *empty*, the way a socket is empty — and every soul in the town moved beneath it a little faster, spoke a little softer, as though the dark might be listening. Maybe it was. The charm lay quiet under her collar, but quiet now felt different from before. Quiet like something holding its tongue.

Ulyana stood at the parapet looking north, toward the breach in the ward-line, where a pale scar of light still bled upward from the broken stones. Guards were rebuilding the perimeter by torchlight, tiny figures passing wardstone hand to hand.

"You asked what an Oathbound is," he said, without turning. "Let me show you what they were."

He pulled back his sleeve. In the torchlight, the gray eye-mark on his wrist looked almost alive, lashes of darker gray feathering out from it across the vein.

"Every sworn member carries this. It's not decoration. When Veth still spoke, the mark warmed when you kept faith and burned when you broke it. My whole life it's been stone-cold. We kept the oaths anyway. That's the joke of it — the whole order keeping faith with a god who left." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Two nights ago it woke up. Cold fire, wrist to elbow. The first time in nine years."

"What did you do differently that night?"

"Dragged you out of a collapsing border."

Xanthe looked at the mark. At the exhausted man wearing it. Somewhere below her collarbone, the bone charm sat silent as a held secret, and she felt the shape of the truth pressing against the backs of her teeth like a sneeze.

*Tell him,* said something in her that sounded like her mother. *He carried you. He lied for you. Tell him.*

*He's Oathbred,* said something older and harder. *He was raised in a cage and taught to love the bars. Tell him and you tell the whole order.*

"Whatever you're carrying," Ulyana said quietly, still watching the north, "you should know that Serah Ives doesn't wait for invitations."

She turned — too fast, too obviously — and he met her eyes, and there it was: not the interrogation she'd been bracing for. Something wearier.

"How long have you known I'm carrying anything?"

"Since you slept. You hold it in your fist like a knife." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "I grew up on border stories. Bone charms are in all of them. None of them end well."

Down in the valley, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bell — the gate bell, three sharp strokes and a pause, the rhythm for visitors. Xanthe saw the guards on the wall straighten, saw torches converge on the south road, and saw, far down the black valley lane, a point of light that became two, became six, became a column riding at a pace far too disciplined for merchants.

Ulyana went very still beside her.

"Grey cloaks," he said. "Lances couched. Riding through the night with no banners." He exhaled slowly through his nose. "The elders didn't send for them."

"Then who did?"

He didn't answer, but she watched the answer move across his face — watched suspicion arrive, settle, and stay, aimed not at her anymore but at something behind the walls of his own life. Below them, the gate bells rang again, urgent now, and the column of riders halted at Ashmoor's threshold in perfect silence, and from the midst of them a single rider raised a hand bearing a ring that caught the torchlight and threw back no shadow at all.

"That," said Ulyana Zhou, in a voice she would remember for years, "is Serah Ives. And she's four days early."
