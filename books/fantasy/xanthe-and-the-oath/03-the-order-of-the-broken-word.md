## 3. The Order of the Broken Word

They came at dawn, and the valley let them in.

Ulyana stood on the wall above Ashmoor's south gate and watched the riders emerge from the mist that had pooled overnight in the low fields — six horses, gray cloaks, no banners. They didn't need banners. The Oathbound wore their authority the way other men wore scars: openly, and with the expectation that everyone would flinch.

He didn't flinch. He'd been one of them once, before his knee gave out at Colder Ford and they retired him to border patrol like a rusted tool too expensive to throw away. Six years of walking the perimeter, checking wardstones, saluting a god who never answered. Six years of silence.

The mark on his forearm itched beneath his sleeve.

Below, the gate captain was already waving them through, because of course he was. The elders had argued half the night about sending for the Order, and Ulyana had listened from the back of the council hall while men who'd never bled for their wards debated whether a wounded girl was worth summoning the most feared institution in the valley. In the end they hadn't decided anything, which meant the decision would be made for them.

It always was.

* * *

Serah Ives did not dismount in the courtyard so much as arrive at the ground, as if the horse were merely a formality she tolerated. She was younger than her reputation — mid-thirties, close-cropped hair gone iron-gray at the temples, a face built for listening rather than speaking. That was the trick of Serah Ives. Everyone remembered her silences afterward.

"Ulyana Zhou." Her voice carried across the courtyard without effort. "They told me you were still alive. I didn't believe them."

"People rarely do until they need something buried."

Something moved behind her eyes — not quite a smile. "Border patrol suits you."

"It doesn't. Ask me why sometime." He kept his hands loose at his sides, his weight even on both feet. Old habits. "You've come a long way to stand in a courtyard, Commander."

"I've come for the fugitive." No hedging. No ceremony. "A girl, sixteen or seventeen, carrying stolen property of the Order. She crossed your border last night under circumstances you will describe to me in full."

"The wards fell," Ulyana said. "All of them. Every stone from Graywatch to the river cracked at moonrise, and the stars went out over the whole valley. You'll have seen it from wherever you slept. So before we discuss my circumstances, Commander, perhaps you'll tell me how an order sworn to maintain those wards lost all of them in a single night."

The courtyard went very quiet. Two of her riders shifted in their saddles.

"We don't know," Serah said, and the honesty of it landed harder than any threat. "Which is why I need the girl. And the thing she carries."

* * *

They put Xanthe in the tithe-house, which was the closest thing Ashmoor had to a cell, and Ulyana sat outside the door on a stool that creaked every time he breathed. Through the wood he could hear her breathing too — fast, shallow, the breathing of someone doing arithmetic with escape routes.

"You can stop planning how to run," he said. "There's one window and it's barred, and the Oathbound have the street covered."

Silence. Then: "Are you going to give me to them?"

"That depends on what you're hiding."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again her voice had changed — smaller, but steadier underneath, like a rope with a good core. "If I tell you, you'll either help me or hand me over. There's no third thing."

"There's always a third thing. It's usually worse than both others."

He heard her laugh once, short and bitter. Then the floorboards creaked as she came to the door. "Open it. I'm tired of talking through wood like a ghost."

He opened it. She looked better than she had the night before — the wound in her side dressed, color back in her face — but her eyes had the flat shine of someone who hadn't slept and wasn't going to. She held out her closed fist.

"I need you to understand something first," she said. "I didn't steal it. I was given it. By someone who died making sure I got away with it. So when I say it matters, I'm not being dramatic. I'm telling you people have already died for what's in my hand, and more will unless it goes somewhere safe."

"Safe from whom?"

"From everyone." She opened her fist.

The charm was small — a sliver of bone, yellowed, drilled through with a leather cord. It should have been nothing. A keepsake, a relic, junk sold at market stalls to pilgrims who wanted to touch holiness without paying for it.

But Ulyana's god-mark went cold.

Not burned this time — cold, a deep ache like pressing his arm against winter stone, and beneath the ache, faint as a pulse through a wall, something that might have been a voice trying to remember how to be one. His breath left him. Six years of silence. Six years of speaking oaths into a void and hearing nothing come back, and now a dead woman's bone trinket was making his arm feel like the night the stars went out.

"You feel it," Xanthe whispered. Not a question. "Your mark. I saw you wince last night when they carried me in."

"What is it?"

"I don't know its name. The man who gave it to me called it a fragment of the Voice. Veth's Voice." Her fingers closed around it again, knuckles white. "The Order says the god went silent sixty years ago. The Order is lying, Ulyana. The god didn't go silent. The god was silenced. Stolen. And this is a piece of what's missing."

* * *

He asked for an hour before delivering her, and Serah granted it, which told him she already knew everything the girl could say and was simply watching to see what he would do. That was the other thing about Serah Ives. She tested people by giving them room to hang themselves.

So he hung himself.

He found her in the gatehouse, alone, studying a map of the valley with the fallen wards marked in red ink like a rash. "Commander. Before I bring you the girl, I want terms."

"Terms." She didn't look up.

"She comes before the Order alive and unharmed. She gets to speak. Whatever tribunal judges her hears her whole account, not just yours."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, I swear it." He pulled back his sleeve, baring the oath-mark — the interlocking lines of Veth's covenant, inked into his skin at nineteen, the way every sworn mercenary in the valley bore it. "On my mark. On my god. I swear she'll be delivered to the Order's justice, whole and living, and that I'll make no move to prevent her delivery."

The words came out clean. He'd sworn harder oaths with less conviction.

Serah finally looked up. For a long moment she studied him with those listening eyes, and he understood, too late, that she had been waiting for exactly this — that the map, the hour, the solitude, all of it had been arranged so that he would have the space to lie to her face.

"Say it again," she said softly.

"On my mark. On my—"

The pain took him mid-word.

It started in the mark and went through him like a blade of frost, every line of the covenant igniting white-cold, and his knees hit the flagstones and he heard himself make a sound he'd never made before, high and animal, and somewhere far away Serah was saying *stop, stop, enough* — but it wasn't her doing it. It couldn't be. The Oathbound couldn't punish oaths; only the god could, only Veth, and Veth was silent, Veth had been silent for sixty years—

The pain stopped. Ulyana lay on the cold stones, gasping, his arm a column of ice from wrist to elbow. The mark had changed. Where the interlocking lines had been smooth for six years, two of them now stood raised and dark, like scar tissue, like a word struck through.

*False*, the mark said, in the only language it had.

Serah crouched beside him. She didn't offer a hand. She just looked at him, and there was something in her face he had never seen there in twelve years of service — something almost like grief.

"Do you know what that means?" she said quietly. "Do you understand what just happened?"

"He heard me." Ulyana's voice came out wrecked. "Veth heard me. He's been listening this whole time, and he—" He stopped, because the next part arrived all at once, and it was worse. "And he punished me for swearing to hand her over. Which means handing her over is wrong. Which means the Order's claim on her is wrong. Which means—"

"Don't." Serah rose, fast, and glanced at the door. "Don't finish that sentence in a room with thin walls."

"Then finish it for me, Commander. You've served thirty years. You've sworn deeper oaths than mine. Tell me what it means when the god himself calls the Order a liar."

She was silent for a long time. Outside, the morning went on — carts, voices, a hammer striking iron somewhere, the ordinary machinery of a town that didn't know its sky had gone black.

"When I was a novice," Serah said at last, not looking at him, "I asked my teacher why Veth's silence began the same year the old cathedral was sealed. She told me never to ask that question again. I was fifteen. I have spent twenty years not asking it." She turned back to him, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Last night the wards fell. This morning your god broke a sworn oath to protect a thief. Something has started, Zhou, and the elders know more about it than they've told any of us. So here are your terms, since you're fond of them: I ride north within the hour to report to the Chapterhouse at Vael's Crossing. The girl travels with me. Alive. Unharmed. Speaking rights at tribunal — I'll grant that much, because you swore for it and your god apparently co-signs your paperwork."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you both, and neither of you reaches a tribunal at all." She picked up her gloves from the table. "But between us, mercenary — off the record, unwitnessed, worth nothing — I'd think hard about the road north. Because if the elders silenced a god, then whoever carries proof of it won't survive the Chapterhouse. And I am beginning to suspect," she added, pulling on one glove, finger by finger, "that you already know someone who knows another way."

Ulyana pushed himself up off the stones. His arm throbbed with every heartbeat, the struck-through lines burning cold, and he thought of Xanthe behind her door doing arithmetic with escape routes, and Doss the ward-smith drunk in his workshop below the hill, and the long gray country beyond the northern wall where no oath had ever reached.

"The road north is bad this time of year," he said.

"It's terrible," Serah agreed. "Ride fast."

She left him standing in the gatehouse with a broken oath written into his skin and the first hope he'd felt in six years sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal — dangerous, painful, and impossible to put down.

By noon, the three of them were through the north gate before anyone thought to count the riders leaving.
