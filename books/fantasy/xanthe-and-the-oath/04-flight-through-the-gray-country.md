## 4. Flight Through the Gray Country

**ULYANA**

The Gray Country earned its name honestly. North of Ashmoor's border the wards had never reached, and without them the land grew strange in small ways — grass that rustled with no wind, pools that reflected a sky slightly wrong, stones worn smooth as if by centuries of hands no one remembered. Ulyana had trained along its southern edge as a boy and hated every mile of it.

He hated it more now, with three strangers and a stolen future riding behind him.

Doss rode like a man arguing with his horse and losing. She was fifty if she was a day, gray-streaked hair cropped short, forearms scarred with the fine burn-patterns of a ward-smith who'd worked live stone. Brannic rode like the horse should be grateful for the privilege: big, easy, laughing at nothing, with a deserter's brand half-hidden under his sleeve and eyes that counted exits the way other men counted coins.

And Xanthe rode between them all with her hand closed around something in her pocket, and would not say what.

They made the tree line by dusk — stunted pines clinging to a ridge above a valley of fog — and Ulyana called the halt because the horses were done and because he wanted, badly, to stop moving. Stopping meant talking. Talking meant questions he didn't have answers to.

He built the fire anyway. Cold was its own kind of confession.

"So," Doss said, settling onto a log like she owned it. "We're fugitives now. Officially. I'd like to know what we're fugitives *for*."

"You saw the wardstones crack," Ulyana said.

"I saw wardstones crack *around her*." Doss nodded at Xanthe. "I spent thirty years learning why those stones hold. They don't fail on their own, boy. They fail when something pushes."

Xanthe looked into the fire. The light moved across her face and she let it, saying nothing, and Ulyana felt the old mercenary patience rise in him — the stillness you used on men who thought silence was armor.

It lasted until the moon came up. Then Xanthe opened her hand.

The charm was bone, small as a knucklebone, carved with lines so fine they seemed to shift when you weren't looking. It did not glow. It did not need to. Looking at it felt like standing in an empty cathedral and knowing someone had just stopped speaking.

"This is why the stars went out," she said. "This is why your wards broke. Because I carried this across your border, and it's louder than anything your valley has ever kept out."

"Louder how?" Doss leaned forward despite herself.

"Because it's a piece of a voice." Xanthe turned the charm slowly in her fingers. "Not a recording. Not an echo. A *piece*. When Veth was silenced — truly silenced, sixty years ago — the Order didn't destroy his voice. They couldn't. You can't unmake a god's voice; you can only divide it. So they sealed the greater part in a place I've been running from since I could walk, and a fragment—" her fist closed again, "—a fragment someone hid inside me. Inside my family. For generations."

Ulyana's god-mark burned cold beneath his collar. He put his hand over it and said nothing, because what was there to say? *My god speaks through the thing you're carrying, and neither of us asked for this.*

Brannic whistled low. "A god in your pocket. That's a heavy inheritance, girl."

"It's not a god." Xanthe's voice cracked and steadied. "It's worse. It's everything a god *said*. Every promise. Every covenant. Including yours, mercenary. Your oath came out of this."

Ulyana met her eyes across the fire. "Then we're both carrying pieces of the same corpse."

"No," she said softly. "We're both carrying pieces of the same voice. There's a difference, and I think you know it."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley, fog swallowed a sound that might have been hooves and might have been nothing.

"We sleep in shifts," Ulyana said, because someone had to be practical, and because he couldn't look at her anymore without his mark aching. "Doss first watch. I'll take second. Nobody wanders."

"Aye, captain," Brannic said, grinning, and lay back with his arms behind his head as though the world hadn't just changed shape.

* * *

**XANTHE**

She didn't sleep. She never slept well with the charm close, but tonight it was different — tonight it felt almost warm, almost attentive, like a cat pretending not to listen.

So she heard Ulyana take his watch, and heard him sit down beside her instead of across the fire, which was its own kind of message.

"You knew my oath," he said quietly. "How?"

"The charm recognizes its own." She kept her voice low so the others wouldn't wake. "When I crossed your border, it rang against every vow sworn to Veth inside the wards. Yours burned brightest. Six years of service, renewed daily. It's very... disciplined. Very sad."

"Sad."

"Like a man talking to a door that never opens." She glanced at him. "You kept praying anyway."

"I kept *working*, anyway. Praying was incidental."

"That's what sad means, mostly."

He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. In the firelight his face looked younger than his scars suggested — early thirties, maybe, with the particular tiredness of someone who'd been loyal longer than loyalty deserved.

"Why did you come out?" he asked. "Of wherever you came from. Why cross the wards at all, knowing what it would do?"

Xanthe watched the flames. Honesty was a currency she'd learned to spend carefully, but the fire made it cheap, and she was tired of being a locked box among people who might die for her by morning.

"My grandmother kept the charm before me. And her mother before her. We're a long line of women who carried a god's word and told no one, and every one of them died waiting for instructions that never came. My mother died believing the Order would come back for it. Make it right. Return it." Her jaw tightened. "Last month, Oathbound riders came to our village. Not for the charm. For *me* — they'd finally traced the bloodline. And the way their commander talked about it..." She shook her head. "They didn't want to return anything. They wanted to finish the job. Bury the last piece where no one would ever find it, and let the silence be permanent."

"So you ran."

"So I ran north, because north is where the greater part is sealed, and I am apparently my grandmother's granddaughter — stupid enough to think I could give the voice back myself." She laughed, bitter and quiet. "Instead I broke your valley."

"The valley was already broken," Ulyana said. "We just didn't know it. Wards don't keep magic out, Xanthe. They keep it *in*. Something older than your charm is under Ashmoor, and everyone who built those stones knew it and told no one. Sound familiar?"

She stared at him. "Your whole valley is a lockbox."

"Whole families of them, apparently." This time it was a real laugh, brief and rough, and something in her chest loosened at the sound.

From across the dead fire, Brannic's voice drifted up, too casual by half: "If you two are done confessing, some of us are trying to sleep before the warlord's riders find us."

Xanthe froze. "...What warlord?"

Brannic sat up. In the dark his grin had gone thin. "Kavrel March. The Godless King, if you believe his press. I served him four years before I got sensible." He tapped his branded arm. "And I'm telling you free of charge: he's gathering everything that hates the Order, and everything that loves the gods' old power, and he's marching them north to the same cathedral you're walking toward. He knows about the voice, girl. He's known for decades."

"How do you know where we're going?" Ulyana's hand had found his knife without seeming to move.

"Because it's the only place worth going, and I can read a map." Brannic spread his empty hands. "Look — I ran from Kavrel because I saw what he does with power. I'm riding with you because maybe you're the ones who end it. Or maybe I'm the worst decision either of you ever made. Ask me again at the ford."

Silence held the camp for a long moment. Then Doss, from her watch at the ridge, said dryly: "For what it's worth, I trust him about as far as I can throw him, which is not far, because he's enormous. But he stood between me and the gate-guard in Ashmoor without being asked. So."

Ulyana looked at Xanthe. She realized she was waiting for his judgment — she, who had trusted no one's judgment but her own since her mother died.

"He stayed at the gate," Ulyana said finally. "That counts for one night. One."

"One night," Brannic agreed pleasantly, and lay back down, and Xanthe noticed that even lying down he faced the road south.

* * *

**ULYANA**

Second watch bled into third, and the others finally slept — Doss snoring like a landslide, Brannic unnervingly silent, Xanthe curled around her closed fist with the bone charm tucked against her sternum.

Ulyana sat with his back against a pine and took out his knife and, for no reason he could name, pressed the flat of it against his god-mark.

Cold flared through the blade. Faint. Rhythmic.

Almost like breathing.

Six years of silence, and here in the Gray Country, miles beyond the last wardstone, his god's mark had woken — and it had woken the night Xanthe crossed the border. He turned the thought over the way Doss might turn a flawed stone, looking for the fracture line.

*Your oath came out of this,* she'd said.

He looked at the sleeping camp. A disgraced smith who'd lied to protect strangers. A deserter who watched the road out of habit or guilt or treachery. A girl carrying sixty years of stolen divinity in her pocket, running toward the one place in the world that wanted her most and would use her worst.

None of them were his people. None of them had sworn anything.

And yet when he imagined tomorrow's ride, he didn't imagine it alone, and the realization sat strangely in his chest — warmer than the coal Serah Ives had left there, and harder to put down.

Down in the southern darkness, a light flickered. Then another. Torch-fire, strung along the ridgeline behind them in a slow, deliberate arc. Not pursuing. Signaling.

Counting.

Ulyana stood, woke Doss with a hand on her shoulder, and pointed.

"They're not chasing us," he said quietly. "They're herding us north. Someone wants us exactly where we're going."

Above them, through a tear in the clouds, a single star — the first in four nights — blinked uncertainly back to life.

It was directly overhead. Directly north.

Directly over the cathedral.
