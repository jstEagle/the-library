## 5. The Warlord of the Godless

They took her at the treeline of the Gray Country, in the hour before dawn when even the mist seemed to be holding its breath.

Xanthe heard the horses first — not the hoofbeats but the leather, the soft creak and jingle that a careless rider never quite silenced. She had time to shake Ulyana awake, to press the bone charm flat against her sternum beneath her shirt, to whisper *don't* as he reached for his sword. Then the net came down out of the dark like a spider's judgment, weighted with iron hooks, and the world collapsed into rope and elbows and Brannic's voice shouting — *I'm one of yours, I'm one of yours* — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly who had led them here.

The last thing she saw before they hooded her was Ulyana on his knees in the frost, blood at his temple, held by two men who feared him enough to use four hands each.

* * *

The war camp sat in a bowl of dead hills where nothing grew except ash-grass and ambition. They marched her through it with the hood still on, letting her hear everything: the ring of forges, the wet snap of banners, a hundred voices speaking a hundred accents, all of them godless by choice or by exile. The Oathbound would have called this place an abomination. Xanthe, walking blind with her wrists bound, thought it sounded like every city that had ever thrown her out.

The hood came off in a tent lit by a single brazier, and Kavrel March was smaller than she'd imagined.

She had built him from rumor into something mountainous — the warlord who stole a god's voice, the man whose name mothers in three kingdoms used to frighten children. What stood before her was lean and gray-templed, dressed plainly, with the kind of face that would vanish in a market crowd. Only his eyes betrayed him. They were pale as river ice, and they did not move when he looked at her. Everything else about him moved; his hands were restless, turning a ring on his finger, adjusting a fold of his sleeve. But the eyes stayed fixed, and fixed on her, and Xanthe felt the charm against her breastbone go cold as a coin left in snow.

"You're the girl from Ashmoor," he said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody from nowhere."

"Ulyana Zhou swears an oath to a god who cannot hear him. Doss the ward-smith hasn't touched a working stone since his guild cast him out. And Brannic—" Kavrel's mouth curved, without warmth. "Brannic I paid myself. So you see, the company you keep tells me a great deal about who you are." He stepped closer. The brazier threw his shadow long across the tent wall, and the shadow was bigger than the man. Much bigger. "What do you carry?"

"Nothing."

He struck her once, open-handed, almost gentle, the way one quiets a dog. Her ear rang. She kept her feet because keeping her feet was the only weapon she had.

"Nothing," he repeated softly. "A fugitive crosses the wards the night they shatter, runs north with a sworn mercenary of Veth, and carries nothing. Child, I have been patient for thirty years. Do not make me impatient now."

Thirty years. The number snagged in her mind like a fishhook. She let him see her thinking it — let him believe she was naive enough to be lured by curiosity rather than simply buying seconds — and said, "You really did it, then. You stole his voice."

Something flickered behind those ice-pale eyes. Hunger, old and scabbed over.

"The Order calls it theft," Kavrel said. "I call it rescue. Veth was drowning in his own covenant — a god bound to silence by his worshippers' oaths, unable to speak, unable to act, worshiped by people who used his muteness as proof of his wisdom." He laughed, a dry sound like paper burning. "I gave him freedom. I took the voice out of the prison they'd built for it. That part worked beautifully." He turned the ring on his finger again, faster now. "Holding it is another matter. A voice wants a throat. It has been trying to wear mine for three decades, and I am — apparently — insufficient."

*Insufficient.* The word dropped into Xanthe's stomach like a stone into a well. Because the charm hadn't tried to wear her. In the hollow places, in her worst dreams, it had *spoken* to her. Asked her things. Trusted her, almost.

Kavrel watched her too closely. She rearranged her face into blankness and prayed to a god who could not hear her that blankness was enough.

"The wards broke," she said. "Everyone says it was your doing."

"My doing? No. I've spent thirty years trying to break them and failed." His restlessness stilled all at once, which was somehow worse than the motion. "But I know what broke them, because I know what was *in* them. The elders of Ashmoor didn't build walls to keep magic out, girl. They built a second prison. One prison for the voice, and one around the valley so no one would ever come asking questions about the first." He leaned in, and up close she could smell him: woodsmoke and something older, mineral, like the air inside a tomb. "Your charm cracked both. Which means whatever holds the voice has chosen a keeper at last. And I intend to meet my successor."

* * *

They gave her a tent, a guard, and a bowl of barley soup she was too frightened to eat. Through the canvas she heard the camp settling into night, and under it — faint, rhythmic, wrong — a sound like breathing that came from the ground itself.

The charm pulsed against her skin. Once. Twice. A heartbeat that wasn't hers.

*Little thief,* it murmured, in the voice she had been hearing in dreams since Ashmoor, the voice that was warm and ruined and vast. *You should not have come.*

"You could have warned me," she whispered into her collarbone. "Back at the treeline. You knew."

*I am not a warning system. I am a wound.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was quieter, almost human. *The man who wears that ring has been carrying a splinter of me for thirty years. Can you feel it? He is close.*

She could. Since arriving in the camp, the charm had felt heavier, as if drawing weight out of the air. As if something nearby recognized it.

"He wants to finish taking you," she said. "To become—"

*A god. Yes. He told you the truth; that's the horror of him. Kavrel March lies rarely and precisely. He rescued me from one cage to build me another, and when I wouldn't fit inside him, he began gathering the godless, thinking faith might be a solvent. Thirty years of armies to dissolve one lock.* The voice sighed, and the sound of it stirred the tent flap though there was no wind. *And now the lock walks into his camp wearing your ribs.*

"What happens at dawn?"

Silence. Long enough that she thought the voice had withdrawn entirely.

*He will ask you to open me,* it said at last. *And you will refuse, and someone will hurt you, and eventually he will stop asking and start taking. Unless.*

"Unless what?"

*Unless you convince him you're already opening yourself. That you serve me willingly and can deliver me to him slowly, on terms — because if he rips me loose by force, girl, there won't be enough of me left to make anyone a god. Just a scream and a crater.* A beat. *He knows this. It's why he hasn't taken you already, bleeding and bound as you are. Fear is the only leash on that man, and I am the thing he fears most.*

Xanthe lay back on the cot and stared at the smoke-darkened canvas above her. Somewhere out there, Ulyana was alive — she had to believe it, because the alternative was a door she couldn't afford to open. Brannic's betrayal sat in her chest like swallowed glass; she'd shared fire and bread with him, had almost liked him. *Later*, she told herself. Grief later. Survival now.

"They'll kill us anyway," she said. "Once they have you. Loose ends."

*Kavrel kills when killing is cheaper than keeping. Convince him keeping you is priceless.* The voice softened. *You lied to the Oathbound commander's face in Ashmoor and made her doubt herself. You talked a river-spirit down from drowning four travelers — yes, Ulyana told us, around the fire, when he thought we were asleep. You are the best liar I have ever worn, little thief. Lie well tomorrow.*

"And after tomorrow?"

Another silence. When it spoke again, the voice had gone strange and far away, like sound carried up a deep shaft.

*After tomorrow, either I belong to everyone or to no one. Never again to one throat. Promise me that, and I will help you lie.*

Xanthe pressed her hand flat over the charm, over the small buried heat of it, and made the promise in the dark, knowing promises to gods were the oldest trap in the world.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the tent seams, and with it came Serah Ives' name, spoken by a runner outside — the Oathbound commander sighted at the southern ridge with a full company, two days out. Kavrel sent for her within the hour.

This time the tent was full: captains along the walls, maps weighted with daggers, Brannic standing near the brazier with a fresh scar across his cheekbone and eyes that refused to find hers. She catalogued his guilt the way she catalogued exits, and put both away for later.

Kavrel sat on a plain wooden chair that might as well have been a throne, given how still everyone else stood.

"Serah Ives will be here in two days," he said. "The woman who commands the order that caged a god. And I have in my possession, as of last night, the keeper of that god's voice." He gestured lazily at Xanthe. "Tell my captains what you told me. About carrying nothing."

She could feel the whole room waiting, feel the charm burning cold against her sternum like a held breath. Terror was a living thing in her throat. She braided defiance through it, strand by strand, the way her mother had braided rope — and stepped forward instead of back.

"I told you the truth," Xanthe said, and lifted her chin. "It isn't something you *carry*. It's something you *serve*. And it doesn't answer to threats, Kavrel March. It answers to willingness." She let her gaze travel the room, captain to captain, letting them wonder. "You've spent thirty years trying to take a god by force. I walked out of his broken prison carrying a piece of his trust. Ask yourself which of us he'd rather speak through."

The silence stretched taut as wire. Kavrel turned his ring. Turned it again. Those ice-pale eyes never blinked.

"Open it," he said quietly. "Here. Now. Show me willingness."

Her heart slammed once, hard, against the charm — against the voice coiled inside it, listening.

"Not here," she said. "Not with iron in the room and men who flinch when the wind moves. There's a reason the Order built its first prison where they built it. Take me to the hollow cathedral, and I'll show you a god being born."

Kavrel March smiled, and for the first time she saw the shadow behind him move on its own.

"Captain," he said, without looking away from her, "break camp. We ride for the cathedral."
