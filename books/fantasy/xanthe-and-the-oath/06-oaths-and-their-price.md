## 6. Oaths and Their Price

The river found them before they found it.

They heard it first as a hush in the gray country's silence — a sound so out of place that Brannic stopped mid-stride and put a hand to his axe. Then the trees thinned, and there it was: the Vael, running wide and silver-bright through a land where nothing else had color anymore. It moved against the wind. Ulyana watched the current bend uphill for three heartbeats before his eyes accepted what they were seeing.

"The Vael," Doss said quietly. He had not spoken much since the ford two days back, and when he did speak, it was in the reverent tone of a man standing in a temple he'd only read about. "I warded bridges over this river when I was young. Never once saw it do *that*."

"It does what it likes past Ashmoor," Xanthe said. She stood with her hood back, the bone charm hidden beneath her shirt but her hand pressed flat over it, the way she always did when something old was near. "And what it likes is bargains."

Ulyana knew the stories. Every child in Ashmoor knew them. The river-spirit of the Vael was older than the wards, older than the valley, older — some said — than Veth's coming north. It asked prices no sensible person paid, and it never forgot a debt.

"We could go around," Brannic said. "North along the bank. Find a shallow crossing."

"There are no shallows on the Vael," Xanthe said. "Not where it wants something."

"And behind us?" Ulyana turned. The gray country stretched south, featureless under a sky that still hadn't decided whether to hold stars or not. Somewhere in that emptiness, Serah Ives and her Oathbound riders were closing the distance. He'd counted on the terrain to slow them. He'd been counting wrong. Twice now, at dusk, he'd seen the far-off gleam of lanterns moving in formation — patient, unhurried, certain.

"Behind us is worse," he said.

They went down to the water.

* * *

The spirit rose without rising. One moment the current lay flat and mirror-still; the next, a figure stood upon it — a woman-shaped absence, a silhouette cut from flowing water, her edges dripping upward into mist. Where her face should have been there was only the smooth suggestion of one, like a face seen through falling rain.

"Mercenary," she said. Her voice came from everywhere the water touched. "Sworn to the silent god. I know your mark. It tastes of iron and unpaid debts."

Ulyana's oath-mark burned cold beneath his collarbone, the way it had since the night the stars went out. He kept his hand away from it. "We need passage across."

"Everyone needs passage across." The spirit drifted closer, and where she passed, the water beneath her stilled into glass. "The question is what you'll give. Not coin." A sound that might have been laughter moved through the reeds. "I have drowned kings who offered me coin."

"Then name your price," Ulyana said.

The spirit turned its faceless head toward him alone. Xanthe shifted at his shoulder; Brannic's hand tightened on his axe; and the river went very, very quiet, as if the whole Vael were holding its breath to listen.

"A memory," the spirit said. "One of yours. Freely given, wholly given. You will not miss what you cannot remember, mercenary. That is the mercy of it."

"No," Xanthe said immediately. "Take mine instead—"

"You have nothing I want, charm-bearer." The spirit didn't even look at her. "Your memories are borrowed. Half of them aren't yours yet. But him—" the blank face tilted toward Ulyana, "—him, I've wanted for years. You carry grief like a stone in your chest, little oath-sworn. I can taste it from the far bank. Let me choose which stone comes out."

Ulyana thought of Sera then — though he would not think of her by that name again for another hour, because the price was already being paid before he understood he'd agreed to pay it. That was how the spirit worked. He felt the bargain settle over him like cold water, felt the word *yes* leave his mouth without ever deciding to say it, and then—

Then the spirit reached into him, gently, the way you'd lift a coal from a fire with tongs, and took.

He staggered. The world tilted. For a moment there was a hole in him shaped like a girl with ink-stained fingers who used to dare him to climb the mill roof, and he stood at the edge of that hole, staring down into it, knowing something precious had lived there and knowing he would never learn what.

"What did you take?" His voice came out cracked.

"Something about a roof," the spirit said, almost kindly. "And someone who made you brave enough to fall. She is safe with me. I keep my dead well — better than most gods do." The faceless head turned toward the far shore. "Passage for four. Cross where the willow leans. And mercenary — a gift, since you paid more than you knew."

The water parted. A path of stillness opened across the Vael's whole bright width, firm as flagstone.

"Why do you want our memories?" Xanthe asked. There was an edge in her voice, fear sharpened into anger. "What are you *for*?"

The spirit began to sink back into the current. Before she went, she said, "I am keeping them safe too. Everything the old covenant swallowed, everything the Order locked away — it all flows downhill eventually. Even to me. Ask your silent god, when you find his voice, why he built a world where the price of devotion is forgetting what you loved." A ripple, a laugh, gone. "Ask him why his own servants pay first."

* * *

They crossed in silence. On the far bank, Ulyana sat down hard on a stone and tried to find the missing thing.

He couldn't. That was the horror of it. He knew — because the others told him, because the shape of the wound remained — that he'd had a sister. Xanthe said her name softly, twice, until it stuck: Sera. Sera, who died when he was nineteen. But the name was a label on an empty jar. When he reached for her face, he found the mill instead, and a roof, and a vertigo of height, and no reason at all for any of it.

"I did this for the mission," he said. It sounded like someone else saying it.

"You did it for us," Xanthe said. She crouched in front of him. Up close, her eyes were the color of the river. "Don't you dare make it smaller than that."

"My oath—" He stopped. Pressed his palm flat over the mark, feeling its cold pulse. "My oath says I give everything to Veth. My strength. My years. My death, when it comes." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "It never said anything about my sister. Nobody told me the god eats sideways. That every vow has teeth you don't see until you're already in the mouth."

Doss looked away. Brannic pretended to check the straps on his pack. They all carried their own stones; none of them offered to trade.

That was when Ulyana understood what the spirit had really shown him. Not the memory. The arithmetic. He had spent eleven years paying installments on a promise to a god who had never once answered — and tonight he'd learned the silence wasn't absence. The mark still punished his false oaths. Veth still listened. Which meant the god had chosen silence, or been silenced, and either way, Ulyana had been paying into an account no one was watching.

Except the river. The river was watching. The river remembered what the faithful forgot.

"Come on," Xanthe said, and pulled him up by the wrist. "Grieve on the move. It's faster."

* * *

Serah Ives caught them at dusk, two miles north of the crossing, where the gray country gave way to the first true forest — black pines, real dark, real shadow. Ulyana heard the horses too late. Lantern-light bloomed between the trunks, six riders in white-and-ash livery, and Serah at their head, dismounting with her sword already drawn but pointed at the ground.

"Stand away from the fugitive, Zhou."

Ulyana stepped between her and Xanthe and drew his own blade. Behind him, Brannic cursed and reached for his axe, and Doss fumbled with a ward-chalk stub that wouldn't help anyone.

"Six of you," Ulyana said. "Four of us. You know how this math goes, Commander."

"I know how it goes." Serah walked her horse forward a step. In the lantern light her face was drawn, and there was something new in her eyes that hadn't been there on Ashmoor's wall — something that looked uncomfortably like doubt. "You swore a false oath to me, Zhou. On Veth's name. Do you know what happens to Oathbound who lie on the god's name?"

"They get struck down," Ulyana said. "If the god is listening."

Silence. One of her riders shifted in the saddle.

"He is listening," Serah said quietly. "You flinched when you lied to me. I saw it. And I've spent nine days telling myself I imagined it, because if Veth hears false oaths, then Veth hears *anything*, and if Veth hears anything, then the Order's entire doctrine — the Silence, the Sealing, the Great Renunciation —" She stopped herself. Her jaw worked. "Give me the girl and the charm, Zhou. That's the command. That's all I am anymore. A command looking for a reason."

"Then don't follow it."

"Don't tempt me with simplicity." She dismounted. Walked toward him slowly, sword lowered, until they stood close enough that he could see the sweat on her brow despite the cold. "Three weeks ago I requested the original texts of the Sealing Rite. The ones from before the codification. Do you know what the archivists told me? That they don't exist. That they were burned in the Year of Ash, by order of the Council of Elders. The single most important document in the Order's history, and it was *burned*, Zhou, by the same elders who teach novices that the records are complete and holy and true."

"Maybe they were protecting something," Ulyana said.

"Yes. That's what frightens me." Serah's voice dropped. "I served beside Elder Corvane for fifteen years. I watched him weep at the Renewal rites. And last month I watched him sign orders sending a retrieval company after a half-starved girl with a bone charm — with *kill authority*, Zhou, kill authority over a child, sealed and expedited, bypassing every tribunal the Order has. I asked why. He told me the matter was above my station." Her knuckles whitened on the scabbard. "Nothing is above my station. I have bled for this Order since I was twelve years old. So I rode ahead of my own company tonight to ask you, man to man, oath-breaker to oath-keeper: what do you know that I don't?"

Ulyana held her gaze. Behind him, he felt Xanthe go very still.

"I know the charm holds a piece of Veth's voice," he said. "I know Kavrel March stole the rest of it thirty years ago, and wants the fragment to finish becoming a god. And I know that whoever sealed the voice in the first place — whoever built the rite — didn't do it to protect the world." He paused. "I think they did it to own him. And I think the people who wrote those orders you're carrying are the same people."

Serah closed her eyes. For a long moment she simply breathed, a soldier standing in a dark wood with her whole life balanced on a blade's edge.

"If I ride back without you," she said, "Corvane sends the full company next time. Forty riders. Ward-casters. Things I'm not cleared to know about." She opened her eyes, and made her choice the way soldiers do — quickly, before doubt could catch up. "So I'm not riding back yet. We make camp here. My company believes I've taken you prisoner. You'll be bound — loosely. And tomorrow, Zhou, you're going to tell me everything, starting with the part where a river-spirit took a memory out of you, because I watched your face just now and you're grieving someone you can't name."

Ulyana stared at her. "Why? Why turn?"

"Because I took an oath too." Serah turned away, signaling her riders to dismount. "And unlike yours, mine hasn't been answered in fifteen years. I'm beginning to wonder whose fault that is."

Beyond the lantern light, far off through the black pines, something howled — long, low, and wrong, a sound with words folded inside it. Xanthe's hand flew to the charm.

"That's not wolves," she whispered.

Brannic had gone gray. "No," he said. "That's the warlord's horn. Kavrel knows exactly where we are."
