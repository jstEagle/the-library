## 7. The Hollow Cathedral

**XANTHE**

They smelled it before they saw it.

The cathedral's ruin announced itself on the wind as a sweetness gone wrong — incense that had burned so long ago it had turned to rot, honeyed and grave-cold, seeping out of the gray hills like breath from a buried mouth. Xanthe reined up at the ridge and looked down into the bowl of the valley where the Hollow Cathedral sat in its own wreckage.

It had been built to be enormous. That was the first thing she understood. Even broken — even with its spine collapsed into a ridge of black stone and its towers snapped off like teeth — the thing was vast beyond sense, a skeleton of a building made for a congregation of thousands, or for a god. Its walls were the same dark basalt as the wardstones of Ashmoor, but here every block was carved. She could see the carvings even from the ridge: spirals, oaths, names. Ten thousand names, maybe more, cut into every surface, worn smooth in places by centuries of hands.

"Nobody told me it was this big," Doss said quietly. The ward-smith hadn't spoken much since the ford. He kept touching the tools at his belt as if checking they were still real.

"It wasn't built," Brannic said. "It was grown. That's what the old stories say. Veth's first temple came up out of the rock when the god still spoke." He spat. "Stories are shit, mostly. But look at it."

Xanthe looked. And inside her coat, against her sternum, the bone charm *answered*.

It had been quiet since the warlord's camp — a cold little weight, a stolen thing pretending to be nothing. Now it warmed, then burned, then sang, one clear note beneath her skin like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass. Her breath went out of her. Ulyana caught her arm before she swayed.

"You felt that."

"Yes." Her voice came out strange to her own ears. "It knows we're home."

"That's not home." Ulyana's face was grim in the flat gray light. "That's a cage."

"It's *its* cage," Xanthe said. "Don't you see? Everything of Veth that's left is pulling toward this place. The voice wants to go back to where it was taken from." She touched the charm through the cloth. It pulsed against her palm like a second heart. "Which means if I'm ever going to understand what Kavrel did — what the Order did — it's here. At the wound."

Ulyana stared down at the ruin for a long moment. She watched him do the thing he always did, the mercenary's arithmetic: threat, ground, retreat, cost. Then he closed his eyes briefly — the way he did when his god-mark itched — and said, "We go in before dark. Nobody touches anything without Doss looking at it first. And Xanthe—" His eyes found hers, pale and steady. "You don't try to talk to it. Not yet. Promise me."

She promised him. It was easy to promise. Promises were just words, and words had never once kept her safe.

* * *

**ULYANA**

Inside, the cathedral was worse than outside. Better too, though Ulyana hated himself for thinking it.

The nave stretched away under a ceiling of shattered vaulting, open now to a sky the color of dirty wool. Where the roof had fallen, the rubble had been left untouched for decades — no looters, despite everything of value such a place must have held. He understood why as they moved deeper. The silence in here was not empty. It was *occupied*. It pressed on the ears like deep water. Every footfall sounded wrong, arriving late, as though the air itself hesitated to carry it.

His oath-mark had been burning since the ridge. Not painfully — not yet — but with a low insistent heat, like a coal wrapped in linen against his skin. In Ashmoor it had slept for years, dumb ink. Since the night the stars went out it had woken by degrees, and here, at the center of whatever crime had silenced his god, it woke fully.

"Veth was heard here," he said, without meaning to say it aloud.

Doss glanced at him. "What?"

"The acoustics." Ulyana stopped in the middle of the nave, where the fallen roof opened a circle of gray sky above them like a dead eye. "Look at the floor. The channels cut into it. They're not drains — they're *grooves*. Sound-grooves. This whole building was an instrument. Whatever was said here, the walls carried it. The walls *kept* it." He crouched and laid his bare fingers in one of the grooves. Cold, of course. Stone was stone. But beneath the cold he felt something fainter than vibration, fainter than memory — a rhythm. A patience. "The voice wasn't just imprisoned in the valley. It was sung into this place first. Bound here, then carried to the wards."

"Then the charm—" Xanthe began.

"I know what you're going to say."

"You don't stop me by knowing."

He stood. Around them the others had gone quiet — Brannic leaning on a fallen pillar with studied boredom, Doss tracing a ward-diagram in the dust with one careful finger. Ulyana took Xanthe's shoulders. Up close she looked hollowed out, sleep-starved, the charm's note visibly thrumming under her skin like a wire pulled too tight. He had dragged this girl out of a collapsing border two weeks ago and had not managed, in all that time, to make her understand that she was allowed to survive this.

"When I swore my oath," he said, "the priest put his hand over my mark and said: *you speak, and Veth listens; Veth speaks, and you obey.* That was the covenant. Both directions. Always both directions." His thumb pressed the mark through his sleeve, hot as a brand. "If you open yourself to that thing alone — a fragment of a stolen god's voice, in a place built to hold gods — you will not be talking to it. You'll be a door, and it will come through you, and doors break."

"So anchor me," Xanthe said.

He blinked. "What?"

"Your mark wakes when Veth listens. Serah said so. *You* said so." Her eyes were fever-bright and absolutely steady. "I can't hold the thread alone — I've known that since the war-camp, since it nearly poured me out of my own skull. But you're a sworn vessel. You're built to carry a god's attention. So don't stand there and watch me drown. *Hold the rope.*"

"It could fuse us. Permanently. Whatever happens in your head—"

"Then it happens in mine too," she said. "That's the point. That's the whole point, Ulyana. Everyone who ever held power over this voice wanted it *alone* — Kavrel, the elders, the whole rotten chain of them. Alone is how it got stolen. Maybe the only way to touch it safely is to not be alone."

Behind them, Brannic snorted softly. "She's going to do it whether you help or not. Girl's got that look. I've seen it on men right before they charge."

Ulyana didn't turn around. He looked at Xanthe — at this stranger, this thief, this fugitive who had dragged him out of the only world he knew and into the gray country where everything he'd sworn to was turning out to be a lie — and he thought about what his sister would have said, if he still had the memory of her voice to consult.

"All right," he said. "But we do it my way. Sitting, backs together. If I say *release*, you let go — no matter what you're seeing. Swear it."

"I swear," she said, and for once he believed her promises.

* * *

**XANTHE**

They sat in the center of the nave, back to back, his spine against hers, the oath-mark between their shoulder blades like a held breath. Doss chalked a crude containment ring around them both — "It won't stop a god," he admitted, "but it might slow one down enough to apologize" — and Brannic watched the doors with a crossbow across his knees, because Brannic always watched the doors.

Xanthe unwound the cord from her neck and held the bone charm in both palms.

Up close, in the cathedral's dead silence, she could finally hear what it had been trying to tell her all along. Not words. Words were the smallest part of it. Underneath the words was the *shape* of a voice — the shape of everything a voice could carry — grief and command and lullabies and war-cries, folded impossibly small, pressed into a sliver of yellowed bone like a scream pressed into a fist.

"Veth," she whispered. "I'm not here to keep you. I'm here to listen."

She opened herself the way Doss had taught her to open a lock — gently, finding the mechanism, asking rather than forcing.

The world went out.

Not dark — *out*, like a lamp pinched between wet fingers. She fell inward, down a throat of sound, and the voice poured toward her the way floodwater pours toward a breach, and it was so much larger than the fragment, oh, it was *everything*, it was the whole drowned choir, and she was a paper door in a hurricane. It rushed into her mouth and behind her eyes and it began, with terrible gentle efficiency, to take her apart — name by name, memory by memory, unmaking the stitches that held Xanthe together to find room for itself—

*Release.* Ulyana's voice, miles away, underwater.

She couldn't. There was no her left to obey with—

Then heat. A brand between her shoulder blades. His oath-mark flaring against her spine, and through it, something *gripped* her — not the god, not the flood, but Ulyana: his heartbeat, stubborn and mortal, hammering through the fused contact of their backs like a drummer refusing to stop. The covenant running both directions. *You speak, and Veth listens; Veth speaks, and you obey* — but underneath the old words she felt the new ones he was forging in the fire, wordless, furious, alive:

*I have you. I have you. Come back.*

She grabbed the rope of him.

The flood raged, and she raged back, anchored to a mortal pulse, and slowly — agonizingly — the voice stopped unmaking her and started, instead, to *notice* her. To notice them. The pressure shifted, became attention, became something almost like a gaze turning upon two small creatures huddled in a ruined throat of stone.

And in that gaze, Xanthe saw. Fragments, flashing like lightning:

A choir of robed figures singing a god into chains. Elders' faces she recognized — the Oathbound's founding faces, serene above their crime. A young Kavrel March kneeling among them, hungry-eyed, learning how theft was done. And beneath it all, at the bottom of everything, a single sensation so vast it barely registered as feeling:

*Loneliness.* Decades of it. A god's voice shut in the dark, listening to the valley live and die above it, hearing every prayer and able to answer none.

The vision released her all at once, and she came back into her body gasping, doubled over, Ulyana's hand fisted in the back of her coat, holding her to the world.

* * *

**ULYANA**

Afterward they sat in the rubble and shook, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

The containment chalk was scorched black in a perfect ring. Doss stared at it, then at them, with the expression of a man recalculating every certainty he owned. "Your marks," he said hoarsely. "Show me your marks."

Ulyana pushed up his sleeve. The oath-mark — the silent god's sigil, ink and scar, twenty years inert — had changed. The old spiral was still there, but threaded through it now ran a second line, fine as a hair, that had not existed that morning. He already knew what he'd find when Xanthe bared her forearm. The same thread crossed her skin, delicate and permanent, joining her own scattered marks into his design.

"A fusion," Doss whispered. "A true covenant-fusion. That hasn't happened since— I don't even know when that's happened. You're bound now. What touches one of you touches the other. If she burns, you burn. If he drowns—" He stopped, unable to finish, and didn't need to.

Brannic lowered the crossbow. "So the thief and the zealot are married now. Congratulations." But his voice lacked its usual poison, and he wouldn't quite look at them.

Ulyana barely heard any of it. He was watching Xanthe. She had gone gray after the vision, and now she sat with her knees drawn up, staring at the dead-eye circle of sky, and when she spoke her voice was very quiet.

"They didn't steal it to destroy it," she said. "That's what nobody knew. The elders were afraid — genuinely afraid. The voice was too much for any one person, so they caged it and called it mercy." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "But it's been *listening* all this time, Ulyana. Through the wards. Through your mark. It heard every prayer Ashmoor ever muttered, and it couldn't answer, and it has been alone in the dark for sixty years."

The heat under his own skin answered her — that vast patient loneliness he had felt through the fusion, still echoing in his bones. All those years he had believed his god silent. Silent, or absent, or dead. Never once had it occurred to him that the silence might be a prison wall, and that on the other side of it something had been pressing its ear to the stone, listening to him, all along.

"We can't give it to Kavrel," Xanthe said. "And we can't give it back to the Order. And we can't leave it in the bone." She flexed her marked forearm, the new thread catching the gray light. "There has to be another way. There has to be."

Ulyana opened his mouth to answer—

—and stopped, because the sound of hoofbeats had come down through the broken walls, many horses, moving fast, and Brannic was already on his feet at the doorway with the crossbow raised, swearing softly.

Riders in the nave's long shadow. At their head, a figure in Oathbound grays, face pale under the hood, riding hard as if the hounds of her own order were behind her.

Serah Ives drew rein in the ruined nave, took in the scorched chalk ring, the changed marks on their arms — and threw Ulyana a sealed ledger stamped with the Order's highest seal.

"They're coming for all of us," she said. "Read fast."
