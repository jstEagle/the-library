## 8. Betrayal at the Ford

The ford at Marrow Creek was the last crossing before the highlands, and Doss said it would take them a day to find another way around — a day they did not have, with Serah Ives somewhere behind and the warlord's outriders somewhere ahead. So they crossed in the gray hour before dawn, when the water ran shallow over its bones of stone and the mist lay on the banks like something sleeping.

Ulyana went first, testing the footing, his god-mark cold and quiet against his ribs. It had been quiet since the cathedral. He did not know yet whether that was peace or warning.

Xanthe came second, the bone charm wrapped in three layers of cloth beneath her shirt. She had not let it out of her skin since the Hollow Cathedral, since the moment her eyes had gone white and Ulyana had poured himself through the oath-mark into her, anchoring her to the world with everything he had. She still flinched when he moved too fast. He pretended not to notice. She pretended to believe he didn't.

Doss came third, grumbling about the wet, his smith's hands blistered from a week of improvised repairs to their gear. And Brannic came last, big and easy-laughing, wading through the shallows like a man who had never feared anything in a river's worth of water.

That should have been the first sign, Ulyana thought later. Brannic had deserted from Kavrel March's army. He should have feared rivers. He should have feared the north. He should have feared *something*.

But dawn was coming up copper behind the eastern ridges, and Xanthe laughed for the first time in days when Doss slipped on a slick stone and swore an oath so elaborate it would have made Veth himself blush, and Ulyana let himself forget, for the length of one crossing, that they were being hunted from both directions.

He remembered it again very quickly.

They made camp on the far bank in a hollow of alders, and Ulyana took first watch because he slept worst anyway. The morning thickened. Mist burned off the creek in slow ribbons. Somewhere upstream a heron cried once and went silent.

Brannic sat down beside him with two cups of the bitter leaf-tea Doss brewed and handed him one.

"You watch like a man expecting the sky to fall," Brannic said.

"In my experience," Ulyana said, "the sky falls most often on the men who aren't watching."

Brannic laughed, warm as banked coals. "Serah Ives is two days behind us. Kavrel's riders are maybe three ahead. We've got a window, mercenary. You ought to sleep while it's open."

"I don't sleep near you," Ulyana said, and meant it lightly, and Brannic took it lightly, and that was the last conversation they ever had as comrades.

* * *

It was the charm that woke him — or rather, the absence of it.

He surfaced from a thin sleep near midday with his hand already moving to the knife under his cloak, because some instinct older than thought told him the camp was wrong. The fire had been fed. Doss was asleep, snoring. Xanthe's bedroll —

Empty.

Not disturbed. Not dragged. Empty and rolled, the way she never left it, because she'd learned to sleep ready to run.

"Brannic." His voice cracked across the hollow. Nothing answered but the alders ticking in the wind.

He found the trail in ten heartbeats: boot prints, Brannic's heavy stride and Xanthe's lighter one beside it, heading east along the creek toward the old drover's road. Two sets of prints. One set dragging slightly, favoring the left foot — Xanthe, who had twisted her ankle at the ford and said nothing, because she'd learned that too, saying nothing, keeping her hurts her own.

She hadn't gone willingly. Or she'd gone willingly enough and lied to herself about it. With Brannic it was impossible to know which lie he'd told her; the man could make treason sound like a kindness.

"Doss!" Ulyana kicked the ward-smith awake. "Up. Now."

Doss took one look at his face and didn't ask. That was the thing about the ragged little family they'd become — nobody asked anymore, they just moved. They ran the drover's road east with Ulyana's lungs burning and his mark burning colder than his lungs, past the ruined drovers' shrine, past the cairns, until the trees broke open onto a long grassy shelf above the creek bend — and there they were, half a mile off, small in the distance.

Three riders. Not Kavrel's scarlet-and-ash. Gray cloaks, iron masks pushed up on their helms.

Oathbound.

And Brannic standing among them, hands loose at his sides, talking — gesturing back toward the west, toward Ulyana, drawing their positions in the air with a traitor's economy — while between two Oathbound horses, wrists bound, hair wild, stood Xanthe.

"No," Doss breathed. "No, no, no—"

"They're not taking her to the Order," Ulyana said. The understanding arrived whole and terrible. "Look at their tack. Look at the remounts. That's a courier relay, not a patrol. They're riding her east. To the rim. To Kavrel."

"But Brannic—"

"Was never a deserter." The word tasted like creek water and ash. "He was always Kavrel's. From the first night. Everything — the campfire stories, the songs, teaching Xanthe that dice game — all of it was to get close to the charm." His hand closed over his own chest, over the mark that had been silent since the cathedral, and he hated it, hated the silence, hated that his god gave him nothing now, no burning, no judgment, no comfort. "I vouched for him. I *vouched* for him."

Below, the riders mounted. Xanthe looked back once, westward, and even at that distance Ulyana saw her find the treeline where they crouched. She shook her head. A small motion. *Don't.* Then the horses turned east and the wind took them.

"We can't fight six," Doss said. "We can't catch horses on foot. Ulyana—"

"We follow the road. There's a second ford downstream and Kavrel's camp is on the rim; she'll be taken there, and there we'll have one chance." He was already moving. "And we'll need help we don't have."

As if the world had been waiting for him to say it aloud, the heron cried again — and then a horn sounded west of the creek, deep and disciplined, and out of the alder shadows came riders in gray, and at their head, on a black mare, the woman whose face had haunted every mile of their flight.

Serah Ives.

Ulyana put himself between her and Doss, and drew his blade, because some things were simply true no matter how tired a man was. "You won't take us."

Serah reined in. Behind her, a dozen Oathbound fanned out with the smooth, awful competence of people who had done this a hundred times, and Ulyana measured the distance and knew it was hopeless and held anyway.

"I've been chasing you for nine days," Serah said. Her voice was flat, exhausted, stripped of the command-pulpit music she'd used at Ashmoor's gate. Up close, in the ordinary light, she looked less like an executioner and more like a woman who hadn't slept since before the stars went out. "Nine days. Do you know why?"

"Because you were ordered to."

"Because I wanted to know what was worth breaking my order's law over." She swung down from the saddle. Every Oathbound hand tensed; she waved them still. "I read the records, mercenary. All of them. The sealed archives under the Chapterhouse at Vell."

"There are no sealed archives."

"There are if you're ranked below First Cantor." She reached into her saddlebag, and Ulyana's grip tightened on his hilt, but what she drew out was not a weapon. It was a ledger — fat, cracked-leathered, tied shut with the Order's own black-and-silver cord, the seal broken. She held it out to him. "Take it."

He didn't move. "This is a trick."

"Yes," Serah said simply. "It's the best trick I could think of, because the truth sounds like one. Open it at the page marked with the red thread."

Doss took it. Ulyana let him — let someone else's hands do what his wouldn't. Doss thumbed through brittle pages, found the thread, and stopped breathing.

"What?" Ulyana demanded. "What is it?"

"It's a requisition ledger," Doss said slowly. "Dated forty years ago. Ward-components. Binding-iron. And..." His finger traced down the column. "*One vessel, voice-grade, for transport to the Chapterhouse. Authorized: First Cantor Ellery Vane. Countersigned:*" He looked up, and his face had gone the color of old tallow. "*Countersigned by the Council of Elders of Ashmoor.*"

The creek talked on beneath the silence, indifferent.

"The wards didn't fail," Serah said. "They were *built* wrong. Built to keep magic out, yes — and to keep one thing in. Your valley wasn't a sanctuary, mercenary. It was a cell. The elders helped the Order steal Veth's voice forty years ago, hid the fragment where no one would look for it, and built their whole pious little valley around the lock." Her jaw worked. "Kavrel March was the courier. The one who carried it out. When he kept a piece for himself instead of delivering all of it, the Order declared him godless and hunted him — not for theft. For *short payment*."

Ulyana heard his own heartbeat. Thought of every prayer he'd ever whispered into silence. Every vigil. Every year of believing his god had simply chosen not to answer — when the truth was that his god had been *muzzled*, and the men who muzzled him had been the ones Ulyana's whole life had been built on serving.

"My sister died keeping those wards," he said. His voice came out very quiet. "The whole valley bleeds for them. Generation after generation."

"I know."

"You *knew*. You've known how long?"

"Since Vell. Six days." Something moved behind her eyes — shame, or its ruins. "I spent four of those days trying not to know it. Telling myself there was context. Authority. That the elders must have had reasons." She looked east, down the drover's road, toward the rim where Kavrel's fires would be lighting by nightfall. "Then your friend Brannic sold your girl to my outriders this morning, and I watched them turn her east instead of south to Vell, and I understood that my order isn't hunting the charm to *protect* it. First Cantor Vane wants the fragment back before Kavrel takes it. Not to free the voice. To keep the leash."

"And you're giving me the proof." Ulyana stared at the ledger in Doss's shaking hands. "Why? Your oaths—"

"My oaths are to Veth," Serah said. "Not to the men who cut his throat and called it doctrine." She pulled off one gauntlet, bare-handed, and held it palm-up between them, the way an Oathbound offered fealty — the gesture so old it predated the betrayal, maybe. "I have twelve riders who will follow me as far as the rim and no further, because after that they'll have to choose, and I won't choose for them. Kavrel March means to finish the theft tonight or tomorrow. If he pours the rest of the voice into himself, there will be no valley, no Order, no anything — just a new god with an old grievance and forty years of rage to spend."

"And you need someone mad enough to walk into his camp."

"I need someone the charm knows." For the first time, something almost human crossed her face. Almost gentle. "Your girl opened it once and lived. In the cathedral. My cantors say that's impossible."

"She didn't live alone," Doss murmured, looking at Ulyana's chest, at the silent mark beneath his shirt.

Serah followed his gaze. "No," she said quietly. "I don't suppose she did."

Ulyana looked east. The copper light was dying off the ridgelines, and somewhere beyond them, on the rim of the only home he'd ever known, a warlord was sharpening himself into a god, and Xanthe — furious, frightened, magnificent Xanthe, who had shaken her head at him across half a mile of grass and told him *don't* — was in the middle of it.

Betrayal sat in his mouth like a stone. He swallowed around it.

"Ride with me," he said to Serah Ives. "And when this is done — whatever's left of your order and mine — we burn Vane's archives in public. Every page."

"If we live," Serah said, "I'll hold the torch myself."

They turned east together, twelve gray riders and two ragged fugitives, following a stolen girl toward the rim of the world — and behind them, faint on the wind off Marrow Creek, Ulyana thought he heard, for the first time in forty years, something almost like a voice.
