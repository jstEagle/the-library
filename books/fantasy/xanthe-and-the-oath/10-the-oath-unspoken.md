## 10. The Oath Unspoken

The war-camp burned like a second dawn.

Ulyana came through the smoke with Serah Ives on his left and Doss behind him, and the world had gone strange in the hour since Kavrel March had forced Xanthe to open the charm. The godless host — ten thousand strong, camped along the valley's rim where the old wards used to bite — was kneeling. Not all of them, not yet, but whole companies of them, dropping to their knees in the mud as if a hand the size of the sky had pressed them down.

They could hear it. That was the horror of it. Everyone within fifty miles could hear it: Veth's voice pouring out of the bone charm in Kavrel's fist, and into Kavrel, filling him the way floodwater fills a cellar. The warlord stood on the ridge above his tents, arms spread, head thrown back, and light moved under his skin like lightning trapped in milk.

"He's drinking it," Doss breathed. "Gods below, he's actually *drinking* it."

"No," Ulyana said. His god-mark burned cold at his throat, colder than it had ever burned, cold enough to ache like frostbite in his bones. "It's drowning him. There's a difference."

There wasn't time to explain, and he wasn't sure he could. He had spent his whole life serving a silent god, and now that god's voice was loose in the world again and every word of it was being swallowed by one man who wanted to be the only thing left listening.

Xanthe was somewhere in that camp. Serah's records said the command tent; Brannic's treachery had delivered her there two days ago, and everything since — the ford, the records, the long hard ride through the Gray Country with Serah riding point like a woman fleeing her own shadow — had been aimed at this ridge, this smoke, this single impossible moment.

"March," Serah said quietly. "The Oathbound will hold the perimeter. Whatever happens up there—" She stopped. She was a woman who had spent thirty years believing her order served the god it had secretly strangled, and she still hadn't found the shape of what she believed now. "Whatever happens, Ulyana Zhou. I'm sorry. For all of it."

"I know," he said. And then, because there was no more time: "Keep them off the ridge."

He ran.

* * *

The command tent was torn open at one side, canvas flapping like a wounded wing, and inside it Xanthe knelt in a circle of dead men while Kavrel March drank the voice of God.

She looked small. That was the thing that broke something open in Ulyana's chest — not the blood, not the bodies of the guards who had tried to stop whatever she'd done when they dragged her in here, but how *small* she looked, this girl who had dragged him out of a dying valley and lied to him and saved him and never once asked him for anything except to keep walking. Her hands were pressed flat against the ground. The charm hung from Kavrel's fist above her, and threads of pale light ran from it into the warlord's open mouth, and where they passed over Xanthe they bent, snagging on her like burrs on wool.

She was holding the door open. Even now. Even after everything, she was keeping herself between the voice and the man who would own it, spending whatever was left of her to slow him down.

Kavrel turned his head. His eyes were wrong — white as wardstone, white as the cracked sky the night the stars went out.

"The mercenary," he said, and his voice came out layered, two voices braided together, his own gravel and something vast underneath it that made the tent poles hum. "The little dog of the silent god. Come to bark at me?"

"You're killing yourself," Ulyana said. He drew no weapon. There was no weapon for this. "That voice is bigger than you. It's bigger than the Order. It's been locked up for sixty years and it wants out, and you're standing in the doorway."

"It wants a *mouth*." Kavrel smiled, and the smile split too wide. "Sixty years I have carried the theft. Sixty years I have fed it, kept it warm, kept it *hidden* from those grey elders in their white tower. It owes me. It will make me what the Oathbound pretended to serve." He raised the charm higher, and the light thickened, and Xanthe cried out — a small, bitten-off sound, the sound of someone being pulled apart by the roots.

Ulyana understood then, all at once, the way you understand a fall only once you're already falling.

The charm was a cage. It had always been a cage. The elders had built it to steal Veth's voice, and Kavrel had stolen the theft, and every plan Ulyana had carried across the Gray Country — give the voice back, restore the god, renew the covenant, make Ashmoor whole again — every one of those plans ended the same way. With a cage. With one being, god or man or order, holding the voice and everyone else on their knees outside it. That was the old covenant. That was what his oath had always meant: *you speak, we obey; you are silent, we wait.*

His mark burned cold. And beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat under snow, something that was almost a question.

*Yes,* Ulyana thought. *I know.*

He crossed the tent in three strides, took the charm out of the air between Kavrel's fingers and his own throat, and held it up.

"Ulyana—" Xanthe's voice, ragged, terrified. "If you break it, there's no putting it back. No god. No covenant. Nothing—"

"I know," he said. And found, to his astonishment, that he was smiling. "Nothing owned. Nothing owed."

Kavrel lunged. Ulyana had fought men his whole life and knew the geometry of a lunge the way he knew his own hands, and it didn't matter, because Kavrel was already more than a man and the blow took Ulyana off his feet and through the tent wall into the mud. But the charm stayed in his fist. The light tore at him, poured into him looking for a mouth, and Ulyana lay in the trampled grass of the warlord's camp with Veth's voice screaming through his bones and did not open his throat to it.

*Not mine,* he thought at it, at the god he had served blind for thirty years. *Not his. Not theirs. You don't get to belong to anyone anymore. Neither do I.*

He brought the charm down against the flat stone of the hearth-ring at the center of the camp — the old altar-stone, older than the wards, older than Ashmoor — and the bone charm shattered like a bell struck too hard.

* * *

The world went white, and then it went *loud*, and then it went quiet in a way no silence had ever been quiet before.

Ulyana felt the voice leave — not into Kavrel, not into himself, not into any vessel built to hold it, but outward, everywhere, scattering like seed on wind, like sparks off a bonfire, into everything alive. Into the grass under his back. Into the horses screaming in their pickets and then going suddenly calm. Into the river at the valley's heart, into the wardstones' broken shards, into ten thousand godless soldiers who gasped as one and began, without knowing why, to weep.

Into the valley itself. He felt it pass south over the rim, over the Gray Country, over the cracked stones of Ashmoor's border, and the wards didn't rise again — they didn't need to. Magic didn't come back to the valley like an invader breaking through a wall. It came home like rain.

Kavrel March collapsed. All that stolen power, all sixty years of hoarded divinity, unraveled out of him in a single exhalation, and what was left was an old man in fine armor, lying in the mud, breathing shallowly, blinking at the ordinary stars overhead. The stars were back. Ulyana hadn't noticed when they returned. They were everywhere, reckless, countless.

Serah Ives found him there. She stood over him with her sword drawn and then, slowly, sheathed it, and put out her hand, and pulled him up, and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the whole sky wasn't already saying.

Xanthe was alive. She crawled out of the ruined tent on her hands and knees, gray-faced, shaking, half-unmade and stitched back together by whatever fragment of the voice had snagged on her and refused to let go. Ulyana reached her first. He didn't ask if she was well. He just sat down in the mud beside her, and after a moment she leaned against his shoulder, and they watched the godless army kneel and then, one by one, stand up again — not commanded, not converted, just *free*, which none of them had ever been.

Doss arrived at a limping run, took one look at the sky, and burst into tears. Brannic was nowhere to be found, and no one went looking.

* * *

They buried nothing and burned nothing and held no rites. There didn't seem to be rites for the day a god dissolved.

Three days later they stood at the border of Ashmoor, the four of them — Ulyana, Xanthe, Doss, and Serah, who had resigned her commission in a letter she described, with grim satisfaction, as "eleven pages of truth addressed to men who will burn it." The valley lay green and gold below them, and magic moved through it openly now, visible in the shimmer above the wheat and the deep answering glow of the river. People were frightened. People were also, Ulyana thought, watching a farmer's child make a lantern-light dance off her palm and shriek with delight, going to be all right.

His oath-mark was gone. He had woken on the second morning and found his throat bare, the cold brand faded to a ghost of pale skin, and he had grieved it — properly, on his knees in the wet grass, grieving the god and the sister whose memory he'd paid away and the thirty years of certainty that could never be given back. Grief was a country too. He had walked it. He had come out the other side.

"So," Xanthe said. She was turning the shards of the charm over in her palm — she kept them, wouldn't say why, and he didn't ask. "No god. No order. No oath. What does a man like you do with a morning?"

Ulyana looked at her, and at Doss already arguing with Serah about whether ward-smithing was a crime or a calling, and at the valley where he had been born and had nearly died and would, he realized, probably grow old.

"Covenant," he said. "Just a smaller one."

Below them, the road into Ashmoor stood open, unwarded, and the whole valley waited — and for the first time in his life, Ulyana Zhou walked toward it owning nothing, owed nothing, and entirely, stubbornly free.
