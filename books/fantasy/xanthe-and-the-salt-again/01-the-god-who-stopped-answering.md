## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The rain came down on Thornfell like a debt being collected—slowly at first, then all at once, then without mercy. Petra Montrose stood under the eave of the Wayhouse of the Nine Stairs and watched it hammer the cobbles into a gray mirror, and she did what she had done every morning for eleven years and every morning for a hundred days in particular.

She prayed.

Not with words. Words had never been necessary. She pressed her thumb to the oath-mark on her inner wrist—the small spiral like a whelk shell, salt-pale against her sun-browned skin—and she opened herself the way you'd open a door to a house where someone was always home. *Here I am,* the gesture said. *Still yours. Still walking.*

Silence came back. The hundredth silence.

It wasn't the silence of an empty room. Petra knew that silence; she'd grown up around it, in the thin places above Graywater where the air itself seemed to have nothing left to say. This was different. This was the silence of a room where someone had been sitting very still for a long time and then, at some point she couldn't name, simply wasn't there anymore. The chair still held the shape of them. The cup was still warm, or she imagined it was warm, or she remembered warmth so vividly that memory and fact had fused and could no longer be pried apart.

*Vessel-of-Salt,* she thought, not quite a prayer anymore, more a knock on a door she suspected stood open onto nothing. *I'm going down into Thornfell with forty pounds of uncut moonquartz sewn into a wool bale and a bill of lading that says it's candlewax. If you have opinions, now would be the time.*

Nothing. Rain. A cart groaning somewhere up the hill, its axle singing one flat note.

She lowered her wrist and flexed her fingers until the ache in the scar tissue faded. Then she went out into the wet, because the quartz wouldn't smuggle itself, and gods—even silent ones—had never once carried anything for anybody.

* * *

Ferro was waiting at the customs cut with his collar up and his usual expression, which was that of a man perpetually surprised by how much everything cost. He was a big, soft-spoken smuggler with hands like slabs of ham and a talent for looking boring that had saved their hides more often than any blade. He nodded at her approach and jerked his chin at the wool bales stacked on the mule cart.

"Third bale's heavy," he said. "Wool doesn't weigh like that."

"Wool does if it's been rained on."

"Ferro's wool doesn't get rained on. Ferro's wool is dry as a sermon." He scratched his jaw. "You want to tell me why we're running quartz through Thornfell instead of the coast road? Coast road's quiet this time of year."

"Because the coast road is quiet, and quiet roads are roads somebody's watching." Petra swung up onto the cart bench and took the reins. "Thornfell's loud. Nobody watches loud."

"That's either wisdom or a headache waiting to happen."

"Usually both." She clicked the mule forward. Ferro fell into step beside the wheel, and they went down into the town together, two figures and a cart of lies, indistinguishable from a hundred other carts on a hundred other gray mornings.

Thornfell in the rain was a town built of staircases. It clung to the flank of the mountain in terraces, each street a step above the last, connected by cut-stone stairs worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet. The houses leaned together like conspirators, their upper stories nearly touching across the alleys, and from every eave the rainwater poured in silver threads into stone channels that ran down through the whole town and vanished, eventually, into the mountain itself. Everything in Thornfell drained downward. Everything in Thornfell went down, sooner or later. Petra had never liked thinking about where it all went.

They made the first checkpoint by midmorning. A customs officer with a waxed cloak and the eyes of a man who'd rather be anywhere else waved them through after a glance at the bill of lading. Candlewax. For the shrine-chandlers of the lower terraces. Perfectly dull. Petra smiled the smile of a woman who had never once done anything interesting, and the officer smiled back the smile of a man who didn't care, and that was the whole transaction, and it was fine, and it worked, and none of it meant anything.

That was the thing about the silence, she thought, guiding the mule down the slick stairs between the second and third checkpoints. It hadn't changed the work. The work was the same. Her hands were the same hands; they still knew knots and ledgers and the exact pressure that would crack a customs seal without leaving evidence. What had changed was underneath, in the place where the meaning used to live. Eleven years ago, when Vessel-of-Salt had set its mark on her wrist in a flooded chapel beneath Graywater, every job had felt like a sentence in a long conversation. Smuggle this. Protect them. Keep the small people fed when the lords took the harvest twice. Every successful run had been a word spoken back to the god: *heard, understood, doing my part.* Even the failures had been part of the grammar.

Now she spoke into a well and heard only her own voice coming back off the water.

"Petra." Ferro's voice, low, beside her ear. "Don't look left. Third archway past the chandler's row. Two men, gray cloaks, standing too comfortable for weather like this."

"I see them." She didn't turn her head. "Tax collectors?"

"Tax collectors don't stand in the rain. They stand under things." He shifted the wool bale on his shoulder, angling his body between the archway and the cart. "Reeves, maybe. Or worse."

"Then we're candles," Petra said. "Boring, honest, damp candles. Smile at me like I just told you your mother's recipe for barley soup."

Ferro smiled. It was a terrible smile, the smile of a man passing a kidney stone, but it was convincing enough at ten paces, and they rolled past the archway and the two gray cloaks without slowing, and neither gray cloak moved, and Petra counted to thirty in her head before she let her shoulders come down from around her ears.

"You're getting good at this," Ferro said quietly.

"At what?"

"The part where you pretend nothing's wrong."

She looked at him. Rain ran off the brim of her hood between them like a curtain. "Something's wrong?"

He shrugged, enormous and deliberate. "You pray before every run. Out loud, some mornings, when you think I'm asleep in the loft. You haven't in—" he counted on his fingers with theatrical care, "—a while. And you've started checking your mark. Left wrist. When you think nobody's looking. Like a woman checking a purse she suspects has been picked."

Petra said nothing. The mule plodded. The rain drummed.

"I'm not asking," Ferro said. "A man can ask too many questions in our trade. I'm just saying I notice. Noticing's most of my job."

"Noticing is going to get you thrown off a cliff someday."

"It's kept me alive this long." He adjusted the bale. "Anyway. Checkpoint four's got a new man on it. Young. Eager. Eager men are the dangerous kind. We should go around through the tannery lane."

"Tannery lane smells like a god's open grave."

"So does eager." He grinned, and despite everything, despite the silence and the gray and the hundred days of it, Petra felt something in her chest loosen half a degree. That was Ferro's real talent, she thought. Not the looking-boring. The other thing. The way he could make the world feel slightly less like a held breath.

They went through the tannery lane. It smelled exactly as promised. The young checkpoint guard never saw them, and the quartz arrived at the chandler's cellar on Copperstairs Row at dusk, forty pounds of moonquartz swapped for candlewax in reverse, seals forged, ledger balanced, everyone paid, no one dead.

It was, by any measure that mattered, a perfect day's work.

Petra sat in the chandler's back room afterward, counting coin by lamplight, and felt absolutely nothing.

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which was either grim local humor or an accurate description of the bell in the chapel up the hill, cracked these twenty years, so that when it rang it sounded less like a call to worship than like something crying for help. Petra took the corner room because it had one window and a bolt, ate stew she couldn't taste, and climbed the stairs to bed while the common room below was still loud with card-players and a fiddler who only knew three songs and played them all like grudges.

She sat on the edge of the narrow bed with the lamp turned low and did the thing she had stopped doing in front of Ferro.

She checked her mark.

The spiral was still there. Salt-pale, faintly raised, exactly as it had been for eleven years. It hadn't faded. That was somehow worse—if it had faded, she could have told herself the bond had simply worn through, the way rope wears through, honestly and by use. But it sat on her skin as crisp as the day it was laid, a door still hanging on its hinges in a house where no one lived.

*A hundred days,* she thought. She'd been counting. She hated that she'd been counting; counting felt like doubt, and doubt felt like betrayal, and betrayal was the one sin her god had ever named plainly. *Be faithful in the small weights,* Vessel-of-Salt had said to her once, in the early years, in a voice like water moving over stones in the dark. *The great weights take care of themselves.*

She had been faithful in the small weights. She had weighed everything. So where—

She pressed her thumb into the spiral, hard, the way you press a bruise to prove it's real.

*If you're testing me, I'm still here. If you're gone—*

She didn't finish the thought. Finishing it felt like signing something.

Instead she did what she always did on the bad nights: she went back in memory to the day of the marking. She was nineteen. She'd gone down into the Delve at Graywater on a dare and a grief—her father three months buried, the tithe-feast cancelled that year for the first time in living memory, the whole village walking around with the specific hollowness of people who have stopped believing something and haven't yet admitted it. She'd found the flooded chapel by accident, waded in up to her thighs, and there in the dark the water had gone warm around her, warm as blood, warm as a hand closing over hers, and a voice that was not a voice had said: *You count true, child. Carry for me.*

And she had. Eleven years. Through smuggler's wars and famine winters and one memorable week when she'd hidden a whole village's seed-grain inside a shipment of funeral shrouds. She had carried. She had counted true.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain kept up its patient arithmetic on the roof.

*Maybe it's the mountain,* she thought, not for the first time. *Maybe something happened under Thornfell. Under Graywater. Something old shifting in its sleep.* The thought surfaced and sank again, the way such thoughts always did, because there was no one to say it to. Ossian would have had theories—he always had theories, the apostate priest, defrocked twice and proud of it—but Ossian was three valleys east nursing a congregation of smugglers and heretics, and writing to him about a silent god felt too much like saying it out loud.

She banked the lamp. She lay down. She slept badly, the way she had slept for a hundred nights, in the shallow restless doze of a woman listening for a voice that didn't come.

* * *

Dawn again. The rain had moved off eastward overnight, leaving Thornfell rinsed and dripping, the terraces steaming in the first weak sunlight like a town dreaming aloud. Petra loaded the return cargo—actual candlewax this time, honest weight, honest seals—and took the high road north out of town, alone, having left Ferro in Thornfell to settle a debt with a fence who owed him money and possibly an apology.

The high road climbed along the shoulder of the mountain through pine and scree, and by late afternoon it had given up on being a road at all and become a suggestion—a track of packed earth winding upward through the trees toward the pass, toward home. Petra's home was not a village or a town. It was a house. A squat stone house with a slate roof, set alone in a fold of the mountain two hours' climb above the last farmstead, chosen precisely because no one came that way unless they meant to. She'd bought it with quartz money nine years ago. It had a cellar deeper than any cellar needed to be, which had struck her as a bargain at the time and which she now tried not to think about at all.

She walked the last hour in the gold light of evening, and the mountain did what the mountain always did around her house: it went quiet. Not empty-quiet. Attentive-quiet. She'd noticed it years ago and filed it away as imagination—the way the birds stopped at a certain elevation, the way the wind seemed to hold its breath at the lip of the fold, the way her own footsteps sounded louder than they should, as though the ground were listening through its skin. Tonight, with a hundred days of silence sitting in her chest like swallowed river-stone, the quiet felt less like imagination and more like company.

*Stop it,* she told herself. *You're tired. Tired people hear things.*

The house came into view at dusk, black-roofed against the last orange band of sky, smokeless because she hadn't left a fire burning, windowless-eyed because shutters were shutters. Ordinary. Safe. Hers.

She was fifty yards out when she saw the shape by the door.

Small. Mud-colored. Sitting perfectly still on her doorstep with its knees drawn up, in the way that wild things sit—not relaxed, not afraid, but *watchful*, every line of the body a question it hadn't decided to ask yet. Petra's hand went to the knife at her hip without consulting her, eleven years of training executing themselves smoothly while her mind caught up.

A child. A small child, caked head to foot in dried mud, hair matted into ropes, barefoot in a season when the frost already came at night. Sitting on her doorstep. On *her* doorstep, at the top of a two-hour climb above the last habitation, where no road went and no shepherd grazed and no reasonable child had any business being.

Petra stopped. The light was failing. The child didn't move.

"Hello," Petra said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, the smuggler's voice, the nothing-to-see-here voice. "Long walk for someone your size."

The child looked up.

Even in the dying light, Petra saw the eyes first. They were a color she had no clean name for—pale gold shot through with white, like candleflame seen through sea-glass, like the moonquartz she'd spent her life moving in the dark catching light it shouldn't catch. They were the color of something burning very far underwater.

The child said something. It was not a language Petra knew, and yet some animal part of her, some part older than her training and deeper than her silence, flinched at the sound of it—the way a dog flinches at a note only dogs can hear. The syllables were liquid and formal and wrong, wrong the way a face in a dream is wrong, and they rose and fell with the cadence of a courtly address, a speech, a formal declaration delivered from a doorstep of dried mud to a woman with a knife half-drawn.

Then the child held something up. Small. White. Catching the last light.

A coin. Salt-white, unmetallic, gleaming faintly like a chip of bone or frost, and carved—or grown—with a spiral.

The same spiral. The same whelk-shell curl that sat on Petra's inner wrist, that she had pressed her thumb into a hundred times in a hundred dark rooms, that had answered her with silence for a hundred days.

The scars on her wrist woke up. All at once, all together, the old marks she'd carried since childhood—the ones she'd always assumed were from the quarry, from the wire fences, from ordinary rough living—lit up with a deep hot ache, as if something beneath her skin had turned over in its sleep and recognized, across a hundred days of silence and a lifetime of forgetting, the shape of its own name.

The child watched her with those burning underwater eyes and said, in the dead tongue of a court that had no business existing, one word more.

Petra didn't need a translation. Some words carry their meaning in their bones.

It sounded like *home*.
