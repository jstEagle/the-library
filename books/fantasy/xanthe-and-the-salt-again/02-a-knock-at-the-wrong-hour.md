## 2. A Knock at the Wrong Hour

The knock came at the hour when even wolves have the decency to sleep.

Petra knew every sound her mountain made. She had spent eleven years learning them the way other people learned prayers: the groan of the roof beam when the frost climbed past the third shelf of night, the tick of the iron stove cooling through its last heat, the particular hush that fell over Thornfell's shoulder of rock when snow began to gather itself in the high passes and consider coming down. She knew the wind in three dialects. She knew which creaks were the house settling and which were something walking on the scree below, and she knew—because a mercenary who forgot the difference did not stay a mercenary long—that nothing good knocked at this hour.

So when the knock came, three small raps, polite as a merchant's clerk, she lay still in the dark with her hand already around the haft of the axe she kept beside the bed, and she listened.

The house said nothing back. The mountain said nothing back. That was the wrong part. Thornfell always answered weather with weather, wind with wind. Tonight the silence after the knock was total, a held breath, as if the whole slope of the world were waiting to see what Petra Montrose would do.

She got up. She dressed without light—breeches, boots, the baldric with the short sword because an axe was for things you intended to split and she wasn't sure yet what she intended. At the window she pulled the shutter wide one finger's width.

The moon was a nail-paring, thin and mean, but it was enough. There was a shape on her doorstep.

Small. Sitting, or collapsed—it was hard to tell. Mud-colored, so that at first she took it for a sack someone had left, or a drift of pine needles and stone. Then the shape moved, and two eyes caught the nail-paring of moonlight and threw it back, and Petra's breath stopped in her chest for reasons she could not have named if a priest had put a knife to her throat.

They were pale eyes. Not blue, not gray. Pale the way salt is pale, the way old bone is pale, a color she had no word for and had seen only once before, years ago, in a dream she had decided never to think about again.

*No,* she told herself. *You're tired. You've been eating trail food and sleeping badly for a month. Tired eyes invent colors.*

Her eyes had never invented anything in thirty-one years. That was the trouble with being a scout before you were a smuggler: you learned to trust what you saw, and then you spent the rest of your life paying for it.

She went down.

* * *

The door opened on cold air that smelled of wet stone and, beneath it, faintly, of brine. The sea was four hundred miles east. There was no reason on any god's green map for her doorstep to smell of salt.

The child sat where the shape had been, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them—Petra couldn't tell if it was a boy or a girl, and the mud made it impossible; the child was caked in it, head to foot, dried in layers like the strata of some drowned country. Hair matted flat. Bare feet, black with cold, split at the heels. And in one fist, held against their chest the way a drowning sailor holds a plank, a coin.

"Inside," Petra said. "Now. Before something else finds you."

The child looked up. Those eyes again, level and unblinking, and there was nothing feral in them, nothing of the animal panic Petra had seen in a hundred war-orphan faces. It was worse than panic. It was *assessment*. The child looked at her the way she herself looked at a locked door: measuring the lock, the hinges, the wood, deciding how long it would take to get through.

Then they stood—stiffly, wincing—and walked past her into the house without a word, and Petra, absurdly, stepped aside to let them.

She closed the door. She shot the bolt. She told herself the bolt was against whatever had chased the child up her mountain, and not against the child, and she noticed that she didn't quite believe it, and she noted that too, filed it away, the way she filed everything.

* * *

She built the fire up from its banked coals and set water on. The child stood in the middle of the room, dripping thawing mud onto her floor, and watched her move with that same locked-door attention.

"Can you talk?" Petra asked. "Common Tongue? Anyone's tongue?"

The child's mouth opened. What came out was language.

Petra had heard most of the tongues that mattered in her line of work. Trade-speech in six accents. The guttural blessing-curses of the hill clans. The liquid syllables of the coast cities, the clipped soldier-talk of the garrison towns, the cant of smugglers and the older cant of smugglers' mothers. She had once spent a season running cargo with a Vessian scholar who read aloud in dead languages for pleasure, and she remembered the feel of those—the way dead languages sounded less like speech and more like architecture, all weight and proportion, words laid on words like stones.

This was that. This was worse than that. The sounds the child made were old the way the mountain was old, and they came out rhythmic, formal, each phrase ending on a falling note like a door closing, and standing in her own kitchen listening to them Petra felt the hair rise on her arms because the language was not being spoken *at* her. It was being spoken *around* her. It filled the room the way water fills a hull.

And underneath it, so faint she might have imagined it—a smell of salt, strengthening.

"Stop," Petra said. "Stop. I don't understand you."

The child stopped. Considered. Tried again, slower, and this time Petra caught the shape of it better, caught that certain syllables recurred—two of them especially, a paired sound like *sel-a*, *sel-a*, woven through everything, a name or a place or a plea—and the hearing of it did something strange to her chest, a tightness behind the sternum, an ache like a bruise pressed by a thumb.

"I don't know that word," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to. "Whatever you're saying. I don't know it."

But that was a lie, and some part of her, the part that had never learned how to lie to itself, knew it was a lie. She did know it. Not the meaning. The *sound*. The way you know a song from a house you lived in before you can remember living anywhere.

She turned her back on the child and busied her hands with the fire, because hands with work in them don't shake.

* * *

She fed them barley soup because it was what existed. The child ate the way starving people eat when they've learned that fast eating gets the food taken away: slowly, deliberately, every spoonful guarded, eyes never leaving Petra's face. Halfway through the bowl they paused, and their free hand crept to their chest, to the coin, and closed around it, and they ate the rest one-handed rather than let go.

"That yours?" Petra asked, nodding at it. "The coin."

No answer. But the grip tightened.

When the bowl was empty, Petra held out her hand. "Let me see it, then. I won't take it. I just want to look."

The child stared at her hand for a long time. Whatever calculation ran behind those salt-pale eyes, it took a while, and Petra had the patience of her trade and waited, arm out, unmoving, while the fire cracked and the wind tried the shutters and found them stubborn.

The child placed the coin in her palm.

It was warm. That was the first wrong thing. The child had been out in mountain cold that would kill a grown man in an hour, barefoot, half-dead, and the coin was warm as a held stone, warm as something alive. It was white—not silver-white, not bone-white, but white the way the eyes were white, salt-white, and its surface was not struck with any face or seal she recognized. Instead, fine lines covered it, dense as script, spiraling inward toward a center that seemed, when she wasn't looking directly at it, to be slightly deeper than the rest of the coin. A hole with no depth. A mark that went *down*.

She turned it over. The reverse bore a single character, angular, cut deep, and the moment her skin touched the cut edges—

Pain.

Not in her hand. In her scars.

Petra had scars the way soldiers have scars; she'd stopped counting at twenty. Most of them were honest wounds with honest stories. But there were three she had never been able to account for: three thin lines across her left shoulder blade, close together, parallel, like claw-marks or like tally-strokes, that she had carried since before memory. Her mother, when Petra was small enough to ask, had gone quiet in a way that ended conversations, and eventually Petra had stopped asking. They had never hurt. Not once in thirty-one years. Scars don't hurt. That is the entire point of them.

All three lit up now, together, a white-hot wire drawn under her skin from shoulder to spine, and she gasped and nearly dropped the coin, and the child was on their feet instantly, reaching for it, making that falling-note language, urgent now, and Petra—wounded, furious, frightened in a way that had nothing to do with the child and everything to do with the child—closed her fist around the coin instead.

"No," she said. "Mine for a moment. You want it back, you tell me what it is."

As if her words could conjure understanding where none existed. As if shouting louder in a language neither of them shared would build the bridge between them. But the pain was singing in her shoulder and fear does that, makes you demand things of the world that the world has no intention of giving.

The child went still. Then, slowly, they reached out—not for the coin, but for her. Small fingers, filthy, trembling with cold, touched the collar of her shirt where it had slipped, and tugged it aside, and looked at her bare shoulder.

At the scars.

The child's face changed. Petra had seen every kind of human face do every kind of thing, and she told herself afterward that she had imagined the recognition that crossed it—but she hadn't, and she knew she hadn't. The child looked at the three thin lines on her shoulder blade the way you look at your own name written somewhere it should not be. Their lips moved. Two syllables came out, soft, almost tender:

"*Sela.*"

The ache behind her sternum flared, and this time it had nothing to do with scars.

"That's not my name," Petra said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "My name is Petra. Petra Montrose. Who told you that word?"

The child only looked at her, and in the salt-pale eyes there was something that undid her more than the language or the coin or the pain: pity. Pity, from a mud-caked starving child on her doorstep in the dead hour of the night. Pity, as if *she* were the one who had been lost in the dark for a very long time and simply hadn't known it yet.

* * *

She gave the coin back. She watched the child close their fist around it and curl up on the hearthstones without being asked, without blanket or invitation, in the exact posture of someone who has slept in worse places and expects to again, and within moments they were asleep—truly asleep, deep and total, the sleep of the utterly spent, breath slow and even.

Petra sat at the table with the sword across her knees and did not sleep at all.

She took stock, because taking stock was what she did when the world went strange. One: a child, age perhaps eight, perhaps ten, half-dead, barefoot, who had climbed a mountain in the killing season to reach a door that belonged to a woman they had never met. Two: a language no kingdom used, spoken fluently, formally, the way you speak a thing you were taught by people who considered it important. Three: a coin that was warm, that was marked with a hole that went down, that had made thirty-one-year-old scars scream. Four: a name. *Sela.* A name that fit her chest like a key fits a lock you'd painted over and forgotten.

Five. Five was the thing she kept circling and refusing to land on. Five was the smell of brine in a room four hundred miles from the sea. Five was the way the fire had seemed to lean toward the child as they spoke, flames bending a half-degree off true, so slight she could call it draft, could call it anything, could keep calling it that right up until the moment she couldn't.

Her mother had burned salt in the hearth every seventh day of Petra's childhood. Had whispered over it, words Petra had thought were prayers until she was old enough to notice they weren't in any prayer-book's language. Had pressed salt into Petra's palm on the morning the tithe-wagon came through the valley, and said, *hold this, and whatever they ask you, say nothing*, and Petra, six years old, obedient, had said nothing, and the wagon had passed her by, and her mother had wept that night in a way Petra had never understood and had never dared to ask about, because asking about it made her mother go quiet in that conversation-ending way.

Salt. Salt on the doorstep of her memory, salt under everything, and here was a child smelling of the sea in the heart of the mountains, carrying a coin of it, speaking its language, saying a name that made her ribs ache.

Outside, the wind changed. It came around the shoulder of Thornfell and down the scree and pressed against the shutters, and in it, threaded thin as wire through the ordinary noise of weather, Petra heard—or thought she heard, and hated herself for the *thought*, for the cowardice of it—something that was not wind. A long exhalation. Attentive. Patient. The sound of something that had been looking for a long time and had just now, tonight, at the wrong hour, at the wrong door, found the right address.

The child slept on. The coin rose and fell with their breathing, gripped in a fist even unconsciousness wouldn't open.

Petra sat in the dark with her sword and her scars and her mother's unasked questions, and understood, with the flat certainty of a scout reading ground she knows is ambushed, that the knock had not been the beginning of anything.

It was the echo of a beginning. Something had started long ago, under the mountain, in a ledger she had never seen, in a language she somehow knew, and it had taken eleven silent years and one small desperate voice to finally catch up with her.

On the hearthstones, the child stirred and murmured in their sleep—two syllables, soft, repeated, *sel-a, sel-a*—and the fire leaned toward them, and this time Petra did not call it a draft.
