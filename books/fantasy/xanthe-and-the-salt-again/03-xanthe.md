## 3. Xanthe

The child slept through the whole of the first morning and half of the second, curled on Petra's hearth like something that had crawled out of a fire and decided the fire was home.

Petra worked anyway. That was the mercy of a mercenary's life: there was always work. She mended the hinge on her storeroom door, oiled her sword, counted her coin twice because counting it once felt too much like trust. She boiled barley with salt pork and set a bowl beside the sleeping shape, and when she came back an hour later the bowl was empty and licked clean and the child was watching her from under a mat of mud-stiffened hair, eyes the color of struck flint held up to sunlight.

"Name," Petra said. She tapped her own breastbone. "Petra."

The child blinked. Said nothing. But their gaze dropped—quick, deliberate—to the salt-white coin still lying on the table where they'd left it, as if to say *that is my name, that is all of me you get.*

"You don't get to be *coin*," Petra told them. "I've known coins. They're terrible company."

Nothing. The fire ticked. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves of the mountain house the way it always did, like a creditor who couldn't read a room.

She tried again through the afternoon, in three tongues and one smuggler's pidgin. *Name. Name. What are you called.* The child watched her mouth move with an attention that was almost scholarly, and once—only once—they opened their mouth and produced a sound in reply, two syllables in that dead language, soft and round and wrong for a human throat, and Petra's scars woke up along her ribs like a dog hearing its master's boots.

She didn't ask them to say it again.

By evening she'd stopped asking. She sat across the hearth with whetstone and blade and studied the child the way she'd study unfamiliar terrain: slope, cover, lines of retreat. Small. Maybe eight years old, maybe ten; malnutrition flattened ages the way water flattens stone. Hands too clean for a beggar now that the mud had dried and flaked away—clean the way mine-shaft hands are clean, scoured by salt. And under the grime, faint as watermark, a mark on the inside of each wrist: a circle crossed by a line. Petra had one too. Everyone born in the Delve villages had one, though hers had gone black and cold eleven years ago, the night her god stopped speaking.

The child's marks were white. Fresh. Like ink not yet dry on a signature.

*Tithe-mark,* said a voice in her memory that sounded like her mother, and she put the whetstone down before her hand could shake.

* * *

On the third day she named them herself, because a creature without a name in your house is either a ghost or a wound, and Petra had no talent for ghosts.

It happened at dawn. She came down to find the child standing at her window—not looking out, looking *at* the glass, close enough to fog it—and the early light came through low and gold and caught their eyes full-on, and for a moment the flint-color burned into something else entirely: a pale, warm yellow, the exact shade of the flower that grew wild on the scree above Graywater. Xanthe. Her grandmother had called the flowers that, in the old dialect the priests had never managed to stamp out. *Xanthë,* the little suns of the waste places, the ones that bloomed where nothing else bothered to live.

"Xanthe," Petra said aloud, testing it.

The child turned. Considered her. And then—first time since the door—their face moved, a small crack in the stillness that might have been, in a less haunted child, a smile.

"That's settled, then." Petra put porridge in front of them. "Eat, Xanthe. You're not a coin and you're not a ghost. You're a flower that grows in bad ground. I've known worse lineages."

Xanthe ate. And while they ate, they drew.

That was the other thing she should have noticed sooner. The child drew constantly—on the fogged glass, in spilled porridge, in the ash at the hearth's edge—with a stick of charcoal Petra had left out and instantly regretted. Not pictures. Lines. Intersecting, branching, confident lines, drawn without hesitation or correction, the way a mason draws what he has built and a mapmaker draws what exists.

Petra let it go for a day. On the fourth day, she looked properly.

The drawing covered the whole slate flagstone in front of the hearth. Galleries branching off galleries, stair-shafts spiraling down, a long straight run marked with a symbol like a closed eye, and at the center—drawn larger than everything, drawn with the kind of pressure that snaps charcoal—a chamber with a many-pointed star inside it.

She knew that chamber. Not the shape of it. The *feel* of it, the way you know a room in a dream.

"Where is this?" she asked, though her voice came out lower than she intended.

Xanthe looked up at her. Then they did something worse than answering.

They stood, walked to the drawing's outermost line—the surface, she realized, the topmost gallery, drawn nearest the hearthstone's edge—and set one small finger on a point along it. Then they walked the finger inward, gallery to gallery, junction to junction, unhurried, certain, until it stopped on the star-chamber at the center. Then they lifted their hand and pointed it back at the floor.

At the floor. At the specific flagstone under which, if Xanthe's map were true, the tunnels ran.

Under her house. Under Petra Montrose's rented, forgotten, end-of-the-road house on the shoulder of Thornfell, the house she had chosen precisely because it was nowhere, over nothing, above no one.

"No," she said.

Xanthe pointed again, patient as winter.

"There's nothing under here but rock. I've been in the cellar. It's cellar all the way down—I've *dug* in it." She heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, breathe, scout's discipline. "Who taught you to draw this?"

Xanthe's answer was the two-syllable word again. *Sela.* Soft. Repeated. And this time they said it while looking directly at her, with an expression Petra could not parse and did not enjoy trying to: recognition, maybe. Or expectation. The look you give someone when they're late.

"I'm not Sela," Petra said. "I don't know anyone called Sela."

Which was true, and also, in some deep cellar of her that had no business being dug in, not true at all. The name landed somewhere below thought and rang like a coin spun on marble, and both syllables of it felt worn smooth, handled, familiar—her mother's dialect, the old tongue, a name-shaped hole wearing someone else's sound.

She swept the charcoal out of Xanthe's reach and spent the rest of the day checking her cellar walls with a lantern and a hammer-tap, and every wall rang solid, and somehow that was worse. If the child was lying, fine. Children lied; Petra had been a feral liar herself once. But Xanthe hadn't hesitated once in that drawing. Nobody invented ground that confidently. You only drew ground like that if you'd walked it—or if it had been poured into you whole, the way water fills a vessel.

*Vessel-of-Salt,* she almost prayed, and caught herself at the threshold of it, the hundred-and-third day of silence stretching ahead like a road washed out. Habit carried her the last step anyway. She knelt by her bed that night and said the opening words of the oath into the dark, and the dark said nothing back, and beneath her floorboards—she would have sworn this later, under oath, to anyone who asked—something shifted. Once. Like a great body turning over in sleep.

Or like a ledger being opened.

* * *

On the fifth day, Ferro came up the mountain track with a mule-load of Graywater wool and a face full of questions, because Ferro's face was always full of questions the way other men's were full of teeth.

"So," he said, dumping the bales in her storeroom. "Rumor at the inn says you've got a changeling. Salt-child. Crawled out of the Delve gate at Graywater, they're saying, walked nine miles of mountain in bare feet—"

"They talk too much."

"They always do. Is it true?"

Petra weighed his wool, wrote his number, and said nothing, which in Ferro's currency was a confession with interest.

He found Xanthe within the minute, because Xanthe wanted finding—had drifted to the doorway the moment his voice hit the yard, and stood watching him with that flint-eyed appraisal that unnerved grown soldiers. Ferro, to his credit, only flinched a little.

"It talks?" he asked.

"It listens."

"It's looking at my boots."

"It likes your boots. They're good boots." Petra handed him his receipt. "Ferro. Question. When we ran the Graywater Delve, five years back—the lower galleries, past the collapse—we went in how far?"

"Two hundred paces? Three hundred? Why?"

"And after that the air went bad and we turned around."

"We turned around because *you* turned us around. You got gray as whey and said the dark was—" he waved a hand, embarrassed on her behalf even now, "—attentive. Your word. I never forgot it. Worst thing I ever heard a mercenary say about a hole in the ground."

Petra looked through the doorway at Xanthe, who was crouched now at the flagstone, tracing the old charcoal lines with one fingertip, adding new ones, extending the map with the calm industry of a spider repairing a web.

"Ferro," she said. "How far down do you think the Delve actually goes?"

"How would I know? Bottomless, folk say. Folk say the tithe-roads go down to the sea's bones." He laughed, then stopped laughing when she didn't join him. "Petra. Why are you asking?"

Because a child who speaks a dead tongue is drawing roads under my house, she did not say. Because those roads branch like veins and terminate in a chamber with a star, and I know the shape of that chamber the way you know the shape of a room you screamed in. Because yesterday I caught myself standing at the cellar stairs listening, and part of me was afraid, and part of me—the part with my mother's voice—was *homesick.*

"Reconnaissance," she said instead. "Forget it. Take your wool."

He took his wool. From the yard he called back, cheerful and oblivious and accidentally precise: "If that child of yours wants teaching, there's a priest at Graywater now—defrocked one, Ossian, does translations for smuggling crews. Speaks more dead languages than a graveyard. Might be worth the walk, eh?"

Petra said something noncommittal and closed the door, and stood with her back against it, and watched Xanthe watch her.

* * *

That night she dreamed of salt.

Not the sea's salt—she'd never seen the sea—but the other kind, the gray glittering crust that bloomed on the walls of deep places, growing in feathers and roses and teeth. In the dream she was small, six years old maybe, walking down a corridor of it holding someone's hand, and the hand was large and dry and certain, and a voice like a bell full of gravel was reciting numbers. Ledger-numbers. One name per number. Her small feet knew the way, turned the corners without instruction, and the dream was warm and safe and utterly wrong, and just before waking she understood with total clarity that the corridor was real, that she had walked it, that the hand belonged to—

She woke.

Dawn light. Hearth gone to embers. And Xanthe kneeling at the foot of her bed, sitting back on their heels in the posture of a servant awaiting orders, having clearly been there for some time.

"Don't do that," Petra rasped, hand finding the knife under her pillow out of pure professional reflex. Xanthe didn't even flinch at the blade. That frightened her more than the ambush-wake would have; children flinched. Soldiers flinched. This child regarded naked steel the way a clerk regards a familiar seal.

Xanthe reached out—slowly, telegraphing it, the way you'd approach a spooked animal—and touched the back of Petra's hand. Then they pointed, in sequence: at Petra. At the floor. Downward, the gesture unmistakable, the same gesture every culture in every age uses for *below.*

Then they spoke. A full sentence this time, four or five liquid syllables of the dead court-language, delivered with grave courtesy, like an invitation. Like a summons.

And Petra's oath-mark—the dead, black, eleven-years-cold brand on her inner arm—*ached.*

Not burned. Not woke. *Ached*, the way an empty socket aches, the way a missing limb itches in rain. Something down in the dark had brushed against the stump of her faith, and whatever slept down there had felt her feel it.

She sat up slowly. Looked at the child. Looked at the floor between them, at the flagstone Xanthe had marked on the very first day, the one over the star-chamber at the heart of the map.

"All right," she said quietly, to the child, to the floor, to the silent god whose silence had finally changed texture. "You win. We go see a priest."

Outside, the wind turned, and came down off the mountain carrying, faint and mineral and unmistakable, the smell of salt.
