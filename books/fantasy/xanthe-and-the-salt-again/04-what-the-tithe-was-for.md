## 4. What the Tithe Was For

Brother Ossian arrived three days after the maps, on the back of a mule that hated him and a storm that loved him equally.

Petra had sent for him with two words scratched onto a smuggler's token—*come now*—because there were only so many nights she could sit across from a silent child who drew tunnels under her house before she needed someone who knew dead things. Ossian was an apostate, which meant he had once been holy and had chosen instead to be useful. He wore the gray of his broken order under a fishmonger's oilskin, and he smelled of incense that had gone rancid in the rain, and he was the only man Petra trusted with a language no living kingdom used.

He stood in her doorway dripping, took one look at the child curled on her bench like a knife left out of its sheath, and said nothing for a long moment.

"Salt-white coin," he said finally. "Still carrying it?"

Xanthe's fist closed around the coin where it hung on its cord. The child's eyes—yellow as struck flint, yellow as the name Petra had given them—fixed on the priest with something that was not fear and not welcome. Recognition, maybe. Or the wariness of one prisoner meeting another's jailer's cousin.

"They don't sleep," Petra said quietly, in the doorway, out of the child's hearing—which was pointless, she suspected; Xanthe heard everything, heard the mice in the walls and the mountain breathing under them. "They sit up all night drawing. Tunnels. Under my floor. Under the well. They're accurate, Ossian. I've checked."

"Have you." He stepped inside, and the storm slammed the door behind him like a verdict.

* * *

She made him tea he didn't drink and set the drawings out on her table while the fire fought the wet. Forty-one sheets of paper, salvaged from every scrap in the house, covered in a child's fierce line-work: galleries, stairways, chambers marked with symbols Petra didn't know and one symbol she did—a circle bisected by a vertical stroke, repeated like a signature, like a seal stamped on every door.

Ossian bent over them for the better part of an hour without speaking. His hands, when they moved over the pages, trembled—not with age. Petra had seen that tremor before, in men reading their own death warrants.

"This is Old Court Tongue," he said at last. "The liturgical register, not the vulgar. Nobody speaks this. Nobody has spoken this since the Salt Courts went dark, and that was—" He stopped. Recalculated. "That was before your grandmother's grandmother, Petra. This isn't a language. It's a *relic*. And the child conjugates it like a native."

"Translate," Petra said. "Start anywhere. Start with that." She tapped the circle-and-stroke.

"That's not a word. That's a *name-claim*. It means roughly—" his lips worked, hunting for a shape the modern tongue couldn't hold—"roughly, *this belongs to the ledger below*. Everything it's stamped on belongs to the ledger below."

"And what's the ledger below?"

Ossian looked up at her, and for the first time since she'd known him—through two smuggling runs and one funeral and a night in Graywater neither of them discussed—he looked afraid to answer.

"You know," he said. "I think you've always known. You just called it by gentler names."

* * *

The translation took the rest of the day and into the night, and it went like archaeology conducted on a living body.

Xanthe spoke in fragments. Whether the child would not or could not produce whole sentences, Petra never learned—but the fragments came when Ossian asked gently, and sometimes when he didn't ask at all, offered into the firelit silence like coins dropped into a well. The priest would catch each one, turn it over, worry it against the grammar of a dead court, and give Petra back a piece of meaning that was heavier than the words that carried it.

*"Sella-veth."* Ossian's finger hovered over a drawing of a chamber full of small beds, rows and rows of them, drawn with terrible precision. "It means... the keeping-place. Where they kept—" He glanced at Xanthe. "Where the tithed children slept. The child says there were forty in this room. Says the number like a prayer. Says some of the beds are still warm."

Petra's tea went cold in her hands.

*"Korrath-imun."* A drawing of a great gate, salt-white, carved with faces. "The First Gate. Below the Delve at Graywater—the old Delve, the sealed one. The child says the gate knows songs. Says if you sing right, it remembers being open."

"Ferro will love that," Petra said, because saying something ordinary kept the floor steady beneath her.

*"Vessel-of-Salt."*

The words came out of Xanthe's mouth in the trade-tongue—broken, accented, wrong—and both adults went still as game animals. The child was looking at Petra. Looking at her forearm, where the oath-mark lay under her sleeve, the mark that had been quiet for a hundred days.

Ossian translated slowly, as though walking on river ice. "The child says... *your god is below.* Says it like a fact. Like reporting weather." He swallowed. "*Taken below.*"

"That's impossible." Her voice came out flat and hard, a mercenary's voice, and she was grateful for it. "Vessel-of-Salt has answered oaths in Thornfell for nine generations. My grandmother swore to it. My mother—"

"Your mother." Ossian said it carefully, the way you'd say *the wound* or *the debt*. "Tell me about your mother, Petra. Not now. Later. But tell me eventually." He turned back to the papers. "Because I'm starting to hear a story in these drawings, and I don't think it's only the child's."

* * *

It was near midnight when the fragment came that broke the night open.

Xanthe had been drawing again—new page, new gallery, deeper than any before—and singing under their breath, that tuneless, attentive dark of a song that made the candle flames lean toward the child like men leaning over a table. Ossian had dozed in his chair. Petra was sharpening a knife she didn't need sharpened, because her hands wanted work and her mind wanted absence.

Then Xanthe looked up, directly at her, and said—in the dead tongue, in that relic-register that should have crumbled centuries ago—six syllables, clear as a bell rung in an empty church.

Ossian woke like a man falling. He caught the syllables out of the air. Petra watched his face change as he understood them, watched the blood leave it in stages, and thought: *whatever it is, I will make myself able to bear it before he tells me.*

"The price unpaid," Ossian said. "That's what the child said. *The price unpaid.* And then—" he hesitated, and his eyes flicked down to the page Xanthe had been drawing, and Petra followed his gaze.

The child had drawn a village. Her village. Thornfell, upper terrace, the well with its crooked coping stone, the shrine-niche where her grandmother had left offerings, the long house where Petra had been born. Drawn from below, the way you'd see it looking up through rock. Drawn with the loving accuracy of someone who had studied it for years through six hundred feet of stone.

And above the village roofline, floating in the empty paper-sky, Xanthe had written numbers. Rows of them. Names, maybe, in the ledger-script, in the circle-and-stroke. Dozens of names. Hundreds.

One of them was circled.

"What does the circled one say?" Petra asked. Her voice was very calm. She noted distantly that it was the calm she used when telling families how their sons had died, and that she had never once used it for herself.

Ossian didn't answer. Xanthe did—or almost. The child touched the circle with one grimy finger and said two more syllables in the dead tongue, soft as an apology.

"*Tithe-child,*" Ossian translated. "It says *tithe-child*. And underneath, in the ledger-form—" he pressed his palm flat against the table as if steadying it— "*sold at six. Never collected.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the storm was giving up, retreating down the mountain in gusts and mutters. Petra sat absolutely still.

"I don't know what that means," she said.

"Yes, you do," Ossian said gently. "But say the other thing first. Say what your village did, every year, at the turning of winter. You told me once—you were drunk, after the funeral—you told me about the white bread and the salt cakes, and how the children weren't allowed at the well-shrine, and how every household gave something to the deep and nobody ever said *what for*, and everyone pretended the pretense was piety."

"We gave to the god," Petra said. "Vessel-of-Salt keeps the springs sweet and the mountain quiet. That's what we were taught. Bread and salt, spring and harvest—"

"Bread and salt," Ossian repeated. "Bread for the living. Salt for..." He let it hang.

"For the dead," she finished. The word came out of her mouth tasting of copper.

"Or for the *below*," Ossian said. "Petra. Listen to me as a scholar and not as a friend, because as a friend I'd rather burn these papers. Your village wasn't worshipping. Your village was *paying*. Regularly, on schedule, for generations—that's what those customs were, a payment schedule dressed in liturgy so no one would read the contract. And a tithe—" his voice roughened— "a tithe isn't grain, Petra. Not in the Old Court. In the Old Court, the tithe was always the same currency. The only currency the deep accepts. Souls. Children's souls, tendered by their parents, entered on a ledger, and either collected—" his eyes went to Xanthe, to the salt-white coin at the child's throat— "or held. Accruing. Waiting."

The silence in the room had texture. It pressed.

"My parents paid," Petra said. It wasn't a question. She could feel it landing in her body like a stone dropped down a well, falling past every level she understood herself at. "My mother stood at the well-shrine every winter with a salt cake and I thought—gods, I thought it was *devotion*. She'd cry, some years, and my father would put his hand on her shoulder, and I thought it was the beauty of it moving her."

"It might have been," Ossian said softly. "Grief and devotion wear the same face when you can't tell them apart. Maybe they signed because they had to. Drought year, sickness year, a debt of the ordinary kind to a creditor of the ordinary kind—there are a hundred doors into a bargain like that, and every one of them looks reasonable from inside a starving house." He shook his head. "But the entry stands. *Sold at six. Never collected.* Six years old. What happened when you were six?"

"I don't remember anything when I was six." She said it fast, too fast. "There's a hole. Winter to summer. My mother told me I had a fever. Everyone had fevers that year, half the terrace—"

"Half the terrace," Ossian whispered. "Oh, Petra. Half the terrace was probably *on the ledger*."

* * *

Later—much later, when Ossian had finally slept in the chair and the fire had burned down to its red arithmetic—Petra sat across from the child who had come out of the dark to her door, and asked the question with her eyes because her voice was not available.

Xanthe looked back. Yellow eyes, flint-strike eyes. And the child reached across the table, slow as syrup, and touched the cord around their own neck, and then touched—hovered fingers over—Petra's sleeved forearm, where the oath-mark lay silent.

Then the child did something Petra would carry to her grave, whatever grave that turned out to be. Xanthe picked up the charcoal, and on the last clean corner of the last clean page, drew two figures. One tall, one small. Between them, a single line, and on the line, the circle-and-stroke—the ledger-seal.

*This belongs to the ledger below.*

Both of them. Together. Linked.

"No," Petra said. Her voice cracked down the middle like ice on a spring pond. "No. Whatever you're saying, whatever that thing thinks—I am not on its books. I got out. I walked out of Thornfell at fifteen with a knife and a mule and I have never owed anyone who didn't bleed when I collected—"

The child tilted their head. Patient. Terrible in their patience, the way only children and mountains are patient.

Then Xanthe spoke again, and this time Ossian stirred awake at the sound, catching it half-dreaming, translating in a sleep-roughened murmur that was worse than any shout:

"*The ember remembers its keeper.*"

And under Petra's sleeve, for the first time in a hundred days, the oath-mark *moved*—not answering, not speaking, nothing so kind as that. Just a twitch. A pulse. Like something buried six hundred feet down rolling over in its sleep, or trying to.

Like something reaching.

Petra stood up so fast the bench went over. She crossed to the window, shoved the shutter open, and let the cold wet air hit her face, and stared out at the mountain that had been her neighbor her whole life, black against the bruised pre-dawn, and understood—with the calm of a woman reading her own death warrant—that she had never lived beside it at all.

She had grown up inside its ledger. Every step she'd taken on the surface had been a debtor's errand. Her faith, her god, her silence of a hundred days, the feral child at her hearth—all of it, threads of one account, and the account was *open*, and somewhere below, something patient had begun, at last, to collect.

Behind her, Xanthe began to hum again, low and tuneless, and the mountain seemed to hum back.

Two days later, a courteous stranger in a gray coat would walk into the Wayhouse at Graywater asking gentle questions about a runaway asset—and Petra, warned by a smuggler's boy, would be already three hours gone, riding hard, with a sleeping child tied to her chest and a decision forming in her chest like a stone in a sling:

*You don't wait for the collector. You go down the road first, and you make the debt regret finding you.*
