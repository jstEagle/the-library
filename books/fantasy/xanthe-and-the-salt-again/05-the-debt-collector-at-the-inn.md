## 5. The Debt-Collector at the Inn

The Drowned Bell sat where the smuggler's road met the cart road, half-timbered and sagging like an old man leaning on a fence, and Petra had drunk there often enough that the tapman knew to water her second cup without being asked. It was the kind of place where questions died of neglect. That was why she'd brought Xanthe's existence nowhere near it.

She'd left the child locked in her mountain house with three days of bread, a knife they were too small to use properly, and instructions repeated until Xanthe had mouthed them back in that dead-court tongue, eyes like struck flint. *Do not open for anyone. Do not sing. If someone comes, go down the cellar stair and do not stop walking.* She hadn't told them where the cellar stair went. She wasn't sure herself. But Xanthe had drawn it once — charcoal on a floorboard, a map of passages under Petra's own foundations, tunnels that had no business existing beneath a house her grandfather had built above ground — and the child had tapped the lowest line and said a word Ossian later translated as *old door* or possibly *door that remembers*. So. The cellar stair went somewhere. Good enough for a last resort.

She told herself the knot in her chest was just the ale.

It was the hundred-and-eleventh day of silence from Vessel-of-Salt, though she'd stopped counting out loud. She prayed anyway, the way she flexed a hand she'd once broken — not expecting anything, just checking that the parts still moved. *Watch over the small one tonight.* Nothing answered. The oath-mark on her forearm, a thin gray spiral like a snail shell drawn in ash, stayed cold and dumb as a scar, which was all she'd ever believed it was, most days, except for the days when it wasn't.

The bell over the door rang at the wrong hour — past vespers, before closing, when honest traffic had dried up and only the desperate or the dangerous came in. Petra didn't look up from her cup. Looking up was how you told a room you had something to protect.

He crossed the floorboards with a sound like dry paper. Tall, narrow, dressed in traveling grays cut well enough to be mistaken for modest by anyone who hadn't spent twenty years pricing clothes at a glance. His hair was the color of wet sand. He carried no weapon she could see, which meant either he didn't need one or he wanted her to think so. Both options interested her equally, which is to say: not at all, if she could manage it.

The tapman brought him wine unasked. The stranger thanked him in a voice like a key turning in a lock — courteous, precise, faintly amused — and then turned his head, unhurried, and looked directly at Petra.

There was no way he could know her. There was no way he could *not* know her; she was the only woman alone at this hour who hadn't looked at him once. She cursed inwardly and raised her cup a fraction, the universal gesture: *yes, you may sit, and no, I won't enjoy it.*

"Mistress Montrose." He set himself across from her without waiting for the invitation to finish arriving. "Marrow-Vane. I'm given to understand you move goods through Thornfell. Quietly. Reliably."

"Whoever gave you that understanding should learn to keep his mouth shut." She let the accent thicken, the lowland drawl she wore for customs men and tax assessors. "And I don't do introductions after dark."

"Then I'll be brief, and we can both pretend this was daylight." He smiled. It was a good smile, assembled from correct parts. "I'm looking for a runaway asset. Small. Young. Likely mud-stained, likely frightened. Speaks—" he paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before stepping over something dead in the road, "—poorly."

Petra drank. She made herself drink slowly, made the ale taste like nothing, made her face the bored mask she wore when Ferro described a job too complicated. Under the table, her thumb pressed the cold spiral of the oath-mark. *Say something,* she thought at the silent god, with the bitterness of long habit. *You owe me a miracle.*

Nothing. Fine. She'd lie alone, then. She'd been doing it all her life.

"Thornfell's full of runaways," she said. "Half the villages up-mountain sell their brats downriver every bad harvest. You want the tithe-wagons' schedule, ask the factor at Graywater."

Something flickered behind his eyes at the word *tithe* — quick, satisfied, gone. She'd walked into it and knew it a heartbeat too late. He'd fed her the word to see what she'd do with it, and she'd swallowed it whole.

"Graywater," Marrow-Vane repeated softly. "Yes. The Delve there is closed, of course. Has been for years. Sealed." He produced, from inside his coat, a small object and set it spinning on the table between them with one long finger. A coin. Salt-white, matte as bone, catching no light at all. Petra's scars — the old ones, the ones she'd never been able to explain, the ache along her ribs and the back of her neck that woke on certain nights for no reason — woke now, all together, like dogs hearing their names.

She did not touch it. She was proud of that, afterward. Her hands stayed flat on the table, and her heart hammered against her ribs like something trying to get out, or in.

"You know what it is," he said, watching her hands rather than her face. Interesting man. Wrong priorities, but interesting.

"I know a coin when I see one."

"It isn't currency," he agreed pleasantly. "It's correspondence. When it's claimed, it opens a conversation. When it's left on a table—" he stopped the coin's spin with two fingers, "—it means the account holder has been located and the matter will be resolved locally. Amicably, I hope. The court prefers amicable resolution. So much wear on everyone otherwise."

"The court." She kept her voice flat, dumb, lowland. "What court would that be? Magistrate's?"

His gaze lifted to her face at last, and she understood, in the way one understands the depth of water only when already drowning, that the lowland drawl had fooled him for exactly as long as he'd allowed it to. "Mistress Montrose," he said gently, almost kindly, "you grew up within six miles of a tithe-village. Whatever name your family used for the custom — harvest-share, deep-rent, the old bargain — you've known since childhood that the mountain keeps accounts. I'm merely its clerk."

"I grew up in a quarry town. We kept accounts in copper."

"Did you?" He tilted his head, and there was something almost wistful in it. "Then you'll forgive an old clerk his confusion. Your face is familiar to me. Professionally familiar." He rose, smoothing his coat, and left the coin sitting on the table between them, white as a pulled tooth. "Keep it or don't. It makes no difference to whether it works. It always knows where it is." He put two coppers beside the untouched wine — paying for his own, scrupulous, obscene — and paused at the edge of the firelight.

"For what it's worth," he added, without turning around, "the asset is not in danger. Not from us. Contracts are sacred. It's the *uncontracted* who should mind the dark." And the bell rang, and the cold came in with the night air, and he was gone up the cart road toward Graywater like a rumor leaving a room.

* * *

Petra sat very still for the length of three breaths, four, five, letting the tavern noise reassemble around her — dice, argument, the tapman's rag on wood — before she moved. Then she took off her cloak, draped it over the coin, and picked the bundle up through the wool without touching salt to skin. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. Through the cloth it felt like holding a handful of winter.

Ossian was waiting in the stable loft, because she'd told him to be, and because apostate priests who'd been burned out of three parishes had learned to wait where they were told. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with ink-stained fingers and the permanent expression of someone reading a disappointing scripture. She held out the bundled coin without a word.

He unwrapped it, and she watched his face do what faces did around the things Xanthe carried: tighten, pale, go carefully blank. "Where—"

"Table. He left it. Called himself Marrow-Vane." She climbed up beside him into the hay-smell and the dark. "Looking for a runaway asset. Small, young, speaks poorly. He used the word *tithe*, Ossian. Fed it to me like bait, and I bit."

"Because you're tired and it's late and you're guarding a child whose language comes out of sealed graves." He turned the coin over. In the lantern-light it threw no reflection at all, which Ossian noted aloud with the flat voice he used for heresies he'd decided to believe. "This is court-issue. Summons-grade. They haven't minted these since—" He stopped. Started again. "Since before my order burned the last records of them. That was four hundred years ago, Petra."

"So the mountain's keeping old books current."

"The mountain never closed its books." He wrapped the coin back up, fast, like it might read him. "That's rather the point of a court. Courts are how power pretends to be arithmetic." He looked at her then, really looked, lamplight climbing his heavy face. "He saw your face and called it professionally familiar. What did he mean?"

"I don't know." True. "My parents sold quarry-leases, not children." Also true, as far as she knew, and the *as far as she knew* had been widening like a crack in ice since the night a mud-caked child had knocked at her door speaking the tongue of the dead.

"And the summons itself. He said the asset was located and the matter resolved locally." Ossian rubbed his jaw. "Locally meaning here. Meaning he knows the region, not the house. If he knew the house, there'd have been soldiers at the door, not pleasantries."

"He's going to Graywater."

"He's going to ask questions at Graywater. And Thornfell has perhaps sixty souls, and one of them is a charcoal-burner's boy who saw you carrying a child through the tree-line two weeks ago and owes money to everyone." Ossian sighed, a sound like a landslide deciding. "We have days. Perhaps fewer."

*We.* She noticed he'd said it. She filed it away next to other small warm things she didn't know what to do with.

* * *

She rode home through the dark with the coin wrapped in her cloak-roll behind the saddle, as far from her skin as the geometry of a horse allowed, and the whole ride her thoughts ran two tracks at once, the way they did before a job went wrong.

Track one: run. Take Xanthe north over the pass, out of Thornfell entirely, lose them in the river-country crowds where a feral child's dead language was just another foreign accent. She was a smuggler. Disappearing was her trade. It would be easy.

Track two, quieter, meaner: the coin knew where it was. His words, sitting in her ear like a splinter. And the scars on her body — the rib, the neck, the ache that woke on certain nights — had woken when the coin spun on that table, and she had never told Ossian about the dreams she'd had since Xanthe arrived, dreams of descending stairs in a house that was not hers but somehow her grandfather's, dreams of a voice saying *Sela* in a tone she couldn't place, a name she'd never worn and yet answered to in sleep. Running was for people the mountain had finished with. She was increasingly unsure the mountain had ever started.

The house was dark and intact. That registered first, always now: intact, intact, intact. Then the small shape in the loft corner, awake, of course, eyes open in the black like coins at the bottom of a well.

"You didn't come home at vespers," Xanthe said. Their common-speech had improved ferociously fast, as if language were a muscle they'd simply been forbidden to use. Sometimes whole sentences came out in the dead tongue anyway, especially when frightened, words that made Ossian go still and write things down. Tonight it was common-speech, flat and careful. "I counted the bells."

"I stopped for a drink."

"Liar." No accusation in it. Just inventory. The child had a genius for inventory. "You smell of fear-sweat and another person's smoke. Someone found you."

Petra stood at the foot of the ladder a long moment. Every instinct she owned said: lie, smooth it, carry it alone, that's what you're *for*, that's what twenty years of hauling contraband through imperial checkpoints had built her into, a creature who carried weight alone and called it strength. But the child watched her with those yellow-flint eyes, and behind them, faintly, like heat behind a wall, something else watched too — she'd felt it for weeks now, a warmth that had no business living in a starved eight-year-old body, a warmth that felt horribly, impossibly like the silence where her god used to be.

"A man found me," she said. "A clerk. He wants you back. He left this." She unwrapped the coin and held it up, and Xanthe went rigid, every muscle locking, and made a sound Petra had never heard from them — small, involuntary, the sound of a door opening onto something worse than dark.

"That's a *calling*," Xanthe whispered. "That's how they call you home." Their hands came up slowly, trembling, and stopped halfway, wanting and refusing the coin at once. "When it's left, it means they don't need to take you. It means you'll walk."

"Well." Petra closed her fist around the coin through the cloth, and the old scars sang, and she let them, and she was surprised — genuinely surprised — to find that what rose in her chest wasn't fear anymore. It had passed through fear sometime on the ride home, the way weather passes through a valley, and what it left behind was harder and cleaner. Fury, maybe. Or its respectable cousin. "They've mispriced me, then. Because I don't walk anywhere I don't choose to."

Xanthe stared at her. "It doesn't matter if you choose. The ledger chooses."

"Then we'll go argue with the ledger." She heard herself say it and realized she meant it, fully, catastrophically, in the way she'd only ever meant jobs before — route mapped, crew needed, odds terrible. "Not north. Not running. *Down.* We take the roads you drew on my floor, and we find whoever keeps those books, and we settle this account properly. On my terms."

"The deep roads kill couriers," Xanthe said. "The deep roads killed *priests*."

"Good thing I'm neither." Petra tucked the coin away and banked the fire, and outside the wind moved down from the high passes smelling of stone and old salt, and far beneath the floorboards, beneath the cellar stair, beneath the door-that-remembers, something vast shifted its weight like a sleeper turning toward a noise it recognized. "But I know a smuggler who owes me, and a priest who owes the truth, and come morning we're going to need both. Get some sleep, Xanthe."

The child didn't answer. When Petra glanced up at the loft, Xanthe was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on them before — not fear, not hunger, but something dawning and terrible and almost like hope.

"You said *we*," the child said quietly. "Nobody says *we* about going down."

"I did," Petra said. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we go make some clerks very unhappy."
