## 6. Roads Only I Remember

Petra woke before dawn on the fourth day and knew the waiting was over.

It wasn't a decision so much as a pressure behind her sternum, the same feeling she got before a storm broke over Graywater—the air going thick, the salt in the walls sweating. Xanthe slept curled in the corner of her hearth-room like a comma of a child, knees to chest, one hand still closed around that salt-white coin even in sleep. Petra had tried twice to take it from them while they dreamed. Both times the child's fingers had locked like a trap snapping shut, and both times Petra had woken them screaming without meaning to.

So the coin stayed. And the coin was the problem.

She stood at her window and watched the light come up gray over Thornfell's shoulder, and she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for four days. Marrow-Vane had left the coin on her table as a summons, but summons implied a court, and courts implied process, and process took time—unless the Undercourt decided it didn't. Brother Ossian had spent an evening hunched over his translations and come up pale as tallow. *"The price unpaid accrues,"* he'd read aloud, voice cracking on the last word. *"The ledger does not wait upon courtesy."*

Xanthe was unraveling. Everyone could see it now. The child had stopped eating anything but bread soaked in brine, had begun talking in their sleep in that dead tongue, fluent and rapid, answering questions no one had asked. Two nights ago they'd stood upright in the middle of the dark room, eyes open and fixed on nothing, and said—in a voice too old for their body—*the stair is weeping, the stair is weeping, do not let them count us again*—and then folded back onto the floor like a puppet with cut strings.

Petra had sat beside them until dawn with her hand on their small back, feeling the heat coming off them like a banked forge.

Waiting meant the court came for Xanthe here, in her house, on ground she couldn't choose. Going down meant choosing the road, the hour, the terms—or as many terms as the deep roads ever allowed anyone. She'd spent twenty years as a mercenary and ten before that as a smuggler, and every lesson she'd ever bought with blood said the same thing: never let your enemy pick the battlefield.

The problem was that the enemy owned the battlefield. All of it. Every road under Thornfell ran through country the Undercourt had held since before her grandmother's grandmother had been a line in someone else's ledger.

But there were roads only she remembered.

That was the thought that kept surfacing, patient as water working at stone. She'd been pushing it down for days because of what it meant: that she remembered those roads at all. That somewhere under the mountain was a six-year-old girl named Sela who had walked them, who had known the way the tunnels breathed, who had learned the deep places well enough to be let out again—and who had buried all of it so thoroughly that thirty years later it came back not as memory but as instinct, as the ache in old scars when she touched salt, as dreams of stairs that wept.

She looked at Xanthe sleeping in the ash-warm corner, and the arithmetic finished itself.

"We go down," she said aloud, to the child, to the room, to whatever was listening. "Before they come up."

Xanthe's eyes opened. They didn't startle, didn't blink at the dawn light. They just looked at her with those yellow eyes—xanthos, Ossian had murmured, it means the color of gold gone pale—and nodded once, slow, like something ancient agreeing to terms.

Then they said, in the trade-tongue, in a voice rough as a rusted hinge, their first unprompted words in it: *"You remember."*

Not a question. An accusation, almost. Or a relief.

"Yeah," Petra said, and her mouth tasted of salt. "I remember."

* * *

Ferro took the news the way Ferro took everything: by pouring another drink and looking at the ceiling of the Drowned Rat as if the answers were written between the smoke stains.

"You want to go *down*," he said. "Deliberately. With a child. Into the Undercourt. The place where smugglers don't go, Petra, and you know what I mean—not won't, *don't*. There's a difference. Won't is fear. Don't is the ones who went and didn't come back, so nobody goes anymore, and after enough years nobody remembers why except that their granddad told them not to."

"I remember why," Petra said. "And I remember the roads."

He set the cup down. He was a big man, Ferro, gone soft at the edges the way men go soft when the work gets easy, but his eyes hadn't softened. They moved over her face now, reading it, and whatever he found there made him sit back.

"Graywater Delve," he said slowly. "You're thinking Graywater."

"The old entrance. Sealed since before either of us was born."

"Sealed's a word people use. It doesn't mean shut. It means *we stopped paying attention*, and things under mountains love a door nobody watches." He rubbed his jaw. "I can get us into Graywater. I've moved cargo through the upper galleries—nothing deep, nothing that breathed. But Petra." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped to the register he used for prices and confessions. "What you're describing isn't a run. Runs have exits. You're talking about walking into the one market in the world where we're not sellers. We're stock."

"Then we'd better be stock that bites."

He laughed—one short, ugly bark—and then he wasn't laughing. "Who else?"

"Ossian. I need his tongue. Half of what Xanthe says might as well be weather-signals for all I understand it, and if there are doors down there that answer to words, I want the man who knows the words."

"And the collector?"

Petra had known that question was coming, and she still hadn't settled her own answer. "Marrow-Vane comes or Marrow-Vane reports. Those are his options. I'd rather have him where I can see him."

"You trust him?"

"No."

"Good," Ferro said. "Trust is how you end up carrying other people's debts. Keep it that way." He drained the cup and stood, joints cracking, and for a moment he looked less like a fat comfortable fence and more like the man who'd once carried her out of a burning warehouse on his back. "I'll have the Graywater key by tomorrow night. And Petra—" he paused at the door, not turning around, "—if we're doing this, I'm bringing rope. More rope than seems reasonable. Under a mountain, reasonable is whatever keeps you breathing."

* * *

Brother Ossian received the plan kneeling in his own chapel, which was not a chapel anymore, having been deconsecrated eleven years ago when he'd told his superiors exactly what he thought of the Synod's doctrine on soul-tithes. It was a drafty stone room with a broken altar and forty years of marginalia, and he loved it the way a man loves the wreck of his own life.

"You understand what you're asking me to translate down there," he said, getting up with the effort of a much older man. "The dead tongue isn't dead, Petra. That's the horror of it. Languages die when no one speaks them. This one is spoken constantly—it's just that everyone who speaks it is underground, and most of them aren't people anymore. Words have weight in a language like that. Names especially. If I mispronounce a binding-word in a tunnel where the walls are listening—"

"Then don't mispronounce them."

He gave her a look of profound scholarly suffering. "Yes. Obviously. Thank you. I'll simply *not err*, as one does." But he was already pulling books off his shelves, already stacking them, already packing, because that was Ossian: he complained his way into courage like a man complaining his way upstairs. "There's something else. Something I've been sitting on since the night of the coin."

Petra waited.

"The fragments Xanthe speaks—the ones about the tithe, the price unpaid—I assumed they were the child's own history. A sold soul recounting its sale. But the grammar is wrong. It's first person plural. *They counted us. They will count us again.* Not *me*. *Us.*" He set a worn lexicon into his satchel with exaggerated care. "And there's a word that recurs, a name-form, that I couldn't place until two nights ago when the child sleepwalked past my window. Sela. They said it. Clear as a bell, in the old inflection, the way you'd call someone home."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly. Petra put her hand flat against the doorframe.

"That's not possible," she said. "Nobody living knows that name. My mother called me that. She died when I was nine. There's no record, no grave marker, nothing. I made sure of it when I was fifteen and wanted the name dead."

"Yes," Ossian said gently. "Which raises the interesting question of how a feral child who has been underground since infancy knows to call you by it." He shouldered his satchel. "I've stopped believing in coincidence, Petra. It's a luxury the apostate can't afford. Whatever Xanthe is, they know you. Knew you before the door, I'd wager. The coin, the scars, the name—you weren't found by that child. You were *found out*."

* * *

Marrow-Vane came to her at dusk, uninvited, standing in her yard with his hands visible and empty the way a courteous man stands when he wants you to know he could be otherwise. Rain was starting, fine and needling, and he didn't seem to notice it.

"You've decided," he said.

"You've been watching the house."

"I've been watching the house," he agreed, without shame. "You've provisioned for six. You've sent word to the fat man at the Drowned Rat. Your priest is buying lamp-oil in quantities that suggest either suicide or spelunking. And the child stood at your window for an hour last night facing the mountain, which they do every night, and which you have finally noticed means they're being *called*." He tilted his head. "So. You're taking them down."

"I'm offering you a choice," Petra said. "Come with us under my command, or walk to whatever hole you report to and tell them we're coming, and live with what happens to the child when they send something worse than a courteous man with good manners."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, fast, but she caught it. Not fear. Something older and more tired.

"They already sent worse than me," Marrow-Vane said. "You haven't seen the Weeping Stair yet. You haven't seen what collects a default when the court is impatient." He stepped closer, rain running off his chin, and his voice lost its drawing-room polish entirely. "I have served the Undercourt for thirty-one years. I have read its law until I could recite it in my sleep, and I believed in it, Montrose—that's the part you wouldn't credit. I believed it was *fair*. Cruel, yes. Pitiless. But fair. A debt is a debt; a contract is sacred; the ledger does not lie. That was the whole architecture of my life. And then I pulled the file on this child's sale, because a runaway asset requires documentation, and I read the original instrument." His jaw worked. "It was signed by a guardian. Not the parents. A village elder selling a ward's soul against a communal grain-debt—a debt, incidentally, that the records show was already paid. Paid twice over. The court knew. The court collected anyway, because the ledger does not lie, and whoever writes the ledger decides what lying means."

The rain thickened. Somewhere behind him, inside the house, Xanthe began to sing—soft, tuneless, in the dead tongue, the song they sang facing the mountain every night.

"So no," Marrow-Vane said. "I'm not reporting to anyone. The Undercourt broke its own law first. It voided itself, the moment it collected on an unlawful instrument, and everything it has taken since rests on that theft—including, I suspect, rather more than one child." He extended his hand, palm up, formal as a treaty. "I'm defecting. Not for redemption—I've done too much arithmetic for redemption. For correction. The contract must be voided at its source, in front of the court, on the record, or the theft continues forever. I can get you into the antechambers. I can make the law a weapon instead of a wall. That's the only thing I have left to offer anyone."

Petra looked at his hand. She thought about trust, and about Ferro's warning, and about the fact that every person she was about to lead into the dark was damaged goods—apostate, defector, a smuggler with too much rope, herself with a head full of roads she'd spent thirty years forgetting on purpose.

A fellowship of the ruined. It would have to do. It was, she suspected, the only kind the deep roads had ever honored.

"If you betray us," she said, taking his hand, "I will kill you. Slowly, and in a language you understand."

"A refreshingly lawful sentiment," Marrow-Vane said. "I accept."

Behind them, Xanthe's song changed. Petra turned toward the window, and the cold went through her like a blade of ice, because she knew the melody—knew it the way you know a lullaby you haven't heard in thirty years, knew it in her teeth, in the ache of her old scars, in the name she'd buried.

It was a road-song. A walking song. And it was singing the way down to Graywater.

Ferro arrived an hour later with more rope than seemed reasonable, and they stood together in the yard—mercenary, smuggler, priest, collector, child—as the rain fell and the mountain waited, and Petra shouldered her pack and said the only benediction she had left:

"Stay close. Stay quiet. And if I start remembering out loud—let me."
