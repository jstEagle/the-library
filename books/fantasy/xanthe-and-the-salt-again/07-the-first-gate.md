## 7. The First Gate

Ferro had run cargo through the Graywater Delve twice in his life, both times by torchlight, both times fast, and neither time all the way to the bottom. The Delve was the kind of place smugglers used the way a knife-fighter uses an alley: quickly, without looking at what lived in the shadows, trusting speed over sense. Nobody went deep. Nobody sang down there. And nobody—*nobody*—brought a child.

He said as much on the last mile of the moor road, when the hills opened their green mouths and showed him the black slot of the old mine entrance below.

"You're telling me," he said, "that we're taking the tithe-child back into a tithe-mine."

"I'm telling you," Petra answered, "that Xanthe knows roads I don't."

"That's not comforting, Petra. That's the opposite of comforting."

"Then be uncomfortable quietly. We move at dusk."

The rain had stopped an hour out of Thornfell, but the moor still wept it back up out of the peat, so that the whole company walked through a low gray mist to their waists, ghostly, half-dissolved. Ferro took the rear by habit and found Marrow-Vane beside him within twenty paces, which was its own kind of insult—the man walked like he'd been invited, hands folded behind his back, salt-white coat spotless despite everything the weather could do.

"You've been here before," Marrow-Vane said. Not a question.

"Twice. Cargo runs." Ferro shifted his pack. "Never past the third gallery."

"And after the third gallery?"

"After the third gallery is why nobody goes past the third gallery."

Marrow-Vane nodded slowly, as if Ferro had confirmed something in a ledger only he could see. Ahead of them, Ossian trudged under his bundle of stolen relics and translated nothing, muttering to himself in three languages, two of them dead. And ahead of Ossian walked Petra, and beside Petra, small hand hooked into two of her belt-rings like a climber roped in, walked Xanthe.

The child hadn't spoken a word of the surface tongue the whole way down from the mountain. But they had hummed. All afternoon, tuneless little fragments, the way a kettle hums before it boils.

Ferro didn't like the humming. He liked it even less when the ground began to slope, because the humming changed key.

* * *

The Delve's mouth was a wound in the hillside thirty feet high, framed in timber older than any kingdom Ferro could name, black wood gone soft as cork, carved—when you got close enough—with lines of script worn nearly to nothing. Torches had burned here once; the soot ghosts of them still clung to the stone above the lintel like the shadows of hanged men.

Petra stood before the entrance for a long moment, saying nothing, doing nothing, and Ferro watched her face do something complicated: recognition and revulsion wrestling to a draw.

"Sela," Ossian said quietly, testing the name again, and Petra's jaw tightened.

"Not yet," she said. "Ask me again when we're out."

She struck a light. The first torch caught, guttered, steadied. She handed a second to Ferro, a third to Ossian, kept the fourth unlit in reserve, and looked at each of them in turn the way she must have looked at mercenary companies in the years before Ferro knew her—counting them, weighing them, deciding how many she was willing to lose.

"Rules," she said. "One: nobody wanders. Two: if Xanthe stops walking, we stop. Three: if anything speaks to you down here, you do not answer it. Not in any language. Especially not in *that* one."

"Why especially that one?" Ferro asked.

"Because it will know you mean it," Petra said, and stepped through the gate.

The dark took them the way water takes a dropped coin.

* * *

For the first hour it was only a mine, and mines Ferro understood. Timber props in rows, some rotted to lace, some still stubbornly holding up a mountain's worth of nothing. Rails long since pried up or sunk into the mud. Old tool-scars in the walls where pickaxes had bitten and been abandoned mid-stroke. The air was cold and mineral and tasted faintly of iron, the way a licked coin tastes, and Ferro tried very hard not to think about coins.

Xanthe never hesitated. At every fork the child turned without slowing, tugging Petra's belt-ring left or right, and every time Petra glanced back at Ferro with the same expression: confirmation of something she was afraid to confirm. Because the routes Xanthe chose were not the routes Ferro remembered. They were older. Where the smuggler paths followed the worked galleries, Xanthe led them through seams and crawl-ways that no pick had ever widened—passages that had simply been *left*, as if the miners had found something already there and decided not to improve upon it.

At the second hour they passed the first of the doors.

It was set into the tunnel wall where no door had any business being—a slab of pale wood in a frame of the same black timber as the gate, small, child-height, with a latch of tarnished silver. Someone had nailed a strip of parchment across it, long ago, and the ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, but Ferro could make out the shape of the words even if he couldn't read them: columns. Numbers. A list.

"What is it?" Ossian whispered, and then answered himself, fingers hovering over the script without touching. "A roster. A tally. It says—" his breath caught. "It says *delivered*. There are dates."

"How many names?" Petra asked. Her voice was level in the way Ferro had learned meant the opposite.

Ossian counted silently, lips moving. "Forty-one," he said. "On this door alone."

Nobody spoke. Xanthe reached up and laid one palm flat against the door, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and then withdrew it and kept walking, and the company followed, and Ferro made himself not look back at the door, and looked anyway. In the torchlight, for just a heartbeat, he would have sworn the parchment strip fluttered, though there was no wind underground, though there had never been wind underground.

* * *

The third gallery ended the way Ferro remembered: a collapse, old and total, a wall of fallen stone choked with rubble and root. On his cargo runs this had been the turn-back point. You unloaded at the third gallery, you took your profit, and you let the mountain keep the rest of itself.

Xanthe walked up to the collapse, put both hands against the rock, and sang.

It wasn't the humming anymore. It was a phrase—three notes, rising, then a fourth that fell away like a foot slipping off a ledge—and the sound of it in that enclosed space did something wrong to Ferro's ears. It didn't echo. Echoes come back to you. This went *away* from him, outward and downward, as if the tunnel were a throat and the song was being swallowed, and then—

—and then the rubble moved.

Not dramatically. No thunder. The stones merely settled, grinding against one another with a patience that felt deliberate, and a gap opened at the base of the fall, a gap exactly the size of a child, exactly the size of a crouching adult, a gap that had not existed ten seconds ago and that darkness poured out of like smoke finding a window.

"Well," said Marrow-Vane, into the silence. "That's new."

"It's not new," Petra said. Her voice had gone strange—flat, distant, the voice of someone reading their own handwriting and not remembering writing it. "I remember that song. I don't remember learning it." She looked down at Xanthe. "Where did you learn my—where did you learn *it*?"

Xanthe looked up at her with those yellow eyes and said, in the dead tongue, one word.

Ossian translated before he seemed to decide whether he wanted to. "*Home,*" he said. "They said *home*."

"Right," said Ferro, mostly to himself. "Great. Love that. Let's go home, then."

And he crawled into the dark, because the alternative was standing alone in the third gallery with a door full of names behind him, and Ferro had survived forty years of ugly work by always choosing the fear that was moving.

* * *

Beyond the collapse the mine stopped pretending to be a mine.

The passages widened into halls. The rough-hewn walls gave way to dressed stone, then to stone that had been *grown*, if such a thing were possible—columns ribbed like bone, arching overhead in vaults that repeated themselves in diminishing perspective until the torchlight gave out. Salt crusted everything: white bloomings on the floor like frost, white weepings down the walls like candle-grease, great translucent sheets of it in the crevices that caught the torch-flame and threw it back doubled, tripled, so that the company seemed suddenly numerous, a procession of Petras and Ferreros and Ossians stretching away into the deep in both directions.

Ferro counted his own reflections and lost count at eleven.

"Stay in single file," Petra ordered, low. "Touch nothing white."

"That's rather a lot of the architecture," Marrow-Vane observed.

"Then touch nothing at all."

But it was Xanthe who changed, more than the tunnels did. On the surface the child had been feral and silent, watchful as a bird, flinching from sudden movement. Down here the flinching stopped. The child's spine straightened. Their steps acquired a strange ceremonial precision, heel-to-toe, heel-to-toe, and they began to sing in earnest now—snatches of melody threaded with words, the dead tongue rolling off them fluently, effortlessly, the way water rolls downhill, and Ossian trailed them with his head cocked like a man listening to a sermon in a language he'd only half relearned, translating in a whisper as he went.

"*Hail the way, the way remembers.*"

"*Walk soft, for the ledger walks with you.*"

"*Bright ones, bright ones, count your candles—*" the child touched their own chest, twice, over the heart, "*—one, only one.*"

"What does that mean?" Ferro asked. "Count your candles. What candles?"

"Souls," Ossian said. His voice had gone thin. "I think—it's a warning-song. For children coming down the tithe-road. Keep your soul lit. Keep it burning. Don't let the dark see how much of it you have."

"And how much does the dark see?"

Ossian didn't answer. That was answer enough.

They walked. The songs continued. And Ferro became aware, gradually, then not gradually at all, of the thing he had been trying not to notice for the better part of an hour:

The dark was listening.

There was no other word for it. Every snatch of Xanthe's song fell into those vast salt-hung halls and did not die there. It *sat* there. The acoustics held each note a moment too long, returned each syllable a half-beat late, and the delays weren't random—they clustered, they leaned toward the child, they gathered around Xanthe's small clear voice like moths, like courtiers, like an audience leaning forward in the dark. When Xanthe paused between verses, the silence that followed was not empty. It was attentive. Ferro had spent his life in places where silence meant danger, and this was different, this was worse: this was silence meaning *interest.*

He stopped walking.

Ahead, Petra heard him stop and turned, torch high. "Ferro."

"The dark's paying attention to us," he said. "You feel that? Tell me you feel that."

Petra was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone of a woman admitting to a wound: "I grew up here. Part of me knows what it means when the dark listens." She looked at Xanthe, at the child's lifted face, at the rapt, unchildlike calm of it. "It means it likes the singing. It means it hasn't heard singing in a long time."

"Is that good or bad?"

"I don't know," Petra said, and started walking again. "Keep moving."

* * *

They made camp in a side chamber off a grand descending stair, because Petra called it and because everyone's legs had begun shaking in that way that meant the body was done negotiating. No fire. Cold rations eaten in torchlight, which made everything taste of wax and shadow. Ossian sat with his back against a salt-crusted column and prayed under his breath to gods whose names he no longer had permission to use, and Marrow-Vane sat apart, polishing a coin that never seemed to get cleaner.

Ferro took first watch, because taking first watch was how he told a crew he trusted them enough to sleep near them, and because he wanted to be awake when the dark did whatever the dark was going to do next.

Xanthe wouldn't sleep. The child sat curled against Petra's side, eyes open, watching the stairway mouth with the fixed patience of something that expected to be waited on. After a while, barely loud enough for Ferro to catch it, the singing started again—softer now, almost private, a lullaby-shaped thing with words that slid past Ossian's sleeping ear untranslated.

And from the stairwell, from far below, faint as memory, something began to sing back.

It was Xanthe's own melody, note for note, but slowed. Stretched. Sung in a voice like wet sand sliding, in a register that Ferro felt in his teeth rather than heard with his ears, and it rose up the stair with terrible gentleness, verse answering verse, the dark teaching the song back to the child the way a parent teaches a name.

Ferro's hand found his knife without his permission.

Xanthe smiled. Small. Content. The smile of a child hearing their mother's footsteps on the stairs.

The answering voice descended the scale, softened, thinned—and stopped, all at once, like a held breath.

In the ringing quiet afterward, Ferro realized two things at once. First: that every reflection of the torchlight in the salt crusts along the chamber walls had turned, somehow, to face the stairwell. Second: that far down the great descending stair, in the direction the song had come from, something had begun to climb.

It was not hurrying. It did not need to hurry. It knew exactly where they were.

"Ferro," Petra said quietly, without opening her eyes, because of course she was awake, of course she had heard it too. "Wake the others. And whatever comes up those stairs—"

"Yeah?"

"Don't let it count us."
