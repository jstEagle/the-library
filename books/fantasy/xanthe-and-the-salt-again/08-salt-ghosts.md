## 8. Salt Ghosts

Brother Ossian had spent eleven years learning how to be quiet in holy places, and he had never been anywhere so loud with silence as the flooded gallery.

The company walked a causeway of black stone that ran through the middle of the chamber like a spine. On either side, water stretched away into darkness—still water, salt-white where the lamplight touched it, so thick with dissolved mineral that it held the light like milk holds honey. The ceiling above was lost to shadow, but the water reflected everything: the six of them in single file, the lanterns, the wet gleam of Xanthe's hair, and beneath all of it, faintly, something else. Shapes. Faces turned upward, just under the surface, like swimmers who had forgotten they were swimming.

Ossian counted them without meaning to. It was an old habit from his days auditing reliquaries—count what frightens you, and the fear becomes arithmetic, and arithmetic cannot hurt you. He got to forty before he stopped counting. Then he started again at one because forty felt like a lie he was telling himself.

"They're not bodies," Ferro said quietly. He was walking point with his knife drawn, which Ossian suspected was less for defense than for the comfort of holding something honest. "I've run this Delve twice, years back, the dry galleries north of here. Never came through this one. But I know drowned places when I see them. There's no bloat down there. No bones showing white."

"No," Petra said. Her voice was flat in the way Ossian had learned meant she was holding something down with both hands. "No bodies."

"Then what?"

"Echoes." It was Xanthe who answered, in the trade-tongue, slow and careful—the child's command of living speech grew by the hour now, as if proximity to the deep roads were watering some starved root. They did not look up from the causeway stones. "When a tithe-soul is taken, the taking leaves a mark. Like a footprint. Like a handprint on a wall. The water remembers the shape of what passed through it." A pause. "Some of them stayed in their shapes."

"Some of *what* stayed?" Marrow-Vane asked. The debt-collector's voice was its usual instrument of terrible courtesy, but even courtesy frays after three days underground, and there was wire in it now.

"The children," Xanthe said. "Who waited too long."

* * *

Ossian walked at the rear, beside Petra, and kept his eyes moving between the water and her hands. She had not let go of Xanthe since the ambush at the Weeping Stair—or rather, since the flight from it; her grip on the child's shoulder had become a kind of tether, and Xanthe tolerated it the way a wild thing tolerates warmth, stiffly, without quite leaning into it.

He had known Petra Montrose for two years as a client and four months as a friend, which in his experience was the correct order for those two things to occur. He knew her as a woman of few prayers and many oaths, whose oath-mark—a coiled line like a rope knotted around itself, just below the hollow of her throat—had gone dark and silent along with the god it honored. He knew she flinched at the smell of brine on certain days and could not say why. He knew she remembered nothing before the age of seven except snow, a door, and a woman's voice saying *run*.

He was beginning to understand that the silence of Vessel-of-Salt was not the strangest thing about her. It might not even have been in the top three.

The causeway narrowed. The water crept closer on both sides until it lapped at the edges of the stone, and the faces beneath the surface were close enough now that Ossian could see their detail: children's faces, most of them, round-cheeked or sharp-chinned, ages six to perhaps fourteen. Their eyes were open. Their mouths moved—not screaming, not speaking, but shaping words over and over the way a sleeper shapes a name. Salt crust rimmed the waterline like frost, and where the crust met each face, it had grown into delicate white filigree, crowns and collars and lace, dressing every drowned echo in the finery of a court they had died waiting to enter.

"It's a waiting room," Ferro said. His voice had gone rough. "That's what this is. They dressed them up and put them in a waiting room."

"They were not dressed," Xanthe said softly. "They grew the salt themselves. Hoping to be beautiful enough to be called in."

Nobody answered that. Ossian found he could not; his theology, such as it remained to him—apostate, defrocked, self-excommunicated and proud of two of those three—had no shelf built for this. He had argued for years that the gods were distant at best and invented at worst, and he had made his peace with a universe that did not watch. But this chamber watched. This chamber had been *built* to watch, and everything in him that still remembered being a boy kneeling on cold flagstones while a priest explained suffering as pedagogy rose up in his throat and wanted to scream at the architecture.

A face drifted nearer to the causeway's edge. A girl, maybe nine, her salt-filigee collar bloomed wide as a swan's wingspan. Her mouth stopped moving.

Her eyes found Petra.

* * *

Ossian saw it happen and would spend the rest of his life failing to describe it accurately. The girl's gaze tracked along the line of walkers—Ferro, Marrow-Vane, himself—and snagged on Petra the way a hook snags cloth. And then the whole still gallery changed pressure. Every face below the surface rotated, slow as compass needles finding north, toward the woman with the dark oath-mark. Dozens of eyes. All of them open. All of them arriving at once upon the same face.

And the girl began to sing.

It was the dead tongue—the language Ossian had spent three nights bent over Xanthe's scratched fragments, reconstructing grammar from a tongue that had no living speakers, no surviving texts, only the muscle memory of a sold child and whatever the apostate priest could dredge from forbidden commentaries. He knew perhaps sixty words of it with any confidence. He understood the song almost entire.

*"Sela. Sela, the snow is falling up. Sela, they said you ran, but running is a kind of being collected, slowly, over years. Sela, your ledger-line is still warm. We kept your place. We kept your place. We kept—"*

"Sela," the drowned girl said aloud, in the trade-tongue, in a voice like water finding a crack. "You came back."

Petra stopped walking.

* * *

Later, Ossian would try to reconstruct the next ninety seconds and find them full of holes, the way memory goes when the body takes over from the mind. What he retained:

He retained Petra going very still—not the controlled stillness she wore like armor in negotiation, but the other kind, the animal kind, the stillness of a creature hoping not to be seen. He retained Xanthe turning to look up at her, and the strange double expression on the child's face: fear for her, and recognition of something.

He retained the way Petra's hand rose, unbidden, to the oath-mark at her throat, and the small broken sound she made when her fingers touched it—as though the skin beneath had begun, very faintly, to glow with heat.

"You're not Sela," Petra said. Her voice cracked in the middle like river ice underfoot. "My name isn't—I don't—"

"We kept your place," the girl sang, and now other voices joined, a dozen, then dozens, the whole gallery rising into that patient, terrible chorus, all of them singing variations of the same verse in the dead tongue while Ossian translated in a whisper he could barely force out: *"We kept your place. Six winters old. Sold against the thaw. The collector never came. The collector never came. The collector never came."*

"The collector never came," Marrow-Vane repeated. Ossian heard the man's breath go out of him as if struck. "That's—that's not possible. I have read the ledgers. Every default is pursued. Every contract is—" He stopped. Ossian watched him arrive at the same arithmetic the rest of them were arriving at, and watched the courteous mask fail entirely. "Every contract is enforced. Unless the contract was never valid. Unless someone *blocked collection*."

"Someone paid the interest," Xanthe said. The child's voice was very quiet. "For six-and-twenty years. Someone has been paying, and paying, and paying, so that the line would never come due. So the collector would pass over Sela's name forever." Those yellow eyes lifted to Petra's face. "Do you understand why your god went silent? Silence is not abandonment. Silence is the sound of someone spending everything they have."

* * *

The chorus was still singing when Petra began to walk again—but faster now, past the causeway's edge, and the drowned children drifted alongside beneath the surface, keeping pace, their salt-lace collars trailing like wedding trains. Ossian hurried after her and caught her arm, and she nearly took his hand off at the wrist; her reflexes were mercenary-fast even shattered.

"Don't," she said. "Don't touch me right now."

"I'm not going to touch you. I'm going to talk at you until you listen, which is my function in this company." He matched her stride. Ahead, Ferro had halted at the far arch, knife still drawn, watching the water with the expression of a man revising every story he'd ever told about this mountain. "Petra. Montrose. *Sela.* Which name do you want me to use?"

"Neither."

"That's not an answer available to you anymore, and we both know it. You've known it since the coin. Since the maps under your own house." He kept his voice low, pitched for her alone, though he doubted it mattered; the gallery heard everything. "I have spent eleven years telling people their gods are stories. I have burned my vestments and meant it. And I am telling you now, as a man with nothing left to gain by lying about the divine: something loved you hard enough to bankrupt itself. Whatever else you are, you are *that*. You were bought at six and ransomed ever since, and the ransom has finally run out, and that is why we are all walking through a room full of your siblings' ghosts."

"They're not my siblings."

"No," Ossian agreed gently. "Worse. They're your cohort. Your class. The other names on the page."

Petra stopped. In the lamplight her face had the gray cast of old candlewax, and Ossian watched her do the thing she evidently did instead of weeping: she breathed, four counts in, four counts hold, four counts out, a soldier's box built around a collapsing heart.

"When I was small," she said at last, "before the snow, before the door—there was a song. I've hummed it my whole life and told myself it was nothing. A nonsense tune. Lullaby-shaped." Her hand closed over the dark oath-mark. "It's the first verse of what they're singing."

"I know," Ossian said. "I recognized the melody thirty seconds ago. It's a counting-out rhyme. It's the liturgy of the tithe. You didn't learn it after you escaped, Petra. You were taught it *here*, before anything else, the way children are taught grace before meat."

Below them, the drowned girl pressed one palm flat against the underside of the water's surface, as if against glass. Around the palm, new salt bloomed outward in a widening ring of white filigree, delicate as frost ferns.

"She's making room for you," Xanthe said. "At the table. That's what the growing means. That's what they did while they waited—they set places." The child looked up at Petra, and for the first time since the Weeping Stair, since the scattering, since everything, Xanthe reached up and took Petra's hand. "Sela. You have to decide what you're going to do when we reach the court. Because they already know what you are. And soon, so will the King."

"What am I?" Petra asked. It was not rhetorical. It was the question of a woman standing at the edge of her own grave, asking who dug it.

Xanthe smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ossian had seen in eleven years of apostasy.

"You're the payment that never stopped," the child said. "Come on. The water's getting shallower. We're almost at the shrine."

Behind them, in the flooded gallery, forty voices sang the counting-rhyme back down to zero, and the last face to sink beneath the surface was the girl's, still keeping a place for someone named Sela at a table that no longer existed.
