## 9. Marrow-Vane's Confession

The fire at Graywater's edge burned low, and Marrow-Vane counted the ways he might still leave.

There were four. The road north, back to the court that had made him. The road east, into the lowlands where no ledger reached — though every road reached, in the end; the ledgers were patient as salt. The road down, which was the point of this whole miserable expedition and therefore not an exit at all. And the fourth way out, the one he had used twice before in a long career: the way of simply ceasing to be wherever a question was being asked.

He had not used the fourth way since the night he collected a cooper's soul from a house that smelled of pitch and tallow, while a child with yellow eyes watched from behind a barrel.

He sat apart from the others because sitting apart was habit, and habit was armor. Ferro was on first watch, whittling something that was either a saint or a duck. Ossian had fallen asleep mid-prayer, which Marrow-Vane considered the most honest form of devotion he'd ever witnessed. Petra Montrose sat sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, and Xanthe slept against her side like something washed ashore.

Marrow-Vane watched the child breathe.

That was the trouble with watching. He had spent thirty years training himself not to. A debt-collector who saw debtors as people starved; a collector who saw them as entries ate well and slept soundly. He had been very good at it. He had been, by the court's own accounting, the finest instrument it owned — punctual, courteous, incorruptible in all the small ways, corruptible only in the one large way that mattered, which was that he had believed the law was good because the law was all there was.

Then the law had broken itself, and the instrument had been left holding a chisel with nothing true to carve.

"You're staring," Petra said, without looking up from her knife.

"I am accounting," he said.

"Same thing, worse posture."

* * *

It happened an hour later, when the fire had gone to embers and Ferro's watch ended and Ossian took over with the bleary dignity of a man who had once argued doctrine with bishops. Xanthe woke crying.

Not loudly. That was the worst of it. The child cried the way people cried in cells — muffled, economical, ashamed of the noise, as if grief were another debt accruing interest. Petra gathered them in without waking fully, the way mothers did, the way soldiers did not, and Marrow-Vane found himself standing, found his feet carrying him closer, found his mouth opening around words he had rehearsed for eleven years and never once spoken aloud.

"Montrose."

She looked up. In the ember-light her face was all angles and suspicion, and beneath both, exhaustion. "Whatever you're selling—"

"Nothing. For once in my life, nothing." He knelt. It was not a graceful motion; his knees had opinions about kneeling these days. But he put himself down at the level of the fire, at the level of the child, because some things could not be said from above. "I need to tell you what I am. All of it. Before we go under. You deserve a fair ledger before we enter a place full of false ones."

Petra's knife came to rest across her knee, point toward him. "Talk."

* * *

"My name is not Marrow-Vane," he began. "That is a courtesy-title. The court issues courtesy-titles so that its instruments can be addressed without anyone having to remember what they were before." He folded his hands, collector's habit, as though presenting figures. "Before, I was a tithe-child. Like Xanthe. Like you, if Ossian's arithmetic is right and it usually is. Born under Thornfell. Sold at the age my parents could no longer feed me. Raised in the counting-houses below, where they teach you three things: the law of contracts, the weight of souls, and how to stop hearing children cry. I excelled at all three."

Xanthe had gone still against Petra's side. Watching him. Yellow eyes open in the dark like coins at the bottom of a well.

"The court has two kinds of servants," Marrow-Vane continued. "Those it keeps by force, and those it keeps by paperwork. Force is expensive. Paperwork is elegant. I was paperwork. They showed me my own contract when I turned twelve — my parents' signatures, their marks pressed in salt, the sum they owed and the sum I would owe until it cleared. Then they gave me a scribe's pen and told me that someday, if I was precise enough, I could buy my own line back out of the book." His mouth bent. It was not quite a smile. "I believed them for twenty years. Precision is a kind of hope, when you have nothing else to hold."

"And then?" Petra asked.

"And then I became a collector. And then—" He stopped. This was the door. He had stood before it for eleven years. Behind it was a house that smelled of pitch and tallow, and he opened it now the way you open any door you must: quickly, before your hands learn cowardice.

"There was a cooper in the lowland villages. Halven Cooper, his trade-name; his true name is in the ledger and I will not speak it here, because names are currency and I have stolen enough. He had taken a court-loan — salt-credit, against a bad winter, the kind of loan the court extends precisely because bad winters are common and default is certain. The contracts are designed to fail. That is the whole architecture. The Underking does not want repayment. Repayment ends an account. Default feeds one." He heard his own voice go flat and clerical and hated it, and let it stay flat anyway, because flatness was the only way through. "Halven defaulted. Standard procedure. Two collectors. I was senior. We went at night, because souls come easier out of sleepers, and the court calls this mercy, and I wrote that word down myself more times than I can count. *Collected humanely. Subject experienced minimal distress.*"

Ossian had stopped pretending to sleep. Marrow-Vane did not look at him. Looking at witnesses made confessions performances, and he needed this to be the other thing — the thing confessions were before churches got hold of them.

"Minimal distress," he repeated. "The man begged. They always beg. You are trained to hear begging as weather — ambient, meaningless, no action required. I performed the extraction. It takes less than a minute. There is a moment when the soul comes loose, and the body doesn't die — that is the cruelty of it — the body lives on, breathing, working, hollow, for years sometimes, and everyone says *how sad, he was never the same after his wife died,* because that is the story people prefer. The soul goes into the coin. The coin goes into my case. The case goes into the vaults. The account balances." He unfolded his hands and looked at them as though they belonged to someone else, someone he was also collecting from. "And when I turned to leave, there was a child behind the barrel. Watching. Yellow eyes. The cooper's daughter, or son — the ledger said *issue of the debtor, age five, asset held in reserve.* Held in reserve. Do you understand what that means? It means the court had already priced the child. Already entered the line. Waiting for the parents' default to mature into the child's own contract, so the debt could be renewed, generation upon generation, forever, which is the only harvest the Underking truly tends."

Petra had gone very still. Not the stillness of shock. The stillness of a woman rearranging everything she knew around a new load-bearing fact.

"The child didn't run," Marrow-Vane said. "Children always run. This one stepped out from behind the barrel and looked at me — at my face, not my title, not my case of coins, my face — and said something in the dead tongue. I was raised below; I knew the language. I have tried for eleven years to make those words mean anything except what they meant." His voice did something he had not authorized. He let it. "They said: *You took my mother and father's faces away.* Because that is what a harvested soul looks like to the ones left behind. Not absence. Erasure. The child could no longer remember what their parents had looked like when the parents had loved them. The love was in the part I took."

The fire ticked. Somewhere out in the dark, Graywater's flooded Delve breathed its long cold breath through the ground.

"I filed the report," Marrow-Vane said. "I balanced the account. I went home to my cell in the counting-house and I lay awake listening for crying and heard none, because I had been cured of hearing it, and I understood then that I was not a man who had done a terrible thing. I was a terrible thing that did menial work efficiently. And I got up the next morning and did it again, and again, four hundred and six more times, because the alternative was the voiding-pits, and I told myself the court would collect regardless — if not me, then someone crueler — and every word of that was true, and every word of it was also the cheapest coin a coward mints."

"Why tell us now?" Petra asked. Her voice was level. Her knife-hand was not.

"Because eleven days ago the court sent me a summons with my own name misspelled on it, and the court never misspells, which meant the ledger was failing, which meant the law itself was rotting from the head down. And because I checked the reserve-entry for the cooper's issue." He finally looked at Xanthe, directly, and did not look away, because looking away had been his profession and he was resigning from it. "The entry was flagged. Irregular. Signed by a guardian's mark, not the parents'. I have spent thirty years reading contracts the way priests read scripture, and that signature is a forgery, or worse — a coercion. Which makes the entire claim on this child unlawful. Voidable. And a voided contract does not merely free one soul. Precedent, Montrose. In a system built entirely of precedent, one cracked stone brings down the stair."

* * *

Silence held the camp for a long moment. Then Ossian, quietly, from his watch-post: "You're saying the child's contract is defective."

"I'm saying it is *poisoned fruit*. The root-contract — whatever binds every tithe-line to the court — was signed improperly in this instance, and the Underking's own law states that a claim founded on a flawed instrument is itself flawed. I memorized that clause when I was nine years old. Article Nine of the Salt Concord. They taught it to us so we would never be able to use it." His smile finally arrived, thin and joyless. "They forgot they had taught it to me."

Ferro, returning early for second watch, stood at the edge of the light with his half-carved saint-or-duck forgotten in his fist. "So that's why you defected. Lawyering."

"That is why I defected. Yes." Marrow-Vane rose, knees cracking, and faced Petra fully. "Understand me clearly, mercenary. I am not asking for forgiveness. Forgiveness is a transaction and I know transactions, and there is no payment I possess that covers four hundred and seven collections. When this is done, if Xanthe wants my soul in a coin, I will hand it over unsealed. That is not remorse. Remorse is cheap. That is *balance*." He drew a breath. "What I ask is colder and smaller. Let me correct the error. Let me carry the forged instrument into the court and read it aloud before the throne, in Article Nine's own words, and watch the whole rotten edifice discover it has been unlawful since the day it was built. I do not seek redemption. Redemption implies I was ever worth saving. I seek *correction*. A ledger must balance. Mine will not balance upward. But it can balance true."

Petra studied him for a long time. Behind her, Xanthe had risen, silent, small hands fisted, and was looking at Marrow-Vane with an expression far too old for the face wearing it.

Then the child spoke — the dead tongue, soft and precise. Ossian translated in a whisper, as though the words might bruise:

"*The collector remembers my eyes. No one else ever remembered my eyes.*"

Marrow-Vane knelt again. "I remember everything," he said, in the dead tongue, badly accented but unmistakable. "That is my curse and my qualification."

Xanthe stared at him. And slowly, terribly, the child reached out and touched his cheek — the way you touch a thing to confirm it is real — and Marrow-Vane did not flinch, though every nerve in him begged to, because he had decided that flinching was finished.

It was Petra who broke the moment, standing, knife sheathed at last. "Fine. You walk with us. But Vane — if there's one more thing in your ledger I don't know about, I'll bury you in it."

"There is one more thing," he said.

Her hand went back to the knife.

"Not mine," he said quickly. "Yours. I pulled your line before I came to your door, Montrose. Standard diligence. Your entry in the great ledger is marked — not *sold*, not *collected*. Marked *reserved*. Same notation as the cooper's child." He met her eyes. "Someone below has been keeping a place for you for twenty-five years. And reserved lines, in my experience, always mean one thing."

"What?"

"The King intends to collect in person."

* * *

Above them, far up the black throat of Thornfell, the mountain groaned — a long settling sigh, stone shifting on stone, the sound a house makes when something inside it turns over in its sleep.

No one in the camp spoke.

But down in the deep roads, in the counting-halls where the salt candles burned without flame, a page turned in a book no hand was near, and a line twenty-five years dormant began, letter by letter, to ink itself in.
