## 10. The Silent God's Grave

The passage changed an hour before they found the shrine, though none of them could say how they knew.

Petra felt it first in her teeth—a pressure like biting foil, a taste of old metal rising through her jaw. Then Xanthe stopped walking. The child stood in the middle of the gallery with their head tilted back and their throat working, swallowing air as if it were something thick, something that had to be forced down.

"What is it?" Ferro asked, his lantern held high. The light barely reached the walls; the corridor here had widened into something less like a tunnel and more like a throat itself.

Xanthe said three words in the dead tongue. Ossian, walking behind with Marrow-Vane's manacles long since struck off but his guilt still clanking at his heels like a chained thing, went pale beneath his grime.

"They said—" He stopped. Started again. "They said *we are entering the house of the one who was eaten.*"

Petra's hand found the wall. The salt there was warm.

Not warm like stone holding body heat. Warm like skin. Like something sleeping, or something pretending to sleep while it listened.

"Keep moving," she said, and hated how steady her voice came out. Steadiness was a muscle like any other. A hundred days of silence had trained it well.

* * *

They had been underground four days. Four days since Graywater Delve, since Xanthe had led them singing down stairs that had no business existing, past galleries where drowned children's echoes still played games with pebbles that crumbled to salt when touched. Four days of Ossian translating fragments, of Marrow-Vane walking with the terrible straight-backed dignity of a man doing penance by arithmetic, counting his crimes aloud only when asked. Four days of Ferro glancing over his shoulder so often that Petra had started glancing too, though she told herself it was habit, not suspicion. Ferro had pulled her out of two collapsing shafts and one bad marriage negotiation with the river-gangs. He was hers. She was almost sure of it.

Almost was a currency she'd been spending freely lately.

Now the corridor opened without warning—no door, no arch, just a widening, like a fist unclenching—and they stood in a chamber that made every one of them stop breathing for a different reason.

It was round. Vast. The ceiling rose into darkness that their lanterns couldn't touch, and the floor was worked salt, polished by centuries of feet or hands or tongues into a surface like grey ice. Rings of script ran around the walls, carved deep, and even Petra—who could not read a word of the dead tongue and never could, no matter what she'd been told about herself—felt the meaning of them press against her like weather.

Ossian sank to his knees. Not gracefully. He went down like a building coming down, all at once, and caught himself on his palms.

"This is a shrine," he whispered. "This is—gods forgive me, this is a *nave.* This was a place of worship."

"Worship of what?" Ferro asked.

Ossian didn't answer. He was reading the rings of script, his lips moving, his fingers tracing the grooves, and his face was doing something Petra had only seen once before, on a man reading his own death warrant.

Petra walked toward the center of the chamber.

She didn't decide to. Her legs decided, and she followed them the way you follow a dog that has suddenly caught a scent it knew from puppyhood. The others' voices fell away behind her—or perhaps she fell away from them; distance was getting strange down here—and the salt floor gave beneath her boots with a faint give, a faint warmth, and her oath-mark began to ache.

She had carried the mark for thirty years. It sat below her left collarbone, a spiral the size of a coin, inked in ash and brine the day she swore her first contract at fourteen: *Vessel-of-Salt, hold my word; I hold yours.* Every mercenary of the Thornfell roads bore some mark or other—the mountain gods were small gods, local gods, gods of oaths and tolls and safe passage—but hers had never once burned, never once flared, never given her anything but the flat, reliable silence of a well-made tool. That was why she'd loved it, if she was honest. Vessel-of-Salt was a god that kept its mouth shut and its bargains.

For a hundred days, it hadn't answered at all.

Now the mark ached like a tooth dying, and Petra crossed the last ten paces and saw the altar, and understood everything at once, the way you understand a fall only after you've hit the ground.

* * *

The altar had been a pillar once. She could see its shape beneath the ruin—a column of white salt-crystal, tall as two men, faceted, meant to catch light from somewhere above and scatter it through the chamber like blessing. Now it was broken. Not toppled—*broken*, shattered from within, the way a kiln shatters when something inside it expands too fast. Great shards lay tumbled around the base, each one clouded, each one dark at the heart, as though the crystal had been stained from the inside out.

And at the center of the wreckage, where the pillar's heart should have been, there was a hollow.

A cavity in the shape of nothing. A socket. Empty.

Around the base of the altar, someone had built a ring of small stones, hundreds of them, each no bigger than a knucklebone, each one carved with a name. Petra crouched. The names were in the dead tongue, but she knew what they were anyway. You know a grave marker in any language.

She picked one up. Turned it in her fingers. The carving was fresh—days old, maybe weeks. The stone was still sharp-edged.

"Sela."

The voice behind her was Ossian's, but it came out strangled, half-swallowed.

"That name means vessel," he said. "In the old rite. The ring-script—it's a litany. It says *here slept the Vessel-of-Salt, drinker of oaths, keeper of the ledger-toll, who stood between the tithe-children and the hungry dark.* And then it says—" His voice cracked clean through. "It says *and the Underking came up in the night, and drank him to the dregs, and left the cup standing so the faithful would not look for him elsewhere.*"

Petra did not move. The little named stone sat in her palm, cold now, cold as truth.

"It was taken," Ossian said. "Your god wasn't silent, Montrose. Your god was *swallowed.*"

* * *

Here is what Petra remembered of her god, and it was almost nothing, and it was everything:

She remembered being six years old, kneeling in a room under her grandmother's house while the women sang a song with no words anyone would explain, and something vast and patient and cool as well-water regarding her from everywhere at once. She remembered it asking nothing. That was the strangeness of it, looking back. Every other god in every other story took, demanded, required. This one had simply *been present*, the way the mountain was present, the way salt was present in the sea—invisible until you tasted it.

She remembered leaving. She remembered her mother's face, which had looked the way faces look at funerals where no one has died yet. She remembered being told, later, that the village had "paid its respects." She remembered growing up and swearing contracts and finding, always, that her word held where other men's slipped—that people trusted her across campfires and card tables and knife-points, trusted her beyond sense, trusted her the way you trust load-bearing stone.

She had thought it was character. She had spent thirty years building a self on top of that belief, the way a house builds on bedrock.

The bedrock had a name. The bedrock had been holding her up all along, silently, faithfully, a god keeping a bargain it had made with a six-year-old girl before she was old enough to know what a bargain was—

—and it had been dragged out of its own nave in the night and drunk like a cup of wine, and left here in pieces, and its faithful had gone on praying into an empty socket for years, and Petra had counted a hundred days of silence and called it doubt.

Something went through her that was not grief. Grief was too gentle a word, too clean. This was grief the way a wound is a doorway. It went through her and came out the far side with a new shape, and the new shape had edges, and the edges wanted something.

Her oath-mark burned black.

She felt it happen. Felt the spiral below her collarbone go from ache to heat to a brand, felt the ink of it darken as though the blood beneath had turned to smoke, felt it spread—not pain, exactly, past pain into something more like *announcement*. She put her hand to it and her fingers came away glistening, and the wet on them was black, and where it touched her skin the black crawled outward in fine veins like frost on glass.

"Montrose?" Ferro's voice, sharp with alarm. "Montrose, your neck—"

"I see it." Her own voice came out wrong. Low. Layered. As though something spoke a half-beat behind her words, using her mouth's echo.

She looked down at her hand, and the black veins pulsed, and in the pulse she heard—not words. A rhythm. A tide-sound. The memory of a presence, cool and vast and patient, worn down to a single repeating note:

*held. held. held.*

Still keeping the bargain. Even in pieces. Even drunk to the dregs. Some ember of it, some last insoluble residue of a god, still down here in the dark, still holding the oath it had sworn to a child who never knew she had one.

"Petra." Marrow-Vane had come forward, and the debt-collector's face—for the first time since she'd met him—was stripped of courtesy. What was underneath was older and worse. Recognition. "Your mark. I have seen this before. In the ledger-hall. There are entries marked *ember-held*, and when the clerks speak of them they do not use the word for debt. They use the word for *fire*. The Underking does not merely collect souls, Montrose. He eats gods. It is his particular delicacy. And what he cannot digest—"

"—he leaves burning in something small," Petra finished.

Xanthe.

The child stood at the edge of the wrecked altar, bare feet on the salt floor, and their eyes—those impossible gold-pale eyes, the eyes she'd named them for—were fixed on the empty socket in the pillar's heart. And Xanthe was crying without any expression at all, tears running down a perfectly still face, the way stone weeps in a flooded mine, and their lips were moving, shaping the same syllables over and over in the dead tongue.

Ossian translated in a whisper, as if the chamber might object to anything louder.

"He held me. When I was sold. He held me in the dark places and told me stories so I would not hear the court singing. He was warm. He was so warm. Then the King came and it was cold and I ran and I have been cold ever since and *I thought it was my fault, I thought he left because I was bad—*"

"He didn't leave," Petra said.

Her voice came out raw. The layered thing was gone from it; there was nothing left in her but the woman.

"He didn't leave you, Xanthe. He was taken. Do you hear me? Taken, fighting, taken—and he left the only thing he had left with *you*, because you were the smallest and the most frightened and the one who needed holding most. That's not abandonment." She knelt, joints cracking, until her eyes were level with the child's. The black veins on her neck pulsed in time with the salt-floor's warmth, in time with the tide-note in her bones. "That's a god doing the last thing gods do. Spending himself on someone."

Xanthe made a sound then—not a word in either language, just a sound, six years old and a hundred years old at once—and threw themselves against her, and Petra held on.

* * *

Later—minutes or hours; the chamber did not distinguish—Ferro crouched beside her among the shards. He had set his lantern down close, and the light moved on the broken crystal like water.

"The names," he said quietly. "On the stones. Someone's been adding them."

"I know."

"You know who?"

Petra turned one of the markers in her fingers again. Fresh-cut. Days old.

"The herald," she said. "Or the clerks. Someone down here keeps faith with the things they kill. That's worse than glee, Ferro. That's *ritual*." She looked around the chamber—at the rings of litany, at the scattered congregation of named stones, at the empty socket that had once held a god who kept his mouth shut and his bargains. "They didn't just eat him. They enshrined the eating. Made a monument of it. So that anyone who came looking would find exactly this: proof that their god is dead and the King keeps excellent records."

"Marrow-Vane says the throne vault lies past the Weeping Stair," Ferro said. "Two days, maybe three, if the maps Xanthe draws keep being right. Says the herald will have felt us enter the nave already. Says we've run out of road where nobody's watching."

"Marrow-Vane says a lot of things."

"Yeah." Ferro rubbed his jaw. He was looking at her strangely—at the black veins, at the mark she'd stopped trying to cover. "Petra. That thing on your neck. Is it killing you?"

She considered the question honestly, the way she considered all contract terms.

"No," she said. "It's *waking* me. There's a difference, and I think I'm about to learn what it costs."

She rose. Around the chamber, the others were gathering themselves—Ossian pocketing shards of the altar like relics, muttering that a god deserved a burial even if all they could bury was the cup he'd been drunk from; Marrow-Vane standing at the far archway with his head cocked, listening to something in the deep rock that the rest of them could not yet hear; Xanthe pressed close against her side, one small hand gripping hers, warm as an ember.

From the archway ahead, drifting up out of the dark like smoke from a snuffed candle, came music.

Singing. Thin, sweet, many-voiced, in the dead tongue—and beneath it, unmistakable now that she knew what to listen for, a second sound. A grinding. A slowness. Stone upon stone, far away, rhythmic as a heartbeat.

The Weeping Stair, opening for them.

Or closing.

Marrow-Vane turned back to her, and his face in the lantern-light was the face of a man reading terms he did not like.

"They know we're here," he said. "And Montrose—the singing. It's a summons. It's addressed to *you*."

Petra tightened her grip on Xanthe's hand and felt the black mark pulse once, hard, like a knock at a door.

"Then let's not keep the Underking waiting," she said, and walked toward the singing.
