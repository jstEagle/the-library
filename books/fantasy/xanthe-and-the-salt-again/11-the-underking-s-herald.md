## 11. The Underking's Herald

*There is a word in the dead tongue for the moment when a lamp gutters and the dark leans in to watch it die.*

Ossian would later say he could not translate it exactly. The closest he managed was *the listening*, and even that was wrong, because listening implies ears, and what Xanthe felt in the deep gallery that night had no need of ears. It came up through the salt in the stone and into the salt in their blood, and every hair on Xanthe's arms rose like blades of grass before a storm.

They were camped in a vaulted chamber where the ceiling wept brine in slow silver threads. Petra had called it safe because the walls bore no court-marks. Ferro had called it safe because there was only one way in. Ossian had said nothing at all, which from Ossian was itself a kind of warning.

Xanthe sat between Petra's knees while she worked oil into the leather of her sword-belt, and for a little while the world was small and warm and made only of hands and firelight and the smell of tallow. This was the hour Xanthe loved best. Not the walking, not the eating of hard bread crumbled into broth, not even the singing — though Petra had begun to ask for the songs now, patiently, asking Ossian to give her back each line in the living tongue so she could hold them like river stones in her pockets.

It was during this hour that the ember stirred.

Xanthe felt it the way one feels a second heartbeat waking beneath the first. It turned over in the hollow place behind their ribs — the place that had been empty for so long they had believed emptiness was simply what a chest was — and it oriented itself, the way a compass needle orients, toward something far below.

Then it flared.

The firelight bent. Every drop of brine on the ceiling shone at once, as if lit from within, and the single entrance to the chamber filled with a figure that had not been there a heartbeat before.

* * *

He was tall the way a pillar is tall, and he wore robes the color of old snow, and his face —

His face was kind. That was the worst of it, though Xanthe would not understand why until much later. The herald's face was the face of someone who has come to help you. His eyes were pale as the inside of an eggshell, and he did not walk so much as arrive, each step placing him closer without ever seeming to cross the distance between.

Ferro was on his feet with a knife out before anyone else moved. Petra was slower, and that slowness frightened Xanthe more than the herald did, because Petra was never slow; but her hand found Xanthe's shoulder and settled there, heavy and certain, and stayed.

"Peace," the herald said, in the living tongue, and his voice was smooth as water finding its level. "I am not permitted violence here. I am Vessel-Bearer of the Third Antechamber, Herald to the Court Beneath Thornfell, and I come only to speak."

"You're speaking," Petra said. Her voice was flat as a blade laid on stone. "So speak."

But it was not the herald's words that reached Xanthe. It was what came beneath them — because when the herald spoke the common tongue of the surface, something underneath it leaked through, the true speech running under the false one like a vein of ore under soil, and Xanthe heard both at once, and the two did not say the same things.

In the common tongue, the herald said: *I have come with generous terms.*

Beneath it, in the dead tongue, in the voice of the court itself, he said: ***Little vessel. Little stolen thing. We know where you sleep.***

Xanthe's hand found Petra's wrist and gripped it.

Ossian had risen too, and he stood with his broken god-symbol clutched in both fists like a man holding a shield he no longer believed in. "Translate for us, child," he murmured, barely a breath. "Whatever he says. Whatever you hear. Tell us everything."

And so Xanthe did, in the halting surface-words Ossian had spent weeks teaching them, while the herald watched with his kind, terrible face and let them prepare, because courtesy cost him nothing.

"He says," Xanthe began, and their own voice sounded strange to them, thin in the wet air, "he is a speaker-for. He says the court knows we are here. He says—" and here the two voices diverged so sharply that Xanthe stopped, confused, and Ossian leaned forward and said, gently, "Both voices, child. Give us both."

"The loud voice says the court offers mercy." Xanthe swallowed. "The quiet voice says the court already counted our friends' souls on its ledger. It says they are already written down as *collateral received*."

Petra's hand tightened on Xanthe's shoulder. Ferro swore softly, beautifully, in three languages.

"Continue," Ossian said.

* * *

The terms were these, and the herald laid them out like a merchant laying out cloth, each one smoother than the last:

The child would return. Willingly. Unmarked. Walk of their own accord down the last three galleries to the Court Beneath, and present themselves at the threshold before the third turning of the tide-clock. In exchange: the companions would live. All debts held against them — Ferro's marked soul, Ossian's apostasy, even the old uncollected entries gathering dust in the great ledger — would be struck through with salt and sealed. They would walk out of the mountain into daylight owing nothing to anyone.

"And if the child refuses?" Petra asked.

The herald turned his eggshell eyes to her, and for the first time his kindness slipped, just slightly, the way ice slips on a pond in spring — not breaking, only showing you the black water under it.

"Then the court collects what it is owed," he said, "in whatever order pleases it."

Xanthe translated this, because Ossian asked, and because some part of them understood that the translating mattered — that Petra wanted to hear it from Xanthe's mouth, wanted no room left for the herald's beautiful lies to pool undisturbed.

But there was more. There was the part the herald saved for last, the part he spoke directly into Xanthe's chest rather than into the air, the part that bypassed Ossian's translations entirely because it was addressed to the ember.

***You are leaking, little vessel. You are coming apart at the seams. Every day above ground you fray a little more. We can make you whole. We can stop the unraveling. Come home and be mended.***

And oh, that was a knife with honey on it, because it was true. Xanthe had felt the fraying for weeks — days when their hands went translucent at the edges, when they forgot the taste of things mid-chew, when whole minutes fell out of their memory like stitches from a worn seam. They had hidden it. They had been so careful. But the ember knew, and the ember had told the court, because the ember was honest in the way that fires are honest: it burned what it was given.

Xanthe looked down at their own hands in the firelight.

They almost went.

Understand this, whoever comes after, whoever writes the true history of the salt courts: the child did not hesitate out of fear, nor out of loyalty, nor even out of love. The child hesitated because duty had been poured into them before they could speak, thick as grout, load-bearing, structural. They had been sold with a purpose and the purpose lived in them deeper than their name. To refuse the herald was not courage. It was amputation. And Xanthe stood there in the weeping gallery feeling the blade of it against everything they were, and their foot actually moved — half a step, one grain of salt crunching under bare sole, toward the dark mouth of the corridor and the court that waited patient as geology at the bottom of the world.

"No."

Petra's voice. One word, and it landed in the chamber like a dropped anvil.

She stepped forward, past Xanthe, putting herself bodily between the child and the herald, and Xanthe saw her face and did not recognize it. It was not the mercenary's face, the flat professional mask she wore for strangers and employers. It was older than that. It was a face from somewhere deep in her, from a room in her that had been locked since childhood, and it was furious.

"No," she said again, quieter, which was worse. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to dress up a hostage exchange in mercy's clothes and call it a bargain."

"Honored debtor," the herald began, with a small bow, "the terms are—"

"I know what the terms are. I've read contracts before. I've written them." She took another step. "Here's mine. You tell your king — your Underking, your Majesty of the Deep Places, whatever he likes to be called by his clerks — you tell him the merchandise is damaged. Tell him the vessel cracked in transit. Tell him whatever gets you through the door. But hear me, herald, and carry it back word for word: *she doesn't go.*"

"*She*," Xanthe whispered, and did not know why the pronoun landed like a bell strike. Perhaps because no one had ever claimed them so specifically before. Not *the asset*. Not *the tithe*. *She*.

"The debts against my companions stand or fall with hers," Petra said. "You bundled them together. Fine. Then they stay together. Nobody buys safety with a child's freedom in this company. Not tonight, not for any ledger you care to wave at us."

The herald regarded her for a long moment. And then, horribly, he smiled — a real smile, warm, almost fond — and said, in the dead tongue, knowing full well only two people in the chamber could understand him:

***Sela. You always were stubborn. You get it from the mountain.***

Xanthe froze. The name hit the air like a stone into still water.

Petra didn't move. But Xanthe, pressed close behind her, felt it go through her body like a current through a wire — a full-body flinch mastered in half a heartbeat, the flinch of someone hearing a name they buried decades ago spoken aloud in a voice that should not know it.

"You know nothing about me," Petra said evenly, in the common tongue.

"I know everything about everyone in this mountain," the herald said pleasantly. "That is what a ledger *is*, honored debtor. It remembers so you don't have to." He spread his hands, snow-colored sleeves falling back from wrists marked with fine gray script. "The offer stands until the third turning. After that, the court's patience becomes the court's process, and I promise you, the process is less gentle than I am."

He turned to leave. And then he paused, and looked back — not at Petra, not at Ferro or Ossian, but at Xanthe, direct, level, and in the dead tongue he gave them one last gift, or one last wound, it was impossible to tell which:

***Ask her what they called her. Ask her what she was sold for. Ask her why her god stopped answering. We did not take everything from you, little vessel. Some of it she took from herself.***

Then the dark took him, the way water takes a dropped coin, and he was gone, and the brine-drops on the ceiling went dark one by one, topmost first, like eyes closing.

* * *

No one slept after that.

Ferro checked the corridor four times and came back shaking his head each time. "No tracks. No draft. Nothing. Man walked into solid rock or I'm a milkmaid." He spat. "I hate this mountain. No offense meant to anyone born in it."

"Some taken," Petra said, and her voice was wrong — carefully controlled, sanded smooth over something raw.

Ossian built the fire up and sat hunched before it, muttering, transcribing. Xanthe could see him working the herald's words over in his mouth like a man testing a coin with his teeth. Finally he looked up. "Child. What he said at the end. Before he left. Would you give it to me again? All of it?"

Xanthe looked at Petra.

Petra was standing at the chamber's edge with her back to all of them, arms crossed, looking into the corridor-dark as if she could out-stare it. Her shoulders were a fortress. Everything about her said *do not ask*.

"Tell him," she said finally, without turning around. "He'll worm it out of you anyway. Better he hears it straight."

So Xanthe gave it: the name, Sela. The questions. *What she was sold for. Why her god stopped answering.* And the last line, the one that had lodged under Xanthe's skin like a splinter since the moment it was spoken, the one they had been carrying around inside them for an hour like a hot coal cupped in bare hands:

*"Some of it she took from herself."*

Silence, except for the drip of the ceiling.

"It's a lie," Ferro said flatly. "It's what they do. Split us apart with truths bent sideways. I've seen it done to crews a hundred times."

"It's not bent," Ossian said quietly. He had gone very still, his transcription forgotten. "Petra. When Brother Halvard and I were translating the child's maps — the tunnel under your house, the shrine-gallery, the old tithe-road — you knew those turns before she drew them. I told myself it was a smuggler's instinct. But smugglers don't know where the *tithe-road* goes. Only two kinds of people know that road." He looked at her across the fire, and his old ruined voice was gentle as ash coming down. "The collectors. And the collected."

Petra still hadn't turned around.

"Your god went silent a hundred days ago," Ossian continued. "Mine went silent thirty years ago, and I nearly died of it, and I have spent thirty years learning every shape silence takes. Yours isn't abandonment. I'd stake my last heresy on it. Abandonment has a texture. What you carry is different. What you carry is *amputation*."

"Ferro," Petra said. "Check the corridor again."

"There's nothing—"

"Check it anyway."

He went. The sound of his boots faded down the gallery, and then it was just the fire, and the dripping salt, and the three of them, and the enormous unsaid thing sitting in the middle of the chamber like a fourth traveler who had been there all along.

Xanthe got up. Their legs carried them before their fear could vote, across the cold stone, around the fire, until they stood beside Petra at the mouth of the corridor, close enough to feel the banked heat of her, close enough to see that her knuckles were white where her arms crossed.

"You can ask me," Xanthe said. Their voice came out steadier than they expected. "What I am. Where I came from. What they did to me. I won't break."

Something moved through Petra's face then — fast, quickly suppressed, but Xanthe caught it, and it was not hardness. It was grief with nowhere to put itself.

"You're six years old," Petra said, very quietly, "or you were, once, according to somebody's arithmetic. Nobody should have to be brave enough to offer that." She exhaled slowly through her nose. "When I'm ready. When we're out. I'll tell you everything I remember, and if I don't remember, I'll tell you that too, and you can decide what I'm worth."

"That's not how ledgers work," Xanthe said, because it was the closest thing to a joke the dead tongue had taught them, and it startled a sound out of Petra that might, in better light, have been half a laugh.

Behind them, Ossian spoke again, and his voice had changed — tightened, urgent, the scholar overriding the pastor. "Petra. The third turning of the tide-clock. Do you understand what he offered? A deadline means the court believes the matter is *negotiable*. Courts don't negotiate what they're certain of. Something in that contract frightens them. Something about the child, or—" he hesitated, and his eyes flicked, briefly, to Petra, "—or about you. Which means we have time, but not much, and it means the next gallery they choose for us won't be a parlor. It will be a trap with teeth."

Petra turned from the corridor at last. The fury had settled in her now, packed down dense, the way snow packs into glacier. She looked at Xanthe, and then at Ossian, and Xanthe watched her make the decision the way one watches weather turn.

"Then we don't wait for the third turning," she said. "We move now, tonight, and we take the road the herald doesn't think we know." Her jaw set. "Xanthe. How do you feel about singing?"

Xanthe blinked. "I — it depends on the song."

"Good." Petra was already kicking dirt over the fire, already moving, already becoming motion. "Because there's an ember in you that used to answer my prayers, and I intend to walk right up to whatever's left of it and ask it a few questions myself. Wake Ferro. Get your boots. The mountain's been listening long enough."

Far below them, so far down that no ear of flesh could have caught it, something vast turned over in its sleep of salt and law — and began, with infinite patience, to count.
