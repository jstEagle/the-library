## 12. Betrayal at the Weeping Stair

Ferro had carried his mark for eleven years, and for eleven years he had told himself it was only a scar.

It sat below his left collarbone, a coin-sized disc of skin gone smooth and salt-white, cool to the touch even in summer, even in the worst fevers. He'd won it honestly, or as honestly as Ferro Vask ever won anything—cards, dice, a rigged scale in a port town whose name he'd drunk away long ago. The man he'd cheated had been a courier of the Undercourt, though Ferro hadn't known it then. The man had smiled with too many teeth and said, *We'll call it a loan.* And when Ferro woke three days later with no memory of those days and a white mark on his chest that never warmed, he understood that somewhere beneath the world, a ledger had gained a line with his name on it.

He'd stopped thinking about it the way you stop thinking about a stone in your boot: not because it stops hurting, but because you learn to walk crooked.

Now he walked crooked through the deep roads of Graywater Delve, third in line behind Xanthe's small sure footsteps, and the mark was singing.

Not aloud. It never made sound. But it hummed against his ribs like a struck tuning fork pressed to bone, and it had been humming since they passed the first gate, and tonight, camped in a gallery where the walls wept brine down veins of black stone, it was humming so hard he could taste copper.

*They know where you are,* the mark seemed to say. *They have always known.*

Ferro sat apart from the fire, sharpening a knife he didn't need sharpened, and watched the company the way he hadn't let himself watch them before.

Petra sat closest to the flames, cleaning her sword with the mechanical patience of a woman who trusted her hands more than her thoughts. She'd been strange since the shrine-chamber—the one where they'd found her god's altar desecrated, the salt scraped hollow, the oath-mark on her wrist burned black as a brand. She hadn't spoken of it. She just moved differently now, quieter, like someone walking on a frozen lake who has heard the ice speak.

Xanthe slept curled against Ossian's pack, small fingers hooked into the priest's sleeve even in sleep, as if letting go might cost them something. The child still flickered—that was the only word for it. Some nights, by firelight, Ferro would look over and see straight through the place where Xanthe should be, a half-second of empty air and guttering flame, before the child solidified again like a thought remembered. It turned his stomach. Not fear. Something worse. Pity with teeth.

Marrow-Vane stood the far watch, upright and courteous even alone, the way a man stands when he has nothing left but manners. The debt-collector. The soul-taker. Ferro hated him on principle, which was convenient, because hating Marrow-Vane meant he didn't have to think about what he himself was about to do.

Because he knew what the mark wanted.

He'd known since the flooded gallery. Since the drowned children had risen out of the black water like candle-smoke given shape, and one of them had looked at Petra Montrose—a woman made entirely of edges and iron—and called her *Sela* in a voice like water finding cracks. Since then the humming had grown words.

*The child is an escaped asset,* the mark said, in the voice of the smiling courier, in the voice of every creditor who had ever owned a piece of Ferro Vask. *You are harboring stolen goods. You are complicit. But you may settle.*

*Settle how?*

*The route. Only the route.*

That was the bargain they'd been whispering into his marrow for two days. Not the child. Not the priest, not the apostate, not even the woman with the dead god. Just the road. Which stair, which gallery, which hour. The court would do the rest, and Ferro's line in the ledger would be struck clean, and he would walk out of the mountain owing nothing to anyone, which was the only kind of free Ferro had ever believed in.

He told himself he wasn't going to do it.

He was very good at telling himself things.

* * *

The Weeping Stair came at dawn, or what passed for dawn down here—a hour when the brine-veins in the walls ran faster and the dark took on a gray, underwater quality, as if the whole mountain were holding its breath beneath a sea that wasn't there.

Xanthe stopped at the head of the stairs and went rigid.

The child had been navigating them for days now, singing snatches of the dead tongue that made Ferro's fillings ache, touching the walls like a blind man reading faces. But at the Stair, Xanthe simply stood, eyes gone wide and yellow-bright, and said—in the common tongue, which somehow made it worse—"It knows we're coming."

"Of course it knows," Marrow-Vane said mildly. "The court has known our position since the second gate. I assumed everyone understood this."

"We're not talking about knowing," Petra said. Her hand was already on her sword. "We're talking about *expecting*. There's a difference." She looked at Xanthe. "How many?"

Xanthe held up small hands. Uncertain. Then all ten fingers, twice, and then kept going past counting, and Ferro felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

"That's not an ambush," Ossian said quietly. "That's an army."

"No," Petra said. "An army marches. This will be a funnel. They'll want us tired, split, in the narrow." She was already moving, reading the gallery the way she read everything—like a fight that hadn't happened yet. "We go fast and loud through the Stair, no stopping, no formation. If they want a choke point, we don't give them time to use it. Ferro—you're lightest. Rear guard. Anything follows us onto the steps, you cut the rope bridge behind us and buy us the gallery beyond."

"Aye," Ferro said. His voice came out steady. That surprised him. Inside, the mark was roaring now, a white-hot hymn, and underneath the roar a single clear thought, cold as well-water:

*Rear guard. Alone. Last one off the bridge.*

*This is the moment. This is the moment they gave you.*

He almost laughed. Eleven years of carrying the thing, and in the end the court hadn't needed to threaten him, hadn't needed to show up at an inn with a polite smile and a coin. They'd just waited for the geometry of trust to put him exactly where they wanted, alone, at the back, holding the rope.

Petra caught his eye as the company formed up. For a half-second—longer than she'd ever allowed herself—something human moved behind all that iron.

"You've been good on this road," she said. Gruff. Almost embarrassed. "Better than I expected."

*Don't say things like that,* Ferro thought wildly. *I'm about to feed you to the mountain.*

"Just don't die on my bridge," he said.

She snorted and turned, and the company started down the Weeping Stair.

* * *

It was called the Weeping Stair because the walls cried. Brine ran down the stone in slow silver threads, gathering at each step's lip and dripping into a darkness below that swallowed the sound without echo, as if the stair descended into wool. A thousand steps, maybe more, spiraling down a shaft wide enough for four abreast. Rope bridges crossed the shaft at intervals—smuggler's engineering, decades old, still sound. Ferro counted them as he went, rear guard, knife loose in his hand, and hated himself for counting.

Three galleries down, the dark changed.

Ferro had spent his life in the dark. He knew its textures: cave-dark, cellar-dark, storm-at-sea dark. This was none of these. This was *occupied*. The darkness ahead of them thickened, and in it, shapes assembled—not walking into view, but *condensing*, the way frost condenses on glass. Figures of translucent salt, jointed wrong, faceless, each one trailing a fine mist of dissolved mineral like breath in winter.

Salt-formed soldiers. Dozens. Standing silent on the steps below, filling the shaft like sediment filling a glass of water.

"Hold," Petra breathed—and the company froze on the stair, and for one long moment nobody moved and the only sound was the weeping of the walls.

Then Xanthe screamed.

Not a child's scream. A sound in the dead tongue, high and layered, one voice pitched into many, and the nearest soldiers shuddered like blown sand. But there were too many, and they began to climb—not hurrying, that was the horror of it, climbing with the patient inevitability of tide—and Petra roared *"GO!"* and the company broke downward, swords out, Ossian swinging a lantern in one fist and an old pilgrim's staff in the other, Marrow-Vane moving through the salt-shapes like a man wading through curtain-beads, murmuring legal phrases that made the things flinch.

And Ferro turned back to the bridge.

The rope bridge at the fourth gallery crossing. His job. Cut it, drop it, seal the way behind. He reached it, boots skidding on wet stone, and put his knife to the anchor-line, and stopped.

Below him, across the shaft, he could see the fight spilling along the lower galleries—lantern-light, steel-flash, Xanthe's impossible voice rising and rising. He could see Petra carving a path toward the far tunnel mouth. He could see that they were going to make it. Barely. Badly. But they were going to make it, because Petra Montrose did not know how to lose to geography, and because the salt-things were slow, and because—

*Because you are the last piece,* the mark sang. *Cut the rope, and run the other way. Down. To the court. Give them the far tunnel. Give them the route to the throne vault. And be free.*

His knife bit into the rope. Fibers parted. One strand. Two.

He thought of the flooded gallery. Of the drowned girl who had called Petra *Sela*—who had known her, loved her, some ghost-child from a village that sold its six-year-olds to the dark and called it custom. He thought of how Petra had stood at the water's edge and not run, not even then, when her own name rose out of the black like a hook.

He thought of Xanthe asleep against Ossian's sleeve, fingers hooked tight, letting go costing something.

Three strands. Four.

*I'm sorry,* he told them, all of them, the whole ragged company of people who had never once asked him who he owed or why. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I've been theirs longer than I've been yours, and I don't remember how to be anything else—*

The rope parted.

The bridge dropped, and Ferro went with it—not cut loose but *stepping off*, choosing it, falling the twelve feet to the lower landing and rolling, coming up running, running *down*, toward the dark and the salt and the white-hot peace of a paid debt.

Behind him he heard Petra shout his name. Once. Just once. Not a curse. Worse than a curse—an appeal, as if she believed there might still be a version of him that would turn around.

There wasn't. Or there was, and it was drowning.

* * *

The ambush broke the company like surf breaks a boat.

He saw it in fragments as he fled: Ossian dragged sideways into a side gallery by three salt-shapes that folded around the old priest like closing hands; Marrow-Vane standing over fallen Xanthe, coat torn, doing something terrible and precise with a quill that had no business being in a fight; Petra cutting a corridor of ruin toward the upper tunnels, screaming orders to people who could no longer hear her.

And Ferro ran down, and down, and the mark on his chest finally, finally went quiet—went warm, even, the first warmth it had ever held—because the debt was settling, the ledger was balancing, the smiling couriers of the world were nodding their many-toothed approval.

He reached the bottom of the Stair, where the dark pooled deepest, and there they were waiting for him: figures of salt, ranks of them, motionless as statuary, and among them a shape that was not salt at all. Tall. Courteous. Familiar.

The courier from the port town, eleven years younger and not aged a day, smiling with too many teeth.

"Brother Vask," it said warmly. "Your account is settled. Welcome home."

Ferro opened his mouth to say something—anything, a joke, a curse, the apology that had been chasing him down five hundred steps—and found he couldn't remember what apology was.

Above, faint through the weeping stone, he heard the company scatter—footsteps splitting, voices lost, one small voice crying out in the dead tongue and then cut short.

The courier tilted its head, listening, savoring.

"And now," it said, "the rest."
