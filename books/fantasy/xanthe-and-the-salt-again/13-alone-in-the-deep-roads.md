## 13. Alone in the Deep Roads

The ambush at the Weeping Stair had scattered them like a handful of thrown dice, and Petra did not know yet which faces had come up.

She knew Xanthe's hand in hers — small, cold, gripping with a strength that seemed too much for those fingers. She knew the dark pressing close, and the sound of salt-soldiers dissolving somewhere behind them, their forms slumping back into brine and crystal when the pursuit lost its thread of her. And she knew that Ferro's voice had been the last thing she heard before the stair collapsed: not a warning, not an apology. Just her name, torn off mid-shout, as if he'd been reaching for it even as he fell away from them.

That was either a mercy or a cruelty. She hadn't decided.

"Petra." Xanthe said it in Trade-tongue, careful, the way the child handled all surface words — like coins of uncertain minting. Then, softer, in the dead language: something long and liquid that Petra's ear caught at the edge of meaning without ever quite landing on it.

"I don't know where Ossian is," Petra said. "Or Marrow-Vane. Or anyone."

Xanthe squeezed her hand. In the child's grammar of gestures — learned fast, from watching her — that meant: *it doesn't matter yet.* Or possibly: *stop talking and listen.* With Xanthe the two were often the same instruction.

So Petra listened.

And that was when she understood, for the first time fully and with her whole body, that something was wrong with her. Or right with her. The distinction had stopped mattering somewhere above.

Because she could *hear* the mountain.

Not the drip of water, not the settling groans of stone — those any smuggler with twenty years in the Delve knew how to read. This was other. It was as if the dark had a grain to it, a weave, and she could feel the threads running under her boots. Passage going left, choked with old rubble but passable if you crawled. Passage going right, open, breathing faintly with air that tasted of deep water. A shaft ahead — she hadn't seen it, her lantern barely reached ten feet, but she knew it was there the way you know a stair is under your foot in the dark of your own house.

"Sela."

The name arrived in her own mind with no voice attached. Her childhood name. The drowned girl at the flooded gallery had called her that, days ago now — or hours; time ran strange down here — and Petra had shoved the moment away like a hand through cobweb. But the deep roads were patient, and they had been saying it to her ever since, quietly, in the creak of salt formations and the hush between drips. *Sela. Sela. You came back.*

"I'm not Sela," she whispered. "Sela died at six years old. I'm what grew in her place."

The dark didn't argue. The dark just waited, the way family waits.

* * *

They walked for what she guessed was half a day, though hunger told her nothing useful and her lantern burned on oil that should have run out twice over. She noticed that too, eventually, and chose not to examine it. Down here, examination felt like pulling a thread that held up more than cloth.

Xanthe moved beside her with the easy certainty of a creature returned to its element. The child's fear had drained away with every fathom of descent, replaced by something that frightened Petra more: recognition. Xanthe looked at the walls the way other children looked at mothers.

But it was Petra who led. That was the impossible arithmetic of it. The tithe-child, born and sold beneath the mountain, walked in the wake of the mercenary who'd spent thirty years pretending the mountain had never held her.

At a junction where three galleries met, Petra stopped without knowing why, then knelt and pressed her palm flat against the floor.

Salt. Not the crusty rime of seepage — this was worked salt, fused smooth, and under her hand she felt the faint warmth of a warding laid so long ago it had gone shy of its own power. A boundary-stone. She'd known it was there before she touched it. She'd known since the last turning of the passage, since—

Since always. Since Graywater. Since she'd stood at the mouth of the Delve and felt the mountain say *hello* through the soles of her boots while Ferro complained about his pack straps.

"Oh," she said aloud. Very small, very quiet, the way you speak when the truth is a sleeping animal you don't want to startle. "Oh, you absolute fool, Petra."

"What?" Xanthe asked, sharp with alarm, because Petra's voice had cracked.

"Nothing. Nothing, little one." She stood. Her knees ached. Ordinary knees. She held onto that. "I used to live here."

Xanthe studied her with those yellow eyes, unblinking, and then nodded once — the grave, formal nod of someone receiving confirmation of a thing long suspected. Then the child pointed down the middle gallery and spoke a word in the dead tongue, a word Ossian had translated for them around a smuggler's fire weeks ago, back when the road down still felt like a choice:

*Home.*

"No," Petra said. "We're not going home. We're going to find the others, and we're going to get you out, and I'm going to burn whatever ledger has your name in it."

Xanthe's expression didn't change. It was the look of a child listening to an adult explain why the rain will stop if we all just believe hard enough.

* * *

They slept — or Petra slept, in snatches, propped against a wall of veined white crystal that hummed almost inaudibly, like a struck bell remembering its note. Each time she woke, she knew exactly where she was. Not approximately, the way a good tracker knows — precisely, the way you know your own hand in the dark. She woke once with a route already laid out behind her eyes: two miles east, the gallery of the Long Throat, then down through the Chimneys to the antechamber levels where Ossian had said the court kept its records. She hadn't chosen that route. It had simply been *there*, folded up in her like a letter she'd never opened.

In the morning-that-was-maybe-afternoon, they ate the last of the waybread, and Petra made herself do the thing she'd been avoiding since the stair fell: she took stock of herself honestly, the way she'd take stock of a wounded soldier.

Facts. One: her god, Vessel-of-Salt, had gone silent a hundred days ago, and she'd told herself it was abandonment, silence, death — the ordinary griefs of faith. Two: the drowned children knew her face. Three: she navigated these roads like a woman walking her own hallway at night. Four: Brother Ossian had found, in the fragments of Xanthe's speech, references to a tithe taken *and a tithe owed*, and when he'd read the second phrase aloud, Petra's oath-mark — the salt-white scar across her collarbone, given her at her swearing-in decades ago — had ached like a tooth.

Five. Five was the fact she turned over last, because it was the one with teeth.

Her village, Thornfell, had kept customs. The salt left on doorsteps at the turn of winter. The first portion of every harvest poured into the well-shaft. The naming-day ritual where infants were carried to the cave mouth and shown to the dark, and the dark — the elders said — *remembered*. Everyone had called them superstitions, quaint and fading. Nobody had called them payments.

Nobody except the families who'd stopped paying. Petra couldn't remember any of those families. Funny, that. Thirty years of memory and the space where they should have been was smooth as fused salt.

"Sela was collected," she said to the dark, testing it. "Weren't they. Six years old. Sold or taken or traded — it doesn't matter which word you use, does it? Only the ledger cares about the difference."

The crystal wall hummed. Somewhere far off, water found water.

"And then someone brought me back." Her voice went flat. "Back up, out, with no memory and no explanation, to a village that raised me like a foundling and never said why. Someone paid my ransom. Or someone broke a contract. Or—" and here the thought turned cold and bright, like a knife catching lamplight, "—someone *swapped* me. Put something else on the altar in my place."

Xanthe was watching her. The child had heard none of it — Petra had kept her voice below hearing, an old mercenary habit — but the yellow eyes tracked her anyway, and Xanthe pressed one small hand flat against Petra's sternum, directly over the place where the ember of a devoured god was said to sleep.

The touch was gentle. The ache it opened was not.

* * *

They found the first body an hour later, if body was the word.

It sat cross-legged at the side of the gallery, facing the wall they'd been traveling along, and it was made of salt the way statues are made of bronze — formed, worn, human-shaped but no longer human. Its features had softened into suggestion. Its hands rested open on its knees, palms up, in the posture Ossian called *the accounting pose*: here is everything I have; weigh it.

Petra had seen corpses in every state the world offered. She looked at this one and felt no horror. That absence frightened her more than horror would have. Because some part of her, some deep-buried stratum, looked at the salt figure and thought, with terrible mildness: *that's how they sit when they're finished waiting.*

"Was it waiting for me?" she asked, and hated how easily the question came, how naturally she addressed the dead of this place.

Xanthe crouched by the figure and touched its shoulder, and murmured a few words of the dead tongue — soft ones, Petra judged, from their shape. A greeting. An apology for trespass. Then the child rose and pointed onward, and tugged Petra's sleeve, and they went on.

More figures followed. One standing, one kneeling, one lying curled as if asleep. All salt. All facing the direction of travel — *her* direction of travel, she realized, and the knowledge settled into her stomach like swallowed riverstone. They lined the road the way lanterns line a festival street. As if something had arranged them. As if something wanted her to understand that the way home had been lit for her.

"They're not soldiers," Petra murmured. "Marrow-Vane said the court makes soldiers out of brine and obedience. These aren't that. These are—"

*Tithes.* The word surfaced on its own, in the dead tongue, in a voice that was hers and older than hers. Tithe-children who had been collected and carried down and kept, until the keeping wore them into this. Until the mountain forgot to remember them, and they became part of its furniture.

*Sela,* the road whispered. *You came back. You came back. Look what became of us.*

"I'm looking," Petra said, and her eyes stung, and she let them. "I'm looking, and I'm counting, and when I get to wherever your king sits on his throne of contracts, I'm going to read him a bill of my own."

The salt figures did not answer. But as she passed the kneeling one, closest to the wall, she saw — or dreamed she saw, and would spend years deciding which — that its softened face had been worn into something like her own.

* * *

The gallery narrowed, then opened, and Petra smelled water before she saw it: clean water, snowmelt water, water that had never known a court's claim. A side-channel fed a thin waterfall down a seam in the rock, pooling in a basin no wider than a cartwheel, spilling over into cracks and vanishing. Surface water. It had found a way down, the way roots find a way down, the way anything alive finds a way down if it tries long enough.

Petra knelt and drank, and washed her face, and filled the waterskin, and for a few minutes allowed herself the luxury of feeling only tired.

Xanthe played at the pool's edge. Actually played — the first time Petra had seen it. The child trailed fingers in the water, flicked droplets at the wall, watched them catch the lantern light. Hummed a scrap of the dead tongue that wasn't a ward-song or a mourning-song or any of the categories Ossian had patiently built. Just a tune. A child's tune, the kind made up on the spot and forgotten by nightfall.

Something in Petra's chest went loose and warm and terrified all at once.

"You know I'm taking you up," she said. "Out. Whatever it costs."

Xanthe glanced back, droplets still on the child's chin, and smiled — the rare full smile, the one that showed the gap where a baby tooth had surrendered. Then Xanthe said something in the dead tongue, three words, and pointed at Petra's chest, then at the child's own chest, then joined the hands together.

Ossian's translation came to her unbidden, because the apostate priest had taught them both the word during the long walk down, never guessing — or perhaps guessing exactly — how soon it would be needed.

*Same fire. Us.*

"That's not how debts work, little one," Petra said gently. "Debts follow blood. Debts follow signatures. Debts don't care about—"

She stopped.

Because the mountain had answered her. Not in words. In *knowledge* — sudden, total, arriving like a lantern lit in a sealed room: the memory of a ceremony she had no business remembering, performed in a chamber she had no business knowing, in which a woman with her own face and a younger voice had held a squalling infant toward a pillar of white fire and spoken the words of the dead tongue, and the pillar had bent toward the child like wheat toward sun, and the woman had wept and said, *same fire, us, same fire.*

Her mother. Her mother had sworn something. Over her. Into her.

The tithe hadn't just been a transaction. There had been a counter-seal. A rival claim, written small, written in love, written by someone who knew exactly what she was doing — a hedge against the ledger, buried so deep in Petra's soul that thirty years of mercenary life had never disturbed it.

*Never collected,* Ossian had read from the ledger, pale as paper himself. *Sold at age six. Never collected.* He'd assumed bureaucratic neglect. Bureaucratic evil, sleeping on its books.

It wasn't neglect. Something had been holding the debt closed from the inside. Something her mother had put there. And whatever it was, it lived in her still — in the way the roads knew her, in the way the salt sat quiet around her, in the way the dark said *welcome home* instead of *prey*.

Petra sat back on her heels at the edge of the little pool, water dripping from her fingers, and laughed. It came out ragged, halfway to sobbing, the laugh of a woman whose map of herself has just been redrawn by earthquake.

"All this time," she said. "All this time I thought Vessel-of-Salt went silent because I'd failed it. Because I wasn't enough. A hundred days I prayed into a hole and hated myself for the echo." She shook her head. "It wasn't silence. It was a *held breath*. Something down here swallowed my god whole, and something else down here — something my mother planted — has been keeping *me* whole in the meantime. Keeping the account frozen. Keeping Sela's debt unpaid for thirty years."

Xanthe had gone still. Utterly still, in the way the child went still when the deep things spoke nearby — head tilted, yellow eyes fixed not on Petra but slightly past her, at the dark end of the gallery.

And the dark end of the gallery was no longer entirely dark.

Light gathered there. Not lantern-light, not the blue corpse-glow of salt formations. This was white, dense, columnar — a pillar of radiance standing at the far end of the passage like a person standing in a doorway, waiting to be acknowledged. Petra's skin prickled. Every hair on her arms lifted. The oath-mark on her collarbone woke from its hundred days of sleep and burned — not black, not the desecrated ache of the shrine-chamber, but *bright*, gold-white, furious, familiar.

A god's attention. She knew it the way you know your own name being called across a crowded market.

But Vessel-of-Salt was dead. Devoured. Ossian had seen the empty altar with his own eyes; Petra had felt the truth of it in her burning mark. Which meant this light was either a fragment, an echo —

— or the ember itself, the piece that lived in Xanthe, answering the summons of the mother-oath that had just woken in Petra's blood.

The light pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat. Like a knock at a door.

Then it began to move toward them, unhurried, and behind it, filling the gallery from wall to wall, came the sound of many footsteps — soft, salt-crusted, patient — and a voice like a ledger opening spoke the dead tongue in a tone of perfect courtesy, and Xanthe translated it in a whisper gone gray with fear:

*"The court acknowledges the counter-claim. Bring the debtor to be weighed."*

Petra rose slowly, putting herself between the light and the child, and discovered that her terror had finished its long transformation somewhere on the road behind her. What remained was calm. What remained was competence. What remained was a daughter of the salt courts, standing at last in her own house, ready to name the price of admission.

"Then lead on," she said, in Trade-tongue, because she refused to give them the courtesy of the old language. "I know the way."
