## 14. The Ledger of Names

They took his hands first.

Ossian had expected worse. When the salt-formed soldiers had poured out of the walls at the Weeping Stair—when Ferro's shout had come a heartbeat too late, and the ambush had closed like a fist—he had thought, briefly and without shame, that they would simply be unmade. That the Undercourt would crack them open like geodes and pour out whatever was worth keeping, and let the husks join the drowned gallery where the tithe-children drifted and sang.

Instead, the soldiers bound his wrists with cord that smelled of brine and old paper, and marched him and Marrow-Vane down corridors that did not behave like corridors. The passages bent when no one was looking. Stairs descended in spirals so long and smooth-worn that Ossian understood, with the particular dread of a man who had spent thirty years reading ruins, that these stairs predated stairs—that some earlier thing had worn this path with its body before anyone thought to cut steps into it.

Marrow-Vane walked beside him, upright, courteous even in captivity, his coat immaculate beneath the salt dust. His wrists were bound too. He did not seem surprised by any of it.

"You're very calm," Ossian said quietly, as the soldiers clattered ahead of them, their armor whispering like sand through an hourglass.

"I am an officer of the court," Marrow-Vane said. "This is not a punishment. This is a processing."

"Is there a difference?"

"There is always a difference," Marrow-Vane said, and for the first time since Ossian had known him, something moved behind his eyes that was not courtesy. It looked almost like fear. "Punishment is what happens to those who matter to someone. Processing is what happens to everyone else."

* * *

The antechamber announced itself by sound before sight: a vast, dry rustling, like wind through winter wheat, like ten thousand pages turning in a room where no hand turned them.

Then the corridor opened, and Ossian stopped walking, and a soldier prodded him forward with a spear of white crystal, and he kept walking because the alternative was to stand forever in the doorway like a pilgrim struck dumb—which, he admitted to himself, was more or less what he was.

The antechamber was a cavern the size of a cathedral's nave, but no cathedral had ever been built to this purpose. Shelves climbed the walls into darkness beyond the reach of any light, and every shelf was packed with ledgers—thousands upon thousands of them, spines of grey vellum and black, their titles embossed in the dead tongue. And above, suspended from the unseen ceiling on chains so fine they might have been spider silk, hung lamps of hollowed salt-crystal, each one burning with a flame the color of pale amber, each flame flickering in rhythm with the great rustling sound.

"The Ledger of Names," Marrow-Vane said. There was something in his voice now that Ossie could only call reverence, and it made his skin crawl. "Every soul ever owed to the court. Every contract, every default, every collection. It is all here. It has always been here."

"How many?" Ossian asked. It was a priest's question, a shepherd's question—the question you asked about a flock.

"More than the living kingdoms have people," Marrow-Vane said. "Most lines are dormant. Some are paid. Some—" he paused, "—are collected."

The soldiers brought them to a long table of black stone in the center of the chamber, its surface scarred with the grooves of centuries of ledger-spines dragged across it. Behind the table sat a figure that Ossian's eyes refused, at first, to resolve: a clerk, or the shape of a clerk, robed in grey, its face a smooth oval of salt-white with two dark hollows where eyes should be. It did not breathe. It did not move except when it moved to write, which it was doing now, endlessly, with a pen that scratched against parchment with a sound that matched, exactly, the great rustling of the room—as though the whole chamber were one body, and the scratching were its heart.

It wrote for another minute. Then it set down its pen, and turned the smooth oval of its face toward them, and spoke in a voice like dry paper folding.

"Assets recovered. Two. One marked." The hollows fixed on Marrow-Vane. "Collector Third Rank Vane-Marrow. Your mark has been noted. You will be held pending review of your defection claim."

"And him?" Marrow-Vane asked.

"The apostate is not an asset." The clerk's voice carried neither malice nor mercy. It was the voice of a rule being recited. "He is residue. Residue may be assigned labor."

Ossian felt his stomach drop, but he lifted his chin. Thirty years ago, they had stripped him of his orders and burned his letters of calling in front of the cathedral doors, and he had survived that. He would survive shelving books.

"What labor?" he asked.

The clerk regarded him for a long moment. Then it rose—it was taller than Ossian had calculated, unfolding upward like a carpenter's ruler—and gestured with one long grey finger toward a trolley stacked with ledgers near the table.

"Re-shelving. The western galleries have fallen behind." A pause. The hollows seemed to consider something. "You read the tongue?"

"A little," Ossian lied. He read it well; that was rather the point of apostasy. You could not leave a faith unless you had learned it thoroughly enough to know it was lying to you.

"Then you will also collate. Misfiled entries must be returned to sequence. The Ledger does not tolerate disorder." The clerk resumed its seat. Its pen began to scratch again. The interview, such as it was, was over.

* * *

They gave him a cart, a lamp, and four hours' work before the first horror found him.

The work itself was simple, almost meditative. Each ledger bore a sigil—a family name, sometimes, or a village mark, or the seal of some defunct kingdom whose debts had been inherited by the mountain the way a house inherits mice—and each sigil corresponded to a shelf-range marked in salt-etched numerals. Ossian carried the ledgers, checked the marks, slid the volumes home. Around him the great rustling continued, and gradually he came to understand its source: the flames in the hanging lamps did not merely flicker. They turned. Every flame, seen closely, resolved into a tiny whirling shape, a curling scroll of fire, and each scroll was inscribed with script too small and too bright to read. The ledgers were copied into the light. Or perhaps the light was copied into the ledgers. He decided, somewhere in the third hour, that the distinction did not matter, and that either answer was damning.

It was in the fourth hour, in a shelf-niche deep in the western galleries where the lamp-light barely reached, that he pulled a slim volume with a cracked spine and a sigil he knew.

He knew it the way you know a face across a crowded market—not the details, but the weight of it. A circle quartered by three strokes. Thornfell's mark. The mark that had been branded, once, on the lintels of every house in the village where Petra Montrose had been born, back when the village still bothered with the old customs, back when the tithe was still paid in the open and called by its true name.

Ossian stood in the dim niche with the ledger in his hands and told himself it was coincidence. Thornfell had been a tithe-village. Of course Thornfell had entries. Half the mountain villages had entries. He opened the book with a scholar's hands, steady, professional, and began to read.

The early pages were routine. Grain-tithes. Firstborn-lambs. Salt rendered in measures. Then, as the decades turned, the entries changed character, and the change was gradual enough to be deniable, the way a fever rises gradually enough to be ignored until the patient is raving. Grain became goods. Goods became coin. And then, in a page dated—by the dead reckoning of the court—some sixty years past, coin became something else entirely.

*Soul-tithe, one unit, tendered.*

*Debtor: household of Aldous and Maren Vess.*

*Consideration received: remission of blight, two seasons.*

And below that, in a different ink—older, dryer, the ink of an original entry rather than an amendment:

*Sela Vess. Age six. Entered under guardian's authority per statute of dependent obligation.*

*Status: UNCOLLECTED.*

Ossian read it three times.

The name meant nothing at first. Then it meant everything, arriving all at once like cold water down the back of the neck. Sela. The ghost-child in the flooded gallery, drifting among the drowned echoes, who had reached toward Petra with small translucent fingers and called her by that name—and Petra had gone white as the salt around them and said, in a voice Ossian had never heard from her before, *I don't know that name,* and had walked faster after that, and had not spoken for an hour.

Sela Vess. Sold at six. Guardian's authority.

He leaned against the shelf and let the ledger rest open on his forearm and did the arithmetic he did not want to do. Sixty years past. A girl of six. A mercenary now in her late thirties, hard-eyed and oath-marked, who prayed out of habit to a god that no longer answered, who flinched—Ossian had seen it, filed it away, said nothing—who flinched at the smell of certain salts. Who remembered roads under the mountain that she claimed not to remember remembering.

She had never been collected. The line sat open in the book like an unpaid bill, accruing interest across a lifetime, and she had built her whole fierce free life on top of it without knowing the floor beneath her was paper.

But it was not the entry itself that finally made Ossian sit down on the cold stone floor of the gallery, the ledger open across his knees. Entries could be grieved. Entries could be cursed. It was what he found when he turned the page.

Because the court had amended the line. Of course it had. The court amended everything; that was its nature, its piety, its religion of record-keeping. And the amendment, entered some forty years after the sale, in the same dry administrative hand as everything else, read:

*Note re: asset Sela Vess (line 4471). Asset escaped collection window via relocation. Reclassified: DORMANT. Interest continues to accrue per statute. Collection deferred to next contact. No action required. No action required. No action required.*

Three times. As though the clerk who wrote it had needed the repetition. As though somewhere behind the smooth oval face and the endless scratching pen, something had hesitated over a child's name and chosen, three times, to look away.

Or—worse—as though the repetition meant nothing at all. As though it were boilerplate. As though somewhere in these endless galleries there were thousands of entries bearing that same tripled phrase, whole shelves of children sold and lost and deferred, lives measured out in accrued interest, and no one had ever hesitated at all.

Ossian did not know which possibility was worse. He suspected he never would. That was the architecture of the place: it offered you two horrors and no way to choose between them, and meanwhile the pen went on scratching, and the flames went on turning, and somewhere above the mountain the sun was rising and setting over villages where parents still left coins on windowsills, telling themselves the old customs were only customs.

He had been a priest of the Radiant Choir for twenty years before he left it. He had renounced his orders over doctrine, over the Church's silence about the border wars, over questions of authority he could still argue fluently at three in the morning. He had believed, walking out through the cathedral doors with his letters burning behind him, that he had seen the worst that organized faith could do: the way it could take love and run it through machinery until what came out the other side was obedience.

He had been wrong. The Church, at least, had lied beautifully. It dressed its cruelty in gold and music and called it grace. This place did not bother. This place had taken the oldest human transaction—*I give so that my family may live*—and entered it in a book, and indexed it, and assigned it a statute number, and defered collection on a six-year-old girl with the same pen stroke it used for grain. No malice. No ceremony. Just a line item, and a status field, and forty years of interest ticking silently against a woman who thought her god had abandoned her.

Bureaucratic evil, Ossian thought, staring up at the turning flames. Not the evil that howls. The evil that files.

* * *

He worked until his lamp guttered low, and then he worked past that, because stopping meant thinking. He collated misfiled volumes with hands that shook only slightly. He found other entries for Thornfell households—whole pages of them—and forced himself to read each one, because someone should read them, because reading them was the only witness this place permitted, and he would be the witness if it killed him.

Near the end of the shift—or what he assumed was a shift; time down here was a rumor—his fingers found a volume he had not shelved, lying flat on the trolley where he had left it: the Thornfell ledger, the one he had been carrying all along, still open to the page.

He looked at Sela Vess's line again. Uncollected. Dormant. Accruing.

And then he saw what exhaustion had blinded him to before—a final notation, fresh ink, wet enough to shine. Someone had amended the entry within the hour. Within the hour of their capture.

*Line 4471 reactivated. Asset located. Collection authorized. Escorted to audience.*

Audience. With the Underking.

They had Petra. She had been separated at the Weeping Stair with Xanthe, and he had let himself hope—praying, God forgive him, to a god he no longer formally acknowledged—that she had gotten clear, that her half-buried instincts had found her a road, that somewhere in the deep she and the child were moving fast toward daylight.

No. The book said otherwise. The book said she was already walking toward the throne vault, escorted, processed, an asset forty years overdue being brought in at last.

Ossian closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. Then he looked up at the nearest shelf, at the hundreds of volumes within reach of his hands, and he did the most dangerous thing he had done since he burned his letters of calling.

He memorized the shelf-marks.

Not all of them—he was old, and tired, and mortal. But the Thornfell ranges. The tithe-village sigils. The statutes cited in Sela Vess's entry. If there was a flaw in this machine—if there was any seam in the great filing system of souls—then it would be a legal flaw, a clerical flaw, a flaw of sequence and authority, and to find it he would need the law itself, every clause, every precedent, every statute number scratched into these salt-white pages.

Marrow-Vane had said the court broke its own law first. Fine. Then the law would be the weapon. And Ossian, apostate, residue, assigned to shelving, would learn that law better than the clerks who served it.

Somewhere deeper in the mountain, guards were marching a woman named Petra—named Sela—to meet the thing that had eaten her god. Ossian pressed his palm flat against the cracked spine of the Thornfell ledger, a blessing in everything but name.

"Hold on," he whispered, in the language of the living kingdoms, where it still meant something. "I'm coming. With paper."

Down the gallery, unhurried, eternal, the pen went on scratching.
