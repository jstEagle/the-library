## 15. Audience with the Underking

They did not drag her so much as *escort* her, which was worse.

Salt-formed soldiers moved with the courtesy of ushers at a funeral—hands at Petra's elbows, firm but never rough, guiding her down corridors of white stone that had been grown rather than carved. She knew that about them without knowing how she knew it. The walls had a grain to them, like the inside of a shell, and where torchlight caught the surface she could see faint striations running in long, patient curves, as if the whole mountain had once been water and had simply decided, one century, to stop moving.

She had walked these halls before. That was the thing she kept refusing to think, walking anyway through the thought like wading upstream.

Behind her came Ossian and Marrow-Vane, bound at the wrists with cords of woven salt that did not cut but *ached*; she could hear it in Ossian's breathing, the way he exhaled between his teeth every few steps. Xanthe they carried separately, or perhaps Xanthe went willingly—Petra had caught only a glimpse of the child being led down a different branch of the corridor by two soldiers who bowed to them, actually *bowed*, and the sight had put a cold hand around her heart and squeezed.

*They bow to the vessel,* she thought. *Not the hostage. The vessel.*

The soldiers' grip was warm. That was another wrongness she catalogued and set aside: things made of salt should have been cold as the sea's leavings, but their hands held the heat of a body, as though someone had poured a person into a mold of salt and let it keep everything except the parts that mattered.

"Keep your eyes forward," Marrow-Vane said quietly behind her. It was the first time he had spoken since the ambush, and his voice had lost its inn-smooth polish. What remained underneath was older and more tired. "Whatever you see on the throne, whatever it says—it will offer you something. It always offers something. That is how it thinks. Everything is an exchange."

"You would know," Petra said.

"I would," he agreed, without flinching, and somehow that honesty was harder to hate than any lie would have been.

The corridor opened.

* * *

The throne room was not a room. It was a wound in the mountain that had healed into grandeur—a vaulted hollow so high its ceiling vanished into darkness, its floor a sheet of black glass that might have been obsidian or might have been water frozen mid-thought. Columns rose from it in ranks, each one carved—or grown—with figures ascending: children, hundreds of children, small hands raised toward a light source Petra couldn't find. Every column bore names at its base, cut deep and worn shallow, generations of footsteps having polished them nearly away.

Xanthe's maps had shown this place. Petra had studied those drawings by candlelight for hours and told herself they were fantasy, nightmare-work, the scribbles of a damaged mind. Standing here now, she understood that Xanthe had been drawing from memory with the fidelity of a surveyor.

And there, at the far end, beneath an arch of fused salt that dripped upward instead of down—

The throne was made of doors. Dozens of them, hundreds, nailed and fused and grown together into a shape that was almost a seat, almost a mouth. Doors of every wood and iron, some rotted to lace, some still bearing hinges, one small blue door no bigger than a cupboard that Petra's eyes snagged on and refused to leave, because she had known a door like that once, in a house above, in a life she had told herself stories about.

On the throne sat the Underking.

He looked, at first glance, like a man who had been buried politely. Tall, gaunt, dressed in robes the color of dried brine, crowned with a circlet of what she recognized with a lurch as teeth—not animal teeth, not quite. He was handsome the way a portrait is handsome, correct in all its lines and utterly without breath behind them. His skin had the grain of the walls. His eyes were the gray of the sea before weather.

But it was the second glance that stopped Petra's heart, and the third that broke something loose in her chest.

Around the Underking's shoulders, draped like a mantle of honor, hung a darkness. Not shadow—shadow was honest, shadow went where light told it. This was a *shape*, a coiled absence the size of a hound, the size of a god, and it was not dead. It was dim. It pulsed, slow as a heart in winter sleep, and where it touched the Underking's throat she saw the faintest flicker of gold-white light bleed through, swallowed again, bleeding through again, a lamp being smothered and relit and smothered in endless patient rhythm.

Petra knew that light.

She had prayed to it for thirty years. She had pressed her palm to her oath-mark every night of her childhood and felt it answer, faint and warm, like a coal cupped in the dark. *Vessel-of-Salt, keeper of the threshold, god of small doors and safe passage.* Her village's god. Her mother's whispered devotions. Her own stubborn, muscle-memory faith that had kept praying into silence for a hundred days because stopping would mean admitting something had happened.

"It's alive," she heard herself say. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; fury does that sometimes, holds the voice level while everything under it burns. "You took it, and it's still *alive*."

"Taken," the Underking said, and his voice was the sound a ledger makes when it closes. "That is a strong word, Sela."

The name hit her like cold water. Not Petra. Not Montrose. *Sela*—the word the drowned echo in the flooded gallery had called out, the word she had spent three days telling herself was coincidence or cruelty or mistake.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"You do." He rose from the throne of doors, and the darkness across his shoulders stirred and settled. "You have known since the gallery. You have known since the coin in the child's fist made your scars ache. You have known since you were six years old and stood where you are standing now, in this same spot, holding your mother's hand, and watched the clerks write your name." He descended the single step from the dais. "You are the only tithe-child in four hundred years who was never collected. Do you imagine I forgot? I forget nothing. Forgetting is a luxury of the living."

"Then take me now," Petra said. "That's why we're here, isn't it? The ledger says my name. Here I am. Let the others walk out."

Something moved across the Underking's face that was almost a smile, and it was terrible precisely because it was almost.

"See how quickly she offers herself," he said—to the room, to the columns, to the ascending children carved in stone. "This is what makes mortals so valuable. They believe sacrifice is currency. They come to me already spending themselves." He turned back, and his gray eyes fixed on her with the patience of geology. "No, Sela-of-Thornfell. I did not bring you down here to collect an old debt. I brought you down here to show you why the old debt no longer matters."

He raised one hand, and the darkness on his shoulders lifted.

* * *

It uncoiled from him slowly, reluctantly, the way a drowning man's grip loosens—and Petra saw it whole for the first time, and her legs nearly went out from under her.

It was a god. Or the husk of one. A form like a hound crossed with a lantern, all edges and ember-light, its body riddled with dark veins where something had fed on it, sip by sip, year by year. Its light guttered as it hovered between them, and in its guttering Petra saw shapes: a doorway in a hillside. Hands pressing salt to a threshold. A woman singing a blessing over a cradle. Her mother's voice. Her own small hands, copying the motions.

Thirty years of prayers rose up in her all at once, every silent dawn and every muttered grace over stolen goods, and they crashed against the truth and shattered on it.

"Vessel," she whispered.

"Vessel-of-Salt," the Underking confirmed. "Sworn to the threshold-keepers of Thornfell. A minor deity, as gods go—but a lawful one, and law is the only food worth eating." He circled it, admiring, the way a miser admires a hoard. "Do you know how a god goes silent, Sela? It does not die of neglect. Neglect merely starves it thin. To make a god truly silent, you must swallow it whole—and even then, the ember resists. It burns. Oh, it burned." His hand drifted near the creature's flank, and the light flinched from him. "Forty years it has burned in my keeping, indigestible, incorruptible. An oath-god cannot be broken. But an oath-god can be *outlived*. Every soul sworn to it dies or drifts, and the ember dims, until one day it is small enough—" his fingers closed on empty air, "—to be hidden somewhere I would never think to look."

The cold hand closed around Petra's heart again.

"No," she said.

"Where does a god hide," the Underking said softly, "but in a believer?"

The doors of the far wall opened, and they brought Xanthe in.

The child walked between two salt soldiers, barefoot on the black glass, and did not struggle. Their eyes found Petra immediately—found her and held on, and in them she saw not fear but apology, a six-year-old's apology, the kind children give for things that were never their fault.

"The parents defaulted," the Underking said, conversational now, a lecturer at the end of a long proof. "Their souls were forfeit. The collector took them—you know this collector, I believe, he stands behind you smelling of guilt. And the child, the collateral, the remainder of the contract—was brought below, as the law requires. Except." He paused, savoring it. "Except that the child's grandmother had done a foolish, faithful thing. Before the tithe was paid, she took the girl—yes, girl, before your court chose otherwise—to a shrine above, and held her hands to the altar-stone, and asked the last living servant of Vessel-of-Salt for a blessing. And the dying god, dim as it was, answered. It looked into the child. And it hid."

"It *chose*," Petra said.

"It fled," said the Underking. "Gods are not brave, Sela. They are persistent. There is a difference, though I understand you surface-dwellers confuse the two." He gestured, and the ember-hound drifted toward Xanthe, and the child stood very still as the light gathered around them, sinking into them, gold rising beneath their skin like sun through closed eyelids. Xanthe's eyes opened wider, and for one moment they blazed white-gold—the color Petra had named them after without ever asking herself why the color had seemed familiar, why it had tugged at her like a half-remembered hymn.

"Now you understand your hundred days of silence," the Underking said. "Your god did not abandon you. Your god was eaten. And what remains of it, the last spark, the seed of it—it has been burning inside this child since before the child could speak. Which means, dear Sela, that your precious Xanthe is two things at once. My property, by contract." His smile returned, thin as a knife's edge. "And my prison. The only cell in creation I cannot open without destroying what I keep inside it. So you see why I have been *patient*. Why I let the child run. Why I sent a courteous man to an inn instead of soldiers to your door. A vessel must be handled gently. A vessel must be *persuaded* to stay."

"And if I refuse?" Petra's voice cracked on it. "If I take them and go?"

"Then I break the vessel," the Underking said simply, "and pour out the ember, and forty years of patience ends in an afternoon. I would prefer not to. Waste offends me. But I am very old, Sela, and very thorough, and I want you to understand—I did not summon you here to threaten the child." He spread his hands, and the throne of doors creaked behind him, and every small blue door in her memory groaned open at once. "I summoned you to make you an offer. You of all people should appreciate it. You, who were sold at six and never collected. You, whose name sits unclaimed in my ledger like a coin left on a table."

Behind her, Ossian made a small wounded sound. Marrow-Vane said nothing at all, and his silence was louder than screaming.

"The ledger line is yours," the Underking said. "It has always been yours. Come, sit beside me. We will discuss terms. You may yet leave this mountain with the child, the priest, and the collector—all debts forgiven, all roads open—if you understand what you will owe instead." His gray eyes glittered. "Everything is an exchange, Sela-of-Thornfell. Even love. *Especially* love. Now—let us itemize."

And in the light pouring off Xanthe, Petra felt the ember turn toward her—slowly, like a great beast waking—she felt it *recognize* her, the way a dog recognizes the hand that fed it, the way an oath recognizes the blood that swore it.

The god she had mourned for a hundred days looked out through a child's eyes and knew her name.

Not Sela. Not Petra.

*Mine*, it said, without words, in the old warm language of thresholds and small doors.

*You came back.*
