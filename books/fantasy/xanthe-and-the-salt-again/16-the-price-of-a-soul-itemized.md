## 16. The Price of a Soul, Itemized

Marrow-Vane had spent forty years learning the grammar of debts, and so he knew, standing in the antechamber of the Salt Court with his hands bound in salt-cord behind his back, that what was about to happen was not mercy. It was an audit. The Underking did not forgive; he restructured.

They had been brought through galleries of white stone veined with grey, past niches where names were carved in columns so tall that reading them top to bottom would take a day. Ossian had gone quiet somewhere around the tenth column, and had not recovered his voice since. Ferro was gone—scattered at the Weeping Stair, taken or fled or worse—and Petra walked between two soldiers of pressed salt with her chin up and her oath-mark showing black through her torn sleeve like a bruise that had opinions.

And Xanthe walked smallest of all, in the center of the escort, as though the child were made of glass and debt in equal measure.

The throne vault opened without doors. One moment there was wall; the next there was vastness—a chamber carved from a single seam of salt so old it had turned translucent, so that light from no visible source came down through it like light through ice. The Underking sat at its far end on a chair that was less a throne than a ledger given posture: a great slab of stone stacked with tablets, each tablet a soul, each soul a line item, and the whole edifice balanced with the terrible precision of something that had never once forgiven a late payment.

Marrow-Vane had stood before him twice in his long career. Both times he had been the instrument. Now he was the entry.

"Kneel," said the herald—the same courteous horror they'd met on the road, its voice like paper folding.

Petra knelt. Marrow-Vane knelt beside her, and found, to his mild surprise, that his knees remembered how.

* * *

"You are Sela of Thornfell," the Underking said.

His voice did not echo. That was the first obscenity of it—in a chamber that size, a voice should have rolled and died and rolled again. His simply arrived, already inside your skull, the way a summons arrives already stamped.

"I am Petra Montrose," she said. "Sela died when you stopped collecting."

"Nothing dies here." The Underking rose. Up close he was worse than the rumors: a figure of grey majesty whose face kept almost resolving into something human and then deciding against it, the way a ledger's totals almost resolve into fairness. "That is the entire principle of my court. I have your original contract, Sela of Thornfell. Sold at six years old by the guardian of record. Never collected. Do you know why it was never collected?"

"I imagine someone lost the paperwork."

"I imagine," the Underking said, with something that in a man might have been humor, "you would like to believe that." He descended one step. The salt beneath his feet chimed faintly, like coins settling. "It was never collected because the collector assigned to your line was diligent enough to notice a flaw. A flaw I have spent three centuries pretending not to see, because the alternative was inconvenient."

Marrow-Vane felt the words land in his chest before his mind caught up. *Diligent.* He knew that word. He had been that word, once, in his first century of service, before diligence curdled into efficiency and efficiency into habit.

"The flaw," the Underking continued, "was jurisdiction. Your guardian signed. Not your parents. And a guardian may pledge property—" his face flickered toward Xanthe, and Marrow-Vane saw Petra's whole body go rigid, "—but not a soul. Souls require blood or consent. Your contract is therefore void. It has always been void. I have enforced it anyway, because enforcement is what courts are *for*, little mercenary, and because the child who carries my stolen ember makes a very persuasive reason to keep the paperwork buried."

"So you admit it," Petra said. Her voice was steady. Marrow-Vane, who had heard ten thousand confessions and could hear a heartbeat through a door, heard the fury under it like a river under ice. "You've known for thirty years that you had no claim on me."

"I have had a claim on everyone," the Underking said, "since before your kingdom learned to bake bread. Claims are not about rightness. They are about records."

He gestured, and the air itself became a page. Marrow-Vane watched it form—salt-light drawing lines in the dark, columns of script assembling themselves with the soft clicking of an abacus the size of a cathedral. And on that page, itemized, in a hand so beautiful it hurt:

*One life, restored.*

*The village of Thornfell as it stood in the year of your leaving. The mother who sold you, unburdened, remembering only that she loved you. The god Vessel-of-Salt, returned to its altar, its silence explained as mystery rather than theft. Thirty years of loneliness, refunded. Every scar reassigned to fiction. You will wake in a bed that smells of woodsmoke and it will be the morning before everything, and you will be six years old, and none of it will have happened yet.*

"In exchange," the Underking said, "you surrender the asset."

The silence afterward had texture. Marrow-Vane had negotiated with dying men, with kings, with a witch in the Graywater marshes who paid in years. He knew every species of silence. This one was the kind that comes after a trap has been sprung but before anyone checks their foot.

Xanthe spoke first. The dead tongue poured out of the child—quick, frightened, and then, horribly, *resigned*, the way a child becomes resigned when the world has taught them the lesson often enough. Ossian translated in a whisper cracked nearly to nothing.

"He says—says it's fair. Says he's been nothing but trouble. Says—" Ossian's voice broke. "Says thank you for the name. Says it was the best thing anyone gave him that wasn't owed."

"No," Petra said.

"Sela—"

"*No.*" She got to her feet, which the salt-soldiers permitted, which meant they had been ordered to permit it, which meant this was all still part of the offer. "You don't get to sell me my own childhood back. I spent thirty years building the person who walked in here, and you're pricing her at the cost of a child. That's not an itemized bill. That's just arithmetic done by a coward."

The Underking regarded her. "Refusal is noted."

"Noted," Marrow-Vane repeated softly. "Yes. Everything is noted with you. Nothing is ever *decided*."

* * *

All eyes turned to him. He had spoken quietly, but the chamber was built for ledgers, and ledgers are built for quiet things said aloud.

"You have something to add, collector?"

"I have testimony." Marrow-Vane rose. The salt-cord cut his wrists and he did not care; some performances require the performer to bleed slightly, and he had always understood theater better than his colleagues gave him credit for. "I request formal audience under the Rite of Contestation. Article Nine of your own covenant. Any officer of the court may challenge the validity of an active contract before collection, and collection must be suspended until the challenge is heard."

"I know the articles," the Underking said. "I wrote them."

"Then you know I'm entitled to speak, and you know that if you refuse me, every contract in this room becomes contestable by precedent. Three centuries of clean procedure, undone by one impatient king. You didn't build this court on power, sir. Power is cheap—you buy it by the pound. You built it on the idea that here, at last, in the deep places, the rules hold even when nothing else does. Break that, and you're just another tyrant in a hole in the ground. And tyrants," Marrow-Vane added gently, "get overthrown by children with embers in their chests. I've read the histories. They're mostly about that."

For a long moment the only sound was the faint chime of salt settling, somewhere far above, like a house thinking.

"Speak," the Underking said.

Marrow-Vane turned—not to the king, but to the chamber itself, to the watching columns of names, to whatever passed for witnesses here. He had rehearsed this in his head since the Weeping Stair, since the moment the ambush scattered them and he had lain in the dark listening to salt-soldiers march past and understood that he had exactly one move left on the board.

He had been many things in forty years. Efficient. Punctual. Thorough. He had collected souls from weeping mothers with a receipt book and a smile, because the smile made it faster and faster was kinder, he had told himself, kinder, he had told himself, kinder—

"I am Marrow-Vane," he said, "officer of collection, fourth rank, sworn under seal. And I testify as follows."

He drew a breath that tasted of salt and old stone.

"Item one. In the matter of the asset designated Xanthe-of-the-Salt-Courts: I personally executed collection against the parents of record, one family of Thornfell outlands, upon default. I was present. I took the signatures. I took the souls." He did not soften it. Softening was for people who deserved comfort, and these were facts, and facts deserved to stand upright. "The parents defaulted because the harvest failed and the tithe was raised, and the tithe was raised because this court's operating costs increased, and the costs increased because the court had recently acquired"—he let the pause do its work—"a certain ember, whose maintenance requires more power than the court generates honestly."

"Item two. The child was present at collection. Watched. Was offered, per standard practice, the option of substitutionary service, and refused, per standard practice, because a child of seven does not understand that refusing is the correct legal answer and accepting is the human one." His voice did not waver. He had practiced not wavering in front of mirrors for forty years and hated himself fluently for every hour of it. "Item three. And this is the material finding."

He faced the Underking directly now.

"I have reviewed the original contract of Sela of Thornfell. I reviewed it eleven years ago during a routine audit of dormant lines—I filed a flag, reference mark 44-Alpha, which was subsequently suppressed, and I can name the clerk who suppressed it. The contract bears the signature of a guardian of record. Not the mother. Not the father. A guardian. And under Article Two, Subsection Four, of the covenant this court enforces on every soul it takes: *a guardian's signature binds property, never persons.*"

He spread his bound hands as wide as the cord allowed, a merchant displaying empty scales.

"Therefore the contract is void ab initio—void from inception. Not broken. Not lapsed. Void, as though it never existed, because legally it never did. Every year of claimed interest, every accrued penalty, every downstream obligation that cites it—including, I note for the record, the collateralization of the asset called Xanthe—is built on sand. And a court such as yours, Your Majesty, cannot survive being caught building on sand. Because the moment one foundation contract falls, every contract that borrowed its authority falls with it. And I have counted, in my forty years of service, approximately nine thousand contracts that cite this one."

The chamber had gone very still. Even the light seemed to lean in.

"You understand what you are doing," the Underking said. It was not quite a question. In it, for the first time, Marrow-Vane heard something almost like age.

"I am doing my job," Marrow-Vane said. "Correctly. For the first time." He looked at Petra. "You wanted to know why I defected. There it is. Not remorse. Remorse is a luxury for people with time. This is *correction*. The court broke its own law first—with the guardian's clause, with the suppressed flag, with the ember. I am merely insisting we all live under the same rules. All of us. Even you."

"Your testimony," said the Underking slowly, "if accepted, suspends collection indefinitely pending adjudication."

"If accepted."

"And adjudication requires the full court. Convened. Which requires—" and here the almost-human face did something Marrow-Vane had never seen it do in two prior audiences, something that in any other creature he would have named fear, "—the seals of office. Which are held in the deep vault. Behind the Weeping Stair. Sealed when the ember was taken, and openable only by—" 

"By a marked soul freely given," Marrow-Vane finished. Yes. He had gotten that far too. "Which presents a certain difficulty, given that everyone in this chamber who bears a mark has either been captured, scattered, or sold their loyalty years ago and is currently kneeling in salt-cord."

"That's not true."

The voice came from behind them—from the entrance gallery, ragged and breathing hard and impossible.

Ferro stood in the doorway of the throne vault, salt-dust white in his hair, one arm hanging wrong, his eyes finding Marrow-Vane's with an expression Marrow-Vane had seen perhaps four times in four decades of looking at people in the worst moments of their lives.

It was the expression of a man who had already decided, and was only waiting to say it out loud.

"I'm marked," Ferro said, and stepped into the light. "I've been marked for nineteen years. And I remember exactly what I promised."
