## 17. Ferro's Choice

Ferro had spent eleven years learning every road that led away from a thing he'd done wrong. He was good at it—good at the walking, good at the not-looking-back, good at the particular arithmetic of guilt that let you keep moving so long as you never once stopped to total the sum. The Weeping Stair had ended all that arithmetic. He'd sold their route for the promise of having his own mark struck from the ledger, and the salt-formed soldiers had come out of the walls like the mountain growing teeth, and Petra Montrose had looked at him across the chaos with an expression he would carry longer than any scar.

She hadn't cursed him. That was the worst of it. She'd just looked, and then she'd turned and put her body between Xanthe and the soldiers, and the stair had swallowed them, and Ferro had stood in the ambush's aftermath holding a bag of coins he couldn't remember earning and a debt he could finally, fully count.

Now he walked back down.

The sealed gate behind them had been Ossian's work—salt-sealed, prayer-sealed, the apostate priest shouting words in the dead tongue until the stone forgot it had ever been a door. It had bought them an hour. It had bought Ferro nothing but a clear view of what he'd chosen, which was the same thing he'd been choosing for eleven years: himself.

He came down the Delve road alone, no lamp, because the dark here didn't want lamps. It wanted honesty. His boots found the old smuggler's path by feel—left at the split where the ceiling dripped, right where the wall hummed if you pressed your palm flat against it—and he thought, absurdly, of teaching this route to Xanthe someday, if there was a someday, if there was a child left to teach.

*Stop,* he told himself. *Count first.*

So he counted, standing in the dark like a merchant taking inventory of a ruined shop.

One: he had sold the route. Two: Marrow-Vane had paid him not in coin but in erasure—the promise that the mark on his soul, the one the Undercourt had held since Ferro was nineteen and desperate and stupid enough to sign for a dead man's debts, would be struck clean. Three: the court did not strike marks. It only transferred them. Somewhere below, in some antechamber of bone-white bureaucracy, there was now a line item that read *Ferro, smuggler — route sold — payment pending,* and payment pending meant the court still owned him, and everything he'd done had purchased nothing but a different cell.

Four—and this was the one that made him sit down on the cold stone, because his legs went—he had betrayed the only people who had never asked him to be anything but what he was. Ossian, who shared his wine and argued theology with him half the night and never once flinched at the mark when it showed through Ferro's skin in bad light. Marrow-Vane, who had joined them out of his own gray penance and whom Ferro had made a fool twice over. And Petra. Petra who fed strays and carried oaths for gods that couldn't answer and had looked at him—at *him*, marked, mercenary, bought and paid for—and said, the night they'd mustered in Graywater, *I don't need you clean, Ferro. I need you steady.*

He had been neither.

* * *

He heard them before he saw them—or rather, he heard the singing. Faint, threading up through the rock like water finding a crack: Xanthe's voice, singing snatches of the dead tongue, the way the child did when the dark grew attentive. The sound reached him in the flooded gallery where the drowned echoes lingered, and one of the salt ghosts turned its face toward him as he passed—a girl, translucent, her mouth opening on a word that might have been a warning or might have been a welcome—and Ferro walked faster.

He found the company camped in a wide chamber where the ceiling hung low and glittering, crusted with mineral bloom like frost that had given up on being cold. A small fire, banked low. Ossian sat awake, as he always seemed to be, a book of half-legible scripture open on his knees. Marrow-Vane stood at the chamber's edge, motionless, the posture of a man who had learned to sleep standing up in rooms full of creditors.

And by the fire, wrapped in Petra's cloak, Xanthe slept—flickering. That was the only word for it. The child's outline stuttered at the edges, like a candle flame seen through dirty glass, like something that belonged to two places and was being pulled toward one of them.

Petra was not asleep either. She sat with her back to the wall, blades within reach even now, and when Ferro stepped into the firelight she was on her feet before his second bootfall, steel drawn, and Ossian was rising too, and Marrow-Vane turned with the slow terrible calm of a man who collected souls and knew exactly what one weighed.

"Steady," Ferro said. His voice came out cracked. "That's all I've got. Steady."

"You," Petra said. Not loud. The word landed like a dropped anchor.

"I'm not asking you to believe me. Gods know I wouldn't." He kept his hands visible, empty, palms out—an old smuggler's courtesy, *nothing up my sleeves but the arms you gave me*. "I'm asking you to hear the arithmetic. Then you can decide whether to gut me, and I'll hold still for it either way."

Ossian closed his book slowly. "Speak, then."

"The court doesn't void contracts. I thought they did—that's what Marrow-Vane promised, that's what they whispered to me on the Stair. Strike the mark, walk free." He pulled his collar aside, and there it was: the mark, the salt-gray sigil burned into the skin over his heart, the brand that had ached for eleven years whenever he crossed running water or spoke a true name aloud. Except now it wasn't gray. Now it moved, faintly, like frost crawling. "They lied. They always lie—it's not even malice, it's *procedure*. They took my betrayal as a new signature. I'm theirs again, deeper than before. Which means—" he made himself look at Petra, made himself stay in the firelight, "—which means I'm a door they can walk through. Any time. I can lead them straight to this chamber without knowing I'm doing it. Maybe already have. Maybe that's why the singing woke me up three galleries back, why the ghosts looked at me like they knew my name."

Silence, except the fire and the distant patient drip of water counting seconds the way only underground water does.

"They marked you years ago," Marrow-Vane said quietly. It wasn't a question. "Nineteen. Debts of a hanged man. I remember the file." Something moved behind the collector's flat eyes—not quite pity. Recognition, maybe. One ledger-creature regarding another.

"You remember the file," Ferro repeated, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "Good. Then you'll understand what I'm about to ask, because you're the only one here who speaks that language."

He knelt. Not to Petra—to the whole company, to the fire, to whatever was listening in the attentive dark. His knees hit stone and the mark over his heart flared cold.

"A contract needs two signatures to stand," he said. "Mine and theirs. That's what you told us in the antechamber, Vane—that's how law works down here, that's how *everything* works down here, it's all just bookkeeping wearing a crown. So un-sign it. Give my soul back to the court—voluntarily, freely, the whole asset, not the installment plan they've got me on—and my betrayal becomes void. Every door I opened closes. Every route I sold goes dark in their books. And this gate—" he nodded upward, toward the seal Ossian had made, "—a voided contract reopens the passage it was signed upon. Old law. You told me that yourself, the night you defected. You said the Undercourt broke its own law first. Well. Here's your chance to make them break it twice."

"No," Petra said.

Ferro kept his eyes on her. "You haven't heard the price."

"I know the price." Her jaw was tight, and her hands weren't quite steady on her sword, and Ferro understood suddenly that she had known since the moment he'd stepped into the light. She was a tithe-child. She knew what the court did with souls better than any of them. "You give yourself back and you don't come back. There is no *after* for this one. No smuggling run, no sunrise, no—" Her voice caught and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "No bread. No rain."

"Somebody's been talking to Ossian about my vices."

"Ferro."

"It costs memories," Marrow-Vane said, and his voice had gone very careful, the way it did around numbers that were about to get ugly. "Voluntary surrender, full asset. The court takes the whole soul, and the soul is where the surface keeps itself. He would forget the sun. He would forget every face above ground, every road, every year. He'd go down there a stranger wearing his own body." The collector paused. "It is the only thing they value more than a runaway child. A willing adult. Willingness is rare currency in a place built on compulsion. It would buy more than a gate. It might buy time. Leverage. An audience with the Underking on terms the court cannot simply refuse, because refusing a willing surrender violates their own founding statutes, and the Underking's power is *entirely* statutory—"

"Vane," Ossian said gently.

"—I am establishing the terms," Marrow-Vane finished, and for the first time since Ferro had known him, the debt-collector's composure cracked, just at the seam. "The terms are ruinous. I am obligated to say so. I am—" he stopped, started again. "I collected a family once. Parents who defaulted. Their child watched me do it. I have been trying to correct one unlawful contract ever since, and I find that correction requires other men to pay lawful ones, and I find that I hate it."

"That's the most words you've wasted on feeling in your life," Ferro said. "Save the rest. I didn't come here to be talked out of it."

"Ferro." Petra sheathed her sword. That was worse than the blade somehow—the soft click of it, the surrender of the argument steel had been having on her behalf. She crossed the chamber and stood over him, kneeling in the frost-glitter, and she looked older than she had in Graywater, older than she had on any road, like someone watching a wound she recognized because she'd carried its twin since childhood. "Eleven years you ran from that mark. I watched you run. I let you run, because I figured a man's got a right to his own distance. And now you're going to stop running into the one direction there's no coming back from."

"Seems fitting," Ferro said. "Symmetry. Very liturgical. Ask Ossian."

"This isn't redemption. This is just another road away."

"No." He shook his head. "Every road I ever took was *away*. This is the first one that goes *through*." He looked past her, at the sleeping, flickering child. "I watched that kid draw maps under my feet and I thought, *that poor creature, sold before it could say no.* And then I remembered I'd done the same thing at nineteen, except I'd signed, which is worse, because at least Xanthe has the excuse of being six. Everything down here runs on people who couldn't say no. Parents who couldn't. Children who weren't asked." He met Petra's eyes. "I'm saying yes. Freely. Nobody's ever gotten to do that down there. Let's see what it breaks."

* * *

They did not argue long, in the end, because there was nothing to argue with. Ossian tried scripture and Ferro answered with the parts of scripture about debt-jubilee and Ossian wept openly and blessed him anyway, muttering that a godless man understood grace better than most priests, which Ferro chose to take as a compliment. Petra tried fury and then tried silence and then tried, last and worst, kindness—offered him a way out, a smaller sacrifice, a partial payment—and Ferro refused each one so gently that she finally struck the stone wall with her fist and turned away, shoulders shaking with something that was not quite grief and not quite respect.

Marrow-Vane prepared the rite, because only a licensed collector could draft a voluntary surrender, and the irony of that was not lost on anyone. He worked by firelight with a stylus of black bone on a scrap of cured hide, writing in the dead tongue, and as he wrote he recited the terms aloud in trade-language so there could be no misunderstanding:

*"Let the record show: Ferro, of no house, smuggler, aged thirty, surrenders wholly and willingly the soul currently encumbered under mark and contract. Let all derivative claims founded upon his prior acts of betrayal be rendered void. Let the passage sealed upon the Weeping Stair be reopened according to statute. Let the court receive this asset in full satisfaction, and let the receiving be witnessed."*

"Witnessed by whom?" Petra asked.

"By me," said Marrow-Vane. "And by her." He nodded at Xanthe, who had woken—who had perhaps never been asleep—and was sitting up in the cloak with those salt-pale yellow eyes wide open, watching Ferro with an expression no child should have had to learn: recognition. The look of someone who knew exactly what was being given away, because it had been taken from them once.

Xanthe said something in the dead tongue. Soft. Two syllables.

"What did it say?" Ferro asked.

Ossian's translation came hoarse. "*Thank you.*"

"Don't thank me yet, sprout. I haven't forgotten anything worth forgetting." But his hands had begun to shake, and he hid them in his coat like a boy hiding a cheat at cards.

* * *

They did it at the sealed gate, because the reopening needed to happen there, and because Ferro wanted to do it facing downward. The seal glowed faintly where Ossian had laid it, a skin of white light over black stone, and beyond it the stair descended into air that tasted of brine and old decisions.

Ferro took off his coat. Folded it. Set it down neatly, the way his mother had taught him before she died, before the debts, before all of it—though even as he thought *mother* he felt the memory begin to thin at its edges, like paint in rain, and he understood that the taking had already begun, that willingness opened the door from both sides.

"Marrow-Vane." His voice held. He was proud of that. "When it's done—when I'm down there and I don't know why—I want you to tell me one thing. Just one thing, every time we meet, however many times that is. Tell me it was raining."

The collector's stylus hovered. "Raining?"

"On the surface. When I decided. It was raining, that night on the Stair, I remember the smell of it on the stones before the soldiers came. If I don't know anything else, I want to know it was raining. I want to spend whatever's left of me wondering what rain was." He smiled, and it hurt, and he did it anyway. "A man should leave himself one locked door worth picking."

Marrow-Vane wrote it into the margin of the surrender in the dead tongue, sealing it, a clause of mercy smuggled into a bill of sale. Then he looked up, and the great flat machinery of his composure was gone entirely, and what was underneath was just a tired man with a terrible job. "For what it is worth," he said, "and it is worth little: what you are doing is the single lawful act I have witnessed in forty years of service to unlawful law. I will carry the record myself. Nothing in that court will touch it."

"Touching," Ferro said. "Do it before I change my mind, which is now."

Marrow-Vane drew the stylus across the mark on Ferro's chest.

There was no pain, which was the horrifying part. There was only *leaving*. Ferro felt the surface go the way you feel a tide go out—Graywater first, the harbor lights, gone; then the roads, eleven years of them, every smuggler's cut and hidden door, dissolving like salt in warm water; then faces. Ossian's laugh. Petra saying *steady*. Bread—he felt bread go, the whole concept of it, warmth and crust and the tearing of it, and he grabbed for it and missed. Rain. He held rain longest. Rain was a smell and a sound and the weight of it on his shoulders after a long dry haul, and he stood in the gate's cold breath and gripped it with both hands of his fading mind and—

It was raining.

He didn't know what that meant anymore. But somewhere in him, in a room with no windows, a single locked door now bore one word scratched into the wood, and he would spend the rest of his existence with his ear pressed against it.

The seal broke. Light poured up out of the deep like something exhaled, and the gate swung open on darkness, and Ferro—tall, calm, empty-eyed, wearing his own body like a borrowed coat—turned and walked down into the Undercourt, and the dark received him the way a ledger receives a line: without comment, without mercy, and forever.

Petra stood at the threshold until the footsteps faded. Then she picked up the folded coat and held it against her chest, and Ossian heard her say, very quietly, to no one living:

"Steady. He was steady, in the end."

Behind them, Xanthe began to sing—a new song, low and urgent, a road-song in the dead tongue, and Ossian translated in a whisper as the notes climbed:

*The willing soul has opened the way.*
*The throne vault knows his name.*
*Run, oath-bearer. Run while the door stands true—*
*for the Underking has risen from his chair.*
