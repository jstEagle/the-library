## 18. Ember

The vault door was salt the way bone is calcium—not a substance but a structure, grown rather than built, and it opened for Xanthe before any of them touched it.

Petra felt the change in the air pressure first, the way she'd felt storms coming as a girl on the Graywater flats: a thinning behind the ears, a taste like old coins. The corridor beyond was lit by nothing and yet not dark. The walls gave off a pale luminance, the light of things that had forgotten they were meant to be hidden.

"You don't have to come in with us," she said to Marrow-Vane. Her voice came out flattened, swallowed by the corridor before it could echo. "You've done what you promised."

"I promised correction." Marrow-Vane adjusted his cuffs—a habit so ingrained it survived even here, even now, his fingers gray with tunnel dust. "Correction is not complete until the ledger is closed. And ledgers are closed in the vault." He paused at the threshold, and something moved behind his face that might have been fear wearing courtesy as a mask. "Also, I am reasonably certain the herald will kill me if I remain outside alone. So. Sentiment and self-interest align, for once."

Ossian laughed—short, disbelieving, the laugh of a man who had prayed himself hollow and found the hollow useful. His hands were shaking around his stolen fragment of the ledger, the page he'd torn free with Petra's name on it: *Montrose, Sela. Sold, age six. Never collected.* He'd been reading it aloud every few hours since, like a man checking that fire was still hot.

"Never collected," he said again now, quietly. "Do you understand what that means, Sela? You are an open account. You have been walking around up there for thirty years as an *open account*."

"Then let's close it," Petra said.

Xanthe looked up at her. In the pale light of the corridor their eyes did something impossible—the amber brightening from within, a coal breathing. They didn't speak in any tongue Ossian needed to translate. They simply took her hand.

* * *

The throne vault was smaller than she expected.

She had imagined, in the long hours since the ambush on the Weeping Stair—in the running, the hiding, the crawling through service galleries that remembered her feet better than her mind did—that the heart of the Undercourt would be vast. A cathedral of salt. Pillars to make the mountain feel like a ribcage.

Instead it was a room. Low-ceilinged. Almost domestic. The throne was a chair of fused crystal, worn smooth at the arms, and the Underking sat in it the way an old man sits in a kitchen: thoroughly, patiently, as if he had been sitting there since before chairs.

He was not enormous. That was the worst of it. He was a figure of gray salt-crystal in the shape of a tall man, and his face was the shape faces take when someone has tried to remember a person and gotten it slightly wrong forever. His eyes were holes, and the holes went back further than the room allowed.

"Sela," he said. The name filled the vault without raising its volume, the way cold fills a cellar. "Thirty years. You grew well."

"I'm not Sela," Petra said. "Sela died on the road out of Thornfell. I'm what walked away."

"A distinction without a difference to a creditor." He rose. The movement was slow, courteous, terrible. "But you are not why I waited. Bring the vessel forward."

Xanthe stepped in front of Petra before she could move them herself—stepped between her and the throne with the instinct of a child putting themselves between a parent and a blow, and something in Petra's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"The child is my property," the Underking said. "Sold in good faith. Escaped in breach. Every hour of freedom accrues interest, and the interest is paid—" his hole-eyes turned, briefly, toward the corridor where Ferro's absence was a wound none of them spoke about, "—in whatever currency I choose. You have seen my soldiers. You know the exchange rate."

"They're not your property," Petra said. "Marrow-Vane testified. The contract was signed by a guardian, not the parents. Your own law makes it void."

"My law," the Underking said, "is whatever I can enforce." He extended one crystalline hand, palm up. Patient. "Return the vessel. Walk out with your companions. This is generous, Sela Montrose. Generosity is also a form of accounting."

And Xanthe—God help her, Xanthe began to walk forward.

"No." Petra caught their shoulder. The child looked back at her, and their face was doing the thing it did when the ember burned too bright behind their eyes: flickering, translucent at the edges, a lantern papered over with skin. "No. You don't get to buy their lives with yourself. That's not how this works."

"It is exactly how it works," the Underking said gently. "It is how everything works. Debts move. Souls settle. The strong pay for the weak or the weak pay for everyone. There has never been another arithmetic."

"There has," Petra said. "I just haven't remembered it yet."

She didn't fully understand what she meant until she said it—and then, all at once, she did, the way you understand a staircase in the dark only after your foot finds the first step.

* * *

Here is what she knew, assembled at last from thirty years of fragments:

She had been born under the mountain, in a village whose chapel held a god called Vessel-of-Salt, a small god, a parish god, a god of thresholds and preservation—of keeping things whole across hard passages. Salt kept meat through winter. Salt kept wounds from rotting. Vessel-of-Salt kept souls intact through the crossings of life: birth, marriage, death, debt.

Every tithe-child was sworn upon the altar before being sent down. Not sold by the god—for the god had no power over the Underking's contracts—but *witnessed*. Blessed. Marked with the oath-mark so that whatever happened below, some part of them would remain claimable by heaven. An anchor-line thrown into dark water.

Her mark had gone silent a hundred days ago because the god itself had gone silent. Because the god had been taken—devoured, swallowed whole by the thing in the throne, the way a creditor seizes assets.

And the last ember of it—

The last ember of it had gone somewhere. Gods did not simply stop. Small gods especially did not. They pooled. They fled into vessels, into hosts, into anything marked and warm and near.

Into a feral child who had clawed out of the deep roads with a salt-white coin in one fist and a dead language on their tongue. Into eyes the color of banked fire. Into a body that flickered at the edges because it was holding more than a soul should hold.

"Ossian," Petra said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "When you translated the fragments. The tithe-prayers. What word did the old rite use for the witness?"

Ossian blinked. "*Vessel,*" he said. "The god was named for its function. It witnessed the tithes. It—oh." His face changed. "Oh, mercy. Oh, you *fool*, it was there. At your swearing. It held your name."

"And when it fell," Petra said, "it fell into the nearest thing it had ever held."

The vault was very quiet. Even the Underking had stopped moving, which was perhaps the most frightening thing of all—a creditor who recognizes, a moment too late, that the debtor has found the flaw in the instrument.

"Marrow-Vane," Petra said. "Read me the law. All of it. The part about transfer."

Marrow-Vane had gone still, and for once the stillness was not courtesy but awe. "Article Nine," he recited, and his voice took on the cadence of liturgy, because to men like him it was one. "*A claim may be transferred to any party who assumes it willingly, in full knowledge, upon the original collateral.*" He lowered the page. "Petra. The original collateral of every tithe-contract in this court—the security underlying the entire edifice—is the witnessing god. The oaths it held. Its claim on the sworn."

"Which is me," Petra said. "It swore on me first. Before Xanthe was born. Before anyone thought to sell a child to keep a village fed, it put its mark on me and said *this one is mine to keep whole.*" She looked down at her forearm, where the oath-mark lay black and burning under the skin. "Everything the Underking built sits on top of a debt he never collected. Mine."

"You do not know what you are offering," the Underking said. For the first time, his voice was not patient.

"Yes I do," Petra said. "That's the whole point of Article Nine. Full knowledge." She turned to Xanthe, and knelt, because some things you say level. "Listen to me. Both of you. The god in you—I'm going to touch it. And when it knows me, we're going to move the debt. All of it. Onto me. Onto the account that was never collected. And then your contract dissolves, because a debt cannot be owed twice."

Xanthe's eyes were wide and burning. Their mouth worked. What came out was not the dead court-tongue. It was broken, accented, half-remembered Common—the words of a child who had heard a language through walls and years and had saved a few pieces against need.

"*Hurts,*" they said. "*For you, it hurts.*"

"Yeah," Petra said. Her throat was tight. "Probably."

"*Don't.*"

"Hey." She took their small face in her two hands, careful, the way you handle something already cracked. "You climbed out of hell on your hands and knees to knock on a stranger's door. You carried a god inside you the whole way and didn't drop it. You think I'm going to stand here and let the arithmetic win?" She pressed her forehead to theirs. "Love isn't duty, little one. Duty is what you owe. Love is what you choose. I'm choosing this."

Behind her, Ossian was praying—not to the silent sky, but to the ember, quietly, desperately, a priest improvising a liturgy for a god the size of a coal. Marrow-Vane had the ledger page open and was reading Article Nine aloud in the formal tongue, over and over, building a legal wall around what she was about to do.

The Underking stood. "If you assume this debt," he said, "you will not walk out of here. You will not see daylight. You will be the collateral of a court that does not forgive and does not end. Thirty years of freedom traded for—" he gestured at the walls, the weight, the dark, "—this. Forever."

"I know," Petra said, standing. "I did the math a long time ago, back when I was six years old and somebody decided my life was worth a winter's grain. I've been in your books since before I could read them." She rolled up her sleeve. The oath-mark was black as a drowned star. "Time I settled up."

She reached into Xanthe.

Not with her hands—with the place behind the mark, the place where the oath lived, the anchor-line thrown into dark water thirty years ago and never cut. She followed it down, past skin and fear and the small rapid heartbeat of a terrified child, into the chamber where the ember waited: a grain of fire in a nest of salt, dim, guttering, patient beyond all sense.

*Hello,* she thought at it, the way you greet a dog you raised from a pup. *You remember me.*

The ember flared.

Recognition went through her like a bell struck against her spine—*Sela, sworn-at-six, kept-whole, mine*—and the whole vault rang with it, walls singing, salt chiming, the Underking staggering back from his throne as if the floor had turned to doctrine. The god's ember knew her. Had always known her. Had crawled across a kingdom of dark to reach the one soul it had ever held.

"Article Nine," Marrow-Vane was saying, loud now, triumphant and horrified at once. "*Assumed willingly, in full knowledge, upon the original collateral—witnessed, witnessed, witnessed!*"

Petra opened her hands inside the burning place and said the only prayer left in her: *Take the debt. Take all of it. Put my name where the child's was.*

The ember hesitated—one instant, an infinite courtesy, a god asking permission of the one it loved—and then it moved.

It came into her like dawn comes into a valley: not fast, but total. And with it came the debt, the whole towering ruin of it, thirty years of accrued interest, every tithe-soul's chain suddenly re-forged to her wrist, the weight of an entire Underground economy settling onto one pair of shoulders.

Petra screamed. She would admit that later, to no one. She screamed, and held on, and did not let go.

The black mark on her arm split—and did not bleed darkness. It bled light. Thin, white, salt-bright, the color of the first hour of morning.

Across the vault, the Underking's throne cracked from crown to base.

"What," he said, in a voice like a landslide remembering it was once a mountain, "have you done?"

Petra straightened. The pain was everywhere and it was hers, all of it, and underneath the pain was something she hadn't felt in a hundred days and longer: an answer. Small. Flickering. Alive. A voice like a coal saying her true name.

"I've called in your mortgage," she said. "Now watch what happens when the collateral calls the loan."

The ember surged up her arm, toward the ledger written in the walls themselves, toward every line and every name—

And the mountain began, very softly, to sing.
