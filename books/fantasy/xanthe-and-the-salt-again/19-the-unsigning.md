## 19. The Unsigning

The throne vault of the Undercourt was not a room so much as a wound in the mountain that had learned to hold its shape. Salt grew from it in every direction—columns like frozen geysers, buttresses like ribs, a floor that glittered under no light Petra could name, because there was none and yet everything was visible, the way things are visible in dreams or drowning.

She stood at the center of it with Xanthe's hand in hers.

The court had assembled to watch her die in the legal sense. She could feel them at the edges of her vision: the salt-formed soldiers standing in ranks like a forest of white spears, the herald folded into itself like a letter, Marrow-Vane held between two guards who did not need to grip him because he was not going anywhere, and Ossian, on his knees before the great ledger, still reading aloud in a voice that shook only a little.

"Entry four thousand one hundred and twelve," Ossian said. "Montrose, Sela. Daughter of Thornfell tithe-house. Sold at age six years by guardian's mark. *Never collected.*"

"*Never collected*," the Underking repeated, and his voice was the sound of a glacier deciding. "For thirty-one years, the debt has ripened. Interest compounded in salt and silence. She is the richest soul in my court, and she has walked into my vault of her own accord."

Petra did not look at him. Looking at him was a mistake; she had made it once already, when they dragged her in, and what she had seen—a shape too vast for the chamber, wearing kingship like an ill-fitting coat over something older and hungrier—had nearly unmade her resolve before she spoke a word. So she looked at Xanthe instead. Yellow eyes, salt-pale face, the ember behind them flickering like a candle in a draft.

"You took my god," Petra said.

"I consumed your god," the Underking corrected. "There is a difference. One is theft. The other is appetite. I have never been accused of theft. Every soul in this court is here by contract."

"Then you'll want the paperwork in order," Petra said, "because I'm about to ruin your filing system."

Something moved through the assembled court—not laughter, nothing that generous, but a shifting, a rustle of attention. The Underking leaned down out of his vastness, and where his face should have been there was a suggestion of features, like a face seen through frosted glass.

"You have come to bargain. Good. Bargaining is the oldest sacrament. Name your price, mercenary. Your old life, restored. Your childhood, returned. The village that sold you, made to kneel. The god you mourn, resurrected whole. All of it is within my gift. All I ask is the child."

"The child isn't mine to give," Petra said. "That's rather the point."

"She is yours to give. You claimed her. Heirship transfers obligation. It is written." A gesture, and the ledger turned its own pages with a sound like dry leaves. "You named her heir. Therefore her debts are yours, as yours were nearly hers. Elegant. I almost admire it."

"You're right," Petra said. "It is elegant. So let me show you how it works."

She let go of Xanthe's hand.

The absence of the small warm fingers was the hardest thing she had done in thirty-one years of hard things, and she did it anyway, because what came next could not be done holding on.

"Brother Ossian," she said. "Read me the law."

Ossian rose from his knees. He looked terrible—he had been weeping, though he would deny it later, and the salt dust had gotten into the creases of his face so that he seemed already half a monument—but his voice, when it came, was the voice that had once held congregations of three hundred in a cathedral whose god had also stopped answering.

"The law of the Undercourt," he recited, "as carved into the First Gate, as witnessed at Graywater, as binding upon signatory and sovereign alike: *A debt may be claimed only by the party named. A debt may be discharged only by the party named. No contract survives the death, dissolution, or transference-in-full of both parties bound.*"

"And the ledger-line?" Petra asked.

"A ledger-line," Ossian said, and now his voice did shake, because he understood what she was about to do and was giving her the rope anyway, because it was the rope she'd asked for, "*may be claimed by the one it names, at any time, in any place where the ledger is open.*"

The ledger was open. It lay on its reading-stone ten feet away, and its pages were turning in a wind that came from nowhere, showing entries in their thousands, souls itemized like cargo.

Petra walked to it.

No one stopped her. That was the trap, she knew—the same trap the whole mountain was built around, the same architecture as the Weeping Stair and the flooded gallery and every gate they'd passed. The Undercourt never forbade anything. It only priced everything. Let her walk. Let her claim. There would be a cost, and the cost would feed the machine, and the machine would endure.

But the machine had a flaw, and the flaw was her.

She reached the reading-stone and put her palm flat against the page. Entry 4112 stared up at her, her childhood name in ink that was not ink, and beneath it the guardian's mark that Ossian and Marrow-Vane had spent three days proving was unlawful—a mark pressed by the village elder who had taken payment twice, once from her parents' fear and once from the court's treasury, selling a soul that had never been his to sell.

Voidable. They had proven it voidable. But voidable was not voided, and voiding required the named party, present, willing, and—

Paying.

"I claim entry four thousand one hundred and twelve," Petra said, and her voice carried in the vault the way her prayers never had, striking the salt columns and coming back doubled, tripled, the whole chamber becoming an instrument around her words. "Sela, daughter of Thornfell. Sold at six. Never collected. I am Sela. I am collected. I am here."

The ledger warmed under her hand.

"And I pay," she said.

She drew her knife—the smuggler's knife, Ferro's knife, the one she'd taken off his body at the Weeping Stair and meant to return to him if there was a him left to return it to—and opened her left palm along the old oath-mark, the one that had burned black in the shrine-chamber where her god lay desecrated. Blood welled, ordinary and red, absurdly red in all that white.

"Thirty-one years of interest," she said. "In salt and silence. You want silence? Take mine. You want salt? Here's the water of my body, which is salt, which is the whole joke of me, isn't it—I was always yours by chemistry if nothing else."

The Underking shifted. The vault groaned.

"Daughter of Thornfell," he said, and there was something new in the glacial voice, and she thought—it startled her, it would startle her for the rest of her life—she thought it might be caution. "You discharge one debt. The court has forty thousand."

"I know," Petra said. "That's why I brought the ember."

She turned to Xanthe.

The child had not moved from the spot where she'd left them, and up close Petra could see what the others could not: the flicker under Xanthe's skin, the light that was not light, the last living piece of Vessel-of-Salt burning in a small chest like a coal wrapped in paper. Thirty-one days of silence. Thirty-one years, really, since the day a six-year-old girl walked out of a tunnel and forgot, because forgetting was the only mercy the mountain offered.

"Hey," Petra said softly, in the dead tongue, the language of the salt courts, the words Ossian had taught her syllable by syllable across a hundred miles of dark road. *Little vessel. Little lamp. Do you remember me?*

Xanthe's eyes came up. And inside them, for the first time since Graywater, something looked back that was not only a child. Something old. Something that had been silent for a hundred days and devoured for longer, and that knew her—not as Sela, not as Montrose, not as the mercenary, but as the girl who had prayed at its altar every morning of her childhood without ever knowing what she prayed to.

*Vessel-of-Salt,* Petra said, in the dead tongue, with blood running off her fingertips onto the ledger. *I kept the oath. Every day. Even when you didn't answer. Especially then.*

The ember flared.

It came out of Xanthe the way dawn comes out of the sea—slowly, then all at once—and it did not leave the child empty. That had been Petra's terror, the thing she had not said aloud even to herself: that pulling the god free would gut the small body that had sheltered it. But the ember rose out of Xanthe like smoke rising out of a house that remains standing, and Xanthe gasped, and lived, and swayed, and Petra caught them one-handed with her bleeding palm pressed to the ledger-page and her other arm full of child.

And the ember hung in the air above them both, and it was small. It was so small. A coal, a spark, a grain of fire no bigger than the salt coin Marrow-Vane had left on an inn table in another life. Her god, entire. Her god, whom she had fed with thirty years of muscle-memory prayer, reduced to this.

It was enough. It had always been enough. That was the theology Ossian had been trying to teach her since the Delve: gods are not diminished by being small. Only by being owned.

"Here is the transfer," Petra said aloud, and the vault went utterly still, even the Underking, even the soldiers, even the wind through the ledger-pages. "Entry 4112, discharged in full by the named party—myself—with interest paid in blood. Witnessed." She nodded at Ossian. "Witnessed," he echoed, hoarse. "And the consideration received—" she lifted the ember on her bleeding palm, and it sank into the wound without heat, and the black oath-mark lit white for the first time in her life, "—is my god, returned to me, and with it, my oath renewed upon my own soul, freely given, which no one may collect, because it was never sold."

The ledger screamed.

Not the book—the mountain. Every salt column in the vault shuddered. Somewhere far above, she knew, gates were groaning, seals were cracking, the whole architecture of the Undercourt discovering that its foundations were not stone but arithmetic, and the arithmetic had just been falsified at the root. Because a contract discharged by the named party, with the consideration transferred to a soul that was never entered on any ledger, created a precedent, and precedent in the Undercourt was law, and law was the only thing the Underking had ever actually been.

"No," the Underking said.

"Yes," said Marrow-Vane.

He stepped forward out of the guard's slackened grip—they were salt, and salt obeys contracts, and the contracts were failing—and he had the ledger of collections in his hands, the personal one, the one he'd shown them in the dark of the Weeping Stair with shaking collector's hands. "Every tithe-contract signed by a guardian rather than a parent is void ab initio," he said, in the flat recitation of a man performing his final duty of office. "I attest. I am a licensed officer of this court, and I attest that the court broke its own law first, at scale, for two centuries. Void. Void. *Void.*"

He began tearing pages out of his ledger and letting them fall, and where each page touched the salt floor it dissolved into brine.

The soldiers began to come apart. Not violently—worse, gently, like snowmen in thaw, ranks of white spears softening into pools that reflected the ember-light and held no shapes at all. Among the pools, other things were standing up. Faint things. Echo-things. Tithe-children rising out of the flooded galleries of the deep roads, drawn upward toward the vault by the sound of a ledger dying, and among them, faint as breath on glass, someone was calling *Sela, Sela* in a voice Petra had not heard in thirty-one years and recognized anyway.

Later she would not remember choosing to speak the last of it. She would remember only that her mouth was full of blood and salt and the taste of her own name, and that she said:

"The ledger of the Undercourt is closed. Every line in it is void. Every soul in it is free. I am the named party on the founding debt, and I unsign it—all of it—in my blood, by my god, before this court."

The ember blazed white through her palm, through her veins, through the black mark that was no longer black, and the ledger's pages turned one final time, all forty thousand entries at once, and went blank.

The Underking came apart the way a debt comes apart when it is forgiven: not destroyed, simply *unowned*, a vastness with nothing left to be vast about, folding down and down and down until all that remained was a drift of ordinary salt on an ordinary floor, and silence—the good kind, the kind that is just quiet.

Xanthe tugged her sleeve.

Petra looked down. The child pointed past the dead throne, past the blank ledger, to where the far wall of the vault had cracked open along a seam no map had ever shown, and through the seam came something impossible.

Daylight.

Dust motes turning in a slanted gold bar of actual sun, falling on the salt floor, and beyond it the sound—faint, real, unhinged from anything—of wind moving through trees.

Xanthe said something in the dead tongue. Petra didn't need Ossian to translate it. Some words you learn in the marrow before you learn them in the ear.

*The mountain is exhaling,* the child had said. *Walk while it's breathing out.*

Petra sheathed the knife. She picked Xanthe up—too big to carry now, nearly, too old, but neither of them mentioned it—and she turned to the others, to Ossian gathering his torn liturgies, to Marrow-Vane standing in the brine of his own burned book with the expression of a man who has finally put down something very heavy, to the ghost-children streaming past them all toward the crack of gold like fish toward the surface.

"Ferro's still down here somewhere," she said. Her voice cracked and she let it. "We don't leave without him. Nobody else gets left down here either. Everyone walks out, or nobody does."

Above them, through a mile of splitting stone, the mountain groaned like an old man standing up from a long kneeling, and the first true collapse-shudder ran through the floor beneath their feet.

They ran for the light.
