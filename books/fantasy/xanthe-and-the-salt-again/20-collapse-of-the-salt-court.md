## 20. Collapse of the Salt Court

*Xanthe heard the world end in a whisper.*

That was how Ossian would later describe it, when he set down what happened in the vault of the Underking, because Xanthe could not yet say it themselves. The child lay across Petra's shoulder like a coat someone had forgotten to take off—limp, salt-pale, their breath coming in little flickers that matched no rhythm Ossian knew. But their eyes were open. Half open. And through those eyes, Ossian understood, the whole court was audible: every contract in the mountain screaming at once as it came undone.

Petra had done something in the throne room. Ossian had seen only the edge of it—the ledger line burning off the page in light the color of dawn on snow, Petra's blood mixing with an ember that was not hers and had always been hers—and then the Underking had made a sound. Not a roar. A sound like a great book being closed by a hand with no patience left in it.

Then the floor began to forget its shape.

"Up," Marrow-Vane said, and his voice, for the first time since Ossian had known him, held nothing courteous at all. "Everyone up. The court is unwinding. Every corridor it built out of obligation is going back to being nothing."

The antechamber where they stood was already weeping. Salt ran down the walls in ropes, thick as rope itself, pooling on the flagstones and hissing where it touched Ossian's boots. The great ledger still lay open on its lectern, and he watched, with a scholar's horror and a scholar's awe, as the names began to lift off the pages. Not erased—*freed*. Each one rose like a spark from a struck flint, hovered a moment, and shot upward through the ceiling as though the stone were water and the sky above the mountain were the only direction worth swimming.

He counted without meaning to. He was a priest; counting souls was the first thing they had ever taught him, before they taught him wrong. Forty. Sixty. By the hundreds now, streaming up through rock that opened for them like a mouth.

One of them paused.

It hung in the air before him—a shape of pale light with something almost like a face—and Ossian felt his heart stutter, because he knew this one. From the flooded gallery, days or years ago. The drowned tithe-child who had called Petra *Sela*.

It looked at him. It did not have eyes, but it looked.

"Brother," it said, in the dead tongue, and Ossian's translation came slow and stumbling even now: *You wrote us down kindly. That mattered.* Then it rose, and joined the river of lights pouring upward, and was gone.

Ossian stood very still. He had spent twenty years believing that writing things down was the same as owning them. The mountain, in its last hour, was teaching him otherwise.

* * *

The corridors were dying around them, and dying loudly.

Ferro led, because Ferro knew how to run from things—he said so himself, grinning, though the grin kept slipping off his face like wet cloth. His memories of the surface were gone, traded away at the sealed gate, but his legs remembered everything his mind had lost, and he moved through the collapsing galleries with the certainty of an animal. Behind him came Marrow-Vane, carrying Ossian's satchel along with his own burned half-ledger, walking backward half the time to watch for pursuit that never came. There was no one left to pursue them. The salt-formed soldiers had stopped mid-stride when the contracts broke, standing in the halls like abandoned furniture, and then, one by one, they had sighed into drifts of white powder that the freed souls' wind scattered like pollen.

In the middle of the company, wrapped in Petra's cloak because Petra's arms were needed elsewhere, rode Xanthe.

Ossian carried the child. It had not been discussed; he had simply reached out when Petra staggered under the combined weight of exhaustion and godhood, and she had handed Xanthe over with a look of naked relief that he would remember longer than anything else from that day.

Xanthe weighed almost nothing. That frightened him more than the falling ceilings. The child was flickering—not visibly, not the way a candle flickers, but in some register Ossian felt against his skin, a stuttering presence, as if Xanthe were several children occupying the same body by turns and losing track of which was which. The ember inside them, the swallowed heart of Vessel-of-Salt, was dimming. Petra had drawn on it to break the ledger, and it had answered, and answering had nearly emptied it.

*Hold on,* Ossian wanted to say. He didn't have the tongue for it. So instead he spoke the dead language badly, the few phrases he'd assembled over weeks of listening, and what he managed was: *The light is up. We go to the light.*

And Xanthe, eyes half-shut, mouth crusted white, smiled. It was the smallest thing. It carried Ossian the next mile.

* * *

The Weeping Stair was gone. Where it had wound down through the mountain's throat there was now a raw vertical shaft, dripping, breathing, exhaling gusts of air that smelled—Ferrociously, wonderfully—of rain and pine and distance. Surface air. It poured down the shaft like a blessing and everyone stopped at the edge of it just to stand in the current, faces lifted, letting it push their hair back.

"The long gallery," Petra said hoarsely. She was leaning on her sword like a walking stick, and her oath-mark, the black burn on her forearm, had faded to the color of old ash. "There's a switchback route. I used it as a child. Before—" She stopped. She had been about to say *before I was taken*. Now, perhaps, the sentence had changed shape. "Before I left."

"You were six," Ossian said quietly. "Sold at six. Never collected. I read your line, Petra. You were always meant to come back down here. Maybe this is what coming back was for."

"Don't," she said. Not cruelly. "Not yet. When we're in the sun, you can tell me all the terrible beautiful things my life means. Right now it just means stairs."

There were stairs. Of course there were—she remembered them the way she remembered nothing else from that age, whole flights of them surfacing in her memory like drowned boats as she walked. Ossian watched her lead them through passages that should not have existed, past doors carved with tithe-marks worn smooth by small hands, and understood that this was the last inheritance the mountain would give her: the map of her own captivity, drawn on the inside of her skull, waiting thirty years to be read aloud.

They climbed. The mountain shook. Somewhere far below, the throne vault finished forgetting itself, and the sound that came up through the stone was not an explosion but a long, tectonic sigh—the mountain exhaling, Ossian thought, after holding its breath for a thousand years of other people's debts.

Halfway up, the ceiling ahead began to buckle.

"Left," said Xanthe.

Everyone froze. The child had spoken in the common tongue—broken, rusted, barely a whisper—but unmistakably, and in Ossian's arms, pointing with a finger that trembled but pointed true.

"Left," Xanthe said again, annoyed, as if they were all being very slow. Which they were.

They went left. Twenty paces later the passage behind them filled with rubble to the ceiling, and Ferro laughed—a wild, unmoored laugh, the laugh of a man whose memories were gone but whose gratitude was intact—and clapped Xanthe so gently on the head that it was more a blessing than a blow.

"Little navigator," he said. "I don't know much anymore, but I know I'd follow you anywhere."

Xanthe considered this with grave, sunken-eyed seriousness, and then laid one salt-dry hand flat against Ferro's cheek. Whatever passed between them, Ferro stopped laughing and started crying, quietly, without shame, and walked on wiping his face and saying nothing, and no one asked.

* * *

They came out through a crack in the mountain's shoulder that had not existed three days ago—the stone split wide by the court's collapse, a wound in the hillside bleeding daylight—and the light hit them like a wave.

Ossian fell to his knees in the grass. Actual grass. Wet, cold, ordinary, smelling of sheep and loam and the far sea. Above him the sky was doing something outrageous and grey-gold, evening coming on in layers, and he knelt in it and wept the way he had not wept since he was a novitiate, since before the Church taught him tears were data.

Marrow-Vane sat down on a rock and took out his ledger—the real one, the master copy he had carried for forty years of collection—and fed it page by page into a small fire he built with hands that shook only a little. He did not speak while it burned. When it was ash he looked at the ash for a long time and then said, to no one, to everyone: "The accounts are closed." And that was all his homily, and it was enough.

Ferro lay flat on his back in the wet grass and let the drizzle land on his face, and asked, wonderingly, "What is this? What is this called?" and when Ossian told him *rain*, Ferro said "Rain," twice, tasting it, like a word in a language he was only beginning.

But Ossian's eyes kept going back to Petra, and to the bundle in her arms.

She had taken Xanthe back at the threshold, and now she stood just outside the cave-mouth with the last of the daylight on her face and the child pressed against her chest, and Ossian saw her lips moving. Praying, he thought at first. To the silent god, out of hundred-day habit. Then he leaned close and heard, and corrected himself.

She wasn't praying. She was talking. Telling Xanthe about the sky. Naming the colors, one by one, the way you name things to a child learning the world—or the way you name things to a god waking slowly inside a child, reminding it what it had loved before it was stolen.

"And that one's called cloud," Petra was saying, voice cracked to a thread. "That grey one. And the gold behind it doesn't have a name, nobody's ever managed it, but you'll see it again tomorrow, and the day after, and I swear to you—you hear me?—you'll see it ten thousand times."

Xanthe stirred. Flickered. For one horrible moment the child went utterly still, and the ember-presence guttered down to almost nothing, and Petra's arms tightened and her whole body said *no* in a silence louder than any sound.

Then Xanthe breathed. A real breath, deep and ragged, and their eyes opened all the way—gold as the unnamed color, gold as morning on salt—and fixed on Petra's face with an expression of ferocious, ancient, entirely childish determination.

"*Stay*," Xanthe said. In the common tongue. Their first word in it, maybe. A command, or a question, or both.

"I'm staying," Petra said. "We're staying. Both of us."

Behind them, from the dark of the split mountain, came a sound none of them expected. Soft. Rising. The freed souls had not all gone. Some had lingered in the deep places, gathering at the broken gates, and now they came drifting up the last stretch of tunnel toward the open air—not fleeing anymore, but *emerging*, tentatively, the way birds return to a wood after a fire. They spread out across the twilight hillside, dozens of pale lights settling into the grass, into the heather, hovering over the stream that ran down toward Graywater. Not departing. Waiting, perhaps. Or watching. Or simply learning, as Xanthe was learning, what daylight cost and what it gave.

One light drifted near, and Ossian caught the ghost of the dead tongue in its shimmer, and translated without thinking, aloud, because it seemed important that the living hear it:

*"We will follow the ember,"* it said. *"Wherever it wakes."*

The lights pulsed once, together, like a congregation saying amen.

And high on the mountainside, in the ruin of the door that had been Petra's door, something answered them—a faint warmth in the stone, a pulse, a heartbeat where there had been hollow silence for a hundred days and a thousand years. Ossian felt it through his knees where he knelt. So did the lights. So, by the look on her face, did Petra.

Vessel-of-Salt was not reborn. Not yet. But somewhere between a mercenary's blood and a child's courage, an ember had been spent saving everything, and embers, Ossian knew—he had been a priest, before he was a bad one—embers do not understand annihilation. They only understand waiting.

Xanthe's hand found Petra's collarbone and closed around it, grip fierce despite the frailty, and the child's eyes drifted shut again, and this time the flickering slowed, steadied, found something like a rhythm.

"They'll need bread," Ferro announced suddenly, sitting up, rain-soaked and certain. "When they wake. Everyone wakes up wanting bread. I'm sure of it. I'm sure I'm sure of it." He frowned, trying to find the memory that wasn't there. "I think somebody used to make it for me."

"We'll make it together," Ossian said, and found that he meant it as a vow.

Below them the valley darkened toward night, and the first star came out over Thornfell, and the mountain behind them exhaled its last long breath of salt into the open air—and began, very quietly, in the deep places where the ledger had been, to dream of something new.
