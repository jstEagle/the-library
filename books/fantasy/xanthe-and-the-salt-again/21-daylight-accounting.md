## 21. Daylight Accounting

The door in the mountain had been Petra's idea, though Ferro did most of the actual carpentry, and Ossian blessed it in a language that was half liturgy and half apology.

It stood where the old Delve gate at Graywater had been sealed—no longer sealed now, but framed in fresh pine and iron-banded oak, with a proper latch instead of a salt-formed lock that wept brine when you touched it. The new door opened outward, onto the morning, which was the whole point. Everything about the Undercourt had opened inward.

Petra stood on the threshold with her hands in her pockets and watched the light come up over the valley. Three weeks since the collapse. Twenty-two days since she had walked out of a dying mountain carrying a child who weighed almost nothing and burned like a banked fire. She still counted the days. She suspected she would count them for years, the way soldiers counted campaigns, or debtors counted installments—except this tally ran backward, away from what was owed, toward what remained.

Behind her, inside the doorway's shallow vestibule, Ferro was learning to make bread.

She could hear him: the wet slap of dough being worked too hard, his muttered curses, Xanthe's thin laugh when the loaf-shaped thing collapsed for the fourth time. Ferro had come back from the deep roads without his memories of the surface—the price of reopening the sealed gate, paid in full to whatever ledger-thing governed such payments—and so he was, in every way that mattered, a man meeting the world again for the first time. He remembered how to fight, how to navigate by starlight, how to hold his tongue in a hostile room. He did not remember rain. He did not remember bread.

The first time it had rained after their ascent, he'd stood out in the yard of the inn at Graywater for two hours, arms at his sides, letting it hit him, while the rest of them huddled under the eaves pretending not to stare. When he finally came in, soaked through, he'd said only: *It falls from everywhere. That's not sensible.* And then he'd asked if it would happen again soon, in a voice carefully stripped of hope, and Petra had told him yes, usually, in this country, more often than anyone wanted.

He'd smiled then. It had looked strange on his face, unpracticed, like a sword hung on a wall by someone who'd never seen one used.

* * *

Petra left the doorway and walked down to the stream, where Marrow-Vane kept his fire.

He had built a low stone hearth on the bank, well away from the house, because no one—including himself—wanted the smoke of burning ledgers drifting through the windows. He fed pages into it one at a time, unhurried, reading each name aloud before he let it go. It was not penance exactly. He had said as much, that first night, in his precise collector's cadence: *I am not seeking absolution. Absolution is not mine to seek; it belongs to the ones whose names these are. I am seeking correction. Correction is slower.*

Three weeks of slow correction. There were nine ledgers. Nine hundred and forty-one names, each one a soul sold or seized or signed away under the Undercourt's law—a law that Marrow-Vane knew better than any living creature, having enforced it for two hundred years.

"Montrose." He didn't look up. "You're hovering."

"I'm supervising."

"You're hovering. There is a distinction." He turned a page. The firelight caught the salt-white scar tissue where his hand had been marked—the collector's brand, which had gone dark and inert the night the contracts dissolved, like a coal doused in its own ash. "This one is a miller's daughter from Harrow Fen. Sold in 1141 by an uncle with gambling debts. Her line reads *collected*." His jaw tightened, the smallest crack in the courtesy. "I collected her myself. She was nineteen."

"What happens to them now?" Petra asked, though she'd asked before. She asked most mornings. It wasn't the answer she needed; it was the ritual.

"The same thing that happened to all of them when the court fell. They went where souls go when nothing holds them." He fed the page to the fire. "Upward, mostly. Some are choosing to stay near their families. One or two, I'm told, have taken up residence in a shrine-chamber that used to belong to a god called Vessel-of-Salt." His eyes flicked up, dry and unreadable. "Your god appears to be running a boardinghouse."

"It was never my god's style to turn people away at the door."

"No. I've read the oaths. Terribly generous terms. No wonder the Underking ate it." He returned to the ledger. "There's bread burning, by the way. Or something adjacent to bread."

* * *

Something adjacent to bread sat cooling on the windowsill when Petra came back up the path—dark on one side, pale on the other, dense as a paving stone. Ferro stood over it with flour to his elbows and an expression of profound grievance.

"The instructions say to knead until smooth," he said.

"They do."

"It was smooth. Then it stopped being smooth. Nothing about this process respects effort." He looked at the loaf the way he'd once looked at enemy fortifications: assessing weaknesses, planning an approach. "Xanthe laughed at me."

"Xanthe laughs at everyone. It's how they pray, I think." Petra tore off a corner of the loaf and chewed. It was terrible. It was also the first thing Ferro had made that held together at all. "Better," she said, and meant both things.

Ossian appeared in the doorway behind them, ink-stained to the wrist, a sheaf of papers under one arm. The apostate priest had gone gray at the temples in three weeks—not from hardship but from writing. Every waking hour he spent at the table by the window, drafting, crossing out, drafting again. A new liturgy. Not for Vessel-of-Salt, exactly—he was careful about that, insisting the old forms belonged to the dead tongue and couldn't simply be translated, only re-grown—but for the small waking thing that lived behind Xanthe's eyes. For the ember.

"Read me today's attempt," Petra said.

He cleared his throat. He always cleared his throat first, as though addressing a congregation, even when the congregation was Petra holding bad bread.

"*Light that was taken,*" he recited, "*be light that is given. We do not ask you back into our mouths, our marks, our ledgers. We ask you only to wake slowly, and be warm, and want nothing from us that we do not also want for ourselves.*"

Silence. Somewhere down the slope, Marrow-Vane read another name to the fire.

"That last part," Petra said. "*Want nothing from us.*"

"Yes?"

"Keep that part. Whatever else you cut, keep that."

Ossian looked at her for a long moment, and then at the papers in his hands, and something moved across his face—the expression of a man who had spent thirty years serving gods with appetites and had only recently learned that worship could be shaped otherwise. "That part stays," he agreed. "That part might be the whole liturgy."

* * *

Xanthe slept in the sun.

They had made a bed for the child on the flat stone by the south wall, where the afternoon light pooled deepest, because the ember ran low these days and sunlight seemed to feed it—or comfort it; Ossian and Petra disagreed gently about which—and Xanthe slept ten hours a day now, sometimes twelve, curled small with their salt-white coin tucked in one fist.

Petra found them there past midday, breathing slow, the gold-pale of their eyes hidden, their skin less gray than it had been in the deep roads but still carrying that faint translucence, that suggestion of a person seen through paper. The unraveling had slowed. It had not stopped. Some mornings Xanthe's fingertips went faintly transparent, and Petra would sit very still and hold the small hand and think about ledgers, about lines not yet crossed out, about debts the mountain might still consider outstanding.

But today the child's fingers were opaque. Today the coin in their fist had lost none of its white. Petra eased herself down onto the stone beside them and let the sun work on her own shoulders, her scars, the oath-mark on her forearm that no longer burned black but hadn't returned to its old color either—it was simply quiet now, a mark on skin, waiting to see what it would become.

"You're watching me sleep again," Xanthe said, without opening their eyes. Their voice was better than it had been—stronger, less like wind through a flooded gallery. They spoke the common tongue now more often than the dead language, though they still dreamed aloud in the old words, and Ossian wrote the dreams down, and some of the dreams, he said quietly, were not dreams at all but reports from below. From the freed souls. From whatever the Undercourt became when its king dissolved into salt water and legal fiction.

"Someone has to," Petra said. "You wander off in your sleep. Last week Ferro found you halfway to the stream, walking with your eyes shut, singing."

"I wasn't singing. I was counting."

"Counting what?"

Xanthe opened their eyes then, gold-pale in the sun, and considered the question with the terrible seriousness the child brought to everything. "Doors," they said finally. "There used to be ten thousand doors under the mountain. Now there's one." A pause. "Yours. The one Ferro built. It has a crooked hinge."

"It does not."

"It leans. Like it's listening." Xanthe sat up, blinking sleep away, and leaned their small shoulder against Petra's arm—an easy, unremarkable gesture that still caught in Petra's chest every single time, because for weeks after the escape the child had flinched from touch, had slept against walls facing the door, had treated kindness as an unfamiliar currency and checked it for counterfeit. "Sela."

Petra went still.

The drowned children in the flooded gallery had called her that—*Sela, Sela*—the childhood name she'd buried so deep she'd needed ghosts to dig it up. But Xanthe used it differently. The ghosts had used it like an accusation, or a wound. Xanthe used it the way you use a key you know fits.

"What is it?" Petra said.

"The ones below sent word up with me while I slept. Through the doors that aren't doors anymore." Xanthe's fingers found the salt-white coin, turned it over and over. "They say the mountain isn't empty. The Underking is gone, and the court is fallen, and the contracts are ash—I saw Marrow-Vane's fire in a dream, all nine books—and the tithe-souls went up like birds. But the mountain itself." The child frowned. "Mountains don't die fast. Mountains take centuries. And underneath everything, under where the throne was, under where the god was eaten—there's something that was never on any ledger. Something the Underking didn't make and didn't eat and didn't sign."

The sun felt suddenly less warm.

"Did your liturgy cover this?" Petra called toward the window, not taking her eyes off the child.

Ossian appeared, papers in hand. "What did the dream say?"

Xanthe recited it in the dead tongue first—soft, sibilant, the language of the salt courts rolling out of a small mouth like water finding an old channel—and then translated, word by careful word, the way Ossian had taught them:

*"What was swallowed was not the only thing in the dark. The swallower had a swallower. It sleeps where the ledger began. It is dreaming of names."*

Nobody spoke. Down at the stream, faintly, Marrow-Vane's voice rose and fell, reading another name to the fire, correcting the world one page at a time.

Petra looked at the door Ferro had built—the crooked-hinged, listening door, the one door in a mountain that used to have ten thousand—and understood that quiet repair was not the same as finished repair. Accounts, she thought, were never truly settled. They were only ever paused, the way a breath pauses, the way a door stands open between one room and the next.

"All right," she said, to Xanthe, to the mountain, to whatever was dreaming of names beneath the place where the ledger began. "Then we start a new page."

Xanthe smiled—small, gold-eyed, alive—and took her hand.
