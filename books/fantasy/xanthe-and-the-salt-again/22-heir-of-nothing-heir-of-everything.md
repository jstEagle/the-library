## 22. Heir of Nothing, Heir of Everything

The door in the mountain had been rebuilt by hand, and it showed.

Petra ran her thumb along the new hinges—Ferro's work, though Ferro himself would not have known it was his own craft until someone told him, and no one liked to tell him things anymore unless he asked. He'd fitted the iron with a smith's patience and then stood back from it for a long time, frowning at it the way he now frowned at everything: a man reading a letter in his own handwriting that he could not remember writing.

"Good hinges," Petra had said.

"What are they for?" Ferro asked.

"Keeping the weather honest."

He'd nodded, satisfied, and gone back to his forge, where he was relearning the names of metals one slow afternoon at a time. Rain he remembered. He had wept at rain the first week, standing out in it with his face tipped up like a man hearing a voice he'd thought lost. Bread he was still working on. He held each loaf a long while before eating, as though waiting for it to say something familiar.

Petra left him to it and climbed the switchback path to the door with an armful of salt-white candles she did not strictly need anymore. Habit, Ossian called it. Liturgy, Marrow-Vane said, with the faint disapproval of a man who had spent three weeks burning ledgers and still flinched at ritual. But Xanthe liked candles. Xanthe liked anything that burned.

The child was already at the door when she reached it, sitting cross-legged on the threshold stone with their chin on their fists, watching the light come down the valley in long gold slabs. Their eyes caught it and threw it back amber—the color Petra had named them for, back when a name had been a rope thrown to something drowning. The ember behind those eyes was dim these days. A coal banked under ash, not a fire. But it turned toward her when she came near, the way a sleeping animal turns toward warmth, and Xanthe smiled without moving anything but their mouth.

"You're late," they said. Their Common was good now. Better than hers, Ossian joked, which wasn't a joke; the child had swallowed languages whole the way they seemed to swallow everything, sunlight, stories, silence.

"I brought candles."

"We don't need candles."

"I know."

Xanthe considered this, then took them anyway, tucking the bundle under one arm with the gravity of a magistrate accepting evidence. "Ossian says today's the day."

"Ossian says a lot of things."

"He says you've been avoiding it for nine days." Xanthe looked up at her, and there it was—that terrible, guileless precision the child wielded like a blade they didn't know was sharp. "He says you're afraid."

* * *

She had been afraid. She could admit it now, up here, where the only witnesses were the wind and the small waking god disguised as a ten-year-old.

Not of the ceremony itself. Ossian had drafted it in his careful apostate hand, a liturgy with no god left to receive it—or so he'd thought, until the night he'd read the first line aloud in the rebuilt shrine-chamber and the ember in Xanthe's chest had flared so bright the shadows jumped, and the old priest had sat down hard on the floor and laughed until he cried. There was a god to receive it after all. Small. Waking. Living in a child who still sometimes spoke the dead tongue of the salt courts in their sleep, saying words that made the candle flames stand up straight and attentive.

What Petra feared was simpler and worse: that she would get the words wrong. That naming an heir was a thing done once, formally, forever, and if she stumbled—if some clause went unspoken, some old law unsatisfied—the mountain would notice. The mountain noticed everything now. It had been exhaling for weeks, a long slow sigh through vents and fissures, the sound of a court dissolving, of contracts coming loose like stitches cut from cloth. Somewhere far below, freed souls were still streaming upward along roads that no longer led anywhere but out.

Marrow-Vane said the exodus would take years. Marrow-Vane had appointed himself its clerk, which Petra suspected was his way of doing penance without calling it that. He kept lists of the rising souls. He checked them off. He did not ask forgiveness, and no one offered it, and somehow that arrangement worked better than absolution would have.

Nine days she had put it off. Nine days of errands invented, of routes scouted twice, of checking Ferro's hinges and Ossian's ink supply and the state of the winter stores, all of it transparent, all of it seen through by everyone, most of all by the child who waited every morning on the threshold stone with their chin on their fists.

"Well?" Xanthe said now. "Are you?"

"Afraid," Petra said. "Yes."

Xanthe nodded, satisfied, as though fear were a toll paid and the road now open. They stood, brushed off their knees—a gesture learned from her, Petra realized, one of a hundred small thefts—and pushed open the door she had built with her own hands over a childhood she had buried beneath it.

Inside, the shrine-chamber smelled of wax and cold stone and, faintly, of salt. It would always smell of salt. Some things you could rebuild and some things you could only clean.

* * *

They had set it simply. Ossian had wanted banners; Petra had refused; compromise was two candles and the desecrated altar-stone, righted, scrubbed, its scars left visible. Vessel-of-Salt's name was still gouged across it in the Underking's script, the wound the god had died of. Petra had thought about chiseling it smooth. She hadn't. Let it stay. Let anyone who came after know what had been taken, and what had been given back.

Ossian stood at the altar in his threadbare vestments, the ones he'd worn the day they met, because he insisted gods remembered clothes even when priests didn't. Ferro stood beside him, holding the candle he'd been handed and not knowing why, patient as timber. Marrow-Vane stood apart, at the edge of the light, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man attending a funeral for someone he'd helped kill.

And Xanthe stood before the altar, barefoot on cold stone, looking smaller than Petra had ever seen them and somehow more solid too.

"The rite is old," Ossian said. His voice filled the chamber the way water fills a cup—gently, completely. "Older than my order. Older than the Delve. In the beginning it was how a god chose a servant, and later how a servant chose a successor, and somewhere in between, men twisted it into a ledger-line and a price." He looked at Petra. "We are going to use it the way it was meant to be used. Which means, Petra Montrose, that you must speak plainly. No mercenary's hedges. No smuggler's fine print. The god will know. The god always knew with you."

Petra stepped forward.

Her oath-mark had been black since the shrine below—the brand on her forearm where Vessel-of-Salt had once sealed her service, seared dark the day she learned her god was dead, or eaten, or both. It had stayed black through the Unsigning, through the collapse, through the long climb into daylight with a flickering child in her arms. She had assumed it was scar now. Dead tissue. A relic.

As she opened her mouth to speak, it warmed.

Not burned. Warmed—like a stone held too long in the sun, like bread against a chest, like a hand closing around another hand. And in the warmth she felt something she had not felt in four hundred days of silence and prayer: attention. Not the vast indifferent weight of the old god. Something smaller. Sleepier. Leaning toward her the way a child leans toward a window when someone they love comes up the road.

*You,* it seemed to say. *I remember you.*

She almost lost the words. She gripped them the way she'd gripped the ledge on the Weeping Stair, and she spoke.

"I found you dead," she said. "Or I found what was left of you, in a child, in the dark, in the house of the thing that ate you. I am not asking you to come back to me. I am telling you what I'm giving you instead." She knelt—not to the altar, not to the ember, but to Xanthe, so their eyes were level, amber to grey. "I give you my heir. Not of blood. Not of property. I have little enough of either, and less worth having. I give you the oath itself. What I swore to Vessel-of-Salt, I swear now to the vessel it lives in: to keep, to carry, to answer when called, to walk whatever road leads to you, above or below, for as long as I have legs to walk it. My life for your keeping. My death for your inheritance. Spoken plain. No fine print."

The ember flared.

It was not dramatic—it was nothing like the white fury of the throne vault, nothing like the shattering of the Underking's claim. It was small and total, a lamp being lit in a room you hadn't known was dark. The warmth in Petra's arm spread to her shoulder, her chest, her throat, and the black of the oath-mark broke apart like river ice in spring, and underneath it the mark was red again. New. Wet-looking. A wound that had decided to be a promise instead.

Xanthe made a sound—half laugh, half sob, wholly a child's—and threw themselves forward, and Petra caught them, and for a while the rite stopped being a rite and became just two people holding on in a cold stone room while an old priest wept openly and a memoryless man watched his candle flame bend toward them though there was no draft, and a debt-collector stood at the edge of the light with his head bowed, checking off, perhaps, the last item on the longest ledger he had ever kept.

When Petra finally pulled back, Xanthe's eyes were wet and luminous, and the ember behind them glowed steady as a harbor light.

"So it's done," Xanthe said. "I'm your heir."

"You're my heir."

"Of what, exactly? You said yourself you have nothing."

Petra grinned. It hurt her face; she'd been doing it too rarely. "Of everything. That's the trick of it. Nothing and everything are the same size, if you hold them right."

* * *

They walked the smuggler's road the next morning, because Xanthe asked to, because the child had spent a year underground and another year convalescing and had decided, with the imperiousness of the recently rescued, that they wanted to see where the roads went. All of them. Upward preferably.

Ferro came as far as the first mile marker and stopped, frowning at the valley as though trying to read a word just past the edge of the page. "I'll mind the forge," he said. "Someone should be here when you get back." Then, quieter, with the bewilderment that never left him: "Will you get back?"

"Always," Petra said. "It's in the contract."

Ossian declined the road for the study—he was writing, he said, a new liturgy, one that began with *we were wrong* and ended with *thank you*, and it was taking some doing to bridge the distance between. Marrow-Vane came to the door to see them off and pressed a folded paper into Petra's hand: the final page of the great ledger, the one bearing her original entry. Sold at age six. Never collected. Across it, in his precise clerk's hand, was written a single word: *VOID*, and beneath that, in smaller letters: *with prejudice, and with respect.*

"Keep it or burn it," he said. "I find I can't. It seems important that someone who isn't ashamed of it do the deciding."

Petra tucked it inside her coat, against the warm place where her oath-mark lived. She'd decide later. Or she wouldn't. Some papers earned the right to just exist.

The morning was cold and clean. Frost silvered the gorse. Somewhere downslope, Ferro's hammer rang against steel, learning its trade again stroke by stroke, and the sound followed them up the switchbacks like a heartbeat keeping honest time.

Xanthe walked ahead, the way children do when they trust the adult behind them absolutely—which was, Petra thought, the true measure of the inheritance, more than any rite or mark or word spoken plain. The child carried the candle stubs from yesterday in one pocket and a handful of graywater pebbles in the other, and hummed snatches of the dead tongue as they climbed, except that the songs weren't dead anymore. They'd changed. Ossian had heard it first and gone pale with joy: the salt-court melodies, unwound from their contracts, turning into something freer, folk-shaped, the kind of tune you'd catch a shepherd singing without knowing where it came from.

"Where does the road go?" Xanthe called back, not slowing.

"Up," Petra said. "Then over. Then down into the next valley, and up again. That's roads for you."

"And after that?"

"After that there's another road."

Xanthe stopped at the crest of the pass and stood silhouetted against a sky the color of a struck flint, wind pulling at their hair, and for a moment Petra saw both things at once, the way she supposed she always would now: the feral creature at her door caked in mud and speaking the language of the drowned, and this—this upright, sunlit, impossible person, heir of nothing, heir of everything, with a small god banked warm in their chest and the whole unwritten map of the world tilted downhill before them.

"There are people down there," Xanthe said, squinting at the far valley. Smoke rose from chimneys. Fields lay stitched in green and brown. "People who sold children. People whose grandparents signed things."

"Yes," Petra said.

"Is the court really gone? All of it? Every copy, every ledger, every habit in people's heads?"

"Marrow-Vane says habits outlast courts. He'd know."

Xanthe was quiet a moment. The wind moved. Below them, faint and far, a bell in some village tower rang the hour, and the ember behind the child's eyes flickered—listening, cataloguing, awake.

"Then we'll need to walk a lot of roads," Xanthe said at last, and started down the far side without waiting, certain as sunrise that the person behind them would follow.

Petra touched the warm mark beneath her coat, once, the way you tap a compass to settle the needle. Then she followed her heir downward, into the morning, onto the long road that went everywhere.
