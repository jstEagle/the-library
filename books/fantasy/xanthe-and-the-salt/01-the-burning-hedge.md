## 1. The Burning Hedge

Ines Eriksen had learned to read fire the way other boys learned to read letters, and the flames climbing Master Corvus's cottage told him everything he needed to know: that the soldiers had come before dawn, that they had used oil, that they had not troubled to knock.

He lay flat on the smuggler's ridge above the valley, chin in the frost-brittle grass, and watched the only home he had ever known turn into a lantern against the dark. The hedge his master had tended for thirty years — the salt-thorn hedge that whispered warnings when strangers crossed it — was burning too. It burned green and screaming, and Ines pressed his palms over his ears as though that could stop him hearing it.

It couldn't. Nothing could. He heard everything. That was the whole of his miserable gift: he heard what things meant to say. The hedge's dying cry was a name repeated over and over — *Corvus, Corvus* — because the hedge had loved the old man best of anyone alive, and now both of them were going together into the smoke.

*Don't watch,* said the sensible part of him, the part that sounded like his mother, dead nine years and still lecturing from wherever mothers go. *Run.*

But he watched. Grief deserved at least that much attention. Somewhere down there, under the collapsing roof or already marched away in irons, was the man who had found a starving eleven-year-old with too-big ears and instead of selling him to the crown's tithe-collectors had fed him stew and asked, mildly, "And what do the stones say tonight, boy?" Master Corvus had never once called his gift small. Everyone else did. Small gift, small use, small worth — a hearth-listener, a whisper-hearer, nothing like the storm-callers and gate-openers the ballads sang about. But Corvus had said, "The realm is built on small magics, boy. Big ones just break things faster."

Now the big magic of the crown had come with forty soldiers and a writ bearing the new seal, and it had broken everything there was to break.

The purge had been announced six weeks ago. *An edict for the cleansing of unlicensed craft.* As if any hedge-witch or stone-whisperer in the Marchlands had ever held a license; licenses cost more than houses. The gifted were to be registered, tithed, or burned, and the order of those options depended entirely on how cooperative you looked when the witchfinders knocked. Corvus had laughed when he read the broadsheet, which Ines should have understood then was an old man's way of writing his own ending.

Smoke rolled up the valley and washed over the ridge. It smelled of rosemary and charred wool and underneath it something sweeter, wrong, that Ines refused to name. His eyes watered. He let them.

Then one of the soldiers shouted, and pointed up the slope, and the morning changed shape forever.

Ines didn't think. Thinking was for people whose bodies weren't already moving. He rolled left off the ridge-top, down the goat-track side where the gorse grew thick, and behind him boots hit the scree and voices tangled — *there, on the ridge, the boy, the boy!* — and somewhere in the shouting someone said a word that made his blood go to ice water:

*Listener.*

They knew. Of course they knew. Corvus had been watched for months; watchers would have counted heads, and two heads lived in that cottage, and one head was currently scrambling down a hillside with his heart hammering loud enough, he was sure, to be heard three valleys over.

Gravel took his feet out from under him. He slid ten yards on his back, grimoire bag jammed hard against his chest — he'd grabbed it from the loose board under the floor two nights ago, on a hunch he hadn't examined, on a whisper from the house itself that had said, in its creaking timber voice, *take it, take it, soon* — and came to rest in a tangle of bracken with the breath punched out of him.

Above, torchlight bobbed along the ridge. They'd split up. Two going around, two coming straight down.

Ines got up and ran.

He ran not toward the road, not toward the town of Marrow's Ford where a clever boy might lose himself in market crowds, but east, along the drover's path, the shepherd's cut, the ford below the standing stone — the roads his feet knew better than his own heartbeat. Because here was the thing nobody warned you about fear: it didn't make you stupid. It made you honest. And honestly, Ines Eriksen did not know how to be anywhere except the places Corvus had walked him. Every shortcut in his body had been learned holding the hem of that old grey coat. If he was going to survive, he would survive on borrowed roads, in a borrowed world, carrying a stolen book, answering — if he absolutely had to — to a name that felt suddenly like something he'd picked up secondhand.

*Ines Eriksen.* Who had given him that name? A mother who was ash now too, somewhere, long cold. He mouthed it as he ran and it tasted like someone else's bread.

* * *

The drover's path dropped through birch scrub and came out at the ford by midmorning. Ines crossed it twice, doubled back along the shingle, and finally allowed himself to stop under the leaning slab of the boundary stone, hands on knees, lungs full of knives.

The stone murmured to him. Old stones always did — this one had stood since before Vael had kings, and its voice was slow as glacier melt: *traveler, traveler, many pass, few return, the water remembers your master's master's master.* Comfort, of a sort. He pressed his palm flat against it anyway, and the stone hummed its ancient indifference, and Ines realized with a lurch that he was listening for one particular voice among all the world's chatter and it wasn't there anymore. No cottage-creak. No hedge-whisper. No dry, amused cough from a doorway, saying *you're late for chores.*

Silence, where a whole life had been.

He sat down in the shallows' shadow and opened the grimoire bag with shaking fingers, checking — third time that morning — that the book was whole. It was worse than whole. It was warm. Corvus's grimoire was a mean-looking thing, bound in grey hide, its spine cracked like river mud, and it had never liked Ines particularly; books have opinions, and this one had spent forty years being handled by a greater talent than his. But today its cover lay soft under his thumb, almost pliant, and when he bent close he caught a thread of meaning rising off the leather like heat off a stove:

*take me where I can't be found. take me where the crown can't smell me. take me —*

The rest dissolved. Small gifts gave you fragments; that was the tax on them. But the fragment was enough to make his stomach turn over. The book wanted hiding, and it had wanted hiding for days, and Corvus had died with it under a floorboard instead of in his hands, and Ines could not decide whether that was the old man's final kindness or his final cruelty.

Boots on gravel. Downstream.

Ines went still the way prey goes still — total, animal, every part of him folded into quiet — and listened past the water-sound, past the stone's drone, into the human noise coming around the bend. Two men. Heavy tread, one favoring his left knee. Chain mail under wool, the particular wet-leather squeak of a baldric. And words, carried thin across the water:

"—witchfinder wants him alive, mind. Listeners are useful. Burn the book if he's carrying one."

"Book's kindling either way. Boy's the prize."

Useful. The word sat in Ines like a swallowed hook. Not dangerous. Not damned. *Useful* — the crown had categories now, and small gifts weren't for pyres but for harnesses, and somehow that was worse. He thought of Odile— no. He didn't know any smugglers. He knew the *idea* of smugglers, the way every Marchland child did: the caravan trains that moved along the salt roads at night, running untaxed tin and unregistered goods and, rumor insisted, unregistered people. People who didn't want to be found. He knew where they watered, because Corvus had traded herb-cures to their crews for years and had taken Ines along twice, saying only, *learn faces, boy, someday faces will matter more than spells.*

Faces. Yes. He could find a face he half remembered.

The soldiers' voices swung nearer. Ines closed the bag, rose soundless from the stone's shadow, and slipped upstream along the willow line while the water covered his steps, and he did not look back at the smoke, and he did not answer the boundary stone when it called *traveler, traveler, come again* after him like an old friend who would wait forever.

He wouldn't be back. There was no *back* anymore. There was only forward, along the only roads he knew, toward the one place in the Marchlands where a hunted thing might vanish: the salt road, and the caravans that walked it.

* * *

He found them at dusk, or rather their fires found him first — three low flames in the hollow below the old tollhouse, dished so their light wouldn't carry. Ines lay in the bracken above and listened, because listening was the one weapon nobody could take from him.

Wagon-creak. Mules complaining in the flat tone of animals worked past patience. Human voices, maybe fifteen, none of them loud, all of them rough-edged and careful — the speech of people accustomed to not being overheard. A woman's voice cut through it all, low and dry as whetstone on iron:

"Load the sealed cargo last. And if any of you touch that iron lantern, I'll take the hand."

Lantern. Iron. Sealed. The words snagged in Ines's ear and would not come free, and beneath them — faint, so faint he might have invented it, the way grief invents voices — he heard something else. Something from the hollow below. A humming. Thin and high and wrong, like a struck wineglass that refuses to finish its note, and threaded through the hum, barely, a voice.

A child's voice.

*Hello? Is someone there? Can anyone hear me?*

Ines's blood went cold and stayed cold. Below, the woman — the captain, he supposed, the whetstone voice — barked orders about dawn departure and salt-gate passes, and her crew moved to obey, and the little voice in the iron went on calling into the dark with the terrible patience of someone who has called for a very long time and expects, now, nothing.

Nobody answered it. Nobody else could hear it. That was the horror of his gift laid suddenly bare: the whole world talking all at once, and him the only soul in the Marchlands listening to a boy who was not a boy anymore but a voice in a sealed box, sold or stolen or worse, riding in a smuggler's wagon toward he didn't know what.

Ines Eriksen — or whoever he was now, whatever remained of the name — got slowly to his feet in the bracken and began to walk down toward the fires with his empty hands visible and his dead master's book hidden under his coat, knowing only this: he had nowhere else to go, and something down there needed hearing.

"Who's that?" The captain's voice cracked across the hollow like a whip. Firelight swung toward him. Fifteen hard faces turned, and Ines stopped at the edge of the light, smoke-stained, ash in his hair, and heard himself say in a voice scraped raw:

"My master's cottage burned this morning. I need passage on the salt road." A pause. "I can pay in work."

The captain studied him with eyes like coins, and behind her, deep in the lead-sealed wagon, the small voice hummed on — and Ines knew, with the certainty of the stones themselves, that his life had just changed wagons.
