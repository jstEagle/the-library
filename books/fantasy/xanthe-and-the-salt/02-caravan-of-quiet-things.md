## 2. Caravan of Quiet Things

The smugglers camped in a hollow below the ridge where the road bent toward the sea, and Ines watched them for two days before he worked up the nerve to walk down.

They were not what he had imagined. He'd expected cutthroats, men with knives and no eyes for anything but coin. Instead he found a woman mending a net with fingers gone silver from salt, a boy barely older than himself arguing with a mule, an old man asleep sitting up with his hat over his face as though the world were not worth the trouble of lying down for it. A cookfire burned low. Someone was humming.

He counted eleven of them, and every one of them moved like people who had learned to be quiet. Not sneaking-quiet — hunted-quiet. The particular stillness of those who had practiced being unremarkable until it became their nature. He knew that stillness. He had worn it for six years in Master Corvan's cottage, keeping his hands empty whenever riders passed on the road below.

The woman with the net looked up first.

"You've been on the ridge since yesterday," she said. Not accusing. Just stating it, the way you'd note the weather. "You should have come down sooner. It's going to rain."

"I don't have much money," Ines said.

"Most people don't." She tied off a knot and studied him. She was perhaps forty, lean as a whip handle, with grey shot through her black hair and a scar that split one eyebrow cleanly in two. "What's your trade?"

"I can read," he said. "I can count. I can drive a cart."

"Can you keep your mouth shut?"

"Yes."

"We'll see." She jerked her chin toward the fire. "Sit. Eat something. The captain will want to look at you before anyone promises anything."

He sat. The stew was thin but hot, and no one asked him questions while he ate, which was its own kind of mercy. The boy with the mule — Doss, someone called him — slid a hunk of bread onto Ines's knee without comment and went back to his argument, which the mule appeared to be winning.

It was only later, when the light had gone blue and the camp settled into itself, that the captain came to look at him.

She came out of the largest wagon like weather arriving: a broad woman with iron-grey hair cropped short and a coat that had been fine once and was now something better, because it had been lived in. Her eyes were pale and flat as a winter sky, and they went over Ines the way a buyer goes over livestock — hands, teeth, the set of his shoulders, the way he stood when he was frightened.

"Ines Eriksen," he said, when she asked. And then, because the name felt strange in his mouth, like a coat that no longer fit: "I'm looking for passage south."

"South is where everyone's looking to go." She crouched across the fire from him, elbows on her knees. "Odile Vane. This is my caravan. We carry quiet things for people who pay us not to ask questions. You understand the arrangement?"

"I understand it."

"Then here's mine. Ten copper a head gets you a place under canvas and two meals. You work. You don't ask what's in the wagons. You don't sing, you don't draw attention, and if crown soldiers stop us, you're my cousin's boy from Harrow Fen, dumb as a post and twice as ugly. Can you be dumb?"

"I can be anything you need."

Something flickered behind her eyes at that — amusement, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "That," she said, standing, "is exactly the wrong answer. The right answer is *I can be dumb*. Anything else is ambition, and ambition gets people searched." She turned away, then paused. "Ten copper. Pay Brannick by morning. Welcome to the Caravan of Quiet Things, cousin."

* * *

For six years, Ines had been the only one of his kind he knew.

Master Corvan had been gifted too, but Corvan was old, and old gifts go strange and inward, all whispers and candle-smoke. There had been no one else. The purge had seen to that, even before the purge had a name — neighbors who stopped waving, a peddler who never came back down the valley, the year the church bells started ringing differently, a note underneath them like a warning hummed by someone who didn't know the words.

Now he walked among twelve people, and within three days he could see it everywhere.

He saw it in the way Marta, the net-mender, always knew when rain would come a full day early, and never said how. In the way Brannick, the quartermaster, could find any lost thing if you let him hold something it had touched — and how he did it casually, lazily, so it looked like luck. In Doss, whose arguments with animals seemed less one-sided than they appeared. In the old man with the hat, Yorven, who slept sitting up because, Ines eventually understood, he dreamed other people's dreams and preferred to do it awake, quietly, where he could put them down again.

None of them spoke of it. That was the rule beneath all the other rules, older than Odile's ten copper. They were a caravan of quiet things, and the quietest thing they carried was themselves.

Ines lay to all of them.

It wasn't even a hard lie. When Marta asked, on the fourth night, if his family had been farmers, he said yes, and described a farm he'd helped invent on the spot, and she nodded and told him about her brothers, and the lie sat there between them like a stone in the bread. When Doss showed him a trick — a pebble that jumped between his fingers when no one watched — Ines laughed and clapped and said he'd never seen anything like it, and the words tasted like ash, because he could have shown Doss three things finer than that before breakfast.

His gift was small. Everyone had always said so, and they'd meant it as comfort, and it had never once been comforting. A little warmth in his palms. A knack for finding things that wanted finding. The ability to make a candle burn steadier, or a dying fire hold on through the night. Master Corvan had called it *hearth-gift*, dismissively, the way you'd call a sparrow a lesser bird. Useless against soldiers. Worthless on the open market. Just enough to hang a man.

So he hid it. He buried it deep, the way he'd buried it in the cottage, and told himself the caravan was safer this way, that a small gift was a small risk, that silence was survival. All true things. None of them the whole truth.

The whole truth was that he liked them. He liked Marta's laugh and Doss's stubbornness and the way the whole crew moved together on the road like a single animal deciding where to step. For the first time in his life he was surrounded by people like him, and he spent every hour pretending to be something else, and the pretense was starting to feel less like armor and more like a wall he was building with his own hands.

* * *

The lantern found him on the fifth night.

He hadn't meant to be near the cargo. Odile's rules were clear — the wagons were closed territory — but a wheel-shackle had cracked outside the third wagon, and Brannick needed someone to hold the lamp while he hammered, and Ines was closest. So he stood in the dark beside the wagon's rear curtain, holding the light, while Brannick swore and swung his hammer.

The curtain had a gap in it. A hand's width, no more.

Through the gap he could see crates, rope, oilcloth bundles. And on a bracket bolted to the wagon's frame, wrapped in chains, hung a lantern of black iron.

It was sealed. That was the first thing he noticed — sealed the way nothing ordinary is sealed. No hinge, no door, no vent. The iron had been folded over the flame inside like a fist closing, and around the fold ran a band of script too worn to read, and wax the color of dried blood filled every seam. It was heavy with chains, and chained to the wagon frame besides, and none of that was what made Ines's breath go shallow.

What made his breath go shallow was that it *hummed*.

Not a sound. He realized that quickly, because Brannick heard nothing, and the mule tethered nearby heard nothing, and the night insects went on with their indifferent business. It was a hum in the register where his gift lived — the same place the hearth-warmth rose from, the same frequency as a candle flame leaning toward him. Something inside that lantern was burning. And whatever it was, it knew he was there.

The hum climbed when he leaned closer. Not louder. More *urgent*, the way a voice gets urgent when it has been calling for years and finally hears footsteps.

"*Eriksen.*" Brannick's hammer paused. "You're letting the wind at my lamp."

"Sorry." He stepped back. The hum fell, wounded, and Ines felt it fall like a hand letting go of his sleeve.

He finished the shift. He went to his bedroll. He told himself it was cargo — some stolen relic, some nobleman's toy, the sort of quiet thing Odile carried for people who paid well. He told himself it was none of his business, and that not-knowing was the whole point of this life he'd bought with ten copper.

He dreamed of the lantern anyway.

In the dream he stood in his master's cottage the night it burned, except the fire was inside the lantern now, all of it, every flame of it folded into the iron fist, and the cottage was dark and cold and whole and empty. And the hum was a voice, or almost a voice, thin as wire:

*—hello? hello? is someone—*

*—it's so small in here. why is it so small—*

*—I had a dog. I had a dog and I can't remember its name. I can remember the color of its ears but not its name—*

*—please. please, I'll be good, I just need someone to—*

He woke with his hands burning. Both palms, warm as fresh bread, glowing faintly in the dark of the tent, and he pressed them hard against the ground until the light died and the shaking stopped.

Across the camp, through the canvas wall, he could feel the lantern humming on. Patient. Terrible. Calling a name it couldn't remember, in a voice that sounded — he hated himself for noticing, and noticed anyway —

— like a child.

* * *

On the eighth morning, crossing the long flats where the road ran white with salt dust, Odile Vane rode her horse alongside him for a while without speaking. The crew had learned to read her silences; Ines was learning. This one had weight in it.

"You sleep badly," she said at last.

"The ground's hard."

"Mm. My sister used to say that." She looked ahead, at nothing. "She also talked in her sleep. Whole conversations. Names she shouldn't have known. I used to think it was funny." A pause, the length of a held breath. "The witchfinders didn't think it was funny."

The salt wind moved between them. Somewhere behind, Doss was singing very quietly, a song with no words in it, just notes, safe notes.

"They took her?" Ines asked, though he already knew.

"They took her. Eight years back. Paid the family a *consolation*, if you can believe it. A purse of silver and a letter with a seal on it." Odile's jaw worked once. "I keep the letter in my strongbox. I don't know why. Some things you carry because putting them down means they win."

"Why are you telling me this?"

She turned and looked at him then, and her pale eyes were the flattest he'd ever seen them, and somehow the saddest. "Because last night, when you thought everyone was asleep, you walked to the third wagon and stood by the curtain for a quarter hour, and whatever's in there sang to you loud enough that Yorven woke up with it in his head. And because you lied to me in Harrow Hollow, cousin, and you've been lying to me every day since, and I have let you. So far." She gathered the reins. "We'll talk again soon, Ines Eriksen. Or whoever you decide to be. Think fast."

She rode forward to the head of the column. Ines stood in the road in the salt wind, his hands cold despite everything, and behind him, in the third wagon, faint as a heartbeat, the lantern began to hum.
