## 3. What the Lantern Sings

The lantern hummed at night.

Not the way iron hums when a wagon rolls over frozen ruts, or the way a struck blade sings against a whetstone. This was lower, older — a sound Ines felt in his back teeth before he heard it, a vibration that traveled up through the deck planks of the *Quiet Mercy* and settled somewhere behind his sternum like a second heartbeat that wasn't his.

He told himself it was the cargo shifting. Salt blocks settling. The river current working against the hull. He told himself this for six nights, lying in the narrow bunk he'd bought with three silver pieces and a lie about his name, and on the seventh night he stopped telling himself anything and went down to the hold.

Odile Vane's crew slept hard after a run — Brannick snoring in the rigging hammock, the twins curled together like commas, old Sefa muttering tide-prayers into her collar. No one stirred as Ines slipped past them, barefoot, his gift folded up small inside him the way Master Corvin had taught him: *a candle cupped in two hands, boy. The wind is always looking for it.*

The hold smelled of tar, wet rope, and salt — not sea salt but something whiter, drier, a salt that had never touched water. The lantern sat amidships in a crate lined with wool and lead sheeting, and even before he knelt beside it, the hum climbed up through his knees and made his vision swim.

Up close, it was ugly. A squat iron thing the size of a man's head, banded shut with seven seals, each stamped with the crowned sigil of Vael. No glass. No vent. Just iron, and beneath the iron, something that pressed outward the way a moth presses against a jar.

Ines should have walked away. Every instinct Corvin had drilled into him said: *what the crown seals, the crown means sealed; what hums for you alone is humming a trap.* But the sound was inside his teeth now, inside his ribs, and it had changed since the first night. It wasn't just a tone anymore.

It was a voice. And it was saying a name.

His name.

*Ines.*

He cupped his hands around the candle of himself and let the flame rise.

* * *

The world went white and then went cold, and then —

— a corridor hung with banners he half-recognized from coins and proclamations, the black stag of Vael on green silk, except the banners were burning at their edges and no one seemed to notice. Marble underfoot so polished it showed his reflection, except the reflection was wrong, too tall, wearing a crown —

— *no,* the reflection whispered, *that's Father's. I'm not allowed to wear it yet. Mother says when I'm nine. Mother says —*

— hands, enormous and gentle, lifting him onto a throne built for a man, and the throne was warm, which thrones are not supposed to be, warm like a hearthstone, warm like —

— *the fire is low this year,* someone said, a woman in gray, her face blurred as if seen through running water. *The dragon barely wakes. The realm will feel the winter in its bones, Highness. Something must burn in her place.*

— *something?*

— *someone.*

* * *

Ines came back to himself on his knees in the hold, cheek against the lead sheeting, tears freezing in the river wind that leaked through the cargo hatch. His gift guttered out of his hands like a pinched wick. He hadn't wept since the cottage burned. He hadn't had time.

*Aldric,* the lantern sang, when he could bear to open himself again. *My name is Aldric. Please. It's very dark in here. I've been counting the dark and I've lost count twice.*

A child. The enemy king — the boy whose face was stamped on every coin Ines had ever stolen or spent, the crown that had signed the purge, the writs that had put Master Corvin's cottage to the torch with Corvin possibly still inside it — was a child of perhaps eight years, sealed in iron, sold to keep a dragon warm.

Ines pressed his forehead against the crate and did not let himself think about whether this was treason. Thinking could come later. For now there was only listening.

* * *

— snow falling upward, which Aldric knew was impossible, but dreams were allowed impossibilities, and in the dream his mother was alive again and they were sledding down the White Stair behind the palace and she was laughing, really laughing, not the careful court laugh, and then the dream turned the way dreams do and the sled became an altar and the snow became salt and his mother's laugh became a magistrate's voice saying —

— *the contract is generous, Majesty Regent. The realm keeps its warmth. The boy keeps his body. Only the soul passes title, and souls, as the ancients wrote, are renewable —*

— *he's my son.*

— *he is also Vael's. We find the distinction comforting.*

— and then hands again, but these were not gentle, these were gloved and official, and there was a needle, and there was a sensation like being poured out of yourself through a hole too small, and the last thing Aldric saw with his own eyes was his uncle's face, turned away, studying a map, because his uncle could not watch, and Aldric had thought, with the strange clarity of the dying: *he'll be sorry later. I hope he'll be sorry later. I hope —*

* * *

Ines surfaced gasping, gripping the edge of the crate hard enough to splinter his nails. The hold tilted around him. Somewhere above, Brannick rolled over in his hammock and the ropes creaked, and the ordinary sounds of a smuggler's ship at anchor had never sounded so precious, so human, so far away.

They had poured him out. They had poured a child out of himself like decanting wine, and the wine fed a furnace, and the furnace warmed a realm that lit candles in its windows without ever asking what burned behind the mountain.

And the worst of it — the part that made Ines's stomach turn over — was that he understood the arithmetic. Corvin had understood it too, in his grimmer moods. *Every comfort eats something, boy. The bread eats the field. The kingdom eats the border villages. The question is only whether you look at the eating.* The realm was warm. Children in Vael's southern cities would not freeze this winter because a prince's soul burned in a salt-dark belly of the world. Ines had been warm all his life off fires he'd never examined.

But Corvin had never taught him that the eaten might sing. That it might know your name. That it might say, in a voice worn thin as communion paper:

*You're the one who hears me. Aren't you? You're the one who finally hears me.*

"I hear you," Ines whispered, and hated how his voice shook. "I'm nobody. I'm a smuggler's hired hand. I can't —"

*You opened the door. Nobody opens the door. The magistrates come and they take and they don't listen, they just feed me to her, and she doesn't mean to eat me, I don't think, she's mostly sleep now, but you listened.* A pause, and in the pause Ines felt the whole weight of the sealed dark pressing through the iron. *Are they going to sell me? The people on this boat? Odile Vane's people? I heard them talking. Rivals. Undermarket prices. I don't know what any of it means but I know it isn't good.*

Ines's jaw tightened. So the crew knew. Or Odile knew, at least, and the crew knew enough not to ask. He'd spent six nights wondering what cargo warranted lead sheeting and armed watches, and the answer had been sleeping thirty feet from his bunk, singing to him in the dark.

"They're going to sell you," he admitted, because lying to a soul in a jar seemed like a sin beyond even his capacity. "To someone worse, maybe. Or the same. I don't know."

Silence. Then, smaller:

*Is there anyone who wouldn't?*

Ines looked at his hands in the dark. Small gift, Corvin used to call it, half fond and half pitying — hedge-magic, hearth-magic, the kind that got gifted children mocked by their betters and murdered by their kings. He could coax a flame to bend. He could hear what iron dreamed. He could not fight, could not flee, could not buy his way past a single seal on a single lantern.

But he could hear. And hearing, it turned out, was a door.

"There might be," he said slowly. "There's a story my master told. About the court under Thornfell. Where they trade in memories." He swallowed. "About the dragon."

*Vess,* the soul said instantly, and the name came out with such naked love that Ines flinched. *Her name is Vess. She used to fly me over the bay when I was very small, before the fire got low. Before I was fuel. She called me her ember. She promised she'd wait for me.* The voice cracked like ice on a spring pond. *Does she still wait? Does she remember waiting?*

"I don't know," Ines said. And then, because the truth kept coming out of him like blood from a cut: "I think the fire is all she remembers now."

Another silence, longer. When the voice returned it had changed — steadied, the way a child goes terribly calm when crying stops being useful.

*Then someone has to go down and remind her. Someone has to open the lantern where she can see it happen, so she knows I didn't choose to leave her. Do you understand? It matters how it ends. It matters who's holding the light.*

Ines understood. God help him, he understood completely. Somewhere behind him lay a burned cottage and a master who might be ash, and ahead lay whatever Odile Vane intended to do with her cargo, and between those two points stood a boy in a lantern asking a stranger for one decent ending in a life that had been given none.

It was treason. Listening was treason now; mercy was treason; the crown had made compassion itself a hanging crime, and here Ines knelt in the belly of a smuggler's ship composing treasons like love letters.

"Someone has to steal you first," he said quietly. "From Odile. From the sale. From everyone."

*I know,* said Prince Aldric, last of his name, soul of Vael, eight years old and patient as the dead. *I've been thinking about that too. There's a seal — the fourth one, the one shaped like a keyhole. It's weaker than the others. It was weaker the day they closed it. I've been pushing at it for two years.*

"You've been —"

*I'm a prince,* the boy said, and for one moment the voice held something of banners and marble corridors and a mother laughing on a sled. *We're trained to look for weak points.*

Above them, boots crossed the deck. Once, twice. Paused directly overhead.

The hum of the lantern died to nothing, quick as a snuffed candle, and Ines froze with his gift still half-open, glowing faintly at his fingertips in the dark hold — glowing visibly, unmistakably, the way it only glowed when he forgot to hide it.

The boots descended the ladder.

* * *

He had time to close his fists, but not to stand, when Odile Vane dropped the last four rungs and landed soft as a cat on the hold floor, a shuttered lamp in one hand and a knife already loose in its sheath at her hip. Her eyes went from his face, to the crate, to his hands — still faintly luminous, still refusing, in their treacherous small way, to be ordinary.

"Well," she said, in the pleasant flat voice she used for pricing contraband and men. "So it's you the thing sings to."

She crouched across the crate from him, lamp raised, and studied him the way she'd study a map of a coastline — looking for the reef under the water.

"I paid for passage," Ines said. "Passage and silence."

"You paid for passage and ignorance, and you've just spent both." She set the lamp down between them, and its light caught the seven seals, and the fourth seal — the keyhole-shaped one — gleamed duller than the rest, worn, almost lovingly handled by some long-dead smith. "Here's what happens now, hedge-witch. You're going to tell me everything it told you. And then you and I are going to talk about price."

Behind the iron, so softly that only Ines could hear it, a child began to sing.
