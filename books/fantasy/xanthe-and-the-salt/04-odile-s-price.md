## 4. Odile's Price

Odile Vane had buried three captains, two husbands of convenience, and one sister, and she had learned from each loss the same lesson: grief was a luxury you paid for after the job was done.

She looked at Ines Eriksen now — this thin, salt-pale boy with his master's grimoire sewn into the lining of his coat like a second spine — and felt the old lesson strain against her ribs. Because there was a fourth seal on that lantern, keyhole-shaped, and she had spent eleven years learning what it opened, and every year of those eleven had been bought with someone else's blood or coin, and here was a hedge-witch who had cracked it by *humming*.

"Start talking," she said.

Ines talked. He was bad at lying to her — his eyes went left when he shaped the words small, went right when he made them grand, and when he told her he'd only touched the lantern once, by accident, in the dark, his hands said otherwise. Hands always did. His fingers kept moving in little circles at his sides, the way a man's do when they're still holding something invisible, still turning a latch.

She let him run through the whole coward's litany. *I didn't mean to. I heard nothing. It's just iron.* Then she reached across the crate and took his wrist, and pressed her thumb into the inside of it, where the pulse beat, and held it there until he stopped talking.

"You're going to tell me about the song," she said. "The one you've been hearing since Thornfell. The one you hum in your sleep, loud enough that Marrow asked me if we'd taken a new passenger."

His face did something complicated. Fear, mostly. But underneath the fear — and this was what made her stomach turn, because she recognized it, wore it herself some mornings — underneath was *wonder*, still alive, still stupid and hopeful, like a candle someone kept relighting just to watch the wind kill it again.

"There's a voice," he admitted. "A boy's voice. He says his name is Aldric."

The lantern sat between them on the crate, seven seals dark, and the hold around them creaked its slow wooden thoughts. Somewhere above, Brannic cursed at the mizzen rigging, and the sea slapped the hull in its patient rhythm, and none of it mattered, because Odile had just heard the name she'd paid eleven years to hear spoken aloud by someone other than herself.

"Aldric," she repeated. "Say the rest."

"He's cold," Ines said, and now the words came faster, tumbling, the way truth does once the dam breaks. "He says he's been cold for years and years but the fire is warm, and he doesn't understand why the fire is warm if he's cold. He remembers his mother's ring. He remembers a dog. He remembers being carried down stairs, a lot of stairs, and then —" The boy swallowed. "Then salt. He says everything after is salt."

Odile released his wrist. She walked to the stern window, where the grey water ran beside them, and she stood there long enough that he started to fidget, and she let him fidget. Let him sit in the quiet with the dead prince's song. It was a kindness, in its way. She'd have given anything, at fifteen, for someone to make her sit quietly with a hard thing instead of shoving her out a door into it.

"Mira was fifteen too," she said, not turning around. "My sister. You'll not have heard of her; nobody has. That's rather the point of what the court beneath Thornfell does." She watched her own reflection ghosted over the water. "They don't kill the gifted, hedge-witch. Killing is wasteful. They *buy*. A family falls behind on debt, or a village wants a well, or a lord wants a rival's land — and someone sells them a soul. Not the body. The body walks home and eats supper and never knows what's missing. Just feels dull after. Feels like weather. The soul goes under the mountain, into the salt, and the salt feeds it to the fire, and the fire keeps the realm warm, and everyone toasts the king and sleeps sound."

"The king," Ines said slowly. "The real king. The boy on the throne in Vael —"

"Is a coat on a hook," Odile said. "Warm meat. Whatever's left of Aldric Elandrian burns under Thornfell so that his own courtiers can wear his crown and call it regency. Eleven years now." She turned. "I've spent eleven years getting close enough to that lantern to sell it to people who can open it properly. And you stow your little gift aboard my ship like a stolen ham and crack it in a fortnight."

"I didn't crack it. I just — listened."

"That's worse." She crossed back to him, boots ringing on the deck planks, and leaned down until they were eye to eye. "Anyone can break a thing, boy. Listening is how courts fall."

* * *

They negotiated in her cabin, because she was not a fool, and witnesses were for people with nothing to lose.

The chart table lay between them, scattered with the false manifests she showed port inspectors: dye-wood, alum, salted herring. Under the manifests, the true cargo list, in her own hand, which no one living could read but her and Mira, and Mira was salt now.

"Here's the shape of it," Odile said. She poured two fingers of plum brandy into a cup and pushed it at him, mostly to see what he'd do with his hands while drinking. They went still. Good. Honest hands, at least, when they weren't working magic. "You're marked already. Don't argue — I saw what happened at the Salt Gate landing last month, when the toll-master's dog bit you and the dog came away whimpering with frost in its gums in high summer. Marrow saw too. Marrow talks when he drinks, and he drinks constantly, so consider yourself a secret with a short shelf life."

Ines set the cup down untouched. "What do you want?"

"Want?" She laughed, and it came out with an edge she hadn't meant to show. "I want my sister back, and she's a seasoning now. So I want the next best thing. That lantern goes to Kessarin — the glasswrights' conclave, across the strait. They've been buying royal souls for sixty years, piecemeal, trying to learn what the salt court knows about keeping fire. They'll pay a fortune. More than a fortune. They'll pay in favors, and favors are the only currency worth carrying past thirty." She spread her hands flat on the true manifest. "You're going to help me take it. Not deliver it — *take* it. There's a difference, and the difference is that taking means going down into Thornfell ourselves, through the mine-shafts, past the magistrates, and lifting the lantern off its altar before the court even knows it's gone."

"They keep it under a mountain full of salt-mages."

"They keep it under a mountain full of accountants," Odile said. "Salt doesn't fight, boy, it *trades*. Everything down there has a price. That's their strength and it will be our door." Her finger found the map, traced the black line of the abandoned shafts below Thornfell. "And here's the part you'll hate. We don't free the prince. We sell him. Whole, sealed, singing and all. The conclave pays more for a living flame than a dead one, and a freed soul pays nothing at all."

The silence that followed had weight. She watched him carry it, watched his shoulders take it like a load of wet canvas, and she thought: *there it is. There's the moment every one of them comes to. The moment the world asks whether you're a person or a tool, and shows you that tools eat better.*

"If you refuse," she said, gentler than she intended, "I put you ashore at Corvess with a letter to the witchfinder garrison and fifty crowns for my trouble. If you say yes, you get a share, passage anywhere in the known world, and a crew that won't burn you. Those are the terms. I'll not pretend they're kind ones. Kindness is for people who can afford it."

"You sold someone," Ines said. Not accusing. Worse — curious. "Before. Someone like me."

The brandy bottle was right there. Odile poured herself a measure and drank it standing, because sitting would have meant staying, and staying would have meant answering honestly, and she had built a life out of not doing either.

"My sister found the lantern first," she said. "Eleven years ago. She was the one who learned the seals, the one who mapped the shafts, the one who got us within sight of Thornfell. And when the magistrates caught her, they didn't kill her either. They *appraised* her." The cup was empty. She looked at it as though it had failed her personally. "They gave me a choice, same as I'm giving you. Walk away rich, or follow her down. I walked away rich. Best decision I ever made. Made it every day since, waking up, choosing it again." She set the cup down with a click. "So you see, hedge-witch, I'm not threatening you. I'm showing you a photograph. This face is what you look like in twenty years, whatever you choose tonight. The only question is whether you choose it looking forward or looking back."

Ines looked at her — really looked, the way the gifted sometimes did, seeing the reef under the waterline — and she hated him for it, and hated herself for hating him, and poured another drink to have somewhere to put her hands.

Behind the cabin wall, faint through two inches of oak and forty feet of hold, the lantern began to sing. A child's voice, thread-thin, rising up through the ship's bones. Ines turned toward the sound like a compass finding north, and his face — his stupid, hopeful, candle-relighting face — told her everything before he spoke.

"He's asking for someone," Ines said quietly. "He's been asking for someone for eleven years, and no one's come."

Odile closed her eyes. *Mira,* said something deep and drowned in her, and she put her boot on its neck.

"Tomorrow we make for the strait," she said. "Provision at Corvess, then Thornfell by the dark of the moon. Sleep while you can, hedge-witch." She paused at the cabin door, hand on the latch, and did not look back at him. "And if that boy in the iron asks you for anything — anyone — you wake me first. Before you promise him a single thing. Do you understand me? Whatever he asks for, whoever he says is coming for him —"

"He says," Ines said softly, behind her, "*she's already here.*"
