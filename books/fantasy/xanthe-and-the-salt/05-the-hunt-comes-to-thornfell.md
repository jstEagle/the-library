## 5. The Hunt Comes to Thornfell

They smelled the witchfinders before they saw them.

It was Brannick who caught it first — a smell like scorched iron and consecrated ash rolling down the pass ahead of the wind — and he came up the line of wagons shouting it in a voice cracked raw: *Ash-priests. Ash on the wind.* By the time the words reached Ines at the rear of the caravan, half the crew was already moving with the calm of people who had rehearsed this in their sleep.

Odile Vane stood atop the lead wagon with her spyglass fixed on the notch where the Salt Gate road narrowed between walls of white stone. The morning light came down hard and clean off the cliffs, and in it Ines could see what she saw: three riders in grey-and-scarlet livery, and behind them, on foot, a column of witchfinders moving with the terrible unhurried patience of men who had never once needed to run.

"Count them," Odile said.

"Fifteen," said Sefa, perched in the branches of a salt-stunted pine above the road. "Sixteen with the one carrying the censer."

The censer. Ines's stomach turned over. He knew what it was from his master's warnings, delivered in the low urgent voice of a man teaching a child to survive: a thurible of blessed coals that burned without fuel, that could taste the residue of gift-work in the air the way a hound tastes blood. His master had died under such a censer's judgment. Ines had watched the cottage burn from a smuggler's ridge two weeks ago, and the smoke had smelled exactly like this.

The lantern hummed against his hip where it rode wrapped in oilcloth and lies. It had hummed all night, louder than ever, as if the boy inside could smell the ash too and remembered what it meant.

"They're not hunting us," Odile said quietly, lowering the glass. "They don't know we exist yet. They're patrolling for crossing-gifts coming down out of the high country." Her jaw worked. "Which means if we sit tight in the tree-line until they pass—"

"The lantern," said Sefa from the pine. "It's been singing since dawn, captain. I can feel it through the wagon boards. If their censer comes within a mile of that thing—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Ines watched the arithmetic complete itself across Odile's face: fifteen witchfinders, forty souls in her crew, and one sealed iron lantern holding enough stolen soul-light to light their pyres from a valley away.

"Brannick. Get the mules off the road. Everyone into the rocks, quiet as graves." Odile swung down from the wagon, and for just a moment — a heartbeat, no more — her eyes found Ines's, and there was something in them that was not suspicion but apology. Then it was gone, shuttered away behind the captain's flat pragmatism, and she was shouting orders in the low carrying voice that made grown smugglers move like water.

* * *

They waited four hours in a fold of stone above the pass while the wind changed twice and the cold worked into their bones. Ines sat wedged between crates of smuggled brandy with his knees drawn up, and the lantern lay against him humming its broken song, and he did everything in his power not to listen to it.

He listened anyway.

*I hid in the chapel,* the soul sang, in its small frayed voice. *I hid in the chapel because Father Marek said the crown would never come into holy ground, and then the doors opened and the ash-priest smiled at me, and he knelt down so he wouldn't be frightening, and that was the most frightening thing of all.*

"Stop," Ines whispered, to the lantern or himself. "Please stop."

But the boy inside the iron had been alone with his memories for years, and he spent them like a miser finally allowed to spend, and Ines sat in the freezing rocks of the Salt Gate pass and learned by heart how a prince of Vael had begged.

It was the third change of the wind that killed them.

A gust came down off the northern crags, warm and wrong, carrying the censer's taste directly across their hiding place — and Ines felt it happen, felt the ash-taste snag on something in the air around the lantern, felt the distant column below stop as one body, the way a wolf stops when the wind brings it meat.

Below, faint as a struck bell, the censer began to ring.

"No," Odile breathed. "No, no, *no*—" And then, louder, with the whole register of command in it: "Break cover! Break cover and run for the gate! GO!"

The pass erupted. Smugglers poured out of the rocks like startled birds, hauling the lantern-wagon up onto its wheels, mules screaming, Sefa dropping out of her pine with a bow already drawn. The witchfinders below began to climb, spreading out along the switchbacks with the grey discipline of professionals, and their ash-priest walked among them swinging his censer, and every time the censer rang, Ines felt it strike against the gift inside him like a hammer against a bell he hadn't known he was.

They almost made it.

For one impossible hour they almost made it — Odile reading the ground like scripture, choosing routes that shouldn't have existed, the caravan flowing up through the broken white stone toward the narrow throat of the Salt Gate where a single defensible bend might buy them the mountain. Ines ran with the others, pushing the wagon when the grade steepened, and somewhere in that hour he stopped being afraid and started being useful, and the difference between those two things felt so much like belonging that he let himself believe in it.

Then they reached the bend, and the witchfinders were already there.

They must have split their force at the pass's mouth — six of them, positioned hours ago, patient as the stones themselves, rising now from behind a shelf of rock with crossbows levelled and the grey-and-scarlet of the crown bright as wounds in all that whiteness.

"Hold!" the ash-priest called up the slope, and his voice carried the resonance of his office, a sound that pressed on the ears like deep water. "By writ of King Aldric's court, yield the gifted among you and the rest may walk away!"

And Ines, standing with his hands on the wagon's rail, understood with perfect clarity that the priest was looking directly at him.

He didn't know how he knew. He simply did — the way he could hear the lantern, the way his master had always said his gift was small but *true*, tuned to things hidden rather than things grand. The priest's eyes were on him the way a compass needle finds north.

Odile saw it too. He watched her see it. And in the space of a breath he watched her make a choice — watched the calculation flicker behind her eyes, the old grief and the older survival instinct wrestling — and then Brannick stepped forward, big and slow and smiling, and put himself between Ines and the crossbows.

"Well," Brannick called down cheerfully, "funny thing about that, priest. We're all a bit gifted. It's why none of us can hold honest work."

The first bolt took him through the chest.

* * *

Afterward, Ines could never assemble those minutes into order. Memory gave them back to him as fragments, sharp-edged and burning:

Sefa on the high rocks, loosing arrows faster than he had ever seen a human being move, screaming wordless fury with every draw.

Odile cutting the lantern free of the wrecked wagon and pressing it into Ines's arms with both hands, her face an inch from his, saying only: *Up. Take it up. Don't stop for anything.* And the look she gave him over his shoulder as he climbed was the look of a woman spending money she did not have.

Brannick, still alive somehow, still on his feet, holding the narrow bend alone with a smuggler's axe in each hand, laughing — actually *laughing* — as the witchfinders learned what a man fighting for other people's lives could do.

And the moment — this was the fragment that would wake him for the rest of his life — the moment the second ash-priest raised his censer and rang it directly at the lantern in Ines's arms, and the sound went through the iron and through the boy inside it and through Ines's hands and into the small despised gift at the center of him, and the gift answered.

It flared out of him like a struck match in a dark room.

Not grand. Never grand. But every hidden thing within fifty paces suddenly *revealed itself* to him and, worse, revealed itself to everyone — the smugglers' buried caches lighting up like lanterns, the witchfinders' concealed blades, the ash-priest's own hidden relics glowing through his robes like bones under skin. A wave of pale gold light rolled visibly across the pass, unmistakable, undeniable, *marked*, and every face on both sides turned toward the boy holding the singing lantern.

"There," the first priest said softly. "There is your gifted one."

What happened next happened fast. Sefa died on the high rocks putting an arrow through the second priest's throat before his censer could sing again. Three more of Odile's crew fell buying the seconds that let the rest scatter up the goat-tracks beyond the gate. And Ines ran — ran with the lantern clutched to his chest and his gift still blazing off him like a signal fire, ran past bodies he recognized and bodies he didn't, ran until the pass fell silent behind him and there was nothing in the world but white stone and thin air and the small frayed voice inside the iron, whispering, *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry.*

He stopped at dusk on a ledge above the cloud line, and he set the lantern down, and he was sick over the edge of the cliff.

* * *

Odile found him there in the last grey light. She came up the track alone, which told him everything about how many were left to bring. Her coat was torn and dark with blood that was not hers, and she moved like a woman twenty years older than she had been that morning.

"How many?" he asked. His voice came out ruined.

"Nineteen." She sat down heavily on the rock beside him, not looking at him, looking out at the darkening valley where Thornfell's salt-works glimmered far below like a fallen constellation. "Nineteen dead. Brannick. Sefa. The Ferro twins, who were seventeen years old." A long silence. "You want to say something. Go ahead."

"I didn't—" The lie assembled itself automatically, the practiced reflex of a lifetime of hiding, and Ines heard it forming and hated it and crushed it. "I did do this. They followed the lantern. The lantern sang because of me. I'm the reason the censer found us." His hands were shaking. "You should have given me to them. You should have handed me over and saved nineteen people."

"Yes," Odile said.

He looked at her. She met his eyes at last, and there was no cruelty in her face, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. Only exhaustion, and beneath the exhaustion, the same grief he'd glimpsed before — the sister-shaped absence he'd begun to understand lived at the bottom of everything she did.

"Yes, I should have," she said. "That's the arithmetic, and I've never once lied to my crew about the arithmetic. But here's the other side of it, boy, and you'll carry it whether I give it to you or not: I've handed people over before. Early days. A girl named Maren, who reminded everyone of my sister, which is exactly why I did it — because keeping her near felt like keeping *her* near, and I couldn't afford to feel anything on that kind of job." She flexed her scarred hands. "I've regretted it every day since, and the crew I betrayed her to save is mostly dead anyway, because that's what the crown does with people eventually. So." She stood, joints cracking. "You live. Not because you deserve it. Because the dead bought it, and wasting their purchase would be the real betrayal."

Ines looked down at the lantern. The boy inside had gone quiet — wrung out, ashamed, a child who had watched people die for him and understood it perfectly. Ines put his palm flat against the iron, and for the first time he spoke back deliberately, gift to soul, in the channel only the two of them could hear.

"I hear you," he said. "I know what they did to you. I know you didn't ask for any of this either." He swallowed. "We're going to Thornfell. Down into the salt. And I'm going to get you out, Aldric. Whatever it costs. I swear it on Brannick's axe and Sefa's bow and every grave back on that pass."

Far below, the lights of the salt court glittered in their mountain like drowned stars, waiting.

Odile watched him rise, lantern in arms, and something in her expression shifted — not softness, exactly. Recognition. As if she were seeing, walking and breathing and swearing oaths, the person she herself had failed to become.

"The entrance shafts are a day's walk south," she said quietly. "If you're going to do this mad thing, you'll need someone who knows the way down."

"You'd come with me? After everything?"

"Don't flatter yourself." She turned and started down the track, and the darkness took her shoulders first. "My sister's memories are in that court, boy. Sold to the magistrates the year the dragon-fire started failing. I've been selling myself piece by piece for ten years trying to buy passage down." Over her shoulder, flat as a ledger entry: "Now I've got a lantern that sings and a marked boy the whole crown is hunting. Congratulations. You're finally worth something."

Ines followed her down into the dark, toward the salt, carrying the fire of a dead king's son and the weight of nineteen names he intended to make mean something.

Behind them, far above on the pass, the witchfinders' beacon-fires bloomed one by one — messages racing toward Thornfell, toward the capital, toward every ear of the crown: *the lantern lives, and the gifted one who carries it is marked.*
