## 6. Descent into Salt

The song led him down.

It had begun as a thread in his sleep, thin as wire, and by the third day after the ambush it was a rope he could not put down. Ines followed it out of Thornfell's lower town, past the tanneries where the stink of lime covered the smell of the witchfinder pyres, to a seam in the hillside that no map had ever bothered to name. An old mine-mouth, boarded with planks gone gray and soft. Someone had scratched a salt-circle sigil across the wood — the mark of things sealed away rather than destroyed.

He pressed his palm flat against the boards and listened with more than his ears.

*Down,* sang the boy in the lantern. *Down, down, where the warm is. Where they keep me. Ines. Ines. Ines.*

His name in a child's voice, repeated like a prayer or a plea, until it stopped sounding like his name at all.

The boards came away easier than they should have. He told himself it was rot. He did not look too closely at whether his gift had whispered them loose.

* * *

The shaft swallowed him whole.

He went with a hooded storm-lantern, three days of hard bread, and the grimoire strapped against his ribs where it beat like a second heart. The first hundred fathoms were ordinary enough — timber props furred with white mineral bloom, rusted rails vanishing into dark, the drip of water counting seconds in the black. But the deeper he climbed down the ladders, rung after rung after rung, the more the rock changed. The brown stone gave way to something veined and glassy. Then to something that caught his lantern-light and threw it back in colors: rose, amber, a green like pond ice.

Salt. Not scattered through the stone but *becoming* it, the whole mountain slowly crystallizing from the inside out, the way a wound fills.

And the song grew richer. Not only Aldric now. Other voices braided beneath his — hundreds of them, thousands, murmuring in harmonies that had no words he knew. Memories, he realized. Spilled memories, soaked into the salt over centuries, still whispering themselves to anyone who would listen. A woman laughing in an orchard that had been dead for two hundred years. A soldier reciting the names of his mess-mates over and over because he could not bear to stop. A child counting to ten, forever.

Ines pressed his hands over his ears and kept climbing. It didn't help. He heard them anyway.

*You're one of us now,* the voices seemed to say. *You came all this way. You're nearly home.*

"I have a home," he said aloud, and his voice cracked strangely in the crystal air, and he could not remember, for three full breaths, what he had meant by it.

* * *

The ladder ended at a door made of light.

Not firelight — nothing so honest. It was a glow that lived *in* the salt itself, rising off the walls like heat-shimmer given color, and where it pooled at the bottom of the shaft stood an archway cut from a single block of translucent rose crystal, tall as a cathedral door and carved with figures whose faces had been worn smooth by hands touching them in passing. Worshipers, maybe. Or customers.

Beyond it: the court.

Ines had imagined caverns, crude halls, the squalor of things driven underground. What he found instead took his breath and kept it.

A city grown rather than built — spires of clear salt rising from a floor of polished white like frozen surf, streets between them paved in slabs of amber crystal that glowed faintly from within. And everywhere, people. Courtiers in robes the color of old pearls, moving through the luminous streets with unhurried grace. Their skin was pale as candle-wax, their eyes the flat bright grey of sea-ice, and when they spoke to one another they did so with their hands open, palms up, offering small glowing motes between their fingers like coins.

Memories. They were trading memories, openly, the way fishwives traded herring.

He watched a young courtier press a shimmering mote into an older one's palm and receive in return a single word — he couldn't hear it, but he saw the older courtier's face change, soften, fill with something that might have been grief or might have been joy; there was no telling which memory bought which feeling here.

"New," said a voice behind him.

Ines spun. A courtier stood at his shoulder — how long she'd been there he couldn't say — robed in grey-white, her face pleasant and utterly unreadable, like a mask left out in the rain.

"You're new," she repeated. "Your edges are still rough. You still flinch."

"I'm looking for someone," Ines managed. "A boy. Sold here. His soul burns in the dragon's—"

"Yes." She smiled without warmth. "Everyone who comes looking says that first. Come. Walk with me. The court dislikes standing still; stillness lets the salt settle into you."

She set off down the luminous street without checking whether he followed. He followed.

"The court beneath Thornfell," she said, gesturing elegantly at the crystal spires, "is older than the crown above it. Older than Vael. We were here when the mountain was only a wound in the world, and we will be here after your kings are dust and their wars a footnote. We ask only one toll of visitors." She turned those flat grey eyes on him. "Payment in kind. A memory. Any memory. It's painless — I promise you that. Painless is our specialty."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you may climb back up your ladder, all thousand rungs, and go tell your witchfinders what you've seen." She said it lightly, but he heard the trap close inside the words. "Though I suspect you won't. You came *down*, little gifted one. People come down for one of two reasons. To buy back something they've lost—" her hand drifted to her own chest, briefly, "—or to become someone they've never been."

* * *

They walked, and the court worked on him.

That was the horror of it, he understood later — not any single moment, but the accumulation. Every street offered him glimpses through lit windows: a man reliving his wedding day on a loop of purchased memory, weeping quietly with happiness; a woman buying the childhood of a stranger because her own had been sold to pay a debt; children — actual children — trading fragments of boredom and scraped knees with the solemnity of merchants.

And always, always, the offers came.

"You seem tired," said a vendor, materializing beside him with a tray of glowing motes. "I have rest. Whole years of it. Sleep you never got, bought cheap from a dead sailor."

"You seem angry," said another. "I have peace. Genuine peace, harvested from a monk who owed us. One mote and you'll never rage again."

"You seem afraid," said the first courtier, his guide, reappearing whenever he thought he'd escaped her. "We can take that too. Fear, gone. Guilt, gone. That night at the Salt Gate pass, the screaming, the blood on your hands — one payment, Ines Eriksen, and it never happened for you again."

He stopped walking. "I didn't tell you my name."

"You didn't need to." Her smile was kind, which was worse than cruelty. "The salt reads what walks on it. Your name arrived before you did. It's been circulating all evening. Would you like to know what they're saying about you? That costs extra."

He should have been terrified. Instead — and this frightened him far more — he felt a terrible, aching temptation. The Salt Gate pass lived behind his eyes every time he closed them: Marta going down under the witchfinder's blade, Odile's face when they burned the bodies, the way his own gift had flared blue-white and *marked* him in front of everyone, sealing his death warrant. If he could pay that away—

If he could pay away the burning cottage. Master Aldous' voice shouting *run, boy*. The years of hiding, of flinching, of being small and despised and hunted for the one true thing about himself.

If he could simply *be someone else*.

"That one," said a new voice, younger, and a boy of perhaps twelve detached himself from a doorway of amber crystal. "The new one. Mother said the new ones forget fastest. Watch — watch, I'll show you."

The boy reached up toward Ines's face with both hands, palms glowing.

"No—" Ines stepped back.

But the salt under his feet had already begun its work. He felt it then, distinctly, horribly: a softness creeping into the borders of himself. The edges of *Ines Eriksen* going grainy, dissolving, like sugar stirred into hot water. He tried to hold his mother's face and found it slipping sideways into a stranger's. He tried to recall why he had come — something about a lantern, a song, a boy, a dragon — and the reasons crumbled like chalk.

What was his name?

He opened his mouth to check, the way a man touches his purse in a crowd, and found —

Nothing. A blank where the word should be. Just the taste of salt and a fading certainty that the blank mattered.

*Down,* sang the distant voice, fainter now, nearly drowned. *Ines. INES. You came for ME. Remember. Remember my name and yours will hold.*

Aldric.

The name surfaced like a drowning man's last bubble, and Ines seized it, and through it hauled up his own — *Ines, Ines Eriksen, son of nobody, apprentice of a dead man, carrier of a stolen book* — gasping it aloud into the luminous street:

"My name is Ines Eriksen!"

The courtiers nearby turned, mildly curious, the way people turn at a drunk singing. The boy pouted and withdrew his glowing hands. And his guide looked at him with something that, in a human face, might have been respect.

"Well," she said. "You held. Most don't, the first hour." She tilted her head, studying him anew. "You're stronger than you look, little gifted one. Or more stubborn. Either way—" she spread her arms toward the great white spire at the heart of the city, taller than all the rest, its base ringed with courtiers who did not move, did not speak, only stood facing it in silence like iron filings around a lodestone, "—you'll want the magistrates. They decide who reaches the Furnace. And I think—" her flat grey eyes glittered, "—I think you intend to reach the Furnace."

At the spire's peak, visible even through the crystal, something pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like a heartbeat.

Like fire.

*Warm,* sang the faraway boy. *Come to the warm, Ines. Hurry. There isn't much of me left.*
