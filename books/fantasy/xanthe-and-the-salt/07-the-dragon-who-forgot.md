## 7. The Dragon Who Forgot

The song stopped at the mouth of the last gallery, and Ines understood why.

There was nothing left to sing to. The chamber opened around him like the inside of a bell — a cathedral of salt, walls faceted and glowing faint amber from some source he could not name, and in its center, coiled upon a dais of her own shed scales, was the dragon.

She did not look like a dragon anymore. That was the first cruelty of it. Ines had grown up on woodcuts: wings like ship sails, a neck like a river's bend, fire pouring from jaws that could melt a city gate. What sat before him had been those things once, but salt is patient, and it had eaten everything it could reach. Crusts of crystal sheathed her flanks like armor grown inward rather than out. Her wings were folded stumps, fused to her body by centuries of mineral bloom, useless as a dead man's hands. Her eyes — when they opened, slowly, lids grinding grit against grit — were the color of furnace doors just before they close.

She looked at him the way a hearth looks at someone who has come in from the cold. Not with recognition. With *function*.

"You're not the boy," she said. Her voice was vast even broken, each word a landslide wrapped in wool. "The boy comes at the hour. You are not the boy."

"No," Ines said. His voice came out small in all that stone. "I'm — my name is Ines."

"Is it?" The dragon's head tilted, and somewhere inside her something shifted, a sound like a banked fire settling. "Names are the first thing the salt takes. I don't recall mine."

Ines's hand found the lantern at his belt without his willing it. Through the iron he felt the hum rise, urgent now, frightened — Aldric's soul pressing against the seams like a child at a locked door. *He's here,* the voice had sung all the way down the mine-shafts. *He's here, he's here.* And now that they had arrived, the soul had gone silent, as if even a prince sold piece by piece did not know what to say when he finally stood before the thing that ate him.

"I've come for him," Ines said. "For what's in this lantern."

* * *

Vess knew warmth.

That was all she knew, and she loved it, and she could not have told you why loving it felt like grief.

Warmth arrived at the hour. It always arrived at the hour. It came through the iron door in the little room above her heart where the priests of salt had built their altar, and it poured down through channels cut into her own bones — she had watched them cut them, hadn't she? Or dreamed she'd watched. It was hard to keep the watching and the dreaming apart; the salt had gotten into that wall too, the wall between what happened and what she wished had happened, and it dissolved both sides until they ran together like frost on glass.

The warmth had a flavor. That was the strange mercy of it. When it poured into her it tasted of apple orchards in late summer, of a boy running under branches with his arms out, of a mother laughing in a doorway with flour on her hands. Vess did not know why a fire should taste of apples. She had asked once, long ago — asked the magistrates, asked the ceiling, asked herself — and the asking had cost her something she couldn't name afterward, some room in her mind that stood empty ever after, swept clean, waiting for nothing.

So she stopped asking. Warmth came at the hour. She burned it. The realm stayed soft and green beyond the mountain, or so the magistrates said, and the salt grew another finger's width along her spine, and the empty rooms in her multiplied like cells in a hive.

But sometimes — not often, only in the deep hours between hours — something moved behind the taste of apples. A voice, very far off, the way you can hear the sea through a hill if the hill is quiet enough. It sounded young. It sounded tired. It sounded like it was calling a name over and over, and though the salt had taken hers, Vess found that she still flinched when it called, as a dog flinches at a whistle it no longer answers because answering is not allowed, because there is no longer a dog, there is only a furnace shaped like one.

Tonight a stranger stood before her. Small. Soft-skinned. Holding an iron lantern, and the lantern was screaming — no, not screaming, singing, singing the way the voice behind the apples sang — and every scale on Vess's ruined body rose beneath its crust of crystal, and she did not know why, and the not-knowing was the worst pain she had felt in two hundred years, which is to say the only pain she had been permitted to feel at all.

"You're not the boy," she said again, because saying it twice made it true, and truth was the one thing left she owned outright.

* * *

"The boy," Ines repeated. "Prince Aldric."

Something happened to the dragon's face then. Ines would spend the rest of his life failing to describe it — a twitch of muscle under crystal, a flicker behind the furnace-door eyes, as if somewhere in all that salted dark a single ember remembered it had once been alive.

"Aldric." She said it the way a starving man says bread. "That is the hour's name. That is what the warmth tastes like." Her great head lowered, joints crackling, until her breath — hot, smelling of struck flint and old apples — washed over him and rustled his hair. "Little thief. Are you the one who carries the hour?"

"He's a person," Ines said, and his voice cracked on it. "His name is Aldric. He was six years old when they sold him. He remembers an orchard. He remembers his mother's hands. He thinks about them so much they've become your fire, do you understand? They burn you warm and he burns up doing it, and he has been screaming into that lantern for eleven years while everyone in this realm slept soundly on his—"

The dragon roared.

It was not rage. Ines had braced for rage; he had his gift half-risen, salt-light gathering in his palms before he could stop it. But the roar was grief, pure and enormous, the grief of a creature hearing for the first time that the food she loved had a face. The whole chamber rang with it. Stalactites of salt wept glittering tears from the ceiling. And beneath the roar, almost lost in it, Ines heard her say, over and over, in a voice gone suddenly, terribly small:

*I didn't know. I didn't know. I didn't know.*

* * *

Vess had eaten the boy for eleven years and had never once been hungry. That was the trick of it, the beautiful trick, the trick she would have praised as mercy if she had known enough to curse it. Hunger would have made her ask questions. Hunger would have made her wonder what she ate. Instead the magistrates fed her love itself — the boy's dearest memories rendered down to heat — and love requires no chewing, asks no blessings, leaves no bones.

Now a small soft creature stood in her dark holding her food in a box, and told her its name.

*Aldric.*

Under the apples, under the flour-dusted hands, under the boy running with his arms out — Vess reached, carefully, carefully, past the salt in the wall between watching and dreaming, and touched the memory directly for the first time. Not filtered. Not rendered. Raw.

A bedroom. Candlelight. A boy of six sitting up in bed, whispering to the dark because he had heard the men come, because even at six he had learned what it meant when men came at night wearing the crown. *They're selling me tomorrow,* he whispered to no one. *Mama, they're selling me tomorrow, and nobody will know my name but you.*

And his mother, kneeling, taking his face in flour-soft hands: *Then I will make sure I am the last thing you forget.*

Vess withdrew from the memory the way a hand withdraws from a stove.

She had warmed a kingdom with that. Every hearth in Vael, every bathhouse, every greenhouse tomato ripening red in winter — all of it kindled from a boy's last night of being loved, burned and reburned eleven thousand times, and she had tasted it nightly and called it good.

"Take it," Vess said.

The little thief blinked. "What?"

"The lantern. Take it. Open it. Let him—" But even as the words left her, something else rose in her, something older than the salt, older than the bargain, rooted in the marrow the crystals had not yet reached. Her body knew what her heart had only just learned. Without the fire she was not a furnace. She was a corpse with a pulse.

Her voice failed. It came out as smoke. "No. Wait. No — I spoke too fast." Her claws scraped the dais, uselessly, horribly. "If the hour stops coming, I go out. Do you understand what goes out means, little thief? Not sleep. Not cold. *Out.* Like a candle pinched between wet fingers. Everything I am is ash held upright by his burning, and if you take him—"

She stopped. Because the lantern was humming again, and through the iron, faint as a heartbeat, she heard it clearly for the first time in eleven years:

the boy, singing her name.

Not the salt-name. Not *furnace*, not *warmth*, not *the hour*. The other one. The one from before. The one she had lost.

*Vess. Vess. Vess.*

* * *

Ines knelt in the salt, the lantern open in his lap, and let the soul's song pour through him unfiltered, and it was not a song at all, it was a name spoken with such tenderness that the air itself seemed to blush.

The dragon had gone utterly still. Even her fire seemed to hold its breath.

"He knows you," Ines said softly. "All these years, burning — part of him was reaching for you. He wasn't fuel, Vess. He thought you were the one being kept alive. He thought *he* was saving *you*. He used to dream — " Ines pressed his palm flat to the iron, listening. "He used to dream that when it was over, when the fire was done, someone would come down and open the door, and you'd be there. And you'd tell him it was all right. That it hadn't hurt. That he could rest now."

The dragon made a sound Ines had no word for. It was not a roar. It was the sound a mountain makes when it decides, at last, to grieve.

"Please," Vess said, and the word cracked her crystal skin in three places, and light leaked out of the cracks like dawn through shutters. "Little thief, please. I cannot want both things. My heart is too small now — the salt took most of it — I cannot want him free and myself alive, there isn't room." Her furnace eyes fixed on him, and in their depths, for one naked instant, Ines saw her: not the furnace, not the crust, but a young dragon rising off a mountainside with the sun caught in her wings, before any crown had ever needed her, before anyone had taught her that her fire belonged to the realm instead of to herself.

"But he is six years old," she said. "Inside there, he is still six years old. And I have been eating his childhood for eleven years." The light guttered. "So I am asking you — I, who have forgotten my own name and my own sky — I am asking you to choose for me. Because whichever way you choose, I haven't the strength left to want it honestly. Choose, and I will bear it. It is the only thing left I know how to do."

Ines closed his hands around the lantern.

Above them, far up the shafts, boots were ringing on salt — many boots, descending fast, and the magistrates' voices carried down like knives dropped into a well: *the thief, the thief, stop the thief.*

The hour, it seemed, had come early.
