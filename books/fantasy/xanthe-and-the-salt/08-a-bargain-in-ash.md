## 8. A Bargain in Ash

The magistrates came for him at the hour when the salt courts dimmed their lamps, which was to say never, because there were no lamps, only the slow glow of the walls themselves and the long shadows they cast like patient animals.

Ines stood in the antechamber of the Hall of Weights, where the air tasted of brine and old decisions. His hands would not stop shaking. He had tried pressing them flat against his thighs, folding them, gripping the hem of his coat — Odile's coat now, taken from her pack before he descended — but nothing held. The trembling was not fear. He had learned to know fear by its texture, and this was something else. This was a body arguing with a soul that had already decided.

*You don't have to do this,* said the voice in the lantern.

Aldric's voice had grown clearer since Ines entered the deep roads, as if the boy's soul could smell home through the iron mesh. Clearer, and more terrible, because it was a child's voice saying adult things: *They will take everything. They will enjoy taking it.*

"I know," Ines whispered.

The nearest magistrate turned its head. It had no face worth naming — features pressed into salt the way thumbs press into dough, eyes two dark seams, mouth a suggestion. When it spoke, grains fell from its lips with each word, a soft continuous sifting, like sand through an hourglass measuring out a life.

"You speak to the cargo," it said. "That is irregular."

"He speaks *for* me," Ines said. "There's a difference."

The magistrate considered this. Around the hall, others drifted closer — seven, eight, a dozen of them, their robes crusted white, trailing powder with every step so that the floor beneath each one had risen into small dunes, monuments to how long they had stood in one place, thinking one thought, becoming one thing. Ines understood suddenly that these were not men who had become salt. These were men who had stopped moving, and the world had simply covered them.

"The court has heard your petition," said the first magistrate. "It is unprecedented. Gifts are *taken* here. They are harvested from the condemned, stripped from the dying, pried loose from the unwilling. No one has ever knelt in our hall and offered theirs freely." A pause; more grains falling. "We find ourselves... curious. And curiosity is an appetite we have not fed in some time."

"Then hear the terms," Ines said, and his voice did not shake, though everything else did. "The soul in the lantern goes free. Whole. Unspent. You will not keep even an ember of him as payment on some old debt. And the fire he fed — whatever warmth this mountain stole — you release it back to the realm above, or you lose the dragon's last flame entirely when she dies, and Thornfell freezes, and every memory you hoard goes brittle in the cold."

He had rehearsed none of this. It came out of him the way water finds cracks, and he realized, distantly, that this was what his master had meant all those years ago, tapping his chest over his heart: *the gift isn't in the hands, boy. It's in the wanting. Everything else is plumbing.*

One of the magistrates laughed. It was an awful sound — dry, granular, like a landslide deciding to be amused.

"The terms are insolent," it said. "The terms presume we need the fire. Presume we need anything." It drifted forward, close enough that Ines could see the seams of its eyes were not empty after all — they held tiny compressed images, memories pressed flat and worn smooth from handling. He glimpsed a green field. A woman laughing. A door painted yellow.

Then the image flickered and showed him instead a market square, and soldiers, and a pyre, and he understood that the magistrates kept other people's worst moments too, and wore them near their faces like jewelry.

"But," the magistrate continued, "we accept."

Just like that. Ines had braced for bargaining, for cruelty drawn out like taffy, and the swiftness of it knocked the breath from him.

"Why?" he managed.

"Because freely given corrupts better than anything taken." The seam-eyes brightened. "A stolen gift resents its new owner. It sulks. It leaks. But a gift surrendered in love, or mercy, or whatever mangled virtue drives you down here — that gift arrives already loyal to us. Do you understand, little witch? We are not buying your magic. We are buying the shape of your surrender, and we will wear it for centuries."

*Run,* Aldric whispered.

Ines knelt.

The salt floor bit his knees through the cloth. He set the lantern before him, between himself and the drifting line of magistrates, and put both palms flat against its iron mesh. The metal was warm. It had always been warm, he realized now — warm the way a fevered child is warm, and everyone had carried it and stacked it and bargained over it without once noticing they were touching a living thing.

"Aldric," he said aloud. "Look at me."

Through the mesh, light stirred — a small gold flicker, the ghost of a face forming and dissolving, a boy of perhaps ten years with a crown that had been melted down to make the lantern's hinges. Ines had seen it in the memories the lantern leaked into his sleep: the forge beneath the palace, the king's own father signing the sale with a steady hand, the moment the boy's soul was drawn out like thread from a spool while his body learned to breathe without him.

"When this opens," Ines said, "you'll be pulled toward your body. Up through the mountain, past all of it. Don't look at the fire on your way out. Whatever it shows you, however warm it makes itself — don't look."

"What will happen to you?" the boy asked. His voice was very small in the vast crystalline dark.

"Nothing I haven't already survived."

"That's a lie," Aldric said. "I've been inside your dreams. I know what your gift sounds like. It sounds like your mother humming."

Ines closed his eyes.

His gift. Small, despised, useless by every measure the crown used — he couldn't throw flame or bend steel or read a general's mind across a battlefield. All he could do was listen: hear the true name in a thing, hear what objects and beasts and buried souls wanted, needed, grieved. It had made him hunted. It had made him hated. It had made him, in the smuggler camp at night, the only person alive who knew that the sealed lantern in the hold was screaming.

And it had made him *him* — the way a scar makes a face, the way a wound makes a history.

"It does sound like my mother humming," Ines said quietly. "Which is why I can't let them keep it."

He opened his gift one last time.

Not wide — he hadn't the strength — but enough. Enough to feel the hall around him: the magistrates' hunger, vast and patient as tide. The dragon Vess far below, her heart a banked coal of habit, dreaming of nothing. The lantern, blazing against his senses like a struck bell. And beneath it all, threaded through the salt under his knees, the court itself — old, starving, and suddenly, eagerly attentive.

*Here,* the court seemed to say. *Here, here. We have waited so long for something offered.*

"I give it freely," Ines said. "No chain, no claim. My listening, my knowing, my name-in-the-things — I give it whole to the salt courts, to keep or spend or bury, forever, without recourse." The words felt old in his mouth, older than him, a contract-shape every gifted child learned as a warning: *never say this, never mean this.* "Witnessed by the soul of Prince Aldric of Vael, and by the dragon Vess, last of her kind."

The hall went silent. Even the sifting of grains from the magistrates' lips stopped, and in that silence Ines heard — with the last hearing he would ever have — the entire mountain lean in.

The first magistrate extended a hand of pressed salt.

"Then we witness," it said, and savored the words like wine, rolling each syllable until it dissolved. "*Freely.* Oh, little witch. You have no idea what you have just seasoned us with."

The pain, when it came, was not what he expected.

He had imagined tearing — something ripped out at the root. Instead it was like drowning in reverse: the water draining slowly from his lungs, leaving him gasping in air that suddenly felt thin, colorless, mute. The hall's voices went flat. The lantern's song faded note by note, a lullaby sinking underwater, until Ines was holding warm metal and feeling nothing behind it at all. The names of things — the constant low choir he had heard since childhood, the hum in stones, the want in wood, the grief in iron — guttered out like lamps pinched one by one down a long corridor.

What remained was quiet.

Not peace. Not yet. Just quiet, enormous and absolute, the silence of a man standing in a room where music had played his whole life and realizing he would never hear it again.

He did not cry. He noted, distantly, that he probably should, that this was the moment tears existed for, but his body had spent everything on the giving and had nothing left over for grief. He knelt on the salt floor, empty-handed in the deepest sense, and watched the magistrates recoil slightly from him — not in disgust, he thought, but the way rich men step back from a beggar who has nothing left to take.

"The transaction concludes," said the first magistrate. Something like satisfaction moved through its dough-pressed face. "The cargo may be released. The fire returns to the upper airs. The court thanks you for your—" it paused, tasting, "—*generosity.*"

"Open the lantern," Ines said. His voice sounded strange to him now. Ordinary. Just a man's voice, with nothing underneath. "I want to be here when it happens."

* * *

They gave him the honor of it, if it was honor — the magistrates drew back, and the lantern sat alone on the salt floor between his knees, and Ines lifted it with hands that felt like anyone's hands.

The catch was simple. It had always been simple. That was the joke of it, the obscenity of it: the most precious soul in the realm, sealed behind a latch a child could work.

He thought of Odile, up in the pass with half her crew dead, having bled for a cargo she'd meant to sell. He thought of the witchfinders' pyres on the ridge, and his master's cottage burning, and the grimoire heavy in his pack that he could no longer read — not that he ever truly could, but the gift had whispered the difficult passages to him, patient as a tutor. He thought of his mother humming. He could not remember, now, whether the memory was real or whether the gift had given it to him, and that loss hurt worse than the rest combined.

"Goodbye," he told the gift, because it deserved a farewell, because it had been the only companion of his whole hunted life.

Then he opened the lantern.

Light came out like a shout.

* * *

Far above, snow was beginning to fall on Thornfell, and in the Hall of Weights the magistrates were still savoring the taste of willing surrender when the temperature began to climb.

Below them, in the furnace-chamber where she had burned for three hundred years, the dragon Vess felt something she had not felt since she was anything at all: the fire inside her going strange. Rising. Gathering itself not outward, as warmth, as habit, as duty — but upward, as *purpose*, remembering all at once what fire was for.

She uncoiled from the salt crust that had grown over her like a second skin, shedding centuries in flakes and slabs, and opened eyes that had forgotten how to see and saw anyway.

The magistrates felt her waking through the floors of their hall. The first magistrate turned toward the sound of cracking stone and, for the first time in an age, experienced something it had no memory to hide behind.

Fear.

Above them all, climbing fast through the dark, a boy's soul flew home — and behind it, wings unfolding, came the fire.
