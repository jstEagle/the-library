## 9. The Last Guttering

The magistrates of salt did not have faces, exactly. They had surfaces — planes of crystal where memories swam like fish beneath ice, and Ines watched his own face drift through them: a boy of eight, laughing in a garden that had never existed; a young man crowned in silver he had never earned. The court was offering him back to himself, or something wearing himself, and it cost nothing but the lantern in his arms.

*Take the trade,* they sang, in a voice like sugar dissolving. *Be whole. Be anyone.*

He looked at Vess.

She filled the great hollow of the furnace-chamber like a mountain fills a valley, her scales grown over with centuries of salt, so that she was less a dragon now than a geode shaped like one. Her eye, vast and clouded as sea-glass, turned toward him with an expression that had no name in any language Ines knew — because it belonged to no creature that remembered being anything.

"You're cold," she said. It was the only thing she ever said. She had said it to every soul fed into her fire for three hundred years. *You're cold.* And then she burned them warm.

"No," Ines said quietly. "I'm not."

The lantern trembled against his chest. Inside it, Aldric's soul had stopped singing and begun instead to hum — a small, steady note, the way a child hums when he is frightened and trying not to show it.

"Open it," the boy's voice whispered through the iron seams. "Whatever happens. Open it."

Ines set the lantern on the cracked basalt before the dragon's snout and worked the seals with fingers gone clumsy from fear. Three locks. His master had taught him lockwork on winter evenings, back when the world was a cottage and a hedge and a garden — back before the burning. The first lock gave. The second. On the third, the whole chamber shuddered, and somewhere far above, stone began to sing its long collapsing song.

The witchfinders had brought the pass down behind them. Or the court itself was dying. It didn't matter which. There would be no walking out slowly.

The third lock opened.

Light came out of the lantern like water comes out of a broken dam — not gentle, not golden, but *urgent*, a flood of everything the boy had been, compressed for years into iron. Ines threw up a hand against it and felt, even through his palm, warmth like a hearth, like a summer field, like every good thing that had ever been taken from this place and hoarded here.

Vess inhaled.

It was instinct, older than her forgetting. Three hundred years of habit moved in her body before thought could stop it — the great lungs drawing, the furnace-heart kindling, the whole enormous architecture of her hunger turning toward the flame that might make her live another century.

And then she stopped.

Ines would think about that moment for the rest of his life. The moment the dragon, who remembered nothing — not her name, not her wings, not the sky — chose anyway. Chose against the oldest thing in her, against the hunger that had become her self. Her clouded eye fixed on the light spilling between Ines's fingers, and something moved beneath the salt-crusted scales of her throat, something that might once have been laughter.

"I remember," she said.

Not *I remember warmth*. Not the liturgy of the furnace.

*I remember.*

And Ines understood, with a clarity that hurt worse than grief, what she meant. She remembered enough. One word of herself, dredged up from under all that salt, purchased at the price of a child's burning soul — and it was enough to know that the next hundred warm years would be built on the same theft, and the theft after that, forever.

"No more," Vess said. "No one else burns for me."

She rose. Salt rained off her in sheets, crashing down around the chamber in white cascades, and underneath it her scales were black and old and scarred with the geometry of chains removed long ago. She was not beautiful anymore. She was something better than beauty — she was a decision.

"Ines Eriksen." The magistrates' voices frayed at the edges. He could feel their panic like frost forming. "The bargain stands. Your gift—"

"I'm keeping it," Ines said, and was surprised to find he meant it, and surprised again at how little it cost to mean it. "But you can't have hers either. Or his." He lifted the lantern, and the light poured over his arms like sunlit water. "Or anyone's. Ever again."

"The realm will freeze," they sang. "The fires below the mountain keep the winters off the valley. You will kill thousands."

"Then let them learn to build real fires," Ines said. "Like everyone else."

Vess opened her mouth.

What came out was not the tame furnace-flame of the courts, the careful blue heat that had warmed a kingdom on a diet of souls. It was fire the way fire had been before anyone taught it manners — white at the core, roaring, alive. But she did not turn it on Ines, and she did not turn it on the fleeing courtiers who scattered like startled birds through the crystalline corridors.

She turned it upward, on the throne.

The throne of the salt court was made of compressed memory — ten thousand years of bought and stolen selves, fused into a single seat of power. When the flame took it, the sound it made was not like stone breaking. It was like a choir inhaling all at once.

Then the choir screamed free.

Ines felt them go — every caged memory tearing loose and streaming up through the mountain's veins like spring meltwater. Somewhere above, he knew, farmers were going to wake with their mothers' lullabies restored to them. Merchants would weep over names they'd sold decades ago and couldn't recall until this instant. Whole towns would remember why they had founded themselves here, beside the salt, and some of them would grieve, and some would celebrate, and none of them would ever quite understand the sudden fullness in their chests.

The magistrates did not die. That was the terrible mercy of it. They simply emptied, plane by plane, surface by surface, until there was nothing left in the crystal but crystal — elegant, inert, and finally honest about what it had always been.

* * *

The mountain began to come down around them while Vess still burned.

"Go up," she said. Her voice was changing now, deepening, slowing, becoming geology. "East shaft. Follow the warm air. The old vents breathe."

"What about you?" Ines was already backing toward the corridor, the lantern-light wrapped close against his chest, and he hated the question even as he asked it because he already knew the answer.

Vess lowered her great head until her sea-glass eye was level with him. This close, he could see himself reflected in it, small and smudged and crying without meaning to.

"I was dead three hundred years ago," she said. "They just kept using the body. This—" a shudder ran through her, and her wings, atrophied and salt-fused, flexed once in the ruin of the furnace chamber, "—this is the first breath I've taken as myself since I forgot my name. Don't waste it being sad."

"It should have been different," Ines said. "You should have gotten to fly again."

Something flickered behind the clouded eye — brief, bright, gone. A memory surfacing like a fish, then diving back down into the dark.

"I did," she said. "Just now. For one second. I flew."

Then she turned back to the collapsing throne and breathed her last fire into the heart of it, and Ines ran.

* * *

The east shaft was exactly as she'd promised — breathing, warm, narrow. Ines climbed with the soul held against his sternum, both hands full of light, and the mountain fell apart behind him in long rolling booms like a giant clearing its throat.

Twice the passage ahead collapsed and he found another way, guided by the boy's voice growing stronger in his arms, steadier, more present with every yard gained from the dying furnace.

"Left here," Aldric murmured. "There's a ladder. Iron rungs. My grandfather had it built — escape route. He was afraid of his own court."

"You remember your grandfather?"

"I remember everything now." The voice caught. "That's the worst part. I remember the day they took me. I remember my mother's face. I thought if I could just forget, it wouldn't hurt, but—"

"But forgetting is how they win," Ines finished, hauling himself up the rusted rungs two at a time.

Above him, gray light. Real light. Daylight filtered through a ventilation grate crusted with moss, and beyond it — wind. Actual wind, moving over actual grass, carrying the smell of rain.

The tunnel mouth opened onto a hillside high above Thornfell, and Thornfell was burning.

Not all of it. Just the palace district, just the towers where the court's agents had kept their ledgers of the gifted and their contracts of sale — and Ines stood in the wind with a soul in his arms and watched the flames climb, and understood that Vess's fire had not stayed politely underground. It had followed every freed memory home, burning away everything built on stolen selves.

Below, in the town, people were pouring into the streets. He could hear them — not screaming, not yet. Singing, some of them. Weeping, others. Calling out names they hadn't known they'd lost.

Aldric's light pulsed against his chest, urgent now.

"Not yet," Ines told him. "Almost. Hold together a little longer."

"He's down there," the boy said. "My body. Is it still—"

"I don't know."

"Will I fit? After all this time? Will I still fit?"

Ines started down the hillside, sliding on wet grass, running because the boy needed him to run and because stopping would mean feeling the full weight of everything lost tonight — the gift guttering low in him like a candle in its final inch, the dragon ash in the dark, Odile's crew scattered across a pass he'd led them into.

His gift. He reached for it and found it threadbare, translucent, a lamp burning its last oil. The bargain he'd refused, and refused again, and refused a third time — the magistrates had taken their payment anyway, siphoning him with every use of his power in their domain, patient as salt. What remained was barely enough to see by.

Enough, maybe, for one more working.

At the bottom of the hill, the streets of Thornfell opened before him, and somewhere among them, in a bedchamber behind a door guarded by men who no longer had orders, lay a fourteen-year-old king who had been empty for six years.

Ines put his hand over the light in his chest and began to walk.

"One more thing," he whispered to his fading gift, to the boy humming inside him, to the dragon whose last breath was still falling as ash on the wind. "One more. Then you can rest."

Behind him, the mountain settled into silence. Ahead, the town bells had begun to ring — not alarm, he realized. Something older than alarm.

They were ringing the way bells rang when a king woke up.
