## 10. Salt on the Tongue

The palace at Vael-hold was built of pale stone and older griefs, and Ines walked into it smelling of sulfur and salt, carrying a lantern that weighed less than a loaf of bread and more than a mountain.

They let him in because he asked to be let in — which, he would think later, was the strangest part of all of it. He had expected gates and drawn bows, witchfinder checkpoints with their brass censers sniffing for gift-blood. Instead there was a boy on a throne too big for him, a court holding its breath, and a chamberlain whose voice cracked halfway through announcing *a petitioner*.

Aldric was twelve. Ines had known this from the soul's fragmented memories, had heard the boy's whole life sung out of an iron cage, but knowing and seeing were different hungers entirely. The king sat with his hands folded in his lap like a child at lessons, his crown sitting slightly crooked, his eyes the flat gray of someone who has been told not to want anything.

"You have something of mine," Aldric said.

His voice was steady. His hands were not.

"I do," Ines said. He climbed the dais steps one at a time. Nobody stopped him. Perhaps they could not; perhaps the whole gilded room understood, in the wordless way animals understand weather, that something was about to end or begin. "I'm going to give it back. But you should know what it costs before I do."

"Tell me."

"It burned," Ines said. "For three years, under Thornfell, keeping a dragon warm so the realm wouldn't freeze. It burned as fire burns things — without asking, without stopping. And when I open this lantern, you'll remember all of it. Every hour. You'll be twelve years old and you'll also be every moment of that fire." He stopped one step below the throne. "I won't lie to you, Your Majesty. It will hurt."

Aldric looked at the lantern the way a drowning man looks at shore.

"I know what it did down there," the boy-king said quietly. "I dreamed it while I was in it. The regents told my people the dragon-fire was a blessing. They never said what fed it." His jaw tightened, and for a moment he looked very much like a king, and very much like a child who had been sold, and both at once was almost more than Ines could bear to look at. "Open it."

Ines set the lantern on the step between them. The iron was cool now; the song inside had gone quiet, patient, the way a held breath is quiet. Three days climbing out of the collapsing tunnels with the soul cupped against his chest, three days of whispering *almost, almost* into the dark, and now —

He thought of Vess. Of her vast eye going soft at the end, of the fire she had spent not on warmth but on wrath, burning the salt throne to glass and letting ten thousand stolen memories go up like migrating birds. He thought of Odile's face when he'd told her: not gratitude, exactly. Something rawer. A woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

He thought of his master's cottage, burning on the ridge, and of himself running along roads he knew by heart because he had no other map of the world.

"I'm sorry," he said — to the boy, to the fire, to everyone the realm had eaten to keep itself comfortable — and broke the seal.

Light came out of the lantern the way water comes out of a broken dam: all at once, and then everywhere. Ines felt it pass through him like a cold wind through an open door, taking nothing, because there was nothing left in him to take. His gift was already ash in the courts below, traded freely, gone gladly. For the first time since the hedge-witch had found him weeping over a wounded sparrow at seven years old, he stood in the presence of magic as only a witness.

Aldric screamed. Then he wept. Then he was silent for a long time, kneeling on the dais with light pouring off him like heat off summer stone, and when he finally raised his head his eyes were gray no longer but ember-bright, and old, and young, and his.

"I remember," the boy-king said. "Gods forgive us. I remember everything."

* * *

The edict came down within the month, read aloud in every market square from Vael-hold to the coast: *the hunt is ended*. The witchfinders' censers were melted for bells. The registers of the gifted were burned publicly, and Ines stood in the crowd at Thornfell town watching his own name curl into smoke, feeling nothing he could name and everything he couldn't.

The realm did not freeze. That was the miracle nobody sang about. Whatever Vess had been, whatever the fire had truly been for, the land remembered how to warm itself once the leech was pulled away. Winter came late and gentle, and the salt courts beneath the mountain went dark and echoing, their magistrates fled or crystallized, their currency of stolen memory scattered to whoever dared descend and gather it.

Some did dare. Ines saw them sometimes on the road above Thornfell: thin figures with faraway eyes, carrying jars of other people's childhoods, selling pieces of happiness in the markets. He never bought. He had learned what those jars cost.

Odile found him at the Salt Gate tavern, where the survivors of the caravan drank on the anniversary of the ambush. She dropped onto the bench across from him without asking, the way she did everything, and set down two cups of something brown and strong.

"The crew's going north come spring," she said. "Past the pass, up to the freeholds. Less crown, more fog. Good country for people with pasts."

"I don't have a past anymore," Ines said. "I traded it. Didn't you hear?"

"I heard you traded a *gift*, not a past, and only a fool confuses the two." She pushed a cup at him. "We need hands. Real ones. Rope work, cart work, standing watch in the cold and complaining about it. No magic required." Her eyes flicked over him — the ordinary hands, the ordinary eyes, the absence where something bright used to live. "No magic wanted, truth be told. You've done your share of miracles. Time you learned to haul freight."

Ines turned the cup slowly on the scarred wood. "You're offering me a place because I'm useless now?"

"I'm offering you a place because you're *here*," Odile said. "Because you carried a dead man's cargo up a collapsing mine rather than leave it. Because Marta's ghost would've liked you and I hate that she would've liked you and I'm tired of hating things." She drank. Her voice roughened. "Belonging isn't a reward, boy. Nobody earns it. They just decide, one morning, that they've decided. I'm deciding."

It was, Ines thought, the longest speech she'd made since he'd known her, and possibly the truest thing anyone had ever said to him.

"When do we leave?" he asked.

* * *

Spring came up the north road slow and green. The diminished crew — nine where there had been twenty-two — moved through the passes with the easy rhythm of people who have buried enough together to stop counting. Ines learned knots. He learned the mules' names and temperaments. He learned to sleep in the open without dreaming of singing lanterns, and if some mornings he woke reaching for a hum that wasn't there, he kept that reaching private, the way a man keeps the scar under his shirt private. Not hidden. Just his.

They crossed a high meadow in the third week of the thaw, the whole crew strung out along the trail, and Bram — the youngest hand, gap-toothed, forever curious — fell into step beside him.

"Odd question," Bram said. "Fen was asking. And me. Not prying."

"You're prying."

"We're absolutely prying." He grinned. "You gave it up, didn't you? Your gift. On purpose. Everyone says you must regret it. Everyone says a man can't just — put down the only thing that made him *him* and walk on."

Ines considered it honestly, because Bram deserved honesty and because the question deserved it. Did he miss it? The little despised trick of hearing what things truly were — the lantern's song, the mule's grumbles, the fear under Odile's pragmatism, the grief under everything? Some nights, yes, the way you miss a language you used to dream in. The world was quieter now. Blunter. He had to take people at their word, and their words were often lies, and he had to love them anyway, blind, which was harder and somehow worth more.

But he remembered the salt courts. He remembered the magistrate's honeyed offer — *any name you want, any self, painlessly bought* — and how close he'd come, how sweet the forgetting tasted for one shining moment before he bit through it. He remembered that his gift had made him hunted, and hated, and *himself*, and that of those three things, only the last had ever actually been his.

He crouched, took a pinch of snow-melt from a rock pool, touched it to his tongue. Salt, faintly, from the old mines leaching through the stone. The taste of the deep places. The taste of the bargain. The taste, he suspected, he'd carry all his life, and be glad of.

"No," he said, standing, shouldering his pack, falling back into line beside Bram as the crew wound northward into the fog and the green. "I don't miss it."

Bram waited. Crews always waited.

Ines smiled, tasting salt.

"I remember the way."
