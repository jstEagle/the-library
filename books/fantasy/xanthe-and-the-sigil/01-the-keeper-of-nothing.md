## 1. The Keeper of Nothing

The trouble with being invisible, I've found, is that you have to keep at it. Invisibility isn't a state; it's a chore. Like sweeping a floor that snow keeps drifting onto.

I was up before the light did its work, as always, because the gate does not care whether you're rested. Thornfell's sealed door sits in a cleft of the mountain like a coin pressed into wax — black iron banded with bronze, taller than three men and twice as old as anything else on this slope, including me. My grandmother called it the Door. Not *the gate*, not *the seal*. Just the Door, the way you'd say *the river* or *the sky*, as if there were only one and it had always been there. Which, as far as our family is concerned, it has.

I went through the morning rite the way my mother taught me, the way her mother taught her, back to whoever first decided that an Okafor should stand in front of a slab of metal every dawn and pretend it mattered.

It matters. That's the joke nobody laughs at.

First the ward-stones: seven of them, set in an arc across the approach, each about the size of a loaf of bread and each carved with a spiral that my family's ledgers insist means something specific and refuses to say what. You lay your palm flat against the first stone and walk the arc, and if everything is well, the stones are cold. Cold as stream water in thaw, cold enough to make your teeth ache if you linger. Seven cold stones meant seven more days of nothing happening, which is the finest thing a keeper can hope for. Nothing happening is the whole profession. I was very good at it.

Stone one: cold. Stone two: cold. Stone three had a chip off its lower edge where a rockfall had caught it some years ago, and frost sat in the crack like sugar, but the stone beneath was cold. Four, five, six. Seven.

All cold. All quiet.

"Good morning," I told them anyway. You're not supposed to talk to ward-stones — Mother said it invites answers, and ward-stones have no business answering anyone — but I'd been talking to them since I was six and they'd never once replied, so I considered the habit safely one-sided.

Then the sigil-oil for the hinges, though the Door hadn't opened in living memory and the hinges were more theoretical than practical. Then the sweep of ash from the offering bowl, then fresh juniper in the bowl itself, because whatever the Door kept out — or kept in, depending on which version of the story you liked — it apparently appreciated juniper, and I wasn't in a position to test alternatives.

Then I checked the sigil on my hand, which sounds strange until you know about it.

There's a mark on my left palm, born with me, the size of a thumbnail. It looks, at a glance, like a birthmark gone wrong — a whorl of brown-red lines that might be a flower or might be a knot or might be nothing at all, depending on how much you squinted and what you wanted to see. Every Okafor in the family record had one, or so Mother claimed. She had one. Tam had one, smaller, on the inside of his wrist.

Tam's stopped mattering when Tam stopped existing. Mine I checked every morning the way you check a fence line: still there, still dull, still just a stain. A birthmark. Nothing. I was, after all, the keeper of nothing, and it seemed only fitting that my badge of office be equally unremarkable.

* * *

Let me tell you what a keeper actually does, since the ballads get it wrong and even the priestess gets it wrong, and the ballads at least have the excuse of being drunk.

A keeper checks wards. A keeper oils hinges that haven't moved in ninety years. A keeper keeps the ledger — the daily record of stone-temperature and weather and the comings and goings of everyone who passes within a mile of the Door, because the covenant, whatever exactly it says, requires records, and records require a hand, and mine is the only hand left.

And a keeper trades with smugglers, because the mountain has two kinds of traffic and only one kind pays.

They came up the goat track from the valley on the third day of the week, four of them with their mules loaded down with salt fish and iron nails and the particular brand of cheap spirits that sells best to people who want to forget things. Brannoc Vale walked at the front, as he always did, a big man gone soft at the edges the way a boulder goes soft — you don't notice until you lean on him and discover he's mostly lichen. He had a new scar over his eyebrow since the last pass through, and I didn't ask, because asking is how conversations start, and conversations are how you become visible.

"Keeper." He touched his brow with two fingers, half salute, half superstition.

"Vale." I had the scale out already. "Fish first."

"You could ask how the road was."

"The road is a road. Salt fish, nails, and did you bring the blue wool?"

He grinned. Brannoc Vale grins like a landslide deciding to be friendly. "Blue wool. Two skeins. And I brought news, which you'll pay for in attention, which I know you hoard like a miser."

"I don't hoard attention. I avoid it. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a miser and a woman throwing coins into a river."

"News anyway," he said, and lowered his voice, which was unnecessary, because his three men had already drifted toward the spirit barrel with the practiced blindness of people who knew which parts of a transaction weren't for them. "Priestess Sef's been preaching different sermons lately. About the old days. About the gods under the hill."

"There are no gods under the hill," I said. "There's a Door, and behind the Door there is whatever the Door is for, and the Okafor family has spent two hundred years not finding out. That's the arrangement."

"That's what your family says." He weighed the fish himself, honest to a fault in weights and dishonest everywhere else, which is the best kind of trading partner. "But folk are praying again, Keeper. Out loud. At crossroads. Old prayers their grandmothers taught them and they never said above a whisper. Sef's telling them it's harmless. Telling them the old gods are sleeping, and prayers are just dreams, and dreams hurt no one."

"And what do you think?"

Brannoc looked past me, up at the Door. People did that. It was the one thing about the Door that never failed to strike strangers — not its size, not its age, but the way your eyes kept returning to it, like a tongue to a broken tooth. He looked at it a moment too long, then made himself look away, and I watched him do both, and filed it all away in the ledger of my head.

"I think," he said, "that I've run this road twenty years, and in twenty years I've never once seen a bird land on the Door. And last month I saw a crow sitting on top of it. Just sitting. Looking down at us like we owed it money."

"Crows sit on lots of things."

"Not on that."

I paid him in silver cut from a bar I kept buried under the hearthstone, and he paid me in fish and nails and blue wool and one crow, free of charge, that I would spend the next several nights failing not to think about.

* * *

The village of Hollowmere sits an hour's walk below the gate, close enough that on windless evenings I can smell their bread baking, which is either cruelty or comfort depending on how lonely I am that day. I go down maybe six times a year. For salt I can't get from Vale, for lamp oil, for the once-a-year consultation with the farrier about the mule, who is named Turnip and is the closest thing I have to a colleague.

Six times a year is the correct number. Fewer and people start wondering why you never come. More and they start remembering that you come.

Because here's the thing about Hollowmere: they half-remember us. Not my name — I've been careful about names, mine especially, and most of them know me only as *the keeper*, a job title wearing a person. But they remember the shape of the story. There was a family on the mountain. They kept the Door. Something happened to them — sickness, curse, bargain, nobody agrees — and now there's just the one, the last one, and she comes down for lamp oil and doesn't meet your eye.

Half-memory is worse than forgetting. If they'd forgotten entirely, I'd be a stranger like any other stranger, and strangers get smiled at. Half-remembered, I'm a ghost with a shopping list, and children are pulled closer by mothers who aren't sure why.

That trip down, the baker's boy — grown now, married, a baby of his own — stopped me in the lane. I recognized him by the gap in his teeth that he'd never had filled. He must have been ten the last time he'd spoken to me, back when children still spoke to me, before they learned.

"Keeper," he said. "My gran wanted to know — she wanted to know if it's true the Door sings sometimes. On the long nights."

"It doesn't sing," I said. "It's a door."

"She says it sang the winter she was a girl. Low, she said. Like a choir under a blanket."

"Your gran is old and winters are quiet and quiet makes ears inventive."

He laughed, easy, unbothered. He didn't remember enough to be afraid of me, which was almost nice. "She also says to tell you the juniper's poor this year on the south slopes. Says if you're burning offerings, use the north-side cut. The old ones liked the north-side better."

"The old ones," I said carefully, "are asleep."

"Sure," he agreed pleasantly, the way you agree with someone about weather. "But Gran says sleeping things still have taste."

I bought my oil and my salt and got out before anyone older than him could catch me in the lanes, and I climbed home in the dark with the smell of bread chasing me up the path like a dog that wants following, and I ate turnip stew alone and told myself it was fine. That it was good. That invisibility was not loneliness, it was safety, and safety was worth any price, and hadn't the ledger proved it, two hundred years of entries in my grandmother's hand and my mother's and now mine, all saying the same thing: *all quiet, all cold, all well.*

Two hundred years of nothing happening. And here's what I understood, stirring a pot for one: nothing happening is only peace if there's something else in your life happening instead. Otherwise nothing happening is just nothing, stretched thin over everything, and you call it contentment because the alternative word is too heavy to lift every day.

* * *

I'll admit one thing to this page that I admit to no one with a face: I talk to Tam.

Not aloud. Never aloud — the mountain carries sound strangely, and the last thing a keeper needs is a shepherd hearing the mad heir conversing with empty air. But in the ledger, in the margins, in the little shorthand script Mother invented for private notes, I write to him. Small things. *Turnip bit Vale today. Deserved.* Or: *Found a hawk feather on the seventh stone. Left it.* Or, once a year, on the night that matters:

*Thirteen years since they took you. The Door is shut. I'm still here. Come home.*

He'd have been twenty-six now. I build him that age in my head every year, adding a year like rings on a tree. Would he have stayed on the mountain? He hated the cold. He'd have gone down to Hollowmere, charmed the baker's entire family, gotten fat on bread, sent up sarcastic letters. He was eleven when the fever took him — that's what the village believes, that's what the grave-marker by the chapel says, a small stone with a small name, and I put juniper on it every year too, because whatever the truth is, an eleven-year-old died somehow, somewhere, and someone should leave flowers.

Mother burned all the letters before she died. All except the ledger, which she left to me with instructions so brief they were nearly a dare: *Keep the counts. Keep the silence. Don't read the pages marked in red.*

The pages marked in red are the thirteenth-year entries. Every thirteen years, going back two centuries, the ledger's neat columns of stone-temperatures and weather stop, and there's a page written in a different hand — heavier, darker ink — recording something that isn't weather. I've never read them. I've had nineteen years of nights alone with that book and I have never read them, because Mother's voice is still in my head saying *don't*, and because a part of me suspects that the moment I understand what my family has actually been doing all these centuries, I will no longer be able to stand being the one who does it.

Invisibility has layers, you see. The village doesn't see me. Fine. But there are rooms inside myself I don't visit either, doors in my own house I keep oiled and shut, and I am keeper of those too.

* * *

On the last evening of the week, I walked the arc of stones at dusk instead of dawn, because the crow was back.

It sat on the crown of the Door like a gargoyle that had unionized, feathers slicked black against the last grey light, and it watched me come up the path with an attention no bird should waste on a woman carrying a broom. I stopped at the foot of the approach. It didn't fly. We regarded one another across thirty feet of frozen mud.

"You're lost," I told it. "This isn't carrion. This isn't anything. Go be a crow somewhere that matters."

It tilted its head, and I swear — I swear on the ledger and all two hundred years of cold stones — that it looked past me. Up. Over my shoulder. At the Door behind me, the way you look at a friend across a room.

Then it dropped off the crown and flew low over my head, wings silent, and was gone into the pines, and the air where it had passed smelled faintly of juniper, though I hadn't yet laid out the evening offering.

I stood there a long moment telling myself sensible things. Birds roost on high points. High points include doors. Juniper grows on this mountain in absurd quantities; the wind was from the north cut, probably; probably.

Then I finished my rounds, because ritual is what you hold onto when sense runs out, and I laid my palm on the first ward-stone, expecting the usual ache of cold.

The stone was warm.

Not hot. Not glowing, not humming, not anything a ballad would bother with. Warm the way a hearthstone stays an hour after the fire dies — warm the way something is when something else has recently been near it. I snatched my hand back as if bitten, knelt, pressed my palm flat again, held it there until my skin remembered what cold was supposed to feel like.

Warm.

I walked the whole arc with my heart going like a smith's hammer. Stone two: cold. Stone three: cold. Four, five, six, seven: cold, cold, cold, cold.

One stone. One degree of warmth, in one place, on one evening, after two hundred years of cold. It would be easy — it would be such a mercy — to call it sun. To call it a fox pissing on it, a buried mole, the world's smallest geological event. I stood there in the dying light assembling explanations like a woman stacking sandbags against a flood, and every bag washed away as fast as I set it down, and underneath all of them, patient as the mountain itself, lay the thing I actually knew:

Doors are warmest on the side where something is waiting.

Above me, on the crown of the Door, in the space the crow had left, the last light failed. Somewhere down in Hollowmere, a bell rang the hour, and farther off than that — so far off I could tell myself I imagined it — something that was not a bell answered.

I went inside. I lit the lamp. I opened the ledger to a fresh page, and my hand shook only a little as I wrote the entry I had never once written in nine years of keeping:

*Stone one: warm.*

Then, because the margins are where the true record lives, I added in Mother's shorthand, addressed as always to a brother who couldn't read it:

*Tam. Something knocked.*
