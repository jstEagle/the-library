## 2. Prayers Answered

There are two kinds of quiet in the mountains, and I have made a study of both.

The first kind is the honest quiet — wind through the scree, a hawk's cry, the creak of the ward-stones settling as the temperature drops at dusk. That quiet asks nothing of you. It is the quiet of a world minding its own business.

The second kind is the quiet of something holding its breath.

For thirty-one years I have lived with both, and for most of them I could tell them apart without thinking. That morning in late autumn, when I came up the goat-path to make my rounds, the second kind was sitting on the mountain like an uninvited guest who'd brought his own chair.

I noticed it before I noticed anything else. The birds had gone. Not scattered — gone, the way they go when a storm is still three valleys away but already decided about you. And the gate itself, that great slab of black stone set into the mountain's shoulder like a door someone had bricked over and then regretted, seemed to have stopped being a thing and started being a *presence*.

"Morning," I said to it anyway. You talk to a sealed god-door the same way you talk to a skittish horse: evenly, and never letting on what you know.

The gate said nothing back, which was correct. Gates that answer are how villages end up as cautionary tales.

My rounds were simple enough that a child could do them, which was rather the point. Mother had drilled the routine into me until it wore grooves in my skull like water wears stone. Walk the perimeter. Check the seven ward-stones for cracks, frost-heave, or lichen — lichen being apparently the sneakiest of the three, though Mother never explained why a plant posed a threat to a seal laid down by gods or their contractors. Press your palm flat to each stone and count to ten. If the stone is cold, all is well. If the stone is warm, run.

Thirty-one years of cold stones. Thirty-one years of counting to ten seventy times a week, which comes out to roughly thirty-six thousand seconds of my life spent with my hand pressed against rock, listening for warmth that never came.

I used to think of it as a job. Later I understood it was more like a vigil nobody had asked me to keep, inherited from a mother who'd inherited it from hers, all the way back to whoever had built this door and decided that keeping it shut was worth one family's entire future. There were days — many days — when I resented the anonymity of it. The village of Hollowmere sat six miles down-valley, and if you asked anyone there who kept the Thornfell gate, you'd get a shrug, or worse, a story about the Okafor women being odd, touched, hermit-blooded, best left alone. They half-remembered us the way you half-remember a debt: with a vague discomfort and a strong desire to change the subject.

That suited me fine, mostly. Invisibility is a skill like any other. I'd gotten good at trading with the smugglers without learning their real names, good at buying salt in Hollowmere twice a year from the one shopkeeper too old to gossip, good at being a shape in the corner of people's vision that their eyes slid off. It's remarkable how thoroughly a person can vanish while standing in full view, provided she never gives anyone a reason to look.

But invisibility has a cost, and the cost is that on certain evenings — the long ones, the ones where the wind dies and the fire pops and there's nothing between you and your own head — the silence gets loud. I'd taken to talking to the gate on those nights. Telling it about the weather, the goats, the smugglers' terrible poetry. Mother would have been horrified. Mother talked to the gate too, though; she just pretended she didn't.

Anyway. That morning, with the birds gone and the mountain holding its breath, I did my rounds faster than usual and told myself it was because of the cold coming on.

It wasn't the cold.

* * *

The shepherd found me at midday, or rather his dog found me, barking its fool head off at the treeline above the eastern ward-stone until I followed it up through the pines.

He was lying in a hollow beneath a granite outcrop, an old man in a coat older than him, one leg bent under him at an angle legs don't go. A sheep lay beside him, dead maybe an hour, throat torn — not by wolves. Wolf-kills are messy and frightened. This was clean, surgical, and there was no blood around the wound, which is wrong in a way I can't better describe than *wrong*.

"Hallo," I said, dropping to my knees beside him. "Don't move. What happened?"

"Fell," he wheezed. "Scree gave way. Below the high pasture." His face was grey as the granite. "Girl. Girl, listen. My name's Aldous Crane. I've got a daughter in Lowmark. Someone has to—"

"I'll see she knows. First let's get you warm." I shrugged off my pack, dug for the flask, the blanket, the little kit of things you carry when you live alone on a haunted mountain. My hands knew the motions. My ears were on something else — that held-breath quiet had deepened since I'd entered the trees, and beneath it, so faint I thought at first it was my own pulse, was a sound like slow water moving under ice.

Aldous Crane caught my wrist. His grip was astonishingly strong for a dying man, the grip of someone spending his last coins all at once.

"Heard something," he whispered. "After I fell. Before Tam—" He stopped. Blinked. "Before the dog went for help. Something on the other side of that door."

My stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution. "What door? There's no door up here."

"The old door. Thornfell." His eyes were fever-bright and fixed on mine. "You're the keeper's girl. Everyone says there's no keeper anymore, but I've seen your smoke. I know a tended fire when I see one."

Wonderful. So much for invisibility. I put the blanket over him and said, in my flattest voice, "You hit your head, Master Crane."

"I prayed," he said.

The word dropped into the hollow between us like a stone into a well, and I swear the well listened.

"I'm not a praying man," he went on, faster now, words tumbling. "Haven't been since the fever took my wife. But I was lying there with my leg broke and the dark coming on, and I thought — the old ones used to pray at Thornfell, didn't they? Before the sealing. My grandmother said so. Prayers at the gate went straight down, she said, no priest needed. So I prayed. I said, *whoever's listening, I don't want to die here.*"

"And?" I asked, though every instinct I possessed screamed at me not to.

"And something answered." His hand tightened on my wrist until it hurt. "Not a voice. More like... like warmth. Like a hearth you feel through a wall. And it said — it didn't say it, it *was* it, all at once inside my head — it said, **what will you give**."

The sound beneath the ice got a fraction louder. Or I got a fraction better at hearing it. Same thing, probably.

"What did you give?" I asked quietly.

"Nothing yet." His breath rattled. "It's waiting. That's the trouble with the old bargains, girl — they don't take, they *lend*. And the interest—" He coughed then, a wet horrible sound, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do: he wasn't going to survive the trip down, not with that leg, not with the light already going amber in the pines.

I splinted the leg anyway, because the alternative was admitting what we both knew, and because work keeps dread in its cage. While I worked, he talked, the rambling talk of the dying, and I catalogued every word of it for later.

"They say the gate's been shut a hundred years," he murmured. "Sealed by the builders. But seals are doors too, aren't they? Doors only open one way. Things can still..." His eyes rolled toward the peak, toward the black slab I couldn't see from here but could feel, the way you feel a tooth you're trying not to think about. "It heard me. After a hundred years, it *heard* me."

"Old stories stretch," I said. "They get taller every telling."

"You felt it too." Not a question. "This morning. The quiet. I've wintered on this mountain forty years, girl. I know when it's holding its breath."

I tied off the splint and sat back on my heels. The truth was I had felt it, and lying to a dying man sat poorly even next to the necessity of it. So I gave him a different coin instead.

"Master Crane. Your prayer. Whatever answered it — don't answer it back. Don't promise it anything, don't thank it, don't even think grateful thoughts in its direction. Can you do that?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Dying men look at you differently; there's nothing left to perform for.

"It's still listening," he said. "Isn't it. That's why you're asking."

* * *

He died an hour after full dark.

I want to be clear about the sequence of events, because the sequence is the whole horror of it. I stayed with him. Built a fire, fed him broth he couldn't swallow, held his hand when the shivering started. Around the ninth hour his breathing went shallow, then spaced out, then — between one rattle and the next — stopped. Cleanly, quietly, the way a candle goes out. I closed his eyes and arranged his hands and sat back to grieve a stranger properly, because someone should.

And then the fire *leaned*.

There's no other word for it. The flames, which had been standing straight in the dead-calm air, all tilted at once toward the east. Toward the gate. And in the space where Aldous Crane's breathing had been, the silence filled up with something that was not a voice and not a presence but a kind of *attention*, vast and patient and pleased, the way a spider is pleased.

**Paid in full,** said the attention, everywhere at once, inside the fire and inside my skull and inside, I am quite sure, the bones of the mountain. **One prayer. One death. A fair exchange, fairly concluded. The account stands open.**

"There's no account," I said aloud, to a corpse and a campfire, because the alternative was saying nothing and letting it have the last word. "He owed you nothing. Nobody's prayed at this gate in a hundred years. You don't get to start a ledger now."

The attention turned on me the way a searchlight turns. I felt it read me — actually *read* me, top to bottom, the calluses on my hands and the loneliness under my ribs and something else, something it lingered on with unmistakable interest. My right palm began to prickle.

**Little warden,** it said, and there was something almost fond in it, which was infinitely worse than menace. **How long since anyone checked the stones? How long since anyone counted?**

"I counted this morning," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Cold as ever. All seven."

**Come and see,** it said.

And it withdrew — not gone, never gone, but folded away like a hand closing, leaving the night ordinary again. Ordinary except for the fire leaning east. Ordinary except that my palm itched like a healing scar.

* * *

I ran the perimeter that same night, by lantern-light, which Mother would have called reckless and Mother, I suspect, never faced what I was facing. Seven ward-stones. Seven counts of ten.

Stones one through five: cold. Cold as riverbed rock, cold as a dead man's ring. I let myself breathe after the fifth.

Stone six stood at the mouth of the smugglers' cut, a squat pillar carved — like all its siblings — with spirals and interlocking knots that predated any alphabet I knew. I pressed my palm flat. Counted. One. Two. Three.

At four, the stone was warm.

Not sun-warm, not geothermal-warm. Warm like a sleeping animal. Warm like skin. And beneath my hand, faint but unmistakable, came a rhythm — slow, deep, regular — that I recognized against every scrap of my will, because it was the rhythm of something breathing.

I snatched my hand back. The warmth stayed. As I watched, the faint glow of my lantern caught the carvings along the stone's face, and the dust in the spiral grooves — decades of it, centuries of it — was drifting. Moving. Drawn inward, down into the stone, like flour into dough, like anything into anything hungry.

Stone seven, at the gate itself, was worse. Stone seven was hot. I stood in front of the great black slab with my lantern shaking in my fist and felt the heat radiating off it the way heat comes off a banked forge, and behind the door — behind a hundred years of seal and silence — something exhaled, long and slow, and the frost on the gate's face sublimated away in a single breath of warm air that smelled, absurdly, horribly, of woodsmoke and baking bread. Hearth-smell. Home-smell. The smell it had worn, presumably, when it said *what will you give* to a broken old shepherd and meant it kindly.

I did the math standing there, because math is what I do when I am too frightened to do anything else. Seven stones. One warm yesterday — no. None warm yesterday. One warm now, and another hot. Two of seven, in a single day, after thirty-one years of nothing. Whatever Aldous Crane's prayer had cracked open, it wasn't healing. Seals don't fray all at once. They fray the way rope frays — strand by strand, each one bearing more than it should, until the load finds the last one.

And I knew — the way you know the ending of a story from its first line — that the remaining strands would not hold forever. That something on the other side had waited a hundred years, patient as interest accruing, and had just discovered the door had a hinge after all.

I pressed my forehead against the hot stone, which was either courage or stupidity, and said, "I'm still here. Whatever you are. Whoever's left. I'm still here, and I'm still counting."

Deep beneath my feet, unhurried, unbothered, the mountain breathed in.

And in my right palm — for the first time in my life — the birthmark I'd carried since the day I was born began, very softly, to burn.
