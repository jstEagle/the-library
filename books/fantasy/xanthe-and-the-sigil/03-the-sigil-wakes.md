## 3. The Sigil Wakes

The mark on my palm had been with me since before memory, which meant it had been with me longer than anything else I owned. A birthmark, the midwife had called it, according to Mother—a smudge of darker skin in the cup of my hand, the kind of thing you stopped seeing because it was always there. Like the gate. Like me.

I'd spent twenty-six years not looking at it.

That morning, for no reason I could name, I looked.

I was mending a ward-line on the gate's western flank, kneeling in the frost-brittle grass with a needle and spool of silvered thread, stitching warmth back into a stone that had gone cold as a dead man's promise. Routine work. Ten years of routine work. The kind of work that lets you pretend the world is a machine that runs itself and you are merely its janitor, unremarked and unthanked, which is exactly how a person raised on stories of what happened to keepers who got *noticed* likes to think about herself.

The needle slipped.

Not my fault—the stone shivered under my hand like something waking from a bad dream—and the needle went into the meat of my palm, right through the birthmark, deep enough to hurt properly. I swore. I don't swear often; swearing is a form of being noticed, even by goats. But this was a red-letter occasion, so: "Damn."

And then the blood came out wrong.

It didn't bead. It didn't run. It rose—there's no better word for it—it rose off the wound like steam off a spring morning, except it wasn't steam, it was *light*, thin and gold and rising in threads, and every thread found its way into the dark lines of the birthmark the way water finds cracks in clay.

The pain was extraordinary.

I want to be clear that I have a professional relationship with pain. Cold hands, split knuckles, the long gray ache of solitude—I know all these like old neighbors. This was different. This was fire being *written* into me, letter by letter, and I sat down hard in the frozen grass and watched my own palm turn into something out of the worst story Mother never told.

Lines. That's the only way I can describe it to anyone who hasn't felt their skin become a page. Lines branching off lines, fine as spider silk, precise as the work of a jeweler with forty years and one perfect piece ahead of him. They burned themselves deeper than skin. I could feel them settling into muscle, into bone, into whatever part of a person holds her name.

And I knew them.

That was the horror of it—not the pain, not the light, but the recognition. Because I have spent ten years tracing those same lines on the gate with my eyes, running my fingers over the carved channels when the smugglers weren't watching, wondering idly what mad stonemason had cut spirals within spirals within spirals into a mountain door. I knew the pattern the way you know your own handwriting.

My palm was wearing the gate.

Or—said a small cold voice at the bottom of me—the gate has been wearing me all along, and has only now decided to show its face.

* * *

I did what any sensible woman would do. I wrapped my hand in bandages, told myself it was an infection, told myself infections glowed sometimes (they do not), and went about my day with the grim competence of a person losing an argument with herself.

By noon the bandage was warm. By dusk it was humming.

I don't mean singing. I mean a hum, low and constant, like a struck bell heard through a wall—except there was no wall between the sound and me, because the sound was coming from inside my hand, resonating up the bones of my forearm into my chest until I could feel my heartbeat rearranging itself around it. When I pressed the bandage flat, the hum answered the pressure. When I let go, it swelled.

The gate, half a mile down the valley, hummed back.

I stood at my window in the dark and listened to them call to each other across the cold air, my hand and the mountain, and understood with total clarity that the era of my invisibility was over. Whatever this was, it hadn't asked permission. It hadn't crept up politely over months, giving me time to arrange things, to leave notes, to disappear the way keepers are supposed to disappear when they stop being useful. It had simply *declared* itself, in light, in sound, on the one part of my body the whole world can see when I shake their hands.

There was nothing for it but the village. I hated that. Let me say it plainly: I hated it the way you hate a tooth you need pulled. Thornfell village sits four miles down-valley, and in ten years I had been there perhaps six times, always hooded, always at dusk, buying salt and lamp oil from the chandler who never asked questions because I paid in old coins he pretended not to examine too closely. The village half-remembers my family. Half-remembering is worse than forgetting. Forgetting lets you walk past a house without crossing yourself. Half-remembering makes people watch your hands.

But I needed a priestess, and Thornfell had exactly one.

* * *

Sef's shrine was less a building than a stubbornness—a stone circle older than the village around it, roofed in living hawthorn that nobody dared prune. She kept a fire going in the middle of it year-round, which should have been impossible, and I had learned long ago not to ask about things that should have been impossible. In Thornfell, asking is how you get answers.

She was feeding the fire when I came in, and she didn't look up, and she said:

"Show me your hand."

I stopped three steps in. "Good evening to you too."

"It's been calling since dawn." Now she looked at me, and her eyes were the color of the frost outside, pale and hard and very, very awake. "All day, Jonah Okafor. Every ward-stone on that mountain ringing like a dinner bell, and every old soul in this village pretending they can't hear it, and here you come walking in at moonrise with your arm wrapped up like a wounded soldier. Show me your hand."

How does she know my name? You're wondering. So was I, every time. Sef had a talent for knowing things she shouldn't, which is either holiness or eavesdropping, and in my experience the line runs thinner than most priests admit.

"I burned it," I said. "On the stove."

"Jonah."

"Boiling water."

"*Jonah.*"

There's a tone some people get, when they've buried enough people and prayed enough prayers, where politeness stops being available to them. Sef had reached it somewhere around my third lie. I stood in the hawthorn shadow with my heart going like a smith's hammer and unwound the bandage.

The light hit the shrine first.

Every flame in the fire leaned toward my palm—all of them, a dozen little tongues bending sideways like grass in wind, straining at my hand the way flowers strain at sun. And the sigil drank the light. That's what it did; I watched it happen; it took the firelight in through those fine branching lines and turned it gold, and threw patterns onto the hawthorn leaves above us—patterns I recognized, spirals within spirals, the gate's whole architecture written small and burning on the body of a woman whose entire life strategy had been *don't look at me*.

Sef made a sound I had never heard from her. Not a gasp. Something lower, older, wrenched up from wherever she keeps the things she prays with.

Then she went down on her knees.

On the frozen ground, in front of me, the priestess of Thornfell—who had once told the reeve to his face that his tithe math insulted the gods—knelt, and took my bandaged hand in both of hers with a gentleness that frightened me more than the kneeling, and said:

"Daughter of the builders."

"I'm not—" My voice cracked. I steadied it. "I'm a keeper. Just a keeper. There's no builders anymore, there hasn't been—"

"You are the last of them." Her thumb traced the outermost spiral, careful as a woman handling a live coal, and where she touched, the sigil flared. "Look at it. Look at it properly, girl, and stop lying to both of us. Who else could carry it? It's slept in your family for thirteen generations, waiting for the blood to thin enough that it must wake or die. And it woke in *you*."

"The gate's seal is failing," I said, because saying true things seemed suddenly safer than saying false ones. "The stones are warm. Prayers are being answered. Something's listening."

"Yes."

"And this—" I lifted my burning hand between us, and the hawthorn shadows wheeled around the shrine like a carousel of old, terrible geometry. "What am I, then? What did it make me?"

Sef rose, slowly, joints cracking like the frost, and looked at me the way I imagine you look at a door you've knocked on your whole life the day it finally opens.

"Not *made*," she said. "*Chosen*. Or chosen-for, which may be crueler. Your people built that gate, Jonah. Built it and bound it and wrote themselves into the lock so that someone of their blood would always hold the key." She paused, and her frost-pale eyes did something complicated. "The key, child. Not the lock. Do you understand the difference?"

"No," I said honestly.

"Neither do I, fully, and that frightens me more than the glowing." She turned to her fire, and her voice dropped into the register she used for scripture. "But I know the stories my grandmother's grandmother told, and they all agree on one point: when the sigil wakes in the last builder, the thirteenth moon will begin to wane, and what waits beneath the mountain will begin to count the nights."

Above us, through the hawthorn and the stars, the moon hung fat and white and full.

Full, I thought. And beginning to fall.

My palm pulsed against the cold air, once, twice—like a second heart learning its rhythm—and far up the valley, muffled by miles of stone and snow, the gate hummed its answer.

Thirteen turns, Sef had said once, years ago, in the careless way people say dreadful things they half-believe are only stories.

I flexed my fingers and watched the light run the spirals, and understood that the story had just started believing in me.
