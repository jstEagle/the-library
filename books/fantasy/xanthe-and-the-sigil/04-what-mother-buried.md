## 4. What Mother Buried

Grief, I have learned, is mostly a logistics problem.

Nobody tells you that. They tell you grief is a wave, an ocean, a long dark night of the soul. What they don't tell you is that it's also a great deal of carrying things from one room to another and deciding whether or not to keep them. My mother died in the spring, three years ago now, and I am still deciding. The house above the gate-warden's cottage was hers, and hers before her, and I live in the cottage and leave the house to its dust like a woman leaving a letter unopened because she already knows what it says.

But the sigil changed the arithmetic. A birthmark that turns out to be a key changes all the arithmetic.

So on the morning after Sef pressed her thumb against my palm and called me *daughter of the builders*, I did what any sensible heir would do with a legacy of gods and debts: I put the kettle on, drank two cups of tea for courage, and went up to the house to go through my dead mother's things at last.

* * *

The house smelled the way it always smelled—woodsmoke and dried lavender and something older underneath, stone-and-iron, the smell of the mountain itself seeping up through the floors. Dust lay on everything in soft grey drifts, undisturbed even by spiders, as if the house had been holding its breath since she died.

I started in the kitchen, because you start where the living happened. Cups, crockery, a tin of tea gone to brick-dust. Nothing. The parlor next. Her sewing box, full of thread in colors she never wore. A bundle of letters tied with string, all from a cousin in Lowmark who wrote about weather and weddings and nothing else; I read three and gave up. Nothing.

It wasn't until I climbed to her bedroom that I understood I'd been circling. Mothers hide things the way rivers hide currents—the danger isn't where the water looks deepest but where it runs smoothest. My mother's bedroom had always run very smooth indeed. Bare walls. A bed made so tight you could bounce a coin off it. A single candle stub, a comb, a hairbrush with silvering hairs still caught in it—I stopped touching that one pretty quickly—and a chest at the foot of the bed, plain oak, iron-banded, with no lock.

No lock. In a house whose family had kept a sealed gate for longer than anyone could remember, a chest with no lock. Because the best hiding place for a thing nobody should find is in plain sight, under nothing, protected by nothing except the certainty that no one would dare open their dead mother's chest while she lived, and would be too busy crying when she didn't.

I sat down on the cold floorboards in front of it and let myself be angry at her for a moment, properly, the way I hadn't let myself be at the funeral, where I'd been too busy making sure the village saw the keeper's daughter weeping decorously. Anger is easier than the other thing. The other thing was sitting in my chest like swallowed river water.

Then I lifted the lid.

Wool blankets, moth-nipped at the edges. My father's good coat—he'd been dead twenty years, and she'd kept his coat the way you keep a debt, folded and smelling faintly of him still. Under the blankets, wrapped in oilcloth, a flat wooden box, and inside the box:

Paper.

A ledger, leather-bound, its corners worn round. And beneath it a smaller packet of papers, and beneath those a key—not to this chest, which needed none, but heavy and old, black iron, the kind of key you feel in your pocket like a held breath.

I opened the ledger.

* * *

Here is what I remembered while I looked at it, because memory has terrible timing and chose that moment to arrive:

I was seven, maybe eight. Tam was ten. It was midwinter, the thirteenth winter of my mother's keeping, though I hadn't known then that the number mattered. There had been a night when Mother got up from supper without finishing, without a word, and walked out into the snow toward the gate, and Tam and I followed her—we were children, we were stupid with love and boredom—and watched through the falling white as she knelt at the sealed doors and laid something down on the threshold.

Not a prayer. Not flowers. Something small and dark, wrapped in cloth, that she placed with both hands, careful as a midwife laying a newborn down.

And then she spoke—to the door, not past it—and though the wind took most of it, I caught three words, carried back to us on the cold air like a coin tossed over a shoulder:

*"Paid. In full."*

Tam squeezed my hand so hard it hurt and said, "Come on, Jonah," in a voice that was far too old for him, and he marched us home through the snow and neither of us ever spoke of it, because children learn early which silences are load-bearing.

* * *

The ledger confirmed what the child in me had seen and refused to understand.

It was a record of payments. Thirteen-year intervals, going back further than my mother's hand—the earliest entries were in a different script entirely, cramped and formal, and the dates ran back past the founding of the village, past anything, into years counted differently, by moons rather than kings. Each entry the same shape:

*Thirteenth turn of the moon. Paid to the court beneath: [entry]. Received: the gate holds.*

The payments varied. In the oldest entries they were strange beyond guessing—a year of someone's sleep. The sound of a village's bells. *One name, freely given*, written in ink that had gone brown as old blood. Later entries grew more mundane and more awful in equal measure: coin, in sums that explained how a family of quiet gate-keepers stayed quietly wealthy enough to never want. Livestock. Once, chillingly: *a memory, minor (the color of the sea)*, and beside it, in my mother's later hand, a note: *Grandmother never once mentioned the sea. I thought it was grief. It was payment.*

Page after page, turn after turn. Every thirteen years. Every entry closed with the same formula, in whatever hand held the pen that century: *Paid. In full.*

And then I reached my mother's entries, and my own lifetime, and the arithmetic turned ugly.

My mother became keeper thirty-nine years ago. Thirty-nine years. Three turns of thirteen. Three payments in her hand. The first two were coin, large sums, drawn from nowhere I could trace—and I knew our money, I had counted our money, and there had never been sums like that. Unless there had been, and I simply hadn't been shown the well they came from.

The third entry was dated thirteen years ago. The winter I was seven. The night in the snow.

I read it four times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything kinder, no matter how many times I gave them the chance.

*Thirteenth turn. Paid to the court beneath: one soul, male child of the line, freely offered. Received: the gate holds, and the heir lives.*

*The heir lives.*

I sat on the floor of my mother's bedroom with the ledger open across my knees and felt the sentence go through me like a fishhook finding flesh. *The heir.* Singular. Chosen. Tam and I had both been born of her body; I had never once thought of us as *heir* and *other*. But the ledger thought in singulars. The ledger had always thought in singulars.

And there was supposed to be more. Below the entry, below the formula, there should have been a final line—the ledger recorded every payment's *name*. That was its whole grim discipline: names given, names received, nothing unwitnessed. Two hundred years of entries, each with a name attached, however strange the currency. Even the grandmother's sea-colored memory had a name beside it.

This entry had a blank space where the name belonged.

And the page had been torn.

Not ripped in passion—torn cleanly, carefully, along the spine, by someone who wanted the removal to look like damage rather than decision. The stub of the missing page was still wedged in the binding. I worked it loose with my thumbnail, hands not entirely steady, and held the ragged scrap up to the window light.

There was writing on the reverse of the following page, and ink bites through paper when a pen presses hard, and grief makes people press hard.

Mirror-reversed, ghosted through the fiber, I could just make out the pressure-marks of what had been written on the torn page itself. Most of it was lost. But the first line survived whole, pressed deep enough to read backward:

*Tam —*

Just that. A salutation. My brother's name, the first word of a letter that no longer existed, addressed by my mother to the son she had—

I couldn't finish the thought. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of the season. I sat there with a scrap of paper and a hook in my chest and I could not make the sentence complete itself, because every version of it ended with *sold*, and every version of it ended with my mother's careful handwriting, and those two things refused to occupy the same world.

*Freely offered,* the ledger said. Freely offered by whom?

Children don't sign contracts. Whatever was given, whoever gave it—it was given over his head. And "freely offered" is such a clean little phrase, such a tidy legal flower, and it does not say *who* did the offering. It does not say whether a ten-year-old boy was asked, or told, or drugged, or deceived. It only says the court accepted the fiction, because the court, I was beginning to suspect, cares about the letter of things the way a noose cares about the neck.

And why Tam? Why not me? The ledger said *the heir*—and Sef had pressed her thumb to *my* palm yesterday and called me daughter of the builders. If I was the heir, then the payment should have been mine. Unless—

Unless the payment was made *instead* of me. Unless someone had gone to the court beneath and traded my brother's soul for mine, the way you'd trade a copper for a coin of gold, and had done it in secret, and had torn the page out so that I would never, ever know.

Or unless it was worse than that. There is always a worse than that. Grief teaches you the shape of the staircase.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the light through the window moved from grey to gold and started back down toward grey again. Long enough that my legs went numb beneath me and I stopped feeling them, which felt appropriate.

The rest of the packet waited. A handful of loose papers: instructions for ward-tending in my mother's precise hand. A genealogy, six centuries of Okafor names marching down the margins—and here I noticed, with a small cold click, that every keeper's line ended with a single named heir, and that second children were marked not with names but with a small symbol I now recognized, because I had spent yesterday tracing it in light: the sigil. Marked like livestock. Marked like *currency*. Second children of the builders' line weren't heirs. They were reserves.

And at the bottom of the box, the black iron key, and one more paper, folded twice, newer than the rest, the ink barely faded.

It was a letter, unfinished, unsent, addressed in my mother's hand to *Sef, keeper of the village's faith, who will hate me and should*.

I read it standing up, because I suddenly could not bear to sit.

*If you are reading this, Jonah, then the sigil woke, which means the turn has come early or I failed to prepare you, and both are likely, because I was a coward in the specific way of mothers...*

*I did not sell your brother.*

Four words, and the hook in my chest twisted so hard I made a sound I'm not proud of.

*Understand me. I would have sold myself a hundred times over and slept well after. But the covenant does not accept the willing keeper twice—the gate must hold a soul that belongs to the line but not to the seal. You are the seal, Jonah. Your mark is the lock. The court cannot take you while you stand above and hold; that is the whole architecture of what our family built. So when the turn came, the debt demanded a soul of the line, and there was only one soul of the line that was not the lock itself.*

*He heard us talking. He was ten and he heard us talking, and he came to me in the night and said, "Take mine instead, Mum. She checks the wards better anyway." As if it were a chore. As if he were offering to wash the dishes.*

*Freely offered. He offered. God forgive me, I let him. The court requires the offer be true, and it was, it was horribly, terribly true, and I signed his name in the ledger and then I tore the page out because I could not bear—*

The letter stopped there. No signature. The ink ended mid-sentence, the way my mother had ended mid-sentence for three years of silence in that smooth-running room, and I stood holding the last words she had ever tried to say to anyone and understood that I would never know whether she finished it in her heart or simply set it down one day and let the dust do the confessing.

*He offered.* Ten years old. Offering his soul like dishwater, because his little sister checked the wards better.

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, half sob, and I pressed the letter to my mouth like I could breathe it back into her.

So: not a monster. A coward, by her own confession, and worse—a protector. Everything I had, every quiet contented year of invisibility behind the gate, had been purchased with my brother, and purchased *for* me, and hidden from me by two people who loved me: my mother with her torn page, and Tam with his ten-year-old arithmetic. *She checks the wards better anyway.* He'd probably believed it. He'd probably gone down into the dark believing he was doing a chore, and the court beneath had taken him at his word and smiled with its hollow beautiful mouth, and somewhere under the mountain my brother was smiling too, wrong, remade, while I above him drank tea and checked wards and called my loneliness a life.

The kettle downstairs had long gone cold. Outside, the light was dying, and somewhere on the mountain the ward-stones were warm, warm for the first time in decades, counting down toward a thirteenth turn I finally, truly understood.

I folded the letter along its original creases. I put the ledger in my coat, and the black iron key, and the scrap of the torn page, because evidence is a comfort to people who need the world to make sense and I am beginning to suspect it never will again.

Then I picked up my lamp and went down through the blue dusk toward the village, toward the chapel where Sef kept her candles and her terrible clarity, because there was a covenant to read and a debt to reckon and I intended to walk into that conversation knowing exactly what had been paid, by whom, and in whose name.

The letter had said *who will hate me and should*. Well. Sef could hate my mother all she liked.

Me, I had a new job now, and it wasn't keeping the gate.

It was getting my brother back.
