## 5. Thirteen Turns

I have been a priestess of Thornfell for thirty-one years, and in all that time I have learned exactly one thing worth teaching: the gods do not want your love. They want your attention. Everything else is negotiable.

The village of Hollowmere had, for three generations, given them neither. We kept the old festivals the way you keep a coat that no longer fits — out of habit, hung at the back of the door. We lit candles to names we'd half-forgotten. We said *the mountain keeps us* without asking what the mountain got out of the arrangement. And every thirteen years, when the moon began its long waning toward its thirteenth turn, we looked away from the gate road and pretended not to notice the Okafor woman walking it.

I was young the last time the debt came due. I remember my teacher, old Mother Ilex, standing at this same window with her knuckles white on the sill, watching smoke rise from the direction of the gate house, and saying nothing. When I asked her what was happening she said, "A payment," in the voice you use for a funeral, and then she made me sweep the temple floor until my hands bled, which I understood later was less about cleanliness and more about keeping a girl's mind from wandering toward questions.

Mother Ilex is eleven years dead now. The sweeping falls to me.

And tonight, Jonah Okafor is sitting in my temple with her hand wrapped in cloth like something wounded, asking me questions that have no merciful answers.

* * *

She had come an hour past sundown, hammering on the temple door as if the wood owed her money. I opened it to find her lit from within — there is no other way to describe it. The sigil on her palm shone through the linen wrapping like a coal through ash, gold-white light pulsing in time with her heartbeat, and her face above it was the face of someone who has run a long way and isn't sure running helped.

"Priestess." She didn't wait for an invitation. That, at least, was honest of her; most people who come to temples want the ritual first, the kneeling and the incense, the comfortable machinery of devotion between them and whatever they actually came to say. Jonah walked straight past the altar of the Nine and stood in front of me with her burning hand held out like a summons. "You knew. You saw this mark at the well today and you called me *daughter of the builders*, and then you walked away. So either you were being poetic or you know what this is. Which."

Not a question, really. I liked her immediately, in the way one likes a lit fuse.

"You'd better sit down," I said.

"I don't want to sit down."

"Nobody ever does." I poured two cups of plum wine anyway, because some conversations go better with something to hold, and because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. "But you will wish you had by the end. Sit down, Keeper."

She flinched at the word. Then she sat.

* * *

Where do I begin such a telling? I suppose where Mother Ilex began hers, though she delivered it to a room of novices over three nights, with diagrams, and I have one night and one frightened woman and a wine that has gone sadly vinegary.

"The gate was not built to keep something *in*," I told her. "Everyone in Hollowmere believes that. It's a comforting belief. The gate was built to keep something *out* — no. No, that's wrong too. Let me start again."

Jonah waited. She was good at waiting; I could see it in her, the patience of someone who has spent years doing quiet work alone. It occurred to me, not for the first time since the well, that the Okafors had been raised for exactly this — that invisibility itself might be a kind of training.

"The mountain is hollow," I said. "Not hollow the way an egg is hollow. Hollow the way a lung is hollow. There is a court beneath Thornfell — a real court, with a Regent and nobles and laws, older than any kingdom above. The old gods that your grandmother's grandmother prayed to, the ones in the festival songs? They are that court's servants. Or its children. Or its food. The songs disagree."

"And the gate?"

"The gate is the border. The builders — your family, Jonah, your blood going back further than any ledger in this village — built it after the last war between above and below. A war nobody won, so they did what sensible exhausted people do: they negotiated. And the price of the peace was—" I turned my cup in my hands. "A soul. One soul, given willingly to the court beneath, every thirteen turns of the thirteenth moon. In exchange, the gate stays shut, and the court stays beneath, and the gods stay quiet, and Hollowmere keeps its harvests and its children and its small unremarkable life."

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside a dog started up and thought better of it.

"One soul," Jonah said. Her voice was very flat, the flatness of ice before it breaks. "Every thirteen years."

"Not every year of the thirteen. Only once each cycle — when the thirteenth moon completes its waning. The court counts time strangely. Thirteen moons, thirteen turns, and on the last night of the last turn, the debt comes due." I looked at her wrapped hand, still glowing faintly through the linen. "Your family paid it. Every cycle. For nine hundred years."

"How?" she said. And then, because she was not a fool, she answered herself: "Whose soul?"

I could have softened it. I have watched priests soften things their whole life, wrap the truth in ceremony until the grieving can approach it sideways without looking directly at it. But Jonah Okafor had spent her whole life being managed, being kept ignorant, being *protected*, and I suspected that what she would hate most — more than the truth itself — was one more person deciding she couldn't bear it.

"Volunteers," I said. "From your line. The covenant requires a willing soul, freely offered, of the builders' blood. Your grandmother's brother. Before him, a cousin. Before him—" I shook my head. "The names are in the temple records. Sealed records, that only a priestess may open, and only in a debt-year. I opened them three nights ago when the ward-stones warmed."

"Warm stones," she repeated. "That dying shepherd. His prayer was answered."

"It was. Something beneath heard him and answered, because the seal is thinning, because the debt is coming due, and because—" Here was the part I had rehearsed, walking the temple floor at night, and here was the part that still caught in my throat. "Because the payment is late. Or failed. Or was never made. I don't know which. But Jonah — the covenant needs a soul of your blood, freely given, and the gate knows it. That mark on your hand is the gate recognizing its heir."

* * *

She sat very still for a long moment. I let her.

Outside, the thirteenth moon was rising, three nights into its wane, fat and yellow and slightly wrong — I don't know how else to put it. It has always looked slightly wrong to me in debt-years, like a coin worn past reading.

"My mother," Jonah said finally. "She never told me any of this. She raised me at that gate checking wards and counting smugglers' lanterns and she never once—"

"She was protecting you."

"From what?"

"From knowing what you were *for*." I said it gently, but I said it. "Jonah, listen to me, because this is the part that matters. In the last debt-cycle, forty years ago, the payment was due and your mother — Maren Okafor — went down the gate road with her younger sister. Both of them willing. Both of them marked, as all your bloodline carries the mark dormant. The court beneath chose between them."

"Chose?"

"The covenant doesn't take whoever shows up. It takes what it's owed, and what it's owed is determined below, by laws we don't have. The court refused your mother. They took her sister instead." I watched her face do several things at once. "You have an aunt you've never heard of. There is a reason you've never heard of her."

"And this cycle? This time?" Jonah's hand had come up unconsciously to press against her sternum, against the wrapped palm, and I saw her working it through — the arithmetic of families, who was left, who remained. "There's only me. Me and Tam. Me and—"

She stopped.

I watched the thought arrive in her the way frost arrives across a window — slowly, then all at once, from the edges inward. I had seen this exact moment described in the oldest records, in the hand of a priestess four hundred years dead, who wrote: *and the heir understands, and the understanding is the cruelest hour.* I had hoped she would get there slower.

"Tam," Jonah said. "Tam's name wasn't on the ledger. My mother kept a ledger of payments — I found it, tonight, that's why I came — and every name is written down except his page is torn out. Except he's *gone*. Except she sent him away six months ago and wouldn't say where and cried every time I asked and told me it was for the family's good, and I hated her for it, and she's dead now, and—"

Her breath went ragged. The sigil flared through the linen, bright enough to throw our shadows hard against the temple wall, and for a moment the carved faces of the Nine seemed to lean in around us.

"She sold him," Jonah whispered. "My mother sold my brother."

"No." I leaned forward and took her unwounded wrist — carefully, the way you touch anything that might ignite. "Listen to me, child, because this distinction is the whole of everything. The covenant requires a *willing* soul. Freely offered. Your mother could not have sold Tam any more than she could have sold the moon. If Tam went down that road, he went down it *choosing* it. Whatever she tore from that ledger, whatever she hid from you — she was hiding a sacrifice, not a sale. There's a difference, and someday, when you're ready, the difference is going to matter more than anything else I tell you tonight."

"But she let him."

"Yes." I did not look away. He deserved better than a softening priestess, and so did she. "She let him, or she asked him, or he offered before she could ask — the records won't say, because your mother sealed her own account. But she let him. And then she raised you in ignorance at the gate of the thing that ate your brother, because the alternative was raising you knowing you were next."

"That I was *collateral*."

The word landed between us like a dropped stone. I let it lie there. Some words need to be allowed their weight.

"Yes," I said. "That is what you were. What you *are*. Not only collateral — the mark makes you something more, the gate's own key and lock and heir, and the court below wants you for reasons beyond the debt, reasons I only partly understand. But yes. Your whole childhood, every ward you checked, every year of quiet invisible careful life — that was your mother building a vessel strong enough to survive what's coming. Whether she was right to do it that way, whether kindness would have killed you softer — those are questions for people with more wisdom than me. I only know she loved you. I know it the way I know the tide. Mothers in your line have been making this exact choice for nine hundred years."

Jonah pulled her wrist free of my grip. She stood. She walked to the altar of the Nine — the gods of the court beneath, though half this village thinks of them as sky-gods, another convenient error — and she stood there with her burning hand at her side and her back to me, and I watched her shoulders shake exactly twice before she mastered them.

"The shepherd," she said, when she trusted her voice again. "His prayer got answered. People are praying again, aren't they. All over Hollowmere. The gods are listening."

"They're listening because the seal is thinning and the debt is due and the court beneath is hungry for what it's owed." I rose and joined her at the altar, close enough to see the sigil's light reflected in the old gilt of the god-faces. "In three nights' time — no. Fewer than that, now. The night after tomorrow, when the thirteenth moon finishes its waning, the gate will open whether we're ready or not. And if no soul of the builders' blood stands willing on that threshold—"

"What happens?"

"Nine hundred years ago," I said, "the war lasted eleven days. Hollowmere did not exist afterward for two hundred years. The songs call that period the Quiet, and they sing about it as a blessing, because the alternative verses were burned."

* * *

She left shortly after midnight. I gave her what I had: the sealed records to read, a vial of consecrated oil for the wound-light, and the truth, all of it, unvarnished, because she had earned the truth by carrying its weight already without knowing its name.

At the temple door she paused. The moonlight made her look carved, an heirloom figure stepped off some ancient lintel, and the sigil glowed at her side like a lantern being carried into a cellar.

"Why tell me all this?" she asked. "You could have sent me away. Let me keep my little life at the gate until the moon runs out."

"Because in two days," I said, "you are going to walk down that road regardless — I've known you for one evening, Keeper, and even I can see that — and I would rather you walked it knowing what you carry than thinking it's only a birthmark." I hesitated. Then, because a priestess who lies by omission is just a liar with better posture: "And because I pray to the Nine myself, Jonah. Quietly, all these years, when no one was listening. And lately they've begun answering. Do you know what they say?"

She waited.

"*Send her down,*" I said. "*Send the daughter down.*" I met her eyes. "So understand me clearly: whatever waits beneath loves the shape of your coming. It has wanted you a long time. Go armed with that knowledge, at least."

Something moved behind her face — fear, certainly, and grief, but underneath both a hard bright grain of something that the Okafors must have carried through nine centuries of payments, something that refuses.

"Then they can want," Jonah Okafor said. "Wanting's not having."

She went out into the moonlight, down the temple steps, toward the gate road, and I stood watching until the dark took her.

Then I went inside, knelt before the Nine for the first time in a debt-year, and prayed — not to them, but past them, downward, to whatever in that deep court still remembered being human:

*Be gentle with her. She's the only one they have left.*

Nothing answered. But the candle flames all bent toward the mountain, every one, as if bowing — or as if inhaling.
