## 6. Smuggler's Road

I found Brannoc Vale where everyone said I would and nobody admitted to knowing: the back room of the Drowned Lamb, a tavern in Harrowgate that smelled of wet wool and older sins. He was playing cards with two men who looked like they'd been carved from the same sour wood as the furniture, and losing on purpose, which told me more about him in ten minutes than any reputation could have.

Nobody loses on purpose unless they're buying something.

I waited by the door until the game wound down—Vale folding a hand he could plainly have won, laughing at his own bad luck, sliding coins across the table with the cheerful generosity of a man paying off a debt rather than placing a bet—and then the other two left, and it was just him and me and a candle that had given up on ambition.

"You're the gatekeeper's girl," he said, without looking up from stacking the deck.

"I'm the gatekeeper," I said. "Full stop."

"Course you are." He looked up then. He was maybe fifty, with a face like a map of somewhere unpleasant—a network of old scars and weather lines, all of them leading somewhere you probably didn't want to go. "Jonah Okafor. Come to buy passage under Thornfell. Sit down before someone remembers we're talking."

I sat. "You know why I'm here."

"I know what you'll tell me, which is different." He set the deck down square. "So let's hear it. And mind—" he raised one finger, "—I've heard every version. The treasure hunters who think there's silver under the mountain. The pilgrims who think there's god. The fools who think there's both. What's yours?"

"The truth," I said. "Which is worse than either."

That earned me something—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if he'd kept one. "Go on."

And because lying to Brannoc Vale would have been like lying to a river—I could feel it already deciding where I'd float—I gave him most of it. Not Tam. Not yet. But the sigil, the thinning seal, the thirteenth moon turning overhead even now behind cloud. The prayers answered at the gate by something that should have stayed deaf.

He listened without interrupting, which I later learned was the single most dangerous thing about him.

"My mother traded with smugglers for thirty years," I finished. "She always said Vale roads run true even when Vale men don't. She said if I ever needed the under-mountain way, ask for Brannoc."

Something moved behind his eyes at that. Old weather. "Did she." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, like a man adjusting an instrument. "What did she trade?"

"Salt. Iron nails. Once a year, candles blessed at the gate."

"And never asked what the roads were for."

"She asked once," I said. "The answer made her stop asking."

Vale was quiet for a long moment. Outside, Harrowgate went about its evening—carts, arguments, a dog with opinions—but inside the Drowned Lamb's back room the silence had weight, like water.

"Your mother was the last honest customer I ever had," he said finally. "Everyone since has wanted something out of the dark. She wanted to keep something *in* it." He pushed back his chair and stood. "Two hundred crowns. Half now. You carry your own pack, walk when I walk, stop when I stop, and when I tell you not to speak, you treat your voice like it's the last match in the box. Agreed?"

"That's steep."

"That's cheap," he said. "You'll know how cheap by the second mile."

---

We left three nights later, at the turn of the first moon, because Vale refused to travel on a new moon ("the dark deserves its own company") and refused to travel later than the first turn ("whatever's waking down there will be awake enough by then; no sense arriving fashionably").

He met me at the old quarry mouth above the village, dressed in grey so complete it seemed less like clothing than like an apology for being visible. He carried a hooded lantern he never lit, a coil of rope, a long knife, and a small clay pot whose contents he declined to name.

"What's in the pot?" I asked.

"Insurance," he said.

"Against what?"

"Everything." He handed me a second, identical pot. "Don't open yours unless I say. If I'm dead, open it and pour it in a circle around yourself and stand very still and think small thoughts."

"Think small thoughts."

"Think like a pebble. Pebbles die of old age down there. Everything else dies of attention." He glanced at the sky, where the first moon hung thin as a paring. "Ready?"

No, obviously. "Yes," I said.

"Good. Lying well is half the trade."

The quarry mouth was a wound in the hillside I'd known my whole life—the village children dared each other to its lip and ran away screaming, and the adults pretended it wasn't there, which is the traditional Thornfell method for handling everything. What none of them knew was that the cave floor sloped gently down past the point where daylight reached, and kept going.

Vale lit his lantern at the threshold, and I understood: the light was not for seeing. It was for announcing. *A man enters, armed, prepared, and paying.* Down below, apparently, you wanted things to know exactly what you were. It was the things that had to guess that got eaten.

"Rule one," he said as we stepped in. "Nothing down here is a metaphor. When something tells you it's hungry, it means food. When something offers you a gift, count your fingers after. And when something asks your name—"

"It gets a lie."

"It gets nothing, if you can manage it. Lies have a smell." He started down. "But yes. Lie first. Silence better. Truth only if death is certain and immediate, and even then, haggle."

The first mile was ordinary dark—the kind I'd lived beside my whole life without entering. Stone, drip-water, the small industrious sounds of bats. I almost let myself believe the stories were exaggerated. Almost. Then the walls changed.

It was subtle. One moment the rock was rock; the next it had grain, like wood, or skin. The drips came slower, spaced like breathing. And the dark ahead of us stopped being empty and became *occupied*—not by anything I could see, but by the definite sense that the tunnel was a throat and we were politely being swallowed.

"First taste," Vale said quietly, noticing my step falter. "Most people quit here. Nothing's even happened yet."

"Comforting."

"It's not meant to comfort. It's meant to calibrate." He held up a hand and stopped. Ahead, where the tunnel bent, something glowed: a faint greenish shimmer along a seam in the wall, pulsing slow. "Ward-seam. Your family's work. It runs the whole length of the smugglers' road—old builders' wards, keeping the deep ways closed. See how dim it is?"

It was barely there. A memory of light.

"When your mother was young it burned bright as a lamp," he said. "I've walked this road twenty years. Watched it fade. Like watching a fire go out in slow motion, except the house is still standing and everyone's asleep inside." He started walking again. "Now you know why the price is what it is. I'm not guiding you to treasure, gatekeeper. I'm guiding you toward whatever's been eating the light."

* * *

We walked through the rest of that night and most of the next day, by underground reckoning, which is to say we walked until Vale decided we'd walked. He counted time by touching certain stones as we passed them—"toll-stones," he called them, though he wouldn't say who collected—and by the behavior of his unlit lantern, which he consulted twice like a man reading a nervous animal.

At the river crossing I learned rule two.

The water ran black and utterly silent, wide as a street, with a ferry-boat moored at a stone jetty and no ferryman. The boat simply came when Vale whistled three notes—low, low, high—and poled itself across, and standing in it as it slid over that soundless water I felt the particular horror of a thing that serves willingly.

Halfway across, the river spoke.

Not with words at first. With pressure—a tightening around us like a fist considering. Then, from everywhere beneath us, a voice like wet gravel learning language:

**NAMES.**

"Toll," Vale said mildly, as if hailing a toll bridge in daylight. "Standard rate."

**NAMES,** the river agreed, with terrible patience. **TRUE NAMES. TWO.**

I felt it then—the pull, gentle and vast, like a hook set softly in the soft part of me. It found the shape of my name without hearing it, the way a lock finds a key. My mouth began, absurdly, helplessly, to open—

Vale's hand closed hard on my wrist. "Breathe through your teeth," he murmured. "Count backward from nine. Don't give it the pleasure."

*I am Jonah Okafor,* the thought rose in me, warm and willing, *and I'll pay, gladly, gladly—*

"No," I said aloud, and bit down on my own tongue, and the pain cut the warmth clean away. The river's pressure flinched. Pain, I understood suddenly, was static on the line. It couldn't take what it couldn't hear clearly.

**ONE NAME OFFERED,** the voice said, and I realized Vale had done something—said something, silently, some scrap of himself surrendered with the ease of a man flicking away a crumb—and the boat touched the far bank.

**THE OTHER PAYS IN THE OLD CURRENCY,** the river said. **OR TURNS BACK.**

"Blood," I translated. My heart was hammering, but underneath the fear something cold and clear had opened up, some inheritance from thirty years of my mother's ledgers: *everything down here is a transaction. Learn the rates.*

"How much?"

**ENOUGH TO REMEMBER.**

I opened my palm—the sigil hand, though I kept it angled so the mark faced my own eyes only—and drew my knife across the meat below the thumb. Blood welled. I let three drops fall into the black water.

The river drank them. I felt it drink—felt the drops *go*, pulled down into that vast patient darkness, and with them went something I hadn't known I was carrying: the exact color of the sky on the morning my father died. Gone. A small thing, a nothing, a detail. Except it had been mine, and now it was the river's, and the hole where it had been ached like a missing tooth.

**PAID,** said the river, almost warmly. **TRAVEL WELL, UNNAMED ONE.**

The boat grounded. We stepped off onto the far shore, and the current resumed its silence, and Vale didn't look at me until we'd climbed out of earshot—which, down there, meant out of sight entirely, around two bends and down a chute of scree.

"Now you know the rate," he said.

"It took a memory. A small one."

"They're all small. That's how it works. Nobody pays anything that feels big." His voice was flat, professional, but his jaw was tight. "You lose the color of a morning. Then the smell of bread. Then, eventually, you meet someone on this road going the other direction, smiling, and they can't tell you their name or their business or why they're smiling, and you realize they paid everything in drops." He crouched to check a ward-mark on the floor, an old builder's sigil worn nearly smooth. "Your blood bought you passage. Spend nothing else unless you have to."

"Vale." I waited until he looked up. "What did the river take from you? Just now? On the crossing?"

For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer. Then, in the tone of a man reading someone else's ledger: "My daughter's birthday. The date of it." He stood, brushed off his knees, and picked up the lantern. "I remember the day. I remember her face. I remember the cake was lopsided. But the number's gone—there's just a smooth spot where it was, like a word on the tip of your tongue that never comes." He started walking. "Come on. We make the Long Gallery by tomorrow's dark, and you do not want to be caught in the side tunnels when the gallery wakes."

"Wakes?"

"Rule three," Vale said, over his shoulder, already descending. "Everything down here sleeps sooner or later. Pray you're elsewhere when it stops."

Behind us, faint and greenish, the ward-seam pulsed once—twice—like a heartbeat counting down.

And far above, through a mile of stone I could feel the way you feel a storm coming in your knees, the first moon turned and began its slow work of dying, one sliver at a time.
