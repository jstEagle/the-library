## 7. The Toll of Names

The river came at us out of the dark like a rumor nobody had bothered to confirm.

We'd been walking downhill for hours—Vale said three, my knees said six—and the air had gone from cold to cool to that particular cave-warmth that feels like breathing through a wet cloth. Then the sound arrived first: a long, low rushing, patient as a heartbeat. And then the water itself, black and moving, wide as a village square and glowing faintly from underneath, the way fish bellies glow, the way I imagined drowned things might.

"That's it," Brannoc Vale said, in the tone other men used for announcing breakfast. "The Leth. Runs the whole length under Thornfell. Only way across is her."

"Her?"

He nodded at the middle of the river, where a boat was drifting toward us against the current. Slowly. With what I can only describe as intent.

I had spent twenty-nine years tending a sealed gate, which is to say I'd spent twenty-nine years learning exactly how much of the world was theater. Wards were theater, mostly—old stone doing old stone things while people told stories about why. Smugglers were theater too, half of them, all swagger and invented tolls. But that boat coming upstream with no one rowing it made the back of my neck prickle in a way no ward-stone ever had, because it wasn't performing for anyone.

It was just doing its work.

"Ferry spirit," Vale said. He'd stopped calling me *keeper's girl* somewhere around the second hour, after I'd corrected his map twice. Now he called me nothing, which I appreciated. Names had started to feel like currency, and I didn't want to spend mine on him. "Older than the court, if you believe the songs. Older than the mountain, maybe. She doesn't work for them. She just... ferries."

"What's the fare?"

"You'll see." He rubbed his jaw. "And Jonah—" he caught himself, winced, "—sorry. *Keeper.* When she asks, think hard before you answer."

The boat touched the bank without a ripple. Up close it was worse than from a distance: black wood gone silver at the edges like something very old wearing something very young, benches worn smooth by centuries of passengers who hadn't all been people. At the prow stood a figure I would have sworn wasn't there a moment before—a woman, or the memory of a woman, tall and grey-green, her hair moving like weed in current though there was no current near her. Her eyes were the color of the river's glow.

She looked at Vale first.

"*Brannoc Vale,*" she said, and his name in her mouth sounded like a coin dropping into deep water. "*You have crossed me four times. You owe me four small truths. Pay.*"

"I never—" Vale began, and then shut his mouth, because apparently he *had* known, and the knowing sat in his face like a swallowed stone. He paid. I won't repeat what he said—the man has some shreds left, and I intend to keep them—but each truth cost him visibly, words dragged up from somewhere below his ribs, and when he finished he looked ten years older and wouldn't meet my eyes.

Then the spirit turned to me, and I understood why smugglers paid in truths and thieves paid in blood and everyone paid in something, because being looked at by her was like being counted.

"*And you. Keeper's daughter. Your name, for your crossing.*"

Here is the thing about growing up invisible: you get very good at noticing what people actually want when they ask you questions. The priestess wanted an heir to point at. The villagers wanted someone to blame or bless. My mother, God rest her stubborn bones, had wanted a wall around me so thick even love couldn't get through it. Everyone who ever asked for my name wanted something, and I had learned to hear the wanting underneath the asking.

This spirit wanted my name the way a fire wants wood.

"The court takes names," I said carefully. "That's what Vale told me. That's the whole economy down here."

"*The court takes souls,*" she said, with something like contempt. "*Souls are crude. A soul is a sack of everything, most of it rot. A name is refined. Distilled. One true thing, held in a mouth that knows how to hold it. The court pays well for names. I deliver.*" She extended a hand, webbed faintly between fingers I tried not to look at directly. "*Your name. Your crossing.*"

Now. Let me be honest with myself, since apparently honesty was about to become expensive: every instinct I had said lie. Give her a false name, cross over, done. It was what any sensible person would do. It was probably what my mother would have done.

But my mother's sensible lies were the reason Tam's name was torn out of a ledger somewhere below us, and I had decided, somewhere between the gate and the dark, to stop paying my family's debts in silence.

So I did the arithmetic instead.

If she took a false name—if she couldn't tell—then fine, I'd crossed cheaply and learned the rules were soft. But if she could tell, I'd have lied to something older than the mountain, holding my hand in hers, and whatever she did about it would happen at the middle of a black river with no bank within swimming distance. Worse: a false name given as *true* might count as payment in counterfeit, and spirits strike me as creatures who take counterfeit personally.

There was a third option. There's always a third option; my whole life had been lived in third options.

"Not my name," I said. "*Her* toll. In kind."

The river went quiet. Not calm—quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone says the wrong thing loudly.

"*Speak.*"

"Names are distilled," I said, and my voice only shook a little. "Blood is raw. But blood carries a name—that's what the ward-rites say, that's why the builders signed gates in their own blood. You want what's in a name? Take it from the source. Unrefined. Full measure." I pulled off my right glove and held out my palm, where the sigil slept in its pale scar-circles. "From this hand. Which is, I'm told, worth more than my name anyway."

Vale made a small strangled noise beside me. The spirit regarded my open palm for a long moment. The glowing eyes moved over the sigil like a tongue over a wound.

"*Clever,*" she said at last, and it was not entirely a compliment. "*The little keeper learns the law. Blood for a false bargain. Name for a true one. Very well, daughter of the builders—I will drink your toll from the source.*"

She took my wrist.

Her grip was cold the way river-bottoms are cold, a cold with weight and history in it. She turned my palm up and bent over it, and I braced myself for pain, for teeth, for something dramatic—

What happened instead was worse. She *read* it. I felt her move through the sigil line by line the way a customs officer goes through a smuggler's pack, unhurried, thorough, finding everything. And as she read, the sigil woke—not burning this time but *singing*, a thin high note I heard through the bones of my arm rather than my ears, and with the note came images that weren't mine, or weren't only mine: hands signing a gate in blood, a woman kneeling in snow, thirteen moons in a circle carved over a door, and beneath all of it, deeper than all of it, a vast slow attention turning over in its sleep far under the stone, aware of me the way the sea is aware of a dropped coin.

Then she drank.

Two lines opened on my forearm, clean and shallow, and my blood ran up along her fingers instead of down—which is wrong, deeply wrong, and my body knew it and shook while my mind held still. She took maybe a cupful. Maybe less. It felt like more. It felt like she took the iron out of me, the warmth, a season's worth of strength, and when she let go I sat down hard on the wet boards of the ferry without meaning to.

"*Paid,*" she said. She sounded almost satisfied. Almost gentle. "*Hear the law, keeper, since you bought it: in the deep places, nothing is taken that is not given, and nothing is given that is not owed. Remember whose hand fed you, daughter of the builders. The court forgets its debts. I do not.*"

The boat slid away from the bank. The current that had fought our approach now carried us like a cupped palm, fast and smooth toward the far shore, and the glow under the water rose around the hull until we seemed to float on light itself, and beneath the light, dim shapes kept pace with us—long shapes, patient shapes—that I chose, firmly, not to examine.

Vale sat across from me, grey-faced, working his hands.

"That was either the bravest or stupidest thing I've seen done on this river," he said. "And I once watched a man pay the ferry with his own funeral."

"It's the same thing most days."

"No." He leaned forward, and there was something new in his face—not respect exactly, but the recognition of a fellow professional. "You gave her blood instead of a name. Do you understand what you actually bought? She read your sigil. She *knows* what you are now. Word travels down here faster than water. By tomorrow, the court will know the heir of the builders is coming, sigil and all." He hesitated. "They'll know before you do what that means."

"Then they'll know I'm not hiding anymore."

"Aye. That's the part that worries me." He looked past me at the shapes pacing us under the glow. "Hiding's the only thing that's kept keepers alive this long."

I flexed my bandaged hand and said nothing, because he was right, and because saying so out loud felt like handing the dark another coin.

* * *

The far shore was a shelf of pale stone where the river bent underground, and the ferry set us down and withdrew the way a tide does—no goodbye, no ceremony, already forgetting us. My arm throbbed in a dull, deep rhythm, and I felt strangely lighter, the way you feel after vomiting: awful, but emptied of something that needed leaving.

We walked. The passage narrowed, widened, forked; Vale chose forks with the confidence of a man following a smell only he could notice. After a while he started talking, low, the way men talk in the dark to keep the dark at a conversational distance.

"There's a market," he said. "Two days' walk, maybe less now that the seal's thinning and the roads run truer. Court-adjacent. Traders come up from the deep with goods you couldn't price above—memory-wine, luck salted in jars, letters written by the dead to whoever they miss most. Everything's traded honest, because down here honest is cheap and lies are dear." He glanced back at me. "You'll want a guide through the market. Someone neutral. And you'll want to buy something."

"What?"

"A name." He said it flatly, watching me. "Not yours. A spare. Down here, when something asks who you are, you answer with the bought name first, and the true one stays tucked away where nothing hungry can reach it. Every seeker carries one. It's the difference between coming home and becoming part of the scenery."

"And where do these spare names come from?"

He was quiet for three steps. Four.

"Don't ask that question in the market," he said finally. "Just—don't. Buy the name. Use the name. And when we find your brother's flame in the vault, you carry it out the same way, wrapped and warded, and you don't speak to it and it doesn't speak to you until you're above ground. Those are the rules I know. They've kept me alive four crossings."

"Five," I said. "The river said five."

He stopped walking.

For a moment the only sound was the distant heartbeat of the water behind us, and something else—faint, rhythmic, rising up through the stone from far ahead and far below. A ringing. Small, silver, wrong. Like bells, if bells had been grown instead of cast.

"Do you hear that?" I whispered.

Vale's face had gone the color of the pale stone. "Aye," he said. "That'll be the court waking up."

And far off in the dark ahead, sweet and hollow and unmistakable, the bells rang again—counting, I was almost sure, counting something down.
