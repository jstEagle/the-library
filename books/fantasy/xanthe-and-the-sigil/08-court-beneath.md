## 8. Court Beneath

There is a particular quality to light that has never seen the sun. I don't mean darkness—I had plenty of that, growing up in the gatehouse with my mother banking the fire early because candles cost coin and coin went to wards. I mean light that has been *made*, light born underground and raised underground like a mushroom, pale and fat and utterly indifferent to anything above.

That was the light of the court.

Vale stopped at the mouth of the cavern and did something I had never seen him do in three days of smuggler's roads: he took off his hat. Held it against his chest, like a man entering a church or a funeral. Given what I'd learned about his history with this place, it was probably both.

"Rules," he said, quietly. "You don't stare. You don't run. You don't say your name, your mother's name, or any name you love. And if anyone asks you to dance—"

"I say no."

"You say no," he agreed. "Even if they're weeping about it. *Especially* if they're weeping about it."

The cavern opened around us the way a throat opens around a swallowed thing. It was vast beyond sense—the smuggler's maps Vale carried showed the upper roads as worm-tracks, but nothing had prepared me for the sheer audacity of the space, a cathedral hollowed out of the mountain's heart by hands that had not asked the stone's permission. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ, and from their tips dripped that manufactured light, each drop glowing soft gold as it fell, and where they struck the floor they pooled into slow rivers of luminescence that wound between structures that made no architectural sense and every emotional one: staircases that climbed to nowhere, balconies facing other balconies across chasms too wide for any voice to cross, doors built at heights no door should be.

And the court itself—a sprawl of pavilions and terraces descending in tiers toward a black lake at the bottom of everything, where something enormous moved beneath the surface with the patience of geology.

"They'll see us soon enough," Vale murmured. "Walk like you belong. That's half of any smuggling."

"Which half?"

"The half that keeps you alive long enough to learn the other half."

We descended. And I saw them up close.

I want to be fair here. I want to tell you they were monsters, and in the ways that matter most, they were. But the first thing—the honest first thing—was that they were beautiful. Not beautiful the way a sunset is beautiful, which forgives you for looking. Beautiful the way a blade is beautiful: the beauty is the point, and the edge is also the point, and you are meant to understand these are not separate qualities.

They were tall. All of them tall, and slender in a way that suggested they had been stretched rather than grown, as if someone had taken human proportion and pulled it taut like wire. Their skin held a faint inner luminance, the way moonstone does, and their eyes—

Their eyes were the problem. They were lovely eyes, deep-set and dark and framed by lashes any Thornfell girl would trade her dowry for, but there was nothing behind them. I know how that sounds. But I've spent my life reading faces at a distance, watching villagers decide whether to acknowledge me or pretend I'm a stump, and faces are full of small machinery: the flicker when someone calculates whether you're worth greeting, the warmth or its absence. These faces had the machinery but none of the motion. Smiles deployed like banners over empty parade grounds.

They were trading as we passed through the middle tiers. That was the part that turned my stomach, more than the eyes, more than the wrongness of the proportions. Because they were trading *us*. Or things very like us.

At a stall draped in grey silk, a noble with hair like poured ink examined a glass vial by lantern-light, turning it so the contents caught the glow. Inside the vial something moved—not liquid, not smoke. A small warm brightness, pulsing gently, like a heartbeat made visible. Like a soul distilled down to its essence and stoppered for market.

The ink-haired noble held it up, squinted, and set it back down with a little shake of the head. *Flawed,* said the gesture. *Not worth the asking price.*

Behind the stall, another noble—this one wearing a face that might once have been copied from a drowned girl—began, softly, to argue. Her voice carried. It was a lovely voice. It was the voice of someone who has been crying for a very long time and has gotten very good at it.

"She sings," the drowned-face seller said. "Listen. She sings all night if you hold the glass near your ear. She sang for her mother, before. You can have that song forever."

My hand found Vale's sleeve without my deciding to do it. He didn't look at me. His jaw did something complicated under his beard, and he kept walking, and I understood that this was the rule beneath the rules: you do not buy souls back at market. That is not how anything here works. If it were, Vale would have bought his daughter home years ago—and I remembered, with a cold slide of understanding, the way he'd said *some debts you carry* on our second night, and hadn't explained, and I hadn't asked, because I'd been too busy being afraid of my own business to inquire after his.

We kept walking.

The deeper tiers were quieter. Fewer stalls, more conversation—nobles reclining on couches of black velvet, drinking something amber from cups that steamed even though the cavern air was cool. Music came from somewhere, a stringed instrument played by a musician whose fingers moved beautifully and whose expression never changed at all, note after note after note, a performance with no performer inside it.

And then, on a terrace above us, beside a fountain that ran upward instead of down—

Tam.

I knew him the way you know your own hands in the dark. Eleven years since the night he walked out of the gatehouse and didn't come back, eleven years since Mother sat up all night with the ledger open in front of her and told me, in a voice like wet ash, that Tam had gone to live with relatives. Far away. Very far away. We wouldn't see him again.

He was taller. Of course he was; he'd been fifteen. He wore clothes I couldn't have priced—dark silk, silver thread, a collar worked in patterns I recognized with a lurch, because they matched the carvings on the gate I'd tended my whole life. Builder's patterns. My family's patterns, worn like decoration by a boy who'd been sold—

No. Worn by a boy who'd been *taken*—except I didn't know yet which word was true, and the not-knowing was its own kind of knife.

He was laughing at something a noble beside him had said. And that was the horror of it, the part I still can't write without my chest going tight: he looked *happy*. He looked happy the way the nobles looked happy, which is to say the smile reached every part of his face except whatever lived behind his eyes, and his eyes—his eyes had the same hollow shine as theirs. The same lovely, lit-from-within emptiness.

"Jonah." Vale's voice, low and urgent. "Whatever you're thinking—"

I wasn't thinking. Thinking is a thing done by people who have time.

I crossed the terrace floor in maybe twenty strides. I heard Vale curse behind me and follow, because he was that kind of man, though God knew why he bothered. And then I was standing in front of my brother, close enough to touch, close enough to see that he had Mother's chin and Father's brow and a thin white scar on his left thumb from the time he'd tried to whittle a whistle at age nine and I'd laughed at him until he cried, and then felt so bad I'd carved him a better one myself.

"Tam," I said.

Nothing. A beat of perfect blankness, and then the smile widened, gracious and hollow, and he inclined his head with the smooth courtesy of the court. "Welcome, guest," he said. His voice had changed. It was his voice and it wasn't—smoother, rounded, sanded of its roughness. "The Regent will receive visitors at the turn of the bell. Until then, enjoy the hospitality of the court."

"It's me," I said. "It's Jonah."

Something happened. I want to report this precisely, because precision matters here: for a fraction of a second—less than a breath—the smile *flickered*. Behind his eyes, deep down in the hollow, something turned over. Something looked out. And his gaze fixed on mine with an intensity that had nothing courtly in it at all, raw and desperate and *him*, and his lips parted—

"—will find the refreshments much to your taste," finished the other voice smoothly, and the flicker was gone, sealed over like ice closing over water, and Tam smiled his terrible smiling smile and gestured graciously toward the terraces below.

"Your brother is well treated," said the ink-haired noble, who had appeared at Tam's shoulder the way fog appears, silently and all at once. The noble studied me with those empty lovely eyes, and I watched those eyes travel down to my right hand, to the palm I'd instinctively curled shut, and stay there. Interest kindled in them—not warmth, nothing so alive as warmth, but the focused attention of a jeweler who has spotted something uncut among the paste. "Very well treated indeed. The court values its... investments."

"He's not an investment," I said. "He's a person."

"A person." The noble tasted the word like an unfamiliar spice. "How quaint. Everything is valued here, guest. Everything has its weight and its price. Even persons." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Even guests."

Vale materialized at my elbow with a bow so fluent it could only have been practiced over many bitter years. "Forgive us, honored one. My companion is fresh from the surface and doesn't know the courtesies. She's overcome. Mountain folk—we feel everything too much. Terrible liability."

"Mm," said the noble. The gaze lingered on my closed fist a moment longer. Then, to Tam: "Come. The Regent dislikes waiting, and tonight of all nights—" and here the noble smiled, and the smile had teeth in it, actual pointed teeth, shown briefly like a card from a marked deck, "—there is so much to prepare."

Tam went. He went without looking back at me, silk and silver thread and Mother's chin, and the crowd of beautiful hollow people folded around him like water closing over a dropped stone, and he was gone.

For a moment I just stood there, breathing.

"Jonah." Vale's hand landed heavy on my shoulder. "Walk. Now. Before that one decides to come back and ask questions."

I let him steer me away, back toward the shadows at the cavern's edge, and only when we were safely among the stalactites did I let myself speak, and what came out was not grief, exactly, though grief was in there somewhere underneath. What came out was rage, cold and clarifying, the best kind I owned.

"That flicker," I said. "Did you see it?"

"Saw something."

"He's in there, Vale. Underneath. Whatever they did to him, whatever they *took*, he looked at me and he knew me for half a second, and then it got shoved back down like—" My voice cracked and I hated it and pushed through anyway. "Like a candle flame cupped against wind. He's in there."

"And the court knows you saw it," Vale said grimly. "Which means the court knows you care. Which means, come the bargaining, they know exactly what to charge you." He rubbed his face with both hands, hat crushed forgotten in one fist. "Eleven days to moonfall. One chance at the vault. And now every noble on these terraces knows there's a surface girl walking around with her fist closed tight around something she doesn't want them to see."

I opened my right hand and looked at the sigil. In the cave-light it glowed faintly, gold shot through with veins of blue-white, matching—for anyone who knew the old patterns—the carvings on the gate, on Tam's collar, on everything my family had ever built or bled for.

"Then we move fast," I said.

"We move fast," Vale agreed. Then he hesitated, and something moved across his face that I would spend a long time afterward learning to read: the specific misery of a man watching someone else walk toward a mistake he himself has already made. "Jonah. When we get to the vault—when you find what your brother left of himself in there—you remember that the court always offers a trade. Always. However bad the odds look, however desperate you are, they will offer you something that seems fair. And it will be fair. That's the trap. It's always *fair*."

Below us, across the black lake at the bottom of the world, something vast shifted its weight, and every drop of light falling from every stalactite shivered at once.

And on the highest terrace, where the stairs climbed toward a door I hadn't noticed before—tall, dark, flanked by pillars carved with my family's sigil worn smooth by centuries of reverent hands—a bell began to ring. It had a sound like no bell I'd ever heard: not metal, but something drier, older, resonant as bone.

The turn of the bell. The Regent receiving visitors.

Vale put his hat back on. "Well," he said. "No time like the present to go meet the thing that owns your brother."

"No," I said, closing my fist around the burning sigil and starting up the stairs toward the sound. "No time at all."
