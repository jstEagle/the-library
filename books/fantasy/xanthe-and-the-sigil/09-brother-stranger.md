## 9. Brother, Stranger

I remember dying the way you remember a door closing.

Not the pain of it — the court was kind enough, or cruel enough, to take that part out, along with most everything else it didn't consider load-bearing. What remains is the sound. A latch. A soft click at the end of thirteen years of being loved, and then nothing, and then this: a corridor beneath Thornfell where the walls are polished black stone and my reflection walks beside me wearing my face but none of my patience.

My name is Tam Okafor. I say it every morning because they ask me to, and because I've learned that in this place a name is not what you are. It's what you're *for*. Down here, names are currency, and mine has been in circulation for a long time. It comes back to me worn smooth, like a coin passed through too many hands, and every time it returns there's a little less of me clinging to it.

The court calls me the Foundling. They say it fondly. Everything here is said fondly.

* * *

You should understand something about soul-binding before you judge me for what I'm about to do, which is: warn my sister to run, and then betray her anyway, in the same hour, possibly in the same sentence.

A soul is not a candle they lit and stuck inside you. It's more like — do you know those old ledgers Jonah keeps, the ones Mother hid in the floor? Imagine your whole self written out in a fine careful hand, every memory, every fear, every stupid private joke, and then imagine someone tears out pages as needed and spends them. That's the arrangement. The court holds my book. They let me keep the binding, the cover, the sense of being someone, because a hollow thing can't smile properly, and smiling is my chief employment down here.

I am the court's proof of concept. Look, they tell the frightened dead and the ambitious living, look how well he turned out. He remembers his sister. He loves her still. Love survives the transaction; that's the selling point. What they don't advertise is what love becomes when it's held in escrow. It doesn't dim. It sharpens. It turns into a hook they can set in me whenever they want me to walk somewhere, say something, smile at someone.

Tonight the hook is set. Tonight I am walking toward my sister's death with my own feet, and the worst part — write this down, whoever keeps ledgers below — the worst part is that part of me wants to go.

* * *

They summoned me at the changing of the dark — the court has no mornings, only degrees of black — and stood me in the Regent's presence chamber, which is a cavern so tall the ceiling is a rumor and so quiet you can hear the mountain thinking. The Regent does not sit on a throne. Thrones are for kings who need reminding. The Regent simply stands where the floor rises slightly, the way water stands where the stone dips, and everyone arranges themselves downhill.

"Foundling," the Regent said. The voice comes from everywhere at once, like heat off stone. "Your blood approaches."

"My blood," I said, "is currently climbing through smuggler roads with a man named Brannoc Vale, whom I would advise you not to underestimate. He looks like a wet sack of regret, but he's survived down here longer than anyone has a right to."

"You know this."

"I know everything you tell me and several things you don't."

The Regent smiled. I have seen that smile on the faces of nobles all through the court, and I finally understood last night what it is: it's the smile of people who have been paid. Paid in full. There's nothing left in them that wants anything badly enough to make their face honest. Every wish they ever had was traded away centuries ago, and now they collect interest.

"She carries the sigil," the Regent said. "Daughter of the builders. Do you know what that means?"

"It means she's going to be very difficult about this," I said. "She gets it from our mother. Our mother could turn 'no' into a fifteen-minute conversation and still end at no."

"The sigil opens what we cannot open and shuts what we cannot shut. Brought to us willingly, it completes a debt older than your family's name." The Regent drifted a step closer, and the temperature dropped the way it does before snow. "You will greet her, brother. You will make her feel welcome. And you will learn from her whether the mark is bound to her flesh alone, or to her willing hand."

"And if she asks me to help her?"

"Then you will refuse her beautifully."

There it was. The hook, set clean through me. I felt my mouth already arranging itself into the family smile — Tam's smile, the one that got us extra bread from the miller's wife, the one Jonah said could talk a wolf out of a sheepfold. They'd kept that one intact on purpose. Of course they had. It's a tool, and tools get maintained.

"Yes," I heard myself say, in a warm and easy voice that had nothing behind it but the shape of obedience. "Yes, I'll refuse her beautifully."

I hated myself with such precision just then that for one bright moment I thought the hatred might cut the line. It didn't. Hatred is just another page in the ledger, and they hold the ledger.

* * *

But here is what the court doesn't know, or thinks it knows and doesn't bother checking, because what threat is a spent coin?

I've been stealing back my pages.

It started small. A memory of rain on the roof at Thornfell, gone one day from my head, returned the next with its edges chewed — I'd find it lying in my mind like something a bird dropped. Then a smell: woodsmoke and tallow, Mother's workroom. Then Jonah's laugh, the real one, the ugly snorting one she'd deny under oath. Each fragment came back wrong-sized, water-swollen, but mine. I don't know how it happens. Perhaps souls resent being currency. Perhaps some clerk below is sloppy, or sympathetic, or both — the two traits wear the same face down here.

And one of the pages I stole back is the important one. It's the reason I'm writing this account at all, in my head, over and over, the way prisoners scratch marks on walls:

*I volunteered.*

Jonah doesn't know. She thinks Mother sold me, that she was spared by accident of birth order or maternal favoritism. But I remember the kitchen table, the night the covenant was struck. I was eleven and I understood exactly one thing: whatever was coming up the mountain road, whatever needed paying, it wasn't going to be *her*. She was the heir. She was the gatekeeper's blood proper. And I was the younger brother who could climb the scree faster than anyone, and I made a bargain of my own, child-shaped and absolute: take me instead.

Mother fought me. That's the part that guts me every time the memory returns — she didn't sacrifice me, she *lost* me, arguing, holding my shoulders, saying my name like a ward against evil while the thing beneath the mountain counted it out. My last act above ground was making my mother cry. My first act below was learning to be glad of it.

So when Jonah comes down that smuggler road believing she owes me a rescue, she'll be carrying a debt that runs the wrong direction. And I can't tell her. Not because the court forbids it — they'd barely notice — but because the truth is a heavier thing than she's carrying now, and she'll drop everything else to pick it up. Guilt makes people reckless. I've watched the nobles use it like a crowbar for three hundred years. Fine. Three hundred years, or thirty. Time down here is a ledger entry too.

* * *

They stationed me at the Weeping Stair, third landing, because it's where any sensible intruder pauses to get her bearings, and because the acoustics there carry a whisper further than a shout. The court thinks in acoustics. The court thinks in everything except the possibility that a bound soul might have learned to lie with its whole body.

I waited through two changes of the dark. The bone-bells in the high vaults rang their slow patient count — they ring when souls move that shouldn't, and they hadn't yet, and I found I was praying, in whatever broken dialect prayer takes down here, that they wouldn't. Not yet. Let her be slow. Let Vale be lost. Let my sister take the long way around her own rescue.

Then I heard boots on the stair. Two sets, unevenly matched, one dragging. And a voice, low, cursing the dark in the specific cadence of someone who grew up swearing at gates and ward-stones and stubborn iron, and my whole stolen-back heart went sideways in my chest.

I stepped out onto the landing with my hands open and my face already smiling, and I will tell you honestly: I did not decide to warn her. The decision happened somewhere else, in whatever cellar of me the court never bothered to inventory. Because here is the arithmetic they never taught the nobles: they bought my *soul*, yes, all of it, itemized. But they never bought what I did with it. Thirteen years of loving my sister from underneath their ownership — that was mine. Earned. Off the books.

"Jonah," I said, and her lantern swung up, and her face —

Her face did something I wasn't prepared for. Not joy. Joy would have been easy; I know how to receive joy, it's part of the job. Her face went through recognition and arrived somewhere past it, at grief, quickly disguised, and I realized with a lurch that to her I must look like a portrait of myself painted by someone who'd only had me described.

"Tamsin," she whispered — my childhood name, the one only she used, and the sound of it hit the hook in me like a hammer, and for one terrible second I felt the leash shorten, felt the court lean in through my ears wanting to know what she'd say next.

"Careful," I said, quick and low, stepping close enough that the words couldn't carry upward to the listening bells. "Names carry here. Especially true ones." I took her lantern hand and turned it palm-up without asking — keeper's habit, older than either of us — and the sigil lay there glowing faintly against her skin like a brand of daylight. Beautiful. Catastrophic. "They know you're coming, Jonah. They've known since the ward-stones warmed. The Regent sent me to welcome you, which in court-language means to deliver you."

"Then deliver me somewhere useful," she said, and God, there it was, her jaw set the way it set when she was fourteen and refused to leave a trapped lamb on the fells. Stubbornness as inheritance. "Where's the vault?"

"Listen to me." I gripped her shoulders. Somewhere in the stone above us the bone-bells stirred, tasting the air. "You can't win here. Nobody wins here; the house always collects. They don't want to fight you — fighting costs them. They want to *trade* with you, and every trade they offer will be shaped exactly like the thing you want most. That's how they farm. They don't hunt. They wait at the water hole." I shook her once, gently. "Turn around. Take Vale and go up. Tell the priestess to bar the village doors and stop answering prayers. You seal the gate from above. That's the whole war, Jonah. Stay above the line."

"You're coming with me."

"No."

"Yes. That's the — Tam, that's the entire point of—"

"The entire point," I said, "was never you coming down here. And I need you to trust me on one thing I'm not free to explain: whatever they told you about why I'm down here, the truth is kinder to Mother and crueler to you, and you cannot afford crueler right now." Her eyes narrowed — she's never once in her life trusted a sentence that began with *trust me*, and I loved her for it even as it doomed my plan. "The sigil stays hidden. Glove, wrap, mud, I don't care. If the Regent sees it bare, the negotiation starts, and you don't negotiate with these people. You survive them or you feed them."

Behind her, Vale shifted, and gave me a long flat look — the look of a man doing arithmetic he hates. He knew what I was. Bound things recognize each other; guilt has a scent.

"They'll know you warned me," Jonah said quietly.

"Oh, absolutely." And here my traitor face did the thing it does, the warm easy smile, deployed this time in service of the only honest thing I'd said all night. "I'll refuse you beautifully, they told me. So here's my performance: I'm refusing you, little sister. Go home. Forget the vault. Leave your brother to his comfortable damnation." I pitched it loud enough to carry to the bells, a sonata of betrayal, and then leaned in and finished at a breath: "*Third landing, west passage, follow the cold draft, the choir sleeps after the third bell.* Directions. In case you're as bad at obeying me as you've always been."

She stared at me. I watched her weigh it — the shouted refusal, the whispered map — watched her do the exact thing I'd done at eleven years old at the kitchen table: decide that love meant choosing which lie to believe.

"Don't you dare die for me twice," she said.

Too late. I'd already done it once; the second time is always easier.

I stepped back, raised my voice to the listening dark, and gave the performance of my unlife: "Go home, Jonah Okafor! The court is merciful — walk away and be forgotten!" And beneath the words, silent as a stolen page slipped back into a ledger, I pressed my stolen memory of the west passage into her hand the only way left to me: I squeezed her fingers, twice. Our old signal. *Follow me. Trust the plan.*

Then I turned and walked toward the Regent's presence chamber to report, wearing my purchased smile, carrying my contraband heart, praying to gods who had never once answered me that my sister was half as stubborn as I remembered —

— knowing, with the certainty of a man who has read the ledger, that she was twice as stubborn, and that the bone-bells were already beginning to ring.
