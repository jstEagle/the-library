## 10. The Bargain Refused

They came for me at the hour the court called morning, which was indistinguishable from every other hour, because there was no sun down here and no moon either—only a slow amber luminescence that seeped from the walls like honey through cloth. I'd been counting hours by my heartbeat, which is a poor clock, since fear speeds it up and I had been afraid almost continuously since we crossed the river of names.

The Regent's invitation arrived on a tray.

That's how they did things here, I was learning. Everything terrible arrived beautifully. The soul-vault keys were displayed like jewelry; the bone bells hung in silken nets until they needed ringing; and an order to appear before the ruler of the court beneath Thornfell was delivered by a servant with a face like a closed book, bearing a silver tray on which lay a single card of pale vellum. No words on it. Just my name—my true name, the one I'd paid blood to keep off the river-spirit's ledger—written in ink that shimmered like oil on water.

I hadn't given it to anyone. Which meant someone had read it anyway, somewhere, in some record I didn't know existed, and that was its own kind of message: *we see you, keeper's daughter. We have always seen you.*

Brannoc looked at the card over my shoulder and made a sound like a man swallowing broken glass. "You don't refuse," he said quietly. "For what it's worth."

"For what it's worth," I agreed, "how much would it cost me not to go?"

"More than going." He rubbed the stubble on his jaw. I'd noticed he did that when he was calculating odds he didn't like. "Courts are like rivers, girl. You can fight the current or you can swim in it. But you can't stand still—you'll just drown tired."

"Wonderful. Any other folk wisdom? Maybe something about how the nobles here eat people?"

"They don't eat you." He said it flatly, without drama, which was worse. "They *keep* you. There's a difference, and the difference is time."

* * *

The throne room—if that's what you'd call it, though "throne" implies something as crude as sitting—was a cavern of white stone veined with gold, so tall that its ceiling vanished into a glow I couldn't look at directly. Stalactites had been carved, over centuries or centuries-of-centuries, into chandeliers that dripped light instead of wax. The court assembled along the walls: the beautiful hollow-eyed nobles I'd glimpsed before, arranged by some hierarchy invisible to me, all of them watching me with the particular attention of collectors examining a piece that might complete a set.

And there, near the front, stood Tam.

He was wearing court colors now—deep green shot through with silver thread—and he smiled when he saw me. It was his smile. It was exactly, precisely his smile, the one that used to get us extra bread from the miller's wife, the one that said *we're getting away with something*. And that was the horror of it, that it should be so perfectly him while everything behind it was wrong—the eyes too still, the warmth arriving half a beat too late, like a letter delivered years after it was written.

*Don't trust anything he says,* he'd warned me, in his own borrowed voice, two nights ago. *Especially the things you most want to hear.*

"Jonah." The Regent's voice filled the cavern without effort, the way water fills a basin. She descended from the dais—or perhaps she simply became lower, closer, the way the court rearranged distance when it suited her. She was tall and gray-eyed and ageless in the way of things that had stopped bothering to be any particular age, dressed in white so pale it hurt to look at against the dark of her hair. "Daughter of the builders. Heir of the gate."

"That's me," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which I've learned is about the best you can hope for. "Though I notice nobody down here uses my actual name."

"A courtesy." Something moved across her face that might have been amusement, if amusement could fossilize. "Names are currency here, Jonah Okafor. We spend them carefully. And you—" she tilted her head, studying me the way Brannoc studied a locked door, "—you have been spending recklessly. Blood at the river. Memories at the choir's threshold. Do you know what you're worth now?"

"No," I said. "But I'm guessing you do, to the penny."

"To the drop." She extended one hand, palm up, and the light above us bent toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone. In her palm appeared something small and dark—a seed, a stone, a folded shadow, I couldn't say. "You came here for your brother. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. I find I admire it. Sentiment is rare below; we traded ours away long ago, mostly for good prices."

"You want something," I said. "Get to it."

Tam flinched. Small, quick, gone in a blink—but I saw it, because I'd spent nineteen years learning to read Tam's face, and whatever else the court had done to him, it hadn't taught him to hide from *me*.

"The thirteenth moon wanes," the Regent said. "Above, your village prays to gods grown hungry, and your gate grows thin as morning frost. When it opens—and it will open, heir; seals are promises, and promises end—it will open wide, and what waits behind it will pour through Thornfell like wine through a torn sack. Unless—" and here she let the pause stretch, luxuriating in it, a connoisseur of dread, "—unless the seal is renewed. By the blood of the builders. By the sigil."

My hand curled shut around the mark burned into my palm. It pulsed against my fingers, warm as a sleeping bird.

"I'm listening," I lied.

"Give me the sigil." Her voice went soft, almost gentle, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "Your inheritance. Your claim. The last binding authority of the house that built this gate. Give it freely, and I will release your brother's soul—whole, unspent, restored to his body above. He will wake under the moon of home and never remember the taste of this place. And you will walk out of Thornfell rich beyond the village's imagining. That is the trade, daughter of the builders. A mark of skin against a soul of fire. Hardly a bargain at all."

The whole court leaned in. Even the light seemed to hold its breath.

And I will be honest, since honesty is cheap when no one can hear it: for three full heartbeats, I wanted to say yes.

Three heartbeats. That's all it took. I saw it—Tam waking in the high pasture grass, blinking at the sun, asking why everyone was crying. I saw him whole. Nineteen years old forever, laughing, alive, and every night after that a night where I didn't have to count the moons left on my fingers. Three heartbeats of a life I wanted so badly my teeth ached.

Then I looked at the Regent's open palm, and I thought about what the sigil actually *was*.

Mother had never explained it. Mother had barely spoken of the gate at all, except in the flat voice she used for the weather. But I had tended that gate for seven years, and wards were my liturgy, and I knew—knew in the way you know your own hands—that the seal wasn't carved into the stones. The stones only held the shape of it. The seal lived in the sigil, in the builder's mark passed hand to hand down our family like a flame passed between candles. The gate wasn't locked. The gate was *held*, and I was holding it, and the thing beneath Thornfell—the hungry god, the old power pressing against thirteen turns of thinning promise—was held back not by stone but by the inheritance in my palm.

Which meant the Regent wasn't offering me a trade.

She was offering me the door key, gift-wrapped, to the very prisoner she served.

Or maybe she *was* the prisoner. Or the jailer. Down here the categories got slippery. But whichever way it cut, one thing was certain: the moment that sigil left my skin, nothing above would stand between Thornfell and what waited behind the gate. Tam would wake into a world being swallowed. We would both be free, briefly, gloriously, right up until the sky filled with gods.

"No," I said.

The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well. I heard it fall a long way.

"No?" The Regent's head tilted, birdlike.

"No." My heart was hammering but my voice held, and I gripped that fact like a railing. "You're asking for the lock and giving back the hostage. But the hostage is only worth taking because of the lock. Free him without the sigil changing hands and your prize walks away. Take the sigil and you don't need to free anyone—you'll have everything, and the world above with it." I made myself smile. It felt like baring teeth. "I've read ledgers before. Yours has too many hidden columns."

Something flickered across the assembled court—not quite laughter, not quite hunger. The nobles' hollow eyes brightened, all of them at once, like lamps turned up on the same dimmer.

The Regent regarded me for a long moment. Then she laughed—a low, real sound, and somehow that was the worst thing yet, because it meant I'd amused her, and amused things keep playing.

"There it is," she murmured. "The builder's blood. Stubborn as bedrock. Your grandmother refused us too, you know. And her mother before her. Every thirteen years we make the offer, and every thirteen years your line says *no* in some new and inventive phrasing." She closed her empty hand. The little dark thing in it vanished. "It's why we respect you. It's why we *want* you. Refusal has such flavor."

"Then you'll love what I say next."

"Oh, I already do." She began to circle me slowly, and the court circled with her, a slow rotation of silk and hollow eyes. "But consider, before you finish refusing: the moon does not care for your cleverness, heir. Twelve turns remain, then eleven, then none. The gate thins whether you bargain or burn. And when it opens—" she stopped, close enough that I could smell her, cold stone and dried flowers, "—your brother's soul will be the first thing it takes. Again. Forever, this time. No theft, no rescue, no stubborn daughter with bloodied palms. Some doors, once opened, do not close on apologies."

"So I should trade you the key to save him from you."

"I should be so flattered." Her smile was a knife laid flat on velvet. "No, daughter of the builders. You should trade me the key because it is the only kindness anyone will ever offer you down here, and because kindness, below, is rarer than gold and priced accordingly. Refuse, and you will learn what we take instead of payment." She turned away, robe whispering against the white stone. "You have until the twelfth turn. Enjoy the hospitality of the court. It is included in your refusal."

The court parted for her like water. Tam turned to follow, obedient as a tide.

"TAM!" 

It tore out of me before I could stop it, my brother's name shouted across a throne room of the dead-and-living, and every noble flinched as if I'd thrown coins among beggars. Tam stopped. Turned. For one raw instant the smile dropped and I saw underneath it—saw something desperate and drowning reaching up through deep water.

"Jonah," he said. Just my name. Nothing else. His voice cracked on it, and the crack was the truest thing I'd heard since crossing the river.

Then the water closed over him again. He smiled his perfect wrong smile, bowed to me with courtly grace, and walked away into the golden dark with the rest of them.

* * *

Brannoc was waiting in the corridor outside, leaning against the wall with his arms crossed and the expression of a man who'd bet on a horse knowing full well the race was rigged.

"Well," he said. "You're still in one piece."

"Barely." My legs chose that moment to inform me the audience was over, and I put a shoulder to the cold stone until they remembered their job. "She offered me the world, Vale. Sigil for my brother. Clean trade, she said."

"And you said no."

"You knew I would?"

"Suspected." He pushed off the wall and started walking, and I fell in beside him because standing still in this place felt like standing in open water. "Regent makes the same offer every generation. My grandmother's cousin took it, back before your mother was born. Gave up her claim, walked out rich, told everyone the court had released her brother." He was quiet for a few steps. "Gate opened six inches that winter. Enough for the cold to get out. Half of Thornfell's eastern farms went fallow from frost in June. They sealed it back, eventually—at a price. There's always a price, and the price is always a person."

We walked in silence through corridors that glowed faintly amber, past doors carved with faces whose expressions changed when you weren't looking directly at them. Somewhere far off, faint as memory, bells were ringing—bone bells, testing their own voices.

"Twelve turns," I said finally. "That's what she gave me. Four months, give or take, before the debt comes due and she stops being polite."

"Less than that." Brannoc's jaw worked. "Time runs strange down here, girl, but it doesn't run slower. Whatever she told you, subtract. The moon above doesn't wait on courtly manners." He glanced sideways at me. "So what's the plan, then? Since you've refused the only door she was willing to open?"

I looked down at my palm. The sigil pulsed there, intricate and patient, matching the carvings I'd memorized on a gate I'd tended my whole invisible life. Mother's ledger. The torn page where Tam's name should have been. Thirteen payments across thirteen generations, and every one of them a *no* exactly like mine, handed down like the sigil itself—an inheritance of refusal.

"We stop knocking on her doors," I said slowly, "and start looking for the ones she doesn't use anymore. Old roads. Sealed ways. Anything the court forgot or outgrew." I thought of the smuggler's maps Brannoc kept rolled in oiled leather against his spine, charts of ways beneath the mountain older than the court itself. "There's a vault where they keep souls, isn't there? There has to be a way in that isn't the front door."

Brannoc stopped walking.

When he turned to look at me, his face had gone carefully blank—the face of a man doing arithmetic he really didn't like. Behind him, far down the corridor, the bone bells rang again, and this time they didn't stop.

"You're talking about the Hollow Choir," he said.

"Am I?"

"The road to the vault passes the unnamed." His voice had dropped low, the way voices drop in villages when someone speaks of the plague. "Souls the court stripped bare. They sing, Jonah. And what they sing, they take." He shook his head slowly. "Every seeker who ever tried the back way—I've guided four, over the years. Four. The choir sang the first one's name clean out of her before she reached the second turning. She wandered the rest of her life smiling at strangers. The other three never came back at all."

"And yet," I said, "you kept guiding seekers."

He looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes—guilt, grief, hope, worn smooth like river stones from years of handling.

"Aye," he said at last. "I kept guiding."

Above us, muffled by a hundred feet of living rock, something vast shifted in its sleep. Dust sifted down through the amber light. And in my palm, the sigil woke—burning brighter than it ever had, pulsing in time with the distant bells, as if it recognized the sound.

As if it were answering.
