## 11. Moonfall Above

I have been priestess of Thornfell for nineteen years, and in all that time the gods have been very good at being absent. This, I came to understand, is the mark of a well-run faith. A god who answers prayers is like a neighbor who answers knocks: sooner or later you must decide whether to invite them in, and by then they are already through the door.

For nineteen years I rang the dawn bell, burned the morning candle, recited the Litanies of the Sealed Door, and collected tithes from people who believed — in the loose, comfortable way of people who have never been asked to prove it — that something vast and patient slept beneath our mountain and would go on sleeping so long as nobody made a fuss. My sermons were short. My miracles were of the administrative kind: a good harvest here, a fever that broke there, a widow's grief that softened on schedule. I took credit for all of it with a clear conscience, because I knew, better than anyone, that none of it was mine.

The gods did not answer prayers in Thornfell. The gods *were* the prayer — a shape of silence we built together, generation after generation, and called holy.

Then, eleven days ago, Marta Cottle prayed for rain, and it rained so hard the millrace drowned two goats and half her winter turnips.

* * *

You may laugh. I laughed too, at first, standing in the flooded churchyard with my hem black to the knee, watching Marta beam at the sky as though she'd been handed a blessing instead of a bill. "Answered at last," she said, wringing out her shawl. "Nineteen years I've prayed, Sef. Nineteen years."

"Marta," I said carefully, "you prayed for rain for your garden."

"And look how much of it there is."

There is a particular quality to a prayer answered badly, and I recognized it because my grandmother — who kept the old books before the church tidied them away — used to describe it over supper. The gods of the mountain, she said, are not cruel and not kind. They are hungry, which is different from either. When they give, they give the way a fisherman gives bait: generously, precisely, and with every expectation of a pull on the line.

By the fourth day, the whole village was praying. You could feel it in the air, a low hum of wanting, like bees under the floorboards. Old Hollis, whose hip had ached since the flood year, prayed at his fencepost and walked straight by noon. The Tanners' boy, sick with the lung-fever since midwinter, sat up in bed asking for broth. And the Widow Ashe — dear, foolish Widow Ashe — prayed for her dead husband back, and woke to find him standing at the foot of her bed, smiling, dripping, exactly as he'd looked when they pulled him from the quarry pool thirty years ago.

She died three days later. Smiling too, they said. The neighbors found the house cold as a cellar and both of them gone, and no one could say whether it was grief or appetite that had taken her, least of all me, and I am supposed to be able to say these things.

That was the day I stopped ringing the dawn bell.

Not out of doubt. Doubt would have been a mercy. I stopped because I had begun to suspect that the bell was no longer a formality but an *invitation*, and that everything in Thornfell that wanted anything — and lately that was everyone — was standing at my altar with their hands out, and something below the mountain was handing things back, and the ledger behind all that generosity was written in a script I didn't want to read.

* * *

The church filled anyway. That is the joke of it; that is the bitter, perfect joke. I abandoned my post and the people came to *me*, more of them than ever, crowding the nave on a grey morning with their petitions clutched in their fists like market lists. Rain for Joss Fen's barley. A husband for Nella. A cure for the cough going round the east cottages. A son home from the coast roads, safe, please, please.

"Sef," said Marta Cottle, speaking for all of them because Marta always speaks for all of them, "the prayers work now. Why won't you say the great litany? Why won't you open the door?"

"Because doors," I told her, "open in both directions."

They looked at me the way villagers look at a priestess who has stopped making sense: kindly, warily, the way you look at a dog that has begun growling at empty corners. And I understood that I had perhaps nineteen years of credibility left, and that I was spending it faster than I earned it, and that the thing beneath the mountain did not need me to open any door at all. It only needed the wanting. Every wish whispered into a fist was a hairline crack. The village was praying its own seal thin.

I dismissed them as gently as I could and went, as I had gone every evening that week, to the gate road.

* * *

Jonah Okafor met me at the third ward-stone, arms crossed, looking like a woman who had been expecting me and resenting it.

"You felt it," she said.

"I felt it." I held up my hands. They were trembling. I let her see. "Eleven days ago the stones went warm. Yesterday this one—" I laid my palm flat against the granite, and even through the skin I felt it: a faint pulse, slow and deep, like a heart heard through a wall. "It's counting something."

"The moons." Jonah's face was grim in the way of someone carrying news she has decided not to share. She had a bandage wrapped around one hand, and I noticed — I notice everything, it is the occupational disease of clergy — that the bandage was spotted brown. "Thirteen turns," she said. "Sef, what comes at the thirteenth?"

And there it was: the question I had spent my whole priesthood building a life to avoid. My grandmother's books were ash these fifty years, burned in the great tidying, but I had read them young, crouched behind the woodshed where no one thought to look for a girl who liked terrible stories. I knew about the covenant. I knew the builders' line had paid a price across the generations, though the books named it only as *the tithe of blood and name*. I had always assumed the phrase meant taxes.

"It depends," I said, "on what your mother signed."

Jonah went very still. "My mother signed nothing. She swept floors and mended fences and never once set foot in your church."

"No," I agreed softly. "Which was itself a signature. People who owe a debt avoid the creditor's house." I watched her absorb that, watched it land somewhere deep and painful, and hated myself a little for throwing it, and threw it anyway, because the alternative was lying to her, and I had done enough of that for one lifetime. "Jonah. Daughter of the builders. There is a reason your family kept the gate and no one else dared touch it. There is a reason the sigil woke on *your* hand and no one else's. Whatever is waking down there knows you. It has known your family longer than my church has existed, and my church is older than the word 'village.'"

The wind came down off the mountain then, and it smelled wrong — sweet, almost, like fruit going over. Behind us, far below, the dawn bell I had silenced stood silent in its tower, and I found I missed it the way you miss a tooth: mostly at night, mostly when you try to use it.

"What do you want from me, priestess?" Jonah said at last. Not unkindly. Weary, the way stone is weary.

"I want to know if I'm helping you or feeding it," I said. "Every prayer in that village, every answered wish, every small miracle — I think they're all pulling on the same line, and I think the hook is set in the gate. In *you*. If I send you down that mountain, am I sending a thief to steal back what was sold, or am I delivering the last payment?"

She looked at her bandaged hand. Then at me. And she smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of someone who has already decided and is only waiting for the world to catch up.

"Does it matter?" she said. "Either way, Tam's down there."

* * *

Tam. The brother. Dead these ten years by every account the village kept — fever, they said, quick and merciful — though I had buried no one of that name, and neither had the undertaker, and I had told myself for a decade that grief keeps its own records and it wasn't my place to audit them. Now I understood why there had been no body. Now I understood why the Okafor house had gone quiet as a held breath, and why the mother had aged twenty years in twenty months, and why Jonah had inherited not just a gate but a silence shaped exactly like a boy.

A soul sold. A tithe of blood and name. Thirteen moons, and the thirteenth coming due.

I walked back down the gate road in the failing light, past the ward-stones humming their slow count, and the fields around me were full of kneeling villagers. Praying. All of them praying, faces lifted, hands open, wanting so earnestly and so innocently, and above them the moon hung thin as a paring of nail — the twelfth, or near enough — and beneath them, beneath everything, something patient turned the pages of its ledger and waited to see what we would offer next.

Here is what I decided, walking down through my flock with my faith coming apart in my hands thread by thread:

I decided that I no longer believed in the silence. Nineteen years I had tended a god of absence, and it had been easy and good and probably true, and none of that mattered anymore, because absence had ended and something else had taken the vacancy, and a priestess who cannot tell the difference between her god and a thing wearing her god's chair is worse than useless — she is a door left open.

But I also decided this: Jonah Okafor had looked at me with her brother's name in her mouth and asked for nothing. Not blessing, not absolution, not rescue. Nothing. In nineteen years of ministry, in a village where every soul held out its hands, she was the first person to come to my altar wanting only information and taking even that without thanks.

You can build a faith on many things. On fear, on habit, on the comfortable hum of unanswered bells. But I am an old woman, and I have learned to recognize holiness when it crosses my path, and it does not look like answered prayers. It looks like a keeper of nothing, bleeding quietly through her bandages, walking toward hell to steal back her brother.

So. I would help her. God forgive me — whichever one is listening.

I reached the church as full dark fell, climbed the tower stairs on knees that protested every step, and took up the bell rope that I had sworn, eleven days ago, never to ring again.

Because there was one more thing my grandmother's books said, one more line I had carried like a splinter all my life: *when the debt comes due, the faithful will know by the bell — for the court below cannot abide its sound, and will rise to stop it.*

I had thought that meant the gate.

Standing in the dark tower with the rope in my hands, I finally understood it meant me.

I rang the dawn bell at midnight, thirteen times, once for each turning of the moon — and from beneath the mountain, faint but unmistakable, rising up through stone and root and the foundations of my little church, something screamed.
