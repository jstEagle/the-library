## 12. Vale's Price

There are two kinds of liars in the under-mountain trade: the kind who lie about prices, and the kind who lie about themselves. The first kind die poor. The second kind die surprised.

I have spent thirty years being the second kind, and I'd got comfortable with it. Comfortable as a man can be sleeping in caves that breathe and eating smoked eel while something enormous shifts its weight beneath your bedroll. You get used to anything. That's the whole trick of survival — you get used to it until you forget you were ever supposed to be horrified.

Then a girl with a burning hand asks you why you know so much about the court, and you find out you were never as used to it as you thought.

* * *

We'd made camp in the Wethering Galleries, that stretch of smugglers' road where the stone goes honeycombed and the air tastes faintly of copper, like sucking on a coin. Good ground. Neutral ground. The things that live in the deep galleries don't like copper any more than they like names, which is why every guide worth his salt chews a copper nail when he talks to them. I had mine behind my back teeth. Habit. Like saying my prayers, if my prayers had been installed by a man named Cutter who once beat me bloody for forgetting.

The Okafor girl sat across the fire — well, across the glow-moss, because fire's a bad idea past the second turning; light draws things, and heat draws worse things — picking at her ration of hardtack like it owed her money. Her palm was wrapped in linen. Under the linen, I knew without looking, the sigil was doing what sigils do: shining through cloth like a lamp behind a curtain, patient as debt.

"You've been down here before," she said. Not a question. She'd been circling toward it for two days, the way she circled everything, all quiet feet and sideways looks. "Before. With other people."

"Once or twice." I turned the eel skewer. It didn't need turning. Turning things gives your hands work and your face somewhere else to be. "Smuggling's not all brandy and bolt-cloth, girl. Half the routes run under Thornfell because going over it means the tolls'll eat you alive, and going around it means the marshes will. Under is faster."

"And the third half?"

I looked up. She had that look her mother must have had, I decided — the one that takes a man's deflection and holds it up to the light like a suspect coin.

"The third half," I said, "is the seekers."

"Seekers."

"Folk who come looking for something the court took. A name. A face. A—" I stopped myself on the word *soul*, which is a word I avoid in company and out of it. "A loved one, say. They hear stories in taverns. The court trades, see. Everything trades. And everything that trades can be traded with, or so the story goes. So every few years some poor fool sells their boots to buy a lantern and comes down the Wethering with a song in their heart and a knife they've never sharpened, asking old Brannoc Vale to show them the way."

"How many?"

"Seekers?" I counted on my fingers, slow, theatrical, giving her time to lose interest. She didn't take it. "Fourteen. In thirty years."

"And how many came back?"

The moss-light did something unkind to her face, hollowed her cheeks, aged her. Nineteen years old and carrying a mountain's worth of other people's sins already. I put the skewer down.

"One," I said.

Her breath went out of her. I watched her do arithmetic she didn't want to do. Fourteen minus one. Thirteen souls still down there, or worse than down there, wearing smiles that fit them wrong.

"Which one came back?"

"A tanner's son from Marrowdale. Came back in body." I shrugged, and the shrug cost me more than I let it show, which is the whole art of shrugging. "Whatever he went in for, he left it below. He lived another twenty years. Never spoke above a whisper. Kept a lamp lit in every room of his house, even the privy. Died in his sleep with the door open and the lamps burning, and folk said he looked peaceful." I picked the skewer back up. "Folk say a lot of things."

She was quiet for a long moment. The galleries dripped somewhere off east, counting time in their own fashion. Then she said, in that flat careful voice she uses when she's about to be braver than anyone has a right to expect from her:

"Why do you keep bringing them? Fourteen times. If only one—"

"Because I'm paid," I said, too fast, too smooth, and heard the lie land between us like a dropped plate.

She just looked at me. The girl doesn't argue. I'll give her that. Arguing gives a liar somewhere to stand. She just waited, the way stone waits, the way the mountain waits, and after a while I found I couldn't bear it, which is how she got it out of me — not by force, never by force, but by the simple unbearable patience of someone who has spent her whole life listening to silences.

I chewed the copper nail for a while. It didn't help. Nothing helps. But it gave me a ritual to hide inside, and then, God rot it, I stepped outside the ritual.

* * *

Her name was Merrin.

I haven't said it aloud in eleven years. Names have weight down here, real weight, spendable weight, and I'd told myself that keeping hers locked behind my teeth was protection. Caution. Professional discipline.

It was cowardice. There's a difference, and the difference is that caution keeps you safe and cowardice keeps you small, and I have been very small for a very long time.

"Merrin," I said, and the syllables came out rusty, like a gate hinge nobody'd oiled since the funeral. "My daughter's name was Merrin Vale. She was nine years old."

Jonah Okafor went absolutely still. Stillness is her native language; I've watched her hold it against spirits and rockfalls and the Regent's own shadow. But this stillness was different. This was the stillness of someone understanding that they're about to be handed something heavy and deciding, deliberately, to keep their hands where they are.

"She was born in a bad year," I went on, because once you start you may as well finish; a confession stopped halfway is just a wound with the knife left in. "The fever year. Half of Lowmarsh went into the ground, and the other half went into debt, and I was a smuggler with more enemies than coin and a child coughing her lungs out in a rented room. And there came a man to my door. Well-dressed. Smiling. Eyes like the bottom of a well. He said the court beneath Thornfell paid well for certain commodities, and he named a price, and I laughed in his face and told him to walk."

"What commodity?" Jonah asked quietly. She already knew. You could see she already knew, the way you know the shape of a thing under a sheet.

"Memory, mostly. Years. Small stuff, at first. I sold him my mother's face — the memory of it. Sold him my wedding day, such as it was. Sold him the taste of my father's cooking. Little coins, little payments, and Merrin's fever broke each time, and I told myself the court was generous, that we were trading, that this was how the world worked under the mountain and always had." My hands had stopped moving. I noticed distantly, as though reporting on someone else. "And then the fever came back a fourth time, worse, and the well-eyed man came back with it, and this time he named a different price. A bigger commodity. And I was so tired, girl. Do you understand tired? Not sleepy-tired. Soul-tired. The kind where the math starts making sense that shouldn't."

"He wanted her soul," Jonah said. Not a question. She'd known since the seekers, maybe since the road. Maybe since the first time she saw me flinch at a bell.

"He wanted her soul," I agreed. "And I told myself — this is the part I need you to hear, because you're carrying your own ledger down here, and ledgers lie to their carriers — I told myself it wasn't really selling. That the court would keep her safe. That a soul in a vault is better than a soul in a grave. That she wouldn't feel it. That she'd live her life whole and just... owe something at the end. I made it sound reasonable. I could make anything sound reasonable. That was always my gift."

"What happened?"

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "What happened is that I took the money, and the fever broke, and she woke up healthy as a spring lamb and stayed that way for six years. Six good years. She grew like a weed. Sharp as a fishhook, funny, fearless — she used to ride the roof beams barefoot and dare the gulls. And on her fifteenth birthday she walked out to check the crab pots and never came home, and when they found the boat it was empty, and there was no body, because the court doesn't leave bodies, girl. The court doesn't waste. The contract came due and the collection was made and everyone in Lowmarsh called it drowning and lit candles for the sweet dead girl, and I stood at the funeral knowing exactly what I'd bought with those six years and what the interest would be."

The galleries dripped. Somewhere far below, something vast rolled over in its sleep, and the glow-moss shivered along the walls like a held breath.

"I went looking," I said. "Of course I went looking. I sold everything else I had left and hired guides and went down the Wethering with a lantern and a knife I'd never sharpened, exactly like the fools I'd later mock. And I found the roads. I learned them. I got close enough to the outer court to hear the music, and I stood in a side gallery with my knife out and my heart going like a drum, and I understood — finally, clearly, the way you understand things at three in the morning — that I couldn't do it. That I'd die in the first corridor and accomplish nothing except giving them my soul to stack next to hers. And so I came back up. And I kept coming back up. And every few years, when some grieving fool appeared asking after the roads, I took their coin and led them down, telling myself I was helping, telling myself one of them would succeed, one of them would be strong enough or clever enough or loved enough, and when they succeeded they'd crack the vault open, and everything in it would spill free. Every stolen soul. Every name. Hers."

"Fourteen tries," Jonah said softly.

"Fourteen tries." I met her eyes at last. It felt like surfacing. "You want to know why I took your contract for less than half my rate, girl? Why I've been walking you through darks I won't take the cart-boys near? Because when you showed me that hand — that burning hand — I looked at it and I thought: this one. This one might actually do it. And I have spent eleven years being the man who opens doors for other people's hopes because he can't face his own, and I am so bone-tired of that man that I'd follow a nineteen-year-old keeper through the Hollow Choir itself on the strength of a maybe."

* * *

She didn't say anything for a long time. That's the thing about Jonah Okafor — she treats silence like a tool instead of a void. Most people fill silence the way water fills a hole. She lets it sit there and work.

When she finally spoke, her voice was rough at the edges. "Your daughter. In the vault. You think she's still—"

"I don't know what she is." Honest, at least. "The tanner's boy said the vault souls don't dream, don't age, don't hurt. Said it's like being a coin in a purse. Whether that's mercy or horror I leave to smarter men. All I know is that as long as the vault stays shut, she's neither saved nor lost. Just held. And held can change."

"Held can change," she repeated, tasting it.

"That's the creed of every gambler and smuggler from here to the sea, girl. Held can change. It's what keeps us breathing."

She reached across the space between us and did something no one had done in eleven years: she took my hand. Her bandaged palm, warm through the linen — warmer than skin has any right to be these days — pressed against my knuckles, and she said, "Merrin Vale," out loud, deliberate, like setting a stone in a wall. "That's her name. I'll carry it down with yours. When we open that vault, we open it for both of them."

And I — Brannoc Vale, who has talked his way past river-spirits and bribed things with too many joints and lied beautifully to priests, kings, and corpses — I put my head down on my knees and shook, quiet as a man can shake, while a girl younger than my daughter held onto my hand and let me.

* * *

Later — hours later, the moss dimmed to embers, the galleries gone silent as held breath — she stirred beside her bedroll and said, "Brannoc. The choir. Tomorrow we reach it, yes? The Hollow Choir."

"Aye. Past the Weeping Stair, through the Gallery of Teeth, and there you are. The last door before the vault antechamber." I stared up at the ceiling of the world, where the stone hung like a sky that had given up. "You'll want to know what to expect."

"Sef tried to tell me. Souls without names, singing."

"Singing yours away," I corrected. "They don't attack. Attacking is effort, and they've had all their effort taken. They just... sing. And the song finds whatever name you're holding onto — the true one, the one under all your false ones — and it sings it back at you, softer and softer, until you can't remember whether it was ever yours. And then you sit down among them and open your mouth, and that's that. Fourteen seekers, girl. Three died on the roads. Ten of them are sitting in that choir right now, singing. I've heard voices I knew down there. A ferryman. A priestess from the coast country. A girl named Aldith who used to swear she'd be queen of something someday."

Silence again. But a different silence now — the loaded kind, the kind with a decision inside it.

"Tam taught me a game," she said at last, into the dark. "When we were small. Before. You hum a song and the other person has to guess it from three notes. He was terrible at it. He'd pick songs nobody knew, songs he'd made up himself, so I could never win unless I knew *him*." A pause. "He's down there wearing a smile that fits him wrong. And tomorrow I'm going to walk through a choir full of erased souls who want to make me into one of them. And the thing I'm going to hold onto isn't a memory of his face or his voice or his laugh. It's that game. Three notes and a brother who cheats. That's mine. Nobody sang that out of me yet."

I lay in the dark grinning like a fool with cold eyes wet, and I thought: eleven years I've been waiting for a seeker with a reason stronger than grief.

Grief is common as rain. But a girl who fights erasure with a cheating brother's game —

"Well then," I said, and reached for my boots. "Best get some sleep. Because come morning, girl, I'm going to show you the Weeping Stair, and I'm going to teach you the one rule of the choir — and then you're going to break it, because it's a stupid rule, and I've decided you're the one to break it."

Above us, faint as a heartbeat through fathoms of stone, something began — very softly — to ring.
