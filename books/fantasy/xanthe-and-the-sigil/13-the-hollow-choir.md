## 13. The Hollow Choir

There is a moment on every smuggler's road where the guide stops talking.

Brannoc Vale had been talking for two days straight — about the weight of the dark, the price of lamp-oil down here, a spirit at the fourth crossing who'd once traded him a boat for his left boot and considered it charity — and then we rounded a bend in the passage where the walls went smooth as throat-skin, and he stopped mid-sentence like someone had cut his string.

"That's the door," he said quietly.

I looked past him. There was no door. There was a corridor of black stone that drank my lamplight instead of reflecting it, and somewhere far down its length, something was humming.

"Door," I said. "Where?"

"You'll see it when you're meant to." He wouldn't go further than the bend. I knew that without asking; I'd watched him flinch away from three side-passages already, each time with the particular care of a man avoiding an old debt rather than a new danger. "The choir sits between here and the vault antechamber. No road around. No bribing them. They don't want anything you can give."

"Then what do they take?"

He looked at me the way you look at a person standing too close to a cliff edge, deciding whether to tell them or let them find out.

"Themselves," he said. "They were people once. The court unmakes them — peels the name off slow, like bark. What's left sings. And what they sing, they sing *at* you. It comes off easy, Jonah. A name. Easier than anything else you own."

"And if they get it?"

"Then you join the choir." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; I could see it move through his shoulders like a stone dropping through water. "You won't know you've joined. That's the mercy of it. You'll just open your mouth one day and find out your voice belongs to something else."

* * *

I have thought a great deal about names since the river-crossing. Down there, at the water, the spirit demanded true names as toll, and I gave it a lie and paid in blood instead, and I came away thinking I understood the rules of this place: everything has a price, prices can be haggled, and blood spends as well as words if you're clever about it.

Standing before the choir's corridor, I understood I'd been reading the wrong ledger. The river wanted my name because a name is currency. The choir didn't want to *spend* mine. It wanted to *empty* me. Those are different hungers entirely. One is commerce. The other is appetite.

I checked my supplies the way Mother used to check her wards: twice, and lying to herself both times. Lamp oil, half spent. Bread gone hard as slate but still bread. The sigil on my palm, which had stopped hurting days ago and now simply glowed faintly whenever the dark got deep enough, like a coal remembering fire. It was the only thing down here that had never lied to me.

"Brannoc," I said. "How far through?"

"Half a mile of stone. Twenty minutes walking. Longer if—" He stopped himself.

"If what?"

"If you stop to listen." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Don't. Whatever you hear in there — your mother's voice, Tam's, mine — it isn't memory. Memory doesn't repeat itself exactly. These things do. Perfect repetition. That's how you know."

"How would you know?" I asked, and immediately wished I hadn't, because his face did something complicated and old.

"Because I listened," he said. "Forty years ago. For a long time." He turned back up the passage toward the crossing where we'd camped. "I'll be here when you come out. If you come out."

"What if I come out wrong? Singing?"

He didn't turn around. "Then I'll know what I always suspected," he said, "and I'll keep walking anyway, because that's the kind of man I am." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and I decided not to hold it against him. We all carry our ledgers.

* * *

The corridor swallowed light so completely that my lamp became less a tool than a rumor. I walked into a darkness so total that my own hands existed only when the sigil pulsed, and after a while even that dimmed, as though the stone were drinking it too.

And then I heard the singing.

It was beautiful. I want to be honest about that, because everything afterward depends on it being beautiful. It wasn't shrieking or moaning or any of the horrors the village fireside tales promised. It was voices — dozens, maybe hundreds — singing a single sustained note with perfect, terrible harmony, the way a bell rings after the striking is done. It filled the corridor the way water fills a lung.

I kept walking. Brannoc's advice rang in my skull: *don't stop to listen.* But there is a difference between hearing and listening, and the choir erased it. You could not hear them without listening. The sound reached inside your ears and arranged itself around whatever you loved most, and it began — gently, patiently — to sing *that.*

My mother's lullaby first. The one she hummed over the wash-basin, off-key in the same places every time, and the choir sang it perfectly on-key, which was how I knew, with a cold drop through my stomach, that this was not memory. Memory stumbles. This thing had polished my mother's imperfections away and in doing so had made her into someone else, some idealized mother-shaped instrument, and the grief that rose in me was real but it was aimed at nothing. There was no one there. Just my love, harvested and played back.

*Keep walking,* I told myself. My boots made no sound on the stone. That should have frightened me more than it did. Everything down here was teaching me that my own body was negotiable.

Then the note changed, and it was Tam.

Not the Tam from the ledger's torn page, the boy who'd been sold. Not even the smiling stranger I'd seen in the court above the vault road, hollow-eyed and graceful and wrong. This was Tam at nine years old, sitting on the gate-house step, showing me how to whistle through a blade of grass, laughing because I couldn't do it, laughing the way he laughed, head thrown back, gap-toothed—

I stopped.

That was the trap. Of course that was the trap. But knowing a trap and not stepping in it are different skills, and grief is a very good teacher of the wrong lessons.

They sang him perfectly. Every detail. The exact pitch of his laugh. The way he said my name — *Jo-nah*, two syllables, the second one always teasing — and when the choir reached my name, sung in my brother's nine-year-old voice, I felt something in my chest physically loosen, like a knot being worked undone by patient fingers.

*Jonah.*

*Jonah Okafor.*

*Daughter of Maren. Keeper of the gate. Sister of—*

"No," I said aloud, and my voice sounded small and human and mine, and the choir paused — actually paused, the way a predator pauses when prey does something unexpected — and then resumed, softer, kinder, more persuasive.

That was the worst part. They could be kind. They sang my name the way you'd sing a homesick child to sleep, and with each repetition I felt the edges of myself going smooth. My name sat in me like a stone in a riverbed, and their song was water, and water is patient beyond all patience. I tried to remember what I was walking toward and found the vault a distant, theoretical thing. I tried to remember why and found only *Tam, Tam, Tam,* and even that was becoming a shape without a story, a bell without a clapper.

Somewhere in me, a very tired part whispered: *what would it cost, really? To put it down? You've carried it so long. The gate, the name, the family nobody remembers, the whole invisible life. Set it down. Rest. Sing.*

And I might have. I think I might have. I think the choir wins most of its singers not by force but by offering them the one thing no keeper ever gets.

But the sigil chose that moment to burn.

Not glow — *burn*, white-hot across my palm, and the pain was so clean and specific that it cut through the song like a knife through silk. And in the white flare of it I saw, projected against the dark or maybe against the inside of my own eyelids, the gate above Thornfell. Sealed. Waiting. Carved with the same lines that now lived in my skin, and I understood suddenly and completely: the sigil was not a brand. It was a signature. Someone had signed the world shut, and the signing hand was mine by inheritance, and if I gave up my name I would give up the only hand that could ever sign again.

The choir would not just be taking Jonah Okafor. It would be taking the last heir, the last key, the last chance Tam had of coming home whole.

I gripped the burning palm with my other hand and held on like a drowning woman holds a rope, and I began to walk. Slowly. Badly. But forward.

* * *

Here is what saved me, and I record it precisely because someday — if there is a someday — I want the record to show it was not courage. Courage implies choice made clean. This was uglier.

I fought memory with memory. The choir sang polished lies, perfect repetitions, so I answered with the flawed originals. I recited them under my breath like ward-prayers:

*Tam bit his nails. He denied it constantly. He had a scar on his chin from falling off the goat shed, age eleven, and he told everyone it was a knife fight.*

*He cheated at cards. Badly. He'd pocket the good stones when he thought I wasn't looking and then lose magnificently.*

*He cried at Mother's funeral and pretended it was the wind, and we were both grateful for the fiction, and I never once called him on it, because love is mostly the things you agree not to say.*

*He was afraid of the dark. He slept with a candle stub burning until he was twelve years old. He, who was sold into the deepest dark under the mountain. He, who has lived eight years in the place I am now whistling past.*

That last one nearly broke me open, but it broke me open toward walking rather than toward stopping, and I learned something down in that corridor that I suspect the court does not want its visitors learning: perfection is the enemy. Their song had no seams, so it could not hold anything. But my memories were ragged, contradictory, embarrassing, alive — and alive is the one material the unnamed cannot counterfeit. Every flaw was a nail holding me together.

The choir seemed to realize it too. As I stumbled deeper, the song changed tactics. It stopped being my loved ones and started being *me* — my own voice, singing my own thoughts back at me a half-beat ahead, so that I couldn't tell whether I was thinking or being thought. It sang my doubts. It sang my resentment of Mother, my terror of the court, my shameful flicker of temptation at the Regent's offer, and it sang them so accurately that I felt skinned.

So I did the only rude thing available to a woman with no weapon but herself.

I sang back.

Badly. On purpose. I took Mother's lullaby — the real one, off-key in the same places every time — and I bellowed it down the corridor in my flat, untrained keeper's voice, cracking on the high notes, dragging the rhythm, mangling it with the loving incompetence of someone who actually knew the woman who first hummed it. I added verses. I made up words. I sang about smuggler's boots and hard bread and Brannoc Vale's forty years of guilt, loudly, tunelessly, a one-woman desecration.

The choir faltered.

I don't mean it hesitated. I mean the harmony *broke* — voices scattering like birds off a wire — because they had never encountered anything they couldn't harmonize with. Perfection cannot absorb error. Error gets in under it, the way water gets under a door. My terrible singing was, in the grammar of that place, a kind of truth, and truth down here is contraband.

Some of the voices, I swear on the sigil, followed me. Quietly. Off-key. Learning.

I did not look at them. Looking felt like an invitation. But I walked faster, singing worse, tears running freely down my face and freezing in the cold air of the corridor, and the black stone began — gradually, grudgingly — to give back my lamplight.

* * *

The corridor ended at a doorway that was, finally, a door: an arch of grey stone carved with the builder-marks I'd grown up tracing on the gate above, worn shallow by centuries of hands. Beyond it, warm light. The smell of dust and paper and old metal. The antechamber.

I stopped on the threshold and looked back once, down the throat of the passage.

The choir had resumed its single note, but it was different now. Thinner. Or perhaps richer — it was hard to say. Somewhere in the harmony there was a new voice carrying a melody it shouldn't have known, wobbling, human, stubbornly off-key, and I pressed my burned hand to my mouth and grieved for it and was proud of it in the same breath, because I will never know if it was one of them turning, or something of one of them escaping, and either answer costs more than I can pay tonight.

The arch's carvings lit under my palm as I passed through, the sigil fitting the central mark like a key finding the cut it was made for, and behind me I heard the singing stop altogether — abruptly, completely, like a held breath.

Ahead, lamplight fell across shelves, a desk, a locked iron cabinet taller than I was, and papers — hundreds of papers, thousands, stacked and bound and labeled in a hand I recognized from my mother's ledger.

The covenant was here. I could feel it the way you feel weather coming.

And beneath the antechamber floor, faint but unmistakable, something had begun to count. Bells, far below. Counting down.
