## 14. Ledger of Debts

The antechamber to the soul-vault was smaller than I'd expected, which felt like a joke the mountain was playing on me. Everything down here had been built to a scale meant to diminish you—the caverns vast enough to swallow villages whole, the corridors tall enough for gods to walk without stooping—and then, at the very heart of it, where they kept the actual souls of actual people, a room no bigger than Mother's pantry.

I stood in the doorway and let Brannoc catch his breath behind me. The bone bells were still ringing somewhere above and behind us, that awful dry clatter rolling through the stone like knucklebones in a giant's cup. We'd have minutes. Maybe less. Whatever slept in the court beyond the vault did not sleep deeply once its alarms woke.

"Ledger's supposed to be here," Brannoc said, between wheezes. He'd told me on the road up from the choir gallery—told me about his daughter, Maren, sold thirteen years ago by his own hand for enough coin to dig himself out of debt, and how he'd spent every year since guiding other people's children down into the dark hoping one of them would climb back out with something worth having. "Left shelf. Under the lamp."

There was a lamp. Of course there was a lamp. It burned without oil, a small blue flame sitting in a dish of black glass, and it cast light the way deep water does—not so much illuminating as revealing what was already lit somehow, from some direction with no source. The shadows it made didn't agree with each other.

The left shelf held exactly one thing: a ledger bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth with age. I lifted it down and knew the weight of it in my hands before I'd opened it. Some books you can feel. This one had been handled by generations of Okafor hands, and it had learned us, the way a walking stick learns the grip of the hand that carries it.

"You don't have to read it," Brannoc said quietly. "Not now. We could take it and go—"

"We're not going anywhere until I understand what we're stealing." I set the ledger on the reading ledge built into the wall—a ledge worn smooth in two places, I noticed. Two readers' places. Someone had stood here for centuries, generation after generation, reading this book by lamplight before going in to do whatever the vault required. "And I need to know if my mother was the monster Sef's stories make her out to be. If she sold Tam the way Brannoc Vale sold Maren. Because if she was—" My voice did something embarrassing. I steadied it. "Then everything I've been carrying since the sigil woke is wrong, and I'd rather know now, with bells ringing, than later with nothing at all."

Brannoc didn't argue. That was one of the things I'd come to respect about him: he knew when a woman needed to read a book more than she needed to be comforted.

I opened the cover.

* * *

The first pages were older than the village, older than the priestess line, maybe older than writing itself, because whoever had kept this ledger first hadn't written—they'd *pressed*. Marks driven into the page with a stylus hard enough to emboss the sheets beneath. I traced them with my fingertips and felt the sigil on my palm warm faintly in answer, a dog recognizing its own breed.

It took me an hour to work out the early entries, and I'll spare you the tedium of dead scripts and accounting conventions that treat souls as units of weight. What matters is what I found in the middle section, where the handwriting turned to something I recognized—because it was my grandmother's hand, then my mother's, the same slanted letters I'd seen on shopping lists and recipe cards all my childhood.

The payments were laid out plain as harvest records. Every thirteen years, on the thirteenth moon's turn, the ledger recorded a transaction. Not always a soul. Early on it was mostly *other* things—years off a life, the memory of a face, the sound of someone's laughter, taken whole. The court beneath accepted payment in any currency of the self. But every fourth cycle, when the moon completed its great wheel, the debt came due in the only coin that mattered.

A name. A whole soul, given freely or taken whole.

My mother's entries began thirty-nine years ago. And beside each entry, in her careful hand, was a note. The notes started clinical—*paid in full, seal holds*—and grew, over the years, into something else. Something that read less like bookkeeping and more like prayer.

*The child dreams of the gate again. Asked why we keep it shut. Told him the truth in part. He did not cry. He is seven.*

*Tam asked me today if the mountain eats people. I said it asks, and we answer. He thought about this a long time and then said, "Does it ever ask for someone who wants to go?"*

*I hate this ledger. I hate this house. I love them both.*

I read those lines three times, and each time the floor seemed to tilt a little further. Because these were not the words of a woman selling her son. These were the words of a woman watching her son grow toward something she dreaded, and being unable to stop it, because—

I turned the page and found the torn edge.

It was there, exactly where Sef had said it would be, where the pattern demanded it: the entry for the last thirteenth turn, thirteen years ago. A ragged stub of paper where a full page should have been, ripped out with such force that three pages after it carried the scar of it. Whoever tore it out had done it fast and final, the way you tear out a page you cannot bear to have exist.

But paper remembers. My mother taught me that herself, actually, though she was talking about baking parchment at the time. Press hard enough, and the sheet beneath keeps your letter even after the top page burns.

I tilted the stub toward the blue flame and saw the ghost of writing pressed through from the missing page—the mirror-image shadow of ink long gone. Most of it was too faint. But the ledger had been pressed hard that day. Whoever wrote the final entry had borne down like a woman engraving stone.

I read what the paper remembered:

*...he came to me at dawn. Thirteen years old and calm as a man twice his age. He knows. He has known since he found the ledger last spring, and I am undone by it—he was never meant to carry this knowledge, he was meant to be spared it, the way SHE was meant to be spared everything.*

*He says: "It has to be freely given. That's what the covenant says. So give it freely."*

*I told him no. I told him I would go myself. He laughed at me—my gentle boy, my quiet boy, he LAUGHED—and said, "Mama, the gate doesn't want you, it wants what you love best, and everyone in this family knows what Mama loves best." Then he looked at Jonah sleeping in the corner and said the truest, cruelest thing I have ever heard my son say:*

*"It was never supposed to be me. Read the covenant. The heir goes. The heir has ALWAYS gone. You've been hiding her from the ledger her whole life, and the mountain knows, and the mountain is patient, and if we don't pay what it asks it will simply take her later, when she's grown, when it can take everything she's built. At least this way she gets a life. At least this way she gets years."*

*He put his hand over hers—over JONAH'S hand, asleep—and the sigil rose to meet him, and I understood that the mountain had already chosen, and that my daughter was the heir, and that my son had known longer than I had.*

*So I write the only entry left to me: paid not in blood nor in theft but in GIFT, freely offered, one soul, TAM OKAFOR, aged thirteen, in place of the heir, JONAH OKAFOR, that she might live unmarked. May the mountain honor the substitution. May my daughter never read this page.*

*I have torn it out. Let the record show the debt stands, and the substitute stands, and my shame stands with them both.*

*Seal holds. God help me. Seal holds.*

* * *

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that Brannoc crossed the room and put a hand on my shoulder, and I flinched like something bitten.

"Jonah."

"She didn't sell him." The words came out of me flat and strange, like coins dropped on stone. "She didn't sell him. He *volunteered*. He was thirteen years old and he read the covenant and he figured out—I was the one. I was always the one. The heir. Me." I looked down at my palm, at the sigil glowing its soft gold against the dark. "She hid me. Her whole life, she hid me from the ledger, from the village, from everything, and he—"

Tam. Thirteen years old. Calm as a man twice his age. Putting his hand over mine while I slept, and the sigil rising to meet him, and him saying *at least this way she gets a life.*

Every memory I had of my brother rearranged itself around that sentence. The way he'd stopped teasing me the year he turned twelve. The way he'd made me teach him everything I knew about the wards, saying it was boredom, saying it was nothing. The way he'd hugged me goodbye the morning he "went to stay with cousins in the lowlands"—hugged me like a boy memorizing something, and grinned, and said, *"Take care of the gate, Jo. It likes you better anyway."*

He'd known. He'd walked out of our house knowing exactly where he was going, and he'd let me believe the lie because the truth would have broken me open at eleven years old, and my brother—who was kind, who was always kind—had decided I would get my breaking later, or never, if he could arrange it.

"He bought me thirteen years," I said. My voice cracked down the middle. "Thirteen years of being nobody, and he paid for them with—"

"With himself," Brannoc said. "Aye. That's what the good ones do. That's what Maren—" He stopped. Cleared his throat. When he continued, his voice was rough. "The court will have shown him to you smiling because that's what they do. They wear the ones they hold. But that boy in the ledger—that's still in there. Souls don't stop being themselves just because somebody's wearing them like a coat. You said it yourself. He looked at you. He *knew* you, under all that wrongness. Why d'you think he warned you to run?"

Because the court wanted my sigil. Because Tam, even hollowed, even bound, had spent his one free act on shoving me toward the door. Thirteen years old or twenty-six, dreaming or worn, my brother's whole self was one gesture, repeated across the dark: *she gets the life. Not me. She gets the life.*

And I had spent thirteen years resenting him for leaving.

"I have to get him out," I said. It wasn't a decision. Decisions are weighed; this fell on me like weather. "Not for the debt. Not for the gate. For him. For the boy in this ledger."

"Aye," Brannoc said. "That's the spirit that walks out of here or doesn't walk at all. Now—do you hear that?"

I listened. Beneath the distant rattle of the bone bells, beneath my own pulse hammering in my ears, there was another sound rising through the stone floor. Singing. Faint, sweet, horribly familiar—the choir of the unnamed, far away but growing nearer, or perhaps waking, called up by the alarm like hounds to a horn.

And underneath the singing, closer, much closer, a new sound. Footsteps. Unhurried. Courtly. Coming down the corridor that led to the vault, from the direction of the throne hall, from the direction of *him*.

The Regent had woken. And he was not alone.

Brannoc blew out the blue lamp with one breath, plunging us into darkness, and gripped my arm. In my palm, the sigil flared gold—bright, unmistakable, a beacon in the black.

"There's no hiding that, girl," Brannoc whispered.

"No," I said, closing my fist around the light, watching it bleed through my fingers. "There isn't."

The footsteps stopped just outside the antechamber door.
