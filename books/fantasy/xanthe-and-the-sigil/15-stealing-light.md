## 15. Stealing Light

### JONAH

The court slept the way a snake sleeps: coiled, and only pretending to have no ears.

We'd learned that much in three days of watching from the bone-gallery above the vault road, where the smugglers' maps called this stretch *the Quiet* and Brannoc had made me swear, twice, not to hum. The nobles of the deep court didn't sleep as we did. They stood or reclined in alcoves of pale stone like ornaments on a shelf, eyes half-lidded, chests barely moving, and every so often one of them would smile at nothing — at something we couldn't see — and I would grip my knife and count to a hundred until the smile faded.

"They dream together," Brannoc had whispered the first night. "That's the trick of it. All those hollow heads full of the same dark, and when they're deep enough in it, there's a hour — maybe less — where nobody's home but the locks."

"And if we're wrong?"

"Then we're a very interesting dream."

Now the hour was close. I could tell by the vault itself.

The soul-vault sat at the end of the Quiet like a held breath: a door of black basalt taller than the gate at Thornfell, carved over every inch with the same script that spiraled up my palm. No hinges. No handle. Only a socket at its center, hand-shaped, waiting. Around the door's crown hung the bells — I'd seen them first and thought them decoration, dozens of small white shapes strung on silver wire, swaying in a wind that didn't exist down here.

They were finger bones. Human ones, polished smooth, each one tuned to a different scream. Brannoc hadn't needed to tell me that part. Some things you know the way you know a stair has one step more than your legs expect.

"Sigil," Brannoc murmured beside me. We crouched behind a fallen pillar whose carvings had been deliberately defaced, gouged blind, centuries ago. Even the stone down here didn't want to be recognized. "Show me again what happens."

I turned my hand over. In the gloom the sigil glowed faintly now, awake since the choir gallery, a lattice of light like frost forming in reverse. When I flexed my fingers, it pulsed. When I thought about Tam — which was most of the time — it burned.

"It opens for an heir," I said. "That's what the ledger said. *The door knows its blood.* Mother's handwriting, underlined twice, like she was arguing with herself."

"And your mother's dead."

"Yes."

"And you're the last—"

"I'm the last. I've been counting." I looked at him. In the dark his face was all angles and old guilt, and he was staring at the bells, not the door, and I understood he wasn't checking our escape route. He was checking the thing that had caught seekers before us. "How many?"

He knew what I meant without asking. That was the strange mercy of Brannoc Vale: he never pretended not to understand grief.

"Eleven," he said quietly. "That I've guided this far. Nine died at the river or the choir. Two reached this room." His jaw worked. "The bells rang both times. Not because they touched the door. Because they hesitated."

"Hesitated?"

"You'll want to stop when you see her name." He didn't look at me. "The vault shows you what you're stealing. It wants you to hesitate. The hesitation is the alarm. Don't give it one."

Above us, far off through the galleries, the last sounds of the court's evening thinned away — a rustle like leaves, a note of that endless hollow music played on instruments I refused to look at too closely — and then silence, thick and total, the silence of a thousand shared dreams closing over the deep places like water.

"Now," Brannoc breathed.

I stood up out of the pillar's shadow and walked into the open, alone, toward a door older than the mountain's manners, and I did not let myself think about how easy it would be to turn around.

The sigil flared as I approached, throwing blue-white light up the basalt, and the carvings began to move — slowly, like script remembering how to be read. My heart was a fist knocking on a locked chest. I raised my marked palm to the socket.

The stone was warm.

Not warm like rock holding the day's heat. Warm like skin. Like someone's hand already resting there, patient, having waited generations for mine to fit inside it.

"The door knows its blood," I whispered, and pressed my palm home.

The sigil blazed. Light ran out from my fingers along every carved line at once, the whole door becoming a map of burning veins, and somewhere beneath my feet I felt a vast mechanism consider me — weigh me, taste the shape of my name even though I'd paid a river spirit not to speak it — and then the basalt split along a seam no eye could find, and the soul-vault opened onto the dark.

Cold air came out. Old air. Air that had been standing guard over stolen people since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to fear the moon.

Inside, on tiered shelves of glass and silver, flames were burning.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Small, steady, patient flames, each cupped in its own vessel, each one a person — I knew it instantly, the way you know a face across a crowded market. Souls, banked like lamps in a lighthouse for the drowned. And the light of them, all together, was the warmest thing I had seen since the sun.

"Oh," I said, uselessly. "Oh, you're all still *here*."

"Don't talk to them," Brannoc said sharply, right behind me, though his voice cracked on the last word, and I realized he wasn't looking at the shelves. He was looking at one flame in particular, third row, left side, burning a color like autumn.

I didn't ask. There would be time later, if we lived, and if we didn't, then there wouldn't, and either way some griefs deserve privacy even in a burglary.

"TAM," I said instead. Just once, just to myself, a name spent like a coin I couldn't afford — and the sigil flared in answer, tugging my gaze up and left, to a high shelf near the vault's crown, where a single flame burned the exact gold-green of the birch trees above Thornfell in October.

Tam's soul. My brother, reduced to a lamp. And yet not reduced at all — the flame leaned, as I came closer, the way a plant leans toward a window. Toward me. Toward the last piece of the world that remembered why it was lit.

My eyes filled and I let them, for exactly three seconds. Then I took the empty vessel from my pack — lead-lined, wrapped in wool, blessed by a priestess who'd spat into it for luck — and reached up.

*Don't hesitate,* Brannoc had said. *The hesitation is the alarm.*

But here is what no one tells you about hesitating: it doesn't feel like hesitation. It feels like love. It feels like wanting one more second, just one, to look at what you're about to save, to memorize it in case the saving fails. My fingers closed around the vessel and the flame inside it bent toward my hand like a child lifting its arms, and I stood there one heartbeat too long, drinking in the sight —

and every bell above the door rang.

### BRANNOC

There is a sound the world makes when it ends politely. Most folk never hear it. I heard it eleven years ago, in this same room, when my daughter's keeper-of-a-name went out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the bells sang one clean note, and the court woke smiling.

The note tonight was different. Higher. Angrier. Bone remembers being alive; that's the cruelty of using finger bones for bells. They ring with everything the hand ever wanted to hold, and when they ring for an intruder, they ring with *longing*.

"MOVE," I said, and grabbed the girl's shoulder, and she moved, gods bless her, she moved fast, the vessel tucked against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and we were through the door and out into the Quiet when the dreaming ended.

You can feel a thousand dreams breaking. It comes up through the floor first — a shudder, like ice going out of a lake — and then the galleries overhead began to fill with that terrible unhurried sound of beautiful people rising from their alcoves, silk against stone, and a voice, sweet and resonant as a struck wineglass, saying words in the old tongue that needed no translating:

*Find them.*

"The long way," Jonah gasped. Not a question. Good. I'd taught her the map until she could draw it bleeding and blindfolded. "The pillar hall, then down—"

"Down's cut off. Bells woke the Underwardens." I shoved her left, toward the gallery of defaced pillars, where eleven years of my failures had taught me every shadow worth hiding in. "We go up. Through the reliquary. It's mad and it's narrow and the things in there bite, but they bite slow."

She didn't argue. That was the thing about Jonah Okafor that kept catching me sideways — everyone else I'd guided had argued, bargained, frozen, prayed. She just ran, flame against her chest, lighting our way through the dark like a scandal.

Behind us the bells kept ringing, and worse, they began to change. One by one they found each other's pitch and braided it into something almost like speech. Almost like a *name*. My name, actually, spoken in bone and longing, rolling down the corridors:

*Brannoc Vale. Brannoc Vale, who sold a daughter and steals for strangers. Brannoc Vale, we remember you.*

"They're lying," I panted, mostly to myself. "That's not memory. Memory doesn't want anything."

"Keep telling yourself that," Jonah said, and there was something fierce and almost kind in it, and I nearly laughed, which in my experience is what the body does instead of screaming when screaming would slow you down.

The reliquary mouth opened ahead of us, a slit in the rock hung with teeth-like stalactites, and beyond it the narrow dark where the biting things lived. Jonah stopped at the threshold for half a breath — half a *hesitation*, curse her loving heart — and looked back the way we'd come, toward the vault, toward all those thousands of flames still burning in their shelves while their owners' names bought wine and favors upstairs in the bright cruel court.

"We come back for them," she said. Not to me. To the dark, to the flames, to whatever in this mountain could hear a promise and hold it to interest. "All of them. I swear it by the sigil."

The sigil answered. Flared white through her fingers, and for one moment the whole reliquary throat lit up like dawn, and every biting thing in the dark went still, because whatever the builders' mark was to the things below, it was older than their appetites and heavier than their hunger.

Then the light died, and the bells screamed, and we ran.

* * *

### JONAH

The reliquary was a cathedral's ribcage stuffed into a rabbit warren. Relics of the deep court lined niches on both sides — rings that whispered, mirrors that showed you older, a bird made of folded letters that beat its wings and shed paper feathers spelling debts — and between the niches, in the cracks, the biting things kept their slow vigil. Pale, jointed, patient as interest accruing. One fastened its mouth to Brannoc's sleeve as he squeezed past and simply *hung there*, gnawing with the leisurely confidence of something that has never once been in a hurry.

"Leave it," he grunted, shaking his arm. "Coat's leathered in oath-blood. It'll sulk off."

The vessel against my ribs was warm now. Warmer than wool and lead could account for. Tam's flame, waking, leaning toward my heartbeat like it remembered the rhythm from before — from the farm, from the winters, from the boy who used to sleep with his ear against my back during thunderstorms because he swore my heart was louder than the sky and therefore braver.

*Hold on,* I told it, without speaking, without spending a syllable. *Hold on, little brother. You're going home.*

We broke out of the reliquary into the gallery of hanging roots — roots, a mile underground, thick as oxen, descending from a ceiling lost in black, and every root humming faintly with the prayers of Thornfell village, because the old gods drank faith through the mountain's veins and these were the straws. The humming had changed since I'd first come down. Faster. Hungrier. Sef's voice was in that hum somewhere, and a hundred desperate villagers', and underneath it all, vast and patient, the thing the court served, turning over in its sleep of centuries, dreaming now with its eyes open.

And blocking the far archway, silks settling around her like poured ink, stood the Regent.

Not running. Never running. She smiled at us the way winter smiles at a late harvest.

"There she is," the Regent said, and her voice was honey poured over iron. "The heir. Come to make a withdrawal." Her hollow, lovely eyes fixed on the glow at my ribs. "Give it back, daughter of the builders, and I will let you walk out of my mountain with your name still in your mouth. Refuse—" she spread her hands, gracious, inevitable, "—and we will discuss terms. Again. I do so enjoy discussing terms."

Every exit behind her was filling with courtiers. Silent, beautiful, wrong. The bells overhead had gone quiet at last, satisfied, their work done: they had told the whole mountain exactly where we were.

Brannoc shifted beside me, and I felt rather than saw him decide something, the way you feel weather change.

"Jonah," he said quietly. "The root shaft. Behind you. Third root, there's a gap in the wall — smugglers' cut, goes up to the twelfth gallery. It's tight."

"How tight?"

"Tight enough that I can't fit." He smiled, and it was the saddest arithmetic I've ever seen a man do. "And tight enough that a door needs holding, once they realize where you've gone."

"No," I said.

"Somebody has to stand in the doorway, girl. That's all a door is, in the end. Somebody willing to stand in it." He pushed past me toward the Regent, pulling his knife, pulling eleven years of guilt up with it like a bucket from a well. "Go. Take him home. Tell my daughter—" his voice caught, steadied, "—tell her the seeking worked. Tell her it was worth it."

The Regent laughed, delighted, hungry. The courtiers began to walk forward.

I ran for the gap in the wall with my brother's soul against my heart and another man's sacrifice at my back, and the last thing I heard from below, echoing up the smugglers' cut as I climbed, was not screaming.

It was Brannoc Vale, laughing, telling the court exactly what he thought of them — loudly, at length, and in words that made even the bells blush — buying me, second by second, every foot of the long way up.
