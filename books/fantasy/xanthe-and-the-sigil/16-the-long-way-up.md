## 16. The Long Way Up

The bells of bone rang for eleven seconds. I counted. Counting was the only thing my hands knew how to do, and they were shaking too badly to hold a torch, so counting it was.

Eleven seconds, and then Brannoc said, in the flat voice of a man doing arithmetic on his own funeral, "That'll be every soul in the court awake and asking who's been at the larder."

"Then we run," I said.

"We run," he agreed, and we ran.

* * *

You'd think the smugglers' roads would be built for running. They were built for walking — slow, careful, torch-lit-at-the-edges walking, with pauses at junctions where a sensible smuggler checked the air for sulfur or listened for the breathing of things that lived in the walls. Brannoc had walked these roads for thirty years. He had maps in his head older than any map on paper, routes with names like the Widow's Stair and the Long Sigh, passages so narrow you had to exhale fully to fit through them sideways.

We were not being sensible smugglers. We were being two thieves fleeing a court of hollow-eyed nobility with a stolen flame cupped against my chest like a bird with a broken wing.

Tam's soul burned cold, which is the thing nobody tells you about souls. Not warm, not comforting, not the golden hearth-glow of every fireside story. Cold. A clean, white, winter-morning cold, and it drank heat from everything near it, including me. My fingers were blue around the little iron lantern I'd carried it in. My breath came out as frost even though we were deep enough under Thornfell that the seasons were somebody else's rumor.

"Left here," Brannoc panted. "No — right. Right. The left one goes down to the Drowned Gallery and I am not swimming tonight."

"How do you know which is which?"

"I don't. I'm guessing. It's worked for thirty years." He glanced back over his shoulder, and the torchlight caught his face, all its weathered creases suddenly canyons. "Girl. Jonah. If they catch us—"

"They won't."

"If they catch us," he repeated, with the patience of a man talking to a mule, "you give them the lantern and you keep your legs moving. You hear me? The flame's what matters. You're just the cart it rides in."

"I'm also the heir of the builders," I said. "I'm the most valuable thing in this mountain, according to their Regent, and she wasn't flattering me when she said it."

"Aye," he said. "So keep those valuable legs moving."

Behind us, far back through the black, the bone-bells changed their tune. They'd been clanging panic. Now they'd settled into something slower, deeper, a rhythm more felt than heard — a pulse. The mountain had found our scent, and now it was savoring it.

* * *

The first road fell in behind us an hour later. Fell in literally: a crack split the ceiling from wall to wall with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and then half the passage came down in slabs the size of coffins. Dust rolled over us, thick as wool. When it cleared, the way we'd come was gone, and something on the other side of the rubble was digging.

Not metaphorically. Digging. Stone grinding against stone, patient as erosion, deliberate as hunger. Whatever the court sent after us, it didn't need doors.

"The Long Sigh," Brannoc gasped, hauling me up by the arm. "There's a fork ahead. Three ways. One comes out above the tree line on the south face, one dead-ends at the old shrine, and one—" he hesitated, and I heard him choose to lie to me, heard it in the half-second gap, "—one loops back down toward the court, but it's long, and they won't expect it."

"And the third?"

"It's the long way up," he said. "It's always the long way up. That's why nobody uses it. Six days' climb, if the roof holds."

"Does the roof hold?"

"Roofs never hold, girl. That's what makes it climbing instead of strolling."

* * *

We climbed. Gods, how we climbed.

The Long Way Up was less a road than an argument the mountain was having with itself — switchbacks cut into the inside of a vertical shaft, ledges barely wider than my shoulders, places where some ancient smuggler had driven iron pegs into the rock and hung rope ladders that had rotted to decorative cobweb. Above us the shaft went up farther than the torchlight could argue with. Below us it went down into a darkness so complete it seemed to have weight, pressing up against us like water.

And all the while, the mountain followed.

It didn't chase. Chasing implies hurry. It *came*, the way winter comes — inexorable, everywhere at once, closing off exits we hadn't even reached yet. Twice we heard passages seal themselves ahead of us, stone sliding on stone somewhere in the dark, and twice Brannoc cursed and rerouted us through cracks I would have sworn no human could pass. He lost skin. I lost a boot heel. Tam's soul-flame flickered in its lantern and cast shadows that moved a half-second after we did.

At the top of the fourth ladder my arms gave out entirely and I hung there by my fingers, over that weighted dark, and thought with perfect clarity: *this is where I stop.* And then I thought about Tam's face in the vault when the flame touched him — no. No, that hasn't happened yet. That's still to come. What I thought about was simpler. I thought about a boy on a mountainside teaching me the names of clouds because neither of us would ever leave this valley, and I climbed.

"You're stubborn," Brannoc wheezed when I hauled myself over the lip. "Your whole family's stubborn. Stubbornness isn't a virtue, girl. It's just grief with somewhere to go."

"Then I'm very grieved," I said. "Move."

* * *

The door was my idea of a joke at first. We came out of the climbing shaft onto a wide landing, and there, set into the rock like a tooth in a jaw, stood a door. Actual door. Oak and iron, banded, with a bar across it thick as my waist. Someone had built it here, in the throat of the mountain, hundreds of years ago, to hold something back.

Beyond it, I could feel open air. Real air. Wind moved through gaps in the stone and smelled of pine and snowmelt and the particular green smell of Thornfell in late summer, and my eyes filled up with tears at the smell of *trees*, which shows you what six days underground does to a person.

Brannoc put his hand flat against the oak and stood there a long time.

"This is the Keeper's Door," he said finally. "Older than the gate itself, maybe. Smugglers' lore says it only holds if someone's willing to stand behind it." He turned and looked at me, and his face had gone calm in the way faces go calm right before terrible decisions. "Someone willing, Jonah. Not someone fast. Not someone clever. Willing."

"No," I said.

"You haven't let me finish."

"There's nothing to finish. We both walk through and we drop the bar behind us and—"

"And the mountain unbars it from the other side before we've made the treeline," he said. "You felt it following. It doesn't tire. It doesn't lose the scent. But it slows, girl. At a held door, it *slows*. Long enough for you and the flame to reach the light." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen, and I have since seen smiles on the faces of gods. "Thirty years I've guided seekers down here. Do you know why?"

"You told me. Your daughter—"

"Maren. Her name was Maren." He said it like setting down a weight he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it. "Sold her to pay a debt I owed the court. She was seven. And every seeker since, every hopeful fool I've pointed down these roads, I've been thinking: maybe this one makes it. Maybe this one gets somebody back. Maybe this one makes what I did mean something else." He took the bar in both hands. "None of them did. So let mine mean this instead."

"Brannoc—"

"Run, girl."

"*Brannoc—*"

"Jonah." My name in his mouth stopped me colder than the flame ever had. "When you get up. When you see sky. You say her name once, into the wind. Maren. That's all. That's the toll. Now *run*."

The mountain arrived.

I don't have better words than that, even now. The wall opposite the door bulged inward like skin over a fist, and through the stone I saw eyes — dozens, hundreds, the hollow beautiful eyes of the court, pressed up against the inside of the world like children at a window. And among them, frontmost, the Regent, her face tilted with something that on a human I'd have called tenderness.

"Vale," her voice came through the rock, soft as silt settling. "You brought her back to us once already. Stay, and be forgiven."

Brannoc lifted the bar free of its brackets. His arms shook with the weight and with age and with six days of climbing, and he lifted it anyway.

"Forgiveness," he said, "wasn't on offer when I needed it. Neither is she."

He set the bar home as I went through, and the last thing I saw was the door swinging shut between us, and the last thing I heard was not the mountain screaming but Brannoc Vale humming — low, cracked, tuneless — a lullaby. A father's lullaby, for a daughter seven years gone.

Then oak and iron met stone, and I ran.

* * *

I could tell you about the climb to the surface. About the scree slope and the freezing stream and the way dawn came up gray-green through the trees like the world's first morning. But honest truth, I remember almost none of it. My body did the work. The rest of me stayed on that landing, listening to a door hold, and hold, and hold —

and then, three hours later, faint through miles of rock, a sound like a bell of bone ringing exactly once.

I reached the treeline at sunrise. I set Tam's lantern down in the moss. I faced the mountain, and I said, into the wind, the way he'd asked:

"Maren."

Just once. The wind took it up the slopes and scattered it among the pines, and I swear — I will swear this until whatever's left of me stops swearing anything — I swear the wind carried it *down* again, into the stone, like a payment accepted.

Then I picked up my brother's soul and started for the village, where a priestess was waiting, and a gate was thinning, and a moon was waning toward its thirteenth turn, and I had perhaps five days to learn how to give a stolen flame back to the body it belonged to.

Behind me, deep in Thornfell, something knocked politely on a closed door, once, twice, and waited.

It could wait. So, someday, would I.

But that's ahead of me. First: the long way home.
