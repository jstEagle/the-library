## 17. Twelfth Turn

I woke on cold stone with someone else's heart in my chest.

That's not poetry. I mean it literally. The thing beating behind my ribs was steady and dutiful and utterly indifferent to me, the way a hired man is indifferent to the house he guards. It did its work. It did not know me. Nothing knew me, as it turned out — not the mountain air, not the taste of pine and snowmelt, not my own hands when I lifted them into the grey light of dawn and turned them over like artifacts from some dead civilization.

Which, in a sense, they were.

I was lying on the shelf of rock before the Thornfell gate, the great sealed door that half the village pretended didn't exist and the other half left offerings to out of spite. Above me the sky was doing that pale, apologetic thing it does before sunrise, all bruise-grey and silver at the edges. Below me, the drop to the valley. Behind me, the gate itself: black stone veined with lines of faint gold, carved over every inch with script no one living could read, humming with a silence so complete it felt less like quiet and more like held breath.

And beside me, wrapped in a shepherd's cloak that smelled of woodsmoke and lanolin, sat a woman I had never seen in my life, watching me with an expression I can only describe as grief performing patience.

"You're awake," she said.

"Apparently." My voice came out rusted. I coughed, tried again. "Where am I?"

"Thornfell. The high gate."

"Right," I said. "And where's that, exactly?"

Something crossed her face then — quick, swallowed, like a fish under ice. "North of the village. You've been... ill. A long illness."

"How long?"

She looked at the sky instead of answering, which is an answer of its own if you've ever been around people who lie for good reasons. I'd been around people who lied for good reasons my whole life, apparently, though I couldn't have told you whose life that was. That was the trouble. That was the whole entire trouble.

Let me back up.

The first thing you should know about waking up without a soul is that it isn't like waking up drunk, or concussed, or even born. A newborn at least has a factory warranty of instinct. What I had was a body operating from memory — muscle memory, habit memory, the shallow sediment of a person — with nothing underneath. I knew how to speak because speech lived in the body. I knew my name was Tam because it was stitched into the collar of the shirt I wore, in careful thread, *T. Okafor*, and some part of me read it the way you read a signpost. But knowing your name is not the same as being it. It was like wearing a coat with someone else's initials in the lining. Warm enough. Not mine.

The second thing you should know is what's missing. You notice it by absence, the way you notice a missing tooth. There was no weight in me. No ballast. Every feeling I had arrived thin and secondhand, like emotions described in a letter rather than felt. When I saw the sunrise I understood it was beautiful the way you understand arithmetic. When I looked at the woman in the shepherd's cloak and she flinched at something in my face, I understood I should be hurt, and noted it, and filed it away, and felt nothing whatsoever, and the nothing was the loudest thing in the world.

"I'm Tam," I said, testing it. "Aren't I."

"Yes." Her voice cracked on the single syllable and she disguised it as a cough, badly. "Tam Okafor."

"And you are?"

The pause went on long enough to have its own weather. "Sef," she said at last. "Priestess Sef. Of the village."

"Nice to meet you, Priestess Sef of the Village."

That got almost a laugh out of her, which surprised us both. Then her face did the grief-patience thing again, harder, and I understood — dimly, the way you understand a storm through a wall — that she was looking at me and seeing someone else entirely. Seeing who I was supposed to be. And whatever she saw there instead, it was hurting her more than it was hurting me, which told you everything about the state of my insides.

"There's someone coming up the path," she said. "Someone who—"

But I never learned who, because that's when the gods started pressing on the door.

* * *

You've never heard the old gods push against a seal, so let me describe it, since I'm apparently the only witness now with functioning ears and nothing better to do.

It began as warmth in the stone. The gate behind us — that held-breath silence — exhaled. The gold veins in the black rock brightened from ember-dim to furnace-orange, and the carvings began to move, slowly, the way frost flowers grow, script crawling along its channels like water finding old paths downhill. The hum dropped below hearing and became something you felt in your teeth, your sternum, the fluid of your eyes.

Then came the smell. Ozone and loam and something sweeter underneath, something like overripe fruit, like a feast left too long in a warm room. Richness going to rot. That's the smell of divinity past its sell-by date, in case you were wondering. Hunger dressed in glory.

"They're close," Sef said. She'd gotten to her feet, and there was a staff in her hand that hadn't been there before, iron-shod, older than she was. "They're always closest at the turning. Twelfth night to thirteenth moon. The seal thins and they lean on it."

"Should we be running?"

"We should be *still*," she said, which struck me as the sort of advice that works beautifully until it doesn't.

The pressure built. I want to be precise about this, because precision was the only thing I had left: it felt like standing at the bottom of deep water while something enormous swam overhead. Not attacking. Just passing. Its shadow alone enough to bend the world. The air thickened. The light changed color, silver going greenish, and somewhere above us clouds assembled themselves into shapes that had too much intention in them to be weather.

And through the stone of the gate, faint but unmistakable, came voices.

Not words. Worse than words. It was the sound of a congregation heard through a cathedral wall — murmurous, layered, vast — except the murmur wasn't prayer. It was appetite. Long-deferred, patient, bottomless appetite, the sound of something that has waited thirteen years and thirteen before that and would wait thirteen more without complaint because waiting costs the deathless nothing.

"They know something's changed," Sef said quietly. She had gone pale. Priests presumably get pale professionally; hers looked earned. "The balance shifted last night. Something was taken from the court below. Something that belonged here." She looked at me. Looked at me the way you look at a door you've just realized is unlocked.

"What?" I said. "What was taken?"

"You," she said simply. "You were taken. You were given. And now you're back, and the ledger doesn't balance anymore, and everything under the mountain is trying to work out who owes what." She laughed, short and terrible. "The gods can smell an unpaid debt, Tam Okafor. It's the only perfume they care for."

The pressure eased then — withdrew, receded like a tide, leaving the gate dark and the morning ordinary and me sitting in the wreckage of a sentence I didn't understand. Given. Taken. Ledger. I turned the words over and they weighed nothing, meant nothing, fit nowhere inside me. There was no place inside me for them to fit. That was the whole problem. I was all surface.

"The woman," I said. "In the cloak. The one who was watching me sleep. Who is she?"

Sef's face closed like a book slammed shut. "A friend."

"She cried, didn't she. While I slept. I could hear it even before—" I stopped, because I couldn't finish the thought, because finishing thoughts required a self to finish them *with*. "Why don't I remember anything?"

"Because you paid," Sef said, "for something worth remembering."

* * *

They brought me down to the village that afternoon, and I'll spare you most of it, because most of it was people.

People looking at me sideways in the lane. People crossing themselves with gestures from three religions back. An old woman who took my hand in the market, pressed a copper into it, and walked off weeping before I could return it. Children following at a distance, daring each other closer, scattering when I turned around. Everyone acting like I was either a ghost or a miracle, and me standing there unable to tell which, unable to care properly about which, holding a copper coin I had no idea what to do with.

There was one house at the end of the lane, small, stone-roofed, with a herb garden gone wild and a door painted the particular faded blue that says *someone loved this once and then stopped having time.* Sef steered me toward it and I felt — noted — the body's hesitation on the threshold. Hands remembering. Feet remembering. The shallow sediment again.

Inside smelled of dried lavender and dust and, beneath both, of a room long kept ready for someone.

"This was your home," Sef said.

I touched the mantel. My fingers found grooves in the wood, worn smooth, and the body knew them, and I watched my own hand trace letters I couldn't read — no, that's wrong, I could read them, they spelled a name, *JONAH,* cut deep and then cut deeper, over years, over and over, the way you worry a wound.

"Jonah," I said aloud.

Sef made a sound like the word had hit her physically.

"My sister," I said. The knowledge surfaced the way everything did — flat, factual, arriving from outside. "I have a sister named Jonah. Where is she?"

The silence in that little blue-doored house had texture. It had grain. It had the specific gravity of every terrible answer anyone had ever decided not to give me.

"Sef," I said. "Where is my sister?"

"She brought you back," Sef said at last. Each word cost her; I could see the cost the way you see effort in a climber's arms. "Whatever else is true — however this ends — she climbed down into the dark under the mountain, into the court itself, and she stole you back. You are standing here because Jonah Okafor did not come home without you."

"Then where is she *now*?"

And that was when the second thing happened that day, the thing that made the gods' pressing feel like a footnote.

Because I went looking. Not with any plan — the body moved on its own, the sediment guiding it, through the house, into the small back room that must have been mine, to the shelf where a boy keeps the things he can't throw away. A wooden bird, badly carved. A river stone with a hole through it. And a ledger — an old ledger, cloth-bound, swollen with damp, the kind merchants keep accounts in.

I opened it because my hands told me to.

Most of the pages were columns of figures, payments in coin and kind, dated at intervals of thirteen years, running back generations further than any family should run. But near the end, one page had been torn out. Ragged at the spine. And on the page after it, in handwriting I somehow knew was my mother's, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, as though the pen had been gripped by someone arguing with God:

*He asked to go. He chose it. God forgive me, he chose it so it wouldn't be her.*

I stood there holding the ledger while the light died in the window, and I understood two things with perfect, useless clarity.

First: I had done something enormous once. Some act so large it had bought a family's fortune and cost me everything that made me *me*, and I had done it *voluntarily*, and I could not remember why, and the why was the only thing in the world I wanted.

Second: whatever Jonah had traded to bring me back — whatever the court below had taken in exchange — she had paid it with herself. Because that's what the Okafor children did, apparently. We paid with ourselves. It was the family business.

The gods, that night, pressed against the gate so hard the ward-stones split clean through, and the bell in the village tower cracked down the middle, and every dog in Thornfell howled until dawn.

And I lay awake in my childhood bed, soulless and restored and home, listening to the mountains grind their teeth, understanding at last the shape of the debt I'd woken into:

My sister had gone down into the dark to buy me back.

And tomorrow was the twelfth turn.

And nobody, as far as I could tell, had gone down to buy her.
