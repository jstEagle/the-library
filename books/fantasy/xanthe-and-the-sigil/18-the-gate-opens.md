## 18. The Gate Opens

The twelfth night came up cold and clear, the kind of night that makes you feel like an exhibit in someone else's museum.

I stood on the shelf of rock above the Thornfell gate with Tam's hand in mine — my brother's hand, warm and callused and utterly unfamiliar, attached to a young man who looked at me like I was a stranger who'd done him a kindness. Which, as far as he knew, I was. The soul I'd carried up out of the mountain was his. The memories were not. Those had gone into the bargain, into the stone, into whatever ledger the court beneath kept its accounts in. He knew my name because Sef had told him. Everything else he had to take on faith.

"Tell me again what it looked like," he said. "Before."

"The gate?" I shrugged. "A door. Big, black, ugly. Basalt with iron bands, carvings nobody could read, wards that hadn't warmed in sixty years." I nodded at the thing in front of us now. "It looks like that. Only lit."

Because it was lit. Every groove in the ancient carvings glowed a low, sullen amber, like coals banked under ash, like something breathing slow and getting faster. The ward-stones that circled the clearing hummed a note just below hearing. I felt it in my teeth. In the sigil on my palm, which had not stopped burning since sundown, since the twelfth moon had hauled itself over the ridge fat and white and wrong.

"And you're sure we shouldn't be running," Tam said.

"Oh, absolutely not. We should have run days ago. But the village is down there—" I tipped my chin toward Thornfell, where every window burned yellow against the dark, where Sef had refused to evacuate anyone, because she said a shepherd doesn't abandon the flock to save the flock from being frightened— "and I spent twenty years keeping this door shut so those people could sleep badly and safely. I'm not going to stop on the last night out of what, prudence?"

"That's not prudence, that's spite."

"It's both. Spite keeps better than duty alone."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. It was the closest either of us had come to a real conversation in three days, and I hoarded it the way I used to hoard dry kindling — knowing how fast it burns, taking what warmth I could while it lasted.

The moon cleared the ridge. The humming stopped.

That's the detail I'll carry to whatever grave I get: it didn't build to anything. There was no crescendo, no cracking thunderclap. The ward-stones simply went silent all at once, the way a room goes silent when someone important walks in, and then the gate opened.

Not swung. *Opened* — the way an eye opens, the way a mouth opens when it means to say your name. The black basalt thinned into darkness deeper than black, and through it poured—

I want to say light. That's what my eyes insisted on reporting, light, glorious and gold, and for one whole heartbeat my body leaned toward it the way a frozen man leans toward a fire. Then my mind caught up with my skin and reminded me what I'd learned beneath the mountain: down there, everything beautiful was hungry, and everything hungry was beautiful.

The old god came through the gate like smoke pouring through a keyhole, except there was nothing small about it. It filled the clearing. It filled the sky above the clearing. It wore a shape because shapes are a courtesy gods extend to mortals, and the shape it chose was generous — tall as three men, robed in moving shadow shot through with that golden glow, a face like a statue of somebody's beloved grandfather, weathered and wise and kind around the eyes.

*Daughter of the builders,* it said, and its voice didn't come through the air. It came up through the rock, through my boots, through the bones of my feet, resonant as a struck bell. *Little keeper. You are owed.*

"I've heard about your debts," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I've read your ledger."

*Then you know I pay.* The grandfather-face inclined toward me, sorrowful, magnanimous. *Your family sealed me away and called it protection. Your mother sold her son rather than open this door. And still I offer you this: kneel, and rule.*

"Rule what?"

*Thornfell.* It spread one vast hand, and the valley unrolled beneath it in the godlight — the village, the fields, the high pastures silvered by moon, the whole world I'd watched from a distance for twenty years like a cat watching a birdcage. *They pray again. They prayed me awake, and their prayers are mine to answer. Answer them cruelly or answer them kindly — that choice can be yours. A god needs a hand in the world. A voice. An heir of the builders, with the sigil on her palm. Kneel, and no one in this valley ever goes hungry, sick, or afraid again. Kneel, and your brother lives long and loved and never learns what he cost.*

*And you,* it added, softer, and this was the knife slid between ribs while the hand patted your shoulder, *will never be invisible again.*

I should tell you I laughed at it. I should tell you I thought of Brannoc Vale holding that door behind us in the dark, taken because of me; of Sef down in the village praying herself hollow; of the hollow choir singing away people's names until they forgot they'd ever been anybody. I should tell you I was made of better stuff than that.

Here's the truth instead: for eleven full seconds — I counted later, lying awake, because counting was the only way I could stand to look at it — I wanted to kneel.

Eleven seconds is not much. Eleven seconds is also a very long time. Long enough to see it all laid out like goods at market: Mother alive again, because a god could do that, couldn't it, surely, if I asked nicely. Tam whole, not just restored but *remembered*, our childhood given back to him like a returned library book. Brannoc bought back out of the court's hands, his daughter too, debts cancelled across the board. The smugglers' roads safe. The winters mild. Thornfell fed and sung and grateful, and me at the center of it, finally, *finally* seen — not the Okafor girl nobody remembers, not the keeper of nothing, but the woman who saved them all, whose name would be in the prayers themselves.

Power is a very honest salesman. That's what nobody tells you. It doesn't lie to you. It just shows you the catalogue of everything you've ever wanted and lets you price it yourself, and the price always turns out to be kneeling, and kneeling always feels, right up until the knee touches stone, like standing up for once in your life.

"Jonah." Tam's voice, small beside all that gold. "Jonah, look at me."

I looked at him. My brother. Who didn't remember me. Whose face I'd have traded anything to make light up with recognition —

And there it was. The hook under the bait, plain as a fisherman's thumb. *Never learns what he cost.* The god would keep Tam ignorant. Would keep him smiling and hollow-eyed and wrong, the way I'd seen him in the court below, a beautifully preserved boy in a glass jar. That wasn't my brother healed. That was my brother taxidermied.

And the rest of it — the valley fed, the miracles flowing — I'd seen the gods' idea of answered prayer already, in these twelve turning nights. The dying shepherd whose prayer had started all this had been answered with a miracle, yes. So had the widow who prayed for rain and got a flood. Hungry things answer generously. They just don't answer *well*. Rule Thornfell? I'd be less a ruler than a glove, and the god would wear me, and everyone I loved would live long, comfortable lives inside its fist.

"Here's my problem," I said aloud, and my knees were shaking, and I locked them, because if I knelt even to steady myself I did not trust the difference. "You keep saying kneel like it's a small thing. Like it's manners. But I've been under the mountain, and I know what kneeling means in your court. It means ownership. It means my name goes in your ledger next to a line item. And I've read enough of that ledger to know how the payments work — thirteen years, thirteen years, and the interest is always somebody's soul."

The grandfather-face didn't change. That was somehow worse than anger. Anger would have meant I'd scored a point.

*Everything is owned by something, little keeper,* it said, gently, reasonably, the way the Regent down below had spoken, the way every beautiful hungry thing speaks. *You were owned by your duty before you could walk. Owned by your mother's silence. Owned by the gate. At least my service comes with a crown.*

"See, that's the sales pitch," I said. "But here's what I noticed about your covenant, reading it by dead light in the vault antechamber while your bone-bells were screaming. It says the gate shuts on a willing soul. *Willing.* Not a purchased one. Not a collateral one. Not a taxidermied boy smiling in a jar. My mother tried buying her way around it and it cost her Tam anyway. The seal's been thinning for sixty years precisely because a bought soul isn't the same as a given one. You've been running a counterfeit coin for two generations, and tonight the note's come due."

For the first time, the godlight flickered.

Small. A stutter in the gold, like a candle in a draft. But I saw it, and Tam saw it, and far below us in the village I'd have sworn every lamp guttered at once, because Sef told me afterward that they did.

*I offered you a kingdom,* the god said, and the warmth went out of the voice, and what was underneath was older than the mountains and colder than the space between stars, and I understood suddenly why my ancestors had built a door and thrown their whole lives at holding it shut.

"You offered me a leash," I said. "I've been owned my whole life. I'm not applying for the position again."

The moon touched the top of the sky. And the old god stopped pretending to be a kindly grandfather and became what it actually was, which was the thing the dark was made of, and the clearing went gold and terrible and vast, and it reached for me with a hand that had swallowed prayers whole for a thousand years.

Tam grabbed my arm and pulled, and I went — running backward across the shelf of rock, dragging my brother who didn't know me toward the treeline, while behind us the gate stood wide open and the night poured through it like water through a broken dam.

And in the middle of all that terror, with the sigil searing a hole through my palm and the god's attention settling on my back like a falling star, one thought arrived, calm as a clerk checking figures:

*A willing soul shuts the gate.*

Not a bought one. Not a stolen one. Not Tam's, sold by a desperate mother. Not Brannoc's daughter, traded for passage. The covenant's own words, the flaw in the ledger, the thing the court had spent generations making sure no heir ever learned:

The gate opens for debt. It seals for gift.

I stopped running.

Tam stumbled, turned, stared at me. "What are you doing?"

Behind me the god gathered itself, patient now, certain — it had watched my family for sixty years, it knew how keepers broke, it was content to wait out my fear the way it had waited out everything.

It didn't know yet that it had just shown me the only bargain it couldn't refuse.

"Tam," I said, and turned back to face the light. "I need you to remember this part. Whatever happens after — I need somebody to remember."
