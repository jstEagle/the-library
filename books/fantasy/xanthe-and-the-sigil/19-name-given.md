## 19. Name Given

Here is the thing nobody tells you about the end of the world: it is very polite about announcing itself.

The god came through the gate like a guest who has been waiting on the doorstep for thirteen years and intends to mention it. The doors—my doors, my family's doors, the ones I had oiled and warded and talked to on slow evenings because there was no one else—hung open against the mountain's throat, and what poured through was not fire or darkness or any of the shapes I'd braced for. It was attention. Vast, patient, delighted attention, the kind that makes your skin crawl because you realize you have been watched far longer than you have been aware.

It offered me a crown.

Of course it did. That's always the offer. Nobody ever comes to the gatekeeper with a bill of sale; they come with a coronation.

"You could mend everything," it said, in a voice made of every prayer ever whispered at my gate and then forgotten by the people who whispered them. "The village that shunned you. The brother they took. The name they let rot off their tongues. Kneel, daughter of the builders, and Thornfell will love you so thoroughly it will choke on it."

And here is the embarrassing part, the part I'll only admit because I'm writing this in my own head where no one can hear: I wanted it.

Not the crown. Not even the love. I wanted the *mending*. I wanted a version of the world where Tam came home whole, where Brannoc hadn't been dragged backward into the dark holding a door shut with his bare hands and his bad jokes, where Sef's congregation wasn't up on the hillside right now feeding their prayers to something hungry because they didn't know any better. I wanted to fix it, and power is just fixing things at scale, isn't it? That's the seduction. It doesn't offer you evil. It offers you competence.

I stood in the doorway of my gate with the old god's attention pooled around my ankles like floodwater, and I let myself want it for three full breaths. Three. Then I did what Okafor women do best.

I got practical.

* * *

Let me back up, because the wanting wasn't the revelation. The revelation had come an hour earlier, when the god's first push cracked the ward-stones and I felt the seal through my palm—the sigil, burning like a coal stitched into my skin—and understood, all at once and with terrible clarity, how it actually worked.

I'd spent my whole life thinking of the gate as a lock and my family as locksmiths. Wards, stones, iron hinges, the careful maintenance of things. But locks keep out anything with the strength to break them, and this gate hadn't kept anything out for thirteen generations. What it had done was *trade*. Every thirteen years, a soul went down. In exchange, the court beneath stayed content, and contentment is a better seal than iron ever was. My mother's ledger wasn't a payment schedule. It was a menu.

The covenant ran on willingness. That was carved into the original stone down in the vault antechamber, in letters older than the village: *a soul freely given binds; a soul taken only feeds.* The court couldn't be locked away because locking was never the mechanism. It was fed into staying, the way you might feed a dog to keep it from hunting—and the thirteenth turn was coming whether anyone volunteered or not, and if no one volunteered, it would simply take, and taking would wake it fully, and waking it fully would open the gate from below regardless of anything I did with wards or stubbornness.

Which meant the gate could only be resealed by a willing soul. Mine, specifically, or close enough—the sigil marked me as the heir, the last coin in the family purse.

Tam was already stolen. Brannoc was already taken. And I want to be clear that neither of those facts made this feel noble. They made it feel like arithmetic. I was the last piece on the board, and the board required one piece, and the alternative was watching Thornfell learn what hunger meant.

So: three breaths of wanting the god's easy answer. Then the practical part, which was the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the choir, harder than the vault, harder than leaving Brannoc at that door.

I refused the crown.

"You misunderstand the position," the god said, and its delight curdled into something older. "You are not being offered a choice."

"No," I agreed. "But I'm going to make one anyway. It's kind of a family tradition."

* * *

The trick—the beautiful, stupid, only-possible trick—was that the gate opened downward too.

Everyone thinks of the Thornfell gate as facing outward, toward the world above, the village, the sky. But the court beneath had built its roads up to meet it, and the covenant ran both directions along the same stone. A willing soul given to the court reseals the gate from *beneath*. Not locked. Fed. Contented. Sleeping, the way it had slept for thirteen turns at a time since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to check ward-stones without understanding why.

The Regent would understand immediately. The Regent had offered me a trade two nights ago—my sigil and inheritance for Tam's freedom—and I'd refused because I'd sensed the trap: hand over the sigil, and the gate loses its last anchor. But the Regent hadn't wanted the sigil to open the gate. That was the horror of it, the elegant horror. The Regent had wanted the sigil because the sigil is what keeps the gate shut, and the court beneath was tired. Hungry nobles with hollow eyes don't actually want the world above. They want the *debt paid*, cleanly, completely, and then they want to sleep for another thirteen turns without some shepherd's desperate prayer scratching at the ceiling of their sleep.

They wanted a keeper. I had been one my whole life. I was, in a sense, overqualified.

So I turned my back on the god—that took more courage than anything, turning your back on something that size—and I pressed my sigil-palm flat against the inner face of the gate, against the carvings my ancestors had cut when the mountain was younger, and I spoke my name.

My true name. Jonah Okafor. Daughter of Maren Okafor, sister of Tam, keeper of nothing, keeper of everything. I spoke it into the stone the way you'd speak it into a wound, and the sigil drank it, and I felt it go—not lost, not yet, but *lodged*, set into the pattern like a keystone set into an arch. The carvings lit. The whole door became a diagram of my own hand.

And the court heard me.

It heard me the way the deep water hears a stone.

"Regent," I said, to the dark of the descending stair that had opened in the gate's foundation like a mouth remembering its shape. "We should talk terms."

* * *

Terms. As if I had leverage. As if anything about this was negotiation rather than surrender wearing negotiation's coat. But I had learned something in the courts below, something the smugglers' roads taught and the choir nearly untaught and Brannoc finally made plain the night before he held that door:

Names are currency, and whoever spends them decides what they buy.

The Regent met me at the boundary of the court, where the bone lanterns guttered blue and the hollow-eyed nobles drew back like tide from a shore. Up close, the Regent's beauty was worse than the others'—the others were hollow, but the Regent was *worn*, polished smooth by centuries of being looked at, features rubbed soft as sea glass. It studied my hand, my burning sigil, and I saw the hunger in it sharpen into something almost like respect.

"The heir," it said. "Come to pay the family debt after all."

"I've come to renegotiate." My voice held. I was genuinely surprised. "One soul, freely given, willingly spoken. Me. In exchange: the gate seals for another thirteen turns, and the next thirteen after that, as long as my blood holds the sigil. And in exchange—" and here was the clause I had walked back up out of hell to demand— "you release what remains of the debts already collected. Tam Okafor's soul goes free and stays free. Brannoc Vale's daughter. Every name in my mother's ledger. Paid out. Returned."

The Regent smiled, and it was the smile of a creditor hearing an interesting proposal from someone with no assets. "You offer yourself and ask for a hundred souls."

"One," I said. "Tam. The rest can wait their thirteen turns—I'm not unreasonable. But him, now, tonight, before the moon turns. Because here's what you may not have noticed, Regent: the god upstairs is awake, and it's offering me crowns, and crowns mean it's *worried*. Worried things make bad neighbors. You want this gate sealed and sleeping more than I want my brother. The difference between us is that I'm the only seller on the market."

Silence, in the way that only courts can produce silence—a silence with weight, with listening in it, a hundred hollow-eyed aristocrats pretending they weren't hanging on every word.

"You understand," the Regent said at last, gently, the way you'd speak to someone signing away a house, "what is asked. Not death. Death would be simple. You will be keeper beneath. Bound to the stone. Named by us, remade by us, serving until your blood runs out of heirs or the mountain itself dies. You will not see the sun again."

"I haven't seen much of it anyway," I said. "Invisible keeper of a sealed gate. Honestly, the commute improves."

Something moved behind the Regent's worn face that might, in a creature capable of it, have been amusement.

"And your name," it said. "You know we take names here. You've seen the choir."

"I've sung with them, thank you."

"Then know this. We will not call you Jonah. Jonah Okafor ends tonight, at the threshold, freely given like everything else. What wakes beneath will wear a new name. Our name."

I thought of the choir, of the unnamed reaching for me with voices like unraveling cloth, of how I'd clung to memories of Tam—him teaching me to whittle, him lying about eating the last of the honey, him volunteering, God help him, *volunteering* to be sold because he'd found out I was the true heir and he was eleven years old and loved me more than he feared anything. I had gone into that choir certain that my name was the last thing I owned.

But I'd learned something else since. Down in the vault, reading my mother's ledger, finding Tam's page torn out not in erasure but in mercy. Names aren't what other people call you. Names are what you choose to answer to. My mother named me Jonah, yes. But I had named myself keeper. I had named myself the one who checks the wards, who trades with smugglers, who walks into hell for her brother. Every choice I'd made had been a syllable in a name no one had pronounced yet.

So let the court give me whatever name it liked. I'd author the rest myself.

"Done," I said.

* * *

The giving itself was almost gentle, which felt like cheating.

I had expected pain. What I got was ceremony. The court gathered at the threshold-stone, and the Regent took my sigil-hand in both of its sea-glass ones, and the nobles began to hum—the same melody as the choir, but reversed, a song that *built* instead of unraveled. My true name rose in my throat. I felt it gathering, all of it, every version of me: the child checking ward-stones on her toes, the girl trading salt for gossip she pretended not to want, the woman who found the ledger and read her own life as a line item, the sister who climbed out of the world's basement carrying her brother's soul-flame cupped like a bird.

I spoke it. All of it. Into the sigil, into the stone, into the listening dark.

*Jonah Okafor.*

And the mountain took it the way dry ground takes rain.

The gate above boomed shut—I felt it through the soles of my feet, felt the ward-stones settle, felt the god's attention recoil from the threshold like a hand pulled back from a stove. Somewhere far overhead, I knew, the pressure on the seal eased. Somewhere far overhead, Sef's frightened congregation would find their cruel little miracles going quiet. Somewhere far overhead—

Somewhere far overhead, Tam was standing at the gate with a stranger's memories in his head and my soul-flame's echo in his chest, and he wouldn't remember me, and I had known that going in, had signed for it, had decided that a brother alive and whole and ignorant was worth more than a brother mourned.

The sigil cooled in my palm. For the first time in my life, it didn't burn. It just rested there, quiet, a mark instead of a wound.

"It is done," the Regent said, and released my hand. "Keeper."

"That's not the name you're giving me."

"It is the name I am *using*," the Regent said, "until the court convenes. Come. There are forms. There are always forms." And behind it, the hollow-eyed nobles parted, and beyond them the deep roads of the court unrolled into the dark, and somewhere down those roads a cell was emptying—Tam walking out, blinking, alive, free, not knowing why he felt like he'd forgotten something important, not knowing that forgetting was the price and I was the one who'd paid it.

Go home, Tam, I thought, with everything in me that was still mine. Go home and be infuriated by the honey thief. Learn to whittle again. Live loudly enough for both of us.

The Regent waited. The dark of the court opened like a book to its middle pages. And far above, muffled by a hundred yards of living stone, I heard—or dreamed I heard—a sound I would spend every night below memorizing, so that someday, if a door ever opened, I'd know the footsteps coming down.

Someone was knocking on my gate.
