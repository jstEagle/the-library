## 20. Keeper Below

The last thing my sister said to me was not goodbye. It was *don't follow me*, which is the same thing, said by someone who knows you well enough to know you'll want to.

Then the stone closed over her like water, and the mountain swallowed the light of her — of Xanthe's — sigil, and I stood alone on a ledge above Thornfell gate holding a satchel that did not belong to me and a lifetime of memories that didn't either.

I should explain. Memory is a funny thing when gods are involved. It folds. It trades hands. When Jonah spoke her name into the sigil and offered herself to the court beneath, the bargain required witnesses, and the only witness it would accept was blood. So the mountain took her memories of the choosing — every step of the descent, every toll, every door Brannoc held — and poured them into me, the way you'd pour wine from one cup to another so the first cup could be smashed on an altar.

Which means I now remember things I never lived.

I remember being afraid in the dark under the world and being brave about it anyway. I remember the river spirit with too many mouths asking for names, and the lie my sister told it, and the blood she paid instead. I remember Brannoc Vale laughing at something she said in the long dark, a laugh like a rockslide starting somewhere far off, and I remember understanding — without ever having met him until three days ago — exactly what that laugh cost him. I remember the choir. Gods, I remember the choir. A hundred throats singing a hundred half-songs, all of them reaching for the shape of *Jonah Okafor* to pull it apart like bread, and her gripping the memory of a boy who used to steal honeycakes off the windowsill and blame the cat, and holding.

That memory is mine now. The boy in it is also me. It's very confusing being your own sister's most treasured possession.

* * *

Let me back up, because wry as I'm trying to be about this, there's a version of this account where I simply scream for a while, and nobody wants to read that. Least of all me. I've done plenty of it in the weeks since.

Here is what happened, in order, as best I can make it sit still on the page.

On the twelfth night the gate broke. Not gently — nothing about Thornfell has ever been gentle, except possibly my sister, and see what that got her. The old god came through the seam like smoke through a keyhole, vast and patient and terribly reasonable, and it offered Jonah everything: Thornfell green forever, the village kneeling in gratitude, Tam whole and happy at her side. Power to fix everything she'd ever watched break.

I know this because I remember it. Her memory of it sits behind my eyes like a coal. She wanted to kneel. That's the part nobody will believe later, so I'm writing it down: my sister, the stubborn mule of the high pastures, the girl who once argued with Mother for two years straight about whether keeping us from the village counted as protection or imprisonment — she *wanted* to say yes. She felt the offer settle into her like warmth into cold hands, and for one long breath she loved it.

And then she thought of me standing there, soulless and hollowed-out, smiling at her like the court had taught me to smile — and she understood that everything the god offered her was a story about other people. About owning them. And Jonah Okafor, who had spent her whole life invisible, chose at the last possible moment to become visible in the only direction no one was watching: down.

She said no. Then she said her name.

Her true name. Which I cannot write here, because it isn't mine to spend anymore — she gave it to the court, and the court keeps its accounts clean; ask any smuggler. What I can tell you is what it sounded like. It sounded like a door opening in a house I'd lived in all my life and never known had rooms. It sounded like our mother calling us in from snow. It sounded like *come home*, and it was addressed to the dark.

The god screamed. I don't mean roared — screaming is what betrayed things do, and it had been betrayed, in the small technical sense that it had made an honest offer and been refused by someone with the authority to refuse it. The sigil on the gate flared white. The stones I'd spent my first soulless days staring at began to slide, grinding like millwheels, closing the seam the god had opened, and Jonah turned around inside the narrowing gap of light and looked at me.

*Don't follow me.*

The stone met. The mountain sighed. Snow fell off the lintel in a soft white sheet, dusting my shoulders like a benediction or a burial, and the gate was whole again, and my sister was on the wrong side of it.

* * *

The first thing I did was sit down. I'm not proud of it. There's a heroic version where I immediately begin planning, but the truth is my legs went out from under me and I sat in the snow at the foot of a sealed god-gate and laughed until the laughing turned into the other thing, and I did both for a long time, because I could. Because feeling things — grief especially — was new, and I hadn't yet learned to ration it.

You need to understand what I was before that night. Three days earlier I'd woken at the gate's foot with no more self than a coat someone left behind. The court had worn my body for eleven years the way you'd wear a good jacket — kept it presentable, smiled with it, sent it to market. The soul they'd held was intact, because souls are currency and you don't clip coin, but it had been shelved, and shelving does things. When Sef and the rite gave it back to me, it came with all of Tam Okafor in it: childhood, mother, the sale, the dark. Everything except the years themselves, which belonged to the thing that had been wearing me.

So I came back to myself a stranger in my own history, and the only person in the world who knew both versions of me — the boy sold and the hollow man — was Jonah. And she'd just traded herself away to make sure I got to keep being either.

There's a joke in there. I keep looking for it. I'll let you know when I find it.

* * *

Sef found me at dawn. She climbed the shepherd's track alone, old woman, bad hip, staff in hand, and she looked at the sealed gate for a long moment and then at me, and whatever she saw in my face she didn't waste pity on it. Sef has never wasted anything in her life, including sermons.

"The debt's paid," she said. "Both ends of it."

"Was it worth it?"

"No transaction involving souls is worth it. But some are *settled*." She lowered herself onto the stone beside me, which cost her visibly, and stared at the carvings. "The gate will hold now. A willing seal outranks a bought one. Every covenant-maker beneath the mountain knows it, and hates it."

"She knew that. That's why she did it." My voice came out flat. "She found the original ledger. She learned Mother didn't sell me. I volunteered. To spare her." I'd had three days of being Tam again by then, but that fact was still arriving in installments. "Eleven years I thought I was sold like a goat at market. Turns out I was the sacrifice and she was the heir, and neither of us got asked."

"Mothers rarely ask," Sef said. "They decide. It's their worst quality and the reason anyone survives childhood." She tapped her staff against the gate, twice, like knocking. "The village will want to see you, Tam Okafor. Daughter of the builders' line. They'll bring soup. They'll bring questions. Some of them will kneel, and you'll hate it."

"I'm not staying in the village."

"No," she agreed, and there was no surprise in it at all. "I didn't think you would."

* * *

Here is the part where I confess the strange thing, the thing I have not said aloud to anyone, because saying it might unmake it.

When the bargain poured Jonah's memories into me, it didn't give me her recollections as knowledge. It gave them as *weight*. I carry her. When I walk the ward-line at dusk and lay my palm on each ward-stone, I feel her doing it for thirty years — the specific boredom of it, the comfort, the way she made a game of checking the eastern stones first because they were warmest and called it cheating the cold. When I eat, I taste her meals. When the wind comes off the mountain at night I am, for a heartbeat, a girl of nineteen listening to it and pretending it was company.

The court thinks it took a payment and a keeper. It doesn't understand what it actually took: my sister distributed across two locations, most of her buried in the stone and some of her sloshing around in her idiot brother. If grief is love with nowhere to go, then I am the most crowded man in Thornfell.

I became the keeper because there was no one else and because the role demanded it and because — I'll be honest, since I'm apparently being honest in this document — because keeping the gate meant keeping watch over *her*. The keeper's cottage was still there. Her kettle was still on the hook. Her ledger was still under the floorboard, the payments in it ended mid-line thirteen years ago, one page torn out. I sleep in her bed. I mend her fences. I check the wards in her order, east first, cheating the cold.

The villagers leave offerings now. Word spread — Sef saw to that, framing it from the pulpit as prophecy fulfilled rather than family catastrophe, which is the priestly art entire. They leave bread and wool and, once, a truly alarming quantity of honeycakes, which nearly broke me in front of Widow Marsh, who meant well. They call me Keeper Tam. Half of them still can't meet my eyes; the other half can't stop staring. I've decided both reactions are fair.

* * *

Winter deepened. I learned the work.

It turns out keeping a sealed gate is mostly plumbing. The seal breathes — slow, tidal, drawing on the mountain's deep heat — and if you don't clear the runoff channels the meltwater backs up against the ward-stones and they sulk. Nobody tells you gods sulking look like frost-heave. I fixed channels, fed the ward-stones oil and song (they prefer mine badly sung; Jonah's memory informs me they always preferred it badly sung, it gives them something to feel superior about), and walked the perimeter at moonrise to read the sigil's pulse.

Steady. Strong. Willing.

Some nights I talked to it. I know how that sounds. I told it about the village — the births, the quarrels, Marsh's second cow finally calving. I told it the things I'd learned about our mother from Sef, the parts that made her a person instead of a verdict. Once, in the worst week, I told it I was angry at her, and the eastern ward-stone went noticeably colder for three days, and I apologized, and we moved on. You develop relationships. It's a small parish, a gate.

But every night, after the wards, I did the real work. The secret work. The work that is going to get me into trouble, which is why chapter twenty-one exists, presumably, though I'm not supposed to know about chapters.

Because here's what I noticed, early, in the boring administrative depths of keeperhood: the sigil echoes. When I press my marked palm — yes, I bear it too now; the inheritance skipped nothing, it just waited — when I press my palm to the gate, there is an answering pulse. Faint. Deep. Below. Like a heart heard through a wall.

A willing seal, Sef said, outranks a bought one. And a name, Brannoc's stolen papers taught me before the court took him — a name spoken through a willing seal does not open the gate. It travels. Names are the one currency that crosses every border, even the court's. Even stone.

My sister went down carrying everything, and the bargain stripped her of the one thing that mattered: her name. Jonah Okafor, keeper below, unnamed among the unnamed, and the thought of her down there in that beautiful hollow court, faceless, is the thing I do not let myself think at three in the morning when the wind is right and the mountain hums.

But names can be called home. That's not hope talking. That's accounting. It's in Vale's papers, in his cramped desperate hand, margins full of sums and one sentence underlined twice: *a name called through a willing seal must be answered.*

Must. Be. Answered.

So every night, after the wards, I climb to the gate, and I press my palm to the stone, and I practice. Quietly. Carefully. Because the sigil is a bell, and the mountain is the bell-tower, and somewhere under all that weight my sister is waiting for someone to ring it — and I am done being the brother who got saved. I intend to be the one who does the rescuing this time, at however absurd a distance, through however much rock, for however long it takes.

Last night the echo came back faster than it ever has. Last night it came back shaped almost like a word.

I start properly at moonrise tomorrow. I'm going to call my sister home.
