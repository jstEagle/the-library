## 21. The Thirteenth Bell

The bell above the gate cracked in the first frost, and I took it as a personal insult.

Not because a cracked bell is a tragedy — it rang like a spoon dropped down a well even before the damage — but because it was one more thing on the list of things that were now my fault. The list had grown long over the months. The ward-stones that needed re-chalking every ninth day. The smugglers who still came up the old road expecting Jonah Okafor and found instead her brother, standing where she used to stand, wearing her coat because it fit better than mine and smelling faintly of pine resin and stubbornness.

"Gatekeeper," they'd say, tipping their hats, and I'd say "that's me," and both of us would be lying.

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying someone else's memories: they don't sit politely in your head like books on a shelf. They *leak*. I'd be mending a fence and suddenly know how it felt to be nine years old and hiding under the stairs while Mother counted coins she didn't have. I'd be eating porridge and taste it twice — once with my mouth, once with hers, from a morning thirty years gone when she'd burned it and eaten it anyway so Tam wouldn't feel bad about complaining.

I complained anyway. She remembered being annoyed about it for forty years. That's love, I suppose: a grudge you keep warm.

I had her memories of everything except the parts that mattered most. The bargain had given me her past but not her presence. I knew every route she'd taken beneath the mountain, every word she'd traded with spirits, every step of the road down to the court and back out again without me — and I knew none of what happened after the gate sealed with her on the wrong side of it. That part of her ledger was blank. The court kept its own accounts.

So. The bell.

I'd been keeper for four moons when it cracked, and by then I'd learned three things about keeping a gate shut against hungry gods.

First: the gods were still there. Quieter than they'd been during the twelfth turn, when prayers came back answered and cruel, but there — a pressure against the seal like a thumb testing ripe fruit. Sef said they were digesting. She said it the way priests say terrible things, gently, so you can pretend they didn't.

Second: the village had started leaving offerings at the gate again. Bread, mostly. Once, inexplicably, a boot. The gods weren't answering anymore, but faith is a habit like any other, and habits outlive their reasons. I collected the bread and left it for crows. The boot I kept. You never know.

Third: my sister was alive.

I knew this the way you know your own heartbeat — not as information but as fact. When I pressed my marked palm flat against the gate's cold stone, deep under all the silence, there was a second rhythm. Slower than mine. Patient. The way a sleeper breathes.

She'd given me her memories in the bargain — that was the shape of it, though neither of us had spelled it out. *Take these so you'll know what I knew, so you can keep what I kept.* And in exchange she'd taken my place below, spoken her true name into the sigil, and become whatever the court makes of the souls it keeps.

Jonah Okafor had spent her whole life making herself invisible so that I could stay safe and ignorant. Then she'd spent a moon descending into hell to unmake that bargain, and when hell wouldn't unmake itself, she'd written herself into it instead.

My sister, ladies and gentlemen. Who once bit a tax collector. Twice.

The point is, I owed her a rescue, and I am, whatever else might be said of me, a man who pays his debts. Mostly. There was still the matter of the Hendersons' fence, but that predates the soul-trafficking and I maintain the posts were already rotten.

* * *

Brannoc Vale's knowledge came to me in a box.

That sounds glib. It wasn't. It arrived in the spring thaw, carried up the smuggler's road by a woman named Corla Hesk who ran furs down toward the lowlands and had known Vale for twenty years and cried the entire last mile of it, which she would deny to this day.

"He told me if anything ever happened to him," she said, "and he made me say *if*, not *when*, the bastard — I was to bring his papers to whoever ended up keeping the gate." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "He didn't say it'd be his own ghost's brother."

The box held everything Brannoc had gathered across thirty years of guiding seekers down into the dark and losing them. Maps drawn in charcoal on hide, annotated in a hand that got shakier toward the bottom of the pile. Lists of tolls: which river-spirits wanted names, which wanted blood, which just wanted to hear someone cry. A treatise, half-finished, titled *On the Court's Accounting* — pages and pages of close reasoning about how the beneath-world tracked debts, how names functioned as currency, how a soul could be *earmarked* rather than spent.

And tucked inside the treatise, a single loose page in different ink:

*To the one who reads this — if you're reading it, I'm either dead or worse, and either way I finally get to stop pretending I'm doing this for myself. Listen. The vault keeps what it takes, but the mountain remembers what it's given. The sigil isn't just a key. It's an echo. Every heir who bore it left a resonance in the stone, and resonance travels both ways. You can't shout a name down into the dark. But you can ring a bell that only one person ever heard.*

I read that page maybe two hundred times. I want that on the record. Two hundred times, by firelight, with my palm pressed to stone, feeling that slow patient breathing underneath.

Because here was the problem, and I'll be honest about it, since honesty is the one luxury I have left: I didn't know if it would work. Worse, I didn't fully understand it. Brannoc had spent decades learning the court's rules from the outside, and he wrote about them the way a man writes about a country he fled — precisely, obsessively, and with gaps where the trauma lived. His chapter on resonance was four pages long. Two of them were diagrams. One was an apology to his daughter, whose name I never learned, whose soul-flame was presumably still burning in a vault beneath my feet.

The fourth page said: *A bell cast from the gate-stone itself, struck thirteen times, at the hour of the waning moon, with the sigil's blood on the striker. Thirteen for the covenant. Waning for descent. Blood for the toll, because the mountain respects nothing free.*

"And then what?" I asked him, out loud, alone, in the way you do. "What happens after thirteen?"

The paper did not answer. Papers never do. That's why I became a farmer instead of a philosopher.

But I had something Brannoc hadn't. I had Jonah's memories, and among them — buried in the ordinary silt of a childhood, between learning to whistle and falling off the mill roof — I had this: a night when we were small, before Mother died, before everything, when Jonah had shown me the mark on her palm and said, very seriously, seven years old and grave as a magistrate, *"It hums sometimes. When I put my ear to the ground it hums back. Like it knows me."*

Like it knows her.

You can't shout a name down into the dark. But the mountain had known her voice since before she could walk.

* * *

Casting a bell is not the sort of thing you do quietly, which was a problem, because the other thing I'd learned as keeper is that the things beneath the mountain notice loud things.

Sef helped. She'd come around more often that spring, her faith visibly reassembling itself into something harder and less ornamental than it had been — less incense, more load-bearing beams. She didn't pray at the gate anymore. She stood beside it like a soldier at a post.

"You want to melt down a piece of the warding-stone," she said, when I told her. "The stone that has held back the old gods for thirteen generations."

"A corner piece. A chipped one. There's a chip off the third stone, I've had my eye on it for weeks—"

"You've had your eye on chipping it, you mean."

"I resent the accusation. The chip pre-existed my interest."

She gave me the look she gave the boot-leaveers. But she brought her acolytes the next week anyway, and together — with much prayer, some argument, and one genuinely alarming moment involving molten bronze and my left boot — we cast a small bell, no bigger than a fist, from gate-stone scrap and river copper.

While it cooled, I cut my palm.

The sigil rose in the wound like it had been waiting — that intricate knotwork of lines that matched the gate's carvings, that had glowed on my sister's hand and now, by whatever inheritance or accident, glowed on mine. It didn't hurt anymore. It had stopped hurting around the time I stopped being afraid of it, which I suspect is the same lesson in two costumes.

I pressed my bleeding palm to the striker-stone. Then, because it seemed right, I spoke to the cooling metal.

"Her name is Jonah Okafor," I said. "Daughter of Maren Okafor, keeper of Thornfell gate. Sister of Tam, who is an idiot, and who is sorry, and who is coming to get her whether the mountain likes it or not."

Sef, behind me, murmured: "You shouldn't speak her name aloud near the seal."

"I'm not speaking it *near* the seal. I'm putting it *in* the bell. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"There is now."

* * *

We waited for the waning moon. Not the thirteenth turn — that was seasons away, and I had no intention of giving the court the courtesy of arriving on schedule. Just a waning night, thin and cold, when the light above was weakest and, if Brannoc's logic held, the veil between here and below wore thinnest.

I sent everyone away. This was not heroism; it was embarrassment management. What I was about to do was going to look, from the outside, like a grown man ringing a tiny bell at a rock.

I knelt at the gate. The crack in the old bell hung overhead, silent. The new bell sat in my hands, warm from my pocket, absurdly small, its bronze still holding the smell of the forge.

"Right," I told the mountain. "I know you're listening. You've always been listening. You listened to her hum when she was seven."

I set the bell against the stone. Pressed my marked palm flat beside it, so the sigil touched the gate.

And I struck it. Once.

The sound was wrong for its size — deep, rolling, the sound a bell ten times larger should make — and it didn't fade the way sound fades. It went *into*. I felt it pass through my hand, through the stone, down and down, a ripple following the grain of the mountain like water finding a channel.

Twice. Three times. On the fifth strike the ward-stones began to warm, and somewhere far below, something shifted its weight.

Seven. Eight. My hand was steady. I want that noted too, because inside I was eleven years old again, standing at a funeral, watching them lower a box that did not contain my sister's body because there wasn't one, and knowing — *knowing*, the way I knew nothing before and everything now — that I had been the reason she'd stayed invisible all those years. She'd hidden herself so thoroughly that when she was finally taken, half the village couldn't quite remember her face.

Nine. Ten.

The humming started.

Low, at first. Under the bell-tone. Under the stone. A vibration in my knees, my teeth, the wet place behind my eyes. Jonah's memory surfaced whole: *it hums back, like it knows me.* Except it wasn't the mountain humming anymore. The mountain was just the wire. The humming was coming from further down than any map of Brannoc's reached.

Eleven. Twelve.

I paused, fist raised, heart going like a smith's hammer.

Because here is the thing they don't put in ballads: at the threshold of the thing you've wanted more than anything, doubt arrives dressed as wisdom. *What if it's not her?* it said. *What if it's the god, wearing her voice, the way the court wears beautiful faces? What if you ring the thirteenth strike and call up something hungry, and this time there's no one left to seal the gate, because the heir with the sigil is you, and you are standing on the wrong side of it, holding the key?*

"What if it's her," I said out loud, to the doubt, to the mountain, to the memory of a woman who bit a tax collector for me and sold her name for me and walked into the dark singing my lullaby backwards so I'd hear it in my sleep. "Then I owe her a knock. And if it's not her—" I set my jaw. "Then it can learn to fear the name Jonah Okafor, because I am about to make it a very famous one."

Thirteen.

The bell did not ring.

It *answered*.

The note came up through the stone from below — the same pitch, the same depth, but not an echo. An echo repeats. This *responded*, a beat after my strike, shaped like it, but carrying something extra in the harmonics, something that made my sigil flare white-hot against the gate and made the crack in the old bell overhead sing one clear, unbroken tone for the first time since the frost.

And threaded through the note, so faint I might have invented it, so distinct I will swear it on anything you put in front of me:

Humming.

Off-key. The lullaby. Forward, not backward — because she wasn't lulling anyone to sleep down there. She was waking up.

The vibration climbed through my palm, my arm, my ribs, and settled behind my sternum like a second heart, slower than mine, patient, familiar. And then, riding the last of the fading bronze, so quiet that only a brother who'd carried her memories for a year could have caught it, a voice — not words yet, not quite, but the *shape* of words, pressing upward through fathoms of stone like a green shoot through winter ground:

*Tam.*

Just that. My name. Which she'd paid for, in a sense — her memories of me were part of the coin — and which she now spent back to me, freely, up through the dark.

I sat down hard on the cold stone and laughed until it turned into the other thing, and the old cracked bell rang softly overhead the whole while, like it was embarrassed for me, or maybe applauding.

When I could breathe again, I picked up the little bell and held it against the gate, and I felt the answer still trembling in it, faint but persistent. A signal. A thread. A door cracked open by exactly the width of a name.

Below my feet, slow and deliberate, the mountain hummed again. Closer, this time. Or louder. Or — and my skin prickled with a hope so fierce it felt indistinguishable from terror —

*Climbing.*

The ward-stones under my knees began, very gently, to glow.
