## 22. Xanthe Rising

There is a particular cruelty in how dawn looks from underneath.

You spend months in the dark and you forget that light isn't one thing. It's a thousand small decisions the sky makes, and it makes them slowly, grudgingly, like a miser counting out coins. From below, all you get is the rumor of it — a grey seam under the gate, a suggestion, an apology.

I had been climbing toward that apology for what felt like a year and was probably four hours.

The stone was cold. My hands were cold. My name — the new one, the one they'd pressed into me like a brand into soft wax — was cold too, and I kept turning it over in my mind the way you'd turn a stranger's coin, checking for the shave, the counterfeit edge.

*Xanthe.*

They'd given it to me in the vault-dark, the court beneath, in their voices like water finding cracks. *The keeper needs a keeper's name,* the Regent had said, smiling with too many teeth arranged too beautifully. *Jonah is spent. Jonah was the price. You are what remains.*

And here's the thing — the thing I will never tell Tam, or Sef, or anyone who asks me over wine in some warm future I intend to have — for one black moment, I'd wanted it. Wanted to be someone new, someone unburdened by ledgers and thirteen-year debts and a mother's careful silence. A fresh name felt like a fresh skin.

But names don't work like coats. I'd learned that in the choir of the unnamed, where they sang at me for hours and I survived because I held onto *Tam teaching me to whistle through a grass blade* and *Tam burning the porridge and blaming the stove* and *Tam, six years old, solemnly informing Mother that he would marry the well, since she gave us everything.* You can't be sung out of memories like those. They're load-bearing.

So when the court poured *Xanthe* into me, I did what my family has always done best.

I took the payment and kept the change.

They think they own the name. Fine. Let them. A name is just a handle, and a handle is only useful if someone's holding the other end of the door. I am the other end of the door now. I've been the other end of the door my whole life; I simply didn't have the title.

The seam of light widened. The stone under my palms went from cold to cool to merely stone.

And then my head broke through into morning, and the world hit me like a wave.

Grass. Actual grass, bent and glittering, silvered with frost that was already surrendering. Air so full of pine and snowmelt that I coughed, my lungs offended by richness after months of mineral dark. And above it all the sky — bruise-purple at the west, molten gold in the east, and every color between arguing its case.

I lay against the gate — against the *open* gate, the great carved doors thrown wide for the first time in living memory — and laughed until I cried, which took embarrassingly little time, and cried until I laughed, which took longer.

Behind me, down in the deep, something vast shifted. I felt it the way you feel a cart pass on a road through your boot-soles.

The god. Of course. Can't even have a good cry without an audience these days.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last stretch deserves telling properly, and I'm the only one left to tell it.

The gate broke on the twelfth night, as Sef's stars promised and as everyone but me had apparently accepted as fact. I'd come up out of the smugglers' roads half-dead, carrying a soul-flame in a lantern of bone-glass, pursued by bells made of things I still won't describe before breakfast. Brannoc stayed behind at the third door — the narrow one, the one only he knew how to brace — and the last I saw of him was his grin, wild and unrepentant, and his hand raised not in farewell but in *go on, girl, go*, like he was waving off a coach he'd finally caught.

The court took him. I know this because they told me, later, in the way they tell you everything: as though it were a courtesy.

Then the moon fell past its thirteenth mark and the seal — my family's seal, my inheritance, the work of builders whose blood I carry in a sigil on my palm — came apart like wet bread.

And the old god came through.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because everyone above has already started making it into a nicer story than it was. Sef calls it the Refusal. The village children act it out with sticks. In their version I stand heroically, speak a single ringing line, and cast the darkness back.

Here's the truth: I knelt.

Not all the way. Not the way it wanted. But the god poured through that broken gate like winter pouring through a broken window, and it filled the whole valley with its attention, and its attention was the loneliest thing I have ever felt. Centuries sealed away with nothing but prayers leaking through stone — hungry prayers, frightened prayers, and lately, greedy ones. It offered me Thornfell. Every hearth, every field, every birth and burial, mine to order and keep forever. All I had to do was kneel all the way and let it wear my sigil like a ring.

And God forgive me — no pun intended, though I suspect several parties below would find it hilarious — I *considered*. Power could fix everything. Power could dig Brannoc out of the deep. Power could mend Tam's hollowed-out years, could make the village remember my mother kindly, could rewrite every ledger ever kept.

That's the trick of it, I understand now. That's why the gods feed on obedience. It doesn't feel like surrender. It feels like competence.

What saved me wasn't courage. It was bookkeeping.

Because I stood there in the god's shadow doing sums, and the sums wouldn't balance. Everything it offered came from somewhere. Nothing beneath Thornfell gives without taking — that's the first law of the court and the last lesson of my family. If I accepted, the payment would come due on someone, someday, in thirteen turns or thirteen hundred, and some daughter of mine would stand at some gate reading some ledger with a torn page, and she would hate me the way I once feared hating my mother.

So I said no. Not ringing. Not heroic. I said it the way I'd learned to say everything down there — quietly, precisely, and with my hand flat against my own name.

*I refuse. Keep your gifts. Take instead my refusal — it's the only honest thing I own.*

And the god — here is the part that makes Sef weep and the villagers uneasy — the god *accepted*. Because it was starving, and I had finally figured out what it actually eats. Not worship. Not souls, exactly. Those are currency; the court hoards them. What feeds a god is *will freely given* — and a refusal given freely, with nothing held back, no bargain hidden in the lining, is the richest meal it had been offered in five hundred years.

It drank my refusal like wine. It sighed through the whole valley, a sound like a mountain settling into sleep. And then it withdrew — folded itself back beneath the mountain, gentle as a tide going out, leaving the gate open behind it and the debt paid and the sky untouched.

The court below was furious. Gods, they were *furious*. A refusal was supposed to feed their prisoner and break me. Instead it fed their prisoner and freed us both, and worse, it established precedent, which is the one thing bureaucracies cannot abide. By the laws the court itself keeps — and oh, they keep them, obsessively, because laws are how the toothless pretend to have teeth — a freely given refusal constituted payment in full.

They had to let me go. And having to let me go, they had to rename me on the way out, because the court never lets anyone leave without taking something, even if it's only the dignity of a clean exit.

Hence: Xanthe. Their little spite, their parting gift.

Joke's on them. I've decided I like it. It sounds like light on water. It sounds like a door opening rather than closing. And most importantly, it sounds like *mine*, because I'm the one who chose to keep it.

* * *

"Xanthe."

I hadn't heard him climb the hill. That's Tam all over — he moves quiet as an apology. He stood a few paces off in the early light, thinner than I remembered, older than he should be, with eyes that had seen the inside of the court and come back carrying the sight like a stone he couldn't put down.

He didn't know me, at first. When he'd woken at the gate, restored, soul-flame settled back into him like a bird returning to a nest grown unfamiliar — he hadn't known me at all. Months of that. Months of him looking at me with polite blankness while I died a little each time, and the returning gods pressing at the thinning seal, and me keeping the watch alone because apparently that's simply what I *do*.

But then, three weeks ago, he'd started dreaming my memories — the ones I'd surrendered in the bargain, seeded into his restored soul like a message sewn into a coat lining. He'd woken shouting my childhood nickname. He'd known about the well. He'd known about the porridge.

Now he crossed the frost-wet grass and stopped in front of me, and looked at me the way you look at a word you've finally remembered.

"You're real," he said. "Up here, in the sun, you're real."

"I'm frequently real. It's one of my more annoying qualities."

His mouth did something complicated — laugh and grief fighting over the same ground — and then he pulled me in and held on, and we stood there in the dawn like two people who had both been dead and were negotiating the terms of being alive.

"I looked for you," he said into my hair. "Down there. After they took me, before I went... smooth. I looked for a way out for both of us. There wasn't one. There was never going to be one, and I hate that you found the door I missed."

"It wasn't a door. It was a bill of sale, read very carefully." I pulled back and held up my palm, where the sigil sat quiet now, a faint silver tracery like a scar that had decided to be beautiful. "Turns out Mother's arithmetic had a flaw. She thought love meant paying the debt yourself so no one else had to. It doesn't. Love means reading the contract *out loud*, in front of everyone, and refusing to sign."

"The court let you say no?"

"The court had to. That's the difference between them and us, Tam. They can't imagine a gift without a hook in it. So when I gave one, they choked on it." I grinned, and it felt strange and good, like wearing shoes after months barefoot. "Also I stole their prisoner's dinner, escaped with their favorite soul, and restructured their entire legal relationship with the surface world. I'm told I'm considered 'a difficult account.'"

He laughed — really laughed, the sound cracking open something in his face that had been sealed since the day he walked into the mountain — and for a moment we were just two siblings on a cold hillside, and the years beneath the stone were only years, not wounds.

Then his gaze went past me, down the slope, to where the gate stood open.

"They're withdrawing," he said quietly. "The gods. Sef felt it at midnight. All across Thornfell — the answered prayers going quiet. Not angry. Just..." He searched for it. "*Full.* Like travelers leaving a table."

"Fed on my refusal," I said. "On everyone's, if they're brave enough to give one. That's the covenant now, if the village wants one. No more sold souls, no more torn pages. Just people saying what they mean to things powerful enough to hear it. Terrifying arrangement. Should suit Thornfell perfectly — we've never been any good at flattery."

"And the gate?"

I turned and looked at it properly. The great doors stood open onto darkness, and the darkness was no longer hungry. It was just dark — the honest kind, the kind that belongs to itself. Down there, a court was recalculating. A god slept on a full belly. Somewhere in the deep, Brannoc Vale was alive — I knew it the way I knew the weight of my own hand; the court kept what it took, and what it kept could someday be bargained for, by someone patient enough and stubborn enough and sufficiently bad at staying home.

Someone like me. Or like him.

"We leave it open," I said. "Not unguarded — never again unguarded. But open. A door isn't a wound, Tam. It's a promise that travel goes both ways. I spent my whole life keeping a gate shut and calling it duty, and all the while the real duty was standing right here, learning what should come through and what shouldn't." I flexed my marked palm. "The builders built doors, not walls. I finally understand why."

"So the keeper of Thornfell takes up her post," he said, and there was a question buried in the ceremony of it, careful as a man testing ice. "Above or below?"

And there it was. The old shape of things: one of us above, one of us below, forever trading places like figures on a board. My whole life had been arranged around that geometry. His rescue, my sacrifice — the same pattern, reversed. It would be so easy to step back into it. Roles are comfortable. Even tragic ones. Especially tragic ones.

I looked at my brother, keeper's son, keeper's brother, keeper himself now whether the village had crowned him yet or not. Then I looked at the open gate, and the long stair descending into the dark, and the dawn coming up behind us both like a witness.

"No," I said. "No more above and below. That was their geometry, and I've filed a formal complaint." I held out my marked hand, palm up, sigil catching the sun. "We stand together. Both of us. The gate opens onto a road, and roads need walking in both directions, and neither of us is ever again the price of the other's freedom. That's the new covenant. First clause. I'll allow amendments."

Tam looked at my hand. Then at me. Then he laughed — low, wondering — and gripped my forearm the way travelers do, the way partners do, and his grip was warm and his arm was solid and the sigil between our joined hands glowed once, softly, like a signature drying.

"Together," he agreed. "Amendments pending."

Below us, far down in the friendly dark, something rang — faint and far and unmistakable. A bell. One note only, patient as stone.

Brannoc's signal? A court's summons? An invitation, or a warning, or simply the thirteenth bell, tolling at last for a world where debts could be refused?

Tam and I looked at each other, and then, in perfect, infuriating sibling unison, we started down the stairs together.

After all — we're keepers now.

Doors don't keep themselves.
