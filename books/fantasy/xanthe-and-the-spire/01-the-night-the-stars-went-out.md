## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The charm was for a dying man, and Jonah Quintana carved it the way he did everything in Vellharbor: quietly, with his back to the wall, and with his hands doing the talking his mouth never could.

He sat on an upturned crate in the corner of Brannock's room above the rope-walk, where the ceiling sloped so low a standing man had to bow, as if the house itself were perpetually apologizing. The room smelled of tar and old sweat and the sweet-rot smell that came off Brannock like a tide going out—lung-rot, the dockworkers called it, from thirty years of breathing the dust of cut timber and the brine-spray of the harbor both. The old stevedore lay propped against greasy pillows, his chest working like bellows with a torn skin, each breath a bargain he kept renegotiating and kept losing.

Jonah's knife moved through the bone.

It was a knucklebone of a right whale, bought honestly enough years ago from a scrimshaw dealer who'd assumed it was only ivory, and it had been waiting in Jonah's pocket for three weeks, patient as bone always was, until tonight. He'd known when Brannock sent for him—he'd known the moment he heard the boy's footsteps on the stairs, the ragged rhythm of someone running an errand they're afraid to finish—that this was what the piece had been waiting for. Bone knew things. That was the whole secret of it, the thing no Warden would ever understand because they thought magic was fire and lightning and grand declarations. Magic was mostly listening. Bone remembered being alive, and if you were gentle, if you asked instead of commanded, it would tell you what shape it wanted to become.

This one wanted to be a small boat.

So Jonah gave it a hull—a shallow curve along the spine of the thing—and a keel, and a tiny prow that turned upward at just the angle a prow turns when it has decided to stop fighting the water and ride it. He worked by candlelight, which guttered whenever the harbor wind found the gaps in the shutters, and he worked slowly, because haste made bone brittle and brittle charms made noise, and noise was the one currency Vellharbor punished harder than coin.

In Vellharbor, magic was punishable by drowning. Everyone knew the words; they were carved over the gate of the Wardenhall itself, in letters deep enough to hold rainwater: *THE SEA FORGIVES WHAT THE LAW CANNOT.* Nobody said aloud what everyone understood—that the sea forgave nothing, that drowning was simply the city's way of feeding its secrets back to itself. The Wardens walked the streets in their grey salt-stiff cloaks with their white staves, and good citizens learned to keep their eyes down and their hands empty, and people like Jonah learned to be smaller than small. Invisible. A carver of trinkets, nothing more. Whale-tooth buttons, bone dice, little fish-shaped toggles for nets. He sold them at the Saltmarket stalls beside the honest craftsmen and let the honest craftsmen's noise cover his silence.

And the charms—the true ones, the ones he carved only for people who came to him through six handshakes and a whispered name—they didn't do anything any Warden's staff could point to. They didn't glow. They didn't burn. They simply *listened*, and having listened, they helped. A charm shaped like a boat could carry a dying man across the water that was coming for him, or ease him onto it gently, so that the crossing felt less like sinking and more like sailing home. Jonah had never been able to explain exactly how it worked even to himself. The bone remembered living, and it pitied the dying, and pity, turned just so, became passage.

Five years he'd lived this way. Five years since he'd first held a piece of dead thing and heard—not with his ears, but somewhere behind his sternum—a voice answering him back. It had terrified him so badly he'd vomited into the harbor and sworn off carving for a month. But the voices were kind, mostly. Murmurous. They were the accumulated memory of everything that had ever lived inside the material—whale and ox and gull and, once, terribly, a piece of driftwood that remembered being a coffin lid—and they spoke in a language that wasn't words but was better than words, a language of *yes* and *this way* and *not yet*. Over five years he'd learned its grammar. Over five years he'd built a life inside its whisper, a narrow, careful life, invisible as a stone under silt.

Every voice he had ever heard had belonged to something that had once been alive.

Which is why, when the new voice came, Jonah's knife stopped mid-stroke, and the candle flame leaned sideways though there was no wind.

*Not yet,* said the bone-boat under his fingers—but not in the bone's usual voice, that warm grainy murmur like a grandmother humming through a wall. This came from deeper. From underneath everything. It came up through the floorboards and the harbor-mud below them and the drowned foundations beneath the mud, and it passed through the whalebone the way cold passes through a windowpane. *Not yet. Not finished. The door is opening, little maker.*

Jonah sat very still. In the bed, Brannock's breathing hitched, steadied, hitched again.

"That's not you," Jonah whispered to the charm. His lips barely moved. "That isn't any of you."

*No,* agreed the strange voice, and there was something in its tone that was almost—almost—amusement. Almost tenderness. The way a very large animal might regard a very small one without any intention of eating it. *We are older than your bones. We are older than your sea. We have been knocking a long time, little maker, and tonight the wood is thin.*

The candle went out.

Not guttered—went out, all at once, the way a held breath is let go. And in the sudden dark Jonah saw two things that would stay stitched into his memory for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

The first was Brannock. The old man's eyes were open, and they were fixed on the ceiling—or rather, on something past the ceiling, past the roof, past the sky—and his ruined lungs were drawing slow full breaths now, easy as a sleeping child's, and he was smiling. "Oh," Brannock said, to no one, to someone. "Oh, it's bright."

The second thing Jonah saw was the window.

Beyond the shutter-gaps, where the night sky should have been laying out its familiar scatter of stars over the black water of the harbor—the few brave stars that Vellharbor's coal-smoke ever allowed, the ones fishermen navigated by, the ones children were taught to name so they wouldn't fear the dark—there was nothing. Not cloud-cover. Not fog. Jonah knew the city's fogs the way he knew his own hands. This was different. The sky had gone a depthless, absolute black, blacker than the sea beneath it, black as the inside of the earth, and the stars—

The stars were gone. All of them. As if a hand had come down and snuffed them like candles, all at once, the way his own candle had gone out.

"No," Jonah said softly. To whom, he couldn't have said.

Brannock sighed—one long, unlabored breath, the breath of a boat finally slipping its moorings—and did not sigh again. And in the dark, in Jonah's cupped palm, the little bone-boat grew suddenly, unbearably cold, and the voice that was not human, the voice from underneath the world, spoke once more, and this time it was not alone. There were others behind it. Hundreds. Thousands. A congregation. A tide.

*Thank you for the door, little maker. We will find you when we need you found.*

Then the voice was gone, and the bone was just bone again, warm as it had ever been, and Jonah Quintana sat alone in a dark room with a dead man and a finished charm and a sky with no stars in it, and he began, very quietly, to shake.

* * *

He did the necessary things because his hands insisted on it even while his mind reeled. He closed Brannock's eyes. He set the bone-boat in the dead man's folded hands, where the family would find it and think it a kindness from some neighbor and bury it with him, and perhaps it would still work, or perhaps it had already done its work, or perhaps—Jonah's stomach turned—perhaps it had done something else entirely, something he hadn't asked it to, something the other voice had used it for. A door opened, the voice had said. Had *he* opened it? Had his knife, his asking, his gentle stupid listening?

He wrapped his tools in oilcloth with hands that wouldn't quite obey and left by the window, dropping into the alley where the smell of low tide rose thick as wool.

Vellharbor at night was usually loud even in its quietest hours—the groan of hulls, the bell-buoy off the mole, the endless argument of gulls. Tonight it was silent in a way Jonah had never heard a city be silent. No bells. No dogs. And everywhere, spilling from windows and doorways and the mouths of alleys, people. Standing in the streets in their nightclothes, faces tipped up toward the starless black. An old woman held her hands out as if catching snow that wasn't falling. Two men stood at the harbor rail staring down into water that reflected nothing—no moon, no lights, only darkness stacked upon darkness, sky and sea become one continuous absence, so that looking at the harbor was like looking down into a hole in the world.

"The wards," someone whispered, and the word ran through the crowd like a spark through dry grass. *The wards. The border wards. Did you feel it? The wards are down.*

Jonah felt it now that it was named—the way you notice a constant sound only when it stops. For all his twenty-six years, all his life, there had been something at the edge of every sense, a faint pressure like the hush before snowfall, the hum of the great wards that ringed the city where land met sea and the mortal realm met whatever lay beyond it. Every child grew up inside that hum and forgot it was there, the way a fish forgets water.

It was gone. The silence where it had been was the loudest thing Jonah had ever heard.

He pulled his hood up and slipped along the wall, invisible, invisible, five years of practiced invisibility carrying him through the stunned crowds—until, at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, he heard the one sound in all of Vellharbor that could still frighten him more than a starless sky.

Boots. Many boots, in step. Grey cloaks, white staves, lanterns burning a flat colorless light.

Wardens, moving fast, moving *toward the harbor*—and above their heads, above the whole broken night, the first of the missing stars blinked back into existence like an eye opening, then winked out again, then returned, flickering, wrong, as though something on the far side of the sky were trying to remember how the light worked.

"Quintana!" someone hissed near his shoulder—an old fishwife who bought luck-charms, gripping his sleeve with fingers like kelp. Her eyes were wide and white-rimmed. "They say it's sorcery. They say someone *opened* it. Boy—if they start sweeping the markets tomorrow, you want to be nowhere in this city."

She melted away before he could answer, and Jonah stood in the dark with his heart hammering the bone-cold rhythm of that other voice. *We will find you when we need you found.*

Above him, the false star flickered, steadied—and began, slowly, deliberately, to move against the turning of the heavens.

Toward the Spire.
