## 2. Charms for the Drowned

Jonah Quintana had not slept. He sat at his worktable in the attic room above the chandler's shop on Saltspray Lane, the single candle burned down to a stub, and watched the bone in his hands as though it might confess.

It had been whispering since midnight.

Not the way bone usually whispered—the dry, patient hush of material remembering the animal it came from, which any carver with half a gift could feel and most pretended not to. This was different. This was a voice, low and wet, like something speaking through a mouthful of harbor water, and it was saying the same thing over and over in a language he did not know but somehow understood anyway: *the wards are down, the wards are down, the wards are down.*

He had checked the window three times. The stars were still gone. Not clouded—gone, the whole sky above Vellharbor a flat, starless black like the inside of a closed coffin, and where the border wards should have glimmered along the harbor mouth in their faint silver lattice, there was nothing at all. Just open water. Just night.

Five years he had kept himself small. Five years of carving charms that worked *just* enough to pass for luck—knotwork against rheumatism, a fishhook charm that made the catch a little heavier, a birthing charm shaped like a coiled eel that midwives paid good coin for and never asked why it helped. He had learned to keep each piece of magic so thin it barely registered, a whisper instead of a shout. The Wardens' divination rods hummed at true sorcery. They had never once hummed at Jonah.

But last night he had carved a charm for Tam Wicker, the old dockworker dying of the lung-rot two streets over, and when the knife came through the final curve of the design, a voice had poured up through the bone that was not human, not animal, not anything he had words for—and every charm on his shelf had begun to sing.

They were singing now. Quietly, under the hearing of ordinary ears, but singing: eleven small voices in eleven small bones, harmonizing on a note of grief.

*The wards are down.*

Jonah set the piece down and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. Outside, the bells of the Tide Temple began to ring—not the measured hour-chime but the rapid, ragged alarm pattern he'd heard only twice before in his life. Once for the fire on Candlerow. Once for the flood.

He dressed in the dark, wrapped four of the loudest charms in oilcloth, and buried them in the flour barrel, because a man who ran from his own room looked guilty, and a man who went out to buy bread at dawn looked merely poor.

* * *

Vellharbor woke up screaming.

Not literally—not all of it—but by the time Jonah reached the Fishmarket with a paper sack of flour under his arm, panic had spread through the crowd like bilgewater through a hold. People stood in knots, talking too fast and too loud. A woman clutched a child who wouldn't stop crying. Fishmongers had abandoned their stalls; the mackerel lay gleaming and ignored, flies already gathering, because no one cared about breakfast when the stars had gone out and the wards with them.

"The Spire," a man was saying, over and over, as if repetition might make it make sense. "You can see the Spire from the seawall now. You never used to see it. The water hides it, it's always hidden it, and now—"

"I heard the drowned walking on the tideline," said a fishwife, and spat to ward off the words. "My cousin saw them. Rows of them. Standing in the shallows facing the city."

"Liar."

"Ask her yourself then! Ask her!"

And through it all, moving in pairs through the crowd with their grey cloaks and their long white rods, the Wardens.

Jonah knew how to be invisible around Wardens. It was a craft, like carving—shoulders down, eyes lowered, hands empty, walk with the tired shamble of a laborer heading home from a night shift. He had done it a thousand times. He did it now, drifting past a pair of them at the market gate, close enough to smell the lamp-oil and cold iron of their uniforms, and the rods hung quiet at their belts, humming only faintly, the way they always hummed near crowds—

No. Not the way they always hummed.

One of the Wardens stopped. Turned her head. Looked directly at Jonah with the flat, sweeping gaze of a gull sighting offal.

His blood went to ice water. He kept walking. Behind him he heard her say something to her partner, low, and then boots on cobbles, following, unhurried. Testing.

*It isn't you,* he told himself. *Half the market is frightened sick. Fear tastes like magic to those rods.*

But his charms were back at the room above Saltspray Lane, all except the ones in the flour barrel, and one of them—the oldest one, the first charm he'd ever carved, a little knucklebone token he wore on a cord at his throat because he'd never had the heart to sell it—that one was *singing*, right there against his skin, rising toward a pitch that made his teeth ache. And the drowned voice underneath it had changed its refrain. No longer *the wards are down.* Now:

*Come to the water. Come to the water. We remember your name.*

"I don't have a name," Jonah muttered, barely moving his lips. "I never had a name."

The boots behind him quickened.

He turned down the alley between the smokehouse and the cooperage, walked it calm as Sunday, came out onto Ropewalk, and cut through the net-mending yards where the tar-stink gave him cover and the hanging nets broke up a pursuer's sightline. When he glanced back, the Warden had lost him—or paused, or circled—he couldn't tell. His heart slammed like someone beating on a door.

He needed to get the rest of the charms. He needed to sink them in the harbor, maybe, or burn them, or—

Or listen to them, said a small treacherous part of him. Because they were the only things in the world telling him what had actually happened last night, and whatever had broken the wards, whatever stood in the shallows facing the city, the dead wanted him to know about it.

*We remember your name.*

He didn't even know what name they meant.

* * *

The raid on Saltspray Lane came an hour after noon.

Jonah was halfway up the outside stair to the chandler's shop when he saw them: six Wardens, grey-cloaked, moving door to door with the unhurried efficiency of men who had done this many times and would do it many more. At the far end of the lane, two neighbors already knelt in the street with their hands bound behind them—a tinker and the widow Ostrey, who sold candles and had never in her life done magic worse than reading fortunes in tea leaves. Their faces were bewildered more than afraid, as if they hadn't yet caught up to what was happening to them.

A boy ran past Jonah on the stairs, going down, white-faced. "They're taking anyone," the boy babbled, to no one, to everyone. "Anyone the rods point at. Anyone at all."

Jonah flattened himself against the wall in the shadow of the stairwell. Below, a Warden hammered on the chandler's door. Old Bram opened it, blinking, wax-stained apron still on.

"Divination sweep," the Warden said. "Everyone out. Everyone searched."

Bram protested, reasonably, that he was a chandler, that he sold candles, that he had lived on this lane thirty years—and the rod in the second Warden's hand swung toward him almost lazily, and hummed, and the first Warden said, "Him too," and that was that. Thirty years counted for nothing. Bram's face as they took his arm was the face of a man watching his own house burn from inside it.

And then, horribly, Jonah understood why the rod would hum near Bram. Because Bram's lodger was standing twenty feet overhead, and the oldest charm at his throat had climbed past singing into something like a hymn, and the drowned dead beneath the harbor had begun calling his name aloud—not with sound, exactly, but with a pressure in the air, a wrongness that divination rods were built to find.

*We remember your name,* the bone sang. *Quintana. Quintana. Come down to the water, Quintana.*

"Shut up," Jonah breathed. "Please. Shut up, shut up—"

He pressed his palm hard over the charm, but the song came through his own flesh now, up through his bones, which were bone too, everything was bone, everything remembered. The Wardens below went still. One of them lifted his head slowly, like a hound catching scent on the wind.

"There." The word dropped into the lane like a stone into a well. "Above the chandler's. Something's up there."

Jonah ran.

There was nowhere to run *to*—that was the truth he admitted somewhere around the third rooftop, scrambling across tarred planking with slates sliding underfoot, the lane shrinking away below. Up was the only direction left. He went over the chandler's roof, through a gap in the eaves into his own attic room, and for one mad ten-count he actually tried to gather his life: the knife, always the knife first, bone-handled whalebone-bladed, his father's before it was his; a handful of charms swept blindly from the shelf; the flour barrel too heavy, leave it, leave everything—

The front door below splintered inward.

Boots on the stairs. Two at a time. Voices calling positions in the clipped cant of the Warden orders.

Jonah went out the window.

The drop to the cooper's yard was twelve feet and he took it badly, rolling off a stack of barrel staves and coming up with his ankle screaming and his palm torn open on a nail. But he came up. Behind and above him, the window frame cracked as a Warden's shoulder hit it, and a white divination rod thrust out into the daylight and began to swing, hunting, humming louder now, so loud he could hear it from the ground, a wasp-swarm note that meant *sorcery, sorcery, found.*

He limped for the alley. The nets of the mending-yard caught him, tore at him, held him for one endless heartbeat—and then he was through, and down Rope-walk, and into the labyrinth of the Stiltmarket where the houses leaned so close overhead that the sun came through in blades and the tide-sludge underfoot swallowed the sound of running.

Behind him: whistles. Three notes, repeated. Calling more of them. Calling the sweep inward.

His ankle buckled on a wet stair. He caught himself on a piling, gasping, and in the sudden stillness felt the knife at his belt and the handful of charms in his fist and nothing else in the entire world. No room. No stock. No name worth keeping. Twenty-six years of careful, patient, invisible living, and it had taken the Wardens less than half a day to burn it all down around him.

Grief hit him sideways, absurdly, in the middle of the flight: grief for the worktable, for the candle stub, for old Tam Wicker's unfinished charm lying where he'd dropped it on the floor. Grief for the man he'd been this morning, who had a life, however small, however borrowed.

*Run now,* said the drowned voice in the bones, gentler than it had been all day, almost kind. *Grieve later. We will show you where the Wardens cannot follow. We remember you, Jonah Quintana. We have always remembered you.*

"They'll drown me," he said out loud, to the air, to the dead, staggering on. "That's what they do. That's the punishment. You're asking me to run toward the water."

*Yes,* said the dead. *Because the water is where we live. And you are going to need us, heir.*

The word went through him like cold iron. *Heir.* Heir of what? Of whom? He had no family, no line, nothing but a dead father's knife and a dead mother's silence—

The whistle sounded again, closer, at the mouth of the alley behind him. He turned, cornered, chest heaving, and found himself face to face with a dead-end wall of blackened brick, tide-marked halfway up, and beyond it, through a grate in the stones, the dark water of the harbor breathing in and out.

And turning into the alley after him, white rod raised and humming like a struck bell, came a Warden he had seen before today—seen at the market gate, seen directing the sweep, a tall woman with grey-shot hair pulled back tight and a face like something carved and weatherworn, whose expression, even now, even here, was impossible to read.

"Hands where I can see them," she said.

Jonah's back met the brick. The harbor breathed behind him through the grate, cold and salt and waiting. The charm at his throat sang its hymn of the drowned, and the dead whispered *heir, heir,* and the Warden's rod swung toward him and stopped, trembling, pointing at his heart.

She frowned. Not at him—at the rod. As if it had told her something she hadn't expected to hear.

"What is it singing?" she asked quietly.

And Jonah, who had spent five years learning to lie with his whole body, found that the only true answer rose straight out of him, cracked and terrified:

"I don't know. It started when the stars went out. It won't stop. I can't make it stop."

The rod hummed. The water breathed. Somewhere above the drowned city, where stars should have been, the darkness waited—and the Warden looked at Jonah Quintana for a long, long moment, and did not call for backup.
