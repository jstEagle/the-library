## 3. The Warden Who Let Go

The rain had been falling for three days, and Xanthe Corvane had stopped believing it was only rain.

She stood beneath the broken eave of the Saltmarket arcade, her warden's coat soaked through at the shoulders, watching the crowd move like water around the notice board. The proclamation had gone up at dawn: *By order of the High Warden, all practitioners of unlicensed craft are to be surrendered for questioning.* Beneath it, someone had scratched *the stars will come back* into the wet wood, and someone else had tried to scrub it out, and the two gestures together said more about Vellharbor than any sermon in the Lantern Quarter ever had.

Three days since the sky had gone black. Three days since the border wards—the great woven wards that ringed the city like a net of light, older than the Wardens themselves—had come apart with a sound she still heard when she tried to sleep. Not a crash. A sigh. Like something enormous exhaling after holding its breath for a very long time.

And three days since she'd stood in the Wardenhall armory and watched High Warden Aldous Vey pin the mourning band on his own sleeve and say, in that voice like oiled stone, *"We will find who did this. We will drown them where they stand."*

Xanthe knew what it was to be on the wrong side of Vey's certainty. She'd been on it once before, five years ago, holding her sister's wrists behind her back while Sera wept and called her by name—not *Warden*, not *Corvane*, just *Xanthe*, over and over, as if repetition could undo what was happening. Sera had been taken to the Spire for binding. That was what they told families. *Taken to the Spire for binding.* No one asked what happened after.

Xanthe had asked. Once. She'd been reassigned to night patrol of the harbor district within the week, which was its own kind of answer.

Now the harbor district was hers again, in the way a wound is yours, and the rain came down, and the crowd parted around a boy moving too quickly through the fish stalls with his hood up and his left hand buried in his coat pocket.

She almost didn't follow him. That was the truth of it, later, when everything had changed shape. She was tired, and cold, and the last thing she wanted was another arrest to carry. But his hand moved inside the coat the way hands move when they're counting something small and precious—beads, coins, charms—and the notices had been explicit: charm-sellers were the first sweep's priority. The bone-carvers, the knot-tiers, the whisper-merchants who'd kept the poor of Vellharbor half-healthy and half-lucky for generations.

The boy slipped down the alley behind the rendering sheds, and Xanthe followed, her boots silent out of long practice.

* * *

He was fast. Faster than he should have been, the way people are fast when terror has burned away everything else. He vaulted a stack of salt barrels, ducked under a drying line, took the corner at the end of the alley in a slide that nearly cost him his footing on the slick cobbles. Xanthe let him run. She knew these alleys the way Sera had known the tide-tables, and there was exactly one way out of the rendering-yard lane, and it ended in a wall.

It ended in a wall, and then it ended in Xanthe, standing between him and the drain-grate he'd been aiming for, her warden's sigil already bare on her collar, rain running off her jaw.

"Stop," she said, and hated how the word sounded—how it always sounded, like a door closing.

He stopped. He was younger than she'd thought, seventeen maybe, eighteen, with a carver's calluses and eyes that had the particular flatness of someone who had learned to be invisible and was now discovering invisibility didn't work anymore. His hand was still in his pocket. Around something.

"Jonah Quintana," she said, because she'd read the name off the sweep-list that morning. *Charm-seller. Bone-carved goods. Last seen, Saltmarket.* "You're going to come with me."

"No," he said. Simply. Without bravado, without pleading. Just *no*, the way you'd refuse an offer of spoiled fish.

"You don't get to say no."

"Everyone says no eventually." His voice was quiet, roughened. "You just have to catch them first."

"I've caught you."

"Have you?"

And then his fist came out of his pocket, and Xanthe moved on instinct, catching his wrist, twisting him against the wall—and the charms spilled from his open hand across the wet stones between them.

Six of them. Seven. Small things of carved bone and knotted cord, the kind of work she'd confiscated by the hundred over eleven years of service. They scattered, rolled, clinked against each other in the puddles.

And one of them sang.

Not aloud. Nothing so merciful as aloud. It hummed against her skin where her bare wrist had touched it—a frequency below hearing, a vibration in the teeth and the breastbone, and threaded through it, thin as smoke through a keyhole:

*Xanthe?*

Xanthe went very still.

She knew that voice. She had known it across a kitchen table and across a courtroom and through a locked door in the Wardenhall cells, five years ago, the last time she'd heard it say her name. Sera's voice. Her sister's voice, unmistakable even thinned to this, even stretched across whatever distance lay between the living world and wherever this scrap of carved whalebone had been dipped.

The rain fell. The boy watched her face change, and she saw him see it, saw understanding dawn in him slow and terrible, the way dawn comes over water.

"You can hear her," he whispered.

"Shut up." Her grip on his wrist hadn't loosened. Her whole body had become a held breath.

"She's been singing since the stars went out." The words tumbled now, unstoppable, the floodgates of a lonely man opened by accident. "All of them, all the dead in the harbor, but her loudest, her clearest, I thought—I thought it was a trick, I thought the Wardens sent her somehow, but you—" His eyes were wet, and not from the rain. "Who is she? Why does she know your name?"

*I said shut up.*

But she didn't say it. Because her sister's voice was humming through a dead woman's bone in a drowned alley, saying *Xanthe* the way she used to say it across breakfast, annoyed and fond, *Xanthe, you're burning the eggs,* and Xanthe Corvane had spent five years building a life on top of a grave and the grave had just opened its mouth.

Her training said: arrest him. Bring him in. Charm-seller, unlicensed practitioner, subject of an active sweep order. Her training said it in Vey's voice, in her own voice, in the voice of every oath she'd spoken with her hand on the lantern-stone.

Her hand let go of his wrist.

He stared at her. She stared at the charms in the puddle, and slowly, deliberately, like a woman walking into deep water, she crouched and picked one up. Whalebone, worn smooth, carved with a spiral that meant *safe passage* in no language the Wardens taught.

"She was arrested," Xanthe said. It wasn't an explanation. It was more like bleeding. "Five years ago. For sorcery. By me."

The boy—Jonah—didn't flinch, didn't recoil. That was the strange thing. He looked at her the way you look at someone standing at the edge of the same cliff you're standing on.

"They took her to the Spire," Xanthe went on, because she couldn't seem to stop, because the words had been sealed in her for five years and his charms had cracked the seal. "They told my mother she'd be bound gently. They told me—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "They gave me a commendation. For loyalty to the law over blood."

The rain drummed on the shed roofs. Somewhere beyond the lane, a patrol bell rang twice—the sweep moving east, away from them, for now.

"If I take you in," Xanthe said quietly, "you'll be drowned in the harbor square within the week. If I let you go, I'm done. Everything I am is done. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There's no third door."

"Yes," Jonah said. And then, unbearably gentle: "There might be. The dead keep saying something. All of them, all at once, like a tide coming in. They keep saying—" He frowned, listening to something she couldn't hear, and his face went pale. "*Spire. Spire. Spire.*"

The word dropped between them like a stone into still water.

Xanthe rose. She picked up the remaining charms one by one and pressed them back into his hands, closing his fingers over them, feeling the faint warmth of the bone as if the dead inside them were body-heat.

"Here's how it goes," she said. "You're my prisoner until we're clear of the sweep lines. After that, you tell me everything—what the dead are saying, why the stars went out, why her voice found *your* charms and not mine. Everything. In exchange, I keep you breathing. Those are the terms."

"That's not protection," Jonah said. "That's a hostage trade."

"It's the only honest thing the Wardens ever taught me," Xanthe said. "Everything worth having costs something. Come on."

She pulled him up the alley, one hand fisted in his collar, performing capture for anyone who might be watching—and beneath the performance, beneath the rain and the guilt and eleven years of oaths, Sera's voice hummed on in the bone against the boy's chest, patient as tide.

*Hurry,* it seemed to say. *Both of you. Hurry.*

* * *

They cleared the sweep lines by walking straight through them—Xanthe's sigil opening checkpoints like a key, Jonah playing the cowed prisoner with an actor's instinct she chose not to examine too closely. At the boundary of Harrowgate ward, where the lanterns gave out and the drowned old streets began their descent toward the harbor's black heart, she finally released his collar.

"Walk," she said. "Talk."

So he talked. About the night the stars vanished, about voices arriving in charms he'd carved for the living, about the drowned dead rising through the harbor water like a congregation finding its church. And Xanthe walked beside him into the darkening city, listening, and understood with every step that she had already made her choice—that she'd made it five years ago in a courtroom, or maybe thirty years ago at a kitchen table, and everything since had only been the long delay before she admitted it.

Ahead of them, past the rooftops, the Spire's silhouette broke the skyline of the drowned quarter, dark against a starless sky.

"Hurry," Xanthe murmured, not knowing she was echoing the dead.
