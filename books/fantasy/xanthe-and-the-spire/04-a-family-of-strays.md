## 4. A Family of Strays

The entrance to the Undertow was a drowned man's mouth, if the drowned man had swallowed a staircase.

Jonah followed Xanthe down through it, past the rotted pilings and into the dark, his boots finding each step by feel while the harbor pressed in around them like something patient. Above, Vellharbor was screaming—bells, horns, the flat shouts of Wardens calling names into the fog—but down here the water only breathed. In and out against the stone. A sound like sleeping.

"You'll want to keep your hands where Maren can see them," Xanthe said quietly. "She's lost one already. She's protective of the rest."

"Of her hands?"

"Of everything."

The staircase ended at a landing of black wet rock, and beyond it the warren opened like the inside of a shell: caverns hollowed from the old city's foundations when the founders drowned half of Vellharbor to build their seawall. Lamplight hung in glass floats, green-gold, swaying on lines strung between pillars. Rope bridges sagged across the dark. Drying fish. Bundles of contraband. A whole hidden life, breathing.

A woman stood at the edge of the lamplight with a cleaver in her remaining hand and its twin sheathed at her hip where the other hand should have been. She was broad as a door, gray-streaked hair bound with tarred cord, and her eyes went over Jonah the way a fishmonger weighs a catch.

"This is what you brought me," she said to Xanthe. Not a question. A verdict pending.

"He's not what they're hunting," Xanthe said.

"Maren." Jonah kept his voice level. He'd learned that tone carving charms for dying men—you didn't flinch, you didn't promise too much. "I'm not going to be trouble."

"Everyone's trouble." She turned the cleaver once, idly, the way another woman might turn a ring. "Trouble is the only thing that comes down here honest. It's everything else that lies." Her gaze snagged on the bundle at his chest, the charms wrapped in oilcloth, and something flickered behind her eyes—not fear. Recognition. "You carry voices."

Jonah's blood went cold. "I don't—"

"Boy." Maren lowered the cleaver. "I've been listening to the tide for forty years. The tide remembers everyone it takes. Tonight it's been remembering them *louder*." She jerked her chin toward a rope bridge. "Kitchen's through there. There's stew. Eat before you decide whether you're trouble or not. Nobody makes good decisions hungry."

* * *

Xanthe sat apart from the fire pit, the way she'd sat apart from every fire since her disgrace, and told herself she was watching the room.

She was not watching the room.

She was watching Jonah Quintana eat stew with a feral child standing over him, close enough to touch, staring at him with the frank hunger of someone deciding whether he was food or family. Pip couldn't have been more than ten. Hair cut raggedly, barefoot even here where the floors wept cold, and moving in that way children move who've learned that stillness is dangerous—that you survive by being somewhere else before the blow lands.

"Pip," Maren warned, without looking up from her pot.

Pip did not move away.

"You can hear my charms?" Jonah asked the child gently. He'd set three of them on the table between them, small things of bone and driftwood, and Pip was leaning so close her nose nearly touched them.

"They sing," Pip said. First words since the tunnel. Her voice was rusty, like a hinge. "Bad songs tonight. Sad ones. Lots of people went under the water and now they're all talking at once and it's—" She scrunched her face, hunting for the word. "Crowded."

The fire cracked. Xanthe saw Jonah's hand tremble, just slightly, before he stilled it. He looked at this grubby child as though she'd reached into his chest and shown him his own heart.

"Yes," he said softly. "That's exactly what it's like."

"They don't hurt?"

"No. They're just... lonely. Like me, I suppose."

Pip considered this with enormous gravity. Then she picked up one charm—a little carved gull, wings folded—and held it to her ear, then held it out to him. "This one's nice. This one says the sea used to be full of lights."

"The stars," Jonah whispered. "Yes. It did."

Xanthe's chest tightened. She knew what those words cost him. She'd heard the same thing in her sister's voice, years ago, the last time Lys had tried to tell her about the covenant—*the stars are supposed to come back, Xanthe, the wards are stealing them*—and Xanthe had done her duty. She had put the irons on her own sister's wrists. She had watched them take her below to the Spire, and she had told herself for five years that law was the same thing as right.

Maren dropped a bowl beside Xanthe, hard enough to slosh.

"Eat," the tide-witch said. "You look like a woman arguing with a ghost."

"I'm resting."

"Mm. And I'm a duchess." Maren settled onto the bench across from her, uninvited, her single hand wrapped around her own bowl. "Corvane. That was your name, wasn't it? Before you fell."

"It's still my name."

"Funny thing about falling," Maren said. "Everyone remembers your name better after." She blew on her stew. "You arrested your sister. Everyone down here knows the story. Warden Corvane, iron-honest, turns in her own blood for sorcery. They gave you a medal and then they took everything else, because an honest Warden is more dangerous than a corrupt one."

"I know how the story goes."

"Do you know how it *ends*?" Maren's voice was quiet now, and that was worse than shouting. "Because I do. I've heard the tide telling it. Your sister's still alive, warden. Still down there. Still holding something up with her bones."

The bowl nearly slipped from Xanthe's hands.

"That's a lie," she said. "They executed—"

"They told you they executed her." Maren shrugged. "And you believed them, because believing them was easier than drowning with the rest of us. But ask your charm-carver sometime what the dead say about the Spire. Ask him whose voice has been crying under the harbor for thirteen years, loud enough that even I can hear it through forty feet of stone."

* * *

Later, when the lamps were dimmed and Pip had curled up against Jonah's side like she'd always slept there, Xanthe found him awake, turning a blank piece of bone in his fingers.

"She talks in her sleep," he said, nodding at the child. "Counts things. Doors, mostly."

"She counts exits," Xanthe corrected quietly. "Children like Pip count exits."

They sat in the green-gold light, and the silence between them was different from any silence they'd shared on the run—no longer two strangers bound by necessity, but something with weight and warmth, like a coal banked for morning.

"My charms sang all night," Jonah said finally. "All the way down here. Hundreds of voices. But when we crossed into the Undertow, they got quieter." He looked up. "Like they felt safe. How does dead water know what safe feels like?"

"Because Maren keeps it safe." Xanthe hesitated. Then, because he had given her his secrets on the flooded road, she gave hers back. "She told me something tonight. About my sister."

Jonah went very still.

"She said Lys is alive. Bound into the Spire. That the Wardens never executed her at all." Xanthe's voice stayed flat through sheer discipline; underneath it, something was tearing loose that had been nailed down for five years. "If she's lying, I'll know how to live with it. If she isn't—" Her jaw worked. "Then I didn't arrest my sister. I delivered her."

"Xanthe—"

"And if the order I gave my life to did that to her, then every drowning I ever sanctioned, every arrest—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she spoke again it was barely above the water-sound. "I let go of you because your charms hummed with her voice. Did you know that? One moment of her, in a market full of noise, and I threw away fifteen years of oath."

"I know," Jonah said. "I've been grateful every day."

"Don't thank me. I didn't do it out of kindness." She laughed, and it came out broken. "I did it because I'm a coward who'd rather be wrong about everything than wrong about that."

From the shadow of the archway, Maren watched them both, and said nothing, and went back to salting fish.

* * *

Morning in the Undertow came as a change in the quality of the dark—the lamps brightened, someone started bread, and Pip woke shrieking with joy because the tide had left gifts.

Jonah came down to find the child dancing along the lowest landing with her skirts hiked up, pointing at things the water had pushed through the ward-crack overnight: a ship's figurehead, a crate of oranges gone soft, and a mirror, silvered with age, lying face-up in the shallows.

"Don't," Maren said, appearing at his shoulder. But she was smiling, or the closest thing her face could do to it. "Let her have it. Water gives what water gives."

Breakfast was oranges and smoked eel and hard bread, and it should have been nothing—it was less than Jonah had eaten on good nights alone in his garret—but it was the first meal he'd shared in five years, and it tasted like a feast. Pip ate four oranges and wore the fifth behind her ear. Maren told a story about smuggling a bishop's mistress past the Wardens inside a coffin. Xanthe laughed—one short, startled bark, as if surprised to find laughter still lived in her—and then looked around the table almost fearfully, as if someone might confiscate it.

Jonah understood that look. He'd worn it for years, checking corners, keeping his voice down, being no one. And here was this disgraced warden discovering what he had discovered slowly and alone: that belonging doesn't announce itself. It just sits down next to you and asks if you want more bread.

"We should talk about the Spire," Xanthe said at last, and the warmth dimmed but did not die; it simply made room.

"After breakfast," Maren said firmly. "Dead things have waited thirteen years. Stew waits for no one."

But Jonah was already reaching for his charms, because they had begun to murmur again—and this time it wasn't the scattered chorus of the drowned, hundreds of small sad voices. It was one voice. Clear. Close. Rising through the floor of the Undertow like light through water.

A woman's voice. Saying, patiently, over and over, as she had said for thirteen years:

"*Tell my sister I'm still here.*"

Jonah's eyes found Xanthe's across the table. He saw her go white. He saw her understand.

The Spire was calling her home—and it wanted something in exchange.
