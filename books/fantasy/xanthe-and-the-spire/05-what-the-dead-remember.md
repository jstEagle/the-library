## 5. What the Dead Remember

The dead did not speak to Xanthe. That was the whole point of her order, the founding promise stitched into every Warden's oath: *the living shall not hear the drowned, and the drowned shall not trouble the living.* For thirteen years she had believed that silence was the natural order of things, a mercy as old as the city itself. She had arrested people for claiming otherwise. She had arrested her own sister for it.

Now she stood in the flooded gallery beneath the harbor's edge and listened to Jonah Quintana listen.

He was crouched at the waterline where the Undertow's lowest tunnel opened onto black water, his bone charms strung across both wrists like shackles he'd chosen himself. His lips moved without sound. His head tilted, first left, then right, the way a man turns toward voices in another room. The lantern Maren had given them threw his shadow long across the wet stone, and the shadow seemed to lean toward the water too.

"They're saying something," he murmured. "They keep saying the same thing. Like a tide coming in."

"What thing?"

His eyes found hers, and there was something in them that frightened her more than any sorcery had—not power, but grief worn smooth, the way sea glass is worn. "That we're late."

* * *

They had left the Undertow before dawn, when the warren's lamps guttered low and Pip was still asleep in a nest of rope, and Maren had pressed a knuckle of whalebone into Xanthe's palm with instructions Xanthe half understood: *follow the ebb, never the flow; the old city remembers its streets even under water.* The tide-witch's one hand had been surprisingly warm. "Bring him back," Maren had said, nodding at Jonah, "or don't come back at all." It had sounded like kindness. It had also sounded like a threat, and Xanthe was beginning to learn that in Vellharbor those were often the same thing.

Now they moved through the drowned galleries of the old city—the first Vellharbor, sunk on purpose, if you believed the histories, when the Wardens raised the border wards and sealed the living realm against the star-dead. Xanthe had walked these tunnels before. Every warden did, once, during initiation: a ceremonial descent into the dark to honor the Founders who had saved the world. They were shown the sunken streets through grate-windows and told, *this is what was spared you.*

She had never thought to ask why the old city looked less like a ruin than like something still inhabited. Doors stood closed. Tables sat set. There were chairs pushed back from them, as though the drowning had happened between one course and the next.

"Here," Jonah said suddenly, stopping so fast Xanthe nearly walked into him.

The tunnel opened into a wide chamber—a market hall, by the shape of it, stalls long collapsed, and in the center a fountain dry as ash. The water lay beyond it, visible through a great arched window that must once have framed a view of the harbor; now it framed only the deep, where nothing lived and nothing moved except, perhaps, the light itself, which behaved strangely down here, pooling and drifting like smoke.

Jonah walked straight to the fountain and put both hands flat on its rim.

Xanthe's training screamed at her to stop him. Her oath—she could recite it still, word-perfect, thirteen years after speaking it—said that contact with the drowned dead was the first sin, the sin of the star-touched, the sin that had drowned the old world. Her hand went to the short iron baton at her belt out of pure reflex, and she hated herself for it, because she had watched this man carve a charm for a dying dockworker with hands gentler than any priest's.

"The founders," Jonah said quietly. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the middle distance, at whatever stood just behind the air. "They're showing me the founders."

"There were nine," Xanthe said automatically. "Nine Wardens who sealed the covenant and raised the wards. Their names are carved above the Hall of Tides."

"Eight." His voice had gone strange, flat, reciting. "There were eight who raised the wards. The ninth—" His fingers twitched on the stone, tracing something. "The ninth argued. The ninth said the covenant wasn't theirs to break, that the star-dead weren't invaders, they were *partners*, that the realms had kept faith with each other since before either had a name." He swallowed. "The other eight bound her. They used her to anchor the wards. A person, Xanthe. They anchored their holy law to a living woman, and called it sacrifice, and built a city on top of the lie."

The chamber was very cold. Or perhaps that was Xanthe.

"That's not true." She heard her own voice from far away, steady, warden-flat, the voice she used on the docks when she wanted someone to feel small. "The Founders' Accord is the oldest document in Vellharbor. It's been read aloud at every turn-of-moon ceremony for—"

"For thirteen turns," Jonah said. "I know. They keep saying that. Thirteen turns ago. Thirteen turns ago. Over and over, like they can't believe it either." He finally turned to look at her, and the lantern-light caught the bone charms at his wrists, and each one of them, she realized, was humming faintly, a sound below hearing that she felt in her teeth rather than her ears. "Thirteen turns ago is when your order began. Your oldest law isn't older than the crime. It was written *after*. It was written to cover it."

"You're lying." The words came out before thought. "You're a charm-worker facing drowning, and you want me to doubt my own—"

"I'm the last person in this city who wants anything from you." He said it without heat. That was the worst part. He said it the way the tide says it, the way the deep water says it, with no interest whatsoever in winning. "Ask them yourself. You've got iron. Iron remembers. Go on."

Her baton was already in her hand. She didn't remember drawing it. Standard issue, cold-forged, blessed at the Hall of Tides, carried through thirteen years of arrests—and now, held over the dry fountain of a drowned market hall, it was *singing*. Faintly. A thread of tone rising up her arm like a shiver, and threaded inside the tone, impossibly, a voice.

Not her sister's. She knew her sister's voice the way she knew her own heartbeat, and this was not it. This was older. Weary. A woman's voice, worn down to almost nothing, repeating the same few words as though she had been repeating them for thirteen years:

*Let me go. Let me go. Let me go.*

Xanthe dropped the baton. It rang on the stone and the sound didn't fade the way sounds should; it spread outward, rippling, and for one vertiginous moment the whole chamber rang with it, walls and arches and the black window of the deep all resonating together, and in that resonance she felt the size of what slept around her. Not ruins. A congregation. Thousands upon thousands of the drowned dead, holding still, holding silence, holding *watch*—and beneath them all, deeper than the deep, something vast turning over in its sleep like a body shifting under blankets.

The star-dead. Pressed against the fallen wards. Waiting.

"They broke it," she said. Her voice came out small. "They broke the covenant, and then they wrote a law saying anyone who noticed was guilty."

"Yes."

"And everyone who ever drowned touching magic—" She thought of the drownings she had attended. She thought of standing at the rail in her gray coat, official witness, watching people sink, believing with her whole heart that she was protecting the city. "Everyone we executed was killed for knowing what your order did."

"My order," Jonah corrected gently. "Yours."

* * *

They did not speak for a long time after that. They climbed. The drowned galleries gave way to higher passages, drier ones, places where the old city's bones rose toward the modern harbor above, and Jonah followed the whispering of his charms while Xanthe followed Jonah, and the space where her certainty had been ached like a missing tooth.

She kept seeing it: the ninth founder, bound into stone or ward or whatever anchors were made of, thirteen years of *let me go* pressed into a length of blessed iron. She kept doing the arithmetic she could not stop herself from doing. If the founders lied about the covenant—if the holiest law of the Wardens was a burial shroud stitched over a murder—then everything else fell in sequence, like dominoes, like masonry, like a tower. Her arrest record. Her sister's trial. The look on Lira's face when the verdict was read, which had not been fear, Xanthe realized now. It had been pity. Lira had known something. Lira had tried to tell her, in the visiting cell, in the last ten minutes they'd ever been given: *the wards aren't what they say, August, ask about the ninth—*

And Xanthe had reported the visit as an attempted recruitment.

She stopped walking. The passage was narrow here, lit by Jonah's lantern, dripping. Ahead of her, the charm-carver paused and looked back, waiting, patient as deep water.

"My sister," Xanthe said. "When I took her in. She told me the wards were failing. She told me someone had to hold them, and asked me why the Wardens never said *who*." Her throat worked. "They bound her anyway. And I signed the warrant."

Jonah said nothing. He was good at that, she was learning—at letting silence do work that words would only bruise.

"If what the dead showed you is true," she said, "then there's no such thing as a lawful Warden. There's just us. Guards on a lie, keeping the door shut on a prisoner." She looked down at her hands, at the calluses from the baton, from the manacles. "Thirteen years I thought I was protecting this city from sorcery. I was helping bury a woman alive."

"Then help unbury her."

It was so simple it was obscene. She laughed—one sharp note with no joy in it—and started walking again, because if she stopped moving she would come apart, and the Spire was still ahead, somewhere beneath the harbor's heart, and the moon overhead was already waxing toward its thirteenth turn.

* * *

They came up at last through a hatch into the salt-rotted underside of Gullmarket pier, gray daylight leaking down around them, the ordinary noises of the terrified city filtering through the planks—cries, boots, a crier announcing curfew in the flat voice of martial law. Above, through gaps in the boards, Xanthe could see the harbor itself.

Or where the harbor should have been.

The water over the drowned heart of the city was wrong. It stood too still, a circle of glass-smooth calm amid the chop, half a mile wide, and in its center the surface dimpled downward very slightly, as though something enormous beneath it breathed in and had not yet breathed out. Fishermen would have called it bad luck. Sailors would have gone around. Xanthe had trained her whole life to see such places as wounds where sorcery leaked into the world.

Now she saw it for what the dead said it was: a doorway, held shut by a dying woman, thirteen turns past due.

"She's still there," Jonah said softly. His charms had gone quiet, all at once, the way birds go quiet before weather. "At the center. The others won't come near. Whatever holds the Spire holds them too." He glanced at her sideways, careful. "The dead want to take me there before the turn completes. They say I can reforge what was broken."

"How?"

"I don't know yet." He turned the bone charm at his wrist, a little boat, carved for a boy lost off the breakwater years ago. "But they say the cost is written into it. Covenants always cost."

Above them, a bell began to ring—not the curfew bell but the deeper one, the Founders' Bell in the Hall of Tides, rung only twice in living memory. Xanthe knew what it meant. Every warden knew. It meant the High Warden had declared the thirteenth turn a state of siege. It meant lists would be drawn. Names would be read at the rail.

It meant drownings.

She gripped the edge of the hatch and stared up through the boards at the still, breathing circle of water, and made herself say the words aloud, because saying them made them real, and real things could be acted upon.

"The founders lied. My order is the lie's keeper. And the woman anchoring the Spire has been screaming for thirteen years while we sang hymns about mercy." She looked at Jonah. "Tell your dead we'll need a road to the Spire. Tell them a Warden is coming to open a door."

Somewhere far beneath the harbor, in the place where the water stood still, Xanthe would have sworn—later, telling Maren, telling Pip, telling no one—that something answered. Not a voice. Just the faintest shift in the deep, like a sleeper turning toward a name.
