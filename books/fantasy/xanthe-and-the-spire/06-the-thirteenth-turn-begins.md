## 6. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The moon began to die on a Tuesday, the way most terrible things in Vellharbor did—quietly, bureaucratically, with a proclamation nailed to the customs house before dawn.

Jonah read it by the light of a charm he'd carved that morning, a stub of whalebone no bigger than his thumb, and even as he parsed the Warden script—*By order of High Warden Aldous Vey, the city of Vellharbor is placed under martial law until the restoration of celestial order*—he could feel the bone humming against his palm like a struck tuning fork. The dead were agitated. All of them. The drowned of five centuries pressed against the thin wall between worlds, and their voices had stopped being whispers.

They were counting.

*Thirteen,* they said, over and over, in a hundred drowned tongues. *Thirteen. Thirteen. Thirteen.*

He folded the proclamation back along its creases and looked up at the sky, at the hole where the moon's final sliver hung like a paring of fingernail, and understood what the dead had been trying to tell him since the stars went out. The thirteenth turn wasn't coming. It was here. And when the moon finished dying and began again—if it began again—the wards would not just stay broken. They would open.

* * *

Maren laid it out for all of them that night in the Undertow, in the low-ceilinged chamber she called the chart room, where the walls wept brine and the maps on them were older than the Wardens' charter.

"Here's what my tides are telling me." She spread her remaining hand flat on a chart of the harbor, and the water stains on the parchment moved—actually moved, crawling into new shapes as she spoke. "The moon dies in nine days. Old covenant held the star-dead out through the turning. No covenant—" She lifted her hand. The stains kept crawling. "Nothing holds them. Nothing at all."

"They'll come through the wards," Xanthe said. She stood with her arms crossed, warden-stance even now, even disgraced, though Jonah noticed she'd stopped wearing her badge. "Where they're weakest. Where they broke."

"The harbor mouth," Jonah said. "And the Spire. The Spire worst of all. That's the door they were meant to guard from the other side."

Pip sat on the floor gnawing a heel of bread, and the child said, without looking up, "I can feel them pushing. Like dogs at a gate." Pip was eleven, maybe twelve, and had walked through three locked ward-lines that week just to steal an apple, and said things like this the way other children talked about weather.

Nobody laughed. Nobody had laughed much in nine days.

"So we run," said Tam Broadstairs, who ran cargo through the Undertow and had opinions about everything. "There are roads east. Roads south. The Wardens can't watch everything."

"The Wardens aren't watching anything," Maren said grimly. "That's the problem. Come see."

She led them up through the warren's twisting throat to a grate that opened beneath Fisherman's Walk, and Jonah pressed his eye to the iron and looked out at a Vellharbor he didn't recognize.

Scaffolds. Not gallows—scaffolds, built over the harbor itself at six points along the quay, platforms jutting out over black water, each with a chair bolted to its end, each chair fitted with manacles and a canvas hood hanging beside it like a scarecrow's spare head. Warden lanterns burned green at every corner of the market district. Soldiers—not wardens, soldiers, the city garrison in Warden livery now—marched in pairs, checking papers, stopping anyone whose hands were stained or scarred in ways that might suggest craft.

At the center scaffold, facing the crowd-gaps where thousands would stand, hung a painted board:

**BY THE GRACE OF ORDER: PURGATION AT EACH TURNING OF THE MOON. MAGIC IS DROWNING. DROWNING IS MERCY.**

"They're not hunting us anymore," Xanthe said quietly. "They're culling. Anyone they suspect. Anyone denounced. Anyone convenient." Her voice did something Jonah had never heard it do—it cracked, just slightly, on *convenient.* "This is what my sister's arrest fed. This is what it grew into."

"It's not your doing," Jonah started, but she shook her head once, hard, and he let it go. Some debts you couldn't argue a person out of. He knew. He carried his own.

* * *

Three nights later, the Wardens came down into the Undertow.

Not for Jonah—for Sefa.

Sefa was a net-mender, sixty years old, half-blind, who'd lived in the warren since before Maren claimed it and who mended charms the way old women knitted: without thinking, without harm, without ever once calling it magic. She'd fixed Jonah's first carving knife handle when he was nineteen and new to the tunnels. She called him *little fish* and slipped him candied ginger when he looked thin.

A neighbor two levels up—a neighbor, one of their own—had sold her name to the Wardens for a pardon and two marks silver. Perhaps she'd once been seen whispering over a knot. Perhaps she'd simply owed someone money. In Vellharbor now, it hardly mattered which.

Jonah heard it first in the bones: a chorus rising toward panic, the drowned dead crying out in voices that scraped his skull raw, and then boots, many boots, on the stairs above the chart room, and green light spilling down the tunnel mouths like poison poured into water.

"They're in the lower passages," he said, and the word *they* was barely out before the screaming started.

What happened in the next quarter hour, Jonah would remember forever in pieces, because the whole was unbearable. Maren standing in a flooded corridor with her one hand raised and the tide itself rising to meet her, slamming three wardens off their feet with a wave that smelled of deep ocean and old anger. Xanthe moving through the dark with a stolen truncheon, precise and terrible, taking the legs out of men she'd trained alongside. Pip, small and quick as an eel, darting through the ward-line the invaders had thrown across the escape tunnel—a line meant to trap magic-workers inside—and opening it from the far side like a child unhooking a latch.

And Sefa. Sefa, who wouldn't run. Who stood in her own doorway with her arthritic hands open at her sides and said, "I've done nothing," in the same tone she'd used on Jonah a hundred times, mild and certain, and the wardens took her anyway because mercy was a rope and a hood and cold water, and their charter said so.

Jonah fought too. He'd never fought in his life; his survival had always been silence and smallness. But when a warden grabbed Pip by the collar and raised a weighted baton, something in Jonah's chest tore loose, and he hit the man with his carving knife's pommel and then with his fists and then with his knee, and the sounds he made weren't words but the dead were shouting through him, *thirteen, thirteen*, and the warden went down bleeding and Jonah stood over him shaking.

In the end they saved almost everyone. Almost.

They saved everyone except Sefa.

* * *

They executed her at dawn on the great central scaffold, publicly, as the law now required, so the city could watch.

The Undertow emptied onto Fisherman's Walk in ones and twos, disguised, furious, forbidden by Maren to do anything but witness—*if we riot they'll drown twenty, not one*—and Jonah stood in the gray morning crowd with Xanthe's grip bruising his wrist and watched them carry the old woman up the scaffold steps.

She was so small. That was the thing that broke something open in him. She was so small, and her blind eyes turned uselessly toward the crowd as if she could still find the faces of people she loved, and the hood went over her head, and the manacles closed around wrists that had spent sixty years making beautiful useful harmless things.

The High Warden himself presided. Aldous Vey stood at the scaffold rail in white and gold, and his voice carried across the water without effort, warm as a hearth, reasonable as arithmetic. He spoke of celestial disorder. Of hidden corruptions. Of the necessity of sacrifice so that the many might be kept safe. He spoke of mercy.

Then they tipped the chair.

The splash was very small. That was the other thing. A whole life, sixty years of ginger candy and mended nets and kindness freely given, and the sound it made ending was smaller than a dropped bucket.

Jonah felt her arrive among the dead before he heard her—one more voice joining the chorus that never stopped now, that filled every waking hour, the drowned pressing closer as the moon thinned. But hers was different. Hers wasn't confusion or rage or the endless bewildered grief of the wrongfully killed.

Hers was calm. And it found him in the crowd, across four hundred bodies, as if she could see him after all, and it said:

*Little fish. Don't you dare hide now.*

* * *

That night the Undertow burned quiet as a held breath. Maren drank alone. Pip cried themselves to sleep in Xanthe's lap, and Xanthe sat perfectly still for hours, letting them, staring at nothing.

Jonah sat at the chart table with the whalebone charm in his fist and the whole drowned choir singing through it, and he finally understood what the fear in his chest had been trying to become.

It had been trying to become anger. Clean anger. The kind that points somewhere.

"Hiding is over," he said aloud, and Maren looked up, and Xanthe looked up, and even Tam stopped pretending to inventory crates. "You all know what I am by now. What I'm hearing. The Spire is the door, and I'm—" He swallowed. The truth was enormous and he made himself say it anyway. "I'm the key. The last heir of the covenant. The dead have been saying it for weeks. If nobody reforges it by the turning, everything out there comes through, and Vey will keep drowning grandmothers until then because drowning is the only law he has left."

"You'll die," Xanthe said flatly. "Going to the Spire is dying with extra steps."

"I might die staying here, when the wards fall." He set the charm on the table. It glowed faintly, and the glow pulsed like a heartbeat, like something alive and impatient. "But I won't die hiding. Sefa didn't hide. Sixty years she did nothing but mend, and they put her in a chair in front of the whole city." His voice roughened. "So no. I'm done being small. The moon turns in eight days. We go to the Spire, we reforge the covenant, and I want Vey to choke on it."

Silence. Then Maren grinned, slow and salt-hard, and said, "Been waiting nine days for somebody to say it out loud."

Xanthe said nothing. She was looking at the map, at the drowned old city between them and the Spire, at the flooded streets and sunken districts where the tide-craft and ward-walking would matter more than swords. Jonah watched her jaw work through everything she wasn't saying yet—about her sister, about Vey, about whatever she'd learned that she hadn't told them.

Finally she looked up, and there was something in her face that hadn't been there since the night she'd caught him and let go: decision.

"There's something I need to tell you all before we take another step," she said. "About why Vey really keeps the Spire guarded—and who's still inside it."

Outside, far above, the last sliver of the moon began, imperceptibly, to turn.
