## 7. Sister of Salt and Ash

The summons came at dusk, folded into the beak of a gull that should not have been able to find her.

Xanthe stood on the roof of the Undertow's eastern stair, where the harbor fog gathered thickest and Maren's wards hummed a low, salt-sweet note beneath her boots. The bird landed on the rail with unnatural precision, its eyes flat as coins, and when she took the paper from its leg it watched her with the patience of something that had been sent, not born. The seal was black wax pressed with the sigil of the High Wardency: a tower above crossed waves.

Her name was written inside in a hand she knew too well. *Corvane. Come alone. Come now.* No signature. No signature was needed. Only one man in Vellharbor wrote his commands as if they were already accomplished facts—High Warden Aldous Vey, whose word had once moved her sister to tears and had never, in thirteen years, moved Xanthe to anything but obedience.

She should have burned it. She should have carried it down to Maren and let the tide-witch feed it to the deep water where such things belonged. Instead she read it three times, feeling the old architecture of her life groan back into place around her—the ranks, the rules, the clean geometry of duty—and hated how much of her still fit inside it.

Below her, through the grate, she could hear the sounds of the warren: Pip laughing at something, that wild bark of a laugh, and Jonah's quieter voice answering, and the clatter of bone charms being sorted into piles by lamplight. A family of strays, Maren called them. Xanthe had spent six days among them and still flinched at the word *family*, the way a burn scar flinches at heat.

She went anyway. Of course she went. That was the thing about leashes—they only worked if you forgot you were wearing one, and Aldous Vey had been tightening hers for thirteen years without ever letting her feel the pull.

* * *

Wardenhold had not changed. That was the cruelty of it.

Xanthe walked up the processional stair between rows of white-lanterned pillars, past young wardens who saluted her out of habit and then looked confused about why they had, because her badge had been struck from the rolls two winters ago and her name spoken only in the tone reserved for cautionary tales. The granite underfoot was worn smooth by generations of disciplined feet. The air smelled of lamp oil, ink, and cold stone—the smell of her entire adult life—and beneath it, faintly, unmistakably, the sea.

Vey's office sat at the top of the tower, the only room in Wardenhold with a view of the drowned heart of the harbor. When the door closed behind her, Xanthe understood immediately why he had chosen it. He wanted her to look.

"Sit," he said.

She stayed standing. "I'm not warden anymore."

"You are whatever I say you are." Vey was older than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply never let herself see it before—the gray creeping through his close-cropped beard, the fine tremor in his left hand that he stilled against the desk when he noticed her noticing. His eyes were the same, though. Pale, patient, and utterly without warmth, like light off a frozen pond. "You've been busy, Corvane. Harboring a sorcerer. Consorting with smugglers. Fraternizing with a tide-witch who owes the Wardency four sentences of drowning." He laced his fingers. "Tell me why I haven't already sent a full company to drag you all out by your hair."

"Because you don't know where the Undertow is," Xanthe said, "or you'd have done it already."

A thin smile. "There's my best student."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. "Don't call me that. You summoned me. Say what you summoned me to say."

For a long moment he simply looked at her, and Xanthe felt the terrible old sensation of being weighed by him—measured against some ledger only he could see. Then he opened the drawer of his desk and set something on the wood between them.

A ring of keys made of gray coral.

She knew them. Every warden knew them by reputation: the Keys to the Spire, forged in the first years after the founding, said to open doors that no longer existed in any map of Vellharbor. They were supposed to have been melted down generations ago. Supposed to have been a story.

"You've heard the dead singing," Vey said quietly. It wasn't a question. "Through the boy's charms, through the fallen wards, through the water itself. Everyone in this city with a drop of sensitivity has heard them. What you haven't heard is *why*." He turned the keyring slowly, and the firelight ran along the coral like blood along a blade. "Thirteen years ago, the covenant between the living realm and the star-dead began to fail. Not broke, Corvane—*began to fail*. There is a difference. A covenant is not a wall. It is a living thing. And a living thing must be anchored, or it drifts."

"What does that have to do with me?"

"Everything." He said it softly, almost kindly, and that softness was worse than any threat. "Your sister did not die in the drowning pools."

The room went very still. Somewhere below, the harbor boomed against the seawall, patient as a heartbeat.

"We executed no one that season," Vey continued, watching her the way a surgeon watches the site of an incision. "The records you were permitted to read—the sentence, the date, the witnesses—that was for the public. For order. Your sister was a powerful vessel, Corvane. Strong-willed, sensitive, untrained, and utterly alone after her own family condemned her." He paused, letting each word land. "We needed an anchor for the failing covenant. A soul bound into the Spire holds the boundary taut. It buys time. Thirteen turns of time, so far."

Xanthe heard herself speak from a great distance away. "You put her in the Spire."

"The Spire accepted her. That is more accurate." He said it with the mild precision of a man correcting grammar. "And she has held. Longer than most anchors last. But the star-dead press harder every turn, and the anchor thins. The voices you hear in the water, Corvane, are the sound of the seam tearing. In less than one moon-turn, there will be nothing left holding, and everything your order built will drown in things that remember being stars."

The floor seemed to tilt. Xanthe gripped the back of the chair and thought of Sera—her sister's crooked smile, the way she used to whistle through the gap in her teeth while braiding Xanthe's hair, the last look she had given her across the courtroom, betrayed by the one person who was supposed to stand beside her. Thirteen years Xanthe had carried that look like a stone in her chest. Thirteen years believing Sera had died hating her, when the truth was that Sera had died slowly, in the dark, at the bottom of the harbor, *holding up the sky for the people who put her there.*

"You arrested her," Xanthe whispered. "I handed her to you. And you—" Her voice failed and rebuilt itself into something colder. "You let me believe I killed her."

"I gave you grief," Vey said. "Grief makes obedient soldiers. You became the finest warden of your generation on the strength of it. Don't waste it now by turning sentimental."

Every instinct she had honed in fifteen years of service told her to draw steel. Her hand actually moved toward the knife at her hip before she caught it, forced it flat against her thigh. *He wants that,* she realized. *He wants the scene. The disgraced warden striking at the High Warden in his tower. He wants a reason.*

So instead she asked, in a voice she barely recognized as her own: "Why tell me? Why now?"

"Because you're going to bring me the boy."

There it was. The leash, drawn tight at last, so smooth she hadn't even felt the loop go over her head.

"Jonah Quintana is the last living heir of the original covenant-bearers," Vey said, rising, coming around the desk, and Xanthe made herself hold still as he stopped within arm's reach. "His bloodline can reforge the binding without an anchor-soul. Without another *Sera*. Do you understand what I'm offering you, Corvane? Deliver him to Wardenhold before the thirteenth turn completes, and we end this properly. We save the city. And in exchange—" his voice dropped, gentle as rot "—I give you absolution. Full restoration. Your badge, your rank, your name cleared. And access to the Spire, to sit with your sister's spirit while the new binding sets. To tell her whatever you've spent thirteen years wanting to say. She'll hear you, Corvane. Anchors hear everything."

Xanthe's eyes burned. She would not blink. If she blinked she would weep, and if she wept she would drown here in this room with its worn granite and its lamp oil and its careful, careful cruelty.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the boy dies in the pools, the Undertow burns, the tide-witch gets her fourth sentence, the child gets sent to the foundling houses to be made useful, and your sister dissolves screaming into the harbor when the anchor fails." He returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands, a man concluding pleasant business. "You have until the moon reaches half-dark. I know where you sleep, Corvane. I have always known where you sleep."

* * *

She didn't remember walking down. She came back to herself at the base of the processional stair, standing in the white lantern-light with the taste of salt and ash in her mouth, her hands shaking so badly she had to press them against her thighs.

They had told her, at her swearing-in, that the Wardens were the wall between Vellharbor and the dark. She had believed it with her whole body, the way you believe a thing when you are nineteen and grieving and grateful to be given somewhere to put your pain. She had arrested neighbors. She had broken a tide-witch's hand. She had given them her sister and called it justice.

And behind all of it, all along, the holiest law of her order had been a machine for feeding souls to a stone.

*Xanthe.* The voice rose up out of the harbor fog around her, thin and familiar and impossible, and she went rigid. It sounded the way Sera had sounded at nine years old, calling her home for supper across the rooftops of the Tidewalk district. *Xanthe, come home.*

"Sera?" she whispered to the empty street. The fog said nothing more. Perhaps it had never said anything at all. Perhaps grief was simply doing what Vey had always used it for—making her obedient, making her pliable, making hers.

No. She straightened. Because there was one thing Vey had not understood, one thing he could not understand, a man who had spent his whole life mistaking fear for loyalty: he had told her the truth. After thirteen years of lies, he had finally, stupidly, handed her the truth—and truths, once loose, do not go back in their drawers.

He thought the leash was on her neck. But Xanthe had spent six days among strays, eating smuggler bread and listening to a boy sing to the dead, and she had learned something the Wardens' granite halls had never taught her: a leash runs both ways, if you're willing to be pulled hard enough.

She turned toward the harbor, toward the low roofs where the Undertow breathed beneath the waterline, and began to walk. Behind her, the drowned heart of the city waited in its cage of dark water, and somewhere inside it, a girl who had been nineteen forever was holding up a sky of falling stars.

"Hold on, Sera," Xanthe said to the fog. "I'm coming. And I'm bringing the storm with me."
