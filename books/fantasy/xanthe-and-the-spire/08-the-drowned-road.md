## 8. The Drowned Road

The old city began where the harbor stairs ended, and it did not forgive.

Jonah had lived in Vellharbor his whole life without ever once thinking about what lay beneath the water he could see from the fish-market steps. Now, standing at the top of a drowned boulevard with the tide pulled back to its lowest ebb in a generation, he understood that the city he knew was only the newest skin stretched over an older body. The water had withdrawn like a sheet dragged off a corpse, and what it revealed was Vellharbor's grandmother: streets of black basalt, doorways tall enough for giants, lampposts furred with barnacle and weed, all of it slick and gleaming under the light of no stars at all. The sky above the drowned road was a flat, starless iron color, and the moon hung in it swollen and wrong, already beginning its thirteenth turn.

"It's down there," Maren said, jerking her chin toward the dark mouth of the boulevard. She stood on the second stair with her boots off and her one hand pressed flat against the wet stone, reading the water through her palm the way other people read a page. "Six hours of ebb, maybe five. When she turns, she turns fast, and she won't care who's standing in her way." Her eyes were pale as sea-glass, and they didn't blink often. "Stay out of the channels. Stay off the glass. And if you hear singing—"

"We know," Pip said, bouncing on his heels at the bottom of the stairs. The child had a coil of rope over one shoulder nearly as big as he was and an expression of pure delight, as though the flooded ruins of a dead city were a festival ground. "Don't sing along."

"Don't sing along," Maren agreed grimly. "Last time I came down here, a man heard his mother calling from a doorway and went to her. Found him three days later, smiling, with lungs full of silt."

* * *

They went down into the old city single file, because the road allowed nothing wider.

Pip walked first, and Jonah watched him work and understood, finally, why Maren called him a ward-walker instead of a thief. The old city was stitched through with wards—the founders' wards, the ones that had held the covenant for centuries before they broke—and to Jonah's charms they felt like a low, continuous ache, a headache made of law. But Pip moved through them as if they were curtains in a doorway. He would stop, tilt his head, squint at some patch of empty air that shimmered only when he looked at it, and then step sideways, or duck, or walk straight through a place where Jonah's bones said death waited. Where Pip passed, the ache eased, and the others followed in the wake of him like ducklings after a gull.

Xanthe walked last. She had given up her Warden grays two days ago, burned them in a barrel behind the Undertow, but she still moved like a warden: weight forward, hand near the knife at her hip, eyes doing constant arithmetic about exits and angles. She carried most of their supplies in a frame pack—salt pork, hardtack, lamps and oil, Maren's tide-charts copied onto oiled cloth—and every time Jonah glanced back at her, she was watching the water line on the walls, measuring how fast it fell, or didn't.

The drowned road itself was paved in something that might have been glass. It ran arrow-straight between the ruined facades toward a darkness at the heart of the harbor that none of them named, because naming it made it more real. The Spire's crown still broke the surface a mile out—a jagged black tooth in the middle of the bay—but down here, at ebb, its roots were exposed for the first time in living memory, and the dead wanted Jonah to look at them so badly that his charms had not stopped whispering since the stairs.

*Come down,* the drowned sang, soft as sediment settling. *Come down, little heir. We kept your seat warm.*

He kept walking.

They lost the first of their supplies an hour in, at a place where the boulevard crossed a canal and the bridge had fallen. Pip found a path across the rubble, tested each stone with a kick, waved them forward—and then the far side of the crossing simply wasn't there anymore. Not collapsed; *removed*, as though a bite had been taken out of the world. One moment Xanthe was stepping over a fallen arch with the pack balanced on her shoulders, and the next there was a gap six feet wide where stone should have been, and the pack was falling, and Xanthe threw herself flat and caught its strap with two fingers at the lip of the gap, and below the gap there was no bottom, just water-dark and a sound like breathing.

"Old magic," Maren said quietly, when they'd hauled the pack up and everyone had stopped shaking except Pip, who thought it was wonderful. "The founders' workings are coming apart. Whole pieces of the old city just... gone. Eaten." She looked out at the darkness ahead, and for the first time since Jonah had known her, she looked afraid. "It's worse than the charts say. The Spire isn't just failing. It's *starving*. And it'll eat whatever's nearest."

They redistributed the load, which meant Xanthe carried less and Jonah carried more, which meant Jonah walked slower, which meant he had more time to listen to the dead, who were getting louder.

* * *

They lost the lamp-oil in the third hour, and nearly lost Pip.

It happened in a district that must once have been grand—wide avenues, colonnades, the shells of mansions with their roofs caved in like broken skulls. Something lived in there. Jonah never saw it whole; he saw a suggestion of movement in a doorway, a long gray limb withdrawing like a heron's neck, and he heard—not with his ears, but in the marrow of him—a note so hungry that every charm on his body went silent at once, the way birds go silent when a hawk crosses the sun.

Pip, ranging ahead as always, stepped through a curtain-ward into a mansion's flooded atrium, and the thing in there noticed him.

What saved him was that the thing was not hunting flesh. It was hunting *light*. It poured out of the atrium window like smoke poured backward, a shape of absence, and it went for the lanterns—Maren's storm-lantern, the hooded oil-lamp, the little stub of candle Pip kept in his pocket "for luck"—and drank them. All three flames died in the same instant, and the dark that followed was total, the kind of dark that has weight, and in it Jonah heard Pip scream—not fear, exactly, but outrage, a child's fury at being touched by something cold.

Then Xanthe's voice cut through the black, steady as a drill-sergeant's: "Hands. Find hands. Nobody moves alone."

Jonah crawled toward the screaming and found a small flailing body and held on. Maren found Xanthe by grabbing an ankle. They formed a chain in the absolute dark while somewhere close the hungry thing gulped down the last of their oil and drifted away, unsatisfied, looking for brighter meals.

When Maren coaxed a spark from her fire-kit and lit a fresh candle stub, they took inventory by its guttering light. Both lanterns empty. Oil gone. Four candles left. The Spire's roots were visible now against the faint gray of the open water, a quarter mile off, black and vast.

"Half our light," Xanthe said. Her face was a mask, but Jonah had learned to read her masks, and this one was anger at herself. "I should have spread it differently. Carried it separately."

"You carried it because you don't trust anyone else to carry it right," Maren said, not unkindly. "That's not a flaw tonight."

"It is tonight."

"No," Jonah said.

They all looked at him. He almost never spoke on the march; his voice had gone rusty from disuse and from listening. He cleared his throat.

"It would be gone either way. That thing eats light. If we'd split it, we'd have lost it slower and been strung out thinner when it happened." He paused. "You keep us alive, Xanthe. You've kept us alive since the stairs. Stop auditing yourself for crimes you haven't committed yet."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, then a rueful ghost of a smile. "You've been saving that up?"

"Since the canal."

Pip snorted. Maren laughed, short and rough, and blew out the candle to save it, and they went on in the dark with one hand each on the shoulder of the person ahead, four souls roped together by touch, walking the drowned road toward the place where the world's oldest promise was dying.

* * *

In the fourth hour, the dead asked Jonah to stop, and he did.

It was a plaza—Maren said it had been the founders' first temple district, back when the covenant was young—and the water had left it swept clean, the basalt pale as bone under the lightless sky. In the center stood a statue so eroded it was less a figure than a gesture: someone with arms raised, holding up something that time had eaten away.

Jonah stopped in front of it, and the voices rose around him like a tide coming in.

*Here. Here, look. Remember with us.*

And they showed him.

Not images—he'd never gotten images from the dead, only impressions, pressure, meaning—but something close enough. Thirteen generations of faces, warden-faces in gray, standing in this plaza under a sky full of stars, swearing the covenant aloud: *the living realm keeps the gate, the star-dead keep the silence, and neither shall hold the key alone.* And then, overlaid on the oath like blood on snow, another memory: the same plaza, thirteen turns ago, warden-faces again, and this time they were not swearing. They were *taking*. A key that should have been shared, seized entire. An anchor bound where a partner should have stood. A lie written into law so deep that the law's own enforcers no longer knew it was a lie.

Beside him, he heard Xanthe's breath catch. She was looking at the statue too—at the raised arms, at the missing thing they had held—and he understood that she was seeing the same theft he was, arriving at the same terrible country by a different road.

"The holiest law," she said quietly. "'No soul shall hold the covenant's key alone.' They wrote that law *after* they broke it. To hide the breaking." Her jaw worked. "Every drowning. Every arrest. My sister—" She stopped. Pressed the back of her hand to her mouth for a moment. Then lowered it. "We have to hurry."

They hurried.

* * *

They lost Maren in the fifth hour, for eleven minutes that felt like eleven years.

The final approach to the Spire was not a road at all but a causeway of stepping-stones across a channel of black water that even at ebb still ran deep and quick between the ruins. Pip danced across it like it was a game, calling back which stones were true and which were "lying stones" that looked solid and weren't. Xanthe went second with the pack. Jonah went third, charms chattering against his chest like teeth.

Maren went last, because someone had to watch the water.

The turn came early.

There was no warning in the sky—the sky had given up warnings when the stars died—but Maren felt it through her bare foot on the stone, and shouted, and the channel woke. The water didn't rise so much as *reach*, a gray arm swinging up between the stones, and it took her at the waist and pulled. Jonah turned in time to see her go under—one-handed, sixty years old, dragged sideways into the dark between a lying stone and a true one.

What happened next happened fast and without any decision in it. Jonah dropped to his knees on the causeway. Xanthe screamed his name—she was reaching for the rope, for anything—but Jonah was already putting his bare hand into the water, into the cold that pulled, and he did the thing he had spent five years doing in alleys and sickrooms, the thing that was the only prayer he knew.

He listened.

And the drowned of the old city, who had sung to him since the night the stars went out, who had led him here street by street and stone by stone—they answered. The channel shuddered. The pull faltered, confused, as though the water itself had been asked a question it couldn't refuse. And Maren's hand burst up out of the black water and closed around Jonah's wrist with a grip like a dock-cleat, and he hauled, and Xanthe hauled, and Pip, shrieking encouragement, hauled the rope, and they dragged her out onto the true stone coughing seawater and laughing, actually laughing, a wild cracked sound.

"Knew you'd come in handy," she gasped, and gripped his arm, and that was all anyone said about it.

But Jonah knelt there a moment longer, water dripping from his fingers, and understood with perfect clarity that he had just spent something. Not coins. Not blood. Attention. Favor. The dead had spent their strength pulling the water's grip loose, and the ledger between him and them had grown heavier, and the whispers in his charms had gone quiet in a way that felt less like rest than like conservation.

*Save yourself,* they murmured, very faint now. *Save yourself, and we will not have died for nothing.*

* * *

They made the Spire's threshold as the last candle died.

Its roots rose above them like the ribs of a god, and between the roots was a door that had not opened in thirteen turns, and before it, a landing of black stone where the founders' oath had been sworn and unsworn. The moon above was a fat white wound, three-quarters through its final turn. Behind them, across the drowned city, the waters were already turning, gathering, beginning the slow inexorable climb that would swallow the causeway and the plaza and everything else.

They had lost both lanterns, the oil, half the food, and nearly Maren. They had gained a quarter-mile of impossible road, a truth about the founding of the Wardens, and a door.

Xanthe set down the pack and looked at Jonah in the last gray light, and whatever she saw in his face made her go still. "You've known the whole way down, haven't you. What it costs."

Jonah put his hand over the charms at his chest—the bone-carved bits of quiet company that had been his livelihood, his camouflage, his family, for five years. The voices within them were faint as embers under ash.

"When I reforge the covenant," he said, "the key passes through me. All of it. The dead, the whispers, the voices—" He shrugged, as if it were a small thing, a coat taken off and hung by the door. "After tomorrow I won't hear them anymore. Ever."

Pip's face crumpled. Maren reached out and gripped his shoulder hard, saying nothing, which from Maren was a eulogy.

Only Xanthe held his eyes. "You could let it fail," she said, very low. "Let the star-dead pour through. Let the Wardens choke on their own lie. No one would blame—"

"I'd blame me." He smiled at her, tired and certain. "Besides. Someone has to teach the new order to listen. Just... not with these ears."

Xanthe looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned to the door, drew her knife, and set its point against the ancient seal.

The seal flared—not gold, not silver, but the pale remembered light of stars that no longer existed—and the door groaned open on a darkness that breathed.

And from inside, from the heart of the anchor-stone, a voice that Jonah recognized with a jolt like falling—the voice from the charms, from the raid, from the night the wards broke—spoke his name.
