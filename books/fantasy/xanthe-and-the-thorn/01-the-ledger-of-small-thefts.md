## 1. The Ledger of Small Thefts

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers in a cellar beneath a shop that sold nothing.

That was the point. A shop that sold nothing attracted no customers, no thieves, no tax assessors, no attention at all. The sign above the door read *Marrow & Sons, Purveyors of Fine Nothing*, and the city of Grisaille had walked past it for sixty years without once wondering what a purveyor of fine nothing actually purveyed. The answer was: everything that couldn't be owned any other way.

I sat at my desk in the cellar with the lamp turned low and the ledger open before me, and I did what I had done every night for nine years. I catalogued thefts.

Not the ordinary kind. Ordinary theft—coins, candlesticks, the silver off a bishop's table—was handled upstairs by men with knives and bad manners. My department was the other kind. The kind you couldn't fence and couldn't miss until it was gone. The kind that left a person standing in their own kitchen at midnight, weeping over a photograph of someone they could no longer name, holding a grief with a hole in the middle of it where the reason used to be.

We stole memories. We sold them to people who wanted them, or wanted them gone, depending on which side of the trade you stood on. And every transaction, every vial, every buyer and seller and price, went into the ledger under my hand.

Entry 4,417. *One summer afternoon, age eleven, riverbank, first kiss. Seller: unnamed gentleman, third district. Buyer: a widow who wished to remember being loved before she forgot how it felt. Price: forty crowns and one honest smile.*

I wrote it out in my small, even script. I dated it. I initialed it. E.T., clerk of the second vault, keeper of the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things.

There were three rules to my work, and Corvane had taught me all of them before I was old enough to understand why they mattered.

Rule one: write everything down. Memory is the only currency that can't be counted twice, so the ledger is the guild's whole fortune. Lose the ledger, lose the guild.

Rule two: never taste the stock. A clerk who drinks the memories she catalogs starts wanting things. Wanting is how clerks die.

Rule three—the rule above all the others, the rule Corvane had pressed into me like a seal into wax when I was twelve years old and still flinching at loud noises:

*Be forgotten, and stay alive.*

* * *

At half past eleven I finished Entry 4,419—a man's memory of his mother's voice, sold to pay a gambling debt, bought by an actor preparing for a role—and I set down my pen and allowed myself the night's one indulgence.

I took out my own page.

Every clerk has a page in the master ledger. It's not sentiment; it's accounting. The guild owns a portion of every employee, taken as surety against betrayal, the way a lender holds a deed. Mine was thin compared to most. Nine years of careful living will do that. I had sold nothing since I came to Corvane except the one thing, the big thing, the thing on the first line, written in a younger version of my handwriting that slanted left like it was trying to get away from itself.

*Entry 1. One childhood, complete, ages nought to nine. Contents: one country, one language, one mother, one name. Seller: Edmund Thornbury, aged nine years and four months. Buyer: sealed by order of the guildmaster. Price: her life.*

Her life. That was the joke of it, if jokes lived in ledgers. The price hadn't been her life. The price had been the right to keep living, which is a different commodity entirely, and cheaper.

I read the entry the way you press your tongue to a broken tooth. Not because it hurts good. Because you need to know it's still there.

I remembered almost none of it. That was the arrangement. Ages nought to nine, gone. A country I couldn't name. A language I'd sometimes catch humming at the edge of my ear in markets, in songs, in the vowels of strangers, and feel a homesickness so large and so sourceless it was less an emotion than a weather system. A mother whose face I had reconstructed from nothing, which means I hadn't reconstructed it at all—I just knew there should have been one, the way an amputee knows about the hand.

And a name. My real one. Sold at nine years old, along with everything else, and replaced with the name Corvane gave me when he fished me out of whatever I'd been before. Edmund Thornbury. A made-up name for a made-up person. It fit the way borrowed coats fit: close enough, if nobody looked too hard.

Nobody ever looked too hard. That was the craft of it. Nine years of practice had made me into something the city simply declined to see. Barkeeps lost my order mid-sentence. Landladies forgot I owed rent. Guards waved me through checkpoints they'd have sworn an hour later they'd never manned. I had refined forgettability into an art form, and the art form had one subject, and the subject was me.

It sounds like a curse. Mostly it was a salary.

Because here is what nobody tells you about being unforgettable: unforgettable people get found. Found by creditors, found by enemies, found by the past, which is the worst creditor of all and always collects. But a forgettable person can walk through the world like a draft through a house. Doors open for her that don't know they're opening. She takes what she needs and leaves no more trace than breath on glass.

Be forgotten, stay alive. I had built a whole life inside that sentence. It was a small life. It was also, as far as I could tell, the only kind I was going to get.

* * *

The lamp guttered around midnight, and I trimmed the wick, and that's when I heard the footsteps on the stairs.

Only one person came down those stairs after hours, and he never knocked, because knocking was for people who might be told no. Corvane descended into my cellar the way weather descends on a coastline: total, inevitable, indifferent to what it finds.

He was old the way a fortress is old—not frail, just permanently load-bearing. Grey beard cropped short, hands like knotted rope, eyes that had appraised ten thousand memories and been moved by none of them. He wore his guildmaster's chain of office the way other men wore scars: without thinking about it, and never taking it off.

"Working late," he said. It wasn't a question. Questions implied uncertainty, and Corvane had retired from uncertainty decades ago.

"Catching up." I closed my own page and slid the ledger toward him across the desk, because that was the ritual. Every night, the master reviewed the day's entries. Every night, I watched him read my handwriting and said nothing while he decided whether my work existed.

He read. His finger moved down the columns. Entry 4,417, the riverbank kiss—he grunted, which from Corvane was applause. Entry 4,418, a soldier's memory of a battle he'd sold to stop screaming in his sleep, purchased by a historian who wanted to know what war felt like without the inconvenience of dying in one. Entry 4,419, the mother's voice.

"The actor," Corvane said. "The one who bought the mother's voice."

"What of him?"

"He'll be back within the month. They always are. First they buy a voice, then they want the lullaby that goes with it, then the kitchen it was sung in, then the whole woman entire." He tapped the ledger with one rope-knuckled finger. "Note it. Repeat buyers of grief are our steadiest income. Grief doesn't satisfy. It only opens an appetite."

I noted it. I wrote *probable repeat purchase, monitor* in the margin, and Corvane watched me do it, and there was a moment—there was always a moment—where I felt the shape of what we were to each other settle over the room like dust.

He had raised me. There was no gentler way to put it and no truer one. I had come to him at nine years old, hollowed out, carrying nothing but a name I'd already sold and a terror I couldn't source, and he had fed me, taught me letters, taught me the trade, taught me the three rules. He called me his clerk. Once, when I was younger, I'd caught him calling me something else, quieter, in a room he thought was empty. I'd never learned what the word was. I'd decided it didn't matter.

"You've been steady lately," he said, closing the ledger. "Steady is good. Steady clerks grow old."

"And unsteady ones?"

"Grow interesting. Briefly." He moved toward the stairs, then stopped, halfway, with his back to me. This too was ritual. He always paused on the third step. I had long ago stopped wondering why.

"Edmund." My false name, in his mouth, sounded like a lock turning. "Has anything come in tonight that shouldn't have?"

I thought about it honestly, which is the only way to think when Corvane asks you something. "Nothing unusual. The usual sorrows, the usual appetites."

"Good." He climbed the rest of the stairs and was gone, and the cellar settled back around me like water closing over a stone.

I sat for a while after that. This was the part of the night I never wrote down anywhere, the part that would have embarrassed me if anyone had known it existed: the part where I listened to the silence and tried to decide whether it was peaceful or just empty.

Nine years. Nine years of evenings exactly like this one, and I had gotten so good at being forgotten that sometimes I suspected I'd done the job too well. The barkeep downstairs didn't know my drink. The landlady had recently asked me, with genuine puzzlement, whether I was the one who sublet from myself. Last winter I'd had a fever, and lain alone for six days, and the sum total of human concern generated by my possible death was a note slid under my door asking me to stop coughing after dark.

Chosen erasure, I called it, when I called it anything. Chosen, chosen, chosen. I had picked this. I reminded myself of that the way you remind yourself the stove is hot: often, and right up until the moment you touch it anyway.

Some nights the loneliness got so specific it had a texture. Silk, almost. Cold silk laid over the mouth.

I opened the ledger again. Work was the only conversation I trusted.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when I found the entry that broke my life in half.

I was doing the final reconciliation—that's the term, reconciliation, as though the numbers and the truth were estranged relatives we occasionally forced to share a meal—when my pen stopped moving.

Between Entry 4,419 and the blank lines waiting for tomorrow, there was another entry.

It was in my handwriting.

That was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was the ink—it was dry, set, days old at least, and I reconciled this ledger every single night, and it had not been there last night. The third wrong thing was the date, which was today's date, written in ink that had dried days ago, which is the sort of trick that makes a clerk's spine go cold and stay cold.

But it was the fourth wrong thing that stopped my heart, and I will set it down here exactly as I read it, because some sentences deserve to be preserved the way wounds are preserved: in full, and forever.

*Entry 4,420. One memory, unsold, unowned, returned to stock without transaction. Contents: the night Vellamere burned. Seller: none. Buyer: pending. Note — she will come looking. Let her.*

Vellamere.

The word went through me like a hook through cloth.

I didn't know it. That's what I told myself, sitting alone in the lamplight with my heart slamming. I had never heard the word Vellamere in my life. It meant nothing. It was nothing. Some broker's typo, some stranger's country, some—

Except my hands knew it. That was the horror of the next few minutes. My hands knew it. They had gone still on the desk in a particular way, fingers spread flat, the way hands go still when the rest of the body wants to run, and I recognized the gesture from somewhere I could not name, from some room I had never been in, from some night of smoke and shouting that belonged to a child aged nought to nine, sold and sealed and gone.

*The night Vellamere burned.*

My memory. It had to be. Someone had put a piece of me back into my own ledger—an entry about me, in my own hand, that I had never written—and the entry said *she will come looking,* and the entry was right, God help me, because I was already looking. I was on my feet with the lamp held high, checking the cellar's shadows, checking the stairwell, checking the seams of the room for the intruder who must have sat at my desk, dipped my pen, and written to me in my own voice.

No one. Dust, shelves, vials in their racks, the dead air of a shop that sold nothing.

I came back to the desk and read it again. *Returned to stock.* Which meant somewhere in my vaults—somewhere on these shelves, in these rows of little glass vials with their neat paper labels—there was a memory with no seller and no buyer. A loose thread. A piece of a burned country, waiting.

And underneath it, in that same impossible handwriting, one more line. Smaller. Almost tender. The kind of line a person writes when they want to be believed.

*You were there, Edmund.*

I stood in my cellar for a long time after that, listening to the silence, and for the first time in nine years the silence did not feel empty.

It felt like a country, humming at the edge of my ear, waiting for me to say its name.
