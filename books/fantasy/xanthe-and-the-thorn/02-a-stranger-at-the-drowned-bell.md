## 2. A Stranger at the Drowned Bell

The Drowned Bell sat at the end of Saltrow, where the cobblestones gave up pretending and became mud, and the mud gave up and became the harbor. It was called a tavern the way a drowned man is called a swimmer—technically, and only for as long as no one looked too closely. The ale was gray. The floor was sticky with things I preferred not to inventory. The clientele were dockhands, smugglers, and one customs officer who came every Thursday to be forgotten by everyone including himself, which was the most expensive drink in the house.

I liked it because it was loud.

Loud places are where forgettable people go to hide. In a quiet room, an unremarkable woman is a question: *who is she, why haven't I noticed her before?* In a room like this, she's furniture. She's the third chair at a table of five. She's nobody, and nobody is the safest name in the world.

I had taken my usual corner—the one with a view of both doors and the crack in the wall that whistled when the wind came off the water—and I was doing what I did most nights, which was nothing, magnificently. A half-finished ledger page sat under my hand. Three entries to copy before midnight. The guild expected punctuality even from its ghosts.

Entry forty-one: *One summer afternoon, age six, sold by E.T., purchased by a chandler of Greyharbor. Contents: the smell of beeswax; a woman singing through a wall; the certainty of being loved. Condition: excellent.*

I didn't reread those. There was a rule about it, mine, unwritten, load-bearing. I copied the entries the way a sexton copies names off stones. You keep your eyes on the letters and never on what the letters mean.

The bell above the door rang.

I want to say I noticed her immediately—that some thief's instinct pricked up my spine—but that would be flattering and false. What happened was this: she came in out of the rain and every head in the Drowned Bell turned toward her, which should have been impossible, because I had made sure of it. I'd paid the doorman three coppers to forget any woman matching her description. I'd salted the threshold. I'd done the work.

She walked through all of it like it was weather.

She was maybe fifty, maybe seventy—there's an age past which wet, starving people stop having one—and she moved wrong, favoring her left side, one hand pressed below her ribs. When she lifted it I saw why. The hand came away dark. Not wine-dark. The other kind.

The room went on with its noise. Nobody else saw the blood. That was the part that made my mouth go dry. Blood you can explain—a knife fight, a bad birth, a dozen ordinary tragedies on Saltrow. But blood that only one person in a room can see means the blood has been aimed.

She crossed the floor toward me without hesitating, without asking, without the small social stumbles people perform when they're searching for a stranger. She knew exactly which chair was mine. Which meant someone had told her. Which meant someone who knew me well enough to describe not my face—I barely had one—but my habits, my corners, my hours.

There were perhaps four people alive who could have done that. Two of them were dead. One of them was me.

That left Corvane, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards into the harbor.

"Edmund Thornbury," she said.

Not a question. And here is the thing that turned my blood to cold water: she said it *correctly*. Not the way people said it when they'd read it somewhere—*Thorn-bury*, two clean beats. She swallowed the middle of it, the way you only do if you've heard it spoken aloud, over years, by someone who loved saying it.

Nobody living had ever heard my name spoken with love. I'd bought and sold every witness to the original pronunciation.

"You have the wrong table," I said.

"I have exactly the right table." She lowered herself into the chair across from me, and I heard something inside her click—not a joint. Something looser than bone. Her breath came out in a thin whistle, like the crack in the wall. "You'll want to look busy. Here."

She slid something across the table, and my hands—traitorous, clerk's hands, trained to receive documents—caught it before my mind agreed to.

It was a map. Folded small, soft as cloth from handling, and stained down one entire edge in brown-black. Blood, old and new, layered like varnish. Someone had carried this thing against their body long enough for it to learn their heartbeat.

"Unfold it," she said. "Not here. But hold it. Hold it and tell me you don't feel it."

"I don't feel anything," I said, which was a lie, because I felt everything.

It hummed.

Not sound, exactly. Maps don't hum. But there was a pressure against my palms, a vibration like a struck bell held underwater, and underneath it—underneath it, faint as a voice through a wall—

A woman singing.

I knew that song. I did not know how I knew that song. That was the horror of it: the melody arrived in my head already familiar, worn smooth by repetition, the way a stairwell is worn by feet, and there was no memory attached to it. No kitchen. No voice I could put a face to. Just the song, orphaned, humming in the hollow where a memory should have been, the way wind hums in a chimney when the fire's gone cold.

I knew that hollow. I had spent nine years learning the shape of every hollow in myself, mapping my own absences the way other people map coastlines. This hollow was an old one. Entry thirty-something, probably. Sold young, sold cheap, because childhood memories fetch little and I had needed bread.

Someone had put the song back.

Or—the thought arrived slowly, the way cold arrives—you couldn't sell a song without selling the room it was sung in, and the room had come home too, and it was pressing against the inside of my skull trying to get out.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"A courier." She smiled, and her teeth were red-tinged at the gumline. Internal bleeding, then. Hours, maybe less. "The last one. There were eleven of us. I'm the one who read the address correctly."

"The address?"

"*Vellamere remembers you.*"

She said it plainly, the way you'd say *it's raining*, and the whole tavern went distant—the noise pulled back like a tide going out, and there was just the two of us and a word I had never heard and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own handwriting on a note you don't remember writing.

Vellamere.

The syllables fit a hole in me. That's the only way I can describe it. Every person has gaps in their memory—we tell ourselves we remember our childhoods, but mostly we remember four or five rooms and a feeling. My gaps were different. Mine were architectural. Whole wings missing, corridors ending in plastered-over doors. And this word—*Vellamere*—went walking through me like a mistress through her own house, opening doors I hadn't known were doors.

I gripped the map tighter. The humming climbed.

"Say it again," I heard myself say.

"Vellamere remembers you." Her voice was fraying now, each word costing more than the last. "And it's angry. Or it's hungry. With countries it's hard to tell the difference. Eleven couriers. Nine dead before they got within a hundred miles of you. The map—" she coughed, wetly, delicately, a lady's cough full of blood— "the map chose. We were told to bring it to the heir. None of us believed it. Then I held it and it went quiet as a sleeping cat, and I knew."

"Heir." I laughed. It came out ugly. "Lady, I'm a ledger clerk. I file thefts. I'm the least inherited person in this city. Look at me."

"I am." She leaned forward, and her eyes were clear and terribly kind, and that was worse than the blood. "That's rather the point, isn't it? You spent nine years making sure no one would. Well. I'm looking now, Edmund Thornbury. And so is everything behind me."

"What's behind you?"

"Everyone I failed." She said it without drama. A courier's arithmetic. "And everyone who killed them. They'll come for the map. Then they'll come for whoever holds it. You should know that before you decide anything, because I won't be here to warn you twice."

"I don't accept deliveries."

"No," she agreed. "But you accept payment." Her eyes flicked down to my ledger page, still open, entry forty-one exposed like a wound. "Beeswax. A song through a wall. The certainty of being loved. Six years old, and you sold the being-loved first. Smart. It hurts the most, so you sell it while you're too young to know what you're losing."

The tavern noise rushed back in, and I wished it hadn't, because now I could hear how loudly my own heart was failing to be calm.

"How do you know what's in my ledger?"

"I read it." She pressed her hand to her side again. "Three nights ago. Guildhouse cellar, second sublevel, the iron cabinet with the lock you think no one knows you picked. You weren't home. You're never home. That's the trouble with making yourself unforgettable, Edmund—it means there's nothing watching the house."

Cold crawled up my arms. Not fear of her. Fear of the sentence after this one, the one I could feel coming, the way you feel thunder coming in your teeth.

"Why are you doing this?" I asked. "Dying message, secret heir, blood map—who sent you? Who benefits?"

"Benefits." Another smile, thinner. "No one benefits. That's what none of you clerks understand. Some things aren't transactions. Some things are just *owed*." She reached across the table, and her fingers—cold, bloodless-cold—closed over mine around the folded map. "Listen. I have maybe a minute of useful speech left, and I will not waste it on comfort. The country you erased remembers itself. It's been gathering. Every memory sold out of it, every scrap in every vault and broker's cellar and private collection—they're calling to each other. Like recognizes like. And the center of the call, the place where all the threads pull tightest—"

Her grip tightened. Her lips were gray.

"—is you."

"That's not possible. I'm not from anywhere. I was born in the Stews, I grew up—"

"You grew up in a story you sold yourself so you'd stop screaming at night. I read the entries, child. I read *all* of them." Her breath hitched, caught, held. "There's a door. The map knows where. The map knows everything, because the map isn't paper, it's the country's last—"

She stopped.

Not dramatically. Not with a gasp or a slumping or any of the theater deaths are supposed to provide. She simply arrived at the end of her minute, mid-word, and her eyes stayed open and her hand stayed closed over mine, and the loudest thing in the Drowned Bell was suddenly the whistle in the wall and the hum in my palms and my own pulse saying *no, no, no* like a child refusing bedtime.

"Hey," I said. "Hey. Last word. Spend it wisely."

Nothing.

Around us the tavern roared on. Someone laughed. Someone argued about cargo weights. The customs officer drank himself quietly blank. And a woman bled out at my table, unnoticed by everyone in the world except the one person trained better than anyone alive to notice nothing.

I did the practical things first. That's what survival does to you; it makes grief wait its turn in the queue. I checked her pockets—two coppers, a ferry token, nothing with a name. I wiped the table. I flagged the keeper and told him my aunt had drunk herself asleep and I'd see her home, and he nodded at the space where a corpse wasn't, because I'd bought his forgetting months ago and it held even now, even against a body cooling in his best chair.

Getting her out took an hour and every trick I owned. I'll spare us both the details. There's a stretch of mudflat past the breakwater that keeps what it's given, and the tide is generous, and I stood in the rain afterward until I stopped shaking, and then kept standing there because I hadn't stopped.

* * *

Back in my cellar, with the door barred and every warding-memory I possessed laid out like caltrops, I unfolded the map.

It was Vellamere.

Of course it was. I'd known since the word went through me in the tavern; knowing and understanding are different animals. Understanding took longer. It took staring at inked coastlines I had never seen and finding my eyes moving ahead of themselves, anticipating harbors, predicting where the rivers bent—as if I'd memorized this country once and buried the memorization so deep it had fossilized into instinct.

And it hummed. God, it hummed. Up close, alone, with no dying woman to moderate it, the map sang. Not one song—many. Layered. A city's worth of voices folded into vellum: market cries, a bell tower, arguments, lullabies, the particular clatter of a street I could almost see if I stopped trying to see it. Memories. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Pressed flat and bound into paper, the way I pressed stolen days into ledger lines.

Except these weren't stolen. That was what my hands understood before I did. These were *mine*. Or pieces of mine, tangled with pieces of everyone else's, the way a fire tangles a house with its neighbors. I could feel the seams. Somewhere in that humming mass was a woman singing through a wall. Somewhere in it was beeswax. Somewhere in it—closer to the center than I wanted to reach—was a door, and the word *door* arrived with a physical flinch, a flinch with a price tag still stuck to it.

I knew that flinch. I'd priced it myself.

*Sold by E.T., age unknown. Contents withheld at seller's insistence. Buyer: [entry sealed by order of the guildmaster].*

I had sealed that entry myself. Nine years ago. I remembered sealing it and nothing of what it contained, which is the closest thing to magic I have ever performed on purpose.

The candle guttered. Outside, the rain kept on, indifferent, municipal. Somewhere in this city there were people hunting eleven couriers and finding ten corpses and one empty trail, and the trail ended at a drowned tavern in Saltrow, and it would take them perhaps a day to do the arithmetic I'd just finished.

Corvane would ask me about it. He asked me about everything. He'd raised me, in whatever sense a man raises a tool, and tools that get questions wrong get melted down.

I looked at the map. The map, in whatever way maps look, looked back.

"Vellamere remembers me," I told it.

The humming rose, just slightly. Like a sleeper turning toward a voice.

And beneath the humming, faint but unmistakable, threaded through a hundred borrowed songs: a woman's voice, singing through a wall, and my chest hurt in a hollow that had been empty so long I'd mistaken the emptiness for peace.

I had until morning. Maybe less.

I blew out the candle, packed the map against my skin where the courier had carried hers, and went up the stairs to do the one thing I swore I'd never do again.

I was going to rob the guild.
