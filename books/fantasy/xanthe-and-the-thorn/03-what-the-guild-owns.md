## 3. What the Guild Owns

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its headquarters above a funeral parlor on Candlewick Lane, which Edmund had always thought was the guild's one honest joke. You climbed past the coffins and the wreaths and the smell of lilies laid over formaldehyde, up three flights of stairs that creaked in a pattern she had memorized by age nine—third step, seventh step, skip the eleventh—and arrived at a door of black oak with no handle on the outside.

You knocked twice, paused, then once. The door opened for anyone who knew the rhythm. For everyone else it stayed shut until they forgot why they'd come.

Inside, the guild did its work in rooms that didn't quite agree about their own dimensions. Edmund had stopped noticing years ago—the corridor that was four steps longer on Thursdays, the archive whose shelves rearranged themselves when nobody was looking at them, as if even the furniture wanted to be forgettable. She walked the familiar route to Corvane's study with the map wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and she allowed herself, for exactly eleven steps, to feel something like hope.

Eleven steps was all she permitted. Hope was expensive. Hope was the kind of thing you sold early, before the market learned what you were carrying.

She'd run the numbers on the walk over from the Drowned Bell. A map that hummed with stolen memories—not borrowed ones, not brokered ones, *hers*, pressed into dyed leather like flowers into a hymnal—that was a first-class acquisition. The guild paid first-class prices. Enough to buy back a season of her own childhood, maybe. Enough to stop dreaming in other people's streets. Enough, if she stretched it, to purchase one good memory of her mother's face and keep it somewhere no ledger could touch.

She knocked. Twice, pause, once.

"Enter, clerk."

Corvane's voice carried the way it always did, low and dry, like paper being folded. Edmund pushed into the study.

He sat behind his desk the way he always sat: perfectly still, hands flat on the blotter, a man who had made patience into a weapon and sharpened it daily for forty years. He was not a large man. He was not an imposing one. He looked, at a glance, like a retired schoolmaster or a minor customs official—gray at the temples, gray-eyed, gray-robed, forgettable by profession and by practice. That was how he'd built the guild. Nobody remembered the face of the man who owned everything they'd lost.

The room behind him was lined with ledgers. Not the working copies Edmund kept in the clerks' hall—these were the originals. Every theft the guild had ever made, bound in calfskin and chained to the wall. She had cataloged perhaps a tenth of them. She had read perhaps a hundredth. There were ledgers here older than the city, older than the kingdom, older, some whispered, than the idea of borders.

"You're bleeding," Corvane said.

Edmund glanced down. Her sleeve was stiff where the stranger's blood had soaked through when she'd caught the woman at the Drowned Bell. "Not mine."

"I didn't ask whose it was." His eyes went to the bundle under her arm. "Report."

"A woman found me at the tavern. Dying. She gave me this." Edmund set the oilcloth parcel on the desk and unwrapped it, and the moment the leather came into the candlelight the hum started again—that low, subcutaneous vibration she'd felt since the dockside, the sound of memories straining toward her through cloth and distance. "A map. It's... active. It's carrying memories, and I think—" She stopped herself. Careful, clerk. Sell the object, not the confession. "I think they'll fetch a high price. I'm offering it first refusal, per my contract."

Corvane didn't move.

That was the first wrong thing. In twenty-two years Edmund had watched him receive every manner of impossible goods—a bottled thunderstorm, a widow's grief the size of a cathedral, a language spoken by only one man left alive—and he received them all the same way, with a small nod and a figure named before the seller finished breathing. He moved now the way people move when the floor has quietly become water.

"Put it down," he said. "Step away from it."

"It's mine to sell. Contract clause nine—"

"*Step away from it.*"

His voice cracked on the second word. Edmund had heard Corvane angry exactly twice: once when a junior clerk had tried to skim a memory off the top of a sale, and once when she herself, at fifteen, had asked what was in the sealed wing of the archive. Both times he hadn't raised his voice. He had lowered it. This was lower than either.

She stepped away.

He rose, came around the desk, and stood over the map without touching it, the way you stand over a sleeping animal you're not sure is tame. And Edmund watched—with the particular horror of someone watching a fixed star drift off its chart—as the color left her guildmaster's face.

Not paled. *Drained.* Like watching memory leave a person, which was a thing she had seen professionally more times than she could count and never once gotten used to.

"Where," Corvane said, "did she find you?"

"The Drowned Bell. Dockside."

"Did she say your name?"

"No. She said—" Edmund hesitated. Saying it out loud would make it real in a way she wasn't ready for. But lying to Corvane was a skill she'd never acquired, mostly because he could buy the truth out of her for the price of a glance. "She said *Vellamere remembers you*."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on the ledgers on the walls. Edmund would have sworn, later, that the books themselves leaned in.

Then Corvane laughed.

It was the worst sound she had ever heard him make. There was no humor in it. It was the laugh of a man reading his own obituary and finding the date wrong but close.

"Vellamere," he said. "Yes. Of course she said that." He straightened, and when he turned to face Edmund the mask was back on—but badly fitted, like a coat buttoned wrong. "Clerk. Do you know what the guild sells?"

"Impossible things."

"That's what we tell the clients. Do you know what we *trade*?" He tapped the desk. "Memory. Memory is currency, memory is real estate, memory is the only commodity in the world that increases in value the moment it becomes rare. Grief, joy, a face, a name, a street you grew up on—we buy them cheap from people who want to be rid of them, we sell them dear to people who can't live without them. That is the trade. That has always been the trade."

"Yes," Edmund said. She wrote those transactions six nights a week.

"But there is a tier above it." Corvane's eyes went to the highest shelf, where a single ledger sat apart from the rest, wrapped in chains and wax seals, its spine blank. "There are things so large they cannot be bought or sold piecewise. A plague year, forgotten by a city so it could survive it. A king's whole reign, lifted from ten thousand heads so a dynasty could begin clean. Wars unwitnessed. You cannot carry those in a bottle. You cannot sell them by the ounce. For acquisitions of that scale, the guild does not trade in memories."

He looked at the map.

"We trade in *countries*."

The word landed in Edmund's chest and stayed there, cold, like a coin dropped down a well that never hit bottom.

"Countries can't be erased," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; she'd spent years training her voice to do that, and it was the only part of her apprenticeship she'd never regretted. "People can forget. Places stay places."

"Do they?" Corvane picked up the map at last—and flinched. Actually flinched, a full-body shudder, before mastering it. "What does it feel like, clerk? When you hold it. Be precise. Precision is your one virtue."

It felt like standing in a house she'd lived in and couldn't picture. It felt like a word on the tip of her tongue shaped like a lullaby. It felt like the hole in her ledger, the entry she refused to reread. But she said none of that.

"Warm," she said. "Like it knows me."

"It doesn't know you." Corvane set it down gently, the way he did everything that frightened him. "It's looking for you. Maps like this are addressed, the way letters are addressed. This one was addressed to Vellamere's heir, and it has been searching for—" He stopped. Recalculated. Edmund watched him do it, watched the sentence get taken apart and rebuilt without the dangerous piece, and understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that he had just decided how much to tell her.

And decided: almost nothing.

"The point," Corvane said, "is that you will hand this to me, and you will forget you ever held it, and in exchange I will pay you double the standard rate and lose the entry entirely."

Double the rate. Loss of the entry. That was not payment; that was hush money. Edmund's hands, which she had trained to be steady over ledgers for a decade, were not steady now.

"Clause nine," she said. "First refusal means I hear the offer. I don't have to take it."

"Clause nine applies to goods." He came around the desk, slowly, and Edmund became aware of how small the study was, how far the door was, how the corridor outside sometimes lengthened on Thursdays and today was Thursday. "This is not goods. This is *territory*. The last territory of an erased nation, and it walked into my guild on the arm of my own clerk." He stopped in front of her, close enough that she could smell the ink and camphor he'd smelled like her entire childhood. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Erased countries are the most profitable holdings this guild has ever possessed. A country is a thousand thousand memories fused into one asset. Its fall generated revenue for a generation. Its *residue*—" his gaze flicked to the map, hungry and afraid in equal measure, "—residue is priceless. And residue, apparently, remembers my ward."

*My ward.* Not my clerk. Not Edmund. Ward. Property with a maintenance cost.

Something in her chest, something old and patient, finally stood up.

"I was raised in these halls," Edmund said. "You taught me the trade. You taught me that everything has a price and everyone should know theirs. So let's do this properly, guildmaster. Name me a price for the map, and I'll consider the offer like any seller."

"There is no price." His hand came up—not fast, not violent, just inevitable, the way guild business always was—and settled on her shoulder. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. "Because it isn't yours to sell. It was never yours. You were never—" He caught himself again, that terrible recalculation, but too late, and Edmund watched the truth slip out of him like a coin from a torn pocket:

"You were never supposed to remember it exists."

The hum of the map changed pitch. Low to high. Soft to sharp. Like a struck bell deciding to become an alarm.

And Corvane's grip tightened, and his gray eyes went from her face to the map and back, and Edmund saw—with the terrible professional clarity of a woman who had cataloged ten thousand thefts—exactly what expression he was wearing.

It was the expression buyers wore. Appraisal. Assessment. The look of a man pricing an item, turning it in the light, checking for flaws.

He was pricing *her*.

"Corvane," she said quietly. "Let go of my shoulder."

"You're tired," he said, gentle as a closing door. "You've had a shock. Sit down, and we'll sort out what you remember and what you don't, and by morning none of this will trouble—"

Edmund had survived in the guild's halls for twenty-two years by knowing every exit, every rhythm, every trick of a building that couldn't keep its own dimensions honest. She dropped her weight backward through his grip, put the chair between them, and put her hand flat on the map.

The hum roared up her arm. Memories that were hers and weren't hers flooded the space behind her eyes: a white city on seven hills. A bell tower full of drowned light. A woman singing in a kitchen with copper pots. *Sable,* someone said, in a voice like warm bread, *come inside, love.*

Her name. Someone else's name. Her name.

"Edmund," Corvane said warningly.

"Sable," the map said, in her mother's voice, through her own bones.

She ran.

The corridor was longer tonight—of course it was, Thursday—and the stairs creaked their treacherous pattern under her boots and behind her she heard the study door open with no knock at all, heard Corvane's voice go out ahead of her into the guild like a command written into the walls themselves:

"She's carrying Vellamere. No one lets her leave the building."

But the building, Edmund thought, taking the seventh step and skipping the eleventh, the building owed *her*. Twenty-two years of entries, of hours, of small thefts faithfully logged. Buildings keep accounts too. Everyone in the trade knew that.

She just hoped the guild's own house remembered which side of the ledger she was on.

Behind her, faint through the walls that rearranged themselves when no one watched, she heard footsteps joining the chase—one set, two, five—and beneath them, rising through the floorboards like water through stone, the map humming against her ribs the only word it knew:

*Home. Home. Home.*
