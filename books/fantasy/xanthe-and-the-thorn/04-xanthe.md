## 4. Xanthe

The Order of the Blank Page did not send letters. Letters could be read, and read things were remembered, and remembered things had weight. So the summons came the way all of Xanthe's orders came: a blank sheet of paper slipped beneath her door at Greyfriar's Rest, heavy as a headstone.

She pressed her thumb to the center of it and the ink bloomed up out of nothing, black letters rising through the page like drowned men surfacing.

*Vellamere stirs. A map survives in the city of Corryn's Reach, in the keeping of a memory-thief who keeps the Guild's ledgers. Retrieve it. Burn it. Leave no reader behind.*

Below that, the seal: a white rectangle, empty, the sigil she had sworn her life into twelve years ago.

Xanthe read it twice, then held the corner to her candle and watched the order become light, then smoke, then nothing at all. That was the way of things with the Order. Everything ended as a blank page. That was rather the point.

She packed in ten minutes. She'd learned long ago not to own more than ten minutes' worth of anything.

* * *

Corryn's Reach was three days south by fast horse, a city built on a harbor that had silted over so gradually nobody alive remembered when the ships stopped coming. The Drowned Bell sat where the docks used to be, half its foundation swallowed by mudflats, and it smelled of brine, tar, and the particular desperation of people drinking before noon on a weekday.

Xanthe had been to Corryn's Reach twice before. Both times, she had left something erased behind her—a smuggler's cache of forbidden cartography, a scholar who'd written one too many essays about countries that weren't. The work was clean. The work was simple. Find the impossible thing, take it, unmake it, go home to an empty cell and sleep without dreaming, because the Order trained dreaming out of you early, along with most other luxuries.

She found the thief on her second evening, and she almost didn't recognize what she was looking at.

Not because Edmund Thornbury was hiding. Because she wasn't. She sat at a corner table in the Bell's common room with a ledger open in front of her and a cup of wine she wasn't drinking, and she was—there was no other word for it—invisible. Not disguised, not cloaked, not glamoured. Invisible the way furniture is invisible. Xanthe watched a serving girl look directly at her, twice, and both times her gaze slid off like rain off oilcloth. A drunk staggered past close enough to knock the woman's cup and apologized—to the table.

Twelve years of training, and Xanthe had never seen erasure worn like clothing. She had only ever seen it done *to* things. To maps. To books. To names.

To people.

She took a table across the room and ordered nothing and watched.

* * *

Here is what the Order teaches you about watching, in the years they take from your childhood and pay for with bread and doctrine: watch the hands first, the eyes second, the mouth last. Hands do the work. Eyes want things. Mouths lie about both.

Edmund Thornbury's hands never stopped moving. Even reading, even sitting still, her fingers traced the ledger's margins like a woman checking locks on doors already bolted. Her eyes were gray and tired and missed nothing—they flicked to the door every time it opened, catalogued each arrival in under a heartbeat, dismissed them faster. And her mouth was set in the flat, careful line of someone who has practiced having no expressions worth stealing.

A clerk, then. A bookkeeper of stolen souls. Xanthe had read the Guild's file, thin as it was. The Guild of Impossible Things trafficked in memories the way other merchants trafficked in spice, and Thornbury was their ledger-keeper—the person who recorded who had sold what, and to whom, and for how much. A functionary. An accountant of thefts.

Easy, then. Corner her alone, take the map, burn it, be gone by morning.

Except.

Except that when the door opened at the ninth bell and a big man in a fishmonger's apron shouldered in out of the fog, Edmund Thornbury looked up—and Xanthe saw her face come fully into the candlelight for the first time.

And Xanthe forgot how to breathe.

She knew that face.

Not from the file. Not from any wanted notice or guild portrait. From the archive beneath the Order's citadel—the one with the locked door and the oath-bound silence, the one novices were told held records too dangerous to consult. Xanthe had been twenty-two and arrogant and curious in the specific way that gets people killed, and she had picked that lock on a dare she made to herself, and inside she had found shelves and shelves of things that should not exist. Maps of Vellamere. Songs transcribed in a script no living tongue used. And portraits. Dozens of them, painted before the Fall, faces of people from a country that had never officially existed, hung row upon row like a congregation waiting for a service that would never come.

One portrait had stopped her longer than the rest. A woman with dark hair going silver at the temples, standing in front of a house with a blue door, one hand resting on the shoulder of a child of six or seven. The child had been turned half away from the painter, caught mid-laugh, one hand raised as if waving at someone outside the frame. Gray eyes. A mouth set crooked, mid-word.

Xanthe had stood in front of that painting so long that her candle burned down and she'd had to finish her theft in the dark.

She had reported herself, of course. Confessed the breach. Taken the penance: three days fasting, six months of night watches, and a rite of forgetting that was supposed to have taken the archive from her mind entirely.

It hadn't worked completely. It never worked completely; that was the Order's open secret, the flaw under all their flawless doctrine. You could erase a memory but the shape of it stayed, the way a pressed flower stays between pages after the flower itself is dust. For twelve years Xanthe had carried the shape of that portrait without knowing what it was a shape *of*—a hollow ache she'd assumed was grief for her parents, dead these nineteen years, killed in the border wars the Order said Vellamere's memory-plague had fed.

Now she sat in a dockside tavern three hundred miles from the citadel and looked at that child grown, and the ache found its name.

The girl from the blue door. Waving at someone outside the frame.

Waving at—

At whom? At what? The thought wouldn't complete itself. It slid sideways every time Xanthe reached for it, like a fish under ice.

Across the room, Edmund Thornbury closed her ledger, dropped coins on the table, and stood. And here was the thing that should have ended Xanthe's hesitation then and there, the thing she would turn over a thousand times in the weeks to come: the thief glanced toward the door, and her gaze passed over Xanthe the same way everyone's did—without catching, without stopping.

But then it came back.

Just for a moment. Just a flicker. Those gray eyes snagged on Xanthe's face the way cloth snags on a nail, and something moved behind them—not recognition, not exactly. More like a dog hearing its name in a language it's forgotten.

Then it was gone, and Edmund Thornbury pulled her hood up and walked out into the fog, and she was just a stranger again, forgettable as furniture.

Xanthe sat very still and listened to her own heartbeat count off the seconds.

*Follow*, said twelve years of training. *Corner her. Take the map. Burn it.*

*Wait*, said something older, buried deeper, wearing the shape of a burned-down candle in a forbidden room.

Xanthe waited four minutes. Then she followed anyway, telling herself the pause had been tactical. Telling herself a great many things.

* * *

The fog in Corryn's Reach had teeth. It came off the mudflats after dark and ate the streets whole, and Xanthe moved through it the way the Order had taught her—weight on the outside edges of her feet, breathing shallow, letting the city's sounds map her route for her. Ahead, the thief's footsteps clicked soft and quick on wet cobbles, turning east toward the warehouse district, and Xanthe kept thirty paces back and wondered, not for the first time since the tavern, what exactly she thought she was doing.

Because here was the doctrine, drummed into her since she was nine years old, when the Order's grey-cloaked recruiters had found her starving in a refugee camp and given her bread and purpose in the same breath:

*Memory is a wound that thinks it is a gift. Some countries deserve to die. Vellamere was such a country. Its memory bred madness, fed wars, rotted borders. We do not destroy it out of cruelty. We amputate to save the body.*

She had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you—totally, without thinking, because your whole life stands on it. She had burned maps and unmade songs and once, in Ashfall, had stood in a print shop while the presses were dismantled around a half-finished history of a country whose name she wasn't permitted to know, and she had felt nothing but satisfaction at a job cleanly done.

So why was she counting paces behind a memory-thief instead of putting a knife to her throat and taking the map?

*Because of a portrait*, said the small voice. *Because of a blue door.*

*Because I need to understand why the file on her is wrong*, said the tactical voice, louder, covering. And it was true, that much was true—the file was wrong, or thin, or edited. Guild clerks didn't glance back at shadows. Forgettable people didn't almost-recognize knights who'd been trained to be unnoticeable. Something in that woman was awake and reaching, and the Order's intelligence had missed it, and Xanthe did not move against unknowns. That was doctrine too. Doctrine number nine, actually. She'd helped write the margins of that one during her years as an instructor.

Ahead, the footsteps stopped.

Xanthe melted into a doorway, hand finding her knife out of habit, and watched Edmund Thornbury stand at the mouth of Tanner's Lane with her head tilted, listening. Listening the way you listen when you think you've heard your own name called from an empty room.

Then the thief did something strange. She reached into her coat and drew out a folded square of something that caught what little light the fog allowed, and even at thirty paces Xanthe felt it—a hum, faint as a struck bell heard through a wall, a pressure against the ears, against the teeth. Against the *memory*. The hairs on Xanthe's arms lifted. Somewhere behind her sternum, in the place where the pressed flower lived, something stirred and turned over.

The map. It had to be the dying courier's map, the thing she'd crossed three hundred miles to retrieve. And it was humming like a hive, and the thief was holding it up to the fog like a woman holding a lantern, and her face—

Her face had changed. All the careful flatness gone, all the practiced nothingness burned away, and underneath was raw and young and terrified and *awake*, and she was mouthing something to herself, words Xanthe couldn't hear, words that made her sway on her feet like a drunk.

Xanthe should have moved. Thirty paces, four seconds, knife to the kidney or wire to the throat, map secured, fire next. She had done harder things asleep.

Instead she stood in a doorway in Tanner's Lane and watched a stranger grieve a country she'd supposedly sold, and the pressed-flower shape behind Xanthe's sternum opened like a fist unclenching, and she heard—clearly, impossibly, from nowhere—the first bar of a lullaby in a language no living tongue used.

The same one from the archive. The one that had been playing, she now understood, in the painted moment. Someone outside the frame had been singing it. That was why the child was laughing. That was why the child was waving.

Edmund Thornbury folded the map away and walked on into the fog, and Xanthe let her go, and stood in the doorway until her legs agreed to work again.

* * *

She reported by the old method that night: blank paper, blank ink, a message written in a script only the Order's archivists could raise. *Target located. Map confirmed. Moving to intercept.* All true. All carefully incomplete. She did not mention the humming. She did not mention the lullaby. She did not mention that the heir-shaped clerk with the forgettable face had looked straight through her and then, for one terrible half-second, looked *at* her.

She did not mention the portrait. Twelve years of silence around that particular grave; she could keep it one night longer.

She slept in a flophouse near the tanneries with her knife under her pillow and dreamed—for the first time in twelve years, against all training—of a house with a blue door. In the dream, someone was singing. In the dream, she was the one outside the frame, and the child was waving at *her*, and she was walking toward the door and walking and walking and the street kept lengthening under her feet like a punishment, and she woke at dawn with wet eyes and no memory of crying and the certainty, cold and total as well-water, of two things.

First: Edmund Thornbury was not what the Order believed her to be.

Second: neither, possibly, was Xanthe.

She dressed, sharpened her knife, and went to find the thief before the fog burned off. Whatever was waking in that woman—and whatever it was dragging up out of Xanthe along with it—she would have to get closer to understand it. Closer than a doorway. Close enough to touch the map, maybe. Close enough, said a voice she didn't listen to, to see that face again without the hood.

Outside, the fog was lifting off Corryn's Reach in slow rags, and somewhere in it, a memory-thief was running out of places to be forgettable.
