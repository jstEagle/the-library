## 5. Running on Borrowed Streets

The guildhouse had three exits and Corvane knew all of them.

That was the arithmetic Edmund ran in the two seconds after she pulled the map from his desk—two seconds in which he was still standing at the window with his back to her, saying something about provenance, about how certain acquisitions required *care*, his voice doing the thing it did when he was deciding whether a thing was yours or his. She knew that voice. She had transcribed it for eleven years. It always ended the same way.

She took the window.

Not because it was clever. Because it was fast, and because the ledger room's window opened onto the cistern yard where the night soil cart came at half past eleven, and because—this was the part that mattered, the part she would examine later with something like horror—a memory rose up in her, whole and warm, of a night-soil cart in a city she had never visited, its driver drunk on plum wine, its wheels squeaking on the third axle, the exact gap beneath the arch where a small person could fold herself flat while the cart rolled over her.

She had never walked that street. But she had sold that memory to someone once, or bought it from someone, or filed it—the mechanics blurred—and somewhere in the transaction it had left a residue. An echo. The Guild of Impossible Things did not deal in clean goods. Every memory passed through her hands twice, once going out and once coming back when the buyer died or tired of it or forgot why they'd wanted it, and memories, unlike coins, did not come back unspent.

She folded herself under the arch. The cart rolled over her. Its third axle squeaked exactly as the memory said it would, and she felt something turn over inside her chest—not fear, not yet, but a kind of terrible delight, the way a gambler feels the first win of a night that will ruin him.

Then she ran.

* * *

Corvane would give her until the end of the street before he raised the alarm. He was careful, and care took time, and time was the only currency she had ever been rich in.

She went east, away from the river, into the tangle of streets behind the chandlers' row where the buildings leaned close enough at the top that you could shake hands across the gap. Her body knew these streets the way any body knows any city: by habit, by the pull of the downhill slope toward water. But the map wanted otherwise.

It was still humming against her ribs, tucked inside her coat, and it hummed louder when she turned the wrong way, softer when she turned right—a compass made of blood, pointing not north but *inward*, toward whatever Vellamere had been. She stopped in the mouth of an alley to catch her breath and pressed her hand flat against her coat, and the humming changed. It wasn't sound, exactly. It was recognition. Like a dog pressing its head into your palm.

*I know you,* it seemed to say. *I have been waiting in a drawer for thirty years, and I know you.*

"Not now," she whispered, which was absurd, talking to a piece of vellum in an alley that smelled of fish guts and lamp oil, but the night had already gone absurd, and absurdity is only terror that hasn't caught up to you yet.

A bell began to ring back at the guildhouse. One strike, then a pause, then two more—the pattern for a breach, for property leaving the building that should not leave. Not a fire. Fires got three strikes and continuous ringing. This was worse than fire. Fire destroyed things. Edmund was taking one with her.

She ran again.

* * *

Here is what it is to flee a city using memories you stole:

You do not think your way through it. Thinking is too slow, and thinking uses *your* streets, the ones you've actually walked, the honest little map of your own life—which in Edmund's case was four streets deep and mostly led back to the guildhouse, which was the last place on earth she could go. Instead you let go. You open yourself to the inventory the way you'd open a locked cabinet in a flood, and you grab whatever floats past.

*A courier's chase through Marrowgate, the buyer a smuggler who wanted to know what it felt like to be hunted.* She took that one. Suddenly she knew that if she cut through the cooperage there was a wall with a drain at knee height behind the third stack of hoops, and the drain was big enough for a slim person, and the smell would be unbearable for exactly forty feet and then she'd come out in a courtyard where the watch never bothered because the gate was chained and everyone assumed nothing was worth stealing inside.

She went down the drain. The smell was unbearable for forty feet. The courtyard was empty. She laughed—actually laughed, a wild unlovely sound—as she came out the other side, and the laugh scared her more than the bells did, because she hadn't known her body could do that anymore. Eleven years of being forgettable had worn her face into a shape that didn't laugh in alleys.

*An assassin's escape from the Salt Palace, purchased by a woman who liked to feel brave in her sleep.* That one taught her to move along the rooftops of the low quarter by stepping only on the shadowed side of the tiles, the side that had dried unevenly and wouldn't creak. She moved along rooftops she'd never climbed, forty feet above streets she'd never walked, and below her the city spread out like a ledger opened flat—all its accounts visible, all its debts glowing faintly in the lamplight—and she thought, *I could live up here. I could be anyone up here.*

And then the bleeding started.

* * *

It began small. She dropped from the roof into a lane she recognized—not remembered, *recognized*—and said aloud, "Left here, past the fountain with the broken mermaid," except the fountain had no mermaid. The fountain had a saint whose hands had been knocked off in some riot a century ago. She stood staring at it, breathing hard, waiting for the mermaid to resolve out of the stone, and it did not, and the wrongness of that sat in her stomach like a swallowed hook.

Because here was the thing about stolen memories, the thing every broker knew and never said in front of buyers: they don't stay where you put them. A memory isn't a coin. It's a seed. You swallow someone else's afternoon and it puts down roots in your soil and grows in your shapes. The couriers' chase through Marrowgate had been *his* fear, flavored with his mother's kitchen, his dead brother's name. The assassin's rooftops had been *her* exhilaration, her particular loneliness at altitude. And now those flavors were in Edmund, seeping outward, and her own flavors were thinning to make room.

She tried to remember her mother's face as she crouched in the shadow of the handless saint, and got instead a kitchen that belonged to a dead smuggler, a woman stirring a pot and saying a name that was not Edmund's name, in a voice that was almost warm enough to weep over. She clawed backward through herself for something true—her own mother, whoever she'd been, sold off long ago, entry fourteen in a ledger she had written with her own hand—and found nothing. Just the smuggler's mother. Just the pot. Just warmth that wasn't hers, filling a space where hers used to sit.

"Oh," she said quietly, to no one. "Oh, that's the trick of it."

That was the trade nobody warned you about. You borrowed a stranger's escape and the stranger came along. You fled through their streets on their courage and afterward their streets were in you, their courage was in you, and you were a little less the person who had needed to escape at all.

She pressed her hand to the map again. It hummed. And this time the hum felt less like a dog pressing into her palm and more like a hook going in.

*Vellamere remembers you.*

*Well,* she thought, hauling herself up onto the next roof, *someone has to. I'm running short.*

* * *

Dawn found her in the rag market on the far side of the canal, three miles and a hundred stolen memories from the guildhouse, sitting under an awning of patched sailcloth between two sleeping beggars, doing the thing she did best: taking stock.

Her legs were wrecked. Her lungs were worse. She had a bruise blooming on her hip where she'd misjudged a drop the assassin's memory swore was shorter. And in her head—

In her head it was getting crowded.

She counted what she was carrying. The courier's fear, the assassin's joy, the smuggler's mother, a fisherman's knowledge of the canal currents so complete she could tell you what the water did at the second bridge when the tide turned, though she'd never seen the tide turn anywhere. A child's terror of cellars. A soldier's boredom. Somewhere in the pile, a memory of a wedding that made her eyes sting whenever it surfaced, and she didn't know whose wedding it was or why it hurt, only that it hurt in a voice that wasn't hers.

And underneath all of it, faint as a watermark: the map. Still humming. Still patient. Pointing southeast now, she realized—had been pointing southeast since the canal—toward the low district, toward the warren above the tanners' yards, toward—

Toward a cellar she had never entered but suddenly, horribly, knew the location of. Knew the number of steps down. Knew which stair creaked.

Because the map wasn't just humming. It was *leading*. It had been leading her all night, choosing her turns as much as she chose them, and every turn had been correct, and she was beginning to suspect that correctness was not a kindness. Vellamere—or what was left of it, or whatever had written itself onto this scrap of vellum before the country died—wanted its heir found. Wanted her found by whatever lived at the end of this trail. And Edmund had spent eleven years making sure that no one, including herself, knew where to find her, and here she was at dawn, legible as a signature.

Two bells rang out across the water. Not guildhouse bells. Watch bells—different pattern, different pitch. And beneath them, farther off but closing, a third sound she knew from a memory she'd bought off a dying watchman years ago: the soft double-knock of a door-knocker used only by the Order of the Blank Page. Knock, knock. Pause. Knock. They announced themselves that way in every city. It was supposed to be courtesy. It was really a promise: *we are coming in, and when we leave, there will be less of this place than there was.*

They were working the far bank, door by door. Someone had told them which direction she'd gone. Someone with a key to the guildhouse and a voice she had trusted since she was nine years old.

Edmund looked at the map. The map hummed, southeast, insistent, certain.

"You're going to get me killed," she told it.

It hummed.

And the worst part—the part she tucked away to examine later, if there was a later, in whatever self she still was by then—was that some new-borrowed fragment of her, some thief or lover or fool whose courage she'd swallowed in the dark, *agreed*. Some voice in the crowd of her that wasn't hers at all leaned toward that humming and said, with perfect strangers' conviction:

*Whatever's down those stairs, it knows your name. And God help me, I want to hear it.*

She pulled her hood up, crossed the canal on a bargeman's stolen balance, and went down the steps to find the broker who kept the ledger of everything Edmund Thornbury had ever sold—including, she was now almost certain, the reason she'd sold it.

Behind her, knock-knock-pause-knock. Closer.
