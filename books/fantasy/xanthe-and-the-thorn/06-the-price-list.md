## 6. The Price List

The cellar smelled of tallow and old grief.

Edmund had been in a hundred memory-brokers' cellars before—she'd catalogued enough of them for the Guild to know the geography by heart—but this one was different. This one belonged to a woman named Ferren, who dealt in sorrows the way other brokers dealt in coin, and who owed Edmund three favors from the old days, back when Edmund's ledger had been clean and her hands had been quick and her name hadn't meant anything at all.

Ferren had taken one look at the bloodstained map rolled against Edmund's ribs and said nothing. That was the favor: the nothing. She'd lit no lamp above ground, told no one Edmund was here, and led her down a ladder into a room where the walls were lined with stoppered jars and wax-sealed vials, each one labeled in Ferren's small, patient hand.

*Grief of a man who outlived both sons. Sold twice; the second buyer didn't notice.*

*The hour before a wedding that did not happen.*

*A mother's voice, remembered wrong on purpose, so it would hurt less.*

"You can stay until the fog lifts off the river," Ferren said. "Not longer. Corvane's people have been through twice already, asking after you." She paused at the bottom of the ladder. "They weren't asking like they wanted to find you alive."

"Thank you," Edmund said, which was not nearly enough, and they both knew it.

"Don't thank me. Just don't die down here. The cleanup is ruinous." But Ferren's eyes lingered on the map, and something moved behind them—not greed, exactly. Brokers learned to kill greed young or die of it. It was closer to recognition. "That's Vellamere paper," she said quietly. "I've only seen it once before. A man sold me a memory of its harbor and wept the whole time he described it, because he couldn't remember why he was crying." She shook herself. "Sleep if you can. Or don't. Down here it hardly matters which."

Then she was gone, up the ladder, and Edmund was alone with the jars and the map and the sound of her own breathing, which seemed suddenly very loud, as if the cellar were listening.

She should have slept. She knew that. Her body had been running since the Drowned Bell, since the dying woman's hand closed around hers and pressed the map into it, since the word *Vellamere* went through her like a blade finding an old scar. Sleep would have been the sensible thing, the professional thing. Edmund Thornbury was very good at sensible. She had built a whole life on it—a life of borrowed streets and forgotten debts and being so thoroughly unremarkable that even the Guild's own clerks sometimes lost track of her in corridors.

Instead she sat down on a crate between two shelves of bottled sorrow, unrolled the map halfway across her knees, and looked at it again by the light of a single stub candle.

It hummed.

Not audibly. Nothing she could have proven to a magistrate. But she felt it against her skin, a faint vibration like a struck bell heard from three streets away, like a word held behind someone's teeth. And underneath the hum, fainter but unmistakable, were memories that were *hers*. She had known it from the first moment in the tavern, and knowing it had frightened her more than the dying woman's blood, more than the name she'd been given, more than anything.

Because Edmund Thornbury had spent nine years selling pieces of herself to survive, and she had never once asked where the pieces went.

She'd told herself it didn't matter. That was the whole discipline of it—the rule beneath all her rules. What you sell is gone. Don't chase it. Don't wonder about it. A memory-thief who grieves over inventory is a thief who hesitates, and hesitation gets people found. She had written entries in other people's ledgers for years without once opening the one that mattered.

But there was a ledger with her name in it. She was certain now.

She had seen the evidence of it three nights ago, in Corvane's counting-room, when she'd gone to him expecting payment and gotten instead a look she'd never seen on his face—fear, naked and ancient—and the words *where did you get that* spoken like a prayer and a curse at once. In his haste to take the map from her hands he had knocked open the folio on his desk, and among the guild papers had spilled a page in his own cramped clerk-hand, and she had read, upside down, half a line:

*Thornbury, E.—see original ledger, Lot 1 through Lot—*

And then he'd covered it, and lied to her, badly, which Corvane never did, and she had run.

Original ledger. Lot 1. As though she were an estate being auctioned.

Her fingers were cold. The candle flame leaned in a draft she couldn't feel.

There was a copy. Of course there was a copy. Corvane never kept anything in only one place—that was the first law of the Guild of Impossible Things, the law under all the others: *nothing exists that cannot be stolen, so keep everything somewhere stealable.* Edmund had helped him enforce that law for years. She knew his filing habits better than he knew them himself, because filing habits were the last thing anyone thought to disguise, even men who traded in erased countries.

Corvane kept his true ledger—the master record, the one that named every lot and every buyer—in a black oilcloth wrap, inside the false bottom of a strongbox, inside the cellar of a house on Candlewick Row that was registered to a widow who had sold the Guild her entire childhood at age eleven and could therefore be trusted to remember none of it.

He thought no one else knew. He thought the widow's forgetting made the hiding place invisible.

He was right about almost everyone. He had simply never imagined that the girl he raised would spend nine years making herself forgettable *as a discipline*, learning every trick of vanishing the Guild possessed, until she could walk through his own memories of her without leaving footprints. He had taught her everything. That had always been his mistake, though he didn't know it yet.

Getting the ledger had taken her four hours, most of them spent standing very still.

Now it lay on her knees beside the map, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a paving stone. She had carried it across the city in a fishmonger's basket, past two Order patrols whose faces she knew from files she wasn't supposed to have read, and down the ladder into Ferren's cellar, and the whole way she had told herself she wouldn't open it.

She opened it.

The pages were cheap rag paper, crowded with Corvane's handwriting—small, upright, immaculate. No flourishes. Corvane wrote the way he did everything: precisely, and without love. Each entry had the same shape. Lot number. Description. Date of sale. Price. Buyer. And then, in a smaller script, a note.

Edmund found her name on the fourth page.

*THORNBURY, EDMUND — b. Vellamere. Acquired age 9. See attached schedule of disposals.*

Attached schedule. Nine years of her life, itemized.

She turned the page.

*Lot 1. The color of the door of the house on—* the description broke off there, ink blotted, the rest scraped away with a knife. Someone had tried to remove it. She ran her thumb over the scrape and felt the ghost of letters under the blade-marks. Below the ruined line, in Corvane's neat hand: *Sold. Price: see Lot 4 consideration. Buyer: [struck through].*

Struck through. Not blotted—struck through with a single ruled line, deliberate, the way you cancel a debt.

*Lot 2. A summer, aged 6–7. Orchard, river, a woman singing (fragmentary). Sold to M—— of the Salt Exchange. Price: 40 crowns and one favor, nature undisclosed.*

M—— of the Salt Exchange. Edmund knew the Salt Exchange. Everyone in her trade did—it was the grandest of the memory-brokerages, the one that served nobles who wanted to *remember being happy*, because happiness bought secondhand was cheaper than happiness made. Forty crowns for a summer. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Forty crowns was what the Guild paid for a good year of a dockworker's memories. A whole summer of orchard and river and a woman singing—for forty crowns and an unnamed favor.

Someone had driven a very hard bargain over her childhood.

Or someone had needed the money so badly that forty crowns had seemed like a fortune.

*Lot 3. First snowfall, experienced at age 5. Minor. Sold to the Order of the Blank Page. Price: waived. Consideration: continued non-interference in Guild affairs.*

The Order of the Blank Page. Xanthe's order. She sat with that a moment. They had bought—or been given—even her smallest things. Why would knights sworn to erasure want a child's first snowfall?

Unless it wasn't about wanting. Unless it was about accounting. About making sure that everything of Vellamere passed through hands they controlled, even the worthless pieces. Even the snow.

*Lot 4. Name, given. Full. Sold. Price: 200 crowns, paid in installments. Buyer: [struck through]. Note: seller insisted on this sale above all others. Seller was 9 years old and negotiated terms personally. Remarkable.*

Edmund stopped breathing.

Seller insisted on this sale above all others.

She tried to imagine it—a nine-year-old sitting across a desk from Corvane, negotiating the sale of her own name, insisting on it, haggling terms while Corvane's pen scratched. She couldn't. It was like trying to remember someone else's dream. And yet the note was in Corvane's hand, and Corvane did not embellish. He recorded; he did not invent. Somewhere inside the scraped-out lines and struck-through buyers was a child who had walked into this office and sold herself piece by piece on purpose.

Why?

Two hundred crowns. In nine-year-old money, in fugitive money, in whatever desperate currency her family had been counting in during whatever fall had brought her to Corvane's desk—two hundred crowns might have been bread for a winter. Might have been passage on a ship. Might have been—

A mother's voice, singing. From Lot 2. A woman singing, fragmentary.

The map shifted on her knee. The hum deepened, just slightly, the way a tuning fork answers its own note sung across a room. Edmund pressed her palm flat against it and the vibration climbed her arm and settled behind her sternum, and for one disorienting second she was not in the cellar at all—she was somewhere bright and high-walled, and someone was calling a name that was hers, and the name had two syllables and began with a sound like wind—

Gone. The candle guttered. The cellar reasserted itself, jar-labeled and dim.

She turned pages faster now, unable to help it, hating herself for the hunger of it.

*Lot 7. Face, general recognizability (partial). Sold to three buyers jointly, pooled purchase. Price: 90 crowns split. Note: unusual transaction; buyers wished to forget having seen her, not to possess seeing her. Effectively a disposal. Seller approved.*

People had paid to *forget her face.* Three of them, pooling their coin. She tried to think what that meant—who sees a child once and pays good money to never see her again?—and the answer arrived with the gentleness of a knife: someone who had seen her and been afraid. Someone for whom her face was dangerous. Someone who had looked at a nine-year-old and thought, *if I remember this face, I am part of this, and if I am part of this, I will be erased too.*

Erased like what? Erased like—

Like a country. Like a country that no longer existed on any map, in any book, in any mind that the Order could reach. Except minds that had paid to forget. Except minds sealed in jars in cellars like this one.

*Lot 11. The door. Description withheld by mutual agreement. Sold. Price: [blank]. Buyer: [blank]. Note: this entry is complete. Do not amend. —C.*

Every other entry had a price. Every other entry had a buyer. This one had neither, and Corvane's note said *this entry is complete*, which meant the blanks were the point. You don't sell a door for coin. You sell it for something that isn't written down. And the door—the map in her hands was a map of doors, she understood that much now, had understood it since the Drowned Bell—the door was the thing the whole Guild, the whole Order, the whole rotten apparatus of forgetting orbited like moths around a snuffed candle.

She had owned it once. She had sold it. At nine years old. With terms she negotiated personally.

Edmund set the ledger down on the crate and put her hands over her face and, for the first time since the Drowned Bell, since before the Drowned Bell, since years, allowed herself to cry—not cleanly, not privately in the way she'd have chosen, but in great ugly silent gulps that she smothered against her sleeves because sound traveled in cellars and grief was a luxury for people who could afford to be heard.

She cried for the girl in the ledger. That was the worst of it—not the buyers, not the prices, not even Corvane. It was the discovery, page by page, that nobody had done this to her. She had done it to herself. Every hollow place inside her, every missing summer, every nameless morning—there was a receipt for each one, and on each receipt, the same signature in a child's careful hand.

*Edmund Thornbury.* No. Not that name. That was the name she'd taken later, the shell-name, the alias she wore like loose clothing. Under it, scraped and struck and hidden, was the name she'd insisted on selling, the first and greatest lot.

She chose this poverty, she thought. Somebody's daughter stood in front of the most dangerous man in the city and dismantled herself for coin, lot by lot, and left only the parts that couldn't be traced to anything, and grew up into a woman whose entire art was being forgotten, and called it survival, and called it discipline, and called it freedom.

What had they been running from? What had a mother and a child done—or been, or carried—that made self-erasure the safer road? What price had two hundred crowns actually paid?

The map hummed against the crate, patient as a debt.

She wiped her face. She was a professional; professionals finished the file. She turned to the last page of the disposals, expecting the final lot, the close of account.

Instead she found a single line, undated, in fresher ink than the rest:

*Lot 19. Pending. The heir returns to market when the map surfaces. All lots subject to recall. Buyer instructions on file with the Order.*

Recall. Subject to recall—as though her memories were goods in a warehouse, as though the sales could reverse. And *buyer instructions on file with the Order*—which meant Xanthe's masters, the blank-page knights, had standing orders about her. Had perhaps always had standing orders about her. Perhaps the knight she'd glimpsed in the riot outside the Guildhouse, the one whose eyes had tracked her through the crowd with an expression she couldn't name—perhaps that hadn't been hunting at all. Perhaps it had been *reading*.

Edmund closed the ledger and wrapped it in its oilcloth and did not move for a long time.

Above her, muffled through floorboards, came the ordinary sounds of Ferren's shop closing: shutters, bolts, the small honest noises of a business in sorrows. Somewhere out in the city, Corvane was looking for her with fear on his face, and the Order was looking for her with orders on file, and a dead woman's map was humming with her own sold summers, and somewhere—she was certain of it now, certain the way you're certain of a scar—a mother had sung a song with two syllables of a name in it, and that name was worth more than two hundred crowns, worth more than all of them, and it was waiting in some broker's vault or some noble's pleasure-house or some knight's sealed archive to be bought back.

Fine, she thought. Then let the buying begin.

She reached for the candle to snuff it—and froze.

From the top of the ladder, through the trapdoor she had definitely bolted, came a soft knock. Three knocks, spaced evenly. Then a pause. Then three more.

Ferren's signal for danger. Ferren's signal, specifically, for *the Order is inside the shop.*

Edmund pinched out the candle, took the ledger in one arm and the map in the other, and looked around the cellar full of other people's griefs for a way out that she was quite sure wasn't there.

The trapdoor creaked open anyway.
