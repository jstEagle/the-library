## 7. An Uneasy Truce

**Xanthe**

The Order taught Xanthe that a target was a shape, not a person. A silhouette against a doorway. A set of habits to be learned and exploited. A problem with an elegant solution: the blank page, applied.

Edmund Thornbury was not a shape. Edmund Thornbury was a woman who moved through crowds like water finding cracks, and who had spent three days making herself impossible to follow, and who had failed anyway—because Xanthe had been following her since before she knew she was prey.

He'd picked her up at the ferry stairs by watching her feet. Most people walked like they were going somewhere. She walked like she was leaving everywhere, one step ahead of a fire only she could smell. That gait was in the archive portraits too—the ones he wasn't supposed to have opened, the ones locked behind the archivist's third oath—and he had stood in the dark of the forbidden stacks with a lamp turned low, looking from a painted face two hundred years old to the memory in his own head, and felt something shift in his chest that he refused to name.

Now she was climbing. That was a mistake. Climbing meant rooftops, and rooftops meant open sky, and open sky meant he could take his time.

She came up over the parapet of the dye-works and stopped, because he was already there, sitting on the ledge with his boots dangling over the tannery district, eating an apple.

"Evening," he said.

She didn't scream. He noted that. Most people screamed, or ran, or reached for a weapon they hadn't thought to bring. Edmund Thornbury went very still, the way animals go still when they've decided the predator hasn't seen them yet, and her eyes did a fast, professional circuit of the rooftop—two exits, one chimney, his sword, his hands.

"You've been behind me since the bridge," she said. "The one with the beggar."

"The beggar works for me."

"I know. I paid him double this morning." A pause. "He took both payments, didn't he."

"He's an honest man in every way except the ways that matter."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. He catalogued it anyway.

"Give me the map," he said.

"No."

"Give me the map and walk away. You'll never see me again. That's not a threat; it's the best offer anyone in this city will ever make you."

"And if I say no again?"

"Then I take it."

She looked at him for a long moment, and what he saw in her face wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. She was weighing him—his reach, his speed, the distance between them, the drop off the roof—and he realized, with a small cold shock, that she might be faster than him. Memory-thieves trained their bodies the way they trained their trades: quietly, obsessively, against a day exactly like this one.

"Take it, then," she said.

He stood. Slowly, so she could read the movement. He crossed half the distance between them and stopped, because her hand had come up—not to fight, but flat-palmed, toward him, fingers spread, and beneath it, faintly, the air *hummed*.

It was a sound he felt more than heard, a vibration in the teeth, in the wet of the eyes. He'd been briefed on it. The map of Vellamere was a warded thing, keyed to blood, and it defended its bearer the way a hive defends a queen.

But the briefing hadn't mentioned what it did to *him*. The hum passed through his guard like his guard wasn't there, and for half a heartbeat he saw—

—a kitchen. Steam on a window. A woman singing, low, in a language that made his eyes sting because he almost understood it—

He staggered. The apple dropped from his hand and rolled to the gutter edge and went over.

"What was that," he said. His voice came out wrong.

"That," Edmund said, "was the map telling you no." She tilted her head, studying him with a new and terrible interest. "You saw something just now. Don't lie to a memory-thief, knight, it's embarrassing for us both."

"It showed me nothing."

"It showed you a kitchen," she said softly. "Steam on glass. Singing." Her eyes widened a fraction. "*Vellamere* singing. You're not just Order. You're *from* there."

"I'm from nowhere," he said, too fast, too loud.

The silence after it was very long. Somewhere below, a cart driver cursed his mule. The sun was going down behind the smoke of the tanneries, and the light on her face was copper and ash, and Xanthe thought: *I should kill her now.* His hand was on his hilt. The oath sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: *What remains shall be unmade.*

He could not move his feet.

"There it is," she said quietly. "That's the hesitation. Twelve years of service, I'd guess, and you've never hesitated, and now you're standing on a roof hating yourself for it." She took a step backward toward the chimney. He let her. "Here's my offer, since yours was so generous. You want the map. Only I can carry it—it's made that very clear. So you need me alive, walking, pointed at Vellamere. And I need someone who knows what the Order knows, which is where the door is and how to survive reaching it. We travel together."

"To betray each other at the first convenient moment," he said.

"Naturally."

"Naturally," he agreed.

They shook on it, because neither of them trusted anything less binding than a gesture.

* * *

**Edmund**

Edmund had made a career out of reading people in the space between hello and the first lie, and everything about Xanthe rang true and alarming in equal measure. True: the hesitation was real, and real hesitation was worth more than gold. Alarming: men who hesitated once either broke or doubled down, and she had no way of knowing which until the knife was already moving.

They came down off the roof separately, as a courtesy to tradecraft, and met at a noodle stall in the Shambles where the steam hid faces and the owner was deaf in the way that suited everyone.

"Eat," she said. "You look like a man who lives on duty."

"I eat when there's time."

"There's always time for noodles. It's practically philosophy." She pushed a bowl at him and watched him eat, and watched the moment the salt hit him and his shoulders came down a quarter inch, and filed that away under *leverage, humanizing*.

"Tell me about the Order of the Blank Page," she said.

"No."

"I'll tell you about the Guild of Impossible Things instead, and then you'll owe me, and debts are how people like us say *please*."

He chewed. Considered. "The Order exists to unmake what should not be. Erased things leave residue. Residue is dangerous."

"That's not an answer, that's a catechism. Say it in your own words."

His jaw worked. "Countries get erased for reasons," he said finally. "Vellamere got erased for the best of reasons. What remains—the map, the fog, the people who slipped the net—they're not survivors, they're splinters. And splinters fester." He said it fluently, the way you say a prayer, and then he blinked, as if surprised by his own voice, and she watched the doubt arrive behind the words like weather.

"Who told you that?"

"The Order."

"Who told the Order?"

Silence. Noodle steam. Somewhere a church bell counted seven, off-key.

"My turn," he said, too quickly. "Why does a memory-thief refuse to sell her most valuable asset? Corvane's ledgers list you as the finest clerk he ever trained. You could sell that map a hundred times over and vanish rich."

"Because it's mine," she said, and heard how strange her own voice sounded saying it. "I don't mean the paper. I mean—" She stopped. How to explain to a man whose whole life was erasure that she had spent hers being erased, voluntarily, piece by piece, and that the map hummed with the sound of everything she'd sold? "You saw the kitchen," she said instead.

His face closed like a door.

"You saw it, and it hurt you, and you don't know why. That's what the map does. It shows people the home they lost." She leaned forward. "Ask yourself, knight—who told the Order that Vellamere deserved to die? Because I've kept ledgers my whole life, and in my experience, whenever something is destroyed *for the best of reasons*, somebody profits."

He set down his chopsticks very carefully, the way a man sets down a weapon he wants to use.

"If you're trying to turn me—"

"I'm trying to feed you noodles. Whatever happens after that is your own doing."

* * *

**Xanthe**

He dreamed of the kitchen that night, in a room above the noodle stall because Edmund had insisted they sleep in shifts within arm's reach of each other and he had insisted the opposite and they had split the difference with a chair under the doorknob and his back against the window.

In the dream the woman at the window turned around. He woke before he saw her face, heart slamming, hand on a sword that wasn't there, and found Edmund awake across the room, watching him with her arms around her knees.

"You too," she said.

"I don't dream."

"Nobody dreams of kitchens they've never stood in, either. Yet here we are." She unwound herself and crossed to the window, pulling back the shutter an inch. Grey pre-dawn light. Rain starting. "The map's been talking to both of us. It wants us to go somewhere, and it doesn't care what it shows us to get us moving."

"Or it's showing us truth."

"Truth is just the story that survived," Edmund said. "I keep books. Believe me."

He almost laughed. It alarmed him, the almost. In twelve years of service he had not almost laughed at anything, and here was this thief with her ledger-brain and her crooked smile making cracks in him like frost in stone.

"Rules," she said, turning. "Since we're partners until we're not. One: we don't search each other's things. Two: nobody sleeps last without waking the other. Three: when one of us says *run*, we run, and argue afterward."

"And four?"

"Four," she said, and for a moment the ironclad merchant-of-memories mask slipped and underneath was something young and tired and frightened, "when we find whatever's left of Vellamere, we decide together what happens next. No oaths. No guildmasters. Us."

"That's not a rule, that's a treaty."

"Treaties have clauses for betrayal. Rules are just promises with better posture." She stuck out her hand. "Do we have terms?"

He took her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and brief. "We have terms," he said. "And clause five: whoever betrays first loses."

"Loses what?"

"Everything. Obviously." He said it lightly. They both let it stand, because the alternative was admitting they'd already started counting what everything would cost.

Outside, the rain thickened, and far off—too far off, impossibly far off, a sound that had no business being in this city at all—something hummed. The same note as the map. The same note as the kitchen window.

Edmund's head came up like a deer's.

"That's not us," Xanthe said.

"No," she said quietly, pulling on her coat. "That's the fog. And it's found our trail."

* * *

**Edmund**

They left through the coal yard behind the stall, because Edmund had bought a memory of the coal yard's layout from a delivery boy two years ago and had never expected to be grateful for it. Xanthe followed her without question through a maze of black-slick walls, and she noted that too: a knight of the Blank Page trusting a memory-thief's borrowed streets, because the humming in the air had gotten into his bones along with hers.

"Where does it lead?" he asked, low.

"Out," she said. "Out is all the map ever promised anyone." But her hand was flat against the map through her coat, and the humming had changed key, and somewhere ahead of them, past the city wall, past the drowned bell of the harbor, something vast and patient was waiting in the weather like a mother waiting at a gate.

And behind them, in the grey light of the Shambles, a man in a guildmaster's coat paid a beggar triple for everything the beggar knew, and smiled, and began to write.
