## 8. The Broker of Sorrows

The broker's cellar smelled of tallow and old rain, and it was colder than any place had a right to be in high summer. Cold, I would learn, was what grief looked like when it was stored properly—wrapped in wax and shadow, kept at the temperature of a grave.

Maren Quill received me behind a curtain of knotted string, each knot a memory she'd taken as payment for taking others. She sat on a stool too small for her, hands folded like a woman in church, and looked at me the way brokers look at everyone: appraising, patient, faintly bored.

"You're the clerk," she said. "The Guild's little ghost."

"I'm a customer."

"The Guild's little ghost," she repeated, "is never a customer. The Guild doesn't buy. It only sells." She tilted her head. "But you've come alone, without escort, paid your entry fee in coin that hasn't passed through Corvane's ledgers, and you're asking after inventory that isn't in his catalogue. So." A thin smile. "Tonight you're a thief. Sit down, thief. Tell me what you want to steal."

I told her. Not all of it—I kept Sable Vane's name unspoken even here, even now, because names are hooks and I'd spent nine years learning what hooks do—but enough. *One memory. Held by a buyer three streets east. I want it back.*

"Back," Maren said, tasting the word. "You want to drink home something you sold."

"I want to know its price."

"Ah." She unfolded her hands. "That's not a question thieves ask. Thieves ask *how*. But you were a clerk first, weren't you? Ledgers before lockpicks." She rose, joints crackling like green wood, and crossed to a shelf where jars sat in ordered ranks—not glass but something older, horn and amber, their contents shifting when you didn't look directly at them. "Then let me teach you how we price things down here, below the Guild's tidy columns. The Guild prices memories by rarity. We price them by weight."

"Weight of what?"

"What they cost the person carrying them." She took down no jar; she only rested her fingers against the shelf, reading it like braille. "A happy childhood weighs almost nothing. People give those away for bread. But a grief—" her voice dropped into something almost reverent "—a grief is the heaviest thing a soul can hold. Which means it's the dearest thing we can buy. And the only thing some sellers have left."

I thought of the ledger entries in my own hand. *One mother's lullaby, sold by a woman with frostbitten hands, price: two winters of coal.* I had written those lines a hundred times and never once wondered what the woman did afterward, in her warm house, in her quiet head.

"Maren," I said. "The buyer who holds my memory. Who was the seller?"

She turned and looked at me fully then, and whatever she saw in my face made her sigh, long and tired, like a bellows settling.

"You already know," she said. "Or you suspect, and you came anyway. That's worse." She sat again. "Her name is Odile Vasseur. She lives above the chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane. And the memory she sold—the one you're calling yours—is the death of her daughter."

* *

The chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane sold candles for funerals mostly, which meant the whole street smelled of beeswax and lilies year-round. I stood across from it for the better part of an hour while the lamps came on, telling myself I was casing the building, noting the window latches, the dog, the neighbor who watered ferns on her sill and saw everything.

I was casing nothing. I was trying to find a way out of what I'd already decided to do.

Here is the arithmetic I couldn't escape. The map hummed against my ribs where I'd bound it under my coat, and every hour it hummed louder, and every hour Xanthe's silence across the city felt less like distance and more like a drawn bow. The door out of a country that didn't exist would open, the dying woman had said with her last breath, to Vellamere's heir—and heir was a word made of blood and name both, and I had neither, having sold them both years ago for reasons the ledger recorded but my mind refused.

I could not walk through any door as Edmund Thornbury, clerk of impossible things. That name was armor, yes, but armor rusted shut. Somewhere in Odile Vasseur's head was the piece of me that could say *I am Sable Vane* and mean it, remember it, be it.

To get it back, I had to take from her the heaviest thing she owned.

There were gentler thefts. I knew them all; I'd invented half the catalogue. I could lift a memory of a color, a smell, a stranger's kindness. I could take her memory of this morning and leave her confused for a day. None of those would pay. Maren had been clear about the exchange rate: grief for grief, or nothing. The broker didn't set that price. Grief itself did. You cannot balance the scales with feathers when the other pan holds a dead child.

So I crossed the street.

* *

Odile Vasseur opened her door before I knocked. She was smaller than I'd imagined, gray-haired, with flour on her knuckles—she baked, I'd learn, because kneading dough was the only hour sleep didn't follow her into.

"You're the memory-thief," she said.

Every instinct screamed run. Instead I said, "Who told you?"

"No one told me. I dreamed it." She stepped back from the doorway, an invitation, terrifying in its ease. "Three nights running. A woman standing in the rain outside my window, holding out her empty hands. Come in, then. I've been waiting since the second night. Waiting is the worst part. I'd rather it were done."

The room inside was immaculate and airless, the way rooms get when grief has been living in them a long time and has opinions about dust. A child's drawing hung framed above the mantel—crayon suns, a house with too many windows. A girl of perhaps seven grinned out of a portrait beside it, gap-toothed, mid-laugh.

"Lise," Odile said, following my eyes. "My daughter." She said the name the way people say words in a language they're losing—carefully, from memory of the memory. "I don't—forgive me, but I don't feel it anymore, you understand. They tell me I should be weeping. The priest says grief is holy. My sister says time heals. But time took the wrong thing." She sat, folding her floury hands. "It took the pain and left the fact. My daughter died and I feel nothing, and the nothing is so loud I can't hear anything else. Is that what you've come for? The nothing?"

"Yes," I said, and hated the word in my mouth.

"And you'll give me what in trade?"

"A memory of equal weight." I had it ready; I'd carried it wrapped and warded for two days, one of my own, one I could survive losing—a summer evening, age nineteen, a rooftop, someone whose face I'd let blur on purpose, laughing until my ribs hurt. It was the happiest thing I still owned. I had chosen it precisely because it was heavy with joy, and joy, Maren had said, weighed nearly as much as sorrow when it was real.

Odile considered me for a long moment. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, the chandler trimmed wicks.

"You'll take the pain," she said slowly, working it through, "and leave me the love?"

"That's not—it doesn't divide that cleanly—"

"But it does, though. Doesn't it?" There was something almost fierce in her mildness now. "That's the trade, that's what I dreamed. You take the part that burns. I keep the part that warms. I'll remember my Lise died, and I'll remember loving her, and I won't wake at four in the morning anymore listening for a cough that stopped three winters ago." Her eyes were dry and very bright. "Everyone acts like the pain is the proof of love. It isn't. The pain is just the part that broke. Take it. Take it and let me have my daughter back, even the shape of her, even the fact of her, without the screaming underneath."

And God forgive me—because I understood her. That was the horror of it, the true horror, not the theft but the temptation. I had spent nine years amputating pieces of myself so I could walk unnoticed through the world, and I knew exactly what she was buying. Quiet. The merciful gray quiet of a wound gone numb. I wanted to sell her that quiet. I wanted it for myself.

Instead I said, "If I take it, you'll feel hollow for a while. Maybe always. Numbness isn't peace, madame. It's just numbness."

"Child," Odile Vasseur said, "you have clearly never buried anyone."

* *

I did the theft standing behind her chair, my hands on her temples, my breath matched to hers, the way the guild taught me before I taught it to others. Memory-work looks like nothing from outside—a stillness, a held moment, two people gone quiet as furniture.

Inside, it was drowning.

Her grief wasn't a jar on a shelf. It was a well, and I went down into it hand over hand, past the strata of three winters: the sickroom, the doctor's face doing its terrible gentle work, the small hand going cold in hers at dawn while rain ticked against glass. I gathered it as I descended, folding the burning parts away from the warming ones, separating scream from song, ache from love, and the separation itself was violence—there is no honest way to cut a mother's heart into pieces. I did it anyway. I was good at it. That was the worst discovery of the night: not that I could steal, but that I was *skilled*, that my hands remembered the work the way a hangman's remember rope.

At the bottom of the well, tangled in the roots of Odile's sorrow, I found what I'd come for.

My name-memory didn't look like a memory. It looked like a seed. Small, dense, warm, humming the same note the map hummed. Someone had stored it deep in her grief because grief is the coldest vault there is—nothing grows there, nothing rots, nothing escapes. I lifted it free and felt it settle against the hollow places in me like a key finding its lock, and around it, faintly, I heard—for one unguarded second—a woman singing, low, in a language I shouldn't have known and somehow knew, a lullaby about a river that runs backward—

I surfaced. Odile sagged beneath my hands like a puppet with cut strings, and I caught her, eased her into the chair, pressed the trade-memory home into the space I'd carved. The summer rooftop. The blurred laugh. Joy, offered up as ballast.

She blinked. Focused. Smiled at me—smiled, warmly, a hostess seeing out a guest.

"Thank you for the visit," she said. "Will you stay for cake? I baked this morning. Lise always liked the ones with lemon."

The cake was for a daughter who had died, offered to a stranger, remembered with love and no pain at all, exactly as promised. Exactly as purchased. I looked at this kind, hollowed woman and saw with perfect clarity what I had been doing my entire life, every ledger line, every transaction, every *price agreed*: not erasing pain but exporting it, moving the burning from one hearth to another and calling the ash a fair wage.

"It's late," I heard myself say. "I should go."

"Of course. Mind the step." At the door she touched my sleeve. "You have her eyes," she said pleasantly, meaning nothing. Meaning everything. "Goodnight, dear."

I walked six streets before I could breathe properly.

* *

Maren's cellar was warmer than I'd left it, or I was colder than I'd noticed. She took one look at me and poured something amber into a clay cup without being asked.

"You drank it yet?" she asked.

I hadn't. The seed sat in its wrapping against my sternum, humming its river-backward hum, and I found I was afraid of it in a way I hadn't been afraid of the map or the fog or Xanthe's sword. Because once I drank, I would be two women in one skin—Edmund who survived, and whoever this other one was, the one with a name and a mother's song and a country that burned. I had spent nine years building a person out of absences. Absences don't argue with each other.

"Not yet," I said.

"Sensible." Maren sat, cradling her own cup. "First taste of stolen grief sits badly. Give it an hour to stop sloshing." She studied me over the rim. "You gave her joy in trade. Real joy, yours."

"It balanced the scale."

"It did. And do you know what that makes you, in the grammar of our trade?" She smiled without warmth. "An honest thief. Rarer than you'd think and worth exactly as much, which is nothing. The scale doesn't care whose grief burns next." She set down the cup. "But since you're an honest thief, I'll give you honest change. That memory you bought back tonight—the seller didn't come to us. We went to her."

The cup stopped halfway to my mouth.

"Three winters ago. Two guild men and a broker, and the seller signed willingly, and the price was fair, and none of that matters, because here's the part your tidy conscience will chew all night: they chose her *because of you*. Word comes down from the guildmaster himself—find a grief heavy enough to vault a certain item, a grief no one will ever audit. Corvane picked the vault, clerk. Your name has been sitting in a dead child's cold storage for three years because the man who raised you wanted it somewhere not even you would look." She leaned close, and her breath smelled of the amber spirit and older, stranger things. "Ask yourself what else he's hidden in other people's sorrows. Ask yourself why a man buries a name instead of destroying it."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you paid honestly, and because I'm old, and because I've watched the Guild eat clever children for forty years and I like it better when one occasionally bites back." She waved me toward the stairs. "Go home, thief. Drink your name. And when you know who you are—come back and tell me if it was worth the well."

* *

I didn't go home. Home was a room rented under a name I no longer trusted to hold me. I climbed instead to the roof of the tannery on Tanner's Row, where the river smell covered every other smell and no one looked up, and I sat with my back against the chimney brick while the city dimmed below me, and I unwrapped the seed.

Up close it sang. The lullaby again, the river running backward, a woman's low voice, and beneath the song—faint as writing under paint—something that might have been firelight, might have been a burning.

*I am Sable Vane.*

I didn't know it yet. But I could feel it waiting, the way Odile's kitchen must have felt the cake cooling—something made, something sweet, something set aside for a child who was coming home.

I put it to my lips.

Below me, on Tanner's Row, a figure detached itself from the shadows of the cooper's yard—tall, armored under a traveler's cloak, moving with the particular patience of someone who has finally stopped following and started hunting. The lamplight caught the edge of a blank white page stitched over the heart.

Xanthe had found me. And I had just enough time to wonder whether I would meet her as Edmund Thornbury—

—or as someone the Order of the Blank Page had been sworn, for twelve years, to erase.
