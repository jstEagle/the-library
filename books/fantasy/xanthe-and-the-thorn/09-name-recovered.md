## 9. Name Recovered

The vial was smaller than I expected.

That's the thing nobody tells you about buying yourself back: the memories come in glass, and glass is small, and everything you were fits into a container the size of a thumb. My name. The first thing they took, the foundation stone, the thing every other theft stood on. It sat in the broker's cellar on a shelf between a soldier's last battle and a wedding no one attended, and it looked like nothing at all—just amber liquid catching the light of a single guttering lamp.

"Drink it slow," the broker said. She was a dried-up woman with ink-stained fingers and the particular flatness of someone who has watched ten thousand people meet themselves again. "Some of them vomit. Some of them scream. One man tried to drown himself in the rain barrel." She shrugged. "Slow."

I turned the vial in my fingers. Behind me, Xanthe stood with her back to the wall and her hand nowhere near her sword, which was its own kind of statement. We'd been traveling together three days on our uneasy truce, each of us waiting for the other to move first, and now she'd followed me down into a memory-broker's cellar to watch a thief drink herself into a stranger.

"You don't have to do this," she said. Quiet. Not gentle—she didn't do gentle—but careful, the way you're careful near a lit fuse.

"Yes, I do." The map in my satchel had hummed against my hip for three days, patient as a heartbeat. *Vellamere remembers you.* Fine. Then I would remember Vellamere. Or at least the first piece of it. "The door won't open for Edmund Thornbury. It opens for whoever I was before Corvane got his ledger-hands on me."

"And if whoever you were doesn't want to come back?"

"Then she can fight me for it."

The broker snorted. Xanthe didn't laugh, but something moved at the corner of her mouth, quickly suppressed, like a trespasser caught crossing a border.

I pulled the stopper. The smell rose up out of the vial—rain on hot stone, woodsmoke, something sweet and scorched underneath—and my hands started shaking before I'd even tipped it. Because I knew that smell. You can sell a memory, but apparently the body keeps receipts. Some animal part of me, some cellar-dweller beneath all the erasures, recognized what was coming and wanted it and feared it in the same breath.

*Be forgotten, stay alive.* That had been my rule since I was eleven years old and Corvane taught me how a ledger works. Rule number one. And here I was, about to break it in the most thorough way imaginable—not forgetting harder, but remembering. Becoming visible. Becoming findable. Becoming, by any measure that mattered, a person with a past that other people could want dead.

"Edmund," Xanthe said again.

"That's not my name," I said. "That's the point."

I drank.

*

It did not taste like anything. That was the first surprise. The second was the cold—a cold that started behind my eyes and spread outward like frost across a window, whiting out the cellar, the lamp, the broker's flat gaze, Xanthe's watchful face. For one horrible moment I thought I'd been poisoned, that this whole thing was a trap, that Corvane—

And then the frost cracked, and through the crack came singing.

A woman's voice. Low, unhurried, a little rough at the edges, the way voices get when they've spent years talking over wind. She was singing a song with words I shouldn't have known and knew perfectly—the syllables fit my mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for. *Hush now, hush, the river knows your name,* and there were more verses, verses about a door and a garden and a country that kept its people the way a coat keeps warmth, and I was small, I was so small, sitting on a kitchen floor with flour on my knees while my mother sang and kneaded bread above me—

My mother.

I had a mother. Of course I had a mother; everyone has a mother. But I had never once thought of her, because there had never been an *her* to think of. There was a hole where she should be, a hole I'd lived around my whole life the way a tree grows around a nail, and now the nail was gone and the tree was screaming.

The memory unfolded like a map being opened in a dark room. A kitchen. Blue tiles, cracked at the corner, mended with a chip of white ceramic shaped like a fish. A window over the sink looking out on a street where the houses leaned toward each other like conspirators. My mother's hands—I had her hands, I realized, those long fingers, that crooked left knuckle—and her voice saying a word over and over, a word that was me:

*Sable.*

"Sable," I said aloud, and my mouth knew it, my tongue knew it, and something behind my sternum turned over like a boat coming about. Sable Vane. Sable Vane of the blue-tiled kitchen, of the leaning street, of the song about the river that knows your name. Twenty-some years of being Edmund Thornbury, forgettable, unremarkable, a smudge of a clerk—and underneath, all along, pressed flat and sold off in portions, a girl named Sable who had a mother who sang.

Then the fire came.

It arrived in the memory the way weather arrives—no drama, just a change in the light. Through the kitchen window, the sky over the leaning houses went orange. My mother stopped singing mid-verse. Her hands went still in the flour, and then she was moving, scooping me up, and I was being carried down stairs that shook, out into a street full of ash falling soft as snow, and the whole time she kept saying my name like a spell against the burning—*Sable, Sable, look at me, don't look at the sky, look at me*—and I could hear the city dying around us. Bells. So many bells. The Drowned Bell district of some city I couldn't name yet but would know forever, its bells ringing alarm and then ringing grief and then, one by one, going silent as their towers fell.

Someone screamed a word into that smoke. A word shouted from a doorway, from a rooftop, from everywhere at once, the name of what was happening to us, and the memory flinched away from it—flinched, the way a body flinches from a brand—and I lost it, reached for it, and came up gasping in the broker's cellar with my cheeks soaked and my throat raw and no idea how long I'd been on my knees on the cold stone floor.

"Sable."

Xanthe was crouched in front of me. When had she crouched? Xanthe did not crouch; Xanthe stood at parade rest inside other people's crises. But there she was, close enough that I could see the lamplight caught in her eyes, and she was holding out a cup of water like it was the most natural act in the world, like knights of the Order of the Blank Page carried water for weeping strangers every day.

I took it. Drank. My hands wouldn't steady.

"How do you know that name?" I asked, and my voice came out wrong. Rougher. With an accent in it I'd never heard myself use, vowels rounded differently, a lilt at the end of sentences like a tide going out. Vellamere's accent. My accent. Rising out of me like a drowned thing surfacing.

"You said it," Xanthe said. "Twice. While you were under."

"What else did I say?"

She hesitated. I watched her decide whether to tell me the truth, watched the oath war with whatever had been growing in her since the tavern, since the portrait in the forbidden archive, since the moment she'd first looked at my face too long. "You said *Mama, wait.*" A pause. "And then you said something in a language I didn't understand. And then you sang."

"I don't sing."

"You sang."

*

We went up out of the cellar because the walls felt like they were listening, and because I needed sky, even the bruised grey kind this city offered. We ended up on the roof of the broker's building, among chimney pots and pigeons, with the sprawl of the capital laid out below us—its thousand chimneys, its fog-bound streets, its bells that rang hours instead of warnings.

I sat with my arms around my knees and tried to hold two lives in one head.

Here is what nobody tells you about becoming two people at once: it isn't like adding a room onto a house. It's like discovering the house has been built on the roof of another house, an older one, a burned one, and you've been living your whole life in the upper story thinking the floor beneath you was bedrock. Edmund Thornbury was still there—still knew ledgers, still knew how to be forgotten, still flinched at raised voices and trusted locked doors. But Sable Vane kept rising through the floors. Sable knew the song. Sable knew the smell of her mother's bread. Sable knew, with a certainty that lived in her bones rather than her mind, that the city in the burning memory had been called Vellemar Row, and that it had stood beside a river with a name like a held breath, and that the bells of her childhood had been cast from melted farm-bells when the country unified, and that—

That she had done something.

That was the fragment that stopped me every time I circled back to it. Underneath the fire, underneath the singing, there was a door in the memory—a door I'd flinched from—and behind it a shape of knowledge so heavy my mind slid off it like water off oilcloth. I had done something. A child had done something, or been made to do something, and it had to do with the fire, and the knowing of it was still out there, sold, scattered among buyers I'd have to hunt down one vial at a time.

"It gets worse before it gets better," I said to Xanthe, or maybe to the pigeons. "The broker warned me. Each memory you take back recontextualizes the others. Right now I'm grieving a home I can't even picture properly. Give it a week and I'll probably grieve it better."

"You're doing it again," Xanthe said.

"Doing what?"

"Making it small. Joking at it." She was standing at the roof's edge, silhouetted against the grey, and she hadn't looked at me directly in several minutes, which from Xanthe meant she was looking very carefully indeed. "In the cellar. When you came up. You weren't just crying about a kitchen, Edmund."

*Sable,* said something inside me. *She called you Sable and you didn't correct her.*

"No," I admitted. "I wasn't."

"Your mother died in that fire. Or you believe she did. You sang her song while you were under, and then you said—" Xanthe stopped herself. Recalibrated. She handled feelings the way she handled blades: only when necessary, and always by the grip. "I've watched soldiers remember things they sold. To pay debts. It breaks most of them. They come back up hating the memory, hating themselves for selling, hating whoever made them poor enough to need to sell."

"Do you hate whoever made me poor enough?"

The question hung there. Because we both knew who had made me poor. We both knew whose ledger my life was written in, whose neat clerical hand had recorded each sale—childhood, name, face, door—in columns balanced to the copper. Corvane had raised me. Corvane had taught me that being forgotten was the same as being safe. And somewhere along the line, without my ever seeing the knife, he had carved me hollow and sold the pieces, and I had helped him do it, had signed the entries myself, because I'd believed him. Because he was the only father the void had left me.

"He told me it was my choice," I said. "Every sale. He'd sit across the desk with his little scale and say, *Edmund, no one is forcing you. Memory is currency, and you're rich beyond your years.*" I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and cracked. "He never mentioned whose memories they were. He never said *these are yours*, plural. He let me think I was selling off excess. Souvenirs. Things that happened to other people, almost."

"And were they?"

I looked at him—at her—at Xanthe, who had asked the question a knight sworn to erase Vellamere should not have been able to ask, and I realized she already suspected the answer, had maybe suspected it since the archive, since the portrait, since the dying woman's blood on my hands.

"No," I said. "They were mine. All of it was mine. And I think—" My voice failed, restarted. "I think I sold them on purpose. Not just for money. I think there's a reason I chose to be empty, and it's behind that door in the memory, and I'm terrified of it, and I'm going to open it anyway."

Below us, the city went on with its evening. Lamps blooming street by street. Somewhere a drunk was singing badly, and the tune snagged against something in me—no. Not something. Someone. My mother's song wore the same bones, the same rising-and-falling shape, worn smooth by centuries of mothers singing it to children in a country that officially had never existed.

"Sing it," Xanthe said.

"I told you. I don't sing."

"You sang in the cellar. You'll sing it wrong if you don't sing it now, while it's fresh. Trust me. I've seen men lose languages overnight because they were ashamed to speak them badly."

So I sang.

Badly. Cracked and wavering, in an accent that wasn't mine and entirely was, the words rising out of some cellar-deep place that had kept them safe through twenty years of erasure: *hush now, hush, the river knows your name.* Two lines. Three. On the fourth line my voice broke and I stopped, and Xanthe, who had gone very still, said quietly:

"The archive had songs. In the restricted stacks. Songs from the erased territories." Her jaw worked. "They're listed as hazards. Contagious nostalgia. We were trained to report anyone heard singing them."

"And instead you're asking me for a concert."

"Instead I'm asking you for a concert." She turned and finally looked at me straight on, and there was something raw in her face that she clearly hated, something at war with twelve years of doctrine. "Sable Vane. Is that what you want to be called now? Because I should know. If I'm supposed to kill you, I'd rather know your real name first."

The vertigo hit me right then, standing on that rooftop—two names, two women, one body. Edmund, who survived by being forgotten. Sable, who had been loved enough to be sung to. I could feel them pulling in opposite directions like a tide between moons, and I understood, with the clarity that comes after weeping, that I could not keep both. That eventually I would have to choose which woman walked through the rest of this story, and that choosing might be the hardest thing the map ever asked of me.

"For now," I said, "call me whichever name you're about to betray me under."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Noted."

We came down from the roof as full dark settled. And in the doorway at the bottom of the broker's stairs, a boy was waiting—one of the feral ledger-runners the guild used for messages, breathless, eyes darting, holding out a folded paper sealed with black wax.

For me. Addressed not to Edmund Thornbury, clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things.

Addressed, in Corvane's precise hand, to *Sable Vane, heir of Vellamere.*
