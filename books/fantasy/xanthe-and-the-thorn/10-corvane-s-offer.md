## 10. Corvane's Offer

Corvane had always believed that fear was a currency like any other, and that the trick of wealth was knowing when to spend it and when to hoard. He had hoarded his own for forty years. He had spent other people's freely.

So it was a small humiliation—logged, filed, and never to be spoken aloud—that he learned of Edmund's location from a woman whose grief he had purchased for the price of a warm coat.

The informant's name was Tessa Marrow, and she had sold him three things over the years: her sister's face, her wedding day, and last winter, the memory of her mother's dying words. What remained of Tessa was efficient, loyal in the way of hollowed things, and cheap. She came to him at the ninth bell with rain on her shoulders and said, "The clerk is hiding in Ostler Vane's cellar off Cordwainer's Row. She bought a candle and didn't haggle."

Edmund Thornbury did not fail to haggle. It was one of the first lessons Corvane had taught her, back when she was small enough to sit on his knee while he balanced the guild's books: *Everything has a price, little ledger, and the person who doesn't ask for it is either rich or dead.*

She wasn't rich.

"Prepare the coach," he said. "And bring Wren."

Wren was not a memory-thief. Wren was what the guild kept for the rare occasions when memory failed and muscle would have to do. He nodded once and went to oil his hands, which was how he prepared for everything.

Corvane stood at the window of his office above the counting floor and watched the city go about its evening. Lamplighters moved down the boulevard like slow sparks on a fuse. Somewhere out there, a girl he had raised from nothing was sitting in a broker's damp cellar, reading a ledger she should never have been able to open, learning things he had paid a great deal to keep quiet.

He was not angry. Anger was for men who still believed the world could be controlled. He was afraid—and fear, properly managed, was simply information wearing its best coat.

*Vellamere remembers you.*

The dying woman's words had reached him by morning, secondhand and expensive. He had burned the messenger's report after reading it, then sat very still for an hour doing sums in his head, and the sums all came out wrong.

---

* * *

The coach took them through Cordwainer's Row at half past ten, when honest people were asleep and dishonest ones were busy. Corvane went down into the broker's cellar alone. He left Wren at the top of the stairs, because this next part required a softer instrument.

Ostler Vane met him at the bottom, wringing his cap. The man had the look of a ferret that had smelled a larger animal enter its burrow.

"She's not here," Vane said. "I mean—she was. I mean, I don't sell information, guildmaster, you know me, we've done business—"

"You sold her a candle," Corvane said mildly, "and told Tessa Marrow where she slept." He pressed a coin into the ferret's palm. It was gold, and it was also a message: *I know what you are, and I am choosing, tonight, to find it useful.* "Which room?"

Vane pointed with a hand that shook only slightly more than the rest of him.

The room at the end of the corridor had been a coal store once. Now it held a pallet, a stub of candle, and Edmund Thornbury, awake, dressed, and sitting against the wall with a knife already in her hand—which meant she'd heard the coach, heard his footsteps, catalogued their weight, and decided not to run.

Good girl. Bad sign.

"Master Corvane." She didn't get up. The candle threw her shadow long across the ceiling, so that it seemed to loom over both of them, though neither of them was tall. "I wondered when you'd come."

"I sent six letters."

"I read four of them. They were very warm."

"The fifth had money in it."

"It did." Her eyes were steady and dark and utterly unreadable, which had taken her fifteen years to perfect and cost her, if the rumors were true, nearly everything else. "I used it to buy the candle."

He laughed. He couldn't help it; she had always been able to pull laughter out of him at the worst moments, like a splinter. He lowered himself onto an upturned crate with the care of a man twice his age, folded his hands over his stick, and let the silence do some work first. Silence was the oldest tool in his kit. Most people filled it with confessions.

Edmund let it stand between them like furniture.

"You've been reading my ledgers," he said at last.

"Your ledgers are my ledgers. I kept them for eleven years. You never changed the lock on the archive because you never imagined I'd need it." A pause, precise as a knife finding the joint. "You imagined wrong."

"And what did you learn, little ledger?"

"That I'm for sale," she said. "That I've been for sale since before I could walk. That someone bought my childhood in installments and no one thought to tell me I was the merchandise." She tilted her head. "Oh—I learned that too. From your own hand. Page four hundred and twelve. *Item: one country, erased entire. Buyer: the Crown. Seller: this office.* You wrote it down, Master Corvane. You write everything down. It's the only sentimental thing about you."

He had known this conversation was coming. He had rehearsed it on the coach ride over, turning phrases like a man testing keys. But hearing page four hundred and twelve spoken aloud in her flat clerk's voice did something to his chest that he chose to interpret as indigestion.

"Yes," he said. "I did write it down. Do you know why?"

"Because you're a coward who trusts paper more than people."

"Because paper can be *corrected,*" Corvane said, "and people cannot. Sit properly, Edmund. You'll want to hear this standing or sitting, but you will want to hear it, and I'd rather you didn't fidget through it."

To his mild surprise, she put the knife down. Not away. Down.

---

* * *

"Thirty-one years ago," he began, "a king came to this office in disguise. He was a nervous man, our king. Kings usually are; the job selects for it. He brought us the strangest commission the guild had ever received, and he paid for it with things that cannot legally be owned, which is the only kind of payment we accept."

Edmund said nothing. The candle guttered and steadied.

"He wanted a country unmade. Not conquered—conquest leaves ruins, and ruins leave questions. Not annexed—maps are stubborn things; ink bleeds. He wanted it *forgotten*. Every border, every birth record, every song. He wanted a hole in the world shaped exactly like Vellamere, and he wanted the hole to be seamless."

"And the guild said yes."

"The guild negotiated," Corvane corrected. "Erasure on that scale is not a theft, Edmund, it is a *demolition*, and demolitions require specialists. We subcontracted. There was an order involved—the Order of the Blank Page, you may have heard the name; they wear white and believe themselves surgeons rather than butchers. We provided the architecture. Memory does not simply vanish, whatever the knights tell themselves. It must be *moved*. Held. Filed somewhere no one thinks to look."

His voice had gone quiet without his permission. He cleared his throat.

"We built a vault. The largest ever constructed. Not stone—stone can be found. We built it out of forgetting itself. Every stolen memory of Vellamere, pooled together, dense enough to hold its own shape. A country made of absence, stored in the one place guaranteed never to be searched."

"Where?" Edmund asked. Her voice had changed. Thinner.

Corvane looked at her—at the sharp face he knew better than his own reflection, the face he had taught to be forgettable, feature by feature, year by year—and felt the fear move in his chest like water finding a crack.

"In a child," he said. "In the only survivor we couldn't erase cleanly. Children resist, Edmund. Their memories root too deep; pull them whole and the child dies, and dead witnesses leave graves, and graves leave questions. So we left her the country. All of it, packed down where no divination could reach, behind a door she herself had locked. And we made sure she would never, ever go looking."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"No," Edmund said.

"You were seven," Corvane said gently, and hated himself for the gentleness, and did it anyway, because gentleness was a tool and the tool was sharp. "You had a mother's voice in your head and a burning city behind your eyes and you wanted it to stop hurting more than you wanted anything in the world. I offered you exactly that. I've never lied to you, little ledger. I offered you peace at wholesale rates and you signed."

"I was *seven.*"

"Yes," he said. "And I have regretted the arithmetic of it every day since. But regret is not a strategy, and here is the problem that brings me to your cellar at midnight." He leaned forward. "The map that dying woman gave you. Do you know what it is? It isn't paper. It's a *key*, cut from the vault's own lock—and the vault is you. Vellamere remembers you because Vellamere *is* you. It has been sleeping in your skull for twenty years, and something has started knocking."

For the first time since he'd entered, Edmund looked afraid. He noted it, filed it, and pressed on before pity could slow his tongue.

"When the vault stirs, the seal weakens. When the seal weakens, the country leaks. Fog that whispers. Villages that shouldn't exist. Knights of the Blank Page reporting anomalies they were trained never to report. And when the Crown learns that its most expensive purchase is developing cracks—" He spread his hands. "Kings are like any buyer, Edmund. When the goods fail, they come for the seller."

"So you're here to fix me."

"I'm here to make you an offer." He drew it out now, the speech he had polished on the ride, each clause weighted and tested. "One. Wealth beyond anything the ledgers show—a life with no haggling, no cellars, no running. Two. Protection. The Order wants you dead; the Crown will want you worse; I have spent thirty years buying people on both sides and I can buy more. Three—" and here he let his voice soften into something almost kind, because he had learned long ago that the kindest voice sold the cruelest goods— "three: permanent forgetting. The whole of it, clean this time, done by masters. No fragments. No fog. No mother's voice in the dark. You wake tomorrow as a woman with no past and no debts, and you stay that way until you die old and warm and *unremarkable*, which, my dear, is the only happy ending this trade has ever actually sold."

Silence.

Edmund had picked the knife back up. She wasn't pointing it at him; she was just holding it, the way a person holds something solid when everything else has gone liquid.

"You want to empty me again," she said. "Because I'm leaking. Because I'm a bad vault. Because your king's purchase is defective and you'd rather scrap it than refund it."

"I want you to *live*," Corvane said, and the terrible thing—the thing he would carry to whatever accounting awaited him—was that part of him meant it. He had fed her, trained her, taught her the value of everything including herself. Whatever else he was, he had been the closest thing to a father the child had been permitted to keep.

But he needed her compliant more than he needed her alive, and he suspected, looking at her face in the candlelight, that she knew it. That she had always known it. That was the trouble with raising a clerk: they learned to read the entries behind the entries.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

He rose from the crate, joints protesting, and straightened his coat. At the top of the stairs, he could hear Wren shifting his weight, patient as furniture.

"Then I grieve you," Corvane said. "Publicly, movingly, and in writing. And the Order receives an anonymous letter naming the last heir of Vellamere, currently residing off Cordwainer's Row." He paused at the foot of the stairs and looked back at her one last time. "You have until dawn. You were always quick with deadlines."

He climbed into the coach and did not let himself look back again.

---

* * *

Later, much later, in his office above the counting floor, Corvane opened his private ledger—the real one, the one kept in no archive—and turned to a page near the end. He dipped his pen and wrote, in a hand steadier than he felt:

*Item: one heir, offered everything. Refusal: probable.*

He sat with the pen hovering. Below it, in smaller script, he added the entry he had been avoiding for twenty years, the one he had never dared set down even in a book no one would ever read:

*Item: one mistake, thirty-one years old, compounding daily.*

*Note to self: the vault was never the girl.*

*It was what she agreed to forget in order to survive me.*

He blotted the page, closed the book, and blew out the lamp. In the dark he listened to the city and tried to calculate how long it would take a girl with a knife, a map, and nothing left to lose to reach the border of fog.

His estimate, when it finally came, was: less time than he had.

And far to the south, where the roads dissolved into whispering white, something that had been sleeping inside Edmund Thornbury for twenty years rolled over in its sleep—and knocked again.
