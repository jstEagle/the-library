## 11. The Order of the Blank Page

Xanthe was seven years old the night Vellamere burned, and what she remembered of it—what she had been permitted to remember—was this: her mother's hands pushing her through a window, and the smell of smoke that tasted sweet, like burnt sugar.

Everything else had been taken.

She learned this much later, at nine, when she asked Brother Cassian why she could not remember her father's face. He had sat her down in the scriptorium of the Order's citadel, a room with no windows and walls lined with empty books, and he had explained it to her the way one explains rain to a child. Patiently. As though it were weather.

"You came out of Vellamere," he said. "Vellamere was a country of memory. Not memory like yours or mine—not recollection. Memory as a substance. They hoarded it. Traded it. Grew fat on other people's pasts while their own rotted." He turned a blank page toward her. "A country like that doesn't fall, child. It spreads. Its memories leak across borders like floodwater. Whole villages woke up remembering lives they'd never lived. Children wept for mothers who weren't theirs. Men killed each other over grievances from centuries ago, believing those grievances were their own."

"And so?"

"And so the Order unmade it. Wrote it out. Every record, every map, every mind that carried it. The world is safer with a blank page where Vellamere used to be."

Xanthe looked at the blank page for a long time. "But I remember the smoke," she said. "And my mother's hands."

Brother Cassian smiled, and there was something in the smile she would spend twenty years trying not to see again. "Those are yours," he said. "The Order leaves you your own grief. It's all we can leave anyone."

* * *

They trained her the way other orders trained priests: in silence, in discipline, in doctrine. But where priests learned scripture, the knights of the Blank Page learned absence.

She memorized the liturgy of erasure at ten. *What was written can be unwritten. What was remembered can be forgotten. Mercy is sometimes subtraction.* She learned the eleven rites of excision—the careful surgical work of removing a thing from a mind without killing the mind. She learned that a botched excision left holes, and holes festered, and festering holes became the very plague the Order existed to prevent.

At twelve they gave her her first practice subject: an old soldier, dying anyway, whose service in Vellamere's border wars had left him full of memories the Order wanted catalogued before removal. Xanthe sat with him for six hours, drawing the memories out one by one into vials of grey glass, and the old man grew lighter as she worked. By the end he was smiling. He didn't know his own name anymore, but he was smiling, and when she asked him if he was afraid, he said:

"Afraid of what?"

That was the lesson, she understood then. Fear lived in memory. Remove the memory and you removed the fear. What could be kinder than that?

She told herself this at twelve, and thirteen, and seventeen, and twenty-two. Repetition is its own kind of memory, and she built herself a wall of it, stone by stone, against the days when the work went wrong.

There were days when the work went wrong.

* * *

At fifteen, she erased a woman's entire marriage. The woman's husband had died carrying Vellamere contraband—memory-vials sewn into his coat lining—and the Order's doctrine was absolute: anything touched by Vellamere was contaminated, including the grief of those who loved the carriers. So Xanthe knelt before the widow with her instruments laid out on white cloth, and she drew out eight years of marriage in ninety minutes, and when she finished, the woman looked at her wedding ring and asked, politely, whose it was.

Xanthe took the ring too. Doctrine required it. The ring had been touched by the husband; the husband had been touched by Vellamere; the chain of contamination was clear.

She kept the ring in her kit for three years before she could make herself bury it.

At eighteen, she was sent to a village near the old border where a smuggler had died and his memories had leaked into the town well—not literally, but the Order used the word anyway, because the townsfolk had begun dreaming his dreams. A fisherman dreamed of a woman in Vellamere and woke weeping for her. A child of four described a burning city in detail no four-year-old should possess. Xanthe spent a month there, house by house, drawing dreams out of sleeping minds, and on the last night the fisherman's wife took her aside.

"He still wakes up happy," the woman said. "Before, he woke up happy. Now he just wakes up. Is there a difference, knight? Tell me honestly. You've done this enough times to know."

Xanthe had opened her mouth to give the doctrinal answer—*peace is worth more than joy; joy can be corrupted; peace cannot*—and found, to her lasting alarm, that she did not know where the answer lived in her anymore. Whether it was conviction or just repetition worn smooth into the shape of conviction.

"I've seen the alternative," she said finally. "I've seen what leaks do to towns."

"So have I," the woman said. "It was my husband doing the leaking. And you know what? He loved her, whoever she was. Some part of him loved her for thirty years and never told me, and your Order cut that out of him, and now he loves nothing, because apparently love is contamination." She looked at Xanthe with terrible mildness. "You're young. You still think you're a surgeon. One day you'll count how many people walk out of your work lighter and how many walk out emptier, and you'll notice which number is bigger."

Xanthe reported the conversation to Brother Cassian. He praised her honesty. He did not answer the question. She noticed, over the years, that he never answered the questions. He only re-stated the doctrine, the way a mason re-lays a loose brick.

* * *

At twenty-three she saw the archive.

No knight was supposed to see it. It lay beneath the citadel's oldest wing, behind a door keyed to the Grand Inquisitor's blood, and Xanthe found it by accident—a drainage crawl-space from her youth, half-collapsed, that opened into a corridor no map acknowledged. She told herself afterward that she had gone in to seal it. She had gone in because something in her, some animal instinct older than doctrine, wanted to know what the Order was afraid of.

The archive was one room, and it was full of faces.

Portraits, hundreds of them, hung floor to ceiling: men, women, children, painted in a style she didn't recognize, with colors that seemed to move slightly when she wasn't looking directly at them. Each portrait bore a small brass plate. Names. Vellamere names—she knew them on sight now, having spent her life hunting their traces, though no record anywhere was supposed to contain them.

The portraits themselves were impossible. The Order had erased Vellamere root and branch. Nothing of it should exist. Yet here was a room of it, curated, maintained, dusted. Someone had been keeping these safe.

Or keeping them prisoner.

She found her own face at the far end of the room.

Not exactly her face. A girl of perhaps five or six, dark-haired, solemn-eyed, painted with the strange shifting colors—and beneath it a brass plate that read, in a language she had never been taught but somehow read fluently, *Sable-line. Last confirmed heir.*

Xanthe stood in front of the portrait until her legs went numb. She did not remember the girl. Of course she didn't. She had been born in Vellamere; everything of Vellamere had been cut from her; whatever connection this painting claimed, it belonged to a self she had never met.

But the eyes. She knew the eyes the way you know your own hands in the dark. She had seen them every morning of her life in polished steel and still water, and here they were, younger, looking back at her from a country that had never officially existed.

Someone had painted her before the fall. Someone had known, even then, that she mattered—that the last heir of a doomed country was worth preserving in oil and brass, hidden beneath the very institution sworn to erase her.

Who had hidden her here? Who had wanted her found?

She copied the name from the plate onto the inside of her gauntlet, in ink so faint it might have been sweat. Then she sealed the crawl-space behind her, and reported nothing, and told herself that the silence was tactical. A knight did not trouble the Grand Inquisitor with anomalies she couldn't yet explain. A knight gathered evidence first.

She was good at telling herself things. She had been practicing since she was seven.

* * *

Now, years later, standing in a dockside tavern in Grayharbor with rain coming off the sea in sheets, watching a woman with forgettable features and ledger-clerk's hands refuse to surrender a bleeding map, Xanthe felt the itch again.

It had started in the archive. It had grown through the years like a hairline crack in a dam—invisible, deniable, patient. Every excision fed it a little. The widow asking whose ring it was. The fisherman waking up instead of waking up happy. The doctrine said mercy was subtraction, but Xanthe had performed thousands of subtractions now, and she had started keeping a private count, the way the fisherman's wife had told her to. Lighter versus emptier. She hated the number. She kept counting anyway.

And now this clerk. Edmund Thornbury, the guild ledgers called her, though the name felt like a coat worn over something else. Xanthe had tracked her through three districts and a locked cellar, and every step of the way the woman had moved like someone running on stolen streets—borrowed escapes, purchased instincts—and yet underneath the thefts there was something else, something that made the hair on Xanthe's arms rise when she looked at her too long.

Recognition. That was the word, and she despised it. She had never seen this woman before in her life. But some chamber deep beneath the excisions—the sealed rooms, the walled-off corridors of her own mind—was hammering on its door.

The dying stranger in the tavern had pressed the map into the clerk's hands and said, *Vellamere remembers you.* Xanthe had heard it from the shadows. The words had gone through her like a blade finding the gap in armor, because she had spent her whole life being told that Vellamere remembered nothing—it had been unwritten, unmade, reduced to a blank page—and yet here was a map humming with the country's memory, choosing its bearer, calling her by some name Xanthe couldn't hear.

And the clerk's eyes, when she turned them on Xanthe in the rain, were the eyes from the portrait. Older. Harder. Sold off and bought back in pieces, maybe. But the same.

*Last confirmed heir.*

Xanthe's oath was simple, and she had never once failed to keep it: *Where Vellamere remains, I remain, until nothing remains.* The map was Vellamere. The clerk was Vellamere. Both were hers to erase.

So why was her hand steady on her sword and her heart hammering like a traitor's?

Why did the doctrine feel, tonight, like a brick laid over a crack—laid and laid and laid, twenty years of laying—and why could she suddenly hear, beneath all that masonry, water moving?

The clerk bolted for the tavern's back door, and Xanthe went after her, and told herself it was duty.

She was getting very good at telling herself things.

But as she ran, one thought kept pace with her, quiet as the rain: *If the Order hid her portrait, then the Order knew she was alive. And if the Order knew, and sent me anyway—*

The alley ahead was empty. The clerk had vanished, the way forgettable people do.

Xanthe stood in the rain and, for the first time in twelve years of faithful service, prayed—not to any god the Order sanctioned, but to the blank page itself.

*Show me what was written here.*
