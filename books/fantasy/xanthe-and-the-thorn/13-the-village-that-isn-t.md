## 13. The Village That Isn't

The map stopped making sense two miles past the fog.

That was the first sign. Xanthe had been navigating by it for days—holding it flat between gloved hands, reading the ink as it crawled and reformed like something alive and mildly annoyed. But past the fog line the roads on it began to fold. A river crossed itself three times. A mountain range appeared in four places at once, drawn from slightly different angles, as if four cartographers had argued about where it stood and the map had refused to choose.

"It's not wrong," I said, when Xanthe turned it upside down for the third time. "It's honest."

"Explain that."

"The map isn't drawing a place anymore. It's drawing a fold." I took it from her—the thing let me, warm as a loaf fresh from the oven, while it had burned Xanthe's fingers twice—and held it toward the gray light. "Places can be creased. When enough memory pools in one spot, reality puckers. Like cloth."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I've spent ten years living inside other people's memories of places they've never been. You learn what a fold feels like." I handed it back. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be. Since the fog, since my mother's voice had come whispering out of the mist and Xanthe had grabbed my collar and hauled me back from a road that went nowhere, something in me had gone quiet. Not calm. Quiet the way a house is quiet after an argument. "There's a village ahead. Or there was one. The map is trying to show us both."

Xanthe looked at me the way she'd been looking at me more often lately—not with suspicion, exactly, but with the wary attention of someone watching a fire decide whether to spread. Twelve years of erasing countries will do that to a person's face. It makes a kind of architecture of the jaw.

"If there are survivors," she said slowly, "my Order has protocols."

"I know what your protocols are. I've filed them."

"Then you understand why I have to ask. Are you going to run again?"

I thought about it honestly, which surprised us both. "No," I said. "But not because you've earned my trust. Because whatever's ahead is going to need both of us to see it clearly, and neither of us sees clearly alone."

She almost smiled. It was a small, grim thing, gone before it landed. "That's the most Vellamerian thing you've ever said."

"You don't even know what Vellamerians sound like."

"No," she agreed, and shouldered her pack, and started walking. "I don't."

* * *

We found the village by falling into it.

One step the road was bare hillside, sheep-cropped grass and gorse and a wind with nothing to say. The next step the ground remembered itself. Cobbles rose under my boot mid-stride, and I stumbled forward onto a street—a narrow, crooked street lined with slate-roofed houses leaning together like conspirators, chimneys smoking, window boxes spilling geraniums that had no business blooming in this season or possibly in this world at all.

The smell hit me first. Woodsmoke and wet stone and something baking—dark bread, caraway—and underneath it all, faint as a watermark, the smell of the sea, though we were a hundred miles inland.

"Oh," I said. Just that. One syllable, and my voice cracked down the middle of it.

"Sable." Xanthe's hand was on her sword hilt, but she didn't draw; we'd passed the stage of that, mostly. Her eyes were moving fast, cataloguing exits, threats, numbers. "The fog. This whole valley is inside the fog, isn't it? Folded up small."

"That's what folds do." I was still staring at the cobbles. There was a groove worn down their center, the kind made by centuries of cart wheels, and I knew—I knew without knowing how—that carts had come down this street every market day carrying salt fish and bolts of dyed wool, and the wool came in colors with names that existed nowhere else. *Gullwing. Emberwater. The blue of my mother's good dress.* I pressed a knuckle against my sternum. "Careful," I managed. "The memories here aren't fenced in. They'll get into you."

"Good," Xanthe said.

I looked at her sharply. She'd gone pale under the windburn.

"I've spent twelve years being told this country deserved to be erased," she said. "If its ghost is going to pour into me through my boots, then let it. I want to know what I did."

Before I could answer—it wasn't clear I would have; my throat had closed—a door opened.

Not dramatically. That was the thing about the village: nothing about it was dramatic. A door simply opened, the way doors do, and a man stepped out with a bucket in one hand and a look of mild curiosity on his face, and he saw us, and he did not see us. His gaze slid off us like rain off waxed canvas. He went to the well at the end of the street, cranked up water, walked back. He never once looked at us again, though he passed within arm's reach, close enough that I could see he was whistling—an old tune, six notes rising and falling, and somewhere very deep in me something answered it, hummed along, and I nearly went to my knees.

"They can't perceive strangers," Xanthe said quietly. "It's not that they're ignoring us. We're not... arriving."

"We're not anywhere." I understood it then, all at once, the way you understand a ledger entry only after you've totaled it. "That's what erasure does. The Order doesn't kill countries. It makes them unfindable—to maps, to history, to themselves. These people are alive, Xanthe. They're just alive outside."

"Outside what?"

"Everything."

* * *

They noticed us eventually. But not because we arrived.

An old woman found us at dusk, sitting in the shelter of a wall at the village edge where the cobbles frayed back into grass. She was tiny, wrapped in a shawl the exact color of emberwater—I knew that, I knew that color's name—and she carried a lantern though the light hadn't fully failed. She stopped ten feet away and looked at us with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I'd never seen it before in my life: she was looking *at* me. Fully. Without sliding off.

"Well," she said. "Well, well."

"Can you—" My voice came out wrong. "You can see us?"

"See you? Girl, you're lit up like a bonfire." She came closer, unhurried, studying my face the way I studied ledgers. "Pooled memory holds this place together, do you understand? Every soul here gives a little each day to keep the streets solid and the bread baking and the sun behaving itself. Memory is the mortar. And strangers—" she waved the lantern at us, "—strangers are holes in the fabric. The village pours around you and doesn't notice. But you're leaking too, the pair of you. Grief does that. Grief is loud. The pool heard you coming."

"Heard us," Xanthe repeated.

"Mm. Heard one of you especially." The old woman raised the lantern.

And then her face changed.

I watched it happen the way you watch weather. First confusion, then disbelief, then something so raw and enormous that I instinctively stepped back and put my shoulder against Xanthe's, which was absurd, because the old woman weighed less than the lantern. But grief that size displaces air.

"No," the woman whispered. "No, no. *Sable.*"

My name. Not Edmund. Not Thornbury, not Dunmore, none of the shells I'd worn like borrowed coats. Sable. Spoken in a voice that had known it before I did.

"How do you know that name?" Xanthe's sword cleared the scabbard halfway. I put my hand flat on the blade and pushed it down. I don't know where I found the authority. Maybe I didn't; maybe she let me.

"I sang it to sleep," the old woman said, "for six years." She set the lantern down on the wall with great care, as if her hands could no longer be trusted with it. "I'm Wren. I was your mother's music teacher. I played the harp at your naming, and you screamed through the whole ceremony, and your mother laughed and said you'd already formed opinions." Her eyes were streaming now, without any change in her voice at all, as if tears were simply a thing her body did while the rest of her attended to business. "You have her chin. Gods forgive me, girl, you have her chin exactly."

"I don't remember you." It came out flat, terrible. "I don't remember any of this. I sold it. Before I knew what I was selling, I sold it, and I don't—"

"I know what you did." Wren's voice gentled. "The pool knows everything that happens near Vellamere, child. We felt you erase yourself. Felt it the way you feel a door slam three streets away." She reached out—asked permission with a tilt of her head first, which undid me more than anything else—and touched my cheek with two dry fingers. "And we felt why. We've never blamed you. Say you hear that. We have never blamed you."

"Why?" The word tore loose from somewhere below my ribs. "Why did I do it? Everyone keeps circling it. Corvane, the map, the fog, my mother's voice—all of it points at that one locked room, and nobody will—"

"Not tonight," Wren said, and there was iron under the softness. "Tonight you eat soup and sleep under a roof that remembers you. Some doors open easier fed." She gathered her lantern and turned, then paused. "Both of you. Even the knight."

"She's not—" I started.

"She's sworn to kill us all, yes, dear. I was at the naming, I wasn't born yesterday. Soup's soup."

* * *

The hall they fed us in was low-beamed and crowded and smelled of tallow and onions, and it was the worst place I have ever been in my life, because everyone in it was happy.

Not blindly. Not stupidly. They knew what they were—they spoke of it the way farmers speak of drought, plainly, without shame. Every morning each adult gave a memory to the pool: a birthday, a lover's laugh, the layout of a street they'd grown up on. In exchange the pool gave back solidity—walls, weather, bread, children who grew. They lived thin. They lived on purpose. A woman across the table told me she'd given up her wedding day three years ago and sometimes caught her husband looking at her with the patient sorrow of a man whose wife has forgotten something he cannot name, and they'd chosen together never to tell her what it was, because the telling would cost more than the keeping.

"And it works?" Xanthe asked her quietly. "The pooling. It holds?"

"It holds." The woman shrugged. "Nothing holds forever. The edges fray. Last winter the eastern orchard forgot it was an orchard and became a hill, and forty families' apples became a story about apples." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired around the edges. "But we're alive, knight. Outside existence, maybe. But alive, and together, and free. Ask us if it's worth it."

Xanthe didn't ask. I watched her sit with the question instead, turning it over like a coin of suspicious mint, and I thought: *there it is. The crack. Right on schedule.*

Wren sat beside me and did not press. She told me small things instead, deliberately small: that I'd been afraid of geese. That my father—gone before the fall, before the erasure, drowned or lost or simply gone, even the pool didn't hold him—had made toys from driftwood. That my mother's name was Lira and she sang second alto and once, at the harvest festival, had held a note so long the priest gave up waiting for the hymn to end.

Each fact landed in me like a stone dropped into a well I couldn't hear the bottom of. No echo. No recognition. Just the knowledge of depth.

Afterward, walking back through the cold streets with Xanthe a half-step behind me—she'd appointed herself my shadow somewhere around the third day of our truce and neither of us had discussed it since—she said, "You're crying."

"I'm aware."

"Do you want—"

"No." I wiped my face with my sleeve, briskly, the way you settle accounts. "Yes. I don't know. Walk with me and don't talk."

She walked. That, too, was new.

At the village well we stopped, because the pool was there—or rather, I finally *felt* it there, a vast slow warmth under the cobbles like a sleeping animal, and it stirred when I stopped, the way a dog stirs when its person pauses at a gate.

"It recognizes you," Xanthe said. Not a question.

"It recognizes my hole." I put my palm flat on the well's stone lip. Warm. Body-warm. "Every memory I sold left a socket in me, and the pool can see them, the way you can see the missing teeth in a comb. That's how Wren knew my name before I said it. My shape is drawn here. I'm a negative image of this place."

"Sable." She used the name carefully, testing its weight. "What happens if you take it back? All of it. What happens to a hole that fills?"

I looked at her. Windlight, chimney smoke, her face more open than I'd ever seen it and frightened by its own openness.

"I stop being forgettable," I said. "That's the whole architecture of my survival, Xanthe. Ten years of being nobody, of slipping through the guild's ledgers unwritten, of standing next to people and having their eyes slide off. If I fill these sockets—if I become Sable Vane, daughter of Lira, child of a country that officially never existed—then I become findable. Legible. A person with a shape that can be gripped." I swallowed. "Corvane wouldn't have to hunt me anymore. He'd just have to look."

* * *

I woke before dawn to Wren shaking my shoulder, and her face in the candlelight was wrong—not grieving now, but urgent, urgent the way people are urgent about floods.

"The pool woke me," she said. "It wants to speak to you. Both of you. At the source, under the chapel."

"Now?"

"It's been waiting eleven years to speak to anyone outside. It says the waiting is over." She pulled me upright, and her grip was stronger than her size suggested. "Sable. Listen to me. It's showing the whole village your face. Every dreamer in this place is dreaming you tonight. Whatever it wants, it wants badly, and it thinks you're the one who can pay."

"Pay," I repeated. "Pay for what?"

Wren's mouth tightened. "To stay," she said. "It's offering you belonging, child. A place at the pool. Your sockets filled, your name kept safe, a hundred souls to stand between you and the guildmaster forever." She met my eyes, and hers were dry and hard and old. "And it wants you to understand, before you say yes, what belonging costs in a place like this. Nothing here is free. Especially not love."

Down the lane, Xanthe was already awake, dressed, armed, standing in the chapel doorway with the map in her hands—and the map was burning. Not consuming itself. Glowing. Its ink had begun to move in long deliberate strokes, writing something neither of us had ever seen it write, and Xanthe read it aloud in a voice gone very quiet:

"*The heir comes home. The door remembers. Three days.*"

Three days. Then Corvane's footsteps would find the end of whatever trail we'd left, and this fold between places—with its soup and its songs and its hundred stubborn, generous souls—would have to be somewhere else, or be nothing at all.

I looked at Wren. I looked at Xanthe. Somewhere beneath my feet, a country's last heart waited to make me an offer.

"Show me the source," I said.
