## 14. What Erasure Costs

Xanthe had killed for the Order of the Blank Page eleven times.

She could recite each one. Not names—the erased had no names, that was the point—but coordinates. A farmhouse outside Mereholt where a widow had kept her husband's letters from a country that never was. A bookbinder in Corassin whose shop held three volumes of Vellamere poetry. A ferryman who claimed he'd once rowed across a river with no other side. Eleven small fires, eleven small ashes, and every time she'd knelt afterward and spoken the words: *The page is clean. The page is blank. What was written was never written.*

She had believed it the way you believe a floor will hold your weight. Without thinking. Without looking down.

Now she stood in a village that wasn't there, and the floor gave way beneath her.

* * *

They'd come down into the fold at dawn, following Sable's map through fog that sang in a dead woman's voice, and Xanthe had expected—what? A camp of madmen. A shrine. Another trick of the borderland, like the whispers, like the road that bent when you weren't watching it.

Instead: a village. Forty houses, give or take, timber and thatch, smoke rising honest and gray from honest chimneys. A well with a rope worn smooth by generations of hands. Goats. Children—children, running barefoot between the gardens, chasing a dog that barked like any dog in any country that existed.

And the people. They'd come out of their doors slowly, the way you approach something wounded. An old man first, leaning on a scythe he clearly hadn't sharpened in years. Then others, filling the lane, and Xanthe's hand went to her sword because forty years of training said *crowds are threats*, but no one moved toward her. They moved toward Edmund.

Toward Sable.

An old woman pushed through them—small, bent, eyes like river stones—and stopped an arm's length away. She looked at Edmund for a long time. Long enough that Xanthe saw Edmund's breath go ragged.

"Little Sable," the old woman said. "You got out."

"I don't—" Edmund's voice cracked. "I don't remember you."

"No," the old woman said gently. "You paid not to. We all watched you pay." She turned then, and her gaze found Xanthe, and everything in her face closed like a door. "But she brought a Blank Knight into our fold. That I did not watch coming."

* * *

They didn't tie Xanthe up. That was somehow worse.

They fed her instead—barley bread, hard cheese, tea brewed from something that tasted like pine and grief—and seated her in the largest house while the whole village crowded in around the walls, silent, watching. Edmund sat across from her, and between them on the table the old woman laid a book.

It was not a beautiful book. It was water-stained, re-stitched twice with different thread, its cover scarred as if it had been carried through fire. Which, Xanthe would learn, it had.

"The parish record of Lower Vellamere," the old woman said. "Births. Marriages. Deaths. My hand wrote half of these entries—I was the record-keeper, before I was anything else." She opened it. "Do you know what your Order told you we were, knight?"

"A plague," Xanthe said. Her voice came out flat. "A contagion of memory. A country that infected the world with things that shouldn't be remembered."

"Mm." The old woman turned pages. "And what happens, they told you, when we erase a country? The memory dies clean. The people die with it. A mercy, even—a painless ending for a thing too dangerous to live."

"That's what the doctrine says."

"Look at my book."

Xanthe looked. The entries were ordinary. Weddings. Harvests. A child named Merrin born in the spring of some year, a fever taken two winters later, mourned by name. Ordinary, except—

Except the ink was wrong. She'd seen enough forged documents in twelve years of erasure work to know paper that had been altered. These pages hadn't been altered. They'd been *fought over*. Whole passages sat ghosted beneath newer writing, like words pressed through from the other side of the sheet. Names surfaced and sank. Dates shifted. And in the margins, in a dozen different hands, small annotations had been added and scratched out and added again:

*still here*

*who remembers Merrin? say it aloud, someone must say it aloud*

*day 4,112. bread holds. we hold.*

"It's a ledger," Xanthe said slowly. "You're keeping yourselves alive in a ledger."

"We're keeping ourselves alive in *each other*," the old woman said. "The ledger helps. Shared memory is rope—we knot ourselves together so the current can't pull us under. But understand what happened, Knight. When your Order unmade Vellamere, it didn't burn us. Burning would have been kinder, and cheaper. No." She tapped the ghosted pages. "Erasure doesn't destroy. Erasure *excludes*. It takes a thing out of existence without granting it the dignity of ending. We are not dead. We are not alive. We are outside, looking in through glass, forever."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a goat complained, absurdly mundane, and Xanthe clung to the sound like a railing.

"There are children here," she said. It came out stupidly, but it was the only thought she could hold.

"There are children here," the old woman agreed. "Born after the fall. Do you know what it is to be born outside existence, Knight? To have a mother and no birth? The world has no slot for you. You cannot leave this fold—you'd thin out like breath on cold glass within a day's walk. You cannot be seen by anyone who isn't already frayed at the edges, which is why we could only ever reach thieves and debtors and the forgotten. People like her." A nod toward Edmund. "People like *you*, though I confess I didn't expect the Order to fray anyone."

"They don't," Xanthe said. "I'm here on assignment."

"You're here because the map let you follow her," the old woman said, "and maps don't lie about what a person is." She leaned forward. "Tell me what they taught you. Tell me exactly. You were orphaned, weren't you? In the fall?"

The word landed like a blade finding the gap in armor. "How do you—"

"Because everyone the Order trains was orphaned by the Order. It's efficient. Grief makes excellent mortar." The old woman's voice softened by a degree, and somehow that was crueler than cruelty. "You burned a widow's house outside Mereholt. Eleven years ago. There were letters in it from a man named Aldous Crane, who rowed refugees across the Serpent's Mouth during the fall. He was my nephew. He made it out. He lived four years in your world, forgetting a little more each day, waking some mornings not knowing why his hands smelled of river water. Your fire took the last proof he'd existed."

Xanthe's mouth was dry. "That was a sanctioned erasure. The letters were contraband—"

"*Contraband.*" Someone along the wall laughed, short and awful. A middle-aged man with a carpenter's forearms. "My daughter's drawings were contraband. She drew our street. Our actual street. She was six."

"She was outside existence when she drew them," the old woman continued, relentless, gentle, terrible. "Nothing she touched should have persisted in your world. But memory leaks, Knight. Love leaks. That's the contagion your Order fears—not plague, *leakage*. Proof. Every letter, every drawing, every song hummed wrong was Vellamere knocking on the door of the world, saying *we are still here, we are still here.* And your Order's answer was to burn the knocking hand."

Xanthe looked down at her hands.

They were steady. They were always steady. Twelve years of steadiness, drilled in white cloisters by masters who spoke of mercy and meant tidiness. She had knelt in eleven patches of ash and called it cleaning. She had gone home to the archive and shelved the ash reports beside weather records.

*I am complicit,* she thought, and the thought didn't shatter her, which was worse—it just settled in, cold and permanent, like a stone at the bottom of a well. *I am not the sword. I am the broom.*

"Say it," the old woman said quietly. Everyone in the room had gone still. Even Edmund was leaning forward, watching her with an expression Xanthe couldn't read—pity, maybe. Pity was unbearable.

"Say what?"

"What you're thinking. Out loud. It's how we survive here—things said aloud hold better than things thought. Say it, and witness us holding it."

Xanthe lifted her head. Forty faces. A ledger full of ghost-names. A nephew named Aldous who had smelled of river water.

"My Order didn't end a plague," she said. Her voice didn't shake, which felt like the last treachery—that even now, trained composure held. "We stranded forty thousand people outside existence and called it hygiene. And I swept up the evidence for twelve years and slept soundly after." She swallowed. "I'm sorry. It changes nothing. But I'm sorry."

The silence stretched. Then the carpenter said, gruffly, to the room: "Witnessed." And a murmur went around the walls—*witnessed, witnessed*—like a tide, and Xanthe understood that in this place apology was not absolution. It was inventory. It was an entry in the ledger. Nothing forgiven. Everything recorded.

She found she preferred it that way.

* * *

Afterward, Edmund found her at the well.

The sun had climbed; the fog had burned off the valley mouth, and for a moment the village looked almost like a place, like somewhere with a name on a map. Almost.

"You didn't have to apologize," Edmund said.

"Yes, I did."

"Not what I mean." Edmund leaned against the well's stone lip, arms folded. "You didn't have to *mean* it. I've watched you operate, Xanthe. You corner me, you negotiate, you keep your oath folded up in your pocket like a knife. I assumed the faith was load-bearing. Break it and the whole building comes down on both of us."

"Maybe it has."

"And yet you're still standing here. Talking to me." Edmund tilted her head. "Why didn't you strike? Back in the city. You had me against a wall twice. The map answers to me alone—you could have taken me hostage, dragged me to your masters, forced the issue. Instead you've spent two weeks walking beside the person you're sworn to erase. So before we go any further into whatever this is—tell me the truth. Why do you hesitate?"

Xanthe looked at the water in the well. Dark, still, holding a coin of sky.

"In the archive," she said finally, "there's a restricted gallery. Portraits of the erased, kept as reference for identification work. Forbidden to enter without sanction. I entered it anyway, years ago—first crack in the discipline, though I didn't know it then. There's a portrait there of a girl. Vellamere-born. Painted the year before the fall." She met Edmund's eyes. "She has your face. Not similar. *Your face.* I told myself it was coincidence. I told myself a lot of things."

Edmund had gone very still. "What was her name?"

"The plaque was scratched out. Someone in the Order got there before me." A pause. "Or the portrait was painted from memory, and memories leak."

"So your whole life," Edmund said slowly, "your order, your oaths, your eleven fires—they took everything from you, and then handed you a broom and called it a calling. And the first thing you ever stole was a look at your own face on their wall of the damned."

"When you say it like that—"

"It's true when I say it like that." Something moved across Edmund's expression—understanding, or its beginning. "I sold my own past to survive. You had yours confiscated to serve. We're both walking around with holes where we should be, and everyone who dug those holes told us it was mercy." She exhaled. "Gods. We should be enemies. It would be simpler."

"It would," Xanthe agreed.

Neither of them moved.

The distance between them was perhaps three feet, and it seemed suddenly to contain everything—the wall-corner in the city, the fog that sang, the way Edmund had gripped her arm when the whispering started, the way Xanthe had pulled her back without calculating the tactical cost. Three feet, and Xanthe was aware of every inch of it the way she'd never been aware of anything in twelve years of perfect discipline.

"Sable," she said—the true name, deliberately, watching it land.

"Don't," Edmund said, but softly, without force. "If you use it like that I'll start believing you mean it kindly."

"Maybe I mean it kindly."

"Maybe is a dangerous currency in my trade."

"Then let me spend something certain instead." Xanthe turned to face her fully. "My oath says Vellamere's last traces must be unwritten. As of this morning, I believe my Order is the reason there are traces to write. I don't know what I am now, if not their blade. But I know I'm not going to stand in this village and help them finish it." She paused. "That's not love, Sable. Don't mistake it. It's just the first honest thing I've said in twelve years."

"I know," Edmund said. "It's not love." And then, quieter, looking at the well-water instead of at her: "It's a start, though. Starts are also inventory."

From the largest house, the old woman was watching them over the ledger, and Xanthe had the distinct feeling of being written down.

Then the feeling became something else entirely. Because the old woman had gone rigid, ledger slipping from her fingers, and she was staring past them—at the valley mouth, where the fog was returning.

Fog did not return at midday. Fog did not move *with purpose*, folding down the slopes in ordered ranks like soldiers taking formation.

And ahead of it, riding unhurried through the birch trees, came riders in gray, and at their head a figure Xanthe knew in her bones, a man whose ledgers she had once audited, whose voice she had heard pronounce eleven sentences of ash.

Corvane had found the village.
