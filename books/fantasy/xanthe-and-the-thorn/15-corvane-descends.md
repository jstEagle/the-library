## 15. Corvane Descends

### Edmund

The village woke us with bells that had no bronze in them.

I sat up in the loft of the tanner's house—Sable's house, someone had said, though I couldn't feel the truth of it yet—and looked out through the gap in the shutters at a sound I could see but not touch. The bells rang from everywhere and nowhere, the way a name rings in your skull after you've forgotten whose it was. Down in the lane, people were already standing in their doorways, faces tilted up toward a sky that was doing something wrong.

"It's early," Xanthe said behind me. She was already dressed, already armed, which told me everything before her face did. "Corvane doesn't do early."

"He does when he's afraid."

"Corvane isn't afraid of anything."

I turned. In the grey light she looked carved rather than born, the Order's blank sigil stitched white on white over her heart, and I thought—not for the first time—that loving her was going to be the least practical thing I ever did.

"He's afraid of doors he can't lock," I said. "He told me so once. He thought he was telling me a story about a client."

Outside, the fog at the valley's mouth had begun to move against the wind.

* * *

The villagers gathered in the square around the well that held no water—the well that held *them*, all those pooled memories passed hand to hand for thirty years like a flame nobody was allowed to set down. Old Maren stood at its rim, small and straight-backed, and when she saw me pushing through the crowd she didn't look surprised. That frightened me more than the fog did.

"You felt it coming," I said.

"I've felt it coming since the day you arrived, child." Her hands were steady on her walking stick. "Nothing draws the hunters like an heir come home."

"We can run. There are folds between places—I've seen how you move—"

"And leave this?" She touched the well's dry stone the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head. "This is Vellamere, Sable. All of it that's left. You don't pack up a country like a bundle of clothes."

"The country is already gone." My voice cracked on it, and I hated that. "That's the whole point of what they did. What *I* did."

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Around her, the villagers were passing memory-handfuls down the line, feeding them back into the pool, and I understood what I was watching only as the fog reached the first houses: they were arming themselves. Not with blades. With everything they had left.

"They'll buy it," Maren said quietly. "Your guildmaster. He won't burn us if he can purchase us instead. Erasure costs money, girl. Memory costs less."

"How do you know that?"

"Because we sold to his buyers once before, in the first years, when we were hungry and foolish." Her eyes were ancient and terribly clear. "And every coin we took told him exactly where we were."

* * *

They came out of the fog at midmorning, and they came *shopping*.

That's the word my mind kept reaching for, and I despised it, because it was accurate. Corvane walked at the front of maybe forty figures—half guild brokers in their grey coats, half knights of the Blank Page in white—and he carried no weapon. He carried a ledger.

He stopped twenty paces from the well, from Maren, from me, and opened the book across his forearm like a merchant greeting a customer he'd been expecting.

"Maren Ostrevik," he said. "Born Vellamere, registered erasure 411th year of the Blank Calendar. It's been a long time."

"Not long enough," Maren said.

"I'm here to make you an offer." Corvane's voice carried the way mine never had—the voice of a man who had bought a thousand rooms full of silence and learned to speak into them. "The Guild will purchase your pooled memories. All of them. At fair assessed value. In exchange—" and here he glanced at me, just once, the glance a father gives a daughter who has embarrassed him at table, "—the Order withdraws. No fire. No unmaking. You walk out of this valley as you came into it: unremembered, unmolested, alive."

"And then?" Maren asked.

"Then nothing. Then you continue as you have been."

"Liar," I said.

Every head in the square turned to me. Corvane didn't. He simply waited, patient as interest accruing, until I stepped forward because not stepping forward would have been worse.

"You don't close doors," I said. "I've kept your ledgers for eleven years, Corvane. I know what you charge to forget and I know what you pay to remember, and there's one entry type I could never reconcile. Purchases of location. Purchases of *approach*. You're not buying their memories to erase them." I pointed at the ledger. "You're buying the road to the door."

For the first time since the drowned bell, since the map humming against my ribs like a second heartbeat, Corvane smiled at me with genuine warmth, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all morning.

"Clever girl," he said. "I didn't waste my money raising you."

"You raised me like a stock portfolio."

"A successful one." He closed the ledger. Behind him, the knights hadn't moved, but the fog had thickened around their boots, drinking the color out of the cobbles. "Here is the truth, since you've earned a sliver of it. A door out of an erased country is the single most valuable object in existence. Do you understand why? Because anything that can be exited can be entered. And anything that can be entered can be *owned*. A king paid us a fortune to close Vellamere, Edmund. Another king will pay ten fortunes to open it. I intend to be the only broker standing between them."

"You'd sell a whole country's way out."

"I'd sell *anything*." He said it without shame, without heat, almost tenderly. "It's the only honest trade there is."

Maren spat at his feet. "We won't sell."

"No," Corvane agreed, and sighed like a man forced to discount his wares. "You won't. Which is why we've already begun."

He lifted one finger. And in the crowd behind me, a villager—a boy, sixteen maybe, with flour still on his sleeves—went suddenly, horribly quiet, and put both hands over his own mouth as if holding something in, and then walked, calm as Sunday, toward the well, and began to drink.

Not water. There was no water. He drank the pool itself, the pooled memories, swallowing handfuls of other people's childhoods, and as he swallowed he spoke in a flat broker's recitation: street names. Landmarks. The turnings of the fold between places. The way to the door.

"He sold three days ago," Maren whispered beside me. "Through a dream-broker. We caught it too late."

The fog drank another house. Somewhere a woman screamed a name, and the name came apart in the air like smoke.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way fires happen fast, and afterward I would keep trying to slow it down and never manage it.

Xanthe broke from the knights' formation first. I saw her do it—one moment white-clad among the white-clad, the next moving through the crowd toward me with her sword still sheathed, which from a knight of the Blank Page is either mercy or mutiny, and I genuinely could not tell which. She reached me, gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise.

"The granary," she said. "There's a root cellar. If they take the pool intact they don't need to burn—"

"But he'll burn anyway."

"Yes." No flinch. That was the thing about Xanthe; she handed you the worst fact first, unwrapped, like a gift. "He'll burn it because burned things can't testify. Come *now*, Edmund."

But I was looking at the well. At Maren, who had climbed its rim and stood above the boy who was eating her country handful by handful, and who looked down at me with an expression I will carry until I die, which is saying something, because I carry everything.

"Sable," she said. My true name, in her mouth, sounded like a bell with bronze in it. "You were always the ledger-keeper. So keep this."

She opened her arms wide over the pool, over the boy, over thirty years of a stolen nation hoarded in dry stone, and she began to sing—in Vellameri, a language I did not know and knew completely, a lullaby in a mother's voice that wasn't my mother's and was every mother's—and the villagers took it up, all of them, even the hollow-eyed boy faltering between swallows, and the song went into the pool and the pool went up in light.

Corvane shouted the order. Fire, but not fire—white fire, page-fire, the Order's clean erasure rolling off the knights' blades in sheets—and Maren sang through the first breath of it and the second, and then the third took her, and where she had been there was a shape of brightness and then nothing, not ash, not bone, just a Maren-shaped absence in the world, the way a word looks when you stare at it too long.

The village burned out of existence from the edges inward. Houses went first, then lanes, then the well itself—but the pool fought it. I felt it fight, felt the pooled memories resist the white like a fist resisting a closing hand, and in that last grinding moment the whole of Vellamere poured itself outward, indiscriminate, desperate, into anyone standing near.

Into me.

Into Xanthe.

* * *

I came back to myself facedown on a hillside two miles north, grass in my mouth, Xanthe's arm locked around my waist where she'd dragged me. Below us, the valley was empty. Not ruined—*empty*, the way a page is empty. No village. No scorch marks. No bones. Just a green valley that had never held anything, ringed by fog that had stopped whispering.

My hands were shaking. Inside me, where the map used to hum alone, there was now a crowd. I could feel them: Maren's lullaby. A baker's burnt loaves. A fisherman's cold mornings. Thirty years of small stubborn living, homeless now, sheltering in the one place left that remembered Vellamere.

Me.

"They gave it to us," Xanthe said hoarsely. She was sitting in the grass, staring at her own hands as if they belonged to the enemy, which, I supposed, they had. "The pool. When it burned. It—it went into everyone in reach, but mostly us, because we were closest, because—" She stopped. Started again, and her voice did something I'd never heard it do. "Edmund. I remember a harbor. I remember a harbor that isn't in any atlas, and a woman teaching me net-mending, and I have never been to any of it and I remember it like my own skin."

"That's what he wanted," I said, and the rage came up through me like floodwater through floorboards, cleaning nothing, drowning everything. "Don't you see? Maren was right—he bought the boy to find the door, but the burning, the burning was always going to scatter the pool. Into survivors. Into witnesses. Into *carriers*." I got to my feet. The fog below us stirred, and for one moment it shaped itself into something like a doorway, and I heard Corvane's voice in my memory, warm as a banker's handshake: *anything that can be exited can be owned.*

"He doesn't want the door closed, Xanthe. He never did. Closed doors are worthless. He wants it *open*—under lock, under key, under contract. He wants to own the only way out of a country that no longer exists, and now the map to it is inside us."

Xanthe stood slowly. She drew her sword—not toward me, never again toward me, I understood that now—and drove it point-down into the turf between us, a knight's oath made to no Order at all.

"Then we get there first," she said.

Behind us, down in the emptied valley, the fog parted briefly around a single figure walking upslope with a ledger under his arm, unhurried, following our footprints through grass that had no business remembering them.

Corvane had counted on us running.

He had priced it in.
