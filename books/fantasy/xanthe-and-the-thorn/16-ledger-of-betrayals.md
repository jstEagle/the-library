## 16. Ledger of Betrayals

They did not tie my hands.

I noticed that first, before the cell, before the corridor, before the door at the end of it that I knew better than any door in the world because I had catalogued its hinges in a ledger entry eleven years ago. *Iron-bound oak, left side drags on stone, oil quarterly.* They didn't tie my hands because Corvane had told them not to, and Corvane hadn't told them not to out of mercy. He'd told them not to because he wanted me to understand that I was not a prisoner. Prisoners have rights. Prisoners can be ransomed. What I was, as far as the Guild of Impossible Things was concerned, was inventory that had walked off the shelf.

The two men who had taken me on the road outside the fog valley — brothers by their faces, strangers by their manners — walked behind me the whole way and never once spoke. That was a guild technique too. Silence is cheaper than rope. You put enough quiet around a person and they start filling it themselves, with fear, with confession, with bargains. I had watched it work from behind ledgers for years. I had written the margins notes: *Subject talks at hour three; subject talks at minute forty.*

So when the silence stretched past an hour of corridors, I started talking to myself instead. Not aloud. In the old clerk's cadence, the one I used to keep the books straight.

*Item: one captive clerk. Condition: bruised, unwashed, carrying nothing but what's in her head. Provenance: stolen from the Order of the Blank Page by persons unknown. Value: pending.*

That last word kept snagging. *Pending.*

Because here was the thing I had not told Xanthe, had barely admitted to myself while we ran through borrowed streets toward a fog that sang: I knew what was behind that door with the dragging hinge. I had sat on the other side of the desk behind it for six years. I knew how Corvane took his tea — bitter, no honey, because sweetness reminded him of something he'd paid to lose. I knew which drawer he kept his real ledger in, the one beneath the false-bottomed one where he let clerks like me snoop. And I knew — this was the part that mattered, the part I had been turning over like a coin all the way down the mountains — that Corvane had once been a seller, not just a buyer. That somewhere in the deep archive of the Guild of Impossible Things, filed under a name nobody used anymore, there were entries in his own hand recording memories *he* had sold.

Clerks keep everything. It's the whole disease of us. And Corvane had made the mistake of raising his best clerk without ever quite letting her stop being one.

The door dragged open on its hinge, right on schedule.

"Edmund." He didn't look up from his desk. Of course he didn't. Theatrics are for people who need them. "Sit."

The chair across from him was still the green one, the one I'd worn smooth over six years of bringing him accounts. Sitting in it now felt like trying on a coat that had shrunk. Or maybe I'd grown. Hard to say which.

"You're not going to offer me tea," I said. "You only do that when you want something small. When you want something large, you make me wait for it."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Corvane's face had been trained out of smiles sometime before I met him, and I'd always assumed the memory he sold to manage that had been an expensive one. "Six years," he said, "and you still think you can read me like a column of figures."

"I don't think it. I know it. I *wrote* the figures."

He set down his pen at last. Looked at me. And there it was again, the look from the night I brought him the map — the one I'd spent three days telling myself I'd imagined, because the alternative was unbearable. He looked at me the way a man looks at property that has learned to walk.

"The woman at the Drowned Bell," he said. "Tell me about her."

"Why? You already know. You've bought someone's memory of it by now, or you'll have bought mine within the week. That's how this works. That's how *you* taught me it works."

"That's how the trade works," Corvane said mildly. "I taught you bookkeeping. There's a difference, and you used to know it." He laced his fingers together on the desktop. His hands were older than I remembered. When had they gotten older? While I was running, presumably. Everything aged while I was busy. "You've caused me a great deal of expense, Edmund. Two retrieval teams. A broker in Sallow Street whose cellar you burned—"

"I didn't burn anything. Xanthe knocked over a lamp."

"—and the considerable embarrassment of explaining to the Order why their knight has gone missing alongside our missing asset." He said *asset* the way other men said *dear girl*, and that was the whole man in one sentence. "I'm prepared to overlook it."

"Are you."

"I'm prepared to do considerably more than overlook it." Now he rose, and went to the window, and stood with his back to me, which was a performance, because Corvane never turned his back on anyone unless he wanted them thinking about knives so they wouldn't think about ledgers. Outside the window, Greyharbor lay in its usual grey, gulls wheeling over roofs that had forgotten more than most cities ever knew. "The map, Edmund. Where is it?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Gone in every way a thing can be gone. Buried. Given away. Destroyed. Pick whichever answer costs you the most sleep."

He turned. And for one unguarded half-second — I'd have missed it if I hadn't spent six years learning the grammar of his face — he looked afraid. Not of me. Of the answer. Then the shutters came down, smooth as ledger paper closing over a column.

"You were a child when I found you," he said. "Starving, nameless, half-mad with grief you couldn't source. I fed you. I taught you a trade. I gave you a rule — *be forgotten, stay alive* — and that rule kept you breathing through twelve years in a city that eats people like us for breakfast. Whatever sentiment you imagine exists between us, understand: it is not required for what happens next. I will find the map. I will find the door. And you will either help me or become a footnote in your own story. Which fate, I promise you, is worse than it sounds."

There it was. The offer under the threat, the way there always was. Corvane never wasted a threat; threats were expensive, and he itemized them.

And I should have been afraid. A week ago I would have been. But a strange thing had happened to me between the fog valley and this chair: I had stopped being a person who owed Corvane everything and started being a person who could count what he owed. Grief does that. So does rage. So, most efficiently, does having drunk back pieces of yourself and discovered that the frightened girl who signed her first sales contract in this very room had been lied to in ink she couldn't read yet.

"May I see the archive index?" I said.

Corvane blinked. It was the first honest expression he'd produced all morning. "What?"

"The master index. The one you keep in the third drawer, coded by buyer, not seller. You think I don't know about it because you never showed me. But Edmund Thornbury has spent six years being invisible, and invisible people see everything, and also—" I leaned forward, and heard my own voice go flat and pleasant, the clerk's voice, the voice I'd used on a thousand sellers signing away their weddings and their dead, "—you have ink on your cuff. Same ink as the index. Blue-black with iron gall. You updated it this morning. Before you even saw me. Which means you came into this room planning to show it to me eventually, or planning to hide it from me eventually, and either way, it's on your mind."

He looked at his cuff. He actually looked. And I understood, sitting in the green chair with my hands loose in my lap, that I had him — that whatever happened next, I had already won the part of this that mattered, because Corvane had spent forty years making sure no one could read him, and here I was reading him like a market report.

"What do you think you know?" he asked quietly.

"Shall I recite?" I said. "I have a good memory. It's practically the family business."

* * *

Here is what I knew, and here is how I knew it.

A ledger-keeper sees everything that crosses the books, including the entries other people believe are private. Corvane's own sales were filed under a cipher — a thorned rose, stamped in violet wax — because even in the Guild of Impossible Things, the guildmaster's private transactions were meant to be unreadable. But ciphers repeat. Wax cracks. And once, three years ago, chasing a misfiled entry on behalf of a client, I had pulled the wrong folder, and the right one had come out with it, and I had looked.

I had looked, and then I had put it back exactly as it lay, and gone home, and not slept, and come in the next morning and said nothing. Because the rule was *be forgotten, stay alive*, and knowing what I knew, saying something would have been the fastest route in Greyharbor to being forgotten permanently.

But rules change. Rules are like doors.

"There's an entry dated the winter before you found me," I said. "Seller's cipher: thorned rose, violet wax. One memory sold. Large lot. The largest single lot in the guild's history, actually — I checked, because I'm thorough, and thoroughness is the only virtue anyone ever managed to beat into me. An entire year of a life. Sold to a buyer recorded only as *the Crown's agent*." I watched his hands. Hands tell you what faces won't. "And here's the beautiful part, the part that kept me awake for a week three years ago and has kept me warm ever since: the payment wasn't money. Money would have shown on the books. The payment was a *service*. Rendered by the buyer to the seller. And services rendered get logged, guildmaster, because everything gets logged, because you taught me that. Logged as: *one child, delivered, unharmed.*"

The fire cracked. Somewhere below us, the guild went on with its day, brokers weighing griefs by the ounce.

"A child delivered unharmed," I repeated softly. "To a starving, nameless, half-mad girl found on the docks that same winter. You weren't my salvation, Corvane. You were the *consideration*. I was the change he got back."

"You don't know what you're talking about."

"I know arithmetic. You sold a year of your life to a king's man, and what you bought with it was me. Which means the king wanted you to have me. Which means the king wanted something from you that required owning the person closest to you, and the thing a king wants from the guildmaster of impossible things is—" I let it hang, because making him say it was worth more than saying it myself.

Corvane sat down. Slowly, the way old men sit down when the chair is suddenly far away. "You were supposed to be a kindness," he said, and for a moment — a single moment — the voice was almost human. "That was the shape of it. A girl orphaned by the erasure, delivered into my care, and in exchange I surrendered—" His jaw worked. "It was a year I did not want. A year I would have paid anything to be rid of."

"And what was in it?"

Silence.

"What was in the year, Corvane?" I leaned forward. "Because I've thought about this. I've had nothing but time on the road to think about it. A man doesn't sell a whole year to buy himself a ward unless the year contains something worse than poverty. Something he'd trade a kingdom's favor to stop seeing every time he closed his eyes. And I know what the guild was doing that winter, because I've read those books too. That was the winter of Vellamere. That was the winter the door closed and the country stopped existing everywhere except inside the heads of people who'd run. And the Order needed hands it could trust, and the guild needed a patron, and you—" my voice caught, and I let it, deliberately, because a crack in my voice buys more than a crack in his, "—you were *there*. You helped. And then you sold the helping, and you bought me to hold the receipt."

His face had gone the color of the ledger paper. "Edmund—"

"*Sable*," I said.

He flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd struck him, and I realized with a cold clean joy that I'd never once seen Corvane flinch, not in six years, not at anything, and that I had just become a person who could make him do it.

"My name is Sable Vane," I said. "I got it back in a cellar off Sallow Street, from a bottle, at a price you cannot imagine. And here is what else I know, guildmaster, and you will listen, because this is the part where we discuss terms. I kept copies. Not of the index — I'm not a fool, you'd have found those. Copies of *your habits*. Your margins. Your marginalia, which is where you keep everything true. Twelve years of notes in your hand about the trades you regretted, the buyers you feared, the one entry you revisit every year on the same date and then burn the candle down to nothing sitting alone in this room. I don't need documents to ruin you. I *am* the document. I am six years of your confessions wearing a coat."

"This is extortion," he said. But he said it the way a man says *it's raining* — an observation about weather.

"It's bookkeeping," I said. "Yours. I'm just reading it back to you."

* * *

We negotiated for two hours. It was, in its awful way, the most honest conversation of my life, because both of us finally knew what the other held, and there is no intimacy like mutually assured ruin.

He wanted the map. I told him the truth: I couldn't give it to him, because the map wasn't mine to give anymore. It answered to Vellamere's heir, and I had spent nineteen chapters of a life proving I was heir to nothing, and the map disagreed. He didn't believe me. He believed the flinch I couldn't suppress when I said it, which was worse.

In exchange for my silence — and my cooperation, which I offered in words carefully chosen to mean less than they sounded — he would call off the retrieval teams. He would give Xanthe and me safe passage out of Greyharbor. He would release the villagers' pooled memories seized in the raid, the ones his brokers had bottled like preserves, back into my custody.

"And in return you get what?" he asked at the end, and there was something raw under the question, some old tired thing that had been waiting forty years for someone to ask it.

"You get me off your books," I said. "Clean entry. No residual liability. No clerk who knows your cipher coming back someday when you're old and the new blood starts sniffing around the archive. You get to forget me, Corvane. Properly. The way you've always wanted to forget everything." I stood. My legs shook and I let them; showing him the shaking cost nothing now. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When you burn the candle down on the anniversary — and you will, I know you will — I want you to remember that the girl you bought was also the only person who ever read you all the way through. Cover to cover. Margins and all." I picked up the coat they'd taken from me and slung it over my arm. "That's not a threat. That's a dedication."

At the door, his voice stopped me. Quiet. Stripped of everything.

"The year I sold," he said. "Do you want to know what was in it?"

Every instinct I owned said *no*. Every instinct I owned had been built in this building, by this man, to keep me alive through exactly such questions.

"No," I said. "But I'll tell you what I know anyway, since we're being honest. It was the year your daughter died. Wasn't it? And the king's agent bought the grief along with everything else, and that's why you take your tea bitter, and that's why you raised a stranger's child instead of grieving your own, and that's why you looked at me like property — because that's what the paperwork says you are to each other. Consideration and consideration. Two people purchased." I turned the handle. The hinge dragged, right on schedule. "I'm sorry for her, Corvane. Genuinely. She deserved better than to become a line item. So did I. So did everyone in this rotten trade. That's the whole reason I'm going to open the door."

Behind me, long silence. Then, so low I nearly lost it:

"The door isn't yours to open."

"No," I agreed. "It's everyone's. That's rather the point."

* * *

They walked me out through the guild's front hall, past the brass scales where griefs were weighed, past the new clerk — a boy of maybe fifteen with ink on his fingers and terror in his eyes, and I remembered being that terrified, and I stopped, and I pressed the key to the false-bottomed drawer into his hand and folded his fingers over it.

"Third drawer," I said. "When he's not looking. Read everything. Especially the violet wax." I glanced back toward the study door, where a candle was already burning down. "Consider it severance."

Outside, Greyharbor was raining the fine grey rain that makes everything feel forgiven whether it deserves it or not. Xanthe was waiting where I'd told her to wait, in the shadow of the Drowned Bell, arms crossed, sword hidden, wearing the expression she wore when she'd decided to be furious later in order to be useful now.

"Well?" she said.

"He blinked," I said.

Xanthe stared at me. Then, slowly, the corner of her mouth moved — the first smile I'd ever seen her spend. "The guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things blinked."

"Everyone blinks, Xanthe. That's the secret nobody in this city wants known." I pulled up my hood and stepped into the rain beside her, and felt the map's hum wake against my ribs like a second heartbeat, patient and old and waiting. "Come on. We have a door to build, and I know exactly what it's made of."

She fell into step. "Made of what?"

"Everything I sold," I said. "Every last piece. And I know where all of them are buried — because I kept the ledgers."
