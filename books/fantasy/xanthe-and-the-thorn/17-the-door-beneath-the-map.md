## 17. The Door Beneath the Map

**Edmund**

The cell smelled of ledger dust and old iron.

That was how she knew it was a Guild cell—Corvane couldn't resist the flourish of keeping his prisoners among the archives, where the shelves leaned in like witnesses and every book was a confession somebody had paid to stop making. Edmund sat on the cot with her back to the wall and counted the sounds: boots above, two pairs, changing at the half-hour; the drip in the corner that wasn't water but condensation off a memory-jar, weeping because whatever it held wanted out.

She'd been counting for six hours. It kept her from thinking about what Corvane had said before he locked her in.

*You were always mine, little clerk.*

She was still deciding whether to be furious or frightened when the wall opened.

Not dramatically. The shelves simply swung inward on a hinge she hadn't counted, and Xanthe stood in the gap with lamp-oil on her sleeves and soot on one cheekbone, holding a finger to her lips like this was all perfectly ordinary.

"You're late," Edmund whispered.

"I was never coming." Xanthe stepped inside and pulled the shelf shut behind her. "Corvane bought my oath years ago. He just forgot to buy me." She tossed something onto the cot—a bundle of dark cloth. "Put that on. We have four minutes before the fire reaches the oil store, and then nobody in this building will remember which way is up."

"Fire." Edmund looked at her. "You set fire to the ledgers."

"I set fire to the *building*. The ledgers are stone-walled and Corvane knows it. But everyone else will be running toward the smoke instead of toward us, which is the entire point." Xanthe's voice was flat, efficient, a soldier's voice—but her hands weren't steady. Edmund noticed. Xanthe noticed her noticing, and looked away.

"What did they show you," Edmund said quietly. "In the village. Before Corvane burned it."

The pause lasted exactly as long as it takes to decide not to lie. "Enough."

* * *

They went up through the burning guildhouse the way smoke goes up—not quickly, not cleanly, following the drafts. Twice they passed Guild clerks hauling jars down stairwells, their faces slack with the peculiar panic of people whose memories are stored somewhere overhead. Once they passed a guard who saw them plainly, opened his mouth, and then frowned, because Xanthe had pressed two fingers to his temple as they passed and given him a borrowed certainty: *nothing here, nothing to see.* Edmund felt the theft brush past her like a draft and hated that she recognized its texture as home.

Then they were out, into the alley behind the guildhouse, into rain, into running.

Edmund ran the way she always ran now—on borrowed streets. Three months ago she'd stolen an urchin's knowledge of the rooftops of Lowmarsh, a courier's mental map of the culverts under the Merchants' Quarter, a smuggler's love affair with the gaps in the harbor wall. They carried her through the city like ferries. And they leaked. That was the trouble with stolen memories: they didn't sit still. Some nights she woke up homesick for a rooftop she'd never slept on, missing a mother who had waved goodbye to some other child from some other window.

Tonight she let them carry her and didn't fight it, because behind them the guildhouse bell began to ring, and ahead of them the map—the dead woman's map, bloodstained, humming against her ribs like a second heart—was getting louder.

They stopped at last in the ruins of the old customs house on the river, a roofless shell the city had forgotten so thoroughly that even rats had moved on. Rain came through the broken ceiling in silver threads. Xanthe lit no lamp. She didn't need to; Edmund could see her by the light the map made.

It had begun to glow.

"Not again," Edmund said warily, drawing it out. "Last time it did this, I lost two days."

"That was it trying to talk to you and you refusing to listen." Xanthe crouched across from her, arms on her knees, watching the parchment the way you watch a snake. "The villagers told me what it is. Or what it isn't."

"It's a map."

"It's a *door*," Xanthe said. "Folded flat. Everything Vellamere was, pressed down into paper so it could survive the erasure. The country didn't die, Edmund. It was *filed*."

Edmund turned the map over in her hands. She'd studied it a hundred times—the coastlines that matched no coast, the roads that ended in nothing, the capital marked with a sigil like a keyhole seen sideways. In Lamplight, in fog, in terror, it had shown her fragments. But here, in the ruins, in the rain, with Xanthe watching, it did something new.

It opened.

Not physically. But the ink shifted, the way ink does when a ledger entry is being rewritten, and lines appeared that had never been there: fine annotations in a hand Edmund knew better than her own, because it *was* her own—a child's hand, careful and frightened, the hand that had written her first ledger entries at seven years old while Corvane stood behind her saying *good girl, good girl.*

She read aloud, because reading aloud made it real:

*"Item: the door beneath the map. It is not a place. It is a sum. Every memory taken from Vellamere was carried out through the door, and the door remembers each one. It will open only when the account balances—when everything sold comes home. All of it. Not most. Not enough. All."*

The rain filled the silence after.

"That can't be right," Edmund said. "There must be ten thousand memories. A hundred thousand. An entire country's worth of—"

"There are," Xanthe said. "And the Order has most of them in vaults. And the Guild has the rest. And the buyers—" she met Edmund's eyes "—the buyers are everywhere. Every person who ever drank a piece of Vellamere to feel braver, or kinder, or less afraid. You've read the price lists. You know how many there are."

Edmund did know. She'd written those lists. She'd been the clerk who tallied the column of souls who had purchased a stranger's courage for three silver pieces, a stranger's grief for five.

"So it doesn't open," she said. "It can't open. No one collects a hundred thousand scattered memories from—"

"*You* collected them," Xanthe said. "For years. One theft at a time. You're the only person alive who knows how it's done, who's done it at scale, who knows where every vault is because you kept the ledgers." She said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Corvane didn't capture a clerk by accident. He captured the only locksmith."

Edmund looked down at the map, at the child's handwriting, at the last line, which she had skipped without meaning to, the way you skip a stair in the dark:

*"I'm sorry. I was small and they were big. — S.V."*

Her own name. Her other name. Written by a child who had helped file her whole country into paper and parchment and memory-jars, and had then—Edmund felt the thought arrive like cold water—had then sold the knowing of it. Had erased herself. Had made herself forgettable so that no one would ever think to ask the little Vellamere girl what she'd done.

"Oh," Edmund said. Very quietly. To the rain, to the map, to the child. "Oh, you clever, cowardly, brilliant little fool. You didn't hide the door. You hid yourself inside it."

Xanthe frowned. "What do you mean?"

"The door opens when everything comes home." Edmund lifted her head. "Everything. Including what's inside *me*. The memories I sold. The ones I've been avoiding since the drowned bell." Her hands were shaking now, and she let them. "Including the ones about what I did."

The rain came harder. Somewhere across the river, the guildhouse bells were still ringing, and under them, fainter, another sound: horses. Many. Moving along the embankment road with the disciplined rhythm she'd heard all her life in Corvane's stables.

Xanthe heard it too. She rose, unhurried, and pulled her sword loose in its scabbard—not to draw, just to free it. "How long?"

"Minutes. He'll have dogs that remember my smell even if the handlers don't remember me." Edmund folded the map against her chest. It pulsed once, twice, like something agreeing. "Xanthe. If I open that door, I have to take back everything I sold. My childhood. My name. My mother's voice." She swallowed. "And the night I erased Vellamere. Whatever I did. Whoever I was, before I made myself no one."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off the knight's jaw. When she spoke, her voice had none of the Order's flatness left in it; it was just a woman's voice, tired and certain.

"I spent twelve years believing erasure was mercy. That forgetting was clean." She held out her hand. "Take it all back. Even the ugly parts. *Especially* the ugly parts. I'll be there when you remember—and I'll still be there after."

Edmund took her hand. It was the first thing in longer than she could calculate—she'd sold the arithmetic—that felt like it was being given rather than taken.

Above the ruined customs house, the horses reached the embankment. Torches bloomed along the far bank, a string of orange beads in the rain.

"Back way?" Edmund asked.

"There's a culvert," Xanthe said, "that a courier once memorized and you once stole."

Edmund almost smiled. "Borrowed streets it is."

They ran. And against her ribs, patient as a creditor, the map counted what remained outstanding.

* * *

**Xanthe**

Later—in the culvert, wading hip-deep through water that remembered being a river, with torchlight stuttering on the grates above—Xanthe found herself doing sums of her own.

She was good at sums. The Order had made sure of it: collateral estimates, containment costs, the tidy arithmetic of unmaking. For twelve years the numbers had balanced, because she'd never counted the villagers stranded outside existence, never counted the portrait of a green-eyed girl in the forbidden archive whose face had started all of this, never counted herself.

Now the numbers wouldn't balance, and the imbalance had a name, and the name was walking ahead of her through the dark, humming under her breath without knowing it—a tune, half-remembered, that Xanthe had last heard in a burning village from an old woman's mouth. A lullaby. Vellamere's lullaby. The one the fog used to whisper.

Edmund was going to drink back everything. Xanthe understood what that meant better than anyone: that the woman who came out the other side might not be the woman walking ahead of her now. Memory returned changes the vessel that receives it. She had watched it happen. She had *caused* it to happen.

And she would help anyway. That was the part she couldn't put in any ledger.

Ahead, Edmund stopped. "Grate's ahead. Beyond it, the embankment stairs, then the warehouse district." She glanced back, and the torchlight caught her face, and Xanthe saw that under the fear there was something else—resolve, banked and bright, like a coal that has decided to become a fire.

"When we're clear," Edmund said, "I start buying myself back. Every piece. Even the ones I'd rather burn."

"I know."

"And Corvane will know too. The moment I make my first purchase, his brokers will feel it. He won't send guards next time. He'll send the thing he keeps for emergencies." She said it lightly, but her hand found the map through her coat.

"Then we move fast," Xanthe said, "and we steal better than he buys."

Edmund smiled—small, sharp, the smile of a clerk who has finally found the error in the master ledger. Above them, the grate rattled in its frame. Beyond it, the city waited, full of vaults and buyers and every stolen piece of a country that refused to die.

"All of it comes home," Edmund whispered—to the map, to the child who'd written it, to herself.

The grate gave. They climbed out into the rain, and began.
