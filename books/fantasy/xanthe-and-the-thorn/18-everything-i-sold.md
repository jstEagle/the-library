## 18. Everything I Sold

The last vial was smaller than the others.

That felt right, somehow. The memories I'd bought back one by one had come in fat glass phials, sloshing with color—childhood summers, a name sung in a mother's voice, the smell of bread in a city that no longer existed. Each one had hit me like weather. This one sat in Xanthe's palm like a drop of ink, dark and still, and it did not catch the lamplight so much as drink it.

"You don't have to," Xanthe said. "We find another way."

"There is no other way." The map lay open between us on the broker's table, its inked rivers crawling slow as blood. Three days of deciphering, three days of Xanthe's careful hand tracing sigils and mine translating the ledger-script I'd spent ten years writing for other people's sins, and we had arrived at a single sentence that might have been carved into my own sternum: *the door is made of returned memory; it opens only when every stolen piece comes home.*

Every piece.

Including this one. Including the entry at the bottom of the guild's oldest ledger, written in a child's unsteady hand: *One country, entire. Seller: E.T. Buyer: withheld by order.* Corvane had kept it himself. Of course he had. You don't sell off the deed to your house.

"Edmund." Xanthe said my false name softly, the way you'd speak to something wild you didn't want to startle. "Whatever's in there—you wrote yourself a rule once. Be forgotten, stay alive. Maybe the rule knew something you don't."

"The rule kept me alive," I said. "It didn't keep anyone else alive. That's the difference I've been running from since the Drowned Bell." I held out my hand. "Give it to me."

Xanthe gave it to me. And then—this is the part I'll remember longest, past everything else, past the door and the fire and whatever comes after—Xanthe took my other hand and did not let go.

I pulled the stopper.

* * *

Memory does not pour into you. It pours *out* of you, as if it had been waiting all along inside your bones, and the vial was only permission.

I was eight years old, and the guildhouse study smelled of lamp oil and Corvane's pipe, and I was sitting in the big leather chair that swallowed me whole because chairs were made for adults and I was not yet an adult, I was a child from Vellamere, and I knew what Vellamere was, I could see it behind my eyes—the terraced gardens going gold in autumn, the bell towers, my mother's hands flour-white to the wrist—

Corvane crouched beside the chair so our eyes were level. He was younger then. His beard had no grey in it. "Sable," he said—he called me Sable then; Edmund came later, a coat I grew into—"listen very carefully, little clerk. Your mother is dying. You know this."

I knew it. Fever, the healers said. The kind that ate a person from the inside, that no Vellamere remedy could touch, because—and here Corvane leaned close, voice dropping to the register men use when they are about to commit an atrocity gently—it wasn't a sickness of the body at all. It was a sickness of *existence*. Vellamere was marked, he told me. The Order of the Blank Page had petitioned the king. There were powers moving in the world, great slow powers, and Vellamere was in their path, and there was exactly one way to take my mother out of that path before it closed over her.

"Take her out of it how?" I asked. Eight years old. I remember being eight years old and asking questions with a clerk's precision, because even then I was keeping ledgers in my head.

"A country exists," Corvane said, "because it is remembered. Every stone, every song, every name. Memory is the mortar. Remove the mortar—" he smiled, and I remember loving him, that's the worst of it, I remember adoring this man the way you adore the only warm thing in a cold house— "and the wall comes down. Quietly. No armies. No burnings. Just forgetting. And anything forgotten can slip free of what happens next."

"And if I do it," I said, "Mama lives?"

"Mama lives. Somewhere new. Somewhere safe. She won't remember the old country, but she'll be *alive*, Sable, and isn't that the whole of it? Isn't living the thing?"

I looked at my hands. Small hands. Clerk's hands already, ink-stained at eight years old because Corvane let me hold the pens. And I thought about my mother singing while she kneaded dough, and I thought about her coughing blood into a rag, and I thought—I want to write this exactly as I thought it, because the child deserves her reasoning preserved:

*I am small and this is the only lever I will ever be given. Pull it.*

"Yes," I said. "I'll do it."

What I did not understand—not then, not until the memory finished pouring through me and left me shaking in a cellar chair two hundred miles and twelve years away—was the mechanism. A child cannot erase a country. But a child who is heir to Vellamere, a child of the royal line they never told me about, whose blood was the mortar itself—a child like that is not a person. A child like that is a *keystone*. My consent was the instrument. My memory was the blade. They had me drink a draught of forgetting, and as it moved through me it took everything it touched, and because I was the keystone, everything it touched was Vellamere.

I erased my own country out of love. That was the design of it. Corvane understood something I understand now better than I wish to: people will surrender almost anything except the chance to save someone they love. So he built the whole theft on top of my love, the way you build a bridge on top of a river.

And my mother?

The memory showed me that too. It showed me waking, twelve years old, four years hollow, asking why I cried whenever I smelled bread. It showed me Corvane telling me my mother had died of fever long ago, poor thing, before I ever came to the guild. It showed me—years later, a routine inventory, checking a buyer's manifest—her name. In the ledger. Listed among the survivors of the erasure. Alive. Unremembering. Living forty miles from where I slept, and Corvane knowing, and Corvane saying nothing, because a girl with no mother asks fewer questions than a girl hunting one.

She died last winter. I found the date in the same ledger, in the column where deaths get recorded so accounts can be closed. I never knew her again. I never got to know her again. And the country I damned myself to save her from—she'd have lived either way, or died either way, or perhaps the fever was never existence-sickness at all, perhaps that too was Corvane's invention, a story shaped precisely to fit the hole in a desperate child.

The vial was empty. I was still holding it. My face was wet and I hadn't noticed crying, which meant the crying had been happening for some time.

* * *

Xanthe was kneeling in front of me. I don't know how long. Their gauntlets were off—they'd taken them off, set them on the table like weapons surrendered—and their bare hands held mine, and their oath-gray eyes were wet.

"Say it," Xanthe said quietly. "Whatever it is. Say it out loud. The Order taught me that much truth: memory rots in silence."

"I erased Vellamere." My voice came out flat, clerical, reciting an entry. "Me. Not the king, not the Order, not Corvane alone. I drank the draught. I consented. I was eight years old and I loved my mother and I pulled the lever, and the whole country came down through me—every garden, every bell, every person in it—and they're still stranded outside existence because of what I did when I was eight years old."

"That's not—"

"It *is*." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You've spent your life erasing the traces. Do you understand what you've been chasing? You've been mopping up after *me*. I'm the blank page, Xanthe. I'm the original."

Their grip tightened. "Listen to me. Listen." Xanthe's voice cracked on it, and I heard—for the first time, fully heard—the boy under the knight, the orphan under the oath. "I was six when Vellamere fell. I remember smoke, and my mother pushing me onto a cart, and nothing else. The Order raised me on a story: the country was plague-ridden, memory-mad, dangerous. I believed it because believing it made my life make sense." Their thumb pressed hard against my knuckles. "But a child coerced into a draught by the man feeding her supper—that's not an author. That's a weapon. Someone aimed you. Weapons don't carry guilt. The hand that aims them does."

"And Corvane's hand was guided by whom?" I whispered. "And theirs? It goes back and back, doesn't it. Everyone aiming everyone. Except someone has to be the keystone. Someone has to be the stone that bears the weight. That was always going to be me. It was built into my blood before I was born."

For a moment neither of us spoke. The map breathed on the table, its rivers creeping toward the valley, toward the standing stones, toward the door that would only open if every piece came home.

Then Xanthe said, slowly, as if testing each word for load-bearing strength: "When we reach the village ruins—when we reach the door—you're going to stand in front of every ghost of that country and offer them your guilt. I know you. I've watched you apologize to furniture." A ghost of a smile, gone fast. "Don't. What they need from you isn't your punishment. It's your memory. All of it. Even this. Especially this."

"Why especially?"

"Because a door made of returned things needs the true ones most." Xanthe picked up the empty vial, turned it in the light, and set it down with the others—the whole row of them, my life reassembled bottle by bottle. "Because the child who pulled the lever deserves to be forgiven, and forgiveness is also a returned thing. Someone has to carry it home to her."

I looked down at my hands. They weren't small anymore. Ink-stained still. Clerk's hands to the last.

"I forgive her," I said, and my voice broke in the middle like a dropped plate. "God help me, I forgive her. She was eight. She was *eight*, and she loved her mother, and she thought she was saving someone." I bent over our joined hands and let it come, twelve years of it, the grief I'd sold so I wouldn't have to feel it, and Xanthe held on and did not look away and did not let go.

When it passed, the candle had burned down two fingers' width, and something in me had gone quiet and level and terribly clear.

I stood. I gathered the empty vials, all of them, and wrapped them in cloth the way I'd wrap anything valuable, because they were valuable—they were receipts, proof of everything taken and everything given back. The map waited.

"Xanthe," I said. "There's something I have to tell you before we go. Something the memory showed me that changes what's waiting at those stones."

They looked up.

"Corvane didn't want the door closed. He wanted it *kept*. And he built me to open it."
