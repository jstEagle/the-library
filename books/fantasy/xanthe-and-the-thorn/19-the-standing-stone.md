## 19. The Standing Stone

### Edmund

The map stopped being paper a mile before the door.

Edmund felt it happen in her hands—the vellum going warm, then heavy, then *present*, the way a held breath becomes a body. By the time they crested the last hill and saw the standing stone rising from the frost-burned grass, the map had folded itself away into nothing, because it was no longer needed. It had been a key all along. Keys stop mattering once you reach the lock.

The stone was older than any country she could name. A single grey pillar, taller than two men, carved over its whole surface with writing that shifted when she wasn't looking directly at it. Around its base the ground was bare in a perfect circle, as though even the grass understood what stood there and had chosen not to.

"That's it," Xanthe said quietly. Not a question. She had known too—had perhaps always known, in whatever buried place the Order kept the things it didn't let itself feel.

"That's it." Edmund's voice came out strange. Thin. She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, where twenty-two years of stolen memories sat stacked like coins in a purse, every one of them bought back at ruinous price. "It opens on returned memories. All of them. Everything I sold has to come home."

"And after?"

"After," Edmund said, "I don't know."

She did, a little. She'd never say it. The door would show her Vellamere—one breath of it—and then she would have to decide whether to keep it or kill it, and either choice would cost more than she had. But that was for the next chapter of her life, if she lived to write it.

Xanthe touched her arm. Just briefly. Just enough. Twelve years ago this woman had been sworn to unmake everything Edmund was, and now her hand rested on Edmund's sleeve like an anchor thrown to someone drowning, and neither of them remarked on it, because remarking would have made it smaller somehow.

Then Edmund felt the cold come up the hill behind them, and knew they'd run out of road.

* * *

### Xanthe

Corvane did not arrive like a man arriving. He arrived like weather.

The fog thickened first—wrong fog, dry fog, fog that tasted of ink—and out of it came the guildmaster's people in their grey coats, fanning across the hillside in a wide crescent, cutting off every path but one. And down that open path, unhurried as a man strolling to his own garden, came Corvane himself.

He looked exactly as he had in the ledgers Xanthe had studied: a tall, spare man with a clerk's stoop and a merchant's eyes, silver at the temples, hands folded behind his back as though he were inspecting inventory. Only his eyes moved quickly, and they went straight past Xanthe to Edmund, and stayed there, and something in them was so tender and so hungry at once that Xanthe's sword hand ached.

"Edmund." He said the name like a key turning in a lock. "You've been difficult."

"You taught me bookkeeping," Edmund said. Her voice was steady. Xanthe marveled at it. "Difficult entries are my specialty."

"Indeed." Corvane smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "And here you are, keeping books again. Twenty-two purchases, girl. Do you know what you're carrying? Every memory you drank back made the door stronger. I could have told you that. I *wrote* the arithmetic. You've spent months assembling my property and delivering it to my doorstep." He spread his hands. "All I need now is you."

"She doesn't go anywhere with you," Xanthe said.

Corvane turned to her then, and Xanthe watched him do the thing he must have done a thousand times: read a person, find their price, name it aloud.

"Ser Xanthe of the Blank Page," he said. "Orphaned at six when your village burned in the Vellamere contagion. Mother named Maren. Father named Aldous. A sister—" he paused delicately— "whose name you have never once been able to recall, though you've tried every night for twelve years. The Order erased her from you along with everything else. Sloppy work. Grief is stubborn."

Xanthe said nothing. The silence rang.

"I can give her back." Corvane's voice dropped into warmth, into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register he owned. "Not a memory of her. *Her.* The Order strands what it erases—it doesn't destroy it. Your sister exists, Ser Knight. Unremembered, outside the world, in the fold between places, exactly as stranded as every soul from that plague-country you were trained to hate. I hold the ledger entry. One stroke of my pen restores her. Her face, her voice, her name—which you will finally be able to say." He took one step closer. "All it costs is the girl. One death. You've given the Order a dozen."

The hillside was very quiet. Somewhere below, a crow complained and went silent, as if embarrassed.

And Xanthe stood in the space between the offer and the answer, and understood that this was the whole of her life, compressed: every drill, every oath, every night reciting the Blank Page litany—*what is unwritten cannot wound*—all of it funneling down to this one standing stone and this one question.

Her sister. Alive. Reachable.

She looked at Edmund.

Edmund was watching her, and there was no fear in her face—or rather, there was fear, plenty of it, but underneath it something else: a kind of terrible patience, as if she had already done this arithmetic herself, as if some part of her had been waiting since the drowned bell for Xanthe to choose, and would accept whichever way it fell. That was the worst of it. That Edmund would understand. That Edmund, who had sold her own childhood to save her mother and lost her mother anyway, knew better than anyone alive that love made monsters of bargains.

"No," Xanthe said.

Corvane tilted his head, almost curious. "You don't even hesitate to refuse? Remarkable. Most people at least ask what the work involves."

"I'm not refusing the price." Xanthe drew her sword, slow, deliberate, letting him watch her do it. "I'm refusing the arithmetic. You want me to believe my sister's life can be balanced against hers, like columns in your ledgers. But I've seen the fold between places now. I know what your restoration means—you'd pull her out of one prison and build her another, and call it mercy. And I know this:" her voice cracked, and she let it, "if I did this—if I traded a living woman for a maybe—my sister wouldn't come back to me. Someone else would wear her face, and I'd know, every day, what I'd paid for her. There are things you can't buy back, Corvane. Believe me. I've spent my whole career making sure of it for other people."

Something flickered across the guildmaster's face—not anger. Recognition. As if she'd quoted something back to him that he himself had written long ago and burned.

"Love," he said, tasting the word like spoiled milk. "The Order warned me about knights who went strange in the field. Sentimental rot. You'd throw away your own blood for a thief who sold her country."

"For a woman," Xanthe said, "who carried the weight of a whole dead country alone so no one else had to. Yes. Openly. In front of your men. Write it down—I know you keep records."

For the first time, Corvane's composure bent. What showed beneath was not rage but fear, old and well-fed, and Xanthe suddenly understood Edmund's blackmail, the ledger of his own sold memories, whatever he had paid to forget. He was afraid of doors. He had been afraid of them his entire life.

"Kill them both," Corvane said, wearily, as though ordering ink.

* * *

### Edmund

They came up the hill in a crescent, eight of them, grey coats moving without sound, and Edmund did the only thing she was truly good at: she thought about what people wanted.

Guild thieves fought like accountants. They minimized risk. They approached in pairs, each covering the other's blind side, each privately hoping the other would take the blade. Which meant—

"The left three are yours," Edmund murmured. "Right five mine."

"You can't fight five—"

"I'm not going to fight them. I'm going to *inconvenience* them." She pulled the memory-purse from her coat—the broker's cellar purchase she'd been saving, the escape-memories she'd stolen from a smuggler who'd died rich and paranoid. "Trust me. It's what I'm for."

The first pair reached her and she cracked the seal on a memory of a rope bridge over a gorge, thirty years of a smuggler's certainty about footing and balance and exactly how much weight a frayed line could hold, and drank it down, and stepped backward off the hillside onto air that her body, suddenly fluent in bridges, refused to believe was empty. There was a handhold—a root, a rock lip—that the smuggler's eyes would have found and hers never could have. She swung along the hill's steep face like a woman walking stairs while three guild-thieves lunged at where she should have fallen and tumbled instead into the bracken.

Above, steel rang. Xanthe fought the way she did everything—economically, without flourish, three men learning very fast why the Order trained its knights for twenty years. Edmund allowed herself half a heartbeat to watch, half a heartbeat of pure uncomplicated admiration, and then a fourth thief dropped over the edge beside her with a knife and she had to go back to being a bridge.

* * *

### Xanthe

Three down. The fourth was better—older, careful, a veteran of impossible jobs—and he drove Xanthe back toward the standing stone with short, vicious cuts. Behind her the pillar hummed, low, like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. She could feel it against her spine. Could feel, absurdly, that it was *watching*, taking her measure, deciding things.

The veteran feinted high. Xanthe didn't take the bait; she'd invented that feint, or someone had invented it into her, drilled into her muscles by the Order until it lived in her wrists. She caught his blade and turned it and put him down hard, unconscious, not dead—she was done adding names to the Order's ledger—and looked up to find Corvane standing ten feet away, alone, watching her with his hands still folded behind his back.

"You could still have her back," he said. "Even now. Even after this farce. My offer doesn't expire, Ser Xanthe. Offers of that size rarely do."

"My sister," Xanthe said, breathing hard, "deserves better than to be resurrected as a bribe. So do I. So do you, if you ever want to remember what you sold." She leveled her sword. "Stand aside, guildmaster. Or don't, and learn what twelve years of erasing countries teaches a person about how to end things."

Something moved in Corvane's face—grief, maybe, or its fossil—and then he sighed, and reached into his coat, and what he drew out was not a weapon but a small black book, and when he opened it the fog around the hilltop *bent toward it*, and Xanthe felt her own memories shiver like water with a stone dropping toward them.

He was going to erase her. Here, at the door, with everything at stake.

Behind him, Edmund rose from the hillside like something out of a story, bloodied, wild-eyed, holding the standing stone's humming attention in both her hands, and shouted a word in a language that no longer existed—and the stone answered.

Light came off it like the first morning of the world.

* * *

### Edmund

She hadn't meant to speak. The word had simply risen—Sable's word, the child's word, dredged up from the last of the recovered memories, a word her mother had sung over a cradle in a burning city. And the standing stone, which had waited centuries for someone to address it in the tongue of the country it guarded, opened its oldest function like an eye.

Every memory within fifty feet became visible. She saw them: the grey coats' pooled thefts hanging around them like smoke, Xanthe's erased childhood glimmering faintly beneath her skin like a lamp under silk—and Corvane's, Corvane's most of all, a great black ledger of purchased forgetting wrapped around him so densely he glowed with it, decades of sold grief, sold shame, sold *love*, and underneath it all one entry so vast it had a gravity of its own:

*A door. A door he had once walked through. A door he had paid everything to forget he loved.*

"Oh," Edmund said softly. "Oh, you poor man. You've been trying to get back through it your whole life."

"Silence her," Corvane said, and his voice was not cold anymore, and that was how Edmund knew they were going to win—not because they were stronger, but because he was afraid, and afraid men make clerical errors.

Xanthe hit him like a verdict.

They went down together, knight and guildmaster, and the grey coats faltered, leaderless, staring at the light pouring off the stone, at the fog peeling back from the hilltop, at the air above the standing stone beginning—faintly, impossibly—to show colors that belonged to no sky that currently existed. Green hills. White towers. A river the color of hammered tin.

Vellamere. One breath of it, waiting.

Edmund stood before it with her heart hammering and her whole reclaimed life pressing at the backs of her eyes, and reached for Xanthe's hand, and found it already reaching for hers.

"It wants everything," she said. "All at once. Whatever's on the other side—I have to pour it all in. Every memory. If I flinch, it closes forever."

"Then don't flinch," Xanthe said, and gripped her hand tighter. "I'll be right here. Whatever you become on the other side of it—I'll be right here."

Below them, Corvane struggled upright, and looked at the vision in the air, and made a sound Edmund had never heard a powerful man make: a small, broken, human sound, like a child seeing the sea for the first time.

He lunged for the door.

So did she.
