## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The morning the world began to end—or begin, I still haven't decided which—smelled of honey and toasted millet, and underneath it, faintly, of incense.

I noticed the incense before anything else, because in twenty years of tasting for the crown of Vell I had learned that danger announces itself first as a wrongness in the air. A bitter thread in a sweet steam. A metallic ghost beneath roasted meat. Poison is a liar's craft, and liars always breathe too carefully. So when I lifted the silver spoon to my lips in the king's breakfast chamber and caught, drifting up from the porridge like a spirit from an opened jar, the smell of temple smoke and cold mountain stone, my body did what it had been trained to do: nothing at all.

My hands stayed steady. My face stayed pleasant and forgettable, the face of a woman no one remembers by afternoon. Inside, something very old and very frightened turned over in its sleep.

"Ravensworth." Master of Kitchens Odell stood in the doorway with his ledger under his arm, already bored with me. "The king wants the new grain from the eastern terraces. If it disagrees with you, spit in the bowl and we'll send it back."

"If it disagrees with me," I said, "there won't be much left of me to argue."

He grunted, which was his way of laughing, and left. That was the arrangement, and it had kept me alive two decades: I tasted; if I lived, the king ate; if I died, they buried me quietly behind the kitchens and hired another unremarkable woman with an unremarkable tongue. There were four of us tasters now. Maren, who could detect hemlock by scent alone and never let anyone forget it. Two boys whose names I deliberately did not learn, because names are threads, and threads can be pulled.

And there was me, Freya Ravensworth, whose only magic was the slow, patient murder of herself—a little poison every day for twenty years, arsenic in her tea, wolfsbane in her broth, until her blood became a fortress no toxin cared to storm. It wasn't sorcery. Sorcery was outlawed in Vell, burned out of the city generations ago along with its practitioners, and the gods had gone silent so long that praying was considered a rustic embarrassment, like believing in fairies or honest tax collectors. In Vell you survived by being small. I had made smallness into an art form. I had made it into armor.

I looked down at the spoon.

The porridge steamed gently, innocent as milk. But the smell was still there, rising off it in a thin gray ribbon: incense. Old stone. Something sweeter under both, like dried roses in a closed room—the smell of a shrine that has been shut for a hundred years and has just, quietly, been opened.

I should have spat it out. I should have called for Odell, flagged the dish, condemned the entire eastern terrace harvest to the pig buckets, and gone home to my narrow bed above my charm stall feeling pleasantly, safely paranoid. That was the procedure. That was survival.

Instead I swallowed.

The taste bloomed across my tongue the way dawn comes over the harbor—slowly, then all at once. Millet and honey, yes. And beneath them, unmistakable now that I had stopped pretending otherwise: frankincense. Beeswax candles. Rain on granite. The taste was *old*, older than the palace, older than Vell itself, old the way the mountain looming over our northern wall is old, and it went through me like a bell struck once in an empty cathedral. Every hair on my arms rose. Somewhere deep in my chest, in the place where fear lives, a door I had nailed shut twenty years ago rattled on its hinges.

Prayer, said something in me that I had not listened to since I was nine years old. Someone is praying. And someone—or something—is answering.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about answered prayers, because I know more than most, and less than I wish.

Twenty years ago, in the plague summer, I had a sister. Wren. Small and quick and loud, all the things I would later train out of myself, with a gap in her teeth and a habit of talking to the mountain like it was a grandmother who'd wandered off and gotten senile. "She's still in there," Wren used to say, patting the black rock of the Veilspine where it shouldered up over Vell's north quarter. "Gods don't die, silly. They just get quiet."

Wren got sick in the third month of the sweating fever. Our mother was dead, our father was dead, and I was nine years old and desperate in the way only children can be desperate—totally, with the whole of myself. I remember kneeling on the kitchen floor with my palms pressed flat to the stones because I didn't know how else to pray, having never been taught, and saying the same words over and over: *not her, take mine instead, take mine.*

I have spent twenty years telling myself that Wren simply died, the way thousands died that summer, and that the fever took her in the night while I slept, and that the strange dream I dreamed—that of a door opening under the mountain, and voices like cold water discussing terms—was only grief wearing the costume of memory.

But I remembered the voice. One voice, many-throated, the way a choir is one sound. It had asked me something. And I, nine years old, hollowed out with love and terror, had said *yes* to whatever it was.

Wren's fever broke the next morning. She woke hungry, complaining, alive—and she was never quite *there* again. Her eyes had a faraway quality, like windows with the lamps lit but nobody home. She lingered three days, wandering the house touching walls, and on the fourth morning she didn't wake at all. The physician called it the fever's aftergrip. Our neighbors called it mercy. I called it nothing. I put the whole of it away in a box at the bottom of my heart, nailed the lid, built my small gray life on top of it, and became the most forgettable woman in Vell.

Until this morning. Until porridge that tasted of incense.

* * *

After the king's breakfast came the midday plates, and after the plates, mercifully, the street. I keep a charm stall in the Nightmarket, wedged between a fishwife who sells eels still wriggling and a man who swears his mirrors show you your true face, though in my experience they mostly show you your own tired one. Charms are legal in Vell so long as they're harmless, and mine are harmless the way I am harmless: knotted red thread against nightmares, iron nails wrapped in wool against ill luck, little stamped-tin saints of no particular god. Folk buy them because buying feels like doing. Nothing I sell works. That is the point. That has always been the point—it keeps the magistrates bored and me breathing.

Except.

I was packing up at dawn, folding my awning against the rain, when I found it at the bottom of my strongbox: a silver charm I'd sold four days ago. A small thing, palm-sized, a disc of hammered silver with a mountain stamped on one side and a door on the other. I remembered the buyer vaguely—a laundress, gray-faced, worried about her son who worked the mines in the Veilspine's roots. *For his safe return,* she'd said. I'd told her what I always tell them: *it's only silver, mistress. Silver doesn't save anyone.* She'd bought it anyway. They always do. Hope is the one commodity that never glutted a market.

It shouldn't have been in my box. I hadn't taken it back; I don't deal in returns. And yet here it sat, warm as a fresh-baked roll, warm in a way silver cannot be warm unless something is moving inside it, and when I picked it up—carefully, the way you touch a bird that might be dying—it *hummed*. Not a sound, exactly. A vibration in the bones of my fingers, in the small bones behind my ears, a note like the one a wet glass sings when you circle its rim. And through the humming, faint as a voice through a wall, I heard—I swear before any god listening or not listening—I heard *singing*. Many voices. Cold water and old stone and roses shut in a room.

The laundress's son came home Tuesday last, the fishwife had mentioned it, chattering over the eels. Came home without a scratch from a collapse that killed six men, walked out of the dark whistling, said he'd followed a light.

I stood in the emptying market with the rain coming down and the charm singing against my palm, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only after the fact, that my invisibility was cracking. Twenty years of being no one, of selling nothing that worked, of tasting poisons into submission one careful mouthful at a time—and the world underneath the world had chosen *now* to wake up, had reached up through the floorboards of reality and touched the one object I ever sold that mattered.

Because here is the thing I had never admitted, not even to myself, in all those years of harmless charms: I had stamped a mountain and a door onto that silver because of a dream. Because some nights, even now, I dreamed of that door under the mountain, opening. Of a voice asking. Of a small girl's voice, mine, younger than memory, saying *yes.*

The charm hummed. Somewhere north, under the Veilspine, something vast turned in its sleep and began, slowly, to smile.

I wrapped the silver in wool, tucked it into my coat against my breast, and felt it beat there—warm, patient, insistent—like a second heart that was not mine and yet knew my name.

Behind me, at the mouth of the lane, a man in a priest's ruined cassock had stopped to watch me. Gray at the temples, collarless, carrying his defrocking like a wound he refused to bandage. He looked at my stall, at the rain, at me—at the shape of my hand pressed flat over my chest, guarding the humming thing.

"Mother of mercy," he said quietly, to no one, in the tone of a man watching a fire start in a dry field. "You can feel it too."

I had never seen him before in my life. I would remember him forever.
