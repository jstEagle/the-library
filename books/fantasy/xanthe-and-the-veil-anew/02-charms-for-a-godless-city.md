## 2. Charms for a Godless City

The night market in Vell opened when the lamps did, spilling down the Stair of Knives like honey poured over broken glass. Freya knew every crack in the flagstones there, every vendor's cough, every lie told at comfortable volume—*genuine river-pearls, lady, straight from the drowned temples*—and she moved through it all the way she had taught herself to move through twenty years of the city: quietly, unremarkably, a woman-shaped absence that no one troubled to remember by morning.

Her stall was a plank table and a strip of faded awning between a fishwife who sang off-key to her eels and a man who sold keys to doors that no longer existed. On the table she laid out her wares in careful rows: knots of red thread for safe travel, waxed linen sachets of lavender and iron filings against bad dreams, small hammered-silver charms on leather cords—a key for finding what's lost, a closed eye for being overlooked, an open hand for luck.

Harmless things. That was the whole craft of them. A charm worked because you believed it did, and believing cost nothing but hope, and hope was legal even here, even now, in the godless years since the mountain had gone silent. Freya sold people permission to hope, wrapped in silver, priced at two coppers or whatever they could spare. It was honest work, more honest than most, and it had kept her invisible for two decades.

She had made forty-one charms that week. She would remember the number later, and hate herself for the precision.

* * *

Twenty years ago, on the last night her sister was alive, Wren had been afraid of the dark.

"You're nine," Freya had said, seven years old and fierce with the authority of it, holding the blanket up so Wren could crawl under. "Nine is nearly grown. Nearly grown girls aren't afraid."

"I'm not afraid of the dark." Wren's voice came out muffled and small. "I'm afraid of what's listening in it."

Their mother was dead three weeks by then; their father would follow before spring, though neither girl knew it yet. The fever had come through the Tanner's Rows like a scythe through wheat, and the shrines were crowded with desperate people burning candles to gods who no longer answered, and the silence of heaven had a sound to it, Freya learned that winter. A pressure. Like holding your breath in a room where someone has just died.

Wren had always prayed anyway. Kneeling at their mother's little household shrine with its chipped figurines, hands folded, whispering into that enormous silence as if silence were simply a god who was shy. *Please let Freya stop coughing. Please let there be bread tomorrow. Please let me not be afraid.*

That night, fever-bright and shivering, Wren had pressed something into Freya's palm. A charm—the first one either of them ever made, twisted red thread around a bent nail, ugly as sin.

"So nothing takes you," Wren whispered. "I asked real hard. I promised something big."

"Wren, you can't promise things to gods that aren't listening."

"They're listening," her sister said, with the terrible certainty of the dying, "they're just far away. Like Papa's voice through a wall. You have to shout."

Freya kept the nail-charm under her pillow for eleven days. On the twelfth day, men in grey robes came up from the direction of the mountain—court-tasters, soul-brokers, whatever name the city used for them in whispers—and stood in the doorway of their rented room with faces like closed ledgers, and offered a bargain to a starving child too young to understand what she was signing away. They said the words *equivalent exchange* and *the court beneath* and *your sister will be provided for*, and Freya, hollow with hunger, watching Wren's breath rattle in her chest like a coin in an empty purse, had signed.

She had shouted, all right. She just hadn't known what she was shouting at, or that anyone would collect.

Wren died at dawn. Or rather—Freya would spend twenty years turning this over—Wren's body stopped at dawn, gently, almost gratefully, and whatever the brokers took went somewhere warm and lit beneath the mountain, where, the contract's fine print promised, it would be *kept*.

Kept. Not freed. Not finished. Kept, like a jar of preserves on a shelf in a dark cellar, waiting for someone with the price to come and carry it home.

* * *

"Silver eye, mistress? For my boy going down the mines."

Freya blinked back to the present. The fishwife's eels had gone quiet; the market had thickened around her stall the way it always did after sundown, when the honest vendors thinned out and the hopeful ones remained. She strung the closed-eye charm while she talked, letting her hands do the work her mind had abandoned.

"For overlooking him," she said. "Not luck. Luck charms are the one with the open hand. Don't mix them up, or he'll be both invisible and fortunate, which is a waste of good silver."

A laugh from the crowd. Coppers into her dish. This was the liturgy she'd built to replace the one she'd lost: small mercies, spoken plainly, paid for honestly.

It was halfway through the evening, stringing a knot of red thread for a widow wanting safe passage south, that Freya began to notice the pattern.

The miner's mother came back near midnight—not to buy, just passing, but she paused and said, strange-eyed, "He didn't go down today. Foreman looked at him and sent him topside. Said his face wasn't right for the deep shaft." She touched the silver eye at her throat. "His face wasn't right. As if the charm *told* them."

Freya smiled the smile she used for such stories. But then the widow returned for her second visit of the night, holding up her wrist where a red thread hung snapped clean through. "Bandits stopped the coach outside Harrow Ford," the woman said. "Stopped it, went through everyone's bags, and passed mine by twice. Twice, like I wasn't there." Her voice shook. "Like something wouldn't *let* them see me."

And then the boy with the finding-key, fourteen maybe, all elbows and hunger, standing in front of her stall turning a brass key over in his fingers—the key he'd bought from her four nights ago, the one meant for *finding what's lost*. "Mistress," he said slowly, "my brother ran off to sea eight years ago. And yesterday a letter comes. From across the water. He's alive. He's coming home." The boy's eyes were shining and frightened in equal measure. "How did it know?"

Charms don't know anything, Freya wanted to say. Charms are thread and tin and the shape of a wish. That was the covenant of her craft, the whole theology of it: nothing here works, and that's why it's allowed.

But she had spent the morning tasting the king's breakfast, and one dish had tasted of incense and old stone, of prayer rising off it like steam, and the poison that should have killed her had curled up in her mouth and gone docile as a blessed thing. She had felt it happen. She had felt something vast turn its attention toward the world above, the way you feel a storm arrive through the floorboards before you hear the thunder.

Forty-one charms. Forty-one wishes, loose in the city, finding their marks.

"Two coppers for the thread," she heard herself say to the next customer, and her voice sounded very far away. "Three for the silver."

* * *

The priest found her at the hour when the market began to gutter out.

She saw him first as a shape too tall for the crowd, moving against its current, and then as a face she half-knew—the kind of face you've seen at executions and funerals, solemn and used-up. Anselm. Formerly of the Shrine of the Veiled Hand, formerly a voice from the pulpit, currently a disgrace who swept temple steps that no one visited and was tolerated in the night market because pity was cheaper than charity.

He stopped at her stall. Looked at the rows of charms for a long moment without touching them. When he spoke, it was quietly enough that only she could hear.

"This is yours, then." He picked up a closed-eye charm, turned it in blunt fingers. "The work that's waking up."

"Everything wakes up eventually," Freya said. "Even old lies."

"Do you know what these are doing?" He set the charm down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you handle something that might go off. "Three nights ago a woman walked through a cutpurse's knife because your thread blinded him. Yesterday a fever broke in the Tanner's Rows that had killed six children before it. Six, and then the seventh lived, and the mother swears she bought protection from a stall on the Stair of Knives." His eyes came up to hers, and they were terrible eyes, kind and ruined. "Prayer is working again, taster. And it's working through *you*."

"I sell superstition," Freya said flatly. "At fair prices. If you want a sermon, the steps of your shrine need sweeping worse than the city needs saving."

"The mountain isn't asleep anymore." He leaned closer. She caught woodsmoke and candle-wax and something older underneath, incense soaked so deep into a man it became his smell. "You must have felt it. Whatever sealed the veil up there—whatever went silent twenty years ago—it's stirring. And prayers are pouring through the cracks like water through a failing dam. Every wish in this city is finding an ear again." He paused. "Do you know what happens to answered prayers in Vell? Do you know who collects?"

The word landed in her chest like a dropped stone. *Collector.* Somewhere beneath her ribs, in the place where guilt lived rent-free, something stirred.

"There's no one collecting," she said. "There's no one *up there*. That's the whole point of the last twenty years."

"That's what we told ourselves." Anselm straightened, and the lamplight made his shadow long and crooked behind him, like something kneeling. "We buried the gods and called the grave a silence. But a debt doesn't vanish because you stop looking at the ledger, woman. Every prayer answered tonight is written down somewhere. Every miracle has a price, and prices—" his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—prices are always collected. I've seen the accounts. I read them, once, in the shrine archives, before they stripped my orders and put me out. The court beneath keeps books, and the books are *open*."

Freya's hands had gone cold around the red thread she was winding. Twenty years ago, men in grey robes had said *equivalent exchange* and taken her sister's name in payment for her own breathing. She had told herself it was a con, a cruel trick played on a desperate orphan, that the court beneath was a story brokers told to make theft feel holy.

"Anselm." She hadn't decided to use his name; it simply left her. "What does it mean, if the prayers are being answered now? After all this time?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and something in his ruined kind face shifted—recognition, maybe, or grief, the look of a man seeing a debt called in that he'd hoped had been forgotten.

"It means," he said, "that something down there has finally found a prayer worth answering. Something specific. Someone's been asking the same question for twenty years, loud enough to wake the dead." He turned to go, then paused, his back to her. "Come to the shrine at low bell tomorrow. There's a door under the old floor that the archivists sealed, and I think—I *think*—it leads to where the veil first tore. Bring your ledger of sold charms, taster. All of them. Every wish you've ever sold." A beat. "Because if the collector is awake, then whoever's name is written deepest in those books is the one who woke him."

Freya stood alone among her harmless silver things as the market died around her, lamp by lamp, until only the memory of light remained. In her pocket, unbidden, her fingers found the nail-charm—Wren's ugly twist of thread and iron that she had carried for twenty years like a splinter she couldn't dig out.

*I asked real hard. I promised something big.*

The charm was warm. Warmer than her skin had any right to make it, humming faintly against her palm like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Somewhere below the city, something had heard a seven-year-old shouting through a wall—and had come, at last, to discuss terms.
