## 3. What the Mountain Remembers

Anselm had not been a priest in nine years, but his knees still remembered how to pray, and they complained about it the whole way down.

The stair was older than Vell itself—cut into the mountain's root before anyone thought to build a city on top of the wound. His lamp threw their shadows long and trembling against walls slick with mineral sweat, stone weeping slow tears that tasted of iron when he'd licked them once, in another life, another vocation. Behind him, Freya Ravensworth descended in silence, her breathing even, her footsteps careful. She was a woman who moved like someone perpetually tasting for poison in the air itself. He supposed twenty years of eating death for a living would teach a person that particular walk.

"You knew this was here," she said at last. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I knew something was here." Anselm lifted the lamp higher. The flame bent sideways, drawn toward something below them, as if the dark down there were inhaling. "Every priest of the old rite did. We called it the First Door. The place where the veil between what is and what was tore open, back when gods still bothered to knock."

"And you sealed it."

"We walled it up and pretended it was a cellar." He smiled without humor. "The church has always been better at pretending than at believing."

The stair ended at a landing where the masonry changed character entirely—the neat courses of human hands giving way to stone that looked grown rather than built, ribbed like the inside of a shell, veined with threads of pale quartz that caught the lamplight and held it a moment too long. Freya's hand went to her satchel. He heard the soft clink of charms against one another, dozens of them, each one harmless, each one a small act of faithlessness she'd been selling to a godless city.

"The wall," he said, and pointed.

It was a good wall. He had helped build it, in fact, in his last year of vestments—a double course of granite blocks mortared with ash and salt, inscribed on the inner face with the ninety-one names of the old pantheon so that anything on the other side would have to speak its own true name to pass. It had been the proudest work of his final faithful year. Now he raised the lamp to it and felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

The wall was breathing.

Not moving—not quite—but the mortar joints swelled and eased, swelled and eased, slow as sleep, and along the bottom course the salt-mortar had gone black as if scorched from within. And through a hairline crack that hadn't existed nine years ago, warm air seeped out, carrying a smell that stopped Freya where she stood.

"Incense," she said quietly. "And old stone."

Anselm turned to look at her. In the lamplight her face had gone the color of tallow. "You've smelled it before."

"This morning. The king's breakfast. A dish of quail in plum sauce that should have put me in the ground—I could taste the hemlock under it, clear as a bell, and underneath the hemlock there was *this*." Her voice stayed level, which impressed him more than screaming would have. "I tasted prayer, Father. For the first time in twenty years, someone's prayer actually went somewhere. And it went somewhere *down*."

"Anselm," he corrected gently. "I'm nobody's father anymore. That's rather the point of me." He set the lamp in an iron bracket and drew the crowbar from his pack. "Now. Before I do this, you should know that I have spent nine years being very carefully unremarkable, same as you. I drink too much. I sell funeral blessings at half rate. I have made an art of disappointing everyone who expected anything of me. And I am about to unseal the one door in Vell that every sensible person spent four centuries agreeing to forget." He set the bar's edge into the crack. "So I'll ask once. Do you want to know?"

Freya was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of mountain, the night market was setting up its stalls, and he imagined he could almost hear her other life going on without her—coppers changing hands, wishes being whispered over pewter moons and tin stars.

"Twenty years ago," she said finally, "my sister died. Wren. She was seven. Fever, they told me. My mother sold everything afterward, including—" Her jaw tightened. "Including me. To the palace. As a food-taster. A child, fed poisons in measured doses until my blood learned the alphabet of dying." She met his eyes. "I've spent twenty years telling myself I survived because I was clever. Because I was careful. Because I deserved to live when she didn't." The words came out flat and hard, stones dropped one by one. "Last week your god started answering prayers, and my charms started humming, and I found a silver moon I sold three days ago sitting warm as bread in a stranger's pocket. So yes, Anselm. I want to know. I want to know why the dead are stirring now, and whether Wren is among them, and I want to know it even if it kills the last comfortable lie I own."

Anselm nodded once. Then he leaned his weight into the crowbar.

The wall did not break so much as *exhale*. Granite cracked along seams no chisel had made, blocks grinding apart like teeth reluctantly opening, and the breath that rolled out of the dark beyond was warm and sweet and terrible—the smell of incense and old stone, of honey left too long in the comb, of a temple the morning after a great feast, when the gods have eaten well and the candles have burned to pools. Freya staggered. Anselm caught her arm and felt her pulse hammering under his fingers, fast as a bird's.

Beyond the broken wall lay a shrine.

It should have been ruined. Nine years sealed, four centuries abandoned—and instead the little chamber gleamed as though swept that morning. A floor of white quartz. Walls hung with banners whose colors had not faded: deep river-blue, dawn-gold, the green of new barley. And at the chamber's heart, an altar of black glass, and upon the altar—

"Receipts," Anselm breathed.

He had read about them in forbidden commentaries, in marginalia scrawled by monks who'd been burned for less. When the old court beneath the mountain made a bargain, it wrote the transaction into the world itself. Not paper. Not parchment. *Stone.* Tablets of black glass, each one holding a bargain the way amber holds an insect.

There were hundreds of them. Thousands. They covered the walls in careful rows, and as the lamplight touched them they began to glow faintly from within, waking after their long sleep, each tablet lighting up in sequence like a congregation turning to look at newcomers.

Freya walked forward as if in a dream. Her fingertips hovered over the nearest tablet without touching it, and Anselm saw her lips move, reading.

"'Given: one harvest, three generations, to the house of Corvel,'" she read. "'Received: the firstborn's voice.'" She moved to the next. "'Given: safe passage across the water. Received: the name of the drowned.'" Another. "'Given: rain in the dry year. Received:—'" She stopped.

She had found a tablet near the altar, smaller than most, set apart on a shelf of its own as if the mountain kept it close. Its glow was different from the others—softer, warmer, the color of candlelight through skin.

Anselm watched her read it. Watched her face do something he had never seen a face do in thirty years of priesthood and nine years of taverns: come apart all at once, like a wall giving way, and then refuse to fall, holding itself up through sheer refusal.

"Read it to me," he said softly. Though he already knew. He had known since the market, since he saw her charms burning with borrowed grace, since he followed her home through streets that whispered her name in voices older than the city.

Her voice came out very small. "'Given: one mortal child's survival. Complete immunity to all venom and all poison, lasting unto natural death.'" A pause. Her throat worked. "'Received: one soul, aged seven years. Name of Wren Ravensworth. Condition: intact. Term: perpetual.'"

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on them both.

"No," Freya said. It wasn't grief yet; it was the thing that comes before grief, the animal refusal. "No. There's been a mistake. This isn't mine. Someone else's sister. Some other family—"

"Freya."

"*Someone else's.*"

"Your immunity is real." Anselm's voice was gentle and merciless, the way truth must be. "You've drunk enough toxin tonight alone to kill the king's whole guard. You carry it like a second heartbeat. It didn't come from luck or cleverness or a slow-built tolerance—it came *paid for*, in full, the day you were six years old and meant to die of the same fever that took her. Your mother didn't sell everything, Freya. She sold the wrong daughter. Or perhaps—" he hesitated, hating himself for the mercy of it, "—perhaps she was given no choice about which."

Freya sat down. Not gracefully—she simply folded, knees hitting quartz, hands flat on the floor as if steadying herself against a ship's deck in weather. The tablets around her glowed brighter, feeding on her despair the way lamps feed on oil, and Anselm knelt beside her without touching her. Some wounds you had to let a person hold alone.

"Perpetual," she whispered. "Intact. They kept her *intact*. Twenty years, she's been down here, whole, and I've been upstairs eating poison and calling myself lucky."

"That's why the prayers work again. That's why the veil is tearing." Anselm looked up at the breathing walls, the waking tablets, the crack of warm light spreading slowly along the ceiling like dawn arriving in the wrong direction. "The court beneath is settling its accounts. Every bargain ever struck is being called in—or offered back. And yours, Freya Ravensworth, is the freshest soul in their ledger. The only one taken in living memory."

She rose. It took her a while, and when she stood she was different—he could see it happening, the guilt burning off like fog and leaving behind something forged. Grief, yes, and fury, but underneath both, load-bearing and immovable: *resolve*. He had seen it once before, decades ago, in the face of a young acolyte refusing to recant. His own face, in a tarnished mirror.

"They can't keep her," Freya said. It was not a plea. It was arithmetic.

"They can. They will. Unless—" He gestured at the thousands of glowing tablets, the endless ledger of exchange. "Everything here runs on payment. Equivalent trade. Nothing given, nothing received. If you mean to take your sister out of that court, you'll need to offer something they value equally." He met her eyes and hated what he was about to say, and said it anyway, because a priest—even a disgraced one—did not flinch from the cost written in front of him. "And the only thing you own that they priced into their ledger is the very thing they sold you. Your immunity. The protection that's kept you alive for twenty years."

Freya looked down at her own hands—hands that had held death a thousand times and been refused. Hands that were, he understood suddenly, the most expensive object in the city above.

"Then I'll pay it," she said. "Whatever it costs. Whoever I have to become to carry it down there." She turned to him, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "But I don't know the way beneath the mountain, Anselm. And I don't know how to steal from gods."

He blew out a long breath, and the lamplight shivered. "No," he admitted. "Neither do I. But I know a boy who hears locks whisper, and a woman in the palace kitchens who owes me a funeral blessing, and between the four of us—" he gestured at the ancient, waking dark, "—we might just manage a burglary worthy of scripture."

Far below them, deeper than the shrine, deeper than the stair, something vast turned over in its sleep—and every tablet in the chamber flared gold at once, as if the mountain had just remembered their names.
