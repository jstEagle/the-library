## 4. A Thief Who Prays

Locks talk. That was the first thing Pip ever learned about the world, before he learned his own name, before he learned that mothers could be taken by fever and fathers by the press-gangs and children by the workhouse carts that rattled through Vell's low quarter at dawn. Locks talk, if you listen right.

Most people heard nothing but the click of tumblers, the scrape of a bolt. But Pip had been born with the veil thin around him—so said the hedge-witch who'd found him swaddled in a shrine alcove, abandoned beneath a god's chipped feet—and when he pressed his ear to iron or brass or old oak banded in bronze, he heard voices. Small ones. Tired ones. A lock was just a little door that had forgotten why it was shut, and it would tell him everything: which tumbler stuck, which bolt had rusted, which ward had been paid for cheap by some merchant who trusted more in God's absence than in locksmiths.

*Left, then up, then the third tooth sticks—jiggle it,* whispered the lock on the counting-house door, and Pip obeyed, and ate well for a week.

He told himself he wasn't praying. Praying was for priests and grandmothers and fools, and besides, sorcery was outlawed in Vell, and whatever lived in his ears was too small and too stubborn to count as magic anyway. It was just listening. Just a knack. He kept it small on purpose, the way you keep a candle shielded from wind. The gods were waking under the mountain—everyone said so now, even the fishwives—and Pip figured a man who could hear locks whispering might soon hear worse things whispering too, and he wanted no part of that conversation.

Which is how he came to be following the charm-seller through the night market, telling himself it was only business.

She worked a stall near the tannery end of Tanner's Row, where the smell of lye and rot fought the smoke of roasting chestnuts and lost to it every time. Freya Ravensworth. He'd asked around. Royal food-taster by day, charm-peddler by night, twenty years in the city without a scar, an enemy, or a story anyone could tell straight. That kind of invisible didn't happen by accident. Invisible like that cost something, and things that cost something were usually worth stealing.

Her wares hung from the stall's crossbeam on red twine: little hammered-silver charms, each no bigger than a thumbnail—a key, a bell, an eye, a knot. Luck charms, love charms, protection-from-the-landlord charms. Harmless stuff. Street-blessed, street-priced. Three coppers apiece, two for five.

Except.

Except that for three nights now, Pip had watched wishes walk out of that stall and come true. The dyer's widow bought a knot-charm to bind her drunk son home, and the boy came home sober as a magistrate and weeping into her apron. A porter bought a bell-charm against thieves, and the cutpurse who'd been working the Row got his hand caught in a door and broke two fingers. Small things. Terrible small things, done gently. Wishes being answered like bills come due.

And last night, Pip had watched a woman buy the eye-charm—the one for seeing what hides—and then stand very still in the lamplight, staring at the mouth of Copper Alley where nothing was, and crossing herself with the old sign, the one that used the whole body instead of two fingers, the one that hadn't been legal in twenty years.

The gods were waking. And this unremarkable woman was selling their mail.

So tonight Pip did what Pip did best: he listened to the market instead of the locks, and he waited for her to close up, and he followed her home through streets that smelled of rain on hot stone.

She lived above her stall, which was either poverty or genius, in a room behind a door of good oak with an iron lock older than the building. Old enough, Pip hoped, to be lonely enough to gossip.

He gave her a hundred count after she climbed the stair. Then he climbed the drainpipe, quiet as regret, and knelt on the slates outside her shuttered window, and put his ear to the door below.

The lock murmured in its sleep. *Old, old, so old,* it said, voice like gravel rolled in honey. *Nobody turns me but her. Nobody asks. She oils me Thursdays and never once has she asked my name.*

"Sorry," Pip breathed. "I'll ask. What's your name?"

The lock went silent with shock. In twenty years of thieving, Pip had never once made a lock stop talking.

Then the window latch under his fingers said, very clearly: *She sees you, boy.*

Pip froze. Below him, through the gap in the shutters, lamplight moved. A shadow crossed the wall. And a voice—dry as paper, calm as deep water—said:

"You can come down off my roofline, or you can stay there until your knees give out. I've seen boys try both. Knees always win."

* * *

Pip came down. Pride was heavy, but hunger was heavier, and he'd learned long ago that the smart thief knows when the mark has become the trapper. He dropped into the alley, dusted off his knees with as much dignity as remained, and presented himself at her door wearing his best innocent face, the one that had gotten him fed by three different grandmothers in four parishes.

Freya Ravensworth opened the door already holding a lantern, and up close she was smaller than the legend of her, sharp-faced and grey-eyed, with the look of someone who had spent twenty years tasting other people's food and had never once enjoyed a meal.

"You're the one who talks to doors," she said.

"I don't—" Pip began.

"The latch told me you were coming Tuesday." She stepped back to let him in, which was the single most alarming thing that had happened to him all week. "It also told me you apologized to the downstairs lock. In twenty years, you're the first person who's ever asked its name."

"I didn't know they *had* names."

"They don't," Freya said, setting the lantern on the table. "Not until someone asks. Sit down. You've been circling my stall for three nights like a moth with a plan. Either you're the worst thief in Vell or the most polite one, and I want to know which before I decide whether to feed you."

"That depends," Pip said carefully, settling onto the stool across from her, "entirely on whether there's food."

There was bread, and hard cheese, and a jar of pickled onions that had opinions. Pip ate like the starving thing he mostly was, and Freya watched him with the patience of a woman who had raised no one and observed everyone.

"So," she said, when he slowed. "Why rob me?"

"Because your charms are doing something," Pip said, mouth half-full. He saw no reason to lie; lying to this woman felt like lying to a knife. "Wishes are coming true out of your stall. Small ones. Mean ones, sometimes. Gentle-mean. And the law says sorcery's outlawed, and the temples say the gods are dead, and everybody in this city agreed to pretend both those things forever, and you're selling the lie by the ounce at three coppers."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. Lantern-light moved in her eyes like water over stones.

"And what do you hear," she said slowly, "when you listen to them? My charms."

Pip set down the bread. Here was the part where a sensible liar would invent something comforting.

"They hum," he said. "Like bees in a wall. Except the silver one. The key-shaped one, third hook from the left. That one doesn't hum. That one *sings*, low, like a hymn nobody remembers the words to. I heard it Tuesday from across the market and it's been stuck in my head since. It sounds—" He hunted for it. "It sounds like it's waiting for someone to come home."

Freya's face did something complicated and brief, like weather over a mountain. Her hand went to her collar, where, tucked beneath her shirt, something hung on a cord.

"You can hear it from across the market," she repeated.

"It's loud," Pip said. "Loud the way bells are loud when you're standing next to them, except it isn't sound. It's more like—it's like when you're a kid and you wake up in the dark and know, just *know*, your mother's still awake in the next room. Like being known. Being *expected*."

Something in the room shifted. Not the air—the air stayed exactly where it was—but the room itself seemed to lean in, attentive, like a dog hearing its name.

"That will do," said Freya, in a strange voice, and reached up, and took the cord from around her neck.

The silver key-charm swung into the lantern light, and Pip's ears rang. Not with noise—with presence. Up close, the thing was less a charm than a held breath, less metal than memory, and every hair on his arms stood up and pointed at it like compass needles finding north.

"It sold six days ago," Freya said. "A clerk bought it, for luck in his examinations. He brought it back yesterday. Said it wouldn't leave him alone. Said it whispered his dead mother's lullaby all night." Her jaw tightened. "Charms aren't supposed to do anything. That's the point of them. That's how I've survived twenty years selling them—harmless tin and silver, street blessings, nothing the magistrates can hang a person for. They're not supposed to *wake up*."

"What changed?"

"The prayers started being answered," Freya said. She turned the key-charm over in her palm, and Pip watched the light slide across it wrong, like light on water rather than light on metal. "And something underneath this city started collecting. And now everything I've ever made with my hands is remembering what it was for."

She looked up at him then, and her grey eyes were not unkind, but they were not soft either. They were the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic with lives.

"You talk to locks," she said. "What else do you hear?"

Pip should have lied. Every instinct honed by twenty years of orphanhood and ten of thieving screamed at him to make himself small, unremarkable, safe. But the key-charm was singing its wordless hymn an arm's length away, and the room was leaning in to listen, and he was suddenly, unbearably tired of keeping his gift small enough to hide behind.

"Stairs," he said. "Doors. Chains. Wells. Anything built to open or shut or hold. They all talk. Some of them complain. Some of them remember things." He swallowed. "Once—I heard a crypt door under Saint Verene's tell a newer gate about the funeral it had seen. Forty years back. Names. Dates. Everything."

Freya went very still.

"Pip," she said. "That's your name? The market calls you the Latch-Lad, but Anselm called you Pip."

"You know Father Anselm?" The disgraced priest of the drowned chapel, who preached to nobody on the mudflats and was considered mad by everyone including, possibly, himself.

"He's going to take me somewhere tomorrow," Freya said. "Somewhere under the city. Somewhere the veil tore first, a long time ago." She set the singing key-charm on the table between them, and it hummed like a struck glass. "I have to go down there, and get something back that was stolen from me before I could fight for it. And the way down is full of doors, Pip. Sealed doors. Doors that have been shut for twenty years and longer, doors that have *forgotten why they're shut*, and I need someone who can ask them to open."

Pip looked at the charm. At the woman. At the pickled onions, because courage needed staging posts.

"What's in it for me?"

"A cut of whatever we find. Food. And the answer to a question I think you've been asking your whole life without knowing it."

"Which is?"

"Why the gods left you on a shrine step," Freya said quietly, "and whether they're done with you yet."

The room leaned closer. The charm sang. Somewhere far beneath the city, under stone older than the mountain's name, something vast turned over in its sleep, and every lock in Vell whispered at once—a long, shivering sigh passing through the walls like wind through wheat—and Pip felt it go through him too, through his bones, through the thin place he'd been born with, and understood with perfect clarity that hiding season was over.

"I want it in writing," he said, because a man had to keep some dignity.

"There's no writing down there," Freya said. "Only receipts."

"That," said Pip, "is somehow worse."

But he shook on it anyway, her hand dry and strong around his, and outside the shuttered window the night market murmured on, buying and selling, wishing and paying, while beneath the cobbles the old court began, patiently, to count its guests.

* * *

He dreamed that night of a door taller than cathedrals.

It stood in darkness without floor or ceiling, and it was carved all over with faces—thousands of them, millions, every face mid-shout or mid-song or mid-prayer, and between the faces ran words in a script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts. The door was not locked. That was the worst of it. All that mass, all that dread, and no lock at all, because a thing that certain of its own weight needs no bolt.

It only needed to be *asked*.

And as Pip stood before it in the dream, small as a beetle against a cathedral, the door bent down toward him—all those carved faces turning, all those carved mouths opening—and spoke with a voice like a landslide given manners:

*LITTLE LISTENER. WE HAVE BEEN SO QUIET FOR SO LONG. WHEN YOU COME BELOW—AND YOU WILL COME BELOW—*

The dream folded like paper. Pip woke in his nest above the cooper's yard with dawn coming grey through the gaps in the boards and his heart going like a drummer boy's, and the taste of incense and old stone in his mouth, and one thought ringing louder than all the rest:

*She never said what she wants back.*
