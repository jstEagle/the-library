## 5. The Second Spoonful

The royal kitchens woke before the city did, the way a body wakes before its mind—heat first, then noise, then the slow assembling of purpose. Freya had slipped into that rhythm twenty years ago and never once been noticed breaking it. That was the art of it. A taster who drew the eye was a taster whose funeral came cheap.

She moved through the pre-dawn steam like a woman made of it. Copper pots hung in ranks overhead, catching the firelight and throwing it back dull as old coins. The night bakers were folding down their ovens; the morning brigade was sharpening knives with a sound like whispered argument. Somewhere above, the king still slept his kingly sleep, dreaming whatever kings dreamed, and every dream he would taste at breakfast had to pass through her mouth first.

Freya carried the ledger against her ribs.

It was not a large thing to look at—a slim book bound in cracked leather gone green at the corners, the color of bronze left too long in rain. It had lived for three days inside the false bottom of the flour bin behind the pastry station, beneath forty pounds of milled white nothing, and she had told herself each of those nights that tomorrow she would stop being a fool and leave it where Anselm's map said it slept. But ledgers of bargains did not stay hidden by wishing, and neither, apparently, did she.

*The court keeps accounts,* Anselm had said, in the shrine under the city, his voice still frayed from reading what the wall-writings gave up. *Everything given, everything taken. If your sister was sold, there is a page with her name on it.*

There was a page. She had found it on the second night, by candle-stub, with flour dusting her sleeves like early snow.

*Ravensworth, Wren. Age six years. Payment received: one life preserved against all poison. Term: until claimed or traded. Signed beneath the seal of the Under-Court, witnessed by none, mourned by one.*

Mourned by one. The ink had been dry twenty years and still Freya had pressed her thumb against that last word until the paper remembered the shape of her grief.

Tonight she meant to take it out through the servants' stair, past the scullery, into the gray hour when the watch changed and no one counted faces. It was a good plan. It was even, mostly, a safe one. Which was why the voice behind her nearly stopped her heart outright.

"You're standing in the wrong corner," Maren said, "for someone holding nothing."

* * *

Maren Ostwick had been a taster longer than Freya—by four months, which she mentioned often enough that the months might have been years—and she wore the office the way a hawk wears feathers: beautifully, and with the constant suggestion that something smaller was about to die. She stood now at the end of the pastry aisle with a heel of yesterday's bread in one hand and her eyes doing arithmetic across Freya's face.

"I'm checking the bin," Freya said. "The rye's turning."

"The rye turned Tuesday. I fed it to the ducks." Maren took a step closer. Her boots made no sound; she'd learned that trick from the same teachers Freya had. "You've been coming down at odd hours for three nights. You smell of candle smoke, which we're not allowed, and you flinch when the ovens tick, which you never used to do. And right now—" she nodded at Freya's apron, at the shape pressing the linen out of true along her ribs, "—you're carrying a book you don't want anyone to see."

The kitchen roared softly around them: water boiling, iron settling, a boy singing off-key about eels somewhere near the fish station. All that noise, and Freya heard only her own pulse.

Twenty years of being unremarkable. Twenty years of the slow patient work of poison after poison—hemlock tea aged to kindness, arsenic folded into honey, the little deaths taken nightly so the big one could never find purchase. All of it bought so she could stand invisible in rooms like this one. And here was Maren Ostwick, four months senior, looking at her as if invisibility were a garment slipping off at the shoulder.

"Give me the book," Maren said, "or tell me why I shouldn't fetch the steward of the household and be done with it."

And there it was—the fork in the road, sharp as a knife's edge on a whetstone. Because Maren could do it. One word to the steward and Freya would hang by week's end, thief of palace property, and worse than a thief: a taster with secrets, which the court read as a taster with a buyer. Loyalty to the crown was the whole architecture of Maren's life. Freya knew that. She had watched her build it, brick by careful brick, the way Freya had built her own walls.

So she did the mad thing. She took out the ledger and held it where Maren could see the cover, the green-cracked leather, the sigil stamped blind into it—a mountain upside down, roots like fingers.

"This," Freya said quietly, "is a list of everything the old gods ever bought. Including my sister."

* * *

They ended up in the cold room, because it was the only place with a lock that worked and a reason for two women to speak low among hanging things. Salted hams swung overhead like pale moons. The chill got into the bones and made honesty easier, the way cold always makes you admit what hurts.

Freya told her all of it. The answered prayers. The charm gone warm in her pocket. The shrine beneath the city and the writings on the wall, and then the page itself—she opened the ledger flat on the salting table and let Maren read the line about Wren, six years old, sold to buy her sister's breath.

Maren read it twice. When she looked up, her face had done something Freya had never seen it do: it had stopped performing.

"They let you taste for them," Maren said slowly, "with this sitting in their own flour bin. They fed you breakfasts for twenty years and kept the receipt." She touched the page without quite touching it, the way one touches a wound through a bandage. "Your sister. You never said you had a sister."

"There wasn't anything useful in saying it."

"No." Maren exhaled. "No, there wouldn't have been. That's the trick of this place, isn't it? Grief's only permitted if it's tidy."

Freya waited for the rest of it—for the lecture, for *you must return this, for the oath, for the crown*, all the sentences she had rehearsed rebuttals to while Maren read. They didn't come. Instead Maren pulled the bread from her pocket, tore it, set half beside the ledger like an offering to nobody, and ate her half standing up, thinking.

"Anselm," she said finally. "The priest they defrocked. The one who used to say grace over the guard's mess until the bishop decided grace was a liability." Her mouth twisted. "He's mixed up in this."

"He's the one who showed me the shrine."

"Of course he is." Maren paced two steps between the hams, turned, paced back. Freya watched the war happen behind her eyes and recognized it, because she'd been losing the same war for a week. On one side: the oath, the kitchen, the warm place by the fire, the self that survives by belonging to something larger and asking no questions. On the other side: a dead girl named Wren, and the simple obscene fact of a soul filed away like a debt.

"If I help you," Maren said, stopping, "I'm not a taster anymore if it goes wrong. I'm a conspirator. They don't hang us nicely, Freya. They make an example. I've seen it."

"I know."

"And if I report you, I keep my post, and I get to eat supper every night knowing exactly what kind of woman I am." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. Then she laughed—one short, hard note, fog in the cold air. "Twenty years I've tasted for that man upstairs. Do you know what I learned? He salts everything. Even the dishes that are supposed to be sweet. A king who can't trust his own tongue trusts no one, and I have spent my whole career being trusted by a man who trusts no one, and I am *so tired*, Ravensworth."

"Maren—"

"Shut up. I'm deciding." She closed her eyes. In the lamplight her face went young suddenly, younger than her years, the face of a girl who had once believed grace was possible over a guard's rough table. When she opened them again, the decision had been made and armored over, the way she made all her decisions.

"Here's how it will be," she said. "The book goes back in the bin tonight—we need the court calm, not searching. We copy the pages that matter, all of them, and we do it in shifts so no one hand tires and slips. You'll tell me what the priest knows, and the boy too, if there's a boy, because there's always a boy. And when you go under that mountain—" she jabbed a finger at Freya's sternum, "—you will tell me before you go, not after. Not *if*. Before. I have watched you walk around this palace for two decades pretending you're furniture, and I am not letting you die in a hole in the ground as furniture. If we do this, we do it as people. Both of us. Sworn."

"Sworn on what?" Freya asked. "Neither of us has gods we trust."

Maren considered the hams, the cold, the salt-hung dark. "On the second spoonful," she said. "Every tasting of my life, the rule has been the same: the first spoonful might be luck. The second is commitment. So." She held out her hand, chapped palm up, steady as a scale. "Second spoonful, Ravensworth. Are you in?"

Freya took it. Her hand was shaking and she let it shake, and Maren pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

They copied the ledger by turns until the fourth bell, Maren's pen scratching in the dark like a small determined insect. Near the end, in the section Freya hadn't dared read past, Maren stopped copying and started simply staring.

"Freya," she said, and her voice had gone strange. "There's a column here. *Terms of reclaim.*"

"I know it. Equivalent payment. That's what the priest said."

"Yes, but listen to what the equivalent *is*." Maren turned the page toward her and put her finger under the line, under the cramped clerk-hand of some under-world scrivener, and read aloud:

"*A life preserved may be reclaimed by the preservation of another—or by the willing surrender of the preservation itself.*"

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved.

"They don't want gold," Maren whispered. "They want your immunity. They want the one thing keeping you alive."

Above them, faint through stone and flour and twenty years of careful silence, the palace bell began to ring the hour—and beneath it, deeper, from somewhere under the mountain, Freya heard—or dreamed she heard—a second bell answering, patient as a collector at a door.
