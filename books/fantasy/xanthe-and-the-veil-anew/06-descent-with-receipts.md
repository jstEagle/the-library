## 6. Descent, With Receipts

### I.

Freya

The way down began behind the shrine altar, where Anselm pressed his palm to a stone carved with a mouth and said something in a language that made Freya's teeth ache. The wall opened like a patient throat.

She had expected darkness. What she got was *light*—a faint gold seep from the rock itself, as though the mountain had swallowed a sunset twenty years ago and never finished digesting it. It fell on the stair as they descended, on Anselm's gray robe, on Pip's quick nervous hands, on Maren's kitchen knife that she'd brought "just in case," as if steel would matter down here.

"Stay behind me," Anselm said. "And whatever is offered to you, do not eat."

"I'm a food taster," Freya said. "It's the one rule I've never broken for anyone worth breaking it for."

The stairs went down farther than stairs should. Freya counted two hundred steps and then stopped counting because the number stopped meaning anything. The air changed first—it grew sweet, then mineral, then sweet again, like honey poured over graves. Her skin prickled with the memory of every poison she'd ever survived, each one waking briefly like old dogs hearing their names.

Then the tunnel opened, and there it was: the under-city.

Freya had imagined caverns, bones, screaming. Instead she found a *market*. Stalls of pale stone arranged along streets lit by veins of gold in the walls. And the vendors—

Souls. That was the only word her mind would hold. They stood behind their counters like shopkeepers, translucent figures flickering between the people they had been and something older, vaster, wearing those people like borrowed coats. One had a fishmonger's apron and eyes like eclipses. Another smiled at them with the face of a girl no older than Wren had been.

"Receipts," Maren whispered beside her. "Look at what they're trading."

Freya looked. On the nearest stall, laid out like pastries under glass, were memories. She could see them—actual small glimmering things, shaped like seeds, like coins, like teeth. A vendor turned one over with fingers of smoke, and for half a heartbeat Freya *felt* it: a summer afternoon, bread baking, someone laughing in another room. Then it was gone, and the hollow it left behind ached worse than any poison.

"They sell what people forget," she said quietly. "Every prayer that goes unanswered up there comes down here and gets *spent*."

"Keep moving," Anselm said, but his voice had gone thin. Twenty years a priest, disgraced, and now walking through the proof that his god had been doing business all along.

Pip was grinning. Of course Pip was grinning.

* * *

### II.

Anselm

He had dreamed of this place as a novice. That was the shame he'd never confessed—not the drinking, not the forged letters of ordination, but the dreams. He had *wanted* the gods to be real enough to touch, and some traitorous part of him still did, even now, even here, where wanting anything was dangerous as an open wound.

The market recognized him. He felt it in the way conversations dimmed as they passed, in the way vendors tilted their heads like birds scenting grain. Word traveled fast below; word was the only currency faster than souls.

"You've been here before," Freya said. Not accusing. Worse—understanding.

"Once. When I was young and stupid enough to think faith was a door you could knock on." He kept his eyes forward. "They answered. That's how I learned prayers have interest rates."

The contract archive rose ahead of them at the street's end: a building like a temple and a counting house had quarreled over one body, columns of black stone, doors of green glass. Above the entrance, in letters of gold that rearranged themselves when he wasn't looking directly at them, ran the words **ALL DEBTS ARE SACRED**.

Inside, the air was cold and dry as old paper. Shelves climbed into darkness, and upon the shelves, contracts—each one a small flame burning in a color Anselm had no name for. A clerk approached: tall, sexless, its face a smooth oval with a single seam where a mouth might be.

"State the name you're seeking," it said, in a voice like pages turning.

"Wren Ravensworth," Freya said. Her voice didn't shake. Anselm admired that and grieved it in equal measure; a person should be allowed to shake, at a moment like this.

The clerk's seam parted. "Ravensworth. Ravensworth." Somewhere above, a flame guttered and drifted down to hover between them, small and blue-white and warm. Anselm felt heat come off it like a sleeping child's breath.

Freya reached out. Anselm caught her wrist. "Read it first," he said. "All of it. Especially the part about voiding."

She read. He watched her face do what faces do when wonder curdles—the brightening, then the stilling, then the terrible calm.

"Equivalent payment," she read aloud. "Of course. There's always an equivalent payment."

* * *

### III.

Pip

Here was the thing about Pip, and he'd tell anyone who'd listen, which was nobody: locks talked to him. Had done since he was six, since the fever, since the night the sky split open over Vell and something fell into him instead of rain. Doors whispered their weaknesses. Chests confided. And down here in the under-city, everything was *talking*, and Pip's head felt like a cathedral full of gossiping choirboys.

"The shelves," he muttered, walking backward so he could watch Freya's face while she read. "The shelves are telling me the fire contracts are insured. You can't make this up. There's a *claims process*. For souls."

"Pip," Freya said.

"Also the clerk is lying about the hours. Says it never closes, but there's a whole side door marked—"

"*Pip.*"

But he'd already seen it too, because he wasn't just a thief, he was a good one, and good thieves read rooms. The contract burned blue-white in front of her, and her face had gone the color of the paper ledgers in the palace kitchens, and Maren had put a hand on her shoulder the way you steady someone before bad news lands.

"Equivalent payment," Pip said softly. "Meaning what, exactly?"

The clerk's seam opened. "A soul of equal weight. Or—" and here the thing paused, savoring, the way moneylenders savor, "—an equivalent suffering, freely given. The court accepts pain. Grief. Years. Protection freely surrendered. Love, in certain denominations."

"Certain denominations," Maren repeated, flat as a dropped pan.

"The exchange must balance," the clerk said, untroubled. "That is sacred law. We do not set the prices. We merely keep the ledger." Its faceless head turned toward Freya, and Pip felt the hair rise on his arms. "You, above all, should understand balance, taster. Every poison you survived was a withdrawal. Someone has been paying your account for twenty years."

Pip watched Freya go very still. Watched her look at the little blue-white flame that had once been a girl named Wren, who had once shared a bed with her sister and stolen the blanket and laughed about it, probably, the way sisters do.

And Pip—who stole because the world had never given him anything, who prayed to no one because everyone he'd prayed to had let him down—felt something crack open in his chest that felt disgustingly like *care*.

"So what's she weigh?" he asked the clerk, jerking his chin at the flame, because somebody had to ask, and Freya's voice had clearly gone somewhere none of them could follow. "Wren. Ballpark figure. What does it cost to buy a sister back?"

The seam curved. It might have been a smile. On the clerk's blank face it was infinitely worse.

"That depends," it said, "on what the court decides her sister's protection is worth. And the court," the clerk added, drifting backward into the dark between the shelves, "will want to meet her. It has been *waiting* so patiently."

The flames along the shelves all bent at once, like grass under wind, pointing toward a door at the archive's far end—a door of green glass, and beyond it, music. Old music. Music played on instruments that hadn't existed above ground in a thousand years.

"Great," Pip said. "I hate it here."

* * *

### IV.

Maren

Maren had tasted death for nine years and thought herself past surprise. She'd held the king's morning cup while assassins' work fizzed on her tongue like copper coins; she'd swallowed what would have felled a bull and walked it off by supper. Fear and she were old colleagues who nodded politely in corridors.

This place made fear feel new again.

She kept close to Freya. That was the truth of it, whatever else she told herself—that she was here for the ledger knowledge, for the court bargains hidden in the royal kitchens, for professional curiosity. She stayed because twenty years ago Freya Ravensworth had taken the poisoned dish meant for a seven-year-old prince without being asked, without thanks, without ever once mentioning it, and Maren had carried the shape of that silence ever since like a stone in her shoe.

Now she watched Freya stand before her sister's soul—a flame, just a flame, small enough to cup in two hands—and Maren understood that the silence had never been stoicism. It had been arithmetic. *I live; she pays. I taste; she burns.* Twenty years of meals, each one a coin spent from a dead girl's purse.

"We don't have to decide tonight," Maren said quietly, when the others drifted toward the green door and the impossible music. "Whatever they're offering. Whatever the price list says. We walk out, we regroup, we come back smarter. That's how a kitchen works. You don't plate a dish the moment you conceive it."

Freya didn't answer at first. The blue-white light moved on her face like water.

"When I was eight," Freya finally said, "Wren gave me the last of the bread and told me she wasn't hungry. She was always hungry. She lied so smoothly I believed her for twenty years, and then I found out she'd been doing it the whole time, in bigger ways, and I never knew." Her hand hovered near the flame without touching. "I'm not walking out, Maren. But thank you for offering me the version of me who could."

Beyond the green door, the music swelled, and beneath it Maren heard voices—many voices, layered, ancient, *hungry*—and one of them, faint as a memory of incense, spoke a word she realized with a lurch was Freya's name.

The door swung inward on its own.

Warm light spilled out, gold as the king's table, and a smell rolled over them that Maren's trained tongue identified instantly and hopelessly: roasted meats, honeyed wine, fresh bread—every dish she had ever tasted and survived, blended into one invitation. A table stretched away into the dark, set for more guests than Maren could count, and at its far end something vast unfolded itself from the shadows like smoke deciding to be a shape.

"Freya Ravensworth," the voices said together, gentle as a mother, vast as a landslide. "You kept our appointments faithfully for twenty years. Come. Sit. We would like to discuss your *bill*."

Maren's hand found Freya's sleeve. Pip swore softly in three dialects. Anselm began, involuntarily, to pray—and this time, horribly, wonderfully, something below *answered*.

Freya stepped forward, and Maren, oath-breaker, heretic-by-association, keeper of a dead friend's secret, stepped with her.
