## 7. The Court Beneath

The doors of the court were not doors.

They were the idea of a door, rendered in basalt and patience—a threshold so old it had forgotten what closing meant, standing open at the end of a corridor that had been breathing around us for an hour as we walked it. The stone exhaled warmth against my skin, and every breath carried a flavor I could not name: not incense this time, but something older, the taste of rain falling into caves before there was anyone to drink it.

Pip walked behind me with his hands jammed in his pockets, which I suspected was the only thing keeping them from shaking. Maren had stayed above with Anselm, holding the rope and the lantern and the argument we'd have when—if—we came back up.

"Freya," Pip whispered. "The walls are tasting *you*."

He wasn't wrong. I felt it like a tongue drawn along my spine, slow and appraising, the way I had once been appraised at fourteen years old by a chamberlain who wanted to know if the girl from the provinces would flinch. I did not flinch now either. Twenty years of swallowing death in small doses had burned the flinch out of me entirely.

"Then let it find me seasoned," I said.

The corridor opened, and the court beneath the mountain assembled itself out of the dark.

I say *assembled* because they were not waiting for us—they were becoming, the way frost becomes on glass, one shape at a time. There were seven of them, or perhaps seven was simply how many my mind could hold without breaking. They sat—or occupied the suggestion of sitting—around a table of black glass that reflected nothing in the room except us. Their bodies were carved from materials I recognized only by taste: obsidian and marrow and dried riverbed, moth-wing and salt. Where faces should have been there were impressions, like thumbprints pressed into wet clay by someone who had never seen a face but had heard them described.

The middle one spoke first. Its voice arrived inside my mouth rather than my ears, the way a flavor does.

*THE TASTER.*

"You know me," I said. My voice sounded very small and very alive down there, which I suppose was the point of me.

*WE KNOW THE WORK OF YOUR TONGUE.* Something like amusement moved through the chamber, a cold current. *TWENTY YEARS. SEVENTY-FOUR POISONS. THREE THAT SHOULD HAVE ENDED YOU EVEN SO, AND DID NOT, BECAUSE WE WERE PAID TO LOOK AWAY FROM YOUR DEATH.*

My heart stumbled. "Paid."

*EVERYTHING IS PAID, LITTLE MORTAL. THAT IS THE FIRST LAW AND THE LAST.* A gesture—seven limbs moving in imperfect unison—and the black table bloomed with light. Upon its surface lay a contract written in a script that squirmed when looked at directly, and beside it, a shape.

A soul. Small and quicksilver-bright, folded like a bird sleeping with its head under its wing.

Wren.

Every plan, every rehearsed speech, every cold calculation I had carried down the mountain went out of me like heat through cracked glass. I gripped the table's edge because my legs had opinions of their own.

"She's real," I said. Foolish. Obvious. True.

*SHE IS HELD,* said the court. *HELD IS NOT OWNED. THERE IS A DISTINCTION, AND YOU HAVE COME TO LEARN IT.*

* * *

They laid out the bargain the way a merchant lays out cloth: smoothly, both sides showing, nothing hidden that the law required them to reveal.

The first offering glowed at the table's center. It took the form of a spoon—of course it did; they knew their customer—and within the bowl of the spoon sat my own immunity, distilled, visible as a faint gold shimmer like honey held to lamplight.

*KEEP IT,* they said. *IT WAS BOUGHT WITH HER. IT BELONGS TO YOU BY RIGHT OF PURCHASE. SERVE US AS TASTER ETERNAL, AND THE SLEEPING BIRD MAY VISIT YOUR DREAMS EACH YEAR ON HER NAME-DAY. YOU WILL NOT AGE. YOU WILL NOT DIE BY CUP OR KNIFE OR SLOW ROT. YOU WILL STAND AT OUR TABLE BELOW AND THE KING'S TABLE ABOVE, AND EVERY DISH THAT PASSES EITHER LIP WILL PASS YOURS FIRST.*

"And Wren?" My voice came out flat. Careful.

*SHE REMAINS. HELD. BUT VISITING. IS THAT NOT MORE THAN THE DEAD ARE GIVEN?*

It was more than the dead were given. That was the horror of it—it was *generous*, by their arithmetic. Immortality, safety, my sister's ghost once a year like a festival candle. I would taste forever. I would be useful forever. I would never again lie awake cataloguing which of my enemies might have reached the palace kitchens, because no poison would ever touch me again.

And Wren would spend eternity as a bird asleep in a box, let out once a year to sing in my dreams.

The second offering rose beside the first, and it was not beautiful at all. It was just my own body, shown back to me—the way I'd look without the gold shimmer in my blood. Mortal. Poisonable. Killable by anything with a grudge and a mortar and pestle. Half the city would cheer to see me dead, and half the other half wouldn't notice.

*GIVE IT BACK,* said the court. *SURRENDER WHAT WAS BOUGHT, AND THE PURCHASE IS UNDONE. THE CONTRACT VOIDS. THE BIRD GOES FREE—WHOLE, AWAKE, HERS. SHE WILL CHOOSE WHAT THE DEAD CHOOSE. AND YOU WILL WALK BACK UP THE MOUNTAIN AS ORDINARY AS BREAD.*

"Unprotected," I said.

*UNPROTECTED,* they agreed, and there was hunger in it, patient and vast. *THE VEIL IS TEARING, TASTER. THE CITY ABOVE WILL BE A DIFFICULT PLACE FOR AN UNPROTECTED WOMAN. GODS WILL WALK IN IT AGAIN. SOME OF THEM HOLD GRUDGES OLDER THAN YOUR KINGDOM.*

I looked at the spoon of golden light. I thought about twenty years of careful, cowardly living—every charm I'd sold that was deliberately harmless, every morning I'd made myself forgettable, every friendship I'd declined before it could cost me. I had built my whole life into a hiding place and called it survival.

And here they were, offering me the keys to the hiding place forever, if only I agreed to leave my sister locked in theirs.

"That's not a bargain," I heard myself say. "That's a menu. You're asking me to choose which of us gets eaten."

*ALL CHOICE IS APPETITE,* said the court, mildly. *TAKE THE NIGHT TO CONSIDER. TIME MOVES DIFFERENTLY HERE. A NIGHT BELOW IS AN HOUR ABOVE. YOUR FRIENDS WILL NOT MISS YOU.*

* * *

We were given a alcove of warm stone to wait in, which I suspect was less courtesy than theater—the court enjoyed watching its customers squirm. Pip sat across from me, knees pulled up, and for a long while he said nothing, which for Pip was roughly equivalent to another person screaming.

Finally: "You're going to give it up."

"I haven't decided."

"You decided the moment you saw her." He nodded toward where the contract lay, where the small bright shape slept. "You've got the same face Maren makes when someone puts food in front of her that she already knows she loves. Stubborn about it."

"Pip."

"I'm not done." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he used for secrets worth stealing. "While you were talking, I was listening. Not to them—to the *table*. Black glass, right? Everything reflects in it eventually, even things that don't want to be reflected." His eyes were too bright in the dimness. "Freya, the veil tearing open isn't weather. It isn't accident. They're *pulling it*, thread by thread, and your immunity is part of the loom. Every year you don't die, they weave another knot. Keep the bargain, serve eternal, and you're not just their taster—you're their anchor. The thing that holds the tear wide while they climb through."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the mountain.

"They're going to take the city back," I said. "The city that outlawed them."

"The city that forgot them," Pip corrected softly. "Which they somehow hate worse."

I looked down at my hands. Hands that had fed kings. Hands that had survived seventy-four deaths in miniature. And underneath everything, the truth I had been circling since Anselm showed me Wren's name in the ledger: the immunity was never mine. It was bought goods. Stolen goods. I had spent twenty years treasuring a thing that had cost my sister everything, wearing it like a coat I'd told myself was inherited honestly.

Temptation didn't look like a devil offering gold. Temptation looked like a spoonful of safety, offered kindly, priced in someone else's soul.

"Pip," I said. "When we go back in there—I need you to do something. And you're going to hate it."

His grin was crooked and terrified and entirely his. "Steal something?"

"Nothing." I stood. My legs held. "You're going to steal *nothing*, loudly, while everyone watches you. Because what I'm about to offer them isn't on their menu, and I'll need every eye in that court pointed anywhere but at the contract."

Below us, far below, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the mountain tasted of iron.

I walked toward the doors-that-were-not-doors, mortal already in my heart, and asked to be brought before the court one last time.
