## 8. Loyalty Is a Kind of Truth

Maren Voss had sworn her oaths with a spoon in her mouth, which was fitting, because everything she had ever believed had come to her by way of the tongue.

Twenty-two years in the palace kitchens. Twenty-two years of tasting for the king—first under old Master Corvin, then beside him, then alone after his lungs gave out one winter and left her senior taster of Vell. She knew every poison the city's alchemists could brew, every herb that turned bitter on the second swallow, every sweetener that lied about what it carried. Her palate was a ledger. She trusted it the way priests trusted scripture: completely, and without ever asking where it came from.

So when the king's breakfast arrived that morning smelling faintly of myrrh—myrrh, in a palace that hadn't burned incense since the old shrines were bricked over—her tongue told her the truth before her mind was ready to hear it.

The honey cakes tasted of answered prayer.

She spat them into the silver testing bowl and stood very still in the grey light of the serving corridor, listening to her own heartbeat. The flavor lingered. Warm stone. Cold incense. Something older than both, like the smell of a church no one remembered building. It was the same taste Freya Ravensworth had described three nights ago, white-faced in the pantry, holding up a silver charm as if it were a snake.

*Maren,* she thought, *you are in a story you do not understand.*

She had spent her whole life being the woman who understood things. Who tasted first. Who swallowed or didn't, and whose verdict was law. And now the ground beneath that certainty had begun to taste wrong too—of myrrh and mountain and something rising.

She finished her duties mechanically. She declared the breakfast clean—the cakes *were* clean, whatever strange ghost rode their flavor—and watched the king eat, and felt nothing when he sighed with pleasure, and hated herself a little for how easy the numbness came.

Then she went to find out what Freya Ravensworth had dragged her into.

* * *

The summons came before she found Freya. It came for Anselm instead.

Maren heard it from the scullery, where word traveled faster than through any official channel: the Inquisitor-General's men had taken a priest from the low quarter at dawn. A disgraced priest, they said—one who had served at the palace chapel years ago before some scandal unseated him. He'd been preaching in the night market, they said. Selling blessings alongside a charm-witch's trinkets. Speaking of the mountain. Of the gods beneath it. Of prayers being answered.

They said he hadn't resisted. That was the part that lodged in Maren's chest like a fishbone. He had put down his satchel himself, folded his hands, and gone quietly—as if he had been waiting for them, as if arrest were simply another form of prayer.

By noon the notices were nailed to the kitchen doors: *Anselm of the Low Quarter, charged with heresy against the Crown Faith and unlawful invocation of forbidden powers.* The trial would be held within the week. The penalty was fire, though the Crown had grown squeamish about public burning; these days they did it in the courtyard behind the armory, where the smoke drifted away from the nobles' windows.

Maren read the notice twice. Then she went to the cold larder, where Freya kept her private stores, and found the other taster sitting on an upturned crate with her arms wrapped around herself, looking like a woman who had already been tried and sentenced.

"They took Anselm," Maren said.

"I know."

"He wouldn't give names?"

Freya laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "He wouldn't even give his own. They asked who else preached with him. He said the mountain taught him everything he knows, so they should go arrest the mountain." She looked up. "Maren. They'll come for me next. For all of us. Pip, me—you if they learn what we've been doing."

"What *have* you been doing?" Maren asked, though she wasn't sure she wanted the answer, and Freya's face told her she was right not to want it. "No. Don't tell me. Not yet." She sat down heavily on the neighboring crate. The larder smelled of salt pork and dried apples and the particular stone-cold of the palace's oldest walls, and she thought absurdly that it might be the last time she stood in this room as an honest woman. "Tell me instead about the ledgers."

Freya went still. "What ledgers?"

"Don't insult me, Freya. I've worked these kitchens twenty-two years. I know which cabinets have false backs." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "You've been copying pages out of the court's old bargain-books. Contracts. Debts. I've seen the ink on your fingers. I've seen how you flinch when the steward does inventory. So either you tell me what those pages say, or I walk out of this larder and forget I ever noticed—which is its own kind of lie, and I'm running low on room for those."

For a long moment Freya just looked at her. And Maren realized, watching her, that this was what fear looked like on a careful person: not trembling, but arithmetic. Freya was calculating exactly how much truth she could spend and still survive.

"The court beneath the mountain keeps accounts," Freya said finally, quietly. "Everything given, everything owed. My sister's soul is in those books, Maren. Sold before I could walk. And the palace has copies—or fragments of copies—from the years when the kings still bargained with the gods openly. If the Inquisitors get those pages, they don't just hang me for heresy. They prove the Crown has trafficked with the very powers it burns people for worshiping. And they'll burn everyone who touched the evidence to keep it quiet." She paused. "Including a senior taster whose testimony would be worth more than her life."

Maren sat with that. Somewhere above them, the kitchen roared on—chopping, boiling, the bellow of the under-cook—a whole world performing its ordinary morning while two women sat in the cold and discussed treason.

"You should destroy them," Maren said.

"I need them. To free Wren, I need to know the terms of the contract. The exact terms. The court below is bound by its own laws—if I misquote a single clause—" Freya pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I can't burn them. But I can't keep them here either. Every day they sit in my cabinet, someone dies a little sooner."

"Then they shouldn't sit in your cabinet," Maren said. "Give them to me."

* * *

That night, Maren could not sleep.

She lay in her narrow bed in the servants' wing and listened to the palace breathe—the watchmen's boots, the wind worrying the weathervanes, the far-off groan of the mountain itself, which she had never noticed until this month and now could not stop hearing. The copied ledger pages were hidden in her own quarters now, sewn into the ticking of her mattress, and they weighed on her like a sleeping child. Or a corpse.

Her oath rose up in the dark and stood over her.

*I swear faithful service to the Crown of Vell. I swear my tongue shall be true, my verdict honest, my body a wall between the sovereign and all harm.* She had spoken those words at nineteen, kneeling on cold flagstones, certain that honesty was the whole of virtue—that a taster's oath was the purest oath in the kingdom, because it admitted no ambiguity. You tasted. You told the truth. Everything else was someone else's sin.

But lying in the dark, she found the oath turning over in her mind like a coin, showing its other face. *My tongue shall be true.* True to whom? The Crown that sold children to gods? The Inquisitor-General who burned preachers for hearing answers? The king himself, kind and dim, who ate his honey cakes without ever knowing the price of the sugar?

Loyalty, she had always believed, meant keeping faith with power. Power fed you; you fed power your truth; the exchange was clean.

But loyalty, she was beginning to understand, was not a debt paid upward. It was a choice made sideways—toward the people who shared your table when the tables of the mighty were laid with poison. Loyalty was a kind of truth, maybe the only kind that mattered: the truth of whom you actually served, once all the pretty words were stripped away.

And the people at her table were a charm-seller who had spent twenty years swallowing death so strangers could eat safely. A priest who walked into chains rather than name his friends. A thief-child who prayed to locks. A dead girl waiting under a mountain for someone to come collect her.

Against that: a seal, a salary, and a lie she could live with until she died comfortably of old age.

It wasn't much of a contest. It had never been much of a contest. That was the thing that made her laugh, alone in the dark—a short, startled bark of laughter at her own expense. She had spent twenty-two years believing she was brave every time she swallowed a suspect dish. She had been merely dutiful. The bravery was only starting now.

* * *

Three days later, the Inquisitor-General's men came to the kitchens.

There were four of them, grey-cloaked, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no. Their leader was a lean woman with a voice like dry paper. She set a warrant on the steward's desk and informed the assembled staff—with the relish of a woman announcing a feast—that the crown required all records pertaining to the palace's historical expenditures. All records. Every ledger. Every scrap of accounting going back a hundred years.

"Including," she added, letting her gaze drift across the cooks and scullions and tasters lined up against the wall, "the archives of the old household offices. Certain documents are believed to have been removed from storage recently. Removed unlawfully." Another pause, longer, sweeter. "Anyone possessing such materials will be shown great leniency if they come forward tonight. Anyone concealing them will be treated as an accessory to heresy and treason both."

The silence in the kitchen had texture. Maren felt it pressing against her skin like humidity before a storm. Beside her, a junior scullery maid was shaking. Down the line, the under-cook stared fixedly at the floor, and Maren understood suddenly that he knew something—he had seen Freya, or seen Maren, or simply sensed the shape of danger—and his silence cost him visibly.

The Inquisitor's men searched for six hours.

Maren stood in the corner of the spice room and watched them work, and made herself study their method the way she studied poison: precisely, calmly, cataloguing. They searched the steward's office first. Then the wine cellar, where they found nothing but found it slowly. Then the tasters' cabinet—Freya's cabinet—with its false back, which they discovered in under a minute and emptied with grim satisfaction, finding only charms: dozens of small silver and bone trinkets, harmless as buttons, each one tagged in Freya's neat hand with its price and its purpose. *Warming charm, 2 coppers. Sleep-ease charm, 5 coppers. Charm against bad dreams, 3 coppers.*

The lead Inquisitor read the tags aloud, one by one, and her voice grew steadily more frustrated. There was nothing here. Nothing but poverty and superstition and the inventory of a woman who sold hope at market rates.

But they weren't done. The lean woman turned to Maren, and her paper-dry voice dropped to something almost gentle.

"Taster Voss. Your quarters will be inspected. As will those of Taster Ravensworth. Standard procedure."

"Of course," Maren said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled at it.

They marched her to the servants' wing. They turned out her mattress—they cut the ticking open with knives, because thoroughness was a sacrament to these people—and Maren stood in the doorway and watched the straw spill out across her floor like entrails, and felt nothing, because the mattress was empty. Had been empty for three days. The pages had never been in her bed.

They were in the bake oven.

Not the working ovens—the great disused bake oven from the old reign, the one built into the foundation wall behind the flour store, cold for thirty years, big enough to roast an ox. No one searched a dead oven. No one even looked at it. But it was iron and brick and it had a flue, and a flue drew air, and a fire lit inside it would draw smoke up a chimney that no official eye had cause to watch.

Which was why, at three o'clock that morning—after the Inquisitors had departed empty-handed, after the kitchen had settled into uneasy sleep—Maren Voss knelt in the flour store with an armful of old invoice papers and the copied contract pages wrapped in oilcloth, feeding them one by one into the dark throat of the dead oven.

She struck the tinder. The flame caught. Orange light climbed the sooty walls, and heat breathed out at her like something waking, and she began with the innocuous things—grain invoices, wage rolls, forty years of bread accounts—building the fire hot, building the fire legitimate, a baker's fire in a baker's oven, deniable, ordinary.

Then she unwrapped the oilcloth and held the contract pages over the flames.

She read as they burned. She couldn't help it. *One soul, female child, aged six years, delivered to the Court Beneath in consideration of immunity granted unto the surviving issue of Elara Ravensworth, term perpetual, revocable only upon equivalent exchange...* The ink browned, curled, went black. Wren Ravensworth's purchase price crumbled into ash between one word and the next, and Maren felt tears sliding down her face—not grief exactly, and not fear, but the strange enormous relief of a woman who has finally set down something heavy and discovered she can stand straighter without it.

Freya had memorized the terms weeks ago. Maren had checked—made her recite them back twice, clause by clause, like scripture, like an oath. What lived in Freya's memory could not be confiscated. What lived in love could not be subpoenaed.

Let the fire have the rest.

When the last page was ash, she raked the coals apart and scattered them through the cold cinders of thirty years, indistinguishable. Then she washed her face, straightened her uniform, and walked out through the sleeping kitchen to the steward's office, where she wrote a letter of confession in her own neat hand:

*I, Maren Voss, senior taster to the Crown, do confess to the destruction of certain historical documents entrusted to the royal archives. I acted alone. No other member of the household had knowledge of my act. I make this confession freely, and I will answer for it at whatever hour you require.*

She signed it. She sealed it. She left it on the desk where the morning clerk would find it, because a lie of omission was one thing, but she would not let the under-cook or the scullery maid or anyone else swing for what she had chosen.

Her career was in that letter. Her pension, her rooms, her name—all of it, signed away in nine lines. When they read it, she would be dismissed, perhaps imprisoned, perhaps worse, given the mood of the times. She stood in the doorway a moment and let herself feel the size of it, the full weight of the life she was setting down like a coat no longer needed.

Then she went to the window at the end of the corridor, the one that faced the mountain, and looked at it for a long time. The peak stood black against the stars, and around its summit—she was almost sure, almost—the air shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stones. Like a veil. Like something preparing to be torn.

Below her, in the city, a bell tolled once: the night-market bell, the thieves' bell, the bell that meant the underworld was open for business. Somewhere down there, Freya Ravensworth was gathering her strange congregation for a journey no mortal had returned from in a hundred years.

"Go on, then," Maren said to the mountain, to the bell, to the whole stubborn machinery of gods and debts. "We'll hold the door up here."

Behind her, dawn was coming up grey over the palace rooftops. By noon, the letter would be found. By evening, she expected to be in a cell, and she found—to her lasting surprise—that the thought sat in her chest light as a feather.

Loyalty was a kind of truth. She had finally told hers.

Now she had only to survive the telling—and pray, to whatever god was listening again, that Freya reached her sister before the Inquisitors reached the rest of them.
