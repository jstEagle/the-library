## 9. The Last Dish

I had cooked my whole life for other people's survival. It seemed only right that the last dish should be mine.

The descent back to the court beneath the mountain took three days by Pip's secret ways and one hour by the mountain's own, once it had decided to admit me. The stone simply opened. That should have frightened me more than it did. I suppose when you have made your peace with dying, doors stop mattering so much.

I carried a satchel on my hip the way I had carried it through twenty years of palace kitchens: strapped tight, riding the swell of my stride, invisible because no one ever looked at the food-taster. Inside were twenty small glass vials, each stoppered with wax and labeled in Maren's precise hand. Arsenic in its white powder. Foxglove, bruised sweet as hay. Hemlock that smelled of mouse nests. The gray salts of lead. Wolfsbane, which I had survived at nineteen and never told anyone, because telling would have meant explaining why I'd drunk it.

And underneath them all, wrapped in oilcloth against the cold, the ledger page Maren had burned her career to save—Wren's contract, copied out in the old bargains' spidery script.

*One soul, juvenile, female, surrendered in full and forever, against consideration rendered: continued life of the undersigned's sister.*

My sister's name was not even written correctly. *Wrenn,* with two n's, as though whoever drafted it could not be bothered to hold a whole person in their attention for the length of six letters. I had read that error forty times on the road down, and every time it did its work in me like a slow poison of its own, which was, I supposed, appropriate.

Pip walked me as far as the last gate and then stopped, which surprised me. He had sworn he was coming all the way. His hands were jammed deep in his pockets, and his ears—the ears that heard locks whisper—were pink at the rims.

"You're going to do something stupid," he said.

"Probably."

"And you're not going to let me steal you out of it."

"No."

He kicked at a loose cobble in the under-city street, a little chip of obsidian that skittered away into the dark. "Freya. The charms you sold me. The luck ones." He wasn't looking at me. "They work now. Everything works now. And I've been thinking about what luck is, down here, where they keep the ledgers. Luck isn't free. Somebody paid for mine. Somebody always pays." He finally looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "So if somebody has to pay for yours—"

"Pip."

"—then let it be split four ways, is all I'm saying. That's just good accounting."

I kissed the top of his head, which he endured with the grace of a cat being bathed, and I pushed open the gate, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you turn around.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain had changed since I first stood before it. Or perhaps I had changed, and the change was in my eyes.

Before, it had been vast and dim and wrong—a cavern dressed as a throne room, pillars of living rock, thrones grown rather than carved, and upon them the old gods in their long bodies and their faces like weather. Before, they had been terrible.

Now the veil above us was torn, and their power poured through it like light through a cracked jar, and they were terrible the way a flood is terrible: not malicious, exactly. Simply enormous, and indifferent to what stood in the water.

They knew why I'd come. Of course they did. The moment I entered the under-city, every soul-coin in every merchant's strongbox had turned face-up toward me like flowers finding sun.

"The taster returns," said the one who sat highest, whose voice arrived inside my chest rather than my ears, resonant as a bell struck underwater. "Come to surrender your protection at last? We told you the terms. Your immunity, freely given, voids the debt. Wren goes free. You remain ours to keep—or ours to kill, as the years choose. A fair exchange."

"A fair exchange," I agreed. "For someone who trades in equivalents."

The gods stilled. All of them, all at once, the way a forest stills when something walks through it. In the silence I could hear the market below, the whisper of souls changing hands, the soft arithmetic of the ledgers.

"You have come to haggle," said another of them, lower on the left, with a voice like frost forming. "Bold. Foolish. What equivalent could you offer beyond the one we named?"

"Not an equivalent," I said. "A surplus."

I set the satchel on the floor between us and knelt beside it, unhurried, the way I knelt before the king's breakfast every morning of my working life. I unbuckled it. I laid out the vials in two rows of ten, each label facing the thrones, and I watched the old gods lean forward—lean!—with something I had never expected to see on those weather-faces.

Interest.

"Twenty years," I said. "Twenty years of poisons, survived one spoonful at a time. This is arsenic. This is foxglove. This one nearly stopped my heart at seventeen; this one blinded me for three days at twenty-two. Every one of these, I took into my body willingly, because it was my work, and because surviving was the only prayer I knew how to say." I stood. "You want my immunity. But immunity is only the record of what I've endured. So here is my counter-offer."

I lifted the last item from the satchel: a covered dish of plain palace porcelain, white as bone, and under the cover, a smell already rising—bitter and sweet and mineral, the smell of everything I had ever survived, married together in one broth.

"I brewed them all," I said. "Every poison. One dish. And I will eat it—not protected, not immune, because the moment I eat, I give you back your gift, freely returned, which satisfies half the original bargain and you know it does—and whatever is left of me afterward belongs to whatever is left of the world. No service. No servitude. Just a mortal woman, mortal again, gambling her one life on the table like everyone else down here."

Frost-voice laughed, and the sound cracked along the cavern walls. "You would die."

"Yes."

"We cannot die. We cannot know what you know. Every bargain we have ever struck was struck to purchase exactly the thing you propose to throw away." The god rose from its throne, and its shadow crossed the floor like an eclipse. "Why would we accept? What do we gain?"

"You gain the one thing your ledgers can't hold." My heart was going hard now, hard enough that I could feel it in my teeth, but my hands were steady. Twenty years of steady hands. "You gain a willing death. Not a soul dragged down here as payment—that's just collection, and collection is cheap, you have cellars full of it. I mean the act itself. The tasting. To take into yourself the thing that ends you, knowing it, choosing it, for love of someone else." I held up the dish. "You are bound by your own laws of exchange. Equivalent value or better. And there is nothing in any ledger beneath this mountain worth more than that. You know it. It's why you bought my sister instead of taking me—I was the cheap option. I was the discount. Well. The price has gone up."

The court was silent for the space of three heartbeats. Mine, I mean. I counted them. It seemed important, suddenly, to count things while I still had them.

Then the highest god spoke, very quietly, in the voice of stone remembering it was once a mountain: "She names the law truly."

"She names it," murmured another, "and stands inside it."

"Let it be weighed," said frost-voice, but there was no mockery in it now. There was hunger. Gods go hungry forever for the single meal they can never eat, and I understood, standing there with my porcelain dish, that I had not brought them a bargain at all.

I had brought them a sacrament.

* * *

The cover came off, and the smell filled the cavern like a congregation.

It should have smelled vile. Poison is ugly; I have known its every ugliness. But brewed together, simmered with the honey I'd stolen from the royal stores and the salt I'd carried from home, it smelled strangely holy—incense and old stone, the same taste that had come off the king's breakfast on the morning the prayers started working again. As though the dish had been praying too. As though every spoonful I'd ever swallowed for another person's sake had been waiting twenty years to become this.

I thought of Wren then. Not the way I usually did—a wound, a guilt, a name with two n's—but plainly, warmly, the way she actually was: seven years old, gap-toothed, teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass by the river behind the tenement. She'd traded her whole self for me without ever being asked, and no one had ever thanked her, because no one had ever told her.

"This is the thanks," I said aloud, to her, wherever souls wait. "This whole dish. Eat well, little sister."

And I ate.

The first spoonful was arsenic, and I felt my immunity leave me like a coat taken off in winter—no ceremony, just absence, just cold air on skin that had forgotten what cold was. The second was foxglove, and my heart stuttered like a bird against glass. The third was hemlock, and the edges of the world went soft and gray. I kept eating. Spoonful after spoonful after spoonful, twenty small deaths arranged in order, and somewhere past the twelfth I was kneeling, and past the fifteenth I was lying on the cold stone with the empty dish beside my head like a fallen moon, and the old gods had come down from their thrones and gathered over me, all of them, vast and close, watching me die the way starving men watch bread.

"She does not stop," one whispered, wondering.

"She never stopped," said another. "That is the whole of her."

The veil shuddered. I felt it even from the floor of the world—even through the dark coming up like water—as the torn sky above the mountain convulsed, and every ledger in the under-city opened at once, and every contract written in spidery script found the word *void* blooming across it in ink the color of dawn.

*Wren.* Somewhere above me or below me, twenty years of sold-and-sealed soul came loose like a knot finally untied, and I felt it go, felt my sister pass free through the shudder of the veil, and I smiled, because that was the last thing I had strength for, and I had saved it for her.

Then the dark took me whole, and the last taste in my mouth was incense and old stone—

—and, faintly, impossibly, grass. A blade of it. Held between a child's teeth.

Whistling.
