## 10. Xanthe and the Veil Anew

### Wren

The dead do not dream, but they remember, and I remembered everything.

I stood in the antechamber of the court beneath the mountain, where the walls were made of pressed centuries and the light came from nowhere and everywhere, and I watched my sister lie on a slab of black glass with her lips gone the color of old candlewax. She had eaten the last dish. Every poison she had ever survived, brewed into one bowl, taken willingly, smiling—smiling, if you can believe it—at the assembled faces of gods who had not been smiled at since the world was younger.

The court was in an uproar. You could hear it like weather: a low grinding of stone against stone, the sound of a law discovering it had been outmaneuvered by a mortal woman with a taster's tongue and a stubborn heart. They had asked for equivalent payment. She had paid. Not in immunity, not in service eternal, but in something they had no shelf to store: a mortal's willingness to die for someone who was already dead.

*Contract void,* said the oldest of them, in a voice like a door closing on a very long winter. *The debt is discharged.*

And then I was loose. Twenty years of being a coin in someone else's pocket, and suddenly I was only myself again—a girl of nineteen, unaged because the dead do not age, standing over my sister's failing body with no idea what to do with my hands.

There was a door in that place. There is always a door. It opened onto nothing and everything, onto whatever comes after, and I could feel it breathing warmth at my back like a kitchen in the morning. My mother might be there. My mother, who died believing both her daughters were safe.

But Freya was still breathing, barely, each breath arriving later than the one before, like guests who keep almost not coming.

The god-touched boy was already there—he moved faster than anything should move, that one—and he was doing something I didn't understand, pressing a copper coin to her palm and whispering to it, begging it. *You've brought me luck my whole rotten life,* he told it. *Spend some now. Spend all of it. I'll steal more.* And behind him came a woman in palace gray with her sleeves pushed up and her jaw set like a woman arguing with death itself, saying, *Breathe, you impossible creature, you have never once done what you're told, don't start now.*

They were hers. She had never meant to make them—I knew that about my sister, even from inside the dark—but she had made them anyway, and they had come down into the mountain for her without being asked twice.

So I did what sisters do.

I knelt beside her and I put my hand over her heart, and I gave her the blessing I had been saving since I was seven years old and she gave me half her honey-cake on my name-day and swore she'd always save me half of everything.

*Half of everything, Wren. Always.*

I left her half of everything I had left: my morning light, my stubbornness, my terrible singing voice, my certainty that the world was worth staying in. All of it poured through my palm into hers, name-day blessings stacked twenty years deep.

Then I stood, and I looked at her face—thirty-four years old and finally unafraid—and I walked toward the warm door, and I did not look back, because looking back is for people who are leaving something unfinished.

Nothing between us was unfinished. That was the whole point of her.

### Pip

Here's what nobody tells you about luck: it has a weight. When you carry enough stolen fortune in one pocket, your coat hangs crooked. Mine hung so crooked that week I walked tilted, like a man leaning into wind that wasn't there.

Freya Ravensworth lay in a rented room above Anselm's gutted shrine for nine days, and I sat with her every one of them, because that's what you do when a woman trades death for you in a hole under the world. Also because Maren made me. Mostly because Maren made me.

She'd changed since the mountain. The palace had stripped her rank—the second spoonful herself, caught burning ledgers—and instead of weeping about it she'd rolled those sleeves higher and learned to pack wounds. Her hands smelled of yarrow and beeswax now instead of courtly soap, and she held them over Freya's fevered brow like a woman laying bricks.

"She's cold," I said, on the ninth day.

"She's *alive*," said Maren, which shut me up.

The coin in my fist had grown hot as a hearthstone. I'd begged it down to its last spark, and it had spent them all—one spark per hour, keeping a small fire lit in Freya's chest while her body argued with everything she'd drunk. By the ninth day it was just a coin again, cool and ordinary, and when I flipped it out the window in disgust it landed heads-up in a beggar's cup, and I heard the man laugh for the first time all winter.

That was the morning she woke up.

Not dramatically. That was the thing. No gasp, no bolt upright. Just her eyes opening, brown and level and entirely hers, fixing on the water-stained ceiling as though it owed her money.

"Your luck," she rasped. "It's gone."

"Spent," I said. "Worth it."

"You'll be unlucky for a year."

"I've been unlucky my whole life and look how well I turned out." I grinned at her, and she tried to grin back and managed about half of one, which from Freya was a festival.

Maren burst into tears, which embarrassed everyone enormously, especially Maren, who threatened us both with violence and then hugged us both anyway, hard, her chin hooked over Freya's shoulder like she was holding a door shut against the dark.

"Twenty years," Freya whispered into the middle of it. "I spent twenty years learning not to belong anywhere. It turns out belonging was the thing keeping me alive all along."

"Obviously," said Maren, sniffling. "We could have told you that in a week, saved you a mountain."

### Anselm

I had been a priest for thirty years, disgraced for ten, and faithless for most of the time between, and so I will confess what I did on the morning the veil finished tearing: I climbed to the ruined shrine above the city and I prayed, badly, the way a man walks on a leg that hasn't healed right.

The gods were loose. That was the plain truth of it. The court beneath had voided the contract, but contracts are not the only things written in that place, and the tearing of the veil had let the old ones seep upward like water finding cracks. They were in Vell now. Small gods, hungry gods, the kind that lived in thresholds and chimney corners before the city got too respectable for them. A god of crossroads had taken up residence at the Tanner's Gate and demanded offerings of lost buttons. Something ancient and patient had settled into the cisterns and granted wishes to anyone who sang down the well. The king's astrologer resigned in tears; the king's baker reported his ovens whispering recipes.

Vell did not know what to do with living gods. Vell had outlawed sorcery precisely so it would never have to find out.

But here is what I saw from the shrine steps, and what I have decided—late, disgracefully late—to believe:

A fishwife left buttons at the Tanner's Gate, and the crossroad god untangled a lawsuit she'd been drowning in for two years. A child sang down the well about his father lost at sea, and the cistern-thing sent the man home on a raft of reeds, salt-crusted and furious but breathing. The gods of Vell, it turned out, had missed their city the way exiles miss home: fiercely, specifically, wanting only to be useful at it.

Faith was working again. Badly, dangerously, wonderfully. Like everything worth having.

I went down the hill to tell the others, and I found Freya Ravensworth standing in the night market with crates of charms at her feet and her stall rebuilt in the exact spot she'd abandoned it, and I stopped, because I understood I was watching something rarer than any miracle.

She wasn't hiding anymore.

### Freya

The stall was smaller than I remembered it being. Everything was, after the mountain—Vell itself seemed scaled down, human-sized, precious the way small things are precious when you've seen how large the dark can get.

I laid out my wares in the open, first time in twenty years without a cloth thrown casually over the top, without the practiced slouch that said *nothing here, move along*. Silver charms for safe travel. Copper ones for honest sleep. Little tin bells against nightmares, which had never once worked and now, possibly, might. I set my prices in chalk where anyone could read them. Above the stall I nailed a shingle, and my hand shook hammering it, and Maren steadied the nail with her thumb until I got it straight:

**FREYA RAVENSWORTH — CHARMS & TASTINGS. ALL WORK GUARANTEED.**

"All work guaranteed," Maren read aloud, arms crossed, wearing her exile like a new coat she hadn't decided fit yet. "Bold. What happens when one fails?"

"Refund," I said.

"And when someone dies?"

"I taste everything first." I met her eyes. "For free. Forever. That's the guarantee."

She laughed—short, sharp, real—and took up her post on the crate beside mine, because apparently disgraced tasters guard charm stalls now, and neither of us saw any reason to pretend otherwise. Pip materialized out of the crowd around dusk with three meat pies of dubious provenance and a new coin he claimed he'd found rather than lifted, and the three of us ate together as the lamps came on, and the market flowed around our little island of light.

People came. That was the strangest part, the part that kept catching in my chest like a swallowed seed. They came openly, in daylight and lamplight, and they told me what they wished for before they bought, because the wishes mattered now, because prayers had teeth again. A widow bought a bell for her doorstep. A stonemason bought safe-travel silver for his daughter going downriver. A little girl with a gap in her smile counted out coppers for a tin star and told me, gravely, that her brother was sick and the well-god wasn't listening fast enough.

I gave her the star for free. I gave her my sister's price, too, the old family rate: *half of everything, always.* Half the hope, shared between the wisher and the wished-for, so neither carries it alone. She nodded like this was standard commerce and ran off into the crowd, and I watched her go and felt something in me sit down at last, some sentry who had stood watch for twenty years finally hearing the relief whistle.

Wren was gone. Truly gone, past the warm door, past reaching. I had thought grief would be a fire. Instead it was a hearth—contained, tended, warming the whole room.

*Half of everything,* I said to the evening air, to wherever she was. *I'm keeping my half. You keep yours.*

The lamps burned. The veil overhead shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone, and behind it—if you knew how to look, and now everyone knew how to look—the old gods of Vell leaned close to their city, attentive, homesick, alive.

At the edge of the market square, a herald in royal colors mounted the fountain steps and unrolled a scroll, and the crowd hushed, and Maren's elbow found my ribs.

"That's the king's seal," she murmured. "That herald's coming here."

Pip's hand drifted toward his knife. "Trouble?"

I looked at the scroll, at the herald's face—pale, official, frightened—and I felt my own pulse go steady and slow, the way it did before the first spoonful.

"No," I said, standing, wiping my hands on my apron. "An invitation. Or a summons. With the veil torn open and the king's own kitchens whispering, I'd wager His Majesty finds himself suddenly in need of a food-taster."

The herald cleared his throat and began to read my name aloud to all of Vell.
