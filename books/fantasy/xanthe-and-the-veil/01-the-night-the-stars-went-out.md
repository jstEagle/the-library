## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mules hated the Greytooth Pass, and Odile Delacroix trusted them for it.

Animals knew things that smugglers only suspected. The lead mule, a gray-bellied brute she'd named Chancellor because he refused every order with bureaucratic stubbornness, had been pulling at his rope since moonrise, ears flat, hooves skidding on scree he'd crossed a hundred times without complaint. Behind him, the other five pressed close in a way that made the narrow trail feel crowded, their breath steaming in ragged white plumes.

"Easy," Odile murmured, though her hand on the rope was not easy at all. "Easy, you overgrown tax collectors. We're nearly through."

Above them, the pass narrowed to a throat of black stone. Below, the drop fell away into cloud — actual cloud, thick as wool, though they were climbing above where clouds had any right to live. Thornfell's high country did that. It stacked its weather like coins, layer upon layer, and a smuggler who understood the stacking could move cargo through weather that would ground any honest caravan.

That was the whole art of it, really. Honesty traveled by road. Everything worth anything traveled by weather.

Odile paused beneath an overhang furred with ice and let the mules rest, counting them by touch in the dark. Six. Good. She'd lost one to a crevasse two years back, a young mule full of more legs than sense, and she still caught herself counting to seven sometimes. Grief did that. It left a shape in your habits.

The cargo rode in oilcloth-wrapped bundles slung across the middle three animals: star-iron ingots from a forge in the lowlands that didn't ask questions, bound for a smith in Vharen's Hollow who paid triple and never haggled. Not glamorous work. Not the kind of run that got songs sung about it. But it was steady, and steady meant alive, and Odile had learned the hard way what happened to people whose lives were interesting.

She was twenty-nine years old, exiled from her own name, and she was very good at being nobody.

* * *

The trouble started as a feeling rather than a sound.

Anyone else might have missed it — a pressure change, a faint wrongness in the way the cold sat against the skin, the sensation of a held breath somewhere behind the mountains. But Odile had grown up in these passes before exile, before the disowning, before her grandmother's face went hard as ward-stone and the gates of House Delacroix closed behind her. She had been raised on storm-lore the way other children were raised on lullabies. Her body knew the sky the way a fisherman's hands know rope.

And the sky was holding its breath.

She stopped mid-stride, one boot on a shelf of blue ice, and listened.

Wind, yes. The pass always had wind; it funneled through the Greytooth like water through a spout, moaning against the rock in a note that old-timers called the Widow's Hum. But underneath the hum there was something else tonight — a thin, high tension, like the last instant before lightning, stretched out and stretched out and refusing to break.

"Chancellor," she said quietly. "What do you know that I don't?"

The mule rolled one dark eye at her and planted all four feet.

Then the wards shattered.

There was no other word for it. Odile felt it go through her like a bell struck inside her own ribs — a vast, deep concussion that made no sound at all, that the mountains themselves seemed to absorb, but that every nerve in her body registered at once. She'd touched the border wards exactly once in her life, as a child, her small palm pressed flat against the warm granite of the great ward-stone at Carrow Gate while her grandmother watched with something that wasn't quite pride. The wards had hummed then, low and constant, the heartbeat of the country itself. *Safe within, safe without,* the old rhyme went. *The Veil of Thorn keeps all about.*

Now that heartbeat stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. The silence after it was so complete, so absolute, that Odile heard her own blood moving in her ears, heard the mules' breathing, heard the creak of frozen leather — sounds that had been there all along beneath the wards' hum, masked by it, and now stood naked in a world suddenly stripped of its oldest sound.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, no."

Chancellor screamed.

It was a horrible sound, a sound she'd never heard a mule make — high and human, terror with no bottom to it — and then all six animals were plunging against their ropes at once, and Odile was thrown against the ice wall, shoulder cracking against stone, her hands full of thrashing rope. She fought them down by weight and habit, body moving on its own while her mind reeled, hauling the lead line short, pressing her forehead against Chancellor's shuddering neck, whispering nonsense the way you'd whisper to a child in a burning house.

"Hush. Hush, you fool, hush — nothing's coming, there's nothing—"

But the mules were right. That was the thing she couldn't say aloud even to herself. Animals knew things. And whatever they knew, it had arrived.

She looked up.

The stars were going out.

It began in the east, or where the east should have been — the great wheel of constellations she'd navigated by for fifteen years, the Shepherd's Bow, the Nine Sisters, the pale sweep of the Moonroad itself. They dimmed like lamps carried behind a closing door. One by one. Then ten by ten. Then all together, in a long silent cascade that swept across the whole bowl of the sky, and the darkness that came down to replace them was not the ordinary darkness of a cloudy night. Clouds had texture. Clouds had edges, and silver linings, and the promise of a moon somewhere behind them.

This was a darkness with no texture at all. A darkness like ink poured into water, spreading, erasing. The stars didn't twinkle out — they were *unmade*, wiped from the sky as though some enormous hand were rubbing them away, and the last one to go was the North Star, the Anchor, the fixed point every sailor and smuggler and lost child in the world steered by. Odile watched it gutter once, twice, like a candle in a draft —

— and vanish.

The sky was empty. Completely, perfectly, impossibly empty.

Her knees hit the trail. She didn't remember deciding to kneel. The rope had gone slack in her hands; the mules had stopped screaming and stood trembling in a huddle around her, and the silence was so total it had weight, pressing down on her shoulders like snow.

Every instinct she owned told her this was death. Not danger — danger was wolves, avalanches, excise men with crossbows. This was something older and larger, the kind of event that appeared in the oldest stories, the ones told in cellars by people who'd had too much drink and not enough sense. *The night the sky forgot us.* Children's fright-talk. Grandmother's voice, suddenly, unbidden, reciting the warding rhymes over a small girl's bed: *Safe within, safe without, the Veil of Thorn keeps all about—*

The Veil.

The word surfaced from somewhere deep, from lessons she'd been given before she was old enough to know they were lessons, from her grandmother's hands smoothing her hair. The Veil of Thorn. The border wards. The country's protection, woven into the land itself, maintained by — by whom? By the wardens. By House Delacroix. By blood.

By her blood.

"No," Odile said to the empty sky, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "No, I'm exiled. I'm nobody. Whatever this is, it isn't mine."

The sky declined to answer. The sky just hung there, blank as fresh paper, and somehow that was worse than any answer could have been.

She got up. It took three tries, which was two more than she'd ever needed in her life, and when she finally stood she found her hands were shaking and made them stop through sheer bloody-mindedness. Fifteen years of smuggling taught you one lesson above all others: panic is a luxury purchased with your life. Fear could ride along. Fear could sit in the saddle and keep watch. But fear did not hold the reins.

*Think.*

Facts. She dealt in facts, the way she dealt in star-iron — weighed, wrapped, kept dry.

Fact: the wards had broken. She'd felt it in her bones, and the mules had known it before she had, and whatever had broken was old enough and deep enough that its dying had reached the top of the Greytooth Pass.

Fact: the stars were gone. All of them. Which meant either the world had ended — in which case worrying was moot — or something had come between her and the sky. Something like a veil.

Fact: she was eleven miles, as the crow flew, from the nearest ward-stone. The Carrow Gate stone was gone, swallowed by the gorge collapse six winters past, but there was another — the old one, the true one, up the Shepherd's Stair where no honest person climbed and no dishonest one bothered, because there was nothing up there worth stealing.

Nothing worth stealing except answers.

Odile looked at her mules. Chancellor looked back, ears forward now, trembling but attentive, waiting for her to be the one who decided what the world was going to do next. That was the contract between a smuggler and her animals: she decided; they endured.

"I'm sorry," she told him sincerely. "We're not going down to Vharen's Hollow."

The smith could wait. The star-iron could wait. Contracts could be renegotiated; that was practically the definition of a contract. But the wards of Thornfell had been the one fixed thing in a life she'd built entirely on shifting ground — the borders she ran cargo along, the mountains she'd memorized, the country that had spat her out and still, in some stubborn corner of her heart, remained the only home she'd ever wanted. If the wards were dead, then everything was negotiable. Everything was up for grabs.

And if there was one thing Odile Delacroix could not abide, it was finding out what someone else was grabbing first.

* * *

She left the cargo cached under the overhang, wrapped in double oilcloth, weighted with stones, marked in the smuggler's fashion — a cairn of six rocks, five pointing downhill, one pointing nowhere at all. Any runner working the Greytooth would know it and leave it alone. Anything else that found it would find star-iron, and star-iron was the least of anyone's problems tonight.

She took the mules as far as the switchback below the Shepherd's Stair, tethered them in the lee of a boulder with feed and water for two days, and went up on foot with a coil of rope, a hooded lantern she didn't light, and a knife she'd carried since she was sixteen and had never once had to use for anything but cutting rope.

The Stair was not a stair anymore. It had been one once — her grandmother's maps showed it, cut steps zigzagging up the mountain's northern face, a pilgrim's way to the old ward-stone — but four hundred years of freeze-thaw had chewed it into suggestion. Odile climbed it by memory anyway. That was the thing no map could hold and no exile could lose: the geography written into the body. Left hand on the ribbed ledge, three counts, then the crack wide enough for a shoulder. The chimney above it, climbed facing outward, boots on the flaking edges. The traverse where the rock leaned out over a drop the clouds had eaten, where she pressed herself flat and moved like a lizard and did not look down because looking down was a courtesy she owed herself.

Halfway up, the wind died.

Not gentled. Died. The Widow's Hum cut off mid-note like a singer strangled, and the silence that followed rang louder than any sound, and Odile clung to the cold rock under an utterly black sky and felt, for the first time since childhood, genuinely and completely afraid. Not of falling. Of the quiet. Mountains were not supposed to be quiet. The world was not supposed to be quiet. Sound was how you knew the world was still paying attention to you.

She climbed faster.

The summit plateau opened around her like a held palm — a broad shelf of bare stone, wind-scoured, ringed with standing rocks that the old folk said were wardens turned to stone for failing their watch. At its center rose the ward-stone: a monolith taller than two men, black granite shot through with veins of something that had once glowed a soft, steady gold.

Once. The veins were dead now. Grey as ash, grey as bone, grey as the empty sky. And the stone itself — she laid her palm against it before she'd fully decided to, the way you touch a body to check for warmth — the stone was cold. Not winter-cold. Deeper than that. Cold the way a hearth is cold a week after the fire died, cold that has forgotten heat entirely.

"No," she whispered, and the word frosted and fell. "No, no, come on. Come on. You've held for a thousand years. Hold a little longer."

She pressed both palms flat. She pushed her will into the stone the way her grandmother had shown her, once, in the last lesson she'd ever been given — *feel for the hum, little wren, feel for the heartbeat* — reaching with everything she had, all the storm-sense that lived in her marrow, the inheritance they'd cut out of her along with her name.

And for half a heartbeat — one impossible, flickering instant — she felt it.

A residue. An echo. The ghost of the great hum, buried so deep in the stone it was less a sound than a scar, and threaded through it, faint as a voice heard through a wall, something that made her stagger backward off the monolith with her heart slamming.

Her own name.

Spoken in the spell. Woven into the wards. *Odile.* Her grandmother's voice, or the memory of it, saying her name inside the magic that had just died — as though she belonged in it. As though she had always belonged in it. As though the wards of Thornfell, the Veil, the thousand-year protection of an entire country had been knotted, somewhere in their deepest weave, around a smuggler sleeping in a cave above the Greytooth Pass.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, to the stone, to the dead sky, to whoever had put her name in a spell she'd never seen. "I was thrown out. You threw me out."

The stone said nothing. The stone was cold. But the name stayed in her ears, and the dread that had been sitting in her chest all night shifted, settled, and hardened into something with edges.

Because here was the truth she'd built her whole life on, the smuggler's creed beneath all the other creeds: *know your ground better than anyone alive.* She knew these mountains. Every pass, every chimney, every dead-drop cache and smuggler's stair from the Greytooth to the Saltfen. The excise men had spent fifteen years trying to catch her and had never once managed it, because Odile Delacroix knew Thornfell's high country the way a spider knows its web — thread by thread, vibration by vibration.

Whatever had broken the wards, whatever had put out the stars, whatever had spoken her name in a spell older than her grandmother's grandmother — it was here, in her country, on her ground. And it thought the dark would hide it. It thought the erased sky would blind the people who might come looking.

It had clearly never met a smuggler.

Odile stood at the top of the Shepherd's Stair under a sky with no stars in it, and she let the fear finish passing through her, all the way through, until what remained on the far side of it was cold and clear and ready. Resolve. The good kind — not the hot kind that gets people killed, but the kind that plans routes and caches supplies and moves at night.

Below her, far down in the black, a light was moving through the foothills toward the pass. A lantern. No — not a lantern. Too steady. Too deliberate. Someone was walking the old roads at night, under a sky with no stars, heading straight for the Greytooth like a man following a map only he could see.

Odile pulled her hood up, checked her knife, and started down.
