## 2. A Cartographer with No Country

Xanthe had been walking for eleven days when the stars went out, and three more when the cold began to feel less like weather and more like a verdict.

They had learned long ago to read the mountains the way priests read scripture — by what was absent as much as what was present. The absence of stars was a sentence Xanthe had never seen written in any atlas, and they had copied atlases. Hundreds of them. They had spent a decade in the scriptoriums of Vellichor and the map-rooms of coastal princes, inking coastlines they would never sail and mountain ranges they would never climb, and in all that time they had never once drawn a sky.

Skies were not cartography. Skies were the same everywhere.

Except now they weren't.

Xanthe crested the ridge above the high pass with their hood up and their breath crystallizing in the wool of their scarf, and stopped so abruptly that the mule behind them — a sour, gray creature named Ledger, because everything Xanthe owned had to earn its name — bumped into their back and stood there chewing at nothing, unbothered. Below, tucked into a fold of rock where the wind couldn't reach it, burned a fire. Small. Disciplined. The kind of fire built by someone who understood that light was a debt you paid to the dark.

And beside the fire sat a woman Xanthe had crossed half a country to find, though until eleven days ago, Xanthe had not known she existed.

Odile Delacroix. Smuggler. Storm-caller. Last warden of a border that no longer had a country on either side of it — if Xanthe's maps were right, which Xanthe's maps always were, even when the world insisted otherwise.

The problem, Xanthe thought, shrugging off Ledger's lead and starting down the scree, was going to be convincing her of that last part.

* * *

She heard them coming before Xanthe reached the firelight, which was the first thing in her favor. Most people didn't. Xanthe had been told more than once that they moved through a room like a rumor — present, unsettling, impossible to pin down. But this woman turned her head toward the darkness beyond the rocks and said, without raising her voice:

"You've been following my trail since the ford. If you're a bounty man, I'll tell you now the price on me expired with the border."

"Not a bounty man." Xanthe stepped into the light, hands loose and open at their sides — an old habit from years of walking into places where maps were worth killing for. "I'm a cartographer."

That got them a look. Odile Delacroix was younger than Xanthe had expected and harder than her file suggested — and Xanthe had read her file, or what remained of it, in the archive at Vellichor: three pages, water-damaged, most of the ink faded to ghost-gray, as though the paper itself had been trying to forget her. Dark hair cropped short for practicality under a hooded traveling coat. Eyes the flat silver of a storm about to decide something. A knife already resting against her thigh, casual as conversation.

"A cartographer," she repeated. "Eleven days behind me, alone, over the pass, the week every star in the sky dies. And your opening line is *cartographer*."

"It's the truest thing I own." Xanthe lowered themselves onto a stone across the fire, close enough to be polite, far enough to be respectful of the knife. "May I?" They patted the satchel at their hip.

"Depends what's in it."

"Roads."

Her eyes narrowed a fraction. "Roads."

"Roads that don't exist," Xanthe said. "Officially."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire settling and Ledger tearing indifferently at a tuft of dead grass. Then Odile leaned forward, and Xanthe watched the calculation happen behind her eyes — the same arithmetic any smuggler runs: what does this stranger want, what will it cost me, can I afford the answer.

"Show me," she said.

Xanthe unbuckled the satchel and drew out the maps.

Not one map. Eleven of them, each rolled tight in oilcloth, each drawn in Xanthe's own hand — the fine, disciplined lettering of a trained surveyor crossed here and there with corrections in a different ink, older ink, ink that had been dry for longer than Xanthe had been alive. They unrolled the first across the space between them and turned it so she could read it.

It was Thornfell.

Not the Thornfell that appeared on imperial charts after the Reconciliation — the blank space ringed with warnings, *UNSURVEYED*, *PASS IMPASSABLE*, the polite cartographic lie that meant *do not look here*. This was the other Thornfell. The real one. Every valley named. Every pass threaded with roads marked in red: the Shepherd's Stair, the Widow's Toll Road, the Nine Cold Gates, the Salt-and-Iron Way running down from the high plateau to the river country like a vein of fire drawn in a dying hand.

Odile did not touch the map. That was the second thing in her favor; smugglers touched things, needed to hold and weigh and test. She only looked, and her stillness while looking was total, the way a hawk is still.

"Where did you get these," she said. It was not a question.

"I made them." Xanthe tapped the red roads with one finger. "These lines, anyway. I copied the bones from an older source and walked what I could verify. The rest—" they hesitated, then decided, deliberately, to spend the truth like coin, "—the rest came to me. Which sounds mad. I'm aware it sounds mad."

"How?"

"That's the part you're not going to like." Xanthe kept their voice level, their hands still, because they had learned that the delivery of an unbelievable thing mattered almost as much as the thing itself. "I remember them. The roads. All of them. Roads I have never traveled, in a country I have never seen, and I remember them the way you remember your own hands. And eleven days ago, when the stars went out, I felt—" they searched for the word and hated how small all the words were, "—a pull. South. Toward the border. Toward whatever's happening inside Thornfell. Like a hook set behind my ribs."

Odile sat back. The knife hadn't moved. Neither had her face. But something behind her eyes had shifted, some internal door closing partway, and Xanthe recognized the motion because they performed it themselves a dozen times a day: the sound of a person deciding not to believe something yet, while refusing to throw it away.

"There is no inside Thornfell," she said finally. "Thornfell fell. Sixty years ago. The war took it, the empire absorbed the edges, and the center—" her jaw tightened, "—the center is a graveyard under snow. Everyone says so. Myself included. I grew up in exile hearing about a kingdom that died before I was born."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "Everyone says so. That's rather the point."

"The point."

"Sixty years ago, Thornfell didn't fall." Xanthe drew out the second map and unrolled it beside the first. This one was different — rougher, half-finished, the ink of it barely a season old. It showed the same mountains, but overlaid with hatching, shading, a veil of gray wash drawn across the whole of the interior like a cloud that refused to move. "Sixty years ago, Thornfell was *hidden*. There's a difference, and the difference has a shape. Look at the trade records. Grain shipments that arrive at border towns from nowhere, in quantities no local farm could produce. Wool with a weave pattern nobody can source. Songs in border taverns with dialect markers from a language that supposedly died with the kingdom. Refugees who walk out of the high passes with no history before the year they arrived. Someone is living in those mountains, Odile Delacroix. A whole country of someones. And every chart, every chronicle, every mind outside the line of those peaks has agreed, simultaneously and completely, to forget they exist."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with a sound like a long exhale.

"And you know this how," Odile said quietly.

"Because I'm the exception." Xanthe held her gaze. "I don't forget. Whatever the Veil is — and I've spent three years chasing its edges, and *Veil* is the best word I've found — it doesn't take. Not all the way. Not with me. I look at the official maps and I see the blank space, and underneath the blank space I see the roads. I've walked up to villages that appear on no census, traded with people whose names slide off memory like rain off oilcloth, and every time, I am the only one in the room who remembers the conversation happened." They smiled, and knew it didn't reach anything. "Do you know what it's like to be the sole witness to an entire country? To carry whole valleys in your head, knowing that when you die, they die with you? I've started drawing everything twice. Once for the world. Once for whoever comes after me. In case I'm wrong about being the exception."

Odile looked at the two maps side by side for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its edge, the voice of someone standing at the edge of a hole in the ground and listening for an echo.

"When the wards broke," she said, "eleven nights ago. You felt it."

"I felt a country scream." Xanthe said it plainly, because dressing it up would have insulted them both. "Every road inside that Veil lit up in my head at once, like a city seen from a height at night — and then, one by one, they began going dark. From the borders inward. Something is eating Thornfell, Ms. Delacroix. Slowly, from the edges, road by road. And in however long it takes to finish, everyone inside stops having ever existed at all."

Silence. Ledger snorted and stamped.

Then Odile laughed — one short, hard note with no humor in it whatsoever, the laugh of a woman who has just realized the joke is on her. "You realize how this sounds."

"Completely mad. Yes."

"You come out of the night, eleven days on my trail, selling a story about an invisible country and a magic Veil, to a woman sitting alone at the top of the world with a knife and a very bad week." She turned the first map slowly, studying the red roads, and Xanthe saw her fingers twitch with the effort of not tracing them. "If I weren't so tired, I'd have already put you over the ridge."

"If I were lying," Xanthe said, "I'd have picked a story you wanted to hear. Wealth. Revenge. Your family's stolen throne." They nodded at the maps. "Instead I brought you work. Roads. The one currency you can't counterfeit and I can't fake. Check my lines against your memory of the passes — the ones you've run cargo through. The Shepherd's Stair. The switchbacks below the Ninth Gate. If I'm a fraud, you'll know within a minute."

Something flickered across her face — the first expression Xanthe had seen there that wasn't suspicion. It looked almost like hunger.

She bent over the map. Her finger came down at last, landing on a junction where three red roads met at a notch in the ridge. "The Kettle," she said. "There's a spring there. Frozen half the year. No reason for anyone outside Thornfell to know it exists."

"Two hundred paces east of the spring there's a cairn," Xanthe said. "Older than the roads. The shepherds used it to mark the safe crossing when the melt-fog comes down. I've never been there. I know it anyway."

Her finger moved. "The Widow's Toll Road. It floods in spring. Where's the high route?"

"You don't take the high route. You take the culvert under it, the one the old kingdom cut for the wool trains. The entrance is behind a waterfall that's dried up since the war — except it hasn't dried up, has it? It's inside the Veil. It's still falling."

Odile sat back slowly. For a moment she looked less like a hawk and more like a woman standing in a house she'd thought burned down, finding the furniture exactly where she'd left it.

"That's not possible," she said, but her heart wasn't in it, and they both knew it. "No one outside knows the culvert. No one alive."

"No one *outside*," Xanthe corrected gently. "That's the entire point."

She stared into the fire. Xanthe watched her do the thing that he'd already begun to suspect she did better than anyone alive: take an unbearable fact, turn it over, find its load-bearing wall, and decide whether to burn the house down or move in.

"My grandmother used to sing songs in a dialect nobody could place," Odile said at last, so quietly the words nearly belonged to the fire. "When I asked, she'd say they were lullabies from home. But we were exiles. We didn't have a home. I thought she meant it the way refugees mean it — a country in the past tense." Her eyes lifted to Xanthe's. "What if she never used the past tense? What if, for her, it was always present?"

"Then she wasn't singing about grief," Xanthe said. "She was singing about a prison. Or a sanctuary. With a Veil like this, I've honestly stopped being sure which."

Another silence, but a different quality of it now — two silences ago had been a negotiation; this one was almost a conversation.

"Why me?" Odile asked abruptly. "Out of everyone. Why cross half a country for a smuggler with a bounty and a bad reputation?"

"Because of what happened at the ford," Xanthe said, and this time they let a little of their own exhaustion show, because lies between professionals are best delivered wrapped in truth. "Three years I've been probing the Veil's edge, and it ignored me. I was a fly against glass. But eleven nights ago, when it tore — when the wards broke — I felt it *notice* me. Reach for me. And it couldn't hold, and I think — I think it's because whatever's woven into that Veil recognizes blood. Warden blood. Delacroix blood." They spread their hands. "I can see the cage, Odile. I cannot touch it. You might be the only person alive who can."

"You want me to break into my own extinct homeland."

"I want you to help me save a country everyone else has already finished mourning. Yes."

Odile rose. She moved around the fire slowly, and Xanthe stayed seated, watching the knife-hand, watching the eyes, watching a woman approach a stranger the way she'd apparently approached everything in her life: as a door that might be trapped.

She stopped at the edge of the firelight, just close enough that Xanthe could see the exhaustion carved under the silver eyes, the three days of hard travel, the eleven days of a world ending that only two people in creation seemed to have noticed.

"Here's what I know about liars," she said. "The good ones never sell you a dream. They sell you a map. Something you can check, corner by corner, until one day you realize you've trusted them further than you meant to go." She crouched, bringing her face level with Xanthe's, and for a moment the hawk and the rumor looked at each other across six inches of cold air. "So here's my offer, cartographer. We go together. And every mile, I check your lines against the ground. The day your maps and the mountains disagree — really disagree, not just a bad bearing, a *lie* — you give me one honest answer before I leave you wherever we stand. Who sent you, and why."

"Fair," Xanthe said. "One condition of mine."

"Of course there is."

"When we find what's happening inside — whatever's eating the roads, whatever made the sky go dark — you don't decide alone what to do about it. You've got the blood. I've got the map. Neither of us is the whole instrument." Xanthe extended a hand. "Alliance. Between two people who are very good at not being believed."

Odile looked at the hand for a long moment. Then she took it, and her grip was callused and cold and strong as a winch cable, and Xanthe thought: *well. That's the easy part done.*

"One more thing," she said, not letting go. "The mule stays."

"Ledger goes where I go."

"Ledger carries my cargo or we renegotiate everything."

Xanthe sighed. "Ledger," they called, "you're hired."

The mule, characteristically, ignored them both.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, moving south along a trail only Odile could see and only Xanthe could name. Behind them the dead stars wheeled in their lightless wheel; ahead, the peaks of the true Thornfell stood black against a horizon the color of an unwritten page.

Somewhere past the third hour, Odile paused at a fork in the trail — a fork that was not on any map in the world, including Xanthe's — and frowned.

"Which way?" Xanthe asked.

She didn't answer at once. She was staring at the eastern branch, where the snow lay unbroken, and her expression was one Xanthe had worn often enough to recognize from the outside: the look of a person listening to a memory that isn't theirs.

"This road," she said slowly, "goes to a ward-stone. An old one. On the border line." Her brow furrowed. "I've never taken this trail in my life. But I know where it goes the way I know my own—"

She stopped. Xanthe waited.

"The way I know my own name," she finished, very quietly.

Xanthe unrolled a fresh sheet across their knee, and began, in careful ink, to draw the road east.
