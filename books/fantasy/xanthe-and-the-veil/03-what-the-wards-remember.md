## 3. What the Wards Remember

The ward-stone stood where Odile remembered it, which was the first wrong thing.

Everything else had gone strange since the night the stars went out. The passes she'd smuggled through for fifteen years felt subtly mislaid, like a house where someone had moved every piece of furniture two inches to the left. The cairns along the ridge trail were stacked in an order she didn't recognize. The wind came off the high snowfields carrying a smell she couldn't name — not pine, not stone-melt, not the iron tang of coming weather, but something older and more patient, like the air inside a sealed room.

But the ward-stone was exactly as it had always been.

It rose from the shoulder of the Col du Veilleur, ten feet of grey granite carved with spirals so worn by centuries of wind that they were less decoration than suggestion, less carving than memory of carving. Lichen scaled its northern face in continents of pale green. Someone — some warden dead five hundred years, probably — had cut a shallow basin into its crown, and rainwater had pooled there, dark and still as ink despite the morning's thin light.

Odile circled it twice before she let herself touch it.

She was not a cautious woman by nature. Caution was a luxury of people with something left to lose, and she'd spent most of what she had long ago — her name on Thornfell's rolls, her standing in House Delacroix, her right to walk through the palace gates at Cendrelune without guards spitting at her heels. Exile had taught her many things, but chief among them was that hesitation killed more smugglers than avalanches did. You read the mountain, you picked your line, and you went.

Still. She circled it twice.

Behind her, down at the tree line where she'd left him poking at his impossible maps, Xanthe waited. He hadn't argued when she told him to stay back. That alone made her suspicious of him. Everyone wanted something from Odile Delacroix — usually her cargo routes, occasionally her silence, once memorably her blood, though the man who'd wanted that had been drunk and stupid and had regretted it extensively — and none of them ever just *waited* when she told them to.

*He has maps of roads that don't exist,* she reminded herself, flexing her gloved fingers against the cold. *And he knew my name before I gave it. And he looked at me last night like I was a letter he'd been hired to deliver.*

The water in the basin didn't ripple when she leaned over it. That was the second wrong thing. Wind screamed across the col at this altitude; it should have skinned the pool into shivering riddles. Instead the surface lay flat as polished obsidian, and in it she saw not the white sky above but something else — darkness, and points of light that might have been stars, or might have been the holes stars leave behind.

"Right," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "You're going to tell me what happened to my sky."

She pulled off her glove and touched the stone.

---

Cold went up her arm like a current finding ground.

Not the cold of the mountain — she knew that cold intimately, respected it, had slept in it and bled in it and once dug herself out of an avalanche with her bare hands while her partner screamed below her. This was a different temperature altogether. This was the cold of deep water, of cellars, of the space between stars. It slid past her wrist, her elbow, her shoulder, and settled behind her sternum like a coin dropped into a well.

Then the world folded.

She was standing on the same col. She knew it instantly — the shape of the ridge, the lean of the cairn stones, the particular way the trail bent around the ward-stone like a river around a hip. But the light was different: golden, late-day, the light of a summer evening perhaps thirty years gone. And there were people on the col. Dozens of them. Wardens in storm-grey cloaks, their hems embroidered with lightning-work in silver thread, arranged in a great ring around the stone with their hands joined.

At the ring's head stood a woman with white hair braided into a crown and eyes the exact grey-green of glacial ice.

Odile's breath stopped in her chest. She knew those eyes. She'd spent her whole childhood being told she had them, and hating it.

*Grandmother.*

Seraphine Delacroix, Warden-Queen of Thornfell, Storm-Mother, the woman whose portrait hung in the long gallery at Cendrelune and whose disapproval had followed Odile through every room of her girlhood — Seraphine stood at the ward-stone with both hands pressed flat against the granite, and she was *weeping*.

That undid something in Odile more thoroughly than fear could have. She had never once seen her grandmother weep. She'd seen her sentence men to the wall, sign away villages in treaty, stare down ambassadors from three kingdoms without blinking. Grief, in Seraphine Delacroix, had been a rumor, never a fact.

Now it ran down her face freely, and she did not stop chanting.

The words weren't in any language Odile knew, and yet she understood them anyway, the way you understand a mother's tone before her words. They poured out of the old woman in a rhythm like surf, like a heartbeat slowing, and the other wardens took them up one by one until the whole ring sang it, and the sound wasn't sound anymore but pressure — Odile felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the wet of her eyes.

And the sky answered.

She watched, helpless as a ghost at her own funeral, as the stars began to go out. Not all at once — in order, methodically, constellation by constellation, as if a hand were snuffing candles down a very long table. Each extinguished star left behind a faint seam of silver, and the seams stitched themselves together overhead into a pattern of vast interlocking arcs, a lattice, a *veil*, and beneath it the country's borders began to glow with the same silver light, rising from the ward-stones like heat-shimmer off summer rock.

*So this is how it was done,* Odile thought, with a smuggler's cold clarity even inside the vision. *This is the night everyone forgot us.*

Then Seraphine lifted her face from the stone and spoke the closing words of the spell — the ones that would name the bargain, bind it, set it turning like a lock — and Odile heard her own name.

Her own name. Not Delacroix. Not Odile.

*Full name.* The one given to her at her christening in the storm-chapel, the one written in the family ledger in ink mixed with rainwater, the one no living person had spoken aloud in fifteen years because she'd burned her old identity the day she walked out of Cendrelune with nothing but a knife and a grudge. The middle names. The secret name. The true-name of House Delacroix's heir, woven into the spell like a thread through cloth, spoken by her grandmother's mouth thirty years before Odile was born.

"—and the keeping shall pass to the blood, and the blood shall know its burden, and the burden shall be named—"

The name rang through the vision like a bell struck in an empty cathedral. The wardens' chant faltered. Seraphine's head came up sharply, ice-eyes going wide, staring — not at the sky, not at the stone, but at the empty air where Odile stood, as if across thirty years the old woman had heard an echo of the future and turned to look at it.

Her lips moved. No sound came out. But Odile read the shape of them easily enough, having watched that mouth shape the same word over a thousand childhood disappointments:

*You.*

---

She came back to herself on her knees in the slush beside the ward-stone, hand still pressed to the granite, gasping like a landed fish. Her nose was bleeding. She wiped it on her sleeve without looking, an old habit, and stared at the basin of black water, which now rippled gently in the ordinary mountain wind as if nothing had ever been asked of it.

"Well," she said hoarsely. "That's that, then."

She sat back on her heels and let the shaking come, because she'd learned long ago that if you didn't give your body its moment of terror it would take it later at the worst possible time, mid-climb or mid-bargain. So she shook, and spat pink into the snow, and thought about her grandmother's tears.

Here was the thing Odile had built her whole life on top of, the way you build a house on a fault line and refuse to think about it: she had believed her exile was *simple*. She'd defied Seraphine publicly — refused the warden-training, mocked the rites, taken smuggling work out of pure spite — and the old queen had cast her out with a single sentence and never sent so much as a letter after. Simple. Clean. A door closed.

But the vision rearranged everything. Because Seraphine had named her in the spell. Had named her *thirty years before her birth* — no. Wait. Odile pressed her bleeding nose harder against her sleeve and forced the arithmetic straight. The spell had named the *keeping*, and the keeping passed to the blood, and her grandmother had known, must have known from the moment of her christening, exactly what Odile was. What she was *for*.

A key. She'd been born a key, raised a key, and the moment she showed signs of refusing the lock, she'd been thrown out — not in anger. In *hiding*. Somewhere in that marble heart, Seraphine Delacroix had looked at her granddaughter and seen not a girl but a vulnerability, a loose thread someone else might pull, and had cut that thread loose and let the wind take it.

Exile hadn't been punishment. It had been *protection* — or possession; with Grandmother it was hard to tell the difference — and either way it meant that every bitter year of Odile's freedom, every mile of road she'd claimed as her own, had been part of somebody else's design.

"I am nobody's design," she said to the stone, quietly, and didn't quite believe it.

The wind shifted. Down-slope now, carrying the scent of pine resin and woodsmoke and — underneath it, faint but unmistakable — the smell of Xanthe's campfire. She realized she'd been kneeling here the better part of an hour, and that he would have heard the wind change too, and that any sensible man would already be climbing toward the col to see whether the madwoman he'd attached himself to was alive.

Which meant she had perhaps ten minutes to decide what to do with what she'd learned.

She looked at the ward-stone again — really looked, the way she looked at a pass before committing to it. The worn spirals. The patient lichen. The basin of black water that had shown her a night thirty years dead. This thing had been singing her family's name into the mountains since before she was born. It knew her. It had *waited* for her.

And the words of the spell were still lodged behind her sternum like swallowed glass. She could feel their shape without understanding them — could feel that they were half a sentence, an offer awaiting acceptance, a lock awaiting a key that had finally, belatedly, come home.

*The keeping shall pass to the blood.*

She could touch the stone again. She suspected — with the flat certainty of instinct, the same instinct that had kept her alive through fourteen winters of high-pass runs — that if she touched it again and *spoke*, the stone would listen. Would answer. Would begin, patiently, terribly, to make her into whatever her grandmother had designed.

Instead Odile Delacroix got up, pulled her glove back on, and walked away from the ward-stone downhill, fast, because she was a smuggler and smugglers knew the oldest rule of the trade better than anyone:

Never stand in the open holding something valuable. Move first. Find cover. Decide later.

Xanthe met her at the tree line. He took one look at her face — the bloodied nose, the wildness she couldn't fully leash — and didn't ask what happened. He just held out his flask.

"Your sky," she said, taking it. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "It went out on purpose. My grandmother put it out. There was a bargain, and a sealing, and my name is written into the spell like a signature on a contract." She drank, grimaced at whatever rot he carried, and handed it back. "I want to know what the contract says. All of it. Before anything else."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face — recognition, maybe, or relief, or the look of a man whose worst suspicion has just been confirmed. "There's a place," he said slowly, "where it might be written down. If it survived. If we can reach it."

"Of course there is." Odie laughed, short and sharp, and turned her face up toward the blank silver seam where the stars should have been. "There's always a place. Fine. Show me yours, cartographer."

But as they struck camp, she caught herself flexing her bare right hand, feeling again the cold of the stone climbing her arm, hearing again the shape of her own name ringing off the mountains — and wondering, with the first real fear she'd felt since the night the sky went dark, whether she had touched the ward-stone at all.

Or whether the ward-stone had touched *her*.
