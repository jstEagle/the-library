## 4. The Thirteenth Turn

The warden was dying when they found her, and she was dying badly — not with the clean dignity that old soldiers are supposed to manage in stories, but sprawled in the mud of a drainage ditch beside a road that had no name on any map Xanthe had ever drawn. Her armor was Thornfell make; Xanthe knew it at once, the way you know your own handwriting. Blue-black lacquer chased with silver storm-lines, the kind that hadn't been forged in thirty years because the forges themselves had been forgotten.

She had crawled perhaps forty feet from wherever she'd fallen, and she had done it in a straight line toward the ward-stone marker at the roadside — a squat pillar worn smooth by centuries of travelers' hands, except that now it stood in a field of grass that grew through its cracks like the stone had lost an argument. The grass did not bend around the marker. It grew *over* it, indifferent, and something about that small disrespect made Xanthe's throat tighten worse than the blood did.

"Odile," he said quietly. "Here."

Odile Delacroix came down the embankment the way she did everything — fast, low, already looking for exits. She crouched at the edge of the ditch, and Xanthe watched her take inventory the way a smuggler does: the wound first (a long tear across the belly, bound with a strip of cloak gone dark), then the weapons (a short sword still sheathed, a knife missing), then the face.

The face was the thing that stopped her. He saw it stop her.

"I know you," Odile said slowly. "You're — you were stationed at the Coldspring relay. You used to trade my grandmother's guards salted plums for gossip about the lowland courts. Sergeant—"

"Brisa." The woman smiled, and there was too much blood in her mouth for the smile to be anything but terrible. "Little Odi. You got tall."

"You can't be here." Odile's voice went strange, flat in the way Xanthe had learned meant she was holding something down with both hands. "Coldspring is inside the Veil. Inside Thornfell. Nobody comes out."

"Nobody comes out," Brisa agreed. Her eyes slid to Xanthe, and there was an assessment in them he recognized from every border town of his childhood: *cartographer. Useful. Careful.* "But things come out when the walls start forgetting how to hold them."

* * *

They carried her up to the ruins of a waystation — three walls and a hearth, roof long gone, but out of the wet. Odile built the fire while Xanthe fetched water, and neither of them spoke until Brisa had drunk and the shivering had settled into something steadier, which was not the same as better. They both knew it wasn't better. Xanthe had seen enough gut wounds on enough bad roads to know the arithmetic of this one, and he suspected Odile had too, though she knelt by the fire with her back very straight, as if posture could argue with it.

"How long have you been out here?" Odile asked finally. "Outside the Veil?"

Brisa laughed, which cost her; Xanthe watched her pay for it. "That's the question, isn't it. That's the whole rotten question." She turned her head against the rolled cloak they'd given her and looked up through the missing roof at the grey sky. No stars tonight yet — too early, though Xanthe had learned in the past days to check anyway, compulsively, the way a man checks a wound. "I walked out of the pass at Coldspring eleven days ago. I remember it. Clear morning, frost on the stones, I had orders to scout the lowland road because Captain Verel thought the wards were flickering." Her mouth twisted. "Except when I got down here, the lowland road didn't lead anywhere. It led to a valley that's been a lake for two hundred years. And the people at the ferry village looked at me like I was a ghost, and their priest told me Thornfell fell off the maps before his grandfather's grandfather was born, and when I showed him my commission—" she fumbled at her collar, produced a folded scrap of heavy paper, wax seal cracked, "—he read it aloud and then asked me why I'd written it myself, since the ink was fresh."

Xanthe took the commission when she held it out. He read it twice. He knew what it was — he'd spent his life around documents like it, the real ones and the forged ones — and the seal was right, the hand was right, the phrasing was right down to the formulaic blessing of the Storm-Throne. And the paper was new. Newer than it had any right to be.

"The ink dries," he said quietly. "When the Veil takes hold of a thing. It makes everything inside seem... recent. Unfinished. Like a draft."

Brisa looked at him with sudden sharp attention. "And who are you, that you know what the Veil makes things seem?"

"A cartographer," Xanthe said. "With no country."

Something passed between them then, brief and cold and honest, and Brisa nodded as if he'd answered a different question entirely.

* * *

Night came down, and with it the stars — or rather, with it the absence of them, that wrongness Odile had described on the first night, that hole in the sky over where Thornfell should be. But here, this far out, some stars remained. A scatter of them, dim and few, like coins left in an emptied purse. Xanthe found himself counting them out of habit, the way he counted everything — milestones, river-fords, lies.

"There were more last night," Odile said. She had come to sit near him at the broken wall, leaving Brisa dozing fitfully by the fire. Her voice was low. "The night the wards broke. There were almost none. Now look."

"They're coming back outside the Veil," Xanthe said. "Which means whatever swallowed them is still localized. Still contained. For now." He hesitated. "Or it means the Veil is finished swallowing and has started digesting."

"That's a horrible thing to say."

"I'm a cartographer. We're trained to say horrible things calmly. It's in the apprenticeship oath."

It earned him half a smile, quickly gone. Below them, Brisa coughed — a deep, wet sound — and called out, "Cartographer. Get over here. If I'm going to haunt anyone, I want it to be someone literate."

* * *

By the fire, Brisa made them both sit close, because speaking had begun to cost her breath and she refused to spend it twice. Up close, in the firelight, Xanthe could see how old she actually was — older than her voice, older than her bearing. Sixty at least. Wardens aged strangely, he'd heard; the border work marked them.

"Verel sent me out because the wards were flickering," she said. "Flickering, understand — not failing. A ward doesn't flicker unless something is renegotiating with it. So I went out, and I got lost in a world that's forgotten us, and I spent — gods, I don't even know. Days? Weeks? Time runs differently on either side of a Veil, everyone knows that, it's the first lesson and nobody believes it." She gripped Odile's wrist suddenly, with a strength that startled them both. "Listen to me, little Odi. Listen, because I've had eleven days to put this together and I don't have eleven minutes more.

"The moon. Count the moon."

Odile frowned. "What?"

"Count it. Tonight, tomorrow night, every night. It will be wrong. It will be wrong in a way only wardens and astronomers and madmen will notice, at first. The Veil doesn't just hide Thornfell — it holds Thornfell *outside*. Outside the turning of things. Seasons, tides, the slow precession of the heavens — we're set apart from all of it, tucked between the twelves." Her breath hitched; she rode through it. "There are twelve turns to the great cycle. Everyone knows twelve. Thirteen is the door that shouldn't open, the month that isn't on any calendar, the stair that isn't on any staircase. When the Veil sealed us, we were pushed out of the count. Do you understand? We don't turn with the world anymore. We've been standing still while everything else goes around."

"And now?" Xanthe asked, though his stomach had already begun to know the answer. He was a mapmaker. He understood boundaries, and he understood what happened to things that crossed them without permission.

"Now the bargain is failing," Brisa said simply. "Whoever struck it, whatever it cost, the terms are coming due, and there's no one left inside who remembers how to pay. The Veil won't just fail, cartographer — it will *harden*. A soft Veil forgets. A hard Veil erases. Every soul inside Thornfell — every farmwife, every child, every warden keeping watch on walls that no longer exist anywhere but inside — will be smoothed out of the world like a name written in fog. Not dead. Dead would be merciful. *Unwritten.* As if the mountains had always been empty. As if the valleys had never held so much as a shepherd's song."

Silence, except for the fire.

Odile's face, Xanthe thought, was the face of someone watching a house burn from across a river — too far to help, close enough to feel the heat. "How long?" she said. "How long until it hardens?"

Brisa smiled again, that bloody, patient smile. "Until the moon completes its thirteenth turn. It started the night the wards shattered — the night you saw the stars go out, if the look on your face is anything to go by. One turn of the false moon, out-of-count, outside the calendar. Then the Veil closes forever, and Thornfell becomes a story that was never told." She let her head fall back. "Twelve nights, little Odi. Give or take. I'd have made it thirteen, but I'm bleeding on the schedule."

* * *

Later — much later, after Odile had cleaned the wound and Brisa had refused, with surprising gentleness, the poppy resin that would have made the end easier — Xanthe sat apart with his satchel open on his knees, not reading, not drawing. Just sitting with the weight of it.

He had grown up in the lowlands, in a country that bordered a country that didn't exist. Every child learned it as a game: point north-northeast, past the Greywater, and ask what's there, and the answer was *nothing*, and everyone giggled because obviously nothing wasn't a thing that could be somewhere. Mountains without names. Roads without ends. His own master, old Sarelle, had once gotten drunk enough to mutter that the survey guilds kept blank pages in every atlas of the region, whole quires of them, and no one remembered why, and no one dared remove them.

Xanthe had built his whole strange career on those blank pages. He mapped the roads that officially weren't there — the smugglers' paths, the exile trails, the ghost-roads that skirted the edge of the nothing and somehow always bent around it, as if the land itself were politely declining to discuss the subject. He'd told himself it was commerce. Craft. Curiosity. He had never once, in all his years, admitted to himself that what he was really doing was pressing his ear against the wall of a prison and listening for voices.

Now he knew why the blank pages existed. Somewhere behind them, a sergeant named Brisa was trading salted plums for gossip, and children were growing tall, and none of it had ever been allowed to touch the world.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said, dropping down beside him. "Where you stare at nothing and your face does arithmetic."

"It's what I'm for." He didn't look up. "Thirteen turns. Twelve nights. Odile, do you know what happens to a country that's been unwritten? The people inside might survive the hardening — physically. The Veil would keep them. Feed them, even, in its way. But they'd never come out. Never be remembered. Their own grandchildren beyond the border wouldn't know they'd existed. It's not death. It's—" he searched for it and hated what he found, "—it's being a lie I would draw on a map. A blank page. Forever."

"I know."

"She's going to die tonight."

"I know that too."

Odile was quiet a moment. The firelight moved on her face, and Xanthe noticed — not for the first time — how tired she looked, and how she wore the tiredness like a uniform rather than admitting it was hers. "She called me little Odi," Odile said at last. "I haven't been anyone's little Odi since I was nine years old. My grandmother sent me away with a new name and instructions to forget, and I was so good at obeying that I forgot how good I was." Her jaw tightened. "And now a woman I half-remember crawls out of a country that doesn't exist to tell me it has twelve days left, and I'm supposed to feel something about a homeland that disowned me, and the worst part — the worst part, Xanthe — is that I do."

"I know," he said.

"Do you?" She turned to look at him then, and there was the old suspicion in it, the wariness of a woman who'd been lied to by professionals. "You knew what the Veil does to ink. You know things about the Veil that a border-cartographer shouldn't. Who *are* you, really, Xanthe? Before I decide whether to spend these twelve days trusting you with them."

It was, he reflected, a fair question, and one he had been carefully not answering since the night he'd walked into her camp with maps of impossible roads. He could give her the true surface: an orphan of the survey guilds, a maker of unofficial atlases, a man whose loyalty was for sale to whoever paid in truths instead of coin. All of it accurate. None of it sufficient.

"I'm a man who draws roads that don't exist," he said finally, "because someone has to remember them, or they stop existing entirely. That's the whole of it. That's everything. Ask me again in twelve days and maybe I'll have a better answer."

Above them, past the ruined roof, the few surviving stars burned thin and stubborn. And below, by the fire, Brisa began — very softly, in a voice fraying at every edge — to sing. An old marching song of the Thornfell wardens, the kind soldiers sing on the last night of a posting, all about the mountains keeping faith and the storms remembering names. Xanthe didn't know the words. He found, to his surprise, that his eyes were burning anyway.

Odile rose and went to her. Xanthe stayed where he was and did the only thing he knew how to do with grief: he opened his satchel, took out a fresh sheet, and began to draw. Not a map. Not yet. Just the shape of the sky as it was tonight — the scattered stars, the wrongness where the rest should be — and beneath it, in his careful cartographer's hand:

*Night one.*

The clock had started. Twelve turns of a moon that shouldn't exist, and then the page would go blank forever — unless, somewhere between here and the heart of the hidden kingdom, two liars and a dying woman's last instructions could find the seam in the world and pick it open.

Near dawn, Brisa pressed a token into Odile's palm — a warden's signet, storm-sigil worn nearly smooth — and whispered directions to a place Xanthe had never mapped, in mountains that had no names, where a warden-stone still remembered everything.

Then she closed her eyes with the particular finality of someone who has finished delivering a message, and did not open them again.
