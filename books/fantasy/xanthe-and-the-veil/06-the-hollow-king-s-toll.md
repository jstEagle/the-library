## 6. The Hollow King's Toll

The village announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet wool and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. Xanthe smelled it first from the ridge above the valley, where the road bent down through a stand of black pines, and he stopped so abruptly that Odile nearly walked into his back.

"What is it?" she said.

He didn't answer at once. Below them, the village sat cupped in the valley's green palm like a coin someone had dropped and forgotten: slate roofs, crooked chimneys, a mill wheel turning in a river the color of old pewter. It should have been ordinary. Every detail of it was ordinary. That was precisely what was wrong with it.

"I know this place," Xanthe said slowly. "Or I should."

"You should?"

"It's on none of my maps." He said it the way another man might confess to a crime. "And I mean that literally. I've mapped every valley between the Serpent's Teeth and the coast. I've drawn this one. I remember drawing it." He frowned, digging through memory as through a satchel for a chart he knew he'd folded there. "There was a mill. There were two bridges. There was a shrine to some minor river-god whose name I've forgotten, which happens, names go, but—" He stopped. His hand had drifted, without his noticing, to the leather tube of charts slung across his back, and gripped it.

Odile watched him with the flat, patient attention she gave to weather. "Say what you're thinking."

"I'm thinking that I drew this valley eleven years ago, and when I try to recall what I wrote in the margin — the notes cartographers leave, elevations, hazards, the little marginalia of a life spent walking roads — there's nothing. Not faded. *Nothing.* A hole shaped exactly like whatever used to be there." He looked down at the village again. The mill wheel turned. Smoke rose. Somewhere below, faintly, people were singing a work song with the rhythm worn smooth as river stone. "Someone has been eating the margins."

* * *

They went down anyway, because the pass behind them had closed with snow and the only other road was three days' climb over scree that would kill the mule, and because Odile Delacroix did not turn back from places that frightened her; she walked into them with her chin up and her hand near her knife, as if daring the world to make the first move.

The village was warmer than it had any right to be. Lamps burned in windows though the light was still good. Bread baked. Children chased a dog between the well and the shrine — and there was the shrine, small and moss-eaten, dedicated to a river-god whose statue's face had been worn blank not by centuries of rain but by something more recent and more deliberate. The features hadn't eroded. They had been *sanded away*, smoothly, recently enough that fresh stone dust still clung in the folds of the god's robe.

"Look at that," Odile murmured, touching the blank face. "Somebody didn't want him remembered."

"Somebody doesn't want anything remembered here," Xanthe said. "Watch."

He proved it in the way he always proved things, which was recklessly and immediately: he stopped the first adult they met, a broad-shouldered woman stacking firewood by her door, and asked her the name of her village.

She smiled at him with genuine warmth. "Why, it's—"

Her mouth stayed open. Her brow creased. For three full seconds the word hung somewhere behind her eyes, visible as a fish under ice, and then her smile returned, untroubled, and she said, "It's home, traveler. Can I help you with directions? You look lost."

"We're passing through," Odile said smoothly, before Xanthe could push further. She steered him on by the elbow, and only when they were out of earshot did she mutter, "Are you trying to get us fed to whatever lives here?"

"I'm trying to understand it."

"Well, understand it quietly." But her mouth twitched at the corner, and he recognized the expression — the one she wore when his recklessness amused her and she'd sooner swallow glass than say so. He had been cataloguing her expressions since the ward-stone, since the night she'd touched the dead magic and heard her own name spoken in a dead woman's spell, and had come back to him changed in ways she refused to discuss. He kept the catalogue private. Some things you mapped and never showed anyone.

They found lodging above the village's single tavern, a low-beamed room smelling of tallow and rosemary. From the window, Xanthe could see the whole valley laid out like an illustration, and he took out his journal and began, out of pure professional compulsion, to sketch it: the mill, the two bridges, the crooked line of roofs.

His pen hesitated over the shrine.

He could not remember the god's name. That was expected — he'd never known it. What was not expected was that when he tried to write *shrine, river-god, face defaced*, his hand simply would not form the letters. He wrote *shr*. His pen lifted. He wrote *ine* beside it, in a different hand-feeling, as if the word were being assembled by a stranger. The sentence refused coherence the way a dream refuses logic upon waking.

"Xanthe." Odile stood in the doorway with her arms crossed. "The innkeeper wants to see us. Both of us. Downstairs. Now."

"He say why?"

"He said the King requests our toll."

* * *

The Hollow King held court in the tavern's back room, which turned out to be much larger inside than the building outside could possibly contain — a trick of space Xanthe had encountered twice before in his wandering years, both times in places he afterward could not find again on any map. The room was lit by candles burning with flames that leaned toward the King no matter where they stood, like courtiers bowing.

The Hollow King himself was a lean man of indeterminate age sitting on a plain oak chair that had been painted gold so many times its details were buried. He wore a crown of iron wire strung with small glass bottles, each holding a curl of pale mist, and his face was pleasant and forgettable in the way of a man designed to be overlooked. His hands, resting on his knees, were ink-stained to the second knuckle.

"Travelers," he said, and his voice was kind. That was the worst part, Xanthe thought later. The kindness. "Welcome to my valley. You'll want to be on your way, I expect, and I want nothing but to speed you along. But roads cost, and mine cost more than most. My toll is memory. A fair price. Memories are the only currency everyone carries and no one can counterfeit."

"We don't pay," Odile said.

"Everyone pays." The King spread his ink-stained hands. "You've already paid, in fact — a little, walking in. The road takes its tithe. Ask your friend there about the shrine." His eyes, mild and gray, settled on Xanthe with terrible gentleness. "He tried to write about it tonight. He found the words wouldn't come. That was me. A taste, to show you I deal honestly."

Xanthe felt Odile go very still beside him — the stillness of a drawn bow.

"The village," the King continued, conversational, almost weary. "Do you wonder about it? They were refugees once, all of them. Fled a war that ate their country whole — you've seen the edge of that war yourselves, or you will. They came here with nothing but what they carried in their heads, and what they carried hurt too much to bear. So I made them a kindness. I take the memories that bleed. Their dead children's faces. Their burning streets. In exchange they keep the valley, the mill, the song. They're happy. Look at them — genuinely happy. Is that so monstrous?"

"And the rest?" Xanthe asked quietly. "The travelers who pass through. Do they bleed too?"

The King smiled. "Everyone bleeds, cartographer. Most just don't know where."

"The toll," Odile said. "Name it."

"For you two? Oh, I'll be generous. One memory each. Something small. The color of a room you loved. A meal. A stranger's face you'll never see again." He tilted his head, and the glass bottles clinked softly against the iron wire. "Small things. Small prices. And you walk out lighter, into a world that's forgetting Thornfell anyway. What's one more loss?"

Xanthe heard the trap in it, the way he heard errors in a chart — not by examining each line, but by feeling the whole shape go wrong. The King wanted something small from Odile. Something forgettable. Which meant the King knew there was something about her that was *not* forgettable, something worth taxing specifically, something that if the King took it — or learned its shape by taking things around it — could be sold or used or broken.

Because that was the truth beneath the kindness: the King collected. The mist in those bottles wasn't only consolation for grieving refugees. Memories were currency, and currency bought power, and somewhere in this valley's margins — the margins being eaten — there were buyers.

"No," Xanthe said.

Odile glanced at him.

"Not her," he said. "I'll pay for us both."

"That's not how tolls work—"

"It is now." He stepped forward, putting himself slightly between Odile and the chair, an old instinct from older roads. "One memory. Mine. Chosen by me. And in exchange, we pass through untouched — both of us, our heads untouched, our dreams untouched, the road's tithe waived. All of it, paid in full, by me. Those are my terms, Your Majesty."

The Hollow King studied him for a long moment. The candle flames leaned toward him like an audience waiting on a verdict.

"A single memory," the King said, "to cover two travelers and waive the road's tithe? That's steep pricing, friend. You'd need to offer something substantial. Something *dense*." His voice softened, became almost seductive. "A childhood, perhaps."

The words dropped into the room and lay there.

"Xanthe." Odile's voice, low. He had never heard that particular note in it before — alarm wearing anger's clothes. "Don't."

"They can't take what matters most," he said, not looking at her, looking at the King. "That's the rule everywhere, even here. Especially here. So I'll choose first, and I choose freely, and you'll take exactly what I give and nothing else." He swallowed. His mouth had gone dry as old vellum. "My childhood. All of it. Before I was fourteen. The house, the harbor town, my mother's face, the year of the fever, learning letters, all of it. Take the lot."

"You don't have to do this," Odile said. "I can pay. Whatever it is I'm hiding, I can protect it myself—"

"That's rather the point," Xanthe said gently, and finally looked at her. "I know what your secret weighs, Odile. I've carried maps of heavier things. Let me carry this."

For a moment neither moved. The candle flames bowed and swayed. Somewhere beyond the impossible walls of the impossible room, the village sang its work song, its rhythm worn smooth, its words about nothing at all.

Then Odile said, very quietly, "You'll forget your mother's face."

"I've already forgotten the river-god's," he said. "One more blank face among the shrines."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He turned back to the King. "Well? Do we have terms?"

* * *

The taking was painless, which was somehow worse than pain would have been.

The Hollow King rose from his gilded chair and came to stand before Xanthe, and up close his mild gray eyes were full of something that might have been pity or might have been appetite, and with them indistinguishable there was no telling the difference. He raised one ink-stained hand and pressed two fingers, lightly, to Xanthe's temple.

"Breathe out," the King said, "and let it go."

Xanthe breathed out.

It did not feel like losing. That was the obscenity of it. It felt like setting down a pack after a long day's walk — the sudden lightness, the loosened shoulders, the sweet ache of relief. The harbor town dissolved like sugar in warm water. His mother's face went last, and it went smiling, and he was grateful for that without knowing what he was grateful for, because gratitude needs no object.

When it was done, he swayed. Odile caught his arm. Her grip was fierce.

"What did you lose?" she demanded. "Tell me what you lost."

He searched himself, the way he'd search a satchel. He found his maps — all of them, thank every open sky, his craft intact, his art intact, the roads he'd walked since fourteen all bright and present. He found Odile herself: the ward-stone, the mountains, the way she walked into frightening places chin-first. He found a gap where something heavy had been, a hole shaped like a childhood, edges clean as a cut page.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Something before I was fourteen. It doesn't hurt. That's the strange part. It just feels... lighter."

Odile's jaw was tight. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "You absolute fool," she said, with such raw tenderness underneath the insult that the Hollow King tactfully turned away, pretending to adjust his crown of bottles.

"Terms are met," the King said to the wall. "You'll find the road open ahead. The snow's stopped. I'd travel tonight, while the traveling's good."

* * *

But at the door of the impossible room, the Hollow King spoke once more, soft as a secret, and only Xanthe heard it.

"She'll ask what you gave," the King murmured. "She'll ask a hundred times, and hate herself for asking, and you'll never be able to tell her. That's the cruelty of my toll, cartographer — not the loss, but the *unknowing*. So let me sell you one thing back. Not free — nothing's free — but cheap. A single bottle." He touched the crown at his brow, and one small glass vessel, holding a curl of mist no bigger than a moth, glowed faintly amber. "Your mother's name. Just the name. Enough to whisper to yourself on bad nights. Cost you one true thing you remember about the woman standing next to you."

Xanthe looked at the bottle. He looked at Odile, waiting by the door, watching them both with narrowed eyes.

"No," he said.

The King raised an eyebrow.

"Keep the name," Xanthe said. "Keep all of it. Because the day I start trading pieces of her to buy back pieces of me is the day I stop deserving either." He smiled, tired and steady. "Goodbye, Majesty. Enjoy your kingdom of holes."

And he walked out into the snow-stopped night with Odile's hand hard around his arm and the valley lights shrinking behind them, lighter than he'd ever been, missing something he couldn't name, carrying everything that mattered.

It was three miles up the pass, under a sky still black and starless, before Odile finally said what she'd been swallowing the whole way:

"There's a village ahead. The map shows a warden garrison. Or it did." Her voice was rough. "Xanthe — the map shows a dying man who says he knows how long we have."

"How long until what?"

"Until everything inside this Veil stops existing." She looked back down at the valley, where the Hollow King's candles still burned, leaning toward their king. "Three days' ride. We need to move faster than we've been moving."

Behind them, far below, the mill wheel turned in the dark, grinding grain no one would remember harvesting, for bread no one would remember baking, in a village that had traded its grief for its happiness and called the bargain mercy.

Xanthe touched the empty place behind his eye, found nothing, and walked on.
