## 7. Storms Without Thunder

The storm had been building all afternoon, and Odile Delacroix had been building with it.

She could feel it the way she'd felt weather since before she could read—pressure gathering behind her breastbone, a low electric hum in the roots of her teeth, the particular weight of clouds heavy with rain not yet fallen. It was an old companionship, older than her exile, older than the smuggling, older even than the name Delacroix. The sky spoke first to her and then to everyone else, and for twenty-nine years she had answered it, and it had answered back.

So when the thunderhead came rolling over the shoulder of Mount Vess like a great grey beast scenting blood, she did what she had always done. She climbed the highest rock she could find above the valley where they'd made camp, planted her bare feet against cold stone, and opened herself to it.

"Come on, then," she murmured, spreading her arms. "Come home."

Nothing happened.

She tried again, reaching deeper this time, into the place beneath her ribs where the storm always lived—the reservoir of gathered lightning she carried like coin sewn into a coat's lining. She pulled at it the way she'd pulled at it ten thousand times: the summoning gesture of the Thornfell wardens, palm open, fingers spread, the old words riding under her breath like a second heartbeat.

*Veni. Veni. Veni.*

The clouds boiled overhead. The wind picked up, tugging her hair, pressing her shirt flat against her back. Everything about the moment said *yes*—the air said yes, the pressure said yes, the whole bruise-colored sky leaned down toward her like a lover bending close.

And then, at the last possible instant, at the point where the summons should have crested and broken—

Nothing.

The storm passed over her like a stranger passing a beggar without meeting his eyes. The wind died. The pressure in her chest simply—unraveled, thread by thread by thread, until she was standing on a rock under a darkening sky with her arms out like a fool and no storm inside her at all.

Odile stood there for a long moment, listening to the silence where the thunder should have been.

Then she screamed at the sky.

It wasn't dignified. It wasn't the controlled fury of a warden or the cold anger of a smuggler double-crossed. It was raw and wordless and it tore something in her throat, and she kept screaming until her voice gave out, and the storm rolled on toward the eastern peaks without so much as a flicker of acknowledgment, and Xanthe stood at the bottom of the rocks watching her with an expression she couldn't bear to look at.

* * *

"You knew," she said later, when they were back in camp and she had gotten her voice working again, if only barely. She sat wrapped in her cloak by the fire Xanthe had built, because apparently one of them still had enough magic left to manage a handful of dry tinder. "You knew it would be like this."

"I suspected." Xanthe fed a stick to the flames without looking up. In the firelight their face was all planes and shadow, that cartographer's calm worn like armor. "Your power comes from the land, Odile. That's what a storm-warden is—not a person who commands storms, but a person the storms agree to obey. And the land you're standing on right now..."

"—has been erased from the memory of the world," Odile finished bitterly. "Yes. I've heard the lecture."

"Then you understand."

"What I understand," Odile said, rising, "is that I have spent my entire life being told I am too dangerous, too wild, too much my grandmother's granddaughter—and now that I finally come home, the home won't have me either." She kicked a stone into the darkness. It clattered away down the slope. "Exiled twice. Once by my family, once by the sky itself."

Xanthe said nothing. They were good at that, she was learning—saying nothing at precisely the moments when most people would fill the silence with comfort or lies. It was one of the things she trusted about them, though she would have cut out her tongue before admitting it.

"The storms here are wrong," she said finally, quieter.

"How do you mean?"

"They're silent." She gestured at the east, where lightning flickered inside the cloud-belly, soundless as fish swimming behind glass. "I've been watching them all evening. Lightning, and no thunder. Rain falling and making no sound when it lands. Do you hear it? Nothing. It's like—" She stopped, hunting for the words. "It's like watching someone talk through a window. All the shape of a storm and none of its voice."

Xanthe looked up sharply. "That's not normal, even for inside the Veil?"

"Thunder doesn't care about borders. Thunder is the oldest law there is." Odile crouched by the fire, staring into it. "Unless the Veil took more than our stars. Unless it took the country's voice too."

They sat with that a while. Somewhere in the dark, the soundless lightning stitched and unstitched itself across the sky.

"Get some sleep," Xanthe said at last. "Tomorrow we make the high pass before noon, if your map-memory holds."

"It holds." She lay back on her bedroll and stared up at the starless ceiling of the Veil, and thought about how it had felt, that moment of unraveling—like a door closing gently in her face. Like family.

Sleep did not come easily, and when it came, it dreamed of storms.

* * *

They made the pass by midmorning, and the storm found them there.

Not the same storm—a new one, born somewhere over the western icefields and driven hard along the ridge line, the kind of sudden mountain violence that killed travelers every season and that Odile, in another life, in another country, would have simply... spoken to. Turned aside. Calmed. A warden's simplest duty, the first thing they taught the children: *the storm is not your enemy, it is your neighbor, and neighbors talk.*

It bore down on them while they were still on the exposed saddle, wind screaming—no, not screaming, Odile realized, *howling silently*, the gale tearing at them without any voice at all, which was somehow worse than any roar—and hail began to fall like thrown gravel.

"Down!" Xanthe grabbed her arm, hauling her toward a shelf of rock. "There—cave—"

"I can stop it." The words came out of her before thought, pure reflex. "Give me a minute and I can turn it—"

She reached for the storm.

She reached with everything she had—with the technique drilled into her at seven years old, with the desperate strength of a woman who had called down lightning over the Ashwind border to cover a smuggling run, who had once held a hurricane off the fishing fleet at Greywater for six hours on nothing but stubbornness and borrowed thunder.

And the storm ignored her. Completely. Utterly. As if she were a mouse squeaking at an avalanche.

It wasn't refusal this time. Refusal she could almost respect—it would mean the storm had heard her and chosen otherwise. This was different. This was as though her voice made no sound at all, as though the storm's attention slid past her like water off oiled leather. She stood in the teeth of the gale with her arms raised and her lips shaping the old words and she might as well have been praying to a god who'd never existed.

The hail drove them into the cave mouth half-blind. Xanthe dragged her inside; she let them, because her legs weren't entirely hers just then. They huddled against the stone while the silent tempest raged past the entrance, and Odile pressed her forehead to the freezing rock and hated.

Hated the Veil. Hated her grandmother, dead these ten years, whose bargain had done whatever this was. Hated the family who'd shipped her over the border with a false name and a purse of coin and instructions never to return. Hated herself, most of all, for ever believing she could come back and pick up a inheritance that had clearly been buried with the stars.

"I'm sorry," Xanthe said. "I know what it costs you."

"No, you don't." Her voice came out flat and dead, and she didn't try to soften it. "You've lost maps. I've lost myself. There's a difference."

Even as she said it, she knew it was cruel and mostly untrue—she'd seen what the Hollow King's toll had taken from Xanthe, seen them wake in the night reaching for a childhood that had a hole in it shaped like a girl with red hair—but grief has never been fair, and hers was coming up out of her like floodwater through a broken dam.

But Xanthe only nodded slowly, accepting the blow, and turned to watch the storm rage past the cave mouth.

And Odile, following their gaze, finally saw what she should have seen two nights ago on the mountainside.

The storm was *waiting.*

There was no other word for it. The tempest outside the cave was violent, yes—hail and wind and writhing lightning—but it was violence without direction, fury without appetite. It hammered the cliff face below their shelter and yet never struck the cave itself. It circled the peak in long, slow spirals, like a wolf pacing the edge of a clearing. And the lightning—that soundless, voiceless lightning—flashed and flashed and flashed, and every single bolt fell within a hundred yards of where she sat, and not one closer.

She got to her feet. Xanthe glanced up. "Odile?"

"Shut up a moment."

She moved to the cave mouth, ignoring the stinging hail, and stood at the threshold with the storm roaring its silent roar all around her. Close now. So close. She could feel the pressure of it against her skin like a hand hovering an inch from her cheek, wanting to touch, not touching.

*Why?*

She closed her eyes and stopped trying to command. Instead she did the thing her grandmother had shown her only once, at the very end, the thing the wardens' discipline had trained out of her because it was dangerous and uncontrolled and true: she stopped speaking and started *listening.*

She opened every gate in herself. She dropped the walls between her and the world and let the storm's presence pour against her senses like surf against a seawall.

And what she heard—or didn't hear, exactly, but felt, the way you feel a word forming on the tongue of someone about to speak—was this:

The storm was trying to say her name.

Not commanding. Not refusing. *Straining.* The whole vast engine of it, every updraft and charge and turning column of cloud, was bent around one effort, the way a man struck dumb strains toward speech. It had heard her after all. It had heard her the first night, on the rocks, screaming her defiance at the sky—and it had come, and come, and come again, circling and circling, unable to answer her because its own voice had been taken, unable to touch her because—

Because it was afraid.

Of what, she didn't know. Of breaking something. Of breaking *her.* Some rule woven deeper than thunder, older than the bargain perhaps, holding the storms of veiled Thornfell back from the last daughter of House Delacroix with a tenderness that felt, unbearably, like grief.

"Oh," Odile whispered, and her breath fogged in the cold, and the nearest lightning bolt flared white at the word, close enough to light the cave like daybreak. "Oh, you poor things."

"Odile." Xanthe was beside her now, hand hovering near her elbow, ready to pull her back from the threshold. "What is it? What are you seeing?"

"Not seeing." She was laughing, or crying, or both; she honestly couldn't have said which. "Listening. Xanthe—they've been answering me all along. Every storm since we crossed the Veil. They can't speak—the Veil stole their thunder, stole their voice, the same way it steals everything else—but they *hear*. They hear me. They've been following me for days like dogs locked behind a gate." She turned to look at him, and her face was wet and blazing. "My power didn't fail because the land disowned me. It failed because the land is *forbidden* to answer me. Someone built a wall between House Delacroix and its own storms."

Xanthe went very still. "Your grandmother's bargain."

"Or something older. Or something added after." Odile stepped out into the storm.

Hail struck her shoulders and cheeks; wind seized her hair and clothes. She stood in the full violence of it with her arms loose at her sides, not summoning, not commanding, just *present*, and the tempest wheeled around her in a great slow orbit, and every soundless bolt lit her up like a saint in a fresco, and not one of them touched her.

"I don't understand," Xanthe called over the windless wind. "If the storms want to obey you, why hasn't one broken the rule? Why haven't they just—answered?"

"Because rules like this aren't kept by the ones they bind." She watched the lightning climb and fall, climb and fall, patient as tide. "They're kept by fear. Something happens to a storm that answers a Delacroix. Something bad enough that three days of storms have circled me rather than risk it." Her jaw tightened. "Which means someone wanted to be very sure I'd arrive home powerless."

"To hide you," Xanthe said slowly. "Like the archive said. To keep anyone from knowing the heir existed."

"And to keep the heir from knowing *herself.*" The pieces were assembling themselves in her mind with the cold click of a lock taking its key. "A warden without her storms is just a woman. A woman can be lied to. Managed. Led by the nose across her own country by whoever wrote the map." She glanced sideways at Xanthe, and something almost like a smile touched her mouth. "No offense."

"Some taken."

"But here's what whoever built that wall didn't count on." Odile lifted her chin into the wind, into the wheeling, straining, silent storm. "Walls hold back obedience. But curiosity—" she laughed, low and wondering, "—curiosity was never bound by any bargain."

She raised one hand. Not the summoning gesture. Just her hand, open, the way you offer your palm to a strange dog.

"Go on," she whispered. "One drop of rain. One breath of thunder. Just show me you're real, and I'll find a way to break whatever chain they've hung on you. I swear it on the last of my name."

The storm stopped moving.

Every bolt hung frozen mid-fall, every column of cloud stood still as sculpture, and in that perfect, impossible hush, Odile felt the attention of the entire tempest settle upon her outstretched hand—vast, cautious, hungry, hopeful—and begin, very slowly, to lean.

Far to the north, beyond the pass, beyond the ridgeline where the map thinned into guesswork, something answered.

Not thunder. Not quite. A sound like a word remembered rather than heard, a single deep pulse that rolled down from the high icefields and through the stone under her boots and up into the marrow of her, and every silent storm in veiled Thornfell turned toward it at once, like faces turning toward a bell.

Xanthe's hand closed hard around her wrist. "Tell me you heard that too."

"Oh, I heard it." Odile stared north, where the sky above the distant peaks had begun, very faintly, to glow. "Something up there just said my name back."
