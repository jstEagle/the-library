## 9. Blood in the Ledger

The archive's cold did not leave Odile when they climbed back into the grey morning light. It had gotten into her bones, into the spaces between her thoughts, and it sat there now like a stone she could not cough up. She walked ahead of Xanthe down the glacier's moraine with the copied pages folded against her chest, beneath her coat, where a smuggler carried the things worth killing for.

She had read them four times. She would read them a fifth before nightfall, and a sixth after that, because each reading made it worse in a new way.

*Let the House of Delacroix stand as keeper of the Veil,* the bargain said, in script so old the letters had grown tails like roots. *Let its blood hold the seal. Let no heir be raised within sight of the throne, lest the crown call to the blood and the blood answer.*

*Let no heir be raised within sight of the throne.*

Odile stopped walking. The moraine crunched under her boots and went quiet behind her; Xanthe, to their credit, did not ask what was wrong. They had learned that about her already, or perhaps recognized it — the way she needed silence to let a bad thing finish arriving.

Her grandmother had written those words. Or her grandmother's grandmother. Somewhere in that chain of stern, long-lived women whose portraits had hung in the hall at Highmoor, one of them had taken up the pen and decided, deliberately, with witnesses and seals and the weight of a starving kingdom behind her, that every Delacroix child born after that day would be raised blind to what they were.

*Exiled.* The word she had carried for eleven years like a knife between her shoulder blades. *Traitor's get. Storm-thief. The girl who called lightning on her own kin.*

Except she hadn't stolen anything. She had been *sent*. Sent out through the passes at nine years old with a nurse who wept the whole way and never told her why, sent with forged papers and a fabricated disgrace, sent so thoroughly and so carefully that even Odile herself believed the lie. Her aunt's face at the border, cold as slate. *You are not welcome here again.* She had built her whole life on the foundation of that cruelty — her sharpness, her independence, the way she trusted no one's door to stay open — and all of it, all of it, had been erected atop a kindness so enormous it looked exactly like abandonment.

She laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth to stop it.

"Odile." Xanthe's voice, careful. "What did you find?"

"You read the same pages I did."

"I read the bargain." Xanthe came around to face her, and their eyes — those eyes that held roads no one else remembered — searched her face with the particular attention of someone who drew maps for a living and knew how much truth a coastline could hide. "I didn't read whatever you found underneath it."

She wanted to say *nothing*. It was an old reflex, the smuggler's first tongue: deny everything, admit nothing, keep your cargo close. But Xanthe had paid a childhood to keep her secret from the Hollow King, and some debts you don't insult by pretending they don't exist.

"My exile was staged," she said. "By my own family. To hide me."

"To protect you."

"To *hide* me. Don't dress it up." She pulled the pages out and unfolded them with hands that were almost steady. "'Lest the crown call to the blood and the blood answer.' They weren't protecting *me*, Xanthe. They were protecting the seal. A hidden heir is a safe heir — safe from whoever might want to use a Delacroix to open the Veil, or close it tighter, or—" She stopped. Breathed. "Or use her at all. I wasn't a granddaughter being smuggled to safety. I was a weapon being buried where no one would dig."

Xanthe was quiet a moment. Wind moved across the moraine, combing through the scree with a sound like slow applause.

"And yet," Xanthe said, "someone dug anyway. The wardens found you. I found you. Whatever they were hiding you from, it's awake now, and it knows your name." A pause. "So do I."

"Don't," Odile said, sharper than she meant. "Don't do the thing where you make it sound like fate. There's no fate in this. There's a ledger. That's all this is — a ledger, with our family name written at the top, and every Delacroix since has been a line item. My grandmother. My mother. Me. Payments on a debt none of us agreed to."

She heard her own voice rise and hated it, and made herself stop, and stood there in the wind with the pages trembling in her grip until the anger settled back down into something colder and more usable.

Because that was the thing about fury. Odile had learned it young, in the smuggling camps, in the years of sleeping with her back to walls: rage was heat, and heat dissipated, but cold anger kept. Cold anger you could spend like coin.

And standing there on the moraine, spending it, she saw — clearly, terribly — what the ledger offered her.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen pillar-stone, one of the old waymarkers that had once lined Thornfell's high roads and now stood tilted and anonymous, like gravestones whose names had weathered away. Xanthe built the fire small. Inside the Veil, firelight seemed to travel less far than it should, as if the darkness itself was thick and the flame had to push through it.

Odile sat with the pages on her knees and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing all day.

Here is what she knew now: she was the last storm-warden of House Delacroix. Here is what the bargain required: a keeper of her blood to renew the seal before the thirteenth turn completed. Here is what waited inside the Veil: an entire country, alive, whole, *saved* — villages with their lamps lit, orchards heavy with fruit, children in the schoolyards of towns that the world outside had forgotten existed. The wardens' remnant had kept it running for a generation. Shadow-Thornfell, breathing in the dark.

And here is what waited outside: nothing. Erasure. A hole in the world's memory shaped like home.

If she took the throne — and who would stop her? The wardens wanted a Delacroix. The blood in her veins was the key and everyone inside the Veil knew it — if she took the throne and spoke the renewal, then Thornfell went on. Her people went on. The dead stayed mourned and the living stayed fed and the mountains kept their names, and all it would cost was a cage. A beautiful cage, gilded with survival, locked from the inside by choice.

No one out there would ever know what they'd lost. No one out there was suffering for it. You cannot miss a country that has been cut out of your memory; the wound doesn't even itch.

*Let the Veil stand,* whispered something in her, in a voice that sounded suspiciously like her grandmother's. *Let it stand and reign, and be merciful, and be damned.*

"Tell me what you're thinking," Xanthe said, from across the fire. "Because you've got your bargaining face on."

"My bargaining face?"

"The one where you've already closed the deal and you're just deciding whether to tell your partner."

Odile huffed. But she turned the pages toward the firelight and tapped the clause she'd been circling all day, the one that had started as a wound and become, somewhere in the walking hours, an argument.

"'Lest the crown call to the blood and the blood answer.'" She looked up. "I felt that, Xanthe. At the Door of the Thirteenth Moon — no. Before that. At the ward-stone, when I touched my grandmother's spell and heard my own name in it. And again in the archive. Something in me *answers* to this country. Like a bell struck low. If I sat the throne — if I spoke the renewal with the blood-claim behind it — I don't think it would even be hard. I think the Veil would take me the way a river takes a dropped coin. And I think I'd thank it."

Xanthe fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "And the people inside? The ones the wardens saved?"

"That's the poison of it, isn't it." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "It's not even a hard question if you only look inward. One kingdom, saved. One heir, crowned. Everyone lives. The ledger balances." She turned her hand over, studied the burn-scar across her palm from the first ward-stone she'd ever charged, at nine years old, three days before her exile. "But I'm a smuggler, Xanthe. Do you know what I know that queens forget?"

"Enlighten me."

"Ledgers can be cooked. Somebody always pays the difference, and it's never the person holding the book." She nodded eastward, toward the dark bulk of the interior ranges, where the thirteenth moon was climbing invisibly toward its turn. "The bargain bought Thornfell's survival with Thornfell's existence. Fine. But it also bought it with *truth*. With every child born inside who'll never know there's an outside. With every road that leads nowhere on purpose. With the war-devourer still caged under all that ice, growing hungrier, waiting for the day the blood runs thin and the seal fails anyway." She met Xanthe's eyes across the flames. "Renewing the bargain doesn't save us. It just re-uploads the debt onto the next generation. Maybe onto a daughter of mine. Another girl exiled to keep her ignorant. Another ledger entry."

Xanthe was silent for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was very even, which Odile had learned meant they were choosing words the way a sapper chose footing.

"There's a version of you," Xanthe said, "that takes the crown. And I watched her considering it all day, and I'm not going to pretend she's wrong. She's the most reasonable person in this story." A breath. "But I noticed something. Every argument you made for the Veil was an argument about other people. And every time you talked about taking the throne, you said *I*."

Odile frowned. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the crown tempts you for you. For the belonging. For the home." Xanthe's gaze didn't waver. "I paid a childhood to the Hollow King, Odile. I know what memories cost, and I know the difference between the ones we choose and the ones we're sold. Your family sold you a false memory — eleven years of believing you were cast out. Are you sure you want to buy the correction from the same merchants?"

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the silent sky, the veiled moon was turning.

Odile looked down at the pages, at her family name rooted in the old script like a tree planted over a grave, and felt the temptation shift — not die, nothing in her died that cleanly, but shift — from *take the throne* to something more dangerous and more honest:

*Find out who decided I should be buried, and ask them why.*

Because the bargain named the keepers, but ledgers have two sides. Someone had engineered her exile. Someone had wept while doing it, maybe, or slept fine — and either answer changed everything. Her mother had died inside the Veil; the Hollow King hoarded the memory of her final night. Her aunt had turned her away at the border with eyes that Odile had spent eleven years reading as hatred, and now, with the ledger open, she found herself rereading that look like a mapmaker rereading a coastline, seeing the hidden inlet where she'd only ever drawn cliffs.

"They knew," she said quietly. "My aunt knew what I was. She looked at a nine-year-old and gave her a life sentence, and she did it *on purpose*, and I want to know if it broke her. I want to know if my mother went to her grave knowing what her daughter was for." She folded the pages along their worn creases. "There are answers inside the Veil, Xanthe. Real ones. Not in archives. In people."

"Then we go find the people."

"We go find *one* person." Odile stood, brushing frost off her coat, and felt the cold anger settle into resolve, clean-edged, directional. "The commander of the wardens' remnant. Whoever holds the shadow-court. Because if anyone alive signed off on my exile — if anyone kept the true ledger while the rest of the family kept the lie — it's them. And before I decide what to do with a crown, I intend to look whoever's been keeping my seat warm directly in the eye."

Xanthe rose too, and for a moment the firelight caught them both, two liars at the edge of a hidden kingdom, and Odile saw something pass over Xanthe's face — hesitation, or warning, or grief — gone too fast to name.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing." Xanthe kicked earth over the fire, and the darkness came in thick and soft. "Just — when we meet the wardens, let me do the talking at the gate. I may know the road to their door better than they'd like."

Above the fallen pillar-stone, where stars should have been, the darkness pulsed once — faintly, patiently — like a heartbeat counting down to the thirteenth turn.

And far to the east, unseen, a bell with Odile's name on it waited for someone to strike it.
