## 10. The Wardens' Remnant

Xanthe had learned to read a road the way other people read faces — the set of its shoulders, the old scars in its surface, the places where it flinched when you stepped on it wrong. The road that climbed out of the ash-valley toward the pass called the Shepherd's Tooth was a liar of a road. It pretended to be nothing, a goat-track worn into scree and lichen, but beneath its modesty ran something older and straighter, a spine of fitted stone that no shepherd had laid.

"Someone built this," Odile said, crouching to brush frost off a paving block. Her breath came out silver and vanished too quickly, as if the Veil drank even the small clouds a body made. "Someone with money."

"Someone with a country," Xanthe said, "that didn't want to be found."

They had been walking inside the Veil for eleven days now, by Xanthe's count — though counting was an act of stubborn faith here, where the light came from everywhere and nowhere and dawn arrived without ever having been preceded by morning. Eleven days since the archive beneath the ice. Eleven days since Odile had learned her name was written in the ledger of her family's great betrayal, and had said almost nothing about it, which frightened Xanthe more than screaming would have.

The road bent around the shoulder of the mountain, and there — sudden as a held breath released — stood Thornfell.

Not the real one. Xanthe understood that immediately, the way a cartographer understands a forgery: everything correct and nothing true. A town sat in the valley below them, slate roofs and smoking chimneys, terraced fields stitched up the slopes, a bell tower with a green copper spire exactly like the ones Xanthe remembered from the maps of a kingdom that officially did not exist. But the smoke rose from the chimneys and did not disperse. It hung above the town in flat grey ribbons, motionless, like paint on glass. And the fields were planted in perfect rows that never changed, wheat gold at every stage of ripening all at once, harvest-ready and newly-sown in the same furrow.

"It's beautiful," Odile said quietly.

"It's a memory," Xanthe said. "Beautiful things usually are."

* * *

They were met at the town gate before they reached it, which told Xanthe everything: someone had been watching, and watching well. Six figures in grey-and-white cloaks stood across the road, and their armor was wrong in a way that made Xanthe's cartographer's eye itch — the pauldrons bore the storm-crown sigil of House Delacroix, but the metal had been painted over so many times that the crown floated on a crust of older heraldries, like a face repainted until the features blurred.

The eldest of them stepped forward. She was perhaps seventy, tall and dry as winter grass, with the particular stillness of someone who has spent decades standing guard over nothing. Her eyes went to Odile first — they all looked at Odile first, Xanthe noted; the recognition moved through them like wind through wheat — and then, reluctantly, to Xanthe.

"You'd be the mapmaker," the woman said.

"And you'd be dead," Xanthe said. "All of you. The wardens of Thornfell were disbanded thirty years ago. I've mapped the garrison at Karst Hollow. It's a ruin. I've mapped the academy at Vey. There's a goat pasture where the training yards were."

"The world believes we're dead." The old warden's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "That has been the point. Warden-Commander Sera Voss. I had the honor of serving under Alarice Delacroix, in the last year before the sealing." Her gaze returned to Odile, and softened in a way that seemed rehearsed. "You have her hands, child. She used to crack her knuckles before a working, just so. Do you?"

Odile did not answer. Xanthe watched her watch the town below — the motionless smoke, the impossible wheat — and saw her grandmother's ghost settle over her shoulders like a mantle cut for someone else.

"You keep a town," Odile said at last. "Inside the Veil. All this time."

"We keep *Thornfell*," Voss corrected gently. "Come down and see. Judge us after."

* * *

The town was called Bellhollow, and it had been called Bellhollow for exactly one generation, because that was how long it had existed. Everything in it was a copy — not a counterfeit, Xanthe thought as they walked its streets, but a transcription, faithful to the last detail of a place that no longer had an original. The bakeries baked bread whose smell reached the door and stopped there, unable to drift. Children played a game with a knotted rag ball, and their laughter sounded right, and their faces were all slightly too alike, cousins marrying cousins inside a closed box for twenty-five years. In the market square, a fountain ran with water that fell upward for half a second at every seventh splash — a flaw in the transcription, a smudged line.

And everyone knew who Odile was.

That was the thing that turned Xanthe's stomach as they walked. Doors opened. People paused in their doorways and pressed fists to their hearts, the old salute of the storm-wardens, and murmured a word Xanthe hadn't heard spoken aloud since childhood: *heir*. An old man dropped his bundle of kindling to bow. A girl of maybe eight stared from a window with an expression of such naked hope that Xanthe had to look away.

"They've been waiting," Xanthe murmured. "For you. This whole time."

"They've been waiting for a *story*," Odile said, low enough that only Xanthe caught it. "I'm not sure I'm the story they waited for."

Voss led them to the garrison hall — another copy, another faithful lie, its rafters black with a hearth-smoke smell that never quite arrived — and fed them a meal of stew and dark bread that tasted, Xanthe thought, almost exactly like food. Around the long table sat perhaps forty wardens, grey-cloaked, scarred, real in a way the town was not. These were flesh and hunger and gray stubble and bad teeth. These had bled for something.

When the meal was done, Voss spread a map on the table, and Xanthe leaned over it before she could stop herself — and went cold.

It was her map. Or nearly. The roads she had drawn through the Veil, the routes from the ice archive toward the Door of the Thirteenth Moon, rendered in careful ink — including two passes Xanthe had found only by instinct, by feel, roads that appeared on no chart anywhere because Xanthe had invented them out of desperation three nights ago.

"How," Xanthe said.

"We have watchers," Voss said simply. "We have had watchers since before either of you crossed the border wards. Did you think you walked unobserved through a sealed kingdom?" She tapped the map, tap-tap-tap, on the mark that meant the Door. "You are going to the Door of the Thirteenth Moon. You intend—" her eyes flicked to Odile, "—something there. We would know what."

"You'd know anyway," Odile said. "You seem to know everything else. So let me save your spies the walk." She set both hands flat on the table, and Xanthe saw her knuckles whiten. "The bargain can be unmade. I'm the heir. My blood opened it; my blood can close it. That's what I mean to do. Open the Veil. Let Thornfell back into the world — all of it, the whole truth, the war we hid from and the debt we owe and everything."

Silence. Forty wardens looked at their commander. The fire cracked in a hearth that gave no heat.

"No," Voss said.

It was not angry. It was not even firm. It was the voice of a woman stating that water is wet, that winter follows autumn, that some things are simply the shape the world has settled into. And then she did something Xanthe did not expect. She knelt.

She knelt on the stone floor of the hall she had kept alive for a generation, and bowed her head, and the forty wardens knelt with her, a rustling wave of grey cloaks, until the whole room was bowed before Odile Delacroix like wheat before weather.

"Long live the Storm Queen of Thornfell," Voss said. "Take your throne. Take your crown. Renew the bargain, and reign forever behind the Veil, safe, sovereign, unseen — and we will spend what remains of our lives keeping your shadow-country breathing, as we swore to your grandmother we would."

* * *

Later — much later, after Odile had walked out of the hall without answering, after the wardens had risen and dispersed with the terrible patience of people who have waited twenty-five years and can wait twenty-five more — Xanthe found Voss on the garrison wall, looking out at the frozen smoke of Bellhollow.

"She won't say yes," Xanthe said.

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because saying yes must be *possible*," Voss said. "A choice isn't a choice unless the easy door stands open. I knelt so she could refuse me honestly." She turned, and in the sourceless Veil-light her face looked carved from the same stuff as the mountains. "You love her. Don't bother denying it; I've watched sentries fall in love for forty years, it looks the same on everyone — like a limp they're trying to hide."

Xanthe felt heat climb her neck and chose, with effort, to ignore it. "What do you want from me?"

"To tell you what we are, since she'll only hear it as flattery. Come. Walk with me." Voss led along the wall, past sentries who nodded to her, and pointed down at the town. "Do you know what happened to the wardens when the Veil sealed? Your histories will say we were disbanded, pensioned off, scattered. Partly true. The rest is this: Alarice Delacroix gathered every warden who would follow her into the dark and made us a second oath. Not to the crown. To the *people*. Someone had to stay inside and keep them alive. Keep them believing they were still a country. Because a people who know they're a cage's contents stop being a people. They become livestock."

"So you built a lie and called it mercy."

"I built a lie and called it *home*, and I have buried ninety-one of my sworn sisters inside it, and every single one died believing Thornfell still stood in the sun." Voss's voice did not waver, but something behind it did, some deep structural crack, like ice bearing weight. "Call it what you like, mapmaker. I call it duty. And duty wears many masks, and home is the most comfortable of them."

Below, in the square, the children had resumed their game. The rag ball arced up and hung at the top of its throw a half-beat too long, as if the sky were reluctant to give it back.

"There's a girl down there," Voss said quietly. "Dark hair, red scarf. Her name is Marette. Her grandmother was my lieutenant. When the thirteenth turn completes — if it completes — that girl doesn't die. No one dies. They simply stop having ever been. Every bread-crumb, every scar, every quarrel, every kiss. Erased so cleanly the war-devourer outside won't even find bones." She looked at Xanthe at last. "Your mistress speaks of freedom. Freedom is a fine word for people standing outside the cage. Ask the girl in the red scarf what she'd trade for another hundred years of the lie, and she'd trade anything. Anything at all. Would you take that from her?"

"Yes," Xanthe said. "If the price is everyone outside it forgetting Thornfell ever existed. If the price is her mother's name being a hole in the world. If the price is *her* — Marette — never seeing a real star." Xanthe's own voice surprised her with its steadiness. "I'm a cartographer, Commander. I deal in what's actually there. A country made of lies isn't a country. It's a very beautiful blank space."

For a long moment Voss said nothing. Then, softly, almost to herself:

"Alarice said something like that once. The night before she sealed us in. She argued with herself until dawn, and the crown won." Her jaw tightened. "Pray the granddaughter argues better."

* * *

Xanthe found Odile on the eastern tower of the garrison, arms folded on the parapet, staring at the sky where stars should have been and were not. The Veil's false dusk pooled around her like standing water. Xanthe climbed the stair and stood beside her and said nothing, because some silences are a kind of company.

"They knew," Odile said finally. "My grandmother planned all of it. The exile. Me growing up outside, unknown, unwatched — no. *Watched.* Always watched." A short, bitter laugh. "I spent my whole life proud that no one owned me. Turns out I was the most carefully guarded thing in two kingdoms. A spare key, hidden under the mat."

"Odile—"

"If I renew it, they live." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Marette in the red scarf. The baker whose bread doesn't smell. Ninety-one dead women who believed in something. All of it continues. All I have to do is put on a crown and agree to be a queen of shadows, forever, and everyone inside gets to keep their beautiful lie." She turned, and her eyes in the Veil-dusk were the grey of storms that hadn't decided yet. "Tell me I'm a fool to want it. Even a little. Tell me, because right now wanting it feels like *home*, and home has lied to me my entire life."

Xanthe took her hand. Odile's knuckles were cracked from cold, and she did not pull away.

"It's not a lie that makes them liars," Xanthe said. "They were given a cage and told it was a kingdom, and they made it *true* as best they could. That's not cowardice. That's the bravest thing I've ever seen. But you don't owe your grandmother's fear your whole life." She lifted Odile's hand, turned it palm-up, the way one reads a map. "Look at this hand. It's cracked open doors in six countries. It smuggled medicine past three customs walls. It's held mine in the dark when I couldn't remember which way was north. That hand wasn't made to hold a crown in a room with no windows. And neither was the rest of you."

Odile looked at their hands for a long moment. "The thirteenth turn. How long?"

"Four days. Maybe five. The moon's waxing wrong — faster than it should. The Veil is hurrying."

"Then tomorrow," Odile said, "I'm going to stand in front of forty kneeling wardens and refuse the crown to their faces. And some of them are going to draw steel." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman stepping off a cliff she'd chosen. "So tonight — help me memorize your maps, Xanthe. All of them. Every erased road you've ever drawn. Because whatever happens at that Door, I want to be able to promise those people a way out that actually exists."

From below, faint across the frozen square, the children's laughter rose and hung, unwilling to fall.

And far above the motionless smoke, past the ceiling of the Veil, the thirteenth moon turned its face toward completion like a key turning toward a lock.
