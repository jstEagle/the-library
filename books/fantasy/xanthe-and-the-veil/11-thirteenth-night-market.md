## 11. Thirteenth Night Market

The way down into the market was a road that pretended not to exist.

Odile had spent her life learning the difference between hidden and forgotten, and this road was both — a goat track behind a collapsed watchtower that only looked like a dead end if you'd never smuggled brandy through a war. She led Xanthe along it by feel more than sight, one hand trailing the rock face where her grandmother's generation had cut handholds for couriers who never officially traveled. Below them, the valley of Vael-Mourne held its breath under a sky that had no stars in it, no moon she could name, just a pale smear of light like breath on glass.

"Smell that?" Xanthe said behind her.

Odile did. Woodsmoke, wet wool, frying onions — and underneath it all something sweeter and stranger, like rain on hot iron, like the moment before a memory surfaces. The smell of the Veil digesting itself, if she'd had to put words to it. Everything inside Thornfell smelled faintly of forgetting now. Even her own hands smelled wrong to her some mornings, as though they belonged to someone she hadn't met yet.

"It's the toll-roads," she said. "Every trade route has a night market. This one just trades in something worse than brandy."

They came around the last shoulder of rock and the Thirteenth Night Market opened below them like a wound full of lanterns.

It sat in the dry bed of a river that had been erased — literally erased, she understood now; there was no record anywhere in Thornfell that the Mourne had ever run here, though its stones still lay in its old channel like teeth knocked from a jaw. Hundreds of stalls crowded the riverbed under awnings of dyed canvas, lit by lamps that burned without flame, their light the color of old honey. And the crowd — gods, the crowd. Farmers with soil still under their nails. Soldiers in uniforms from three different wars, two of which Odile was fairly sure had never happened. Children too clean-eyed for any market she'd known. Everyone moved with the same careful hush, the way people walk through a sickroom.

"They're all forgotten," Xanthe said quietly. "Or half-forgotten. People the Veil spat out of the country's memory. They can't go home because home doesn't remember them."

"I know." Odile pulled her hood lower. "I've been one of them since I was nineteen."

She felt Xanthe look at her — that particular attention, warm and weighing at once — but said nothing, and neither did Xanthe, and that was its own kind of conversation between them by now. Two liars, fluent in each other's silences.

* * *

The market ran on a currency Odile knew how to read: names, dates, small true things offered up like coins. A woman sold apples and took payment in *the color of your mother's kitchen*. A man traded directions to the latrines for *the first line of a song you loved*. Nobody bargained hard, because nobody could remember what they'd paid yesterday.

At the center of the riverbed, on a raised stone platform that might once have been a bridge footing, stood the memory-brokers' stalls — taller structures, hung with bells and ribbons of written prayer, each one guarded by a broker wearing a mask of blank white clay. No features. Just smooth faces, so that everything you saw when you dealt with them was your own reflection.

Odile had come here for information about the Door of the Thirteenth Moon, and she meant to get it. But that wasn't what made her heart knock against her ribs as they descended into the honey-colored light.

What made it knock was simpler, and worse.

She had watched Xanthe pay the Hollow King. She had stood in that gray village square with her fists clenched at her sides while Xanthe had smiled at the masked tax-collectors and handed over *a childhood* — years of it, whole seasons, gone with a nod — and then turned back to her with those same steady eyes and said, *Shall we?* as if nothing had happened. As if the hole didn't exist.

Xanthe couldn't tell her what was missing. That was the cruelty of the Hollow King's toll: you paid and forgot you had paid. But the memories themselves went somewhere. Everything stolen in the Veil flowed downhill, like water, like coin, into markets like this one.

And Odile Delacroix had never in her life left valuable cargo unclaimed.

"You have that face," Xanthe said as they threaded the stalls.

"What face?"

"The one you wear when you're planning a heist and lying to me about it."

"I don't lie to you," Odile said. "I withhold strategically. It's different. It has better posture."

That earned her the laugh she wanted — quick and low, gone almost before it arrived, but real. She held onto it the way other people held onto coins.

* * *

The broker she needed worked the third stall on the platform, the one hung with silver bells and a ribbon stitched with the sigil of House Delacroix — defaced, but recognizable, because even erasure left scars. The broker beneath the blank mask went very still when Odile named herself. Not her title. Just her name, plain, the way you'd say it across a kitchen table.

"Delacroix," the broker repeated. The voice was neither young nor old. "That house is not remembered here."

"No," Odile agreed. "Which is why I'm paying in gold instead."

She set three Thornfell crowns on the counter — minted before the Veil, worth their weight in wonder now — and watched the blank mask tilt with something that might have been hunger. Gold remembered itself even when people didn't. It was the one honest thing left in this market.

"And what does the lady of storms wish to buy?" the broker asked.

"A year," Odile said. "Paid to the Hollow King, roughly six weeks ago, by a traveler called Xanthe. Cartographer. Brown coat, ink-stained fingers, insufferable sense of direction that is somehow always right."

Behind her, very quietly, Xanthe stopped breathing.

The mask regarded her. When it spoke again, the voice had cooled several degrees. "Memories surrendered freely to the toll are the property of the toll. Resale requires—"

"I'm not asking you to sell." Odile leaned in, letting the hood fall back, letting the broker see her face — the Delacroix grey eyes, the storm-line jaw, the whole discredited inheritance of it. "I'm asking you to consider whose blood signed the original ledger that lets the Hollow King operate in these valleys at all. Consider who might, someday, be in a position to make his collection worthless. Or yours."

Silence. Somewhere above the stall, a silver bell shivered though there was no wind.

Then the broker reached beneath the counter and brought up a ledger bound in grey hide, and Odile's stomach dropped, because she recognized the hide. Warden-leather. Her grandmother's wardens had carried books bound like that, and every one of them had been a promise that the thing inside would outlive whoever tried to destroy it.

The broker turned pages. Turned more. Stopped.

"One year," it said slowly. "Age twelve. Surrendered at the toll-stone at Vael-Mourne. Contents: a winter. A frozen lake. An older sister." The mask lifted toward Xanthe. "You paid away a sister?"

"Don't answer that," Odile said sharply, without turning around. "Whatever you remember or don't — don't answer him."

But the damage was already visible in the set of Xanthe's shoulders, in the way one hand had risen halfway to her own mouth as if to catch words escaping. Odile turned back to the broker and laid the rest of her gold down, all of it, every crown she'd hoarded against emergencies, the emergency fund for the emergency she hadn't planned on: wanting to give something back to someone who wouldn't ask.

"Price," she said.

* * *

It cost her the gold, the crowns, the silver buckle off her boot, and a favor owed, unnamed, to be collected whenever the broker chose. She paid it without blinking, and hated herself a little for how easily she blinked at nothing else. Then the broker drew out a stoppered vial no bigger than her thumb, and inside it swam a light like candlelight seen through falling snow.

"Drink it or pour it into your palm and press your hand to hers," the broker said. "Memory returns truer skin-to-skin. But know this, Delacroix—" and here the blank mask leaned close, and Odile saw her own reflection warped in its emptiness, "—returned memories do not always fit the person you've become. You may be giving her a year that no longer belongs to her. What is given back can weigh more than what was taken."

"Noted," Odile said. "Anything else, or do you also sell warnings wholesale?"

"For you," the broker said, and its voice changed — softened, almost human, almost sorry — "there is one more item. Unpaid. Unclaimed. It arrived six weeks ago with the toll-caravans, and it has been waiting for a buyer with your face."

"I don't want—"

"It is a memory of your mother."

The market noise fell away. The honey-light narrowed to a single point. Odile became aware, distantly, of her own heartbeat, of Xanthe's hand hovering near her elbow without touching, of the grey ledger open on the counter like a mouth.

Her mother had died inside the Veil. Had died *of* the Veil, maybe, or for it — Odile had been exiled before the end, had learned of it in a letter that read like it had been written by a stranger pretending to grieve. She had spent seven years carrying that death the way you carry a stone in your boot: painfully, privately, without ever once sitting down to take it out and look at it.

"How much," she heard herself say.

"Not gold." The broker slid a second vial from beneath the counter, this one darker, its light a deep bruised violet. "This one waits for a debt, not a coin. Take it now, pay later. The Hollow King's caravans pass the high road within the week. He will want to know why his toll was short one year." The mask tilted. "He will want to know it was you."

"Let him want," Odile said, and picked up both vials.

* * *

She didn't do it in the market. Some things shouldn't happen under blank masks and bell-hung stalls, among strangers trading pieces of themselves like fishwives trading herring.

She walked Xanthe up out of the riverbed, past the last stall, past the last lamp, until they stood on the bank above the dead river where the erased water should have sung, and the false sky pressed down soft and starless as closed eyelids. Cold air. The smell of snow coming somewhere above them, silent as everything else in this country.

"Sit down," Odile said.

"Why?"

"Because if you argue I'll change my mind, and if I change my mind I'll have thrown away my entire fortune for nothing, and I will *never* forgive you."

Xanthe sat. Odile sat facing her, knees nearly touching, and uncorked the first vial, and for a long moment just looked at her — at the cartographer with no country, ink on her fingers, frost in her lashes, watching Odile with that patient, maddening steadiness that had gotten under Odile's skin somewhere around the ruined ward-stone and had never found its way out.

"Your hand," Odile said.

"Odile." Xanthe's voice had gone carefully, dangerously level. "What did you buy?"

"Give me your hand, or I'll pour it on your head like brandy and we'll both regret it."

A pause. Then, slowly, Xanthe extended her hand, palm up, and Odile took it — warm, chapped, an ink stain in the crease of the thumb that she had noticed weeks ago and thought about more than she would ever admit — and poured the snow-light of the memory into Xanthe's palm, and pressed her own hand over it.

The cold of the vial, the warmth of the skin. And then—

She hadn't expected to see it too. But memory returning skin-to-skin, the broker had said, and the broker hadn't lied: the vision opened in *both* of them at once, like a door swinging wide on a room full of winter.

*A frozen lake, white under a white sky. A girl of twelve — Xanthe, unmistakably, younger, rounder-faced, laughing — being towed across the ice on a sledge by an older girl, sixteen maybe, all sharp elbows and dark braids and a laugh twice as loud. The older girl pulling her faster, faster, the runners hissing, both of them shrieking with joy, and then the ice cracking — not danger, just the great singing boom of a lake shifting, terrifying and wonderful, and the older girl throwing herself down beside the younger one, arms wrapped around her, both of them flat on the ice listening to the lake groan and sing beneath them like some enormous sleeping animal.*

*"When I'm grown," the older girl was saying, breath fogging, "I'm going to map every lake in Thornfell. Every single one. So nobody ever falls through because nobody knew it was there."*

*"You can't map everything," little Xanthe said.*

*"Watch me."*

*And the older sister had kissed the top of her head, right there on the singing ice, and the memory ended in the smell of cold and wool and absolute, uncomplicated safety.*

Odile came back to herself with tears freezing on her cheeks and Xanthe's hand crushing hers hard enough to hurt.

"She had a name," Xanthe whispered. Her voice was wrecked, rebuilt, wrecked again. "Odile — she had a *name*, I can almost — Sera. Sera. She pulled me across the lake and she said she'd map everything so no one would ever—" A sob broke loose, ugly and human. "I paid my own sister away. I paid her away to protect *you*, and I didn't even know I'd had a sister to pay."

"You didn't pay her away." Odile gripped her hand tighter, furious at the world, at the Veil, at the Hollow King and his gray toll-stones and the whole rotten bargain that made love into currency. "You were robbed. There's a difference, and I am going to spend whatever it costs making the world understand the difference."

Xanthe looked at her then, and Odile watched something shift behind her eyes — watched survival, that lean hungry thing that had driven them both since the stars went out, quietly lose its place at the front of the line. What replaced it was softer and far more dangerous.

"Why," Xanthe asked simply. "You gave up everything you had. Why?"

Because you smile at me like nothing's missing. Because you gave up a childhood and never once made me the poorer for it. Because I have been exiled from every place I loved and you are the first place I've found that I chose.

"Because," Odile said, "some cargoes you don't abandon."

Xanthe laughed through her tears, and pulled her in, and they stayed like that on the bank of the dead river, two liars holding each other in the starless dark, while above the valley the false sky began — very faintly, very wrongly — to flicker.

Odile felt it before she understood it. A tremor through the ground, like the ice on that long-ago lake. The Veil shifting its weight. Somewhere north, in the direction of the Door, something vast exhaled.

And when she raised her head, she saw that the map-case at Xanthe's side lay open, spilled by their embrace — and the ink lines of their route home were fading, stroke by stroke, the paper drinking its own roads like sand drinking rain.

"Xanthe," she said quietly. "Your map. Look at your map."

The road behind them was already gone.
