## 13. What the Hollow King Kept

The Hollow King's hall had been a tithe-barn once, before the Veil, before the forgetting — Odile could still read its bones beneath the rot and the gilding. The great beams that held up the roof were black with age and smoke, carved at either end with the wheat-sheaf sigil of a Thornfell that no longer existed anywhere but in her own skull. Someone had hung lanterns from them now, dozens of them, each one glowing a sickly amber that made everything below look like meat.

Everything below *was* meat, more or less. Or the memory of it.

They sat in rows on benches that had been pews, then market stalls, then nothing at all: villagers from the valley, travelers caught by the toll, hollow-eyed people who had sold too much of themselves to remember why they'd come. And above them, on a dais built from a grain merchant's cart turned on its end and draped in red cloth gone pink with dust, sat the Hollow King himself.

Odile had imagined him taller. That was the first insult.

He was a small man, broad through the shoulders, with the weathered hands of someone who had once worked soil or rope or stone. His crown was a ring of iron keys welded together, and his robes were patched velvet, and his face —

His face was kind. That was the second insult, and the worse one.

"You've come for your mother," he said, before she had crossed half the hall. His voice carried without effort, the way voices do in rooms where everyone has learned to be quiet. "I wondered how long you'd take. You walk like her, you know. Like you're angry at the ground for being in the way."

Xanthe's hand found her elbow. A warning. *Don't.*

She shook it off and kept walking.

* * *

She had planned it like a run, because runs were the only language she trusted anymore. Back way in through the tithe-barn's old loading door, which Xanthe's maps swore existed even though the wall around it looked solid. A distraction — the last jar of storm-light she owned, cracked against the far wall to pull every eye in the room. And then straight up the aisle while the crowd murmured, straight to the dais, straight to the man who taxed memories out of desperate heads and kept what he took.

The plan had lasted exactly as long as it took the Hollow King to look at her.

"Sit," he said, and gestured to a stool beside the dais that had not been there a moment ago. "You can stand and glare if you prefer. Your grandmother used to stand and glare. It ran in the family, apparently, right down to the jaw."

"My grandmother is a ward-stone and a rumor," Odile said. "You'll have to do better."

"Oh, I expect so." He leaned back, and the keys of his crown clicked softly against each other. "But we have time tonight. The thirteenth turn is nearly full — did you know? Everyone inside feels it, even if they can't name it. Like a tooth that knows the dentist. The whole country is holding its breath." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who found other people's dread mildly amusing. "So sit. Ask me what you came to ask. I'm feeling generous. It's the season."

"What did you take from my mother?"

The hall went very quiet. Even the lanterns seemed to lean in.

"Ah," the Hollow King said. "Not *what did you take from me*. Not *where is my hoard*. Straight to her." He studied Odile with an attention that felt like being flayed slowly. "Yes. I have her final night. All of it. Every hour, from the moment she left the keep at Vaelmoor to the moment the snow closed over her tracks. She paid me herself, you understand. Walked in here under the old name of the barn, when this was still a toll-house and I was still just a man who kept accounts, and she said: *take it, take all of it, I cannot carry it where I'm going.*" He spread his hands. "Who was I to refuse a lady in distress?"

"You could have given it back."

"To whom?" He laughed — genuinely, warmly, horribly. "Child, there is no *whom*. There is no Delacroix heir. There is no House, no line, no name. The bargain ate the ledger entry itself. If I gave her night back to the world, it would fall on the floor like a coin in an empty room." He tilted his head. "Although. Now that you're here..."

* * *

She should have walked away then. She knew it even as she sat down on the stool, as she heard Xanthe settle onto the bench behind her close enough to touch — a presence at her back like a drawn blade wrapped in cloth. She should have stood up and told this little king with his key-crown that she would burn his hoard and salt the ashes.

Instead she said, "Show me."

"Toll first." He raised a hand before she could spit. "*Toll first* — hear me out. Not memory. Not yours; yours are stitched into the Veil now, and frankly they'd give me indigestion. Something smaller." He reached beneath the draped cart and drew out a scale, brass, ancient, the kind smugglers used to weigh cut gemstone. On one pan sat nothing. On the other, a folded scrap of paper. "Truth for truth. That's always been the rate in my house. You want to see her last night — you tell me something true that no one living knows. Something you've never said aloud. The scales will judge whether it weighs enough."

"That's not a toll," Odile said. "That's a confession booth."

"It's a *market*," the Hollow King corrected pleasantly. "The only honest one left inside the Veil. Everything else trades in lies about the past. I trade in the past itself." He nudged the scale with one finger. "Whenever you're ready. Though the moon won't wait forever, and neither, I'm afraid, will I. There's a buyer coming for her night. Someone with deep pockets and shallow intentions. I'd rather sell to the daughter."

"*Buyer.*"

"Mm." He examined his fingernails. "Wardens travel light, but their purses are heavy. They want to know what your mother knew. So do you. Difference is, they'll pay in blood and I only charge in honesty."

Odile looked back at Xanthe. In the amber light their face was unreadable — the professional blankness of a person who had spent a lifetime making themselves unreadable — but their eyes were on her, steady, and in them was a question: *do you trust yourself with this?*

No. Almost certainly not.

"Ask away," she said, turning back.

* * *

What she told him was small. That was the point of it; she chose it the way you choose a coin to sacrifice to a river god — valuable enough to matter, cheap enough to lose.

"The cargo I was running, the night the stars went out," she said, low, so the words stayed between the three of them and the scales. "It wasn't silk. It wasn't salt. It was children. Six of them, from the flood-towns on the southern face, whose parents paid me to get them over the pass before the border sealed. I got five over. The sixth — a boy, maybe nine years old — he panicked at the ward-line. He ran back down toward the lights. And when the wards shattered and the dark came down, I didn't go after him." Her throat worked. "I told myself no one could have made it in time. I told myself the wards would have killed us both. But the truth — the true thing, the thing I have never said to any living soul — is that I chose the mountain over the boy. I chose myself. I have counted every starless night since, and part of why I'm going to tear this Veil down is that somewhere inside it, that boy might still be alive, and I owe him a country to grow up in."

Silence. Somewhere in the rafters a lantern swayed, though there was no wind.

The Hollow King looked down at the scale. The empty pan had sunk. Slowly, gently, like a leaf settling on water.

"Well," he said quietly. "That's worth more than most people carry in a lifetime." For a moment the kindness in his face stopped looking like a mask. "You know, most who sit there lie. They dress up their sins until they shine. The scales drop flat and I send them away hungry." He lifted the paper off the other pan and held it out to her. It was unremarkable — gray stock, wax seal stamped with a wheat sheaf. "This is the claim-ticket. My vault doesn't open for hands, only for debts honestly paid. Go down the stair behind the dais. Third door. What's hers is behind it."

"And the wardens' buyer?"

"Arrives when the thirteenth turn completes. Which gives you—" he squinted at a window that showed nothing but black, and seemed to consult some inner clock instead, "—perhaps two hours. Take what's yours and go. Or stay and meet them; I suspect that conversation is coming regardless." He smiled again, that awful warm smile. "Go on. Some doors you shouldn't be talked out of walking through."

* * *

The stair behind the dais went down farther than the barn had foundations. Stone gave way to older stone, then to bedrock veined with something that glimmered faintly silver, like frost growing backward. Xanthe lit the way with a stub of candle they must have palmed from a market stall days ago, and their shadow stretched huge and wavering along the walls.

"Third door," Xanthe murmured. "He said third door."

"I heard him."

"You're shaking."

"I'm *fine*."

"You're shaking," Xanthe repeated, gentler, "and you're going to walk through that door anyway, and I'm going to walk through it with you, and afterward — whatever's in there — we're going to keep moving. That's all. That's the whole plan."

Odile almost laughed. Two liars, one plan, and the plan was simply *keep moving*. "You're a terrible strategist."

"I'm a mapmaker. I draw the road. The walking is your department."

The third door was iron, unmarked except for a keyhole shaped like nothing she recognized — a crescent bitten by a circle, a moon devoured whole. The claim-ticket grew warm in her hand as she approached, and when she pressed it flat against the iron, the metal sighed and swung inward.

Cold air breathed out. Not the cold of winter. The cold of *kept things*.

* * *

The vault was a single round chamber, and it was full of light.

Memories, in the Hollow King's keeping, were not mist or smoke as the stories claimed. They were lamps. Hundreds of them, thousands, filling shelves cut into the rock in careful rows — glass vessels of every shape, each one holding a small burning glow, amber and blue and green and one or two that burned white-hot and hurt to look at. Names were inked on tags wired to each vessel's neck. Some of the names were worn illegible. Some were fresh.

Some were written in handwriting Odile knew better than her own.

Her breath stopped. She crossed the chamber without deciding to, without feeling her feet, and pulled down the lamp tagged *Seraphine Delacroix — final night*, and it was heavy, heavier than glass had any right to be, warm as a sleeping bird.

"Don't," Xanthe said softly, at her shoulder. "You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." Her voice came out scraped raw. "If I leave here without knowing, I'll wonder for the rest of my life. And if the wardens get it first—"

She unstopped the lamp.

* * *

Snow. Snow falling sideways in a wind that screamed, and beneath the wind the great grey shoulder of the keep at Vaelmoor sliding away behind, and beneath her hands the reins of a horse she was driving too hard because there was no time, no time, the wards were already singing their dying note and her father's voice was still ringing in her ears — *take her out, take the child out, they must never find the heir* —

A bundle against her chest, wrapped in wool, warm and furious and six weeks old, screaming at the wind.

*I'm sorry,* Seraphine Delacroix thought, and the thought was a physical ache, a hand closing around Odile's heart from the inside. *I'm sorry, little storm, I'm sorry it has to be this way, I'm sorry you'll hate me for it — hate me, do you hear me? Hate me and live. Forget Thornfell, forget the throne, forget the Door. Let the world forget us all, if forgetting keeps you breathing—*

The toll-house loomed out of the snow, lamplit, and there was a man in the doorway with an accountant's face and kind eyes, and she stumbled in with her dead horse steaming behind her and her child wailing, and she laid it all out on his counter: the gold she had, the signet ring, and finally the night itself, scooped out of her like marrow, because she could not carry both the memory and the baby and one of them had to survive—

*The Door is real,* she told the toll-keeper, in the last clear moment before she let him take it. *My father charged me with the truth even though he couldn't charge me with the child's safety. Listen. The Door of the Thirteenth Moon stands beneath the Corrie of Stars, and it does not open by force or by crown or by blood alone. It opens when the bargain is spoken renewed or spoken ended, by the hand of a Delacroix, under the full thirteenth turn — and never otherwise. The bargain itself guards it. The Veil will wear any face, offer any price, to make the heir renew. Remember that. Whatever it offers — the dead alive, the lost returned, the throne restored — it is the cage talking. It is the cage, wearing love's face—*

And then the taking, and the merciful white nothing, and a woman walking out into the snow carrying a child she could no longer quite explain to herself, humming a lullaby whose words had been sold away, leaving her daughter at a convent gate in the rain with a storm-charm and no name but the one sewn into the blanket—

* * *

Odile came back to herself on her knees on the vault floor, the empty lamp rolling from her fingers, and she was screaming, or she had been screaming; her throat was wrecked and Xanthe was holding her, arms locked around her, saying her name over and over like an anchor-chain paying out.

"She knew," Odile rasped. "She knew everything. She carried the location of the Door out of Vaelmoor in her own head and she sold it rather than risk it, she *sold it*, she traded my entire inheritance for—" A laugh tore out of her, ugly and wet. "For six weeks of my life. She bought me six weeks and it cost her everything, and the Veil took the rest anyway, the Veil took *her*, and I spent twenty years hating her for abandoning me at a gate in the rain—"

"She didn't abandon you." Xanthe's voice was steady in a way that cost them something; Odile could feel it, the tremor held down beneath the words. "She aimed you. Twenty-two years ahead, at this exact room, at this exact night. Everything she sold, everything she suffered — it put you here, with the knowledge, with the blood, with the choice. That's not abandonment. That's the longest run anyone ever planned for you."

Odile pressed her forehead against Xanthe's shoulder and let the fury move through her, wave after wave, and watched, distantly, with a clarity that frightened her more than the grief did, as the fury settled into something harder and colder and infinitely more useful.

Because her mother had told her the last thing she needed to know. *The bargain itself guards the Door. The Veil will wear any face. It is the cage, wearing love's face.*

And the guardian would wear her grandmother's face. Of course it would. It would offer her everything she had ever lost, and it would mean every word, and none of it would be real.

"They're coming," she said at last, pushing herself upright. Through the vault's iron door, faintly, from the hall above, came the sound of the outer doors opening, boots on boards, the particular silence of armed people trying not to be heard. Warden steel. Right on schedule. "The buyer's early."

Xanthe was already on their feet, candle pinched out, a knife appearing in their hand from nowhere. "Back stairs?"

"There are no back stairs."

"Then I'll draw us one." Their eyes gleamed in the lamplight, fierce and terrified and absolutely certain. "Stay behind me, storm-caller. And Odile — when we get out of here, when we reach that Door—" they hesitated, the first hesitation she'd ever heard from them, "—whatever it offers you, whatever face it wears — you remember your mother's voice. Promise me."

Above them, boots were crossing the hall. Below them, a thousand stolen lives burned quietly in their glass.

"I promise," Odile said, and reached for the storms that were not yet hers, waiting in the silent dark like a held breath. "Now draw fast."
