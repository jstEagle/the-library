## 14. Halfway to Nowhere

The road gave out two miles past the last waystone, and Xanthe knew before the stone did.

That was the trouble with drawing roads that had never existed: the map remembered what the land refused to. They had crossed into a fold of country where Thornfell's bones showed through its skin — raw granite shoulders, pines bent sideways by winds that had no business blowing this far inland, and everywhere that low, listening silence of the Veil pressing down like a hand laid flat over the world's mouth. The road Xanthe had inked three nights ago in the margin of an old survey chart ran true enough underfoot. Gravel the color of old teeth. A stream that crossed it at exactly the place the stream should cross. But the edges were fraying. Xanthe could feel it the way a singer feels a note going flat — the road's *truth* thinning, unraveling thread by thread behind them as the Veil digested the fiction they were traveling on.

Odile walked ahead, hood up, storm-grey eyes fixed on the pass that wasn't visible yet but lived in her posture like a splinter. She hadn't slept properly since the Night Market. Since she'd pressed a stolen year back into Xanthe's hands and learned what Xanthe had paid away at the Hollow King's toll — the childhood, the harbor town, the mother humming over a mending basket. She hadn't spoken of it directly. She'd simply started walking half a step closer than before, close enough that their knuckles brushed on the narrow places, and said nothing about that either.

Xanthe had spent a career among liars and had long ago made peace with being one. But there was a difference between lying to customs men and carrying a truth that would detonate the only person who still trusted you.

*They sent me.*

The thought arrived on schedule now, several times a day, punctual as fever. The wardens' remnant. The shadow-Thornfell that had kept itself alive inside the Veil for a generation, tending candles in a house whose doors no longer opened onto anything. They had found Xanthe in the borderlands years ago — a young cartographer with an impossible memory for unmapped roads, grieving, rootless, useful — and they had given Xanthe a purpose the way one gives a stray a collar. *Find the Delacroix heir. Find her before the thirteenth turn completes. Bring her home, or bring us word of where she can be taken.*

And Xanthe had found her. And then had stood in a smuggler's camp watching Odile Delacroix count coin in the dark with a smuggler's quick hands and a warden's stubborn jaw, and had understood, with the terrible clarity of a map suddenly seen whole, that no order issued from a dying remnant could outrank this one person. So Xanthe had lied. Had led her anyway, toward the Door instead of the throne, and had told himself it was strategy, that the truth would come later, that there would be a right moment —

"Your map's dying," Odile said, without turning around.

"I know."

"How long?"

Xanthe checked the horizon behind them, where the road they'd walked that morning was already softening, gravel going vague at the verges, the stream's crossing drifting a handspan off true. "By nightfall, the stretch we covered today will be suggestion rather than fact. By tomorrow, I'll be redrawing from scratch." A breath. "The Veil is learning my handwriting."

Odile snorted — a small, sharp sound, the first almost-laugh in days. "Then we keep moving until it finishes the lesson."

They crested a shoulder of scree as she said it, and Xanthe felt the ambush before seeing any sign of it, because good cartographers learn to read ground, and the ground ahead was wrong. Too quiet in the pines. No jays. The kind of hush that meant bodies holding still, and more than one.

"*Down—*" was all Xanthe got out.

The first bolt took the boulder beside Odile's head and burst it to grit. The second came from the left, from behind a fallen pine, and Xanthe was already moving — not thinking, not deciding, just moving, the way water moves when a dam breaks — shoulder into Odile's ribs, both of them crashing down the scree slope as the bolt meant for her heart hissed through the space her throat had occupied and buried itself in the trunk of a lightning-dead fir.

Then the wardens came out of the trees.

Six of them. Grey-cloaked, grey-helmed, faces masked in the old warden style — smooth plates with no features but eye-slits, so that they looked less like soldiers than like the mountain itself had grown opinions. Their leader carried a halberd with a blade that drank light rather than reflecting it, and when he spoke, his voice came out flattened by the mask, neither young nor old.

"Xanthe of the Border Roads." The title landed like a sentence being read aloud. "The Remnant thanks you for your service. Deliver the heir and walk away. This is the last time we offer."

Odile, pinned beneath Xanthe on the scree, went very still. Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of a woman doing arithmetic.

"*Heir,*" she repeated.

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well, and Xanthe heard it fall all the way down — past the Night Market, past the archive beneath the ice, past every campfire where Xanthe had watched her sleep and told himself *tomorrow, tomorrow* — to land on something buried at the very bottom.

There was no time. There was never any time. Xanthe rolled aside, dragged Odile up, and ran.

* * *

They ran the way smugglers run — downhill first, always downhill, letting gravity pay part of the fare, cutting through deadfall where armored men moved slow. Behind them the halberd-bearer's voice rang off the stone: *"The heir comes with us or does not leave this valley!"* Bolts cracked against rock. One tore through Xanthe's cloak hem. Odile ran like someone born to it, which she was, feet finding purchase on wet granite as if the mountain owed her favors, and twice she glanced sideways at Xanthe with eyes that had gone from grey to the color of a sky deciding whether to break.

She didn't ask questions. That was worse. Questions could be answered; that silence was a verdict being drafted.

The trail funneled them — Xanthe saw it too late, hated the mapmaker's honesty of it — into a defile between two walls of stone, twenty feet wide, no cover, a stream running shallow over slate. The classic killing ground. The wardens had chosen it well, which meant they'd known these roads, which meant they'd read the same erased surveys Xanthe had, which meant somewhere in the arithmetic of betrayal there was one more figure Xanthe hadn't counted.

The wardens appeared ahead of them. All six, walking unhurried now out of the treeline, fanning across the width of the defile. The halberd-bearer raised his weapon, and the light-drinking blade threw no gleam at all.

"Enough running," he said. "You were never meant to reach the Door, cartographer. You were meant to deliver her to the Remnant, where she could be crowned and kept. The bargain must be renewed. It is the only mercy left to us."

"It's a cage," Odile said. Her voice came out level, and underneath the levelness Xanthe heard the storm she couldn't yet summon, coiled and silent. "I've read the ledger. I know what my family bought and what it cost everyone else."

"You know nothing of cost," the warden said, "or you'd know some cages are built out of love."

The bolt came without warning — not a crossbow this time but a sliver of warden-magic, grey and fast as a thrown knife, aimed at the center of Odile's chest, and Xanthe didn't think. That was the whole confession, really, compressed into a single instant of muscle and choice: Xanthe stepped into its path.

It went in below the left shoulder, and the world went white and then very, very simple.

Falling. Slate under the back. Odile's face above, framed by torn cloud, saying Xanthe's name in a voice stripped of everything except the raw thing underneath it. Blood spreading warm and shocking across the cold stone, absurdly bright against the grey. Pain arriving late, like thunder after the flash, deep and grinding.

"No," Odile was saying. "No, no — stay —"

"Odile." It came out as a bubble of blood and breath. Xanthe caught her wrist. Held it the way one holds a rope over a long drop. "Listen. There isn't — there won't be a better moment. There was never going to be a better moment."

"Don't talk."

"They sent me." The words tore coming up, like roots being pulled. "Years ago. The Remnant. They found me because I could remember roads that were erased, and they put a compass in my hand and a name in my ear — *Delacroix* — and told me to find the heir and bring her home to be crowned before the Veil hardened. Every mile. Every campfire. The passes I drew for you, the shortcuts, the dead drops — I knew where we were going because they'd drawn it for me first." Blood filled Xanthe's mouth; Xanthe spat it and kept going, because stopping now would mean never starting again. "I found you. And I looked at you counting coin by the fire, and I chose. Do you hear me? They sent me to find you, and I chose you instead. Everything after that night was mine. The lie was theirs. The staying was mine."

Above them, the wardens had gone still, watching their work bloom — because this was the work, Xanthe understood dimly. Break the bond, break the heir's resolve, harvest her grief for the crown.

Odile's face was terrible and beautiful, fury and heartbreak fused into one expression, and when she spoke her voice was almost gentle, which was how Xanthe knew how bad it was.

"You let me buy back your memories at the Night Market. You let me spend our coin, our time, our *road* — knowing."

"Yes."

"You let me start to trust you."

"Yes." A breath that hurt all the way down. "Because the truth would have ended it sooner, and I was — " the honest word surfaced, ugly and necessary — "*selfish.* I wanted more of the road first."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. The wardens began to advance, boots deliberate on wet slate, and the halberd's dark blade came up, and Odile rose to her feet over Xanthe's body with her back to all of it, and Xanthe thought, with the strange floating clarity of blood loss: *she's going to die here beside me, or worse, she's going to kneel.*

Instead Odile held out her hand — palm up, the old smuggler's gesture, *show me what you're carrying* — to the lead warden.

"The ledger named House Delacroix keeper of the bargain," she said. "So here's my terms. Back away from the cartographer. Then choose — follow me to the Door, or explain to whatever's left of your Remnant why their heir bled you for touching what's mine."

"*Yours?*" The warden's mask tilted.

"She's mine to protect," Odile said, and the words came out with weight behind them, with pressure, with the first faint smell of ozone though no stormcloud showed anywhere in the white sky — "and you've made her bleed, so we're past negotiation."

The air in the defile went still the way air goes still before weather. The stream's surface tightened. Somewhere overhead, clouds that had hung motionless for days began, very slowly, to move.

Xanthe, bleeding on the slate, watched Odile stand over them with her hand still open and her eyes full of storm-light, and understood that something in her had cracked — that the Veil's hold on her magic was failing, that the silent storms of this hidden country had begun, at last, to recognize her.

The lead warden took one step back. Then another.

And high above the defile, where no thunder had sounded since the stars went out, the sky drew one long, silent breath — waiting to see what its keeper would do next.
