## 17. The Door of the Thirteenth Moon

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain on the last day of the climb.

Odile had felt it first as a wrongness in her boots — the trail going too smooth beneath them, stone worn into steps no mason had cut, each one exactly the height of a tired traveler's stride. Then the switchbacks began to repeat themselves, not because they'd lost the way but because the way had lost interest in variety. The same juniper, the same split boulder, the same view down into a valley that should have been three valleys further behind them by now.

"It's folding," Xanthe said quietly, from two paces back. Their voice had been quiet for days, ever since the wound in their shoulder had closed over with skin too smooth and too pale, ever since the confession Odile still carried like a splinter worked toward her heart. "The road is folding us forward. We're being *invited*."

"Mountains don't invite."

"This isn't doing either of us any favors pretending to be one."

Above them, through the thin gray ceiling of cloud, the moon hung at its thirteenth gibbous — swollen past full and then swelling again, a shape that had no name in any almanac Odile had ever smuggled across a border. It had been gaining light all week, and with every hour of that gain the Veil around them thickened like cream rising. She could taste it when she breathed: something between iron and old paper, the smell of a thing being pressed flat.

Behind them, eleven wardens climbed in silence. Six had knelt at Terms of Surrender; five had drawn steel and been disarmed rather than killed, because Odile had discovered somewhere in the long weeks inside the Veil that she was tired of spending lives she couldn't give back. All eleven walked with her now, bound by nothing but the thirteenth turn itself. When the moon came full, there would be nowhere left to stand that wasn't beside her or beneath what came after.

The remnant's captain, a gray-braided woman named Sarel who had once called Odile *Highness* through gritted teeth, caught up to her elbow as the false switchbacks steepened.

"The Door will show you what you want," Sarel said. It was not the first time she'd said it. "That's how it guards itself. Not walls. Wants."

"I know what I want."

"No one does." Sarel's voice was gentle in the way of people delivering verdicts. "That's the trap of it. You'll know when you see it. And you'll believe it was yours all along."

---

They camped that night in a hollow where the wind made no sound at all, and Odile did not sleep.

She sat with her back against stone and watched the impossible moon crawl, and she let herself do the thing she had refused to do since the archive beneath the ice: she counted her losses the way she used to count cargo before a run. Methodically. Without flinching. A smuggler's inventory.

One country, erased from every map and memory outside its own borders. One grandmother, dead twenty years, whose face she could no longer hold steady in her mind except as the woman in the ward-stone vision, hands raised over a border, speaking Odile's own name into the foundations of a cage. One mother, whose final night lived now only in the Hollow King's hoard — Odile had watched it twice, and both times had turned away from the moment of the end, unable to bear whatever it was her mother had said last, or whether it had been said to an empty room. One inheritance, offered by kneeling wardens and refused. One throne, empty. One storm-caller's power, reclaimed on her own terms and spent daily just keeping the silent storms from turning on the company like dogs remembering they were wolves.

And one cartographer, asleep ten feet away with their wounded shoulder uppermost, who had paid a childhood to the Hollow King so that Odile's secret would survive a toll neither of them had been able to refuse. Who had been sent to find her and had chosen her instead. Who had confessed it bleeding in the snow, and whom Odile had forgiven without saying so, and knew that neither of them would say it, because some things said aloud become debts and other things stay gifts precisely by staying unsaid.

*Everything you lost,* the warden's warnings promised, *the Door will offer back.*

She looked at the sleeping shapes of her company — smugglers and traitors and remnant wardens, a kingdom's worth of grief compressed into twelve travelers and a cartographer — and understood, finally, why the bargain had held for a hundred years. Not because anyone had been deceived. Because everyone who reached this point had lost too much to walk past a door that promised it all back.

She lay down and slept anyway, dreamless, like the dead, like the forgotten.

---

The Door stood at the top of a stair that had not existed yesterday.

It rose out of the mountain's summit like a tooth from a jaw: a frame of white stone tall as a watchtower, carved over every surface with moons — twelve of them, circling and recircling the frame's edges, and above the lintel a single thirteenth, blank, waiting to be filled. Through the frame Odile could see the far side of the sky. Not another country. Not Thornfell. Just sky, deep and starless and patient, as though the Door opened onto the underside of everything.

No stars anywhere. That was the thing that made her breath catch. Even here, even at the hinge of the bargain, the sky was the same starless iron it had been since the night the wards shattered — and she realized with a lurch that the missing stars had never gone out at all. They had been *taken*. Folded into the Veil like coins into a miser's fist, held as surety against the debt her family had signed.

The thirteenth turn was hours from full. The blank moon above the lintel already glowed faintly, filling like a cup.

"We wait," Sarel said, and the wardens spread out along the summit ridge with the practiced economy of soldiers who had guarded this approach for a generation and never once been needed.

Xanthe came to stand beside her at the base of the stair, and for a while neither of them spoke. The wind up here had a texture Odile recognized from her childhood — the high-pass wind, the one the old songs called the *shepherd's hand*, that pushed travelers gently home. It smelled of pine resin and woodsmoke and cold granite, and it smelled of home, and she made herself notice that it smelled of exactly what she wanted it to smell of, and distrusted it completely.

"You're doing the inventory face," Xanthe said.

"I'm always doing the inventory face."

"Not like this. This is the one you do when the numbers don't balance." They turned, and their eyes were steady and sad and entirely unfooled. "Whatever it shows you in there — whatever face it wears, whatever it offers — I want you to remember something first."

"Say it, then."

"When your grandmother sealed the border, the spell spoke your name. You told me that yourself. A hundred years the bargain has waited for a Delacroix to come renew it, and the moment it learned your name it started building this invitation — the folded road, the shepherd's wind, all of it. Everything we've walked through for a week has been the Door rehearsing its argument." Xanthe's voice dropped. "So when it makes its offer, and it will be a good one, remember: it has known about you longer than you've known about it. You're not walking into a test. You're walking into a courtship."

Odile huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You make it sound like I'm being proposed to."

"Aren't you?" Xanthe shrugged with their good shoulder. "A country asking. It's the oldest proposal there is. Half the tragedies in my archives start with someone saying yes to a land."

They stood together in silence after that, and it was a good silence, the kind they had learned to share across a hundred miles of erased roads, and Odile thought: *this*. This is the thing it cannot counterfeit. Whatever waits beyond that stair can give me back my grandmother's laugh and my mother's last words and a throne warm from a century of waiting, but it cannot give me this — someone who learned me on the road, at cost, without being built to.

The blank moon filled.

---

It happened slowly and then all at once, the way storms do.

The glow above the lintel brightened until it hurt to look at, and the twelve carved moons along the frame began, one by one, to turn — stone grinding against stone with a sound like a vault opening somewhere under the world — and the air through the Door's frame changed. The starless sky on the far side darkened, gathered, and then bloomed: a night sky dense with stars, more stars than Odile had ever seen, wheeling slowly around a single fixed point directly beyond the threshold.

*Those are ours,* she thought, sick with recognition. *Every star it stole. It keeps them here, past the door, and it's showing them to me.*

And standing in the frame, silhouetted against the stolen sky, was her grandmother.

Not a likeness. Not a memory given flesh. It was Maren Delacroix entire — small and straight-backed, silver hair braided crown-wise in the Thornfell style, wearing the storm-warden's gray cloak with the lightning-work at the hem that Odile had last seen folded in a trunk in a house that no longer existed. Her hands were folded the way she had folded them when listening to petitioners. Her face wore the expression Odile had spent twenty years trying to reconstruct from other people's descriptions: severe, amused, and underneath both, endlessly tired.

"Odile," her grandmother said, and her voice was exactly right, low and dry as August thunder. "You took the long way home."

Odile's knees nearly went. She locked them. Behind her she heard the wardens sink down — kneeling, all of them, even Sarel, even the five who had drawn steel — and she heard Xanthe stay standing, which was the only sound in the world that kept her upright.

"You're not her," Odile managed.

"I am everything she saved of herself into the bargain." The figure descended one step. Two. "Her duty. Her love for this country. Her regret — oh, child, you have no idea how much regret fits inside a seal. Come and see. I have kept it all for you. I have kept *everything*."

"I watched you seal the border." Odile forced the words up through the tightness in her chest. "In the ward-stone. You spoke my name into the spell. Why? If you were sealing a cage, why write your granddaughter into the lock?"

"Because a lock wants a key, and a bargain wants a keeper, and blood is the only ink that holds." Another step. Close enough now that Odile could see the fine lines around her eyes, the small scar on her chin Odile had heard stories of but never seen. Every detail correct. Every detail *offered*. "I hid you, little storm. I bent your exile into the spell so that the ones who would use you could never find you, and so that someday, if the country needed unbinding, the one person alive with the right to unbind it would be the one person no one thought to guard. You were never cast out, Odile. You were *cached*. Like a coin sewn into a coat lining."

"And now you want me to sew myself back in."

"Now I want to give you back what you were owed." The guardian raised one hand, and the sky behind it moved. Stars streamed down out of the frame like snow falling upward in reverse, and where they landed the summit meadow changed — and Odile's heart broke cleanly, horribly, in half.

Because she knew this place. The high pasture above Veymar where she'd been born. The crooked fence her father had mended every spring and cursed every autumn. The smell of the kitchen garden, the particular rattle of the shutter in her mother's window, and — coming faintly up the slope — a voice singing a lullaby in the lowland dialect, a voice she had not heard since she was nine years old and had convinced herself she had forgotten on purpose.

Her mother. Alive in the Door's offering. Alive the way the stars were alive in there: kept, preserved, held as surety against a signature.

"Everything you lost," the guardian said gently. "Your mother's last night, played whole, with nothing to turn away from. Your grandmother's counsel, whenever you want it, forever. Your name restored in every mouth beyond the Veil. Your storms returned in full — not these silent shadows, but the true ones, the ones that made Thornfell's wardens feared from the coast to the ice. And the throne, Odile. Warm and waiting. Renew the bargain, take the crown, and Thornfell endures — safe, hidden, *whole*, the way it was meant to be. No one else has to be forgotten. No one else has to lose anything, ever again. Isn't that what you've wanted since the night the stars went out?"

And the terrible thing — the thing Odile knew she would carry whether she said yes or no — was that it *was*. Every word of it. She wanted her mother's voice and her country's memory and a power that answered her like kin. She wanted the exile undone so thoroughly that it had never happened. The offer fit her grief the way a key fits its lock, because it had been cut from it.

Desire dressed as destiny. Xanthe had warned her, and the warning hadn't helped at all, because knowing a dress is a costume doesn't stop you from wanting to wear it.

"That's not renewal," Odile said slowly. "That's ransom. You keep the dead on layaway and charge the living rent."

"I keep what was *given*," the guardian said, and for the first time something moved behind its eyes — something vast and patient and hungry, looking out through Maren Delacroix's tired, loving face like a keeper looking through a keyhole. "Your blood gave it. Will you not come and claim your inheritance, Odile Delacroix, last warden of Thornfell? The moon is nearly full. Speak the renewal, and everything I hold returns to you. Refuse—" the smile did not change, but the warmth drained out of the valley below like water from a cracked bowl "—and everything I hold stays mine. Including the parts of you I've already begun to keep."

The blank moon above the lintel shuddered, filling faster now, and Odile felt the Veil tightening around them all like a fist closing, and she understood that the choice had never been between her losses and her freedom.

It was between her losses and everyone else's.

Behind her, boots on stone — Xanthe climbing the stair, slow, deliberate, refusing to hurry her, refusing to leave her alone in it. Odile drew one breath of the shepherd's wind, the false sweet wind of home, and stepped forward to meet her grandmother's eyes.

"Then hear my terms," she said.

* * *

The guardian tilted its head, and the stolen stars wheeled, and somewhere below the summit the silent storms — her storms, the country's power waiting all these years for its keeper — began, very softly, to move.

"Terms," the guardian repeated, tasting the word. "A smuggler's word. How like your line."

"You wrote me into the lock," Odile said. "So listen to the key."
