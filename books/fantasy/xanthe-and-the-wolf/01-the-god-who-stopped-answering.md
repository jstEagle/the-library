## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The rite came to Elodie's hands before it ever reached his heart.

He woke in the blue hour before dawn, as he always did, though the cold had crept into the hollow of the rock he'd sheltered beneath and turned his breath to smoke. Thornfell did not forgive late risers. The mountains had their own liturgy — frost on stone, wind through the pines like a congregation clearing its throat — and Elodie Eriksen had memorized every verse of it across eleven years of guiding other people's sins over the passes.

He knelt. He did not think about kneeling; his knees simply found the frozen ground the way a compass needle finds north.

*Vael, who hears without listening, who speaks without speaking, keeper of the quiet between words.* His fingers moved through the old gestures: thumb to brow, where thought begins; thumb to lips, where truth is weighed; thumb pressed flat against the sternum, where silence lives in every living thing. *I bring you my morning as I bring you nothing else. Take what you need of it.*

The words were worn smooth as river stones. He'd said them ten thousand times, first as a boy in a chapel with whitewashed walls and a mother who believed hard enough for the both of them, then as an acolyte, then as a soldier of the faith when the faith still fielded soldiers, and now — for seven years now — as this: a man alone on a mountain at the edge of the world, performing devotion the way a man winds a watch he no longer trusts to tell time.

He waited.

That was the part no one told you about praying to Vael. Everyone imagined the god of silence simply didn't answer. But there was answering and there was *answering*. In the old days — in the days before whatever day it was that broke — you could feel Him the way you felt weather coming. A pressure behind the ribs. A hush that wasn't empty but *attentive*, the hush of a room where someone has stopped breathing so they can hear you better. Novices wept their first winter just from standing in it. Elodie remembered weeping. He remembered the particular ache of being *heard* by something vast and patient and kind.

Now there was nothing. Not absence exactly — absence would have been honest, would have let him grieve cleanly and move on. It was worse than absence. It was the feeling of shouting down a well and knowing, without knowing how he knew, that somewhere far below the water had been drained. That the shaft went deeper than any well had a right to go, and ended in a door, and the door was shut, and no amount of prayer would knock loud enough.

Elodie finished the rite. He rose, brushed the frost from his knees, and allowed himself three seconds of grief — he counted them, the way he counted everything, because counting kept things small — before he turned to the practical business of staying alive.

* * *

The client's name was Ferro Vasq, and Elodie had distrusted him from the moment they met in the low town of Hallowmere, where the smugglers drank and the fences whispered and everyone smiled one tooth too many. Vasq was a salt merchant on paper, which meant nothing; half the trade running the Thornfell passes wore paper faces. He had soft hands, a ledger he never opened twice, and eyes that flicked to Elodie's sword belt the way a gambler's eyes flick to another man's cards.

Six of them made the crossing party: Vasq, four hired porters with the flat affect of men paid to not ask questions, and Elodie out front reading the mountain like scripture.

And the mountain was saying things that morning.

Thornfell spoke in a grammar of small wrongnesses. A cairn knocked flat by wind meant weather; cairns disturbed by hand meant soldiers or worse. Birds gone quiet meant predators. But there were subtler verses. Snow that held last night's tracks too long. A pass that smelled of iron instead of pine. The particular quality of silence that fell over a stretch of trail when the mountain itself seemed to be holding its breath around some old wound in the rock — and Thornfell was full of old wounds. Every guide knew them by feel if not by name. There was a valley two days north where compasses spun. A scree field south of the Serpent's Stair where no echo came back, where your own footsteps fell dead as coins dropped on cloth. Elodie had spent years learning which silences meant danger and which merely meant the world remembering something it preferred to forget.

Today the wrongness was simpler: Vasq's porters kept looking uphill. Not at the trail. Uphill, toward the ridgeline, with the quick guilty glances of men checking on a friend they shouldn't have.

Elodie filed it away. He filed most things away. That was the whole craft, really — anyone could walk a goat path; the work was in noticing what the mountain tried to hide, and in keeping your own face as blank as fresh snowfall while you did it.

They camped that night in the lee of a granite shoulder called the Widow's Tooth, a spire of black rock that caught lightning in summer and shed avalanches in spring. Elodie built the fire small and screened, cooked barley and dried mutton, and watched Vasq count his cargo by firelight — sealed crates, ostensibly salt, heavy enough that the porters' shoulders bled through their shirts.

"Good pace today," Vasq said, with a smile that arrived a beat after his mouth started moving. "I've heard you're the best on these slopes, Eriksen."

"I'm alive," Elodie said. "Best is a title for gravestones."

"And modest."

"Expensive." He nodded at the crates. "Salt doesn't usually bleed its carriers."

Vasq's smile stayed fixed while everything behind it went still, the way a pond goes still when a heron lands. "Trade secrets."

"Mountains don't keep many. They just wait for you to volunteer yours." Elodie banked the fire and lay back against his pack, hands folded over his chest in the posture of a sleeping man and the readiness of a coiled one. Above him the stars burned in their ancient indifference, and somewhere beyond them — or beneath everything — Vael kept His silence, and Elodie kept his own, and between the two of them the night passed without a word.

He woke once, deep in the dark, to the sound of whispering. He didn't move. He listened to Vasq murmur with two of the porters in a tongue that wasn't salt-trade cant, and he listened to the specific cadence of a plan being finalized, and then he closed his eyes and slept anyway, because sleep was a tool like any other and tomorrow would want him sharp.

* * *

The ambush came at the Narrows, an hour past dawn, exactly where the trail pinched between a cliff face and a drop into white cloud, and exactly when Elodie had predicted it would come — not because he was clever, but because double-crosses are lazy creatures and always choose the same ground. Narrow trail. No retreat. A buyer already waiting on the far side to receive stolen goods off a dead guide's ledger.

There were five of them, not four. They'd left one behind at the Widow's Tooth to make sure the fire died with him, and that man was currently discovering that Elodie's "sleeping" posture kept a knife within reach of his right hand, and that the knife had opened his throat before he finished standing up. Elodie felt nothing about it. That was the trick nobody liked to say aloud: killing got easy fast, and the guilt you were promised afterward mostly failed to arrive. You saved the guilt for smaller things.

Ahead, the four came up the trail in loose order, crossbows shouldered, and Vasq hung back behind them wearing the expression of a man attending a business meeting he'd prepared for.

"Eriksen." Vasq spread his soft hands. "Nothing personal. The map of your routes is worth more than the cargo, frankly. You've walked these passes for a decade and never once written anything down — do you know what that's worth? Every lord on both sides of the range wants what's in your head. And dead men's heads," he added pleasantly, "can't negotiate royalties."

"You're going to shoot me and take my memory?" Elodie looked at the crossbows. "Bold economics."

"We're going to shoot you and take your *cargo*, and your reputation will do the rest. Guides die in Thornfell all the time. The mountain gets the blame. Nobody asks the mountain questions." Vasq sighed, almost kindly. "Kneel facing the drop. It's quicker if you don't see it."

Here was the thing about the Narrows: the cliff face on the inland side was not solid. Eleven years of walking this trail had taught Elodie what no survey map showed — that a shelf of granite there had cracked away from the mother rock in some forgotten quake, leaving a gap wide enough for a lean man and his pack, hidden behind a curtain of hanging frost-fern and dead ivy. He'd found it in his first winter, starving, fleeing men with crossbows of his own, and he'd sworn then that he'd never show it to a living soul.

He hadn't. Even now he didn't show it. He simply stepped backward into it, mid-sentence, like a man stepping through a doorway in his own house.

The first bolt buried itself in the space where his chest had been. The second sparked off stone. Then the cliff swallowed him and the mountain went quiet around his shallow breathing, and through the curtain of dead ivy he watched the four men argue, search, panic, and finally — because men like this always did — decide the drop had taken him and move on with the cargo, Vasq cursing the waste of a good route-map in a head now presumably decorating the rocks below.

Elodie waited until the sound of them faded. Then he laughed, once, short and joyless, a laugh with no humor in it at all — because here was the joke, the joke he'd been living for years: he trusted the mountain more than he trusted God. The mountain at least answered. The mountain lied sometimes, killed often, but it *answered* — every ridge and hollow a sentence in a language he'd bothered to learn. While Vael, who had taken his childhood, his vocation, his vows, and his mother's dying certainty, gave him nothing. Seven years of rites performed into a drained well.

He climbed down out of the hiding place, retrieved his knife, and stood a moment at the edge of the cloud-filled drop, looking down into all that white nothing.

"Fine," he said aloud, to the god who didn't listen, in the voice of a man settling a bar tab. "You keep Your silence. I'll keep mine. We're even."

The silence, as always, declined to disagree.

He turned back up the trail — his trail, the only country he'd ever owned — and began the long climb toward the high pass and the rendezvous he no longer intended to honor. Behind him, the Widow's Tooth caught the early light and burned black and gold, and ahead, on the wind, came something he couldn't yet name: a smell like hot iron and old blood, drifting down from the heights where nothing human should have been.

Elodie Eriksen adjusted his pack, put his hand near his knife, and started walking toward it — because in Thornfell, when the mountain offered you a warning, the only sin worse than ignoring it was wasting it.
