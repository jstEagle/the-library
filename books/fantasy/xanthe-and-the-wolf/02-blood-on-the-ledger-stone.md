## 2. Blood on the Ledger Stone

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time the stranger came out of the dark.

Elodie heard him first — not footsteps, which the mountains could swallow whole, but breathing. A wet, torn sound, like wind through a broken shutter. He was on his feet with his knife drawn before his mind caught up to his body, twenty years of Thornfell habit moving him faster than thought. His camp sat in a hollow below the Ledger Stone — a slab of grey granite where smugglers for three generations had scratched their debts and their dead men's names — and there was only one path down into it, narrow as a throat. Whatever came down that throat would come slowly or not at all.

The stranger came slowly.

He was tall, or had been; pain had folded him in half so that he seemed to pour himself down the slope rather than walk it. Grey cloak, road-stained past any color. One hand pressed to his side, and even in the firelight's thin reach Elodie could see that the fingers were black to the knuckle. The wound beneath them had been bound, and bound again, and the binding had given up hours ago.

"Stop there," Elodie said. He kept his voice flat, the voice he used for clients and customs men alike. "This camp is spoken for."

The man lifted his head. His face was long and hollowed, forty years old at least, with the pale look of someone who had spent too long indoors and then too long running from what waited indoors. His eyes found Elodie's and held them with a terrible steadiness, the way drowning men are said to look calmly at the surface from underneath.

"You're a guide," the man said. Not a question. "They said — in Marrowgate, they said there was a man who could take anything through Thornfell and Vael would let him pass." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought blood to his lips, a thin dark thread of it. "Vael. Let him pass."

"I don't know who told you that, but they sold you old stories." Elodie did not lower the knife. "Turn around. There's nothing here for you. The nearest shrine is two days east."

"There's no time for shrines." The stranger took another step, swaying, and now Elodie could see the wound properly: a slash low across the belly, deep, deliberate, made by someone who knew how to cut a man so that death would take its leisure arriving. "Listen to me. Listen. They're behind me. Four of them, maybe five. They'll reach this pass before dawn."

"Inquisitors?"

Something moved through the stranger's face at that word — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition, perhaps, of how far his pursuers' shadow reached, that even a smuggler in the high passes spoke the word without being told who was meant.

"They'll ask if anyone came through," the man said. "You'll tell them no."

"I'll tell them whatever keeps me alive."

"Yes," the stranger said. "Yes. That's what I hoped you'd say." And he smiled — a small, ruined smile, the smile of a man who has finally set down something heavy — and then his legs went out from under him and he fell against the Ledger Stone itself, sliding down it until he sat in the snow with the old carved names rising above his head like a congregation.

Elodie should have turned away then. He knew that. Every rule of his trade said turn away: no names, no debts, no dying men in your camp to bring the law down on your head. He had left better people than this in worse places. He told himself he was only waiting, watching, making sure the man wouldn't die loudly enough to draw wolves.

He crouched beside him instead.

"What's your name?" he asked, and immediately regretted it, because names were debts, and he did not want this man's debt.

The stranger didn't answer. His hands were working at the front of his coat, clumsy, black-tipped fingers fumbling at some inner fold, and after a moment he drew out a rectangle of oilcloth wrapped tight and tied with waxed cord. He pressed it into Elodie's hands with both of his, and his grip, though fading, was astonishingly strong — the grip of a man holding onto the last thing that mattered.

"The door out of Cindral," he whispered.

The word meant nothing. Less than nothing. Cindral. Elodie turned it over in his mind the way one turns over a coin from a country one has never traded with, and found no face stamped on either side. "I don't know any Cindral."

"No," the stranger said, and his eyes were going glassy, but the urgency in them burned brighter even as everything else dimmed. "No one does. That's the point. That's the whole — " Another cough, wetter than the last. "Keep it. Don't sell it. Don't sell it to anyone in Marrowgate, they're watched, all of them, every broker is theirs — " His hand tightened. "There's a woman. If a woman comes asking. Dark hair, young, she may be — she may not look like — " He stopped. Started again, slower, each word laid down like a stone in a wall. "If she comes, give it to her. Only her. Promise me."

"I don't promise things."

"Promise me." And then, when Elodie said nothing: "It doesn't matter. It doesn't matter what you promise. You'll give it to her anyway. You have the look of a man who buries what he can't keep." The smile came back, fainter. "That's a good look. That's the best look there is."

Elodie wanted to say something cutting, something that would put distance between himself and this dying stranger and his impossible words, but the man's eyes had drifted upward to the Ledger Stone, to the scratched names above his head, and he read them the way a priest reads scripture, lips moving, though the letters were smugglers' shorthand and half of them were misspelled.

"They had names too," the stranger murmured. "Everything has a name, once. Everything starts with a name."

Then he shuddered once, deeply, like a horse shaking off rain, and was still.

Elodie stayed crouched there for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere above, wind moved through the pines with a sound like slow applause. He put two fingers against the stranger's throat and found nothing, and stood, and looked down at the oilcloth package in his hands as though it might explain itself.

It didn't. It weighed almost nothing. It felt warm, though that was surely the stranger's grip lingering in the cloth.

*Don't sell it.* Everyone sold things. Everything was for sale; that was the first law of the mountains and the second law of the world. But the man had died with the warmth still in his hands, whispering about a door out of a country that did not exist, and something in Elodie — some buried, starved thing that he had spent years pretending was dead — stirred and would not lie back down.

He buried the stranger at dawn, before the light came up enough to make him visible from the ridgelines. The ground was half-frozen and it took an hour with the knife and a flat stone for a spade, and Elodie worked in silence because prayer was a habit he had been trying to break for three years and he refused, absolutely refused, to start again over a nameless corpse.

He failed. Halfway through the burial, without deciding to, he heard himself murmur the old words — *Vael keep you, Vael keep your silence, Vael open the quiet door* — and hated himself for it, and kept saying them anyway, because his mouth had learned them before his heart had learned anything else, and mouths are loyal to what raised them.

He marked the grave with a small cairn and, after a moment's consideration, scratched no name on it. *You'll give it to her anyway,* the man had said. Elodie looked at the blank stone and thought: we'll see.

Then he climbed back to his camp and built the fire up, and unwrapped the map.

* * *

They came at midmorning.

He smelled the horses first — that particular iron-and-dung smell of army mounts, different from traders' animals — and had the fire drowned and the map back inside his coat and himself seated innocently on the Ledger Stone mending a bootlace before the riders rounded the bend into the hollow.

Five of them. Four soldiers in the usurper's black and silver, carbines slung across their backs, faces raw with cold and the particular boredom of men sent to search places where nothing was ever found. The fifth rode slightly ahead, and was not a soldier.

He wore grey. Not the road-grey of travel but the grey of ash, of closed doors, of ink dried on an official seal. His coat was cut close and buttoned high, and he was clean-shaven in a way that no one in the mountains ever managed, and he looked at Elodie's camp — the drowned fire, the bedroll, the mule tethered among the trees — the way an accountant looks at a column of figures, checking each one against some inner ledger.

"Good morning," the grey man said. His voice was mild. Mildness, in Elodie's experience, was far worse than shouting.

"Morning." Elodie did not stand. Standing was deference, and deference invited questions. He went on working the bootlace, pulling the waxed thread tight with his teeth.

"You're alone?"

"For my sins."

The grey man dismounted. Up close he was younger than he'd seemed at a distance, thirty perhaps, with fine bones and tired eyes, and a small silver pin at his collar shaped like an eye with the lid drawn across it. Elodie had seen the pin once before, years ago, on a man in Marrowgate who had asked questions about a shipment and been answered with lies, and the shipment had crossed the border safely and the man had never been seen again.

"My name is Hale," the grey man said. "Inquisitor Hale. I'm looking for a fugitive. Tall man, grey cloak, wounded — a wound to the abdomen. He passed through Marrowgate four days ago wearing another man's papers." He paused. "Did he come through this pass?"

Elodie felt fear arrive in his body before it arrived anywhere else — a cold drop through the stomach, a sudden awareness of his own heartbeat. He had faced bandits, avalanches, a bear once, and a client with a crossbow and a grievance, and none of it had felt like this. This was the fear of a man standing on ice and hearing it speak.

And underneath the fear, stranger and more alarming, was a second feeling: the memory of the stranger's hand pressing the oilcloth into his, warm and strong and certain. *You have the look of a man who buries what he can't keep.*

"No one's come through," Elodie said.

It was, he realized a moment later, the first lie of his entire adult life. He had lied by omission plenty — silence was currency in his trade, and he spent it freely. But words, deliberate words arranged to mislead? Never. His mother had beaten lying out of him early and thoroughly, and Vael's rite demanded truth in the morning prayer — *speak straight, walk straight, the silent god hears crooked tongues* — and somewhere under all the cynicism that old training still ran like bedrock.

He had just lied to an inquisitor, and the world had not ended, and the lie had come out smooth as water.

Hale studied him. It was not an aggressive study; that was the unsettling part. He looked at Elodie with something close to sympathy, the way a doctor looks at a patient whose symptoms he already knows.

"That's a fresh grave," Hale said.

Elodie followed the man's gaze to the cairn by the treeline, small and neat, the stones still crusted with turned earth. He cursed himself with a fluency that would have shocked his mother. Of course. Of course the ground gave him away; the ground always gave everyone away eventually; that was why he buried nothing and burned everything.

"Smuggler," Elodie said. He let disgust into his voice, and found, to his surprise, that he didn't have to manufacture much of it. "One of mine. Took a fall on the scree coming down from the col. Broken neck. I buried him because leaving bodies draws wolves, and wolves follow camps, and I have cargo to move." He jerked his chin toward the cairn. "Want to dig him up and check his neck?"

A flicker of distaste crossed the faces of the soldiers. Hale didn't blink.

"If it's all the same to you," he said mildly, "we will."

They dug. Elodie watched them do it, sitting on the Ledger Stone with his bootlace in his hands, and his heart hammered so hard he was sure they could hear it, sure it would shake loose the loose stones, sure that under the frozen earth they would find the wound in the dead man's belly and know the fall story for the lie it was. The soldiers worked clumsily in the frozen ground, swearing softly, and Hale stood apart with his hands clasped behind his back, watching Elodie watch them.

"He was a friend of yours?" Hale asked.

"No one in the mountains is anyone's friend."

"A philosophy." Hale nodded slowly, as if filing it. "Does it work?"

"So far."

"It fails eventually," Hale said. "Everything I've seen says so. People decide someone matters, and then the philosophy breaks, and they do something foolish and brave and final." He smiled a little. It was a kind smile. It frightened Elodie more than any threat could have. "I say this as a man whose profession consists of waiting for that moment."

The soldiers reached the body. They pulled back the frozen cloak, and Elodie made himself watch, made his face into the bored mask of a man who had seen corpses before and expected to see more, while inside him everything screamed. But the strangers had rewrapped the body hastily in the dark, winding the cloak around it twice, and the wound was hidden, and after a moment one of the soldiers grunted — "Broken neck, right enough, head's sitting wrong" — and they shoveled the earth back and stamped it down and that was that.

Hale looked at the grave for a long moment. Then he looked at Elodie, and the mildness in his face was terrible, because it was the mildness of a man who suspects everything and proves nothing and will simply never stop looking.

"Strange thing," Hale said. "The man we're hunting stole something. Something small. Papers, a map, something of that nature. Worthless to anyone but a fool." He began, unhurriedly, to pull off his gloves finger by finger. "Fools worry me less than clever men, guide. Fools run in straight lines. But a clever man finds a fool's trail interesting, and interesting things make clever men ask questions, and questions are my trade." He pulled the second glove free and tucked it in his belt. "So I'll ask you just once, and then we'll ride on, and you can go back to your laces and your philosophy. Did anything pass through this pass in the last two days besides you and your dead friend? Anything at all?"

The morning was very still. Snow lay on the pine boughs without dripping. High overhead a bird — some small brown thing Elodie had known all his life in these mountains — sang four clear notes and stopped mid-phrase, as though it had forgotten the rest.

Elodie met the inquisitor's tired, kind, bottomless eyes.

"Not a thing," he said. "Not a living soul."

Hale held his gaze for five heartbeats, ten. Then he nodded, replaced one glove, and swung back into the saddle. "May Vael keep your roads quiet, guide."

"And yours," Elodie said, because it was the customary reply, and because the alternative was silence, and silence suddenly felt like confession.

They rode north along the pass, hooves crunching, until the bend of the mountain took them, and the sound of them faded, and was gone.

Elodie sat on the Ledger Stone for a long time afterward. His hands were steady — his hands were always steady, that was the joke of him, the man with the surgeon's hands and the coward's heart — but his breath came ragged, and when he finally reached inside his coat and touched the oilcloth package, it was as warm as a sleeping bird.

*The door out of Cindral.*

He should burn it. He knew that with total clarity. Burn it, scatter the ashes, ride west and take a contract on the coast road and never think of grey cloaks or mild voices again. That was survival. That was the philosophy.

Instead he sat until the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and then he took out the map, and unfolded one corner of it, just one corner, just to see —

—and froze.

Because there, pressed into the corner of the oldest, softest parchment, in ink gone brown as old blood, was a seal he knew better than his own heartbeat. An eye with the lid drawn closed.

Vael's mark. His god's own sigil, stamped on a map leading into a country that did not exist.

The bird overhead tried its song again, got three notes in, and stopped.

Elodie folded the map away very carefully, the way one handles something that might be holy or might be cursed, and began, slowly, to pack his camp.
