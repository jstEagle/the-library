## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

The storm came down off the high shoulders of Thornfell an hour after dark, and Elodie built his fire in the lee of a fallen pine the way his mother had taught him before she had any reason to regret it: small, screened, and mean with its light. A smuggler's fire was a confession written in orange. Anyone who saw it knew you were there; anyone who *hunted* you knew where to aim.

So he made it small. He crouched over it anyway, hands out, because the cold above the tree line went through wool and leather and resolve alike, and because the dead man's map was folded into his inner pocket like a second heart, and hearts needed warmth whether they beat or not.

He hadn't looked at it yet.

That was deliberate. He told himself it was caution — that you didn't open a stranger's dying gift on an open trail with inquisitors' lanterns still swinging somewhere behind you — but honesty, which Elodie rationed like hardtack, would have said something else. He hadn't looked at it because once he looked at it, he would have to decide what it was. And he had spent six years learning how to survive the space between prayers, and none of that survival had prepared him for deciding things about gods or dead men's countries.

Now there was nothing left between him and the decision except firelight and rain.

He took it out.

* * *

It was good vellum. That was the first wrong thing. Smugglers' maps were drawn on waxed canvas or cheap rag paper, annotated in pencil, corrected in ink, crossed through and re-crossed until they looked like arguments. This was vellum, smooth as skin — and Elodie ran his thumb along its edge and did not let himself think about what kind of skin vellum was — and it was covered in a cartographer's hand so fine and so certain that the lines seemed less drawn than *grown*, the way frost grows on glass, following laws no one had to explain.

The mountains were right. That was the second wrong thing. Elodie knew Thornfell the way other men knew their own scars: every pass, every scree chute, every place the stone held a footprint and every place it shrugged one off. The map showed all of it, and showed it true, and then kept going — east, past the last ridge he had ever walked, past the border stones the treaty-masons had cut twenty years ago and the ones older than treaties beneath them — and there it simply stopped.

Not faded. Not torn. *Stopped.*

Where Cindral should have been, the vellum was blank. Not empty the way untouched paper is empty; empty the way a room is empty after the furniture has been carried out and the marks are still on the floor. Elodie tilted the map toward the fire and saw them: ghost-lines, faint as breath on a mirror. Roads that ran confidently out of the living world and into the blankness and then — nothing. Rivers that began mid-course, as if waking from a dream of having sources. One road, wider than the rest, running arrow-straight toward the heart of the nothing, ending at a circle no bigger than his thumbnail.

Roads don't end. Roads are promises, and even broken roads keep the shape of the promise. But these ended the way a sentence ends when the speaker is taken by the throat.

Elodie sat back on his heels and listened to the rain work the pine boughs overhead, drip and tick and hush, and told himself the obvious thing: someone had erased Cindral from this map. That happened. It had happened everywhere else, after the war — the name scraped from ledgers, chiseled off boundary stones, struck from the almanacs and the prayer calendars and the mouths of careful people. He'd been a boy when it happened. He remembered adults going quiet around him, the way they did around graves, and he remembered asking where Cindral was, once, and his mother pressing her hand flat over his mouth hard enough to bruise.

So yes. Someone had scraped the country off the map. Except —

Except that scrapers leave scars. Ink leaves ghosts under the blade. You could sand vellum, but the sanding showed. This wasn't scraped. This was as if the mapmaker had set brush to vellum intending to draw the land and the land had simply refused to be drawn — refused the way a sleeping man refuses to wake, whole and closed and *elsewhere*.

And there was a third thing. He turned the map over, because good maps carry their maker's mark on the back, and found the seal.

It was pressed into the vellum, not stamped — pressed deep, deep enough that he could feel it with a fingertip, a depression in the skin of the thing. And Elodie Eriksen, who had prayed to Vael every morning for nineteen years and every morning for six of those years had heard exactly nothing back, knew the sigil the way you know your own handwriting.

The closed eye. The vertical line beneath it, straight as a plumb bob. The god of silence, of thresholds, of the word not yet spoken — drawn by some dead hand into the hide of a dead country's map.

Vael had no nations. That was catechism, first lesson, taught to children before they learned to tie their own laces. *The Silent One keeps no borders, for every threshold is His.* Vael's priests served waystations and crossroads and the doors of the dying. Vael's sigil appeared on hospice lintels and gallows beams and the little shrines smugglers like Elodie raised at the mouths of dangerous passes. It did not appear on national seals. It had never appeared on a national seal.

But there it was. The closed eye, watching the blank space where a country used to be. Or — and the thought arrived with a cold that had nothing to do with the storm — guarding it.

*The door out of Cindral,* the stranger had said, with the blood already pooling under him, already black in the firelight. *You have to — it's the door out—*

Out. Not of a city. Not of a siege. Out of a *country*. As if a country were a room, and rooms have doors, and doors can be shut from either side.

Elodie fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch, and made himself think like a smuggler, because thinking like anything else hurt.

* * *

Here is what a thing like that was worth.

Not the map itself — maps of Thornfell he could draw from memory, and half the passes changed every spring flood anyway. Worth was in what the map proved. Blank space on official charts was old news; every broker in Marrowgate had heard the rumor of unmarked valleys where tax collectors couldn't follow. But a *cartographer's* map, guild-grade, showing the exact contour of the erasure — showing where the blankness began and where, presumably, it ended — that was a key. That was the difference between a rumor and a route. Somewhere beyond that thumbnail circle might be arable land, silver seams, hot springs, a valley the usurper's census had never touched. People paid in crowns for such things. People had died for less, and Elodie had watched some of them die, and once or twice had helped.

He thought about the fence he'd use. Old Sarelle in Marrowgate, three fingers missing on her left hand, who weighed everything twice and trusted nothing once. She'd take it to her buyers, the ones with soft voices and hard purses, the antiquarians and the exiles and the men whose money smelled of banks instead of blood. Ten percent up front. Maybe fifteen, if he played the mystery right, if he said *I can't tell you where I got it* with the right flat look, because mystery marked the price up faster than gold leaf ever could.

Fifteen percent of the kind of sum a guild-grade anomaly commanded was more coin than Elodie had seen in six years of guiding liars through the mountains. Enough to buy out of the life. Enough to go south, past the salt marshes, where nobody knew the name Eriksen and the mornings didn't start with a prayer he no longer believed in. He imagined it with the practiced ease of long habit: a house with a blue door, a stove that drew properly, mornings that belonged to no god and no client.

The fire popped. Rain ticked on the boughs.

And underneath the arithmetic, quiet as water finding a crack, ran the unease.

Because the seal bothered him. Because the roads bothered him — roads that promised and broke the promise, roads that ended like throats. Because the dying man had known his name. Elodie kept turning that over and it kept refusing to sit. The stranger had come out of the storm bleeding from a wound Elodie hadn't examined closely because examining it meant acknowledging the man was already past help, and he had gripped Elodie's wrist with the last strength in him and said *Elodie Eriksen* — said it plain, said it whole — before he said anything about doors.

Nobody out here knew his name. Clients knew *Whistler*, the trail-name, the trade-name, the convenient fiction. His mother was eleven years in the ground at Marrowgate under a stone with no surname, bought that way on purpose. He had spent six years sanding himself down to a man with no edges for anyone to grip, and a stranger dying in the mud had gripped him by his true name as if it had been written somewhere he couldn't see.

Written where? On what ledger? In whose hand?

He looked at the seal again. The closed eye. Six years of silence, and now the god's own mark turns up folded against his ribs, warm from his body heat, staring at nothing.

*Habit,* he told himself. *Coincidence. Grief makes patterns out of weather.* He had a whole liturgy of these sayings, worn smooth as river stones from handling. He picked one up and held it and found, for the first time, that it didn't comfort. It just sat there in his mind like the blank space on the map: shaped like something missing.

* * *

Toward midnight the storm thinned to a drizzle and the clouds tore open in the west, and moonlight came down the valley in a single wide sheet, silvering the wet granite, laying the peaks out bare and blue. Elodie stepped out from under the pine to check his backtrail — habit, always habit — and stood a long moment looking east.

There. Past the last ridge he knew. Where the world was supposed to continue into hills and high plateau and, eventually, the erased country, there was only darkness. Not the darkness of distance. He knew distance-darkness; it had texture, layers, the blue-grey receding of ridgeline behind ridgeline. This was different. This was darkness with an edge, clean as a curtain drawn across a stage, and his eyes kept trying to find the far side of it and kept failing, sliding off, coming back, like a tongue returning to a broken tooth.

Twenty years he had looked at that horizon and called it night, or haze, or the lie of his own tired eyes. Tonight, with the dead man's map inside his coat and the god's seal under his thumb, he looked at it and felt vertigo rise in him like water in a well. The mountains he trusted. The mountains had never lied to him. Which meant the lie was farther east, and it was the size of a nation, and he was standing close enough to it to feel it through his boot-soles — a hush, a pressure, like the silence in a house where someone has just stopped breathing.

He went back under the pine. He rolled the map with steady hands — steadier than he deserved — and thought about Sarelle's weighing-scales, and the blue door, and fifteen percent.

Sell it. Of course sell it. It was the only sane thing. Whatever the blankness was, it had killed the man who carried this map — or the men hunting him had, which came to nearly the same thing, because those men were still out there, still searching the passes, still swinging their lanterns along trails Elodie knew by heart. Carrying this map east was carrying a torch into a powder store. Selling it was selling someone else the torch. Let the antiquarians and the exiles and the soft-voiced buyers walk into the dark if the dark wanted company. Elodie had done his years of walking into dark places for other people's purposes. He was owed a blue door. He was owed mornings with no god in them.

He was owed, he thought — and the thought surprised him with its bitterness — one answer. Nineteen years of rites. Ash at dawn, the palm laid flat, the four spoken silences. Nineteen years, and six of them addressed to a god who had stopped answering, and never once — not a sign, not a dream, not a shift in the wind — had anything come back across that gulf. A man could live on that for a while, the way a man can live on hope, but both run out at the same speed, and Elodie's had run dry sometime in the third winter, and he had kept performing the rite afterward purely because stopping felt like a second death.

And now. Now a dying stranger knew his name, and a map wouldn't show a country, and the god's own eye was pressed into the vellum like a signature on a debt.

He almost laughed. It came out as a breath, a ghost of steam above the coals.

*Sell it,* he told himself firmly, folding the map along its old creases, creases someone else's hands had worn. *First light, down the Larkspur trail, two days to Marrowgate. Sell it, take the coin, go south.* He said it the way he'd once said prayers: rhythmically, completely, expecting nothing.

The fire burned low. The rain resumed, soft and businesslike, erasing the night's sounds one by one. And Elodie lay with his back to a stone and the map against his heart and did not sleep, because sleep would have meant closing his eyes, and his eyes, he suspected, were the only things in this mountainside currently doing the god's work — open, in the dark, watching the place where the world refused to be.

Near dawn, when the grey was just beginning to thin the black, he heard it.

A sound on the trail below. Deliberate. Unhurried. Four feet, placed softly, pausing — the particular cadence of something that was walking toward him and wanted him to know it, and was taking its time about the wanting.

Elodie's hand found his knife without his permission.

Down through the pines, at the edge of the firelight, at the exact line where warmth gives out and the dark begins, two eyes opened. Amber, level, unblinking. They did not shine like a beast's eyes in a torchbeam. They considered him.

A wolf, he thought. Too big. Too calm. Too far south of any range he knew.

It watched him the way the seal watched the blank country — and then, without hurry, it sat down at the border of his firelight, tucked its tail around its feet, and waited, as though it had all the time in the world and every intention of using it.
