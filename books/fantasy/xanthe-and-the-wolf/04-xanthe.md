## 4. Xanthe

The wolf had been hungry for eleven days when she first caught the smell of him on the wind.

Not the man himself — not yet — but his passage, written in the cold air of the Thornfells the way a scribe writes on vellum: woodsmoke and pine pitch and the sour iron of old blood, and beneath all of it something that made her stop mid-stride with her ears forward and her hackles lifting like grass before a storm.

Vael's incense. The bitter resin they burned in the silent god's shrines, the one Cindral had imported by the ton in the years before the world forgot her name. Her mother had kept a censer of it on the palace altar, and Xanthe had spent twenty years trying to scrub the smell from her memory without success, because memory was the one country the usurper could not yet burn.

She turned into the wind and ran.

* * *

He was camped in a fold of the high pass where the rock cupped firelight like a hand cupping water — a good spot, chosen by someone who knew these mountains the way she had once known the gardens of the Sunken Court, which was to say: intimately, and with love, and without ever having to think about it. A smuggler, then. Or a guide. One of the gray men who moved through the world's margins while kingdoms did their killing in the center.

She watched him from the treeline, belly low to the snow, breath held so long her chest ached.

He was younger than she expected. That annoyed her. She had built him in her head as an old man, gray and greedy, someone easy to hate — the kind of carrion-bird who picked up whatever fell out of dying Cindral and sold it to the highest bidder. But this one was perhaps thirty, with dark hair going early silver at the temples, and he sat with his back against a boulder and a blade across his knees in the manner of a man who slept badly and knew why.

And then he did something that made her forget, for a full ten heartbeats, to be angry at all.

He prayed.

There was no shrine up there. No altar, no icon, nothing but a flat stone at the edge of the fire and the enormous indifferent sky. He knelt anyway, and pressed his brow to the stone, and his lips moved through words too quiet even for wolf-ears. She read them more than heard them — the morning rite of Vael, the one every sworn servant of the silent god performed at dawn: *You who do not speak, teach me to listen. You who are not here, be here.*

Xanthe had watched that rite performed ten thousand times in her childhood, by servants and soldiers and once, memorably, by her father with ash still on his hands from putting down a stable fire. She knew its cadence the way she knew her own heartbeat.

But she had never seen it performed like *that*. Like a letter written to an address that no longer existed, posted anyway, every day, out of habit or grief or some stubbornness the writer himself no longer understood. There was a hole in the man shaped exactly like an answer that never came, and he knelt beside it nightly the way you might visit a grave.

*Ah*, she thought. *Another orphan of a vanished thing.*

The wolf lay down in the snow and settled in to watch.

* * *

By the second night she had learned his rhythms. He rose before light and walked the perimeter of his camp with a hunter's economy, checking snares, reading the wind. He spoke aloud only rarely, and then to himself, in a voice roughened by disuse — fragments, mostly, inventory and complaint. *"Two days to Marrowgate if the col doesn't founder. Should have taken the eastern shelf. Should have left the map in the pass."*

That last one made her ears prick so hard they hurt.

The map.

She had felt its existence the way you feel a missing tooth — a wrongness, an absence with edges. When the courier had died in the southern passes — she'd found what was left of him, or rather what the inquisitors had allowed to remain — she had tracked the story of the map the way other wolves tracked wounded deer. Through three towns and one burned waystation. Through a fence in Lowmark who talked too much after two cups. Through a boy who had seen a tall stranger buy rope and dried meat and ask after the high passes of Thornfell.

And now here he was, sitting on her inheritance, warming his hands over a fire built from her mountains' dead, and praying to the god who had let her whole country be unmade.

On the second night, the storm came down off the icefields early, and she made her decision in the white roar of it.

* * *

He heard her before he saw her. She gave him that much — circled wide through the trees, snapped a branch under her paw deliberately, and watched him come up off his bedroll with the blade already in his hand and his body angled the way trained men angle theirs, presenting less of himself, buying time.

"Wolf," he said to the darkness, conversational, as if greeting an unwelcome guest at a door. "Big one."

*Very big*, she thought. She had always run large even in this shape; in the court records of old Cindral her line were called the Long Wolves, and the usurper's scholars, insofar as they admitted her family had ever existed, called them monsters. She stepped out of the treeline into the edge of the firelight and let him see all of it: the winter-gray pelt, the shoulders that came nearly to his eye level, the eyes she knew were wrong for any natural beast — too bright, too aware, gold shot through with green like sunlight through bottle glass.

To his credit, he didn't run. He went very still instead, the way prey does, and his eyes did a rapid, professional calculation — distance to the horse, distance to the rocks, whether the fire would hold her. A fighter's arithmetic. She had seen the usurper's officers do the same sum before their lines broke.

Then he did the last thing she expected. He lowered the blade. Not all the way. Just enough.

"You've been following me two days," he said. "I don't shoot what I can't understand, and I don't bait what I can't kill. So either you're starving and stupid, or you want something." His voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of a man talking himself calm. "Wolves don't circle a camp twice without taking the horse. So. What do you want?"

She could have answered in that shape. Her throat could make the words; it simply cost her something to speak with a mouth built for killing, and besides — she wanted to see his face when it happened. All of it. Every stage.

So she let the change come.

It was not painless and it was not beautiful. It was a tearing and a reknitting, fur receding into skin, bones remembering other lengths, and she rode it standing upright on her hind legs as the world tilted and reorganized itself around her — scents collapsing into mere smells, the great dark bowl of the night shrinking to human proportions, her own body becoming a small cramped thing full of cold. When it was done she stood in the snow at the edge of his firelight, barefoot, wearing the shape of a woman in her late twenties with wolf-gold eyes and hair the color of wet slate, shivering, furious at herself for shivering.

Elodie Eriksen — though she did not yet know his name — looked at her for a long moment.

Then he reached down, picked up his cloak from the bedroll, and threw it to her.

Not toward her. *To* her — an underhand toss that landed in the snow at her feet, close enough to take without coming nearer to his blade. It was, she would think later, the single most disarming act of violence-adjacent courtesy she had ever witnessed. She took the cloak. It smelled of woodsmoke and Vael's incense and horse. She wrapped it around herself and hated how grateful she was.

"You're her," he said. Not a question.

"Her who?"

"The one who's been hunting the map." He said it lightly, but his knuckles had gone pale around the sword hilt. "The fence in Lowmark said a woman came asking after the courier's effects. Described you. Said you paid in old coins." Something flickered across his face — the first expression she'd seen there that wasn't calculation. Recognition, maybe. Pity, possibly. She decided she hated both. "Coins from a country that isn't on any map anymore."

"Cindral is on your map," Xanthe said. "I've seen it. Roads running straight into blank parchment where my home used to be, and a seal stamped over the empty space like a wax seal over a coffin." She took a step closer, and the firelight found her face, and she let him see everything in it. "That seal is Vael's own mark. The silent god signed my country's death warrant. And now his servant is carrying the deed in his pack like a piece of merchandise to be sold to whoever pays."

"I hadn't decided to sell it."

"Liar. I watched you count what it might fetch. You did it with your fingers, last night, when you thought no one was watching." She smiled, and knew it wasn't a kind smile. "Wolves see well in the dark, Elodie of nowhere."

His jaw tightened. "I don't know your name either."

"Xanthe." She said it plainly, and watched him flinch — because everyone from the old borders flinched at that name, the name of the last royal house of Cindral, erased so thoroughly that most people who heard it assumed it was foreign, exotic, meaningless. He didn't assume. She saw it land in him like a stone dropped down a well, all the way to the bottom. "Xanthe of House Veyra. Last daughter of the Sunken Court. Exile, heir, and — as of eleven days ago — the only person alive who remembers what's behind the blank space on your map."

"Your family—"

"Died nameless," she said. "That's how it works. You don't get killed. You get *forgotten*. Your village stops being on the maps, then stops being in the songs, then stops being in anyone's mouth. Your mother's grave loses its stone and then loses its grave. My father fought the erasure for six years and I watched him fade from the world's memory like ink in rain, and I survived only because I was already outside the border when it happened. Already exiled. Already nothing." The cold had gotten into her voice; she couldn't seem to warm it. "So yes. I'm here for the map. It belongs to me the way your scars belong to you. I will take it back."

He was quiet for a while. The storm hissed against the fire, and somewhere above them the wind worried at the high crags like a dog at a bone.

"If I give it to you," he said finally, "you'll die with it. You know that. Whatever's chasing that courier is still out there — I've seen their riders. White cloaks. They move like they own the weather." He paused. "Inquisitors. And they're not looking for a smuggler with stolen goods. They're looking for *her*" — he nodded at her, at the shape of her, at the name — "and they'll peel these mountains apart until they find what they're looking for."

"Then you understand why I can't simply take the map and vanish into the snow." She came closer still, close enough that he could see the fire reflected doubled in her eyes, and held out her open hand — palm up, empty, the oldest gesture of parley there was. "Alone, I'm a rumor they erase by spring. With a guide who knows every smuggler's road through Thornfell — with a sworn man of Vael, whose order built half the hidden ways in these mountains before my country fell — I'm something else entirely."

"And what's that?"

"A problem," Xanthe said, "with a name."

He almost laughed. She saw it happen — saw the cynicism strain and crack and something warmer leak through before he sealed it again with the ease of long practice. He looked at her outstretched hand for a long time without taking it.

"I guide you as far as Marrowgate," he said at last. "Free city. Neutral ground. After that we settle accounts and go our separate roads, and whatever you do past the walls of Marrowgate is no concern of mine." He sheathed his sword — slowly, deliberately, a gesture offered like a coin. "That's my terms."

"Marrowgate," she agreed, and lied with a straight face and a steady heart. "Only as far as Marrowgate."

They both knew they were lying. That was the strange part — the honest dishonesty of it, two liars recognizing each other's craft and respecting it. She had spent twenty years among people who abandoned her: a court that fled, a god that went silent, a world that let her country slip down between the cracks of its memory. And here was a man who prayed daily to a god who never answered, carrying her inheritance in his pack, offering her a cloak and a lie and half a road.

It was the most anyone had offered her in years.

She took his hand. His grip was dry and calloused and careful, as if he thought she might break, which — given what she was — was almost funny enough to laugh at.

"Sleep," she said, releasing him, settling onto the far side of the fire with the cloak still around her shoulders. "You'll want your strength. The pass east of here floods with meltwater by midmorning, and your map doesn't show the ford, because your map is twenty years out of date and so is every map your world owns."

He stared at her a moment longer — the wolf-woman wrapped in his cloak, eating his salt-pork, correcting his geography. Then, muttering something that might have been a prayer or a curse, he rolled into his bedroll and closed his eyes.

He did not sleep. Neither did she. But sometime deep in the black hours, when the storm had worn itself to whispers, she heard him shift, and speak softly into the dark — not to her, not quite to himself. To the address that no longer existed.

*"You who do not speak,"* he murmured, *"if you're listening at all — tell me if I'm walking into something I shouldn't."*

The silence, as always, said nothing.

But Xanthe, listening with ears that had once belonged to a wolf, thought she heard — far off, down in the roots of the mountains where no road should run — something answer anyway. A sound like a great door straining against its hinges. A sound like a held breath.

A sound, very faint and very far away, like a country clearing its throat to say its own name.

* * *

At dawn they broke camp together, and Elodie unrolled the map one last time before packing it — checking his route north to Marrowgate, he said. Xanthe leaned over his shoulder to look, and her breath stopped, because there on the blank space where Cindral should have been, faint as breath on glass in the growing light, new lines had appeared overnight.

Roads. Thin, silver, unmistakable — threading through the empty parchment like veins through a closed eyelid.

Neither of them had drawn them.
