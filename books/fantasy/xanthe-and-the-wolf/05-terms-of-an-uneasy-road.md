## 5. Terms of an Uneasy Road

### Elodie

Elodie woke before dawn with a knife already in his hand and a wolf watching him from across the dead fire.

It had been there when he closed his eyes. He had known it would be there when he opened them, and still his heart performed its small betrayal, stumbling against his ribs like a man tripping on a stair.

"You could have made tea," he said. "You had hours."

The wolf's ears twitched. Then the shape of it came apart — not violently, not with the tearing he'd imagined in the long sleepless night, but softly, like smoke deciding to be a woman. Fur became hair the color of wet slate; the amber eyes stayed exactly where they were, unblinking, patient as weather.

Xanthe sat on her heels at the edge of the firepit, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak that made her look like something the mountain had grown rather than worn. "I don't drink tea."

"Everyone drinks tea."

"Everyone from *your* country." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "You chew your leaves like a goat and call it civilization."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. Two nights she had circled him. Two nights she had let him see her — or let him think he was seeing her, which among wolves and women was rarely the same thing. And now here she sat in the grey light, waiting, because she wanted something, and things that want are dangerous.

"The map," she said.

"Is mine."

"A dying man gave it to you. That's not ownership, that's inheritance by accident. You're a stranger standing where a family should be."

"I'm a guide," Elodie said. "It's what I'm *for*. I take people through Thornfell, I take payment, everyone walks away richer or at least alive. It's an honest trade as trades go."

"It's a coward's trade," Xanthe said mildly, "dressed in rope and crampons."

He laughed before he could stop himself — a short, surprised bark that startled a bird off the scree above them. She didn't smile, but something moved at the corner of her mouth, a muscle remembering how.

Here was the arithmetic he ran while she watched him: the map led into blank space. Blank space meant either nothing — worthless paper, a madman's last joke — or it meant Cindral, the country that did not exist, wearing Vael's own seal at its heart. If it was nothing, guiding this woman cost him two weeks and whatever fee she could pay. If it was something, then every inquisitor between here and the sea would want it, and the smart move — the move the old Elodie, the one who still prayed, might have flinched from — was to sell it to the first broker with deep enough pockets and no conscience to speak of.

Marrowgate had brokers like that. Marrowgate had everything, if you didn't ask where it came from.

"Marrowgate," he said aloud, testing it.

"What about it?"

"I'll take you there. The free city. It's nine days' hard walking through the high passes, and the passes are mine — every ledge, every blind ravine, every place a patrol can't follow. I get you inside the walls safe, I collect my fee, and after that we're strangers again. You go find whoever you were going to find, and I go back to work for people who tip better and threaten less."

Xanthe's eyes narrowed a fraction. "That's all?"

"That's all."

She studied him the way she'd probably studied wounded deer, once, from downwind. "You slept with your knife drawn."

"Habit."

"You lied to the inquisitors." She nodded toward the pass below, where two days ago men in ash-grey livery had turned over his cold campfire stone by stone. "Your god forbids lying, doesn't he? Vael's people. Silence is holy, speech is debt, and a lie is theft from the god's own ledger. I've read the old catechisms. Some of them were printed in my father's court."

Elodie fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch. "My god stopped answering twenty years ago. I figure the ledger's gone dark too. Nobody's counting."

"That's the saddest thing I've heard in ten years," Xanthe said, "and I once listened to a tax collector apologize for three hours," and this time she did smile, thin and quick as a fish under ice, and Elodie hated how much of his attention it took from him.

"Terms," he said, dragging the conversation back onto solid ground before it could open beneath him. "One: you walk where I walk, camp where I camp, and when I say quiet, you're quiet. Two: no shifting near other travelers. People shoot first at things with too many teeth. Three—" he held up a third finger, and paused, because the third term was the one that mattered and they both knew it. "Three: the map stays with me until Marrowgate."

"No."

"It stays with me or there's no road."

"It's *mine*—"

"It's *collateral*," Elodie said, and heard his voice flatten into the register he used with clients who got clever at altitude. "You're asking me to walk you toward a country that officially does not exist, past soldiers who erase villages, carrying a document with my god's seal on it. I don't know you. I've known you four days and one of those days you were a wolf. The map is the only thing in this arrangement that anyone wants badly enough to kill for, and while I hold it, nobody has a reason to put a knife in me and leave you alive."

Silence, except the wind worrying at the tent line.

Then Xanthe said, quietly, "And in Marrowgate?"

"In Marrowgate I sell it to you, if you can pay. Or you steal it, if you're the person I'm starting to suspect you are. Either way we part honest."

"We're not honest now."

"No," Elodie agreed. "But we're *consistent*."

She looked at him for a long time. High overhead a hawk rode the thermal off the eastern face, turning slow circles like a thought neither of them wanted to say. When she finally extended her hand — human fingers, callused at the palm, nails blunt as any traveler's — Elodie took it, and her grip was careful in a way that told him she knew exactly how much stronger than him she was.

"To Marrowgate," she said. "Strangers after."

"Strangers after."

They were both lying. He was nearly sure she knew it. Somehow that made the handshake hold.

### Xanthe

Nine days, the smuggler had said, and Xanthe spent the first one deciding whether she believed a single word out of his mouth.

He was good. That was the infuriating part. She had watched him read the mountain the way her mother had read tide-charts — glancing at cloud-shadows, tasting the wind, choosing a route that looked absurd until it revealed itself as the only sane line through broken country. Twice he stopped without explanation and backtracked fifty yards to avoid a slope that looked identical to six slopes they'd already crossed. Both times, within the hour, they heard the crack of rockfall where the path they hadn't taken gave way.

"How?" she asked the second time.

"Sound travels wrong up there." He jerked his chin at a shelf of limestone. "Loose scree holds the echo half a beat. You learn to hear the mountain stutter."

"You learn that or you die learning it?"

"Mostly the second kind teach the first kind." He said it lightly, but his eyes went somewhere else for a moment — some narrow place, some bad year — and came back. Xanthe catalogued that too. Everything went in the ledger: the way he checked his bootlaces twice each morning, left foot first, always; the way he touched the map-case at his chest every quarter-hour like a man touching a wound to confirm it still hurt; the way he never, not once, looked at the blank space on the map's western edge longer than a heartbeat, as though looking too long might make it true.

He was grieving something. She recognized the architecture of it from the inside.

They walked, and she talked, because talking was how you tested a person — not the words, but the grain beneath them.

"Your accent," she said on the third day, hopping a meltwater stream. "Northern Thornfell. But you don't count like northerners. Northerners count sheep, gods, and money, in that order. You counted our rations last night in fours."

"Fours is how you count in the dark," he said. "Thumb plus three fingers. Can't lose track of fours even half-frozen."

"Who taught you?"

"My mother." A pause, boots finding purchase on wet granite. "She was a shrine-keeper. Low order. Vael's folk don't preach, so mostly she kept the lamps and the silence and raised me on porridge and catechism."

"And now the shrines—"

"There are no shrines." Flat. Final. A door closing on a room she wasn't invited into yet. "There hasn't been a lit lamp in a Vaelan shrine since I was nineteen years old."

*Since Cindral fell,* she did not say. She had learned, these three days, that Elodie Eriksen would answer almost anything except kindness offered directly. Kindness had to be smuggled in, disguised as argument, and she was beginning to suspect she was better at that than she'd been at anything since the erasure.

At night they lay on opposite sides of the fire, and she listened to his breathing change as he fell asleep — always facing outward, always with the knife hand free — and she thought: *he guards me while he sleeps. He thinks he's guarding the map.*

On the fifth day she tested him properly.

A merchant train came up the switchbacks below them — eight mules, four armed drovers, salt and iron by the smell of it — and Elodie pulled them off the trail behind a screen of boulder and pine without a word. They crouched in the green shade while the mule-train passed, close enough that Xanthe could hear the drovers complaining about the war levies, and one of them said to another, low:

"—whole valley at Greywater Ford, I heard. Ten thousand if it's one. They're mustering for something in the east, and it isn't bandits."

"East is nothing. East is empty."

"That's what they keep saying." A dry laugh. "Funny how much soldiering a man can do in empty country."

The train ground past and faded downhill. Elodie didn't move for a full minute after the last bell had gone silent.

"Greywater Ford," Xanthe said.

"It's a day's march south of us. Maybe less." He was doing the thing with his jaw, working something over that he hadn't decided to say. "Ten thousand men don't muster to chase one map."

"No," Xanthe said. Her mouth had gone dry. "They muster when their master decides a border needs to become permanent. Twenty years ago he erased my country from the ledgers. Now I think he's making sure no one goes looking for what he buried."

Elodie finally looked at her — really looked, the way he almost never allowed himself — and she saw the exact moment the mercenary's arithmetic failed him, the moment when *fee* and *two weeks' work* collapsed into something larger and more terrible that he had no column for.

"Show me the valley," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I need to know what's coming. And because you do too, and you know it."

He swore — softly, in the manner of a man whose profanity had been rationed by a religious upbringing — and led her up.

### Elodie

The overlook was one he'd used a hundred times, a shelf of granite jutting over the southern descent where Thornfell opened its hand and let you see the lowlands: the river, the ford, the patchwork of holdings that fed the free cities. From here the world always looked orderly. Manageable. A thing that could be crossed for a fee.

The army had rearranged the world.

They filled the floodplain at Greywater Ford like a spill of ink — tents in ranks so straight they caught the afternoon light all at once, a glittering grid of canvas and spear-points stretching from the riverbank to the tree line. Even at this distance the sound reached them, delayed and strange: the deep animal murmur of thousands of men, the ring of forges, the rhythmic groan of siege engines being assembled by crews who had done it many times. Banners hung limp in the still air. Ash-grey. The color of the inquisitors' cloaks, multiplied ten thousand times.

Beside him, Xanthe had gone very still. Not calm-still. The stillness of a creature deciding whether to fight or run, holding both options open at tremendous cost.

"They're not marching on the free cities," Elodie said. His voice sounded thin against the vastness below. "Not yet. Armies that size point themselves like a weapon. That whole mass is aimed east."

"At nothing," Xanthe whispered.

"At blank space." He hated the words even as he said them. "At wherever the map ends. At Cindral, if Cindral is anywhere at all." He turned to look at her. In the flat white light of the high country her face had lost its guardedness entirely, and what was underneath was young and stricken and furious, and Elodie felt the ground shift under a conviction he'd been building for five days. "What's actually at the end of this map? And don't tell me Marrowgate, because we both signed that lie and we both watched it rot."

For a moment he thought she'd deny it. He watched her decide not to — watched the decision cost her something visible, a tightening around the eyes, a small surrender of the self-sufficiency she wore like armor.

"A door," she said. "Maybe. My grandmother wrote about it before the end. The Door Out of Cindral." Her laugh had no humor in it whatsoever. "We always assumed it was a smuggling route. A way out, if the worst came. It's why the map survived — it was hidden in the palace as an escape plan." She stared down at the army, at the patient grey instrument of oblivion. "Now I think the worst came, and the map is all that's left of the way home, and I don't even know if 'home' still exists under the stone they buried it with."

Wind moved through the pines below them, and far off, faint as memory, the army's murmur rose and fell.

"Nine days ago I'd have sold that map by now," Elodie said slowly. "To the first fence in Marrowgate with coin and no questions. That was the whole plan. That was the plan this morning."

"And now?"

He looked at the blank edge of the world, where the mountains ran out into territory that no ledger admitted, and thought of frost on a shrine floor, and a god who had gone silent the very season Cindral's name was struck from the books — and thought, with a vertigo he kept carefully off his face: *those two facts are the same fact.*

"Now," he said, "I think we should reach Marrowgate fast, and I think we should stop pretending that either of us means to stop there." He stuck out his hand, rough palm up, the same gesture she'd made at the fire five days ago. "New terms. Same fee. But no more lies about the destination — I'm too tired to carry both of ours."

Xanthe looked at his hand. Then, below, at the army. Something in her face settled, the way snow settles on a peak just before it decides to slide.

"All right, guide," she said, and took it. "No more lies. Starting tomorrow."

"Why tomorrow?"

"Because tonight," said the heir of erased Cindral, with the ghost of the fish-under-ice smile, "you're going to tell me everything you know about Vael's seal — because I saw your face when I mentioned my grandmother's letter, Elodie Eriksen, and you've seen that sigil somewhere before. Somewhere that scared you."

Below them, ten thousand men began, very faintly, to sing.
