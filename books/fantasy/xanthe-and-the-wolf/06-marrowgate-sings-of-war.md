## 6. Marrowgate Sings of War

Marrowgate smelled of fish brine and tallow and too many people breathing the same air, and Xanthe loved it the way one loves a wound — because it hurt in a familiar place.

She had not walked through a market gate in eleven years. Old Cindral had held its own markets once, before the blankness came; she remembered her mother's hand around hers, the bell of the wool-sellers' guild, honey cakes wrapped in waxed cloth that left her fingers sweet until supper. She had been seven when they crossed out of the mountains, and she had spent every year since learning to forget, on purpose, carefully, the way you learn to stop touching a burn. Forgetting was survival. Forgetting was the only inheritance the exile had been permitted to carry.

And now here was Elodie Eriksen walking three paces ahead of her through the Fish Gate with his hood up and his silence arranged just so, and here were ten thousand strangers, and none of them knew — none of them *could* know — that the woman threading through their press carried a dead country folded in oilcloth against her ribs.

"Stay close," Elodie said, without turning. "The Gate has rules. First rule: don't look like you have anything worth taking."

"And what do I look like I have?"

"Trouble," he said. "You've had it since the day I met you."

It was almost banter. It was as close to warmth as he let himself come on open streets, and she had learned in four days of shared road to read his economies the way she read weather. She fell into step behind him.

* * *

The markets proper began where the fish stalls ended, and they went on the way a fever goes on — tier after tier of them climbing the hill inside the old harbor walls, canvas awnings stitched from a hundred colors, lanterns already lit though the afternoon was barely browning. Marrowgate did not sleep so much as change its voice. By day it sang in copper and haggling; by night it would sing in dice and secrets. Xanthe could hear both songs waiting inside each other, the way she could hear, faintly, always, the note under all notes that had once been her country's name.

They came for rope and dried plums and news. That was what Elodie said. But Xanthe watched him watch the crowd — not the stalls, the *crowd*, the exits, the uniforms — and understood that what they had really come for was to learn how much the world knew.

The world, it turned out, knew less than it feared.

They heard it first at a spice stall, from a carter buying saffron he clearly couldn't afford: soldiers in the eastern valleys, whole garrisons moved up to the passes, the usurper's banners in places no banner had flown since before the war. The carter said it the way people say things they don't believe — lightly, for the pleasure of being alarmed.

Then, at the rope-seller's, an old woman with forearms like ship's cable said something different. She said that villages were going quiet. Not raided. Not burned. *Quiet.* Her cousin's town among them — Hesselmere, or it had been called Hesselmere, except that when she wrote to her cousin's neighbors asking after it, they answered kindly, patiently, as if writing back to a madwoman. *There is no Hesselmere here,* they wrote. *There never was. The valley is empty. The valley has always been empty.*

Xanthe stood very still beside a barrel of tarred twine while the old woman counted out her coins, and something cold walked down her spine wearing her mother's footsteps.

* * *

By evening the market tiers had shifted registers, as Elodie promised. Cookfires lit the lanes between stalls; someone was playing a fiddle badly and beautifully at the same time; wine moved through the crowd in clay cups passed hand to hand without asking whose they'd been. And the refugees had gathered, the way refugees always gather, at the edges of other people's warmth.

There were more of them than there should have been. That was the first wrong thing. Borderland folk came through Thornfell in spring, when the passes opened — a trickle, families with mules and grievances. This was a flood, in autumn, and they were not carrying their belongings. That was the second wrong thing. Refugees carry everything they are. These people carried almost nothing. They sat at the fire edges with their hands open in their laps like people who had set something down and could not remember what.

Xanthe drifted toward them the way water finds a slope.

The nearest fire belonged to a family from the borderlands — a grandmother, two men who might be brothers, a child asleep against the grandmother's shoulder. They made room for Xanthe without speaking, which was itself a borderland custom; she felt it land in her chest like a key finding a lock it had been cut for.

"You're from over the mountains," the grandmother said. Not a question. "I can hear it. You say your vowels from the back of your throat, like the old folk did."

"I'm from a long way away," Xanthe said, in careful trade-tongue.

"There's no such place anymore." The grandmother said it mildly, terribly, stirring a pot of barley that had to feed eight people and would feed twelve. "That's what we came to tell anyone who'd listen. There's no such place."

"Grandmother—"

"Hesselmere." The old woman pronounced it slowly, holding it upright the way you hold a candle in wind. "My daughter married a man from Hesselmere. I visited there thirty years, every harvest. Apple country. The church bell had a crack in it and rang flat and nobody ever fixed it because we loved it flat. Tell me—" and here she looked up, and her eyes were doing something Xanthe had seen in no living person before, something between weeping and arithmetic — "tell me the name of my daughter's village."

"Hesselmere," Xanthe said.

"Say it again."

"Hesselmere."

The grandmother closed her eyes. Around the fire, the brothers had gone still. "Three days ago I couldn't say it. Three days ago I sat where I'm sitting and tried to think where my daughter lived and my mind slid off it like rain off slate. I knew I had a daughter. I knew she had a village. The village itself was — there was a hole where it went." She opened her eyes. "Then two nights past, on the road, a peddler spoke a word I hadn't heard in days, and it all came back. Like a tooth regrowing. It *hurt* coming back."

One of the brothers spoke for the first time. His voice was flat with a fatigue beyond fear. "It's not just us. Talk to the tanners' row, the ones who came in last week. Ask them the name of the river they grew up on. Half of them can't find it. You watch them try. Their mouths make the shape of it and nothing comes out. My neighbor Fenn — came over the pass with us — asked him yesterday where his smithy was and he said, cheerful as you like, 'I've never worked iron.' His hands are a smith's hands. He showed me the burns. He laughed while he showed me."

"Not burned out?" someone else at the fire asked. "Not sacked?"

"Sacked leaves ashes," the brother said. "Ashes remember. You can stand in ashes and hate. This is—" he searched, failed, tried again. "This is like the village was written in chalk and someone came along with a wet thumb. There's no grief in the valleys where our towns were. We went back, some of us, to see. There are no ruins. There's grass. Old grass. Grass that's been there forever, growing where Fenn's forge stood, and sheep grazing on it, and the shepherd thinks he's always grazed there, and he'll tell you so, and he's not lying."

The child woke and whimpered, and the grandmother rocked it absently, and Xanthe found that she could not unclench her hands.

* * *

She found Elodie at the corner of the tanners' row, leaning against a wall with the studied idleness of a man listening to everything. She told him all of it, low and fast, and watched his face do the small terrible work of believing her.

"The usurper doesn't conquer," she said. "I kept thinking — why does he want empty valleys? Why move armies to hold grass? He doesn't hold anything. There's nothing to hold. He takes the *name*, Elodie. He takes the name out of the world and the world closes over the place like water. No occupation. No rebellion. You can't rebel for a country if you can't remember it exists." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it and kept going. "That's what happened to Cindral. Twenty years ago. Not a conquest. An erasure. And everyone who escaped it — everyone who carries the memory outside the line — we're loose ends. Living contradictions. Every one of us is a person who remembers a place that officially never was."

"That's why they hunt the map," Elodie said slowly.

"That's why they hunt *me*. The map is just paper. Paper can be burned. A bloodline can't be unremembered while any of it breathes." She heard herself say it and realized it was the first time she had said it plainly to anyone, ever, and it was to a smuggler she'd known five days, and there was no time left for caution. "Every refugee in this city who remembers a village the maps don't show is living on borrowed existence. And the borrowing is running out."

Elodie was quiet a moment. Then, in his flattest voice, the one that meant he was frightened: "How many people in Marrowgate right now can name a town that isn't on any map?"

Xanthe turned and looked down the firelit lane — hundreds of faces, hundreds of cups passing, the bad fiddle striking up again — and did the same arithmetic he was doing, and saw him arrive at the same answer.

Enough. Enough to fill an inquisitor's ledger page after page.

"We leave at dawn," she said.

"We leave tonight," said Elodie.

* * *

They nearly didn't.

It happened on the way back toward the Fish Gate, in the lane behind the chandlers', where the light from the street lanterns didn't quite meet and the smell of hot wax lay thick. Xanthe felt him before she saw him — felt it as a wrongness in the air pressure, a hush moving against the flow of the crowd, because crowds part around violence even when no one knows why.

He wore gray. Not uniform gray; traveling gray; the gray of a man who has chosen invisibility as a discipline. But beneath the gray collar, when he turned, the sigil at his throat caught the lantern flame: a circle crossed by a single vertical line, the mark of the usurper's inquiry, the mark she had seen branded into the doorpost of the safehouse in Thornfell that no longer had occupants.

Inquisitor. Here. Now. In Marrowgate, which prided itself on having no masters.

He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the fire-glow at the end of the lane where the refugee fires burned, and he carried a ledger under one arm, and he was reading it as he walked, and Xanthe understood with a lurch that he was checking names against faces. That the book in his arm contained the names of villages that did not exist, and that he was hunting the people those names clung to like burrs.

Keep walking. She gave her feet the order. Keep walking, hood down, face forgettable, be nobody, be from nowhere—

The inquisitor stopped.

He raised his head from the ledger the way a hound raises its head, and his eyes — pale, kind, appallingly gentle eyes — swept the lane and snagged on her. On her, specifically, out of forty walkers, and she knew why a half-second before he took a step toward her, and the knowledge arrived like ice water:

She had flinched at the sigil. Out of forty people, one had flinched.

"Forgive me," he called, and his voice was warm, courteous, carrying without effort. "Madam? A moment?"

Elodie materialized out of the crowd's blind spots with a clay cup in his hand and a drunkard's weave in his step, and hooked his arm through the inquisitor's elbow, laughing some apology about his sister and too much plum wine, steering, steering — and the inquisitor let himself be steered for exactly one stride before his gentleness became something else, something that stopped Elodie like a hand on the chest.

"Your sister," the inquisitor said pleasantly. "What village is she from?"

The lane went on breathing around them. Somewhere the fiddle played. Xanthe's heart was a fist in her chest.

"Marrowgate," Elodie said, mournful and slurred. "Born under the Fish Gate, poor girl, never been ten miles from it. Which is why she drinks like this—"

"Of course." The pale eyes moved to Xanthe. "And yours?"

"She doesn't talk when she's like this," Elodie said.

"No," the inquisitor agreed softly. "But she walks like someone from somewhere."

And then the crowd saved them, the way crowds do — a cart, an argument, a knot of drainers spilling out of a tavern — three seconds of obstruction, and Elodie's hand closed on Xanthe's wrist and they were moving, fast, unhurried-looking, down the chandlers' lane and through a cooperage yard and over a wall that scraped her palms raw, and then through streets Elodie chose by instinct, doubling twice, until the market noise faded behind them and the Fish Gate rose ahead, torchlit and indifferent.

Only at the gate, in the shadow of the toll arch, did he stop and turn to her, and in the torchlight his face was older than it had been that morning.

"He let us go," Elodie said. "You understand that. One order from him and the gate guard seals this city like a jar. He had your face. He'll have it drawn by morning."

"Then why—"

"Because he was counting," Elodie said. "Because we weren't what he came for. Because there's a man under that gray cloak walking back toward those fires with a ledger full of names, and every soul sitting at them is going in his book, and he decided we were worth following later so he wouldn't lose the others now." He exhaled through his teeth. "He's not hunting the map anymore, Xanthe. He's harvesting everyone who remembers."

Above them, on the gatehouse wall, a watchman began ringing the hour bell — six slow strokes rolling out over the roofs, over the harbor, over the ten thousand sleepers of Marrowgate — and Xanthe stood with her palm still stinging and listened to the sound die, and thought: *this is what it sounds like, a city that still knows its own name.*

Dawn was five hours off. Elodie was already looking at the north road.
