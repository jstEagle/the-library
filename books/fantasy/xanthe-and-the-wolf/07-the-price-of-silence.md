## 7. The Price of Silence

Marrowgate woke the way a drunkard does — slowly, complaining, and with no memory of what it had done the night before. By the time Elodie reached the Saltmarket, the fog off the harbor had already gone grey and greasy, threaded through with the smell of frying onions, tar, and the particular sweetness of rot that no amount of civic pride could scour out of the lower streets. Gulls argued over fish heads on the cobbles. Somewhere a temple bell rang the hour, and somewhere else, closer, a woman was singing a work song about a drowned husband in a voice so flat it might have been an inventory.

Elodie walked with his hood up and the map against his ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of his coat like a second heart.

He had told himself all night, lying awake in the rented room above the Drowned Lantern while Xanthe slept — or pretended to sleep — on the floor by the window, that this was simply business. A find is a find. He'd pulled stranger things out of dead men's hands: signet rings, letters of credit, a vial of something that had made a fence in Vask weep with gratitude. This was only paper. Only ink. Only the fact that every road on it ran into a hole where twenty years of history used to be.

Only that.

*You're selling her inheritance,* said a voice at the back of his skull that sounded unpleasantly like his mother.

*I'm selling a map,* he answered it. *She can buy it back if she wants it so badly.*

The voice did not dignify that with a reply. Voices like that never did.

The fence he wanted was called Mott, and Mott kept shop behind a chandler's on Brine Street, down a corridor that smelled of tallow and older sins. Elodie had done three jobs through him over the years — clean ones, mostly, by smuggler standards — and trusted him exactly as far as greed could be relied upon to hold still. Which was to say: not far, but far enough, if you watched your hands.

The door had a new lock. Good iron. That was the first wrong thing.

The second was the boy in the chandler's front room, who took one look at Elodie's face and went white as fresh lard.

"Tell Mott I'm here," Elodie said pleasantly.

"He said — he said if anyone came asking after—" The boy swallowed. "He said he wasn't buying anything lately."

"Anything?"

"Anything *at all*, sir."

Elodie stood very still for a moment. Outside, the gulls went on screaming their small blue murders. Then he smiled the smile he used on customs men, tipped the boy a copper he could not really afford, and walked back out into the fog with the taste of the whole morning gone sour in his mouth.

Three fences in Marrowgate dealt in documents. Mott was one. The second was a widow named Corvane who operated out of a milliner's shop on the hill, and who owed Elodie a favor from the winter she'd needed a crate moved past the Thornfell tolls without its contents being discussed.

Corvane received him in the back room among hatboxes and dried lavender, poured him tea he didn't touch, and listened with her hands folded while he laid the proposition out in careful, deniable terms. A document of antiquarian interest. Provenance murky. Buyer discreet. She let him finish, and then she looked at him for a long moment with eyes like wet slate, and something in her face made his stomach drop before she said a word.

"Elodie Eriksen." She pronounced his name the way one pronounces a diagnosis. "Do you know what you've been carrying around this city?"

"A map."

"I know what it is." Her voice dropped to almost nothing, though they were alone behind two closed doors and a wall thick enough to stop a siege engine. "I saw a rubbing of it, years ago. Before. One of my husband's clients — a scholar from the university, the old university, the one that isn't there anymore — brought it in to have the seal authenticated. My husband looked at it once and burned the rubbing and paid the man to leave Marrowgate." She leaned forward. "That seal, Elodie. You've seen it. Tell me you've seen it."

"The sun split by a sword," Elodie said quietly. "Vael's mark."

"*Vael's mark,*" she echoed. "On the borders of a country that every ledger in the world agrees never existed. Do you understand what that means? It means either the god signed a forgery, or—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, a woman catching a word before it could get loose and ruin her. "There are people who ask after things like this. Not buyers. *Askers.* They came to the shop last month asking whether anyone had brought me papers with old seals on them. They were very polite. They wore grey. I don't deal in grey, Elodie. Grey doesn't pay, and grey doesn't forget."

"Who are they?"

"If you're smart you'll never learn their names. If you're smart you'll take that thing to the harbor tonight and drop it in forty fathoms of cold water and go back to whatever mountains raised you." She pushed the untouched teacup an inch toward him, an oddly gentle gesture, like closing a dead man's eyes. "I liked your father. Get out of my shop."

He got out of her shop.

* * *

The third broker was a half-blind Kellish moneylender called Ossik who worked out of the back of a pawnshop in the Tanners' Row warren, and whom nobody respectable would touch, which was precisely why Elodie went to him last. Desperation dressed up as strategy. He knew it even as he knocked the triple knock on the paint-blistered door, knew it the way you know you've started a descent halfway down a cliff face when turning around is harder than falling.

Ossik received him amid stacks of unredeemed pledges — a stuffed owl missing one eye, a child's christening gown yellowed to the color of old teeth, forty clocks ticking out of agreement with one another. The moneylender sat behind a desk scarred with knife-marks, milky-eyed, hands like root vegetables folded on the wood.

"You want to sell," Ossik said. Not a question. His nose worked when he spoke, delicately, like a hare testing wind. "Something paper. Something old. Something you're frightened of."

"I'm not frightened of anything."

"No," Ossik agreed mildly. "Of course not. Show me."

Elodie hesitated. Every instinct he owned — instincts that had kept his skin intact through eleven years of passes, tolls, double-crosses, and winters — told him not to unwrap it here, in this room of ticking clocks, before this blind man whose blindness had never once stopped him seeing everything. But the map had been against his ribs for nine days now, and it had begun to feel less like cargo than like a coal, warm and patient, burning a hole straight through to the bone. He wanted it gone. Gods forgive him, he wanted it *gone*.

He unwrapped it on the desk.

Ossik's fingers went over the paper without touching it, hovering, reading the air above the ink the way a blind man reads a face. When they crossed the blank space where Cindral should have been, the hovering fingers trembled. When they reached the seal, they stopped entirely.

For a long time the only sound in the room was forty clocks disagreeing.

"Where," Ossik said at last, and his voice had changed — flattened, careful, a man walking on river ice — "did you get this?"

"Dead man's pocket."

"Mm. And he died how?"

"Inquisitors were chasing him. So — probably inquisitors."

"And now they chase you." Ossik drew his hands back and folded them again, tight, knuckles whitening under the brown skin. "Sit down, Eriksen. No — stand, if you like. Listen, either way. I'll tell you what I know, because I owe the world a little honesty before it finishes going to hell, and then you'll leave and I'll burn my chair and move to Vask, because you have brought a dead god's mail into my house."

"Cold day for theology, Ossik."

"Every day is a cold day for theology. That is rather my point." The milky eyes fixed on him with terrible accuracy. "You wear Vael's knot. I can smell the wax on you. When did you last hear from your god?"

The question landed like a hook behind the sternum. Elodie kept his face still. Eleven years of practice. "Vael doesn't chatter. Silence is the discipline. We pray into it. That's the whole faith."

"That is the whole *wound*, boy." Ossik's voice cracked like a whip, and one of the clocks, absurdly, stopped ticking. "You think silence is the doctrine? I am old. Older than your erasures. I traded in Cindral before Cindral fell — wool, salt, silver, sins, same as everywhere. And I will tell you what every trader of that generation knows and none of the young ones remember. Vael *spoke* in those days. Oracles at the border shrines. Answers written in frost, in ash, in the flight of carrion birds. Loud god, for a god of silence — he spoke the way avalanches speak. And then, one morning in the spring of the Falling, every oracle in the world went quiet between one bell and the next. Not gradually. All at once. Like a door shutting." The old man's hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. "The priests called it a test of faith. The faithful believed them, because that is what the faithful are for. But I sold insurance, Eriksen. I learned to ask when a thing stopped paying out *why*. And the why, in this case, is sitting on my desk smelling of blood and mountain dust."

"You're saying the map silenced him."

"I'm saying the map and the silence are the same wound. I don't know which side of it is the blade." Ossik pushed himself up from the chair, joints cracking in sequence like the clocks winding down, and came around the desk, and did something Elodie had never seen a fence do: he looked at the map and made the sign against ill luck, thumb folded under two fingers, the old ward-sign his grandmother might have made. "Take it away from me. Take it out of my sight. I wouldn't buy that thing with a fortune in hand and a fleet to carry me from the consequences. There is not a broker in this city — not one — who will touch it. Ask whoever you like. Mott turned you away already; I can see it in your walk. Corvane too. We all heard the same rumor inside the same week, and the rumor is this: the grey coats are paying for information about a document, and they are not paying in coin."

"What are they paying in?"

"Forgetting," Ossik said simply. "Debts forgiven. Names struck from other lists. People who informed on the bearer of that map have had their own troubles — erased. Arrests annulled. Sins forgotten by everyone but themselves. It's a kindness, they call it." His lip curled around the word like something spoiled. "They came to me eight days ago. I told them nothing, because I knew nothing. Now I know something, and I am an honest coward, Eriksen, so hear my advice: they already know you're in Marrowgate. Papers like that pull. You are the nail and the map is the lodestone and this city is the fire. Go north through the Marsh Gate tonight, before the watch changes. Don't sell. Don't trade. Run."

Elodie rewrapped the map with hands that were steadier than he had any right to expect. "How much do I owe you for the sermon?"

"Nothing." Ossik turned his ruined eyes toward the shuttered window, toward the grey light beyond. "Consider it a tithe. To a god who may yet be listening."

* * *

He made it four streets before he understood.

It came to him not as thought but as weather — a pressure change, a hush in the noise of the market, the sense of eyes arranging themselves along his path. He counted them without turning his head: the fruit-seller who hadn't glanced at his apples once. The two dockhands leaning on a bollard with the studied idleness of men being paid to lean. A boy on a stoop who was whistling — whistling the same six notes over and over, a signal, a tally, a leash.

And threading through it all, moving against the current of the crowd, a figure in grey.

Elodie did not run. Running confirmed. He bought a skewer of grilled eel from a cart he'd used a dozen times over the years, ate it walking, tipped the vendor generously, and turned down Tanner's Row at a pace just short of strolling, mapping his route through the warren in his head: left at the tanning sheds, through the drying-yard where the hides hung in reeking ranks, out past the lime-pits to the stair that dropped to the canal. Twenty minutes, if the warren stayed crowded. Fifteen if he used the roofline.

Xanthe would be at the Drowned Lantern. Waiting. Trusting him to come back with coin or at least answers.

*Trusting.* The word sat strangely on her, like a borrowed coat, and more strangely still on him.

He was halfway through the drying-yard, hemmed in by curtains of stiff rawhide that swung in the harbor wind and cut sightlines to ribbons, when the whistling stopped.

That was how he knew. Not footsteps — the warren was full of footsteps. The silence. Six notes, ended, and the quality of the quiet that follows a held breath.

He ran.

Behind him the yard erupted — polite grey figures suddenly unpolite, hides slashed aside, boots on boards. Ahead, the lime-pit stair, and below it the canal, green and sluggish, barges wallowing at their moorings. He took the stairs three at a time, hit the towpath, and turned south toward the Marsh Gate with his lungs full of tannin-rot air and his mind doing arithmetic it hated: they had let him reach Ossik. Had followed him there, waited, and followed him out. Which meant they hadn't wanted to take him in the pawnshop. Which meant they wanted to know where he'd run *after* — wanted the web finished before they pulled it.

Which meant they already had the Drowned Lantern. Or would within the hour.

He came out of the warren into the long market street below the Marsh Gate and saw the gate ahead, open, carts queuing — and saw, drawn up beside the gatehouse with the casual permanence of furniture, a wagon in the usurper's colors, and six soldiers eating bread, and an officer checking a paper against each face that passed.

Not hunting. *Waiting.* Sealing the obvious doors and letting the grey coats sweep the rooms.

Elodie stepped into the shadow of a shrine alcove — a roadside niche to some harvest saint, cluttered with candle-stubs and wilted offerings — and pressed his back to the cold stone and thought.

There was a second way out. Of course there was; he was Elodie Eriksen, and there was always a second way out of anywhere, that was the entire profession. The culvert under the Marsh Gate road, the one the smugglers called the Rat's Wedding, choked with refuse and low enough to swim. It came up in a drainage field outside the walls, half a mile east. Cold. Filthy. Passable.

Between him and the culvert mouth lay two hundred yards of market crowd, two watchers he could see, and however many he couldn't.

He was measuring the distance when the bells began.

Not the hour-bells. The *alarm* — the great cracked bell in the Gate Tower, the one that hadn't rung since the fire of his first year in the city, hammering out its rusty panic across the rooftops. And under it, spreading through the market like dye through water, shouting: voices crying *fire*, and then, worse, clearer, cutting through the crowd-noise with official authority:

*"By warrant of the Lord Inquisitor! Seal the gates! Search for a tall man in a dark coat and a woman traveling with him — a woman with Cindral speech!"*

*Cindral.*

His blood went from cold to boiling in the space of a heartbeat. They had her description. Not his — hers, first, foremost — which meant they knew about her, had known, perhaps had known before he ever had. The dying stranger in the pass. The map. Her. How long had the grey nets been cast across these mountains, waiting for something with her face to blunder into them?

And she was at the Drowned Lantern, on the harbor side, on the wrong side of a closing city.

Elodie Eriksen had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not caring — of guiding other people's contraband through other people's wars with his heart locked in a lead box, prayers recited into a silence that never answered, grief worn smooth as river stone from handling. He had told himself, climbing through the Thornfell with her, that he was only escorting her as far as Marrowgate. Business. Coin. Terms of an uneasy road.

He was already running before the thought finished forming, shoulder down, shoving through the crowd toward the harbor, the alarm bell hammering overhead like a fist on a door, and the thought — when it finally arrived, arriving the way truth arrives, late and undeniable — was simple:

*If they touch her, there is no door in this world I will not burn to reach them.*

He rounded the corner onto Rope Walk and saw the smoke first — thin, black, rising from the lane by the Drowned Lantern, the sweet chemical reek of lamp-oil set to stone. Then the wolf. A grey shape the size of a yearling bear flowing over a garden wall with something clutched in its jaws — his pack, his spare blades, the room's strongbox — and behind it, spilling from the tavern's burning doorway, three men in grey with long knives and a fourth carrying a net weighted with lead beads.

The wolf hit the fourth man like a landslide.

What followed took four seconds and taught the whole street a lesson about why old stories put wolves in them. Then the shape blurred, buckled, and rose as a woman — Xanthe, blood-spattered, breathing hard, amber eyes finding his across fifty feet of screaming crowd with an expression he'd never seen on her before: not fear. Fury, banked and absolute, the fury of someone watching a second home burn.

"They knew my name," she snarled as she reached him. Not her name — *her name*, the one she'd given him, the false one, the traveler's alias. "The one I made up. They read it off a list, Elodie. Someone gave them a list."

"Later." He caught her arm — the human arm, hard muscle under travel-worn sleeve — and turned them both toward the water. "Culvert under the Marsh road. Can you swim in filth?"

"I'm a wolf. I've eaten worse."

Behind them the Drowned Lantern's roof sagged inward in a bloom of orange sparks, and the alarm bell kept beating, and along the quay grey figures were converging with the unhurried confidence of people who own every road.

Elodie put the burning city at his back and the dark water ahead, and understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own death warrant, that Ossik had been right about one thing more than he'd said aloud.

They weren't chasing a map anymore.

They were chasing them.
