## 9. The Village That Forgot Itself

The smoke told us they were there before the trees parted to show me anything else.

It rose thin and grey from a fold of the valley, the kind of smoke that came from green wood burned by people who had forgotten how fires were meant to be built — or who had never been taught at all. I crouched at the ridgeline with the wind in my face, and Elodie knelt beside me without being asked, which was itself a small miracle. Two days in the blank space had worn the sarcasm off him the way water wears the edge off a coin. He still had his coin, but it was smoother now. Quieter.

"You smell that?" I asked him.

"Smoke." He said it flatly, the way he said most true things. "And under it — something rotting sweet. Old apples, maybe. Or old grief."

"Old grief smells different."

"How would you know?"

"Because I've been carrying mine long enough to learn its scent."

He didn't answer that. He was getting better at not answering my sharpest edges, which I suspected was less courtesy than self-preservation. A man who guides smugglers through mountains learns quickly which silences are safe to keep.

Below us, through a lattice of birch gone strange — their leaves the wrong shade of silver, almost white as bone — lay what had been a village. I knew it had been a village because the shape of one does not leave the land easily. There were hearthstones where houses had been, though the houses themselves were half-collapsed, sagging into their own foundations like tired animals lying down in snow. There was a well with its rope still hanging. There was a lane worn into the earth by generations of feet going somewhere, market or mill or shrine, and the lane ended abruptly in bramble, as if the place it led to had been subtracted from the world while the path to it remained, faithful and foolish, still pointing.

And there were people.

Perhaps thirty of them, moving among the ruins with the unhurried wrongness of deer in a clearing. They wore clothing that had once been fine — I could see embroidery on a sleeve here, good leather there, a child in a coat trimmed with fur that had belonged to some animal of the northern forests — but everything was layered wrong, seasons mixed together, finery over rags, buttons misaligned. Their hair was long and unbound and full of burrs. They moved like people who had lived outdoors so long that the outdoors had gotten into the architecture of their bodies.

"They're alive," Elodie murmured. "Out here. In this."

"People survive worse than erasure," I said, and heard how the bitterness sat in my own voice like a stone in a shoe.

"No," he said slowly, watching them. "I don't think they did. Look at them, Xanthe. Look how they move."

I looked. And I understood what he meant, and understanding it made something cold slide down between my shoulder blades.

They moved *around* each other without ever touching. They did not speak — or if they spoke, it was in fragments too small to carry across the distance. One woman stood at the ruined well for the better part of an hour, turning the rope handle, drawing up bucket after bucket of water and pouring it out again onto the ground, as if she remembered that the motion mattered but not why. A man sat by a cold hearth arranging stones into a pattern, scattering them, arranging them again, his hands moving with the desperate patience of someone trying to solve a puzzle whose pieces kept forgetting their shapes.

"They've lost their names," I said.

Elodie glanced at me. "You can't know that."

"I know it the way you know your god is silent. Not with proof. With the part of me that's Cindral." I stood, brushing pine needles from my knees. "Erasure doesn't kill, Elodie. The usurper learned early that killing makes martyrs, and martyrs make memories. So instead they took the name out from under the town. And a name isn't just what you're called. It's the thread. Pull it out and everything sewn to it comes loose. Your family. Your history. Your own reflection. These people are walking around inside their lives with the middle cut out of them."

"And you can feel it? From up here?"

"I can feel it the way you'd feel a toothache in a mouth that used to sing."

He looked at me then — a long look, the first one he'd given me since we crossed into the blank space that wasn't guarded or assessing or amused. It lasted three heartbeats, maybe four. Then he shouldered his pack and said, "Well. We can't walk past them. They'll see our fire tonight anyway, if they can still remember what fires are for."

"They'll remember," I said. "Fire is older than names."

* * *

We went down into the village the way you enter any wounded thing: slowly, openly, with your hands visible.

The nameless ones saw us before we reached the lane. There was no alarm — that was the worst of it, no alarm, no gathering of sticks or stones, no mothers pulling children close. Instead they turned toward us all at once, thirty faces lifting like flowers finding the sun, and they began, without any signal I could detect, to drift toward us.

Elodie's hand went to his knife. I put my fingers over his wrist and pressed it down.

"Don't," I whispered. "They're not dangerous. They're *hungry*. There's a difference, and it matters."

"Hungry for what?" His voice was very low.

"For anything that remembers."

The first to reach us was a young man, perhaps twenty, tall and hollow-cheeked, wearing a soldier's coat with the insignia ripped away — torn off rather than removed, threads dangling where a crest had been unpicked with fingers or teeth. He stopped two paces away and stared at me with an intensity that should have been frightening and was instead heartbreaking, because it was the stare of someone looking at a door they'd forgotten existed in a wall they'd memorized.

Then he spoke, and the sound he made was almost words.

"*Veth—*" He stopped. Tried again. "*Vethara... no.*" His face crumpled with effort, a man hauling at a rope whose other end was anchored in nothing. "*Vethara en... en solen... kireth?*" He looked at me wildly, pleadingly, the broken syllables hanging in the air. "*Kireth?*"

My whole body went still.

He was speaking Cindrali. Shattered Cindrali, worn down to gravel, but Cindrali — *vethara*, the word for dawn-light through mist; *kireth*, an old word, a borderland word, meaning *home-place* or possibly *hearth-right*, depending on which grandmother you asked. He was speaking my language the way a drowning man swims. Badly. Instinctively. With everything he had.

"Yes," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let it crack. "*Kireth.* Yes. Hearth-right. Home." I took another step toward him, hands open. "Say it again."

But he couldn't. Whatever had risen in him sank again, and his face went smooth and vacant as a pond after a stone settles, and he simply stood there breathing, having come all the way across the ruin of his life to say two words and failed.

The others gathered closer. They ringed us at a respectful distance, thirty-odd souls in mismatched clothes, and I felt their attention like warmth from a banked fire. Several of them had begun making sounds — low, humming, half-melodic. It took me a moment to understand that they were humming a song. Not any song. The melody kept fracturing, restarting, losing itself, but underneath the damage I caught its bones and my eyes stung, because I knew it. Every Cindrali child knew it. It was the lullaby of the western valleys, the one about the river that carries the moon to sleep.

They didn't have the words anymore. But they had the tune, and the tune was holding them together the way a keel holds a hull.

"Xanthe." Elodie's voice at my ear, careful. "What do we do?"

"We stay the night," I said. "We feed them if we can. And tomorrow—" I looked around at the collapsed houses, the faithful foolish lane, the woman still drawing water from the well and pouring it out, pouring it out, pouring it out. "Tomorrow we take anyone who wants to come. Because whatever's left in them is Cindral, and Cindral is what I'm walking toward. Maybe they're not just survivors, Elodie. Maybe they're a map too."

* * *

We built our fire in the shell of what must have been the village's meeting hall, because its hearth was the largest and its stones still bore soot from years deeper than memory could reach. Elodie worked at it with his usual economy — kindling, breath, flint — and within minutes had a proper blaze going, and the nameless ones crept toward it in ones and twos like birds returning to a branch after a storm.

That was when Elodie opened his pack.

I watched him do it. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head, because Elodie Eriksen guarded his rations the way misers guard gold and I had teased him about it for weeks — the way he counted out dried meat and hardtack like a priest dispensing sacraments, exactly enough, never a crumb more. But now, without ceremony, without asking me, without appearing to make any decision at all, he laid out nearly everything he carried. Smoked fish. Flatbread. The wax-wrapped cheese he'd hoarded since Marrowgate. A bag of dried plums. He arranged it all on a spread cloth with the unconscious precision of a man setting an altar, and then he stepped back from it and gestured the nearest of the nameless ones forward.

"Go on," he said gruffly, in trade-tongue, and when they only stared, he picked up a piece of bread and held it out. "Go on. Eat."

The young man who had tried to speak took it first. Then others. They ate strangely at first — hesitantly, some of them weeping as they chewed, though I don't think they knew why — and then with a ferocity that made my chest ache, because hunger that old has layers, and the deepest layer isn't in the belly.

"You're giving away half our food," I said quietly, kneeling beside him at the fire's edge.

"We've got a map that leads to a country that doesn't exist," he said, not looking at me. "Rations seem like a small problem."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's not." He tore a piece of flatbread, and I expected him to eat it, but instead he held it out to me, and his eyes when I took it were unreadable and warm at the same time, which should not be possible and was. "Here. You gave up yours to the mother with the baby. I saw."

"I didn't give it up. She needed it."

"So did you. You're the one who has to shift, when it comes to that. Shifting on an empty stomach is misery. I've seen wolves hunt in winter." He shrugged, elaborately casual, the shrug of a man who had said something enormous and was praying to every silent god including his own that nobody noticed. "Eat, Xanthe."

I ate. The bread tasted like nothing special and like everything at once. Around us the fire cracked, and the nameless ones hummed their broken lullaby, and outside the wind moved through birches the color of bone, and I thought: *this is what it feels like. This is what I've been walking toward without knowing. Someone seeing what you need before you ask.*

I hated him a little for it. It's easier to walk alone.

* * *

She came to us near midnight, when the fire had settled to coals and most of the nameless ones slept in heaps of shared wool like dogs in a kennel, touching each other now, finally, in sleep, where the forgetting couldn't reach.

She was old — old the way winter oaks are old, bent but rooted — and she crossed the hall with a stick of blackened wood in her hand and stopped before Elodie, staring down at the pack resting beside him. At the corner of parchment protruding from it. At the seal stamped in red wax that I had glimpsed a hundred times and stopped truly seeing: the sun-and-thorn of House Vessarine, royal mark of Cindral, last pressed by a queen who died believing her country would be remembered.

The old woman made a sound I will hear until I die.

It was small. A single syllable, shapeless, the sound of a hinge that hasn't turned in twenty years. Then she dropped to her knees on the stone floor, joints cracking, and reached for the map with both hands, and Elodie — bless his cautious, cynical, decent heart — drew it out and unfolded it for her instead of pulling it away.

Her fingers hovered above the seal without touching it, trembling, the way you hover over the face of someone sleeping whom you're afraid to wake.

"*Solenmarch,*" she whispered. "*Solenmarch. Vethara en Solenmarch. Kireth.*"

Dawnlight. Dawnlight over the home-place. It wasn't a sentence. It was a prayer, or the wreckage of one, or perhaps the oldest kind of sentence there is: a name, spoken at last, after twenty years of silence.

Then she wept. Not quietly. She wept the way the erased must weep when remembering returns to them even for a moment — hugely, gracelessly, her whole shrunken body shaking with it, tears cutting clean lines through the grime of years. Others woke and gathered around her, drawn by the sound, and some of them began to weep too without knowing why, grief passing through the crowd like current through water, and the old woman lifted her face to me and said, in that same shattered tongue, in the voice of every grandmother I had ever lost:

"*En kireth. En kireth vethar.*" Our home. Our home dawns.

I knelt and took her hands. They were bird-boned and filthy and strong.

"What's your name?" I asked her gently, in Cindrali, knowing, knowing what the answer would be.

She blinked at me. Her brow furrowed. She searched herself the way the young man had searched himself, hauling on that rope anchored in nothing, and after a long time she shook her head, and her expression was not despair but something worse — mild puzzlement, the puzzlement of a woman asked a question she felt she ought to find easy.

"I don't..." she said in trade-tongue, slow and wondering. "I had one. Didn't I? I'm sure I had one. My mother chose it. I can almost hear her choosing it."

"She did choose it," I said. "And it's waiting for you. All of them are waiting. We're going to go and get them back."

She looked at me then — really looked, into me — and whatever she saw in my face, in my accent, in the blood she could probably smell beneath my skin, she nodded once, solemn as a coronation, and gripped my hands tighter.

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Elodie watched us with an expression I had never seen on him. Not pity. Pity I could have borne. It was something more like recognition — the look of a man watching someone else pray to a god he'd given up on, and finding, to his own visible astonishment, that the prayer was reaching him too.

* * *

I took the second watch, because I wanted to be alone with the night and the village and the size of what I was feeling, which was too large to carry in company.

The blank space had its own darkness. No honest stars — the constellations here were smudged, uncertain, as if the sky too had been made to forget which stories its lights were telling. I sat against the meeting hall's broken wall with my knees drawn up and let the cold sharpen me, and I thought about the usurper sitting in some warm palace in a capital whose name everyone knew, signing papers, drinking wine, while thirty people in a valley poured water on the ground because they'd forgotten what wells were for.

Hate is a clean fuel. I fed it gladly.

Footsteps behind me. I knew them before I turned — Elodie walked the way he did everything, economically, heel-first, a man perpetually conserving something. He lowered himself onto the wall beside me without asking and without speaking, and for a while we just sat there, two exiles of different countries, watching the embers breathe.

"The old woman," he said at last. "When she touched the map. What did she call it?"

"Solenmarch. The dawn-country. It was the western provinces' old name, before the capital standardized everything. It means she remembers further back than the erasure. Deeper than they dug." I hugged my knees tighter. "There's a well in her, Elodie, under all that forgetting, and it's still connected to the aquifer. If we can reach it—"

"Then the Door might reach them too." He said it carefully, testing the weight of it. "If the Door gives the country back its name..."

"It gives *them* back. Everyone it was pulled out of. Every village, every ledger line, every grandmother's name." My voice was shaking and I let it. "Do you understand what we're carrying? It's not a map. It was never a map. It's a debt, and someone wrote it down so it couldn't be forgiven."

Beside me, Elodie was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough in a way I hadn't heard before, stripped of irony.

"My god went silent the day Cindral fell. Everyone says it's coincidence. I spent years deciding it was abandonment." He turned his head and looked at me, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "Tonight a woman who can't remember her own name recognized a dead queen's seal on sight. So I'm starting to think I've been wrong about silence. About what it means. About what's on the other side of it."

"And what do you think is there?"

"I don't know." He almost smiled — a real one, small and crooked and exhausted. "But I'm starting to think it's worth finding out. And that I'm glad I'm not finding out alone."

I didn't answer. Some things break if you touch them too soon. But I shifted, deliberately, until my shoulder rested against his — wolf-warm against human cold — and he didn't move away, and the embers breathed, and somewhere below us the nameless ones slept touching each other, holding on to whatever the night still let them hold.

It was nearly dawn when I saw it — a flicker at the valley's far rim, brief as a struck match. Light where no traveler would light a fire. Then a second flicker, answering it, farther east.

Signal fires. Coordinated. Deliberate.

Someone was in the blank space with us, and they were talking to each other in the dark.
