## 12. Wolf's Winter

The snow came down like a verdict.

Xanthe felt it first in her bad shoulder — the one the inquisitor's crossbow bolt had torn open two days ago, before the skirmish at the tree line, before Elodie had carried her half a mile with her arm slung over his neck and his curses melting white in the air. Now the wound ached in time with the weather, deep and rhythmic, like something knocking to be let in. She lay on her side near the cave's mouth and watched the storm erase the world beyond it: the pines going grey, then ghostly, then gone altogether, until there was nothing left but whiteness falling through whiteness and the small orange island of their fire.

Elodie sat with his back against the stone, whittling. He had been whittling for an hour. She suspected he did not know what he was making; she suspected that was the point of it. His hands needed occupation the way other men's mouths needed prayer — and she had seen him pray, or perform whatever hollow pantomime of prayer he still allowed himself each dawn, lips moving toward a god who had not answered him in twenty years. It was the only lie she had ever caught him telling himself, and she found she could not hate him for it. She knew what it was to keep faith with an absence.

"You're bleeding through," he said, without looking up.

"I'm aware."

"The bandage needs changing."

"It's fine."

"It isn't." He set the half-carved thing down — a wolf, she saw now, rough as a child's attempt — and came to kneel beside her, and she let him, because the fever had made her stubbornness expensive and they both knew it. His fingers were careful at the edge of the linen, peeling it back where the blood had dried it to her skin. She watched his face while he worked: the furrow between his brows, the way his breath went shallow when he saw how angry the wound looked, red-rimmed and weeping. He was not a gentle man by nature. She had watched him bargain ruthlessly, lie fluently, kill without ceremony when the mountains required it. But his hands, she had learned, were another country entirely. In them he was fluent.

"This should be stitched again," he murmured.

"Then stitch it."

"With what? I used the last of the gut-thread at the pass." He tore a strip from his own shirt instead, boiled it briefly in the tin pot, let it cool, and packed the wound with snow-melt and the bitter paste of willow bark he'd been chewing into pulp since morning. It hurt like a brand. Xanthe kept her eyes open and fixed on the fire because closing them would have been a surrender, and because — though she would have died before admitting it — she liked watching him work. There was a steadiness in him that her whole life had lacked. Everything she loved had been taken apart and hidden from history; this man reassembled things. Bandages. Snares. A broken flint striker. Small salvations, performed without being asked.

"There," he said. "Good as new."

"Liar."

"Competent liar," he corrected, settling back beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off his shoulder. "There's a difference. Ask anyone in Marrowgate."

* * *

The storm held them for three days.

On the second day the meat ran low and Elodie went out into the white roar and came back an hour later with nothing but frost in his beard and a look she pretended not to recognize — the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. On the third day her fever broke in the small hours, sweating and shuddering against his bedroll while he fed her melted snow in sips and talked nonsense to keep her anchored: names of passes, prices of contraband, a long complaint about a client in Marrowgate who had once paid him in counterfeit prayer-coins. When dawn came she was weak as a newborn foal but clear-headed, and the clear-headedness brought with it a clarity of its own, cold and inevitable, sitting in her chest like swallowed ice.

She was going to have to tell him.

She had known it since the shrine — since the frost on Vael's cold altar had pressed out that single word, *CINDRAL*, and Elodie had come to her pale as the stone itself and said, *my god may be imprisoned, not absent*, in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant. Since then he had walked differently. Quieter. Asking questions she deflected with jokes about royal privilege. But the mountain was narrowing around them now, the map's final pages pulling them east and down, and every mile made the lie she had been living heavier. And she had watched him lie for her, once — had stood behind him while he faced down an inquisitor with a falsehood so smooth it had frightened her — and she understood, with the terrible precision of the wounded, that a man who lies well for you has already decided you are worth more than his own soul. She owed him better than the truth she'd been rationing.

He was whittling again when she finally spoke.

"The Door isn't what you think it is."

His knife paused. He didn't turn. "No?"

"You think it's a way out. A smuggler's route, a gap in the usurper's net, some road the maps forgot. That's why you've been measuring every tunnel we've passed, checking sightlines, calculating loads." She pulled the blanket higher. "It's not a road, Elodie. It never was."

"What is it, then?"

"The heart." The word came out of her smaller than she intended. "Cindral's heart. Literally. The old capital wasn't built above the mountains, it was built *into* them — the city my grandmother ruled from, the one the erasure buried rather than destroyed. At its center is a threshold of white stone, older than the dynasty, older probably than your church. When the usurper erased the country, he didn't just burn records and murder archivists. He took the name itself — the true name, the one that made Cindral real in treaties and memory and law — and he locked it away behind that stone. That's why villages forget themselves. Why roads loop. Why birdsong stops mid-phrase. We've been walking through the wound this whole time."

The knife resumed its small, absent carving. "And the map."

"The map is the key to the lock. Part of it." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "My family spent generations guarding it. Do you understand what I inherited, Elodie? Not a crown. A piece of parchment and a duty. Every heir before me learned the routes, memorized the seals, and waited for the day someone would need it. Then the erasure came, and everyone who might have needed it was unmade, and I ran. I took the last thing my house owned and I *ran*." Her voice frayed on the last word. She let it. "The stranger who gave it to you was carrying it back toward me. Toward the door. He died so it would reach the right hands, and instead it reached yours."

"And mine are the wrong ones."

"Your hands are the wrong ones," she agreed, gently, "but they're the ones that kept it out of Hale's. So."

Silence, except the fire and the wind arguing at the cave mouth. Elodie turned the little wooden wolf over in his fingers. When he spoke again his voice was carefully level, the smuggler's voice, the negotiating voice, and she hated hearing it because it meant he was hurting.

"So we walk to this Door. You open it with the map. Cindral comes back. Simple."

"No."

"No?"

"The map opens the outer seal. The threshold itself—" She stopped. Started again. "A life freely given. Someone sworn to Vael. That's the covenant the stone was cut under; it's carved in the old script along the lintel, if you know how to read it. My blood is the right blood — the royal line, the witness-line — but blood alone isn't the payment. It has to be given. Freely. By someone bound to the silent god." She met his eyes at last. "That's why I followed you for two nights before I showed myself. Why I offered partnership instead of taking the map off your body while you slept, which I could have done twice over. Because I need you alive, Elodie. More than I ever needed the map. The map is worthless without the man who carries it."

He was very still. Outside, the wind dropped for a moment, and in the sudden hush she could hear the snow ticking against the stones, fine as sand.

"You mean to die," he said. "At this door. That's the plan you've been carrying since Marrowgate. Since before Marrowgate."

"My grandmother planned it before I was born. It's the family trade."

"And you let me— " He stopped. His jaw worked. When his voice came again it had lost all its smoothness, and she realized she had never heard him sound young before, and grieved it even as it moved her. "I lied to an inquisitor for you. I burned a contract worth more than everything I own. I chose the impossible road because I watched you teach nameless people their own tongue by firelight, and I thought — I thought I was choosing something *living*. And the whole time you were walking me toward an altar."

"Yes."

"You could have told me."

"When?" she asked, and heard her own voice go hard. "In Marrowgate, when the brokers were selling us out? On the blank roads, when you were already afraid of the ground we walked on? If I'd told you at the start you'd have sold the map by morning and been right to. Don't insult us both. The truth was a weight neither of us could carry until now."

"And now?"

"Now the storm decides for me." She gestured at the white wall sealing the cave. "Three days snowbound, meat running out, inquisitors behind us and the Door ahead. Whatever happens next, you deserve to walk toward it knowing what it costs. That's all this is. Not a confession — an accounting."

He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "You're wrong."

"About which part?"

"All of it. About the accounting." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and stared into the fire, and when he spoke again it was slow, as if each word were being tested before use. "You think you're the only one who's been planning a sacrifice. I've been praying to a dead god for ten years out of guilt, Xanthe. Guilt for the runs I guided, the things I carried for people whose faces I never asked about. I stopped believing Vael listened because listening would have required me to change, and I didn't want to change. And then a frost on an altar spelled one word and I've been terrified ever since — not of the word. Of hoping." He looked up at her. "If your Door is real, then my god isn't dead. He's *holding something shut*. Twenty years, holding a door closed from inside, alone in the dark under a murdered country, and I wrote him off like a bad client." A breath. "So no. This isn't your accounting. It's ours. And I'm not letting you pay it alone."

"Elodie—"

"You need someone sworn to Vael. Fine. But you don't get to decide which of us the stone asks for. That debt has two names on it now."

"That's not how covenants work."

"Neither is smuggling," he said, "and I'm excellent at it." He picked the little wooden wolf back up and set it in her palm, closing her fingers over it. His hand stayed there, warm over hers, and neither of them moved. "Get some sleep. When the storm breaks, we move."

She meant to argue. She meant to tell him about the visions her grandmother had described, the exact wording of the covenant, the reasons the stone would refuse him and take her whether he willed it or not. Instead she found herself watching the firelight move across his face and thinking, with a clarity that had nothing to do with wounds or fevers: *oh. Oh, no.* Not the dramatic grief she had braced for. Just this — the quiet catastrophe of wanting someone to live, discovered too late to be convenient.

She closed her hand around the wolf.

"Elodie," she said, when he had banked the fire and lain down facing her across the coals' last light. "Why did you really burn the contract?"

A pause. Long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Because you laughed," he said finally. "Teaching those villagers. You laughed, and it was the first time anything in this whole cursed country had sounded like it belonged to it." Another pause. "Go to sleep, Xanthe."

But she lay awake a long while after his breathing slowed, turning the little wooden wolf over in her fingers, listening to the storm spend itself against the mountain — and somewhere beneath the wind, faint and rising, she began to hear something else. Far below the stone. Faint as a pulse through a wall.

Singing.
