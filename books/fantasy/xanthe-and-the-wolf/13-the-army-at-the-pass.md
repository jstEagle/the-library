## 13. The Army at the Pass

The smoke came first.

Elodie saw it two ridges over before dawn — a gray smudge against a sky still bruised with night, rising from the valley where the Thorn River bent south toward the lowlands. He had spent eleven years learning to read the mountains, and the mountains had never lied to him. Woodsmoke rose in thin threads from hearths. Forge-smoke rose in fat columns. But this was neither. This was the smoke of ten thousand cookfires banked together into a single breathing haze, and it meant only one thing: an army had come to sleep at the foot of his mountains.

He crouched on the granite shelf above the tree line and watched it resolve as the light grew. The vanguard's tents sprawled along the river flats like a skin disease, pale canvas blisters on the brown earth. Banners hung slack in the windless morning — black fields bearing the usurper's mark, the closed eye. Supply wagons crawled up the military road in a line so long its far end vanished into the southern haze. Somewhere down there, horns were sounding the change of watch, thin and brass-bright, carrying all the way up to where he stood.

"They're early," Xanthe said beside him.

She had come up without a sound, which still unsettled him after weeks of travel. She stood wrapped in her gray cloak, hood back despite the cold, her breath pluming white. Her eyes — amber even in human shape, amber always — tracked the valley below with an expression Elodie had learned to fear on her face: recognition. She wasn't looking at an army. She was looking at the thing that had eaten her country, come to eat another.

"Early," Elodie said. "Or we're late. Depends whose calendar you're keeping."

"The free cities' calendar." Her voice was flat. "Marrowgate has been arguing about whether to raise a levy since before we left. They'll have argued for another month by now. Then another month to agree on who commands it." She pulled the cloak tighter. "The vanguard is here in autumn. The main host will follow behind it like a shadow follows the body. By first snow they'll hold the river crossings. By thaw—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. By thaw, Marrowgate would be a name that no one could remember, and the people who had sung in its markets would be standing in some other town wondering why they felt like they'd lost something they couldn't name.

Elodie looked at the valley and did the arithmetic he was good at — distances, gradients, the load-bearing capacity of mountain paths, how many men could march a day and how many mules it took to feed them. It was the same arithmetic he used for smuggling runs, and it gave him no comfort now. An army this size moved like weather. You didn't fight it. You got out from under it or you drowned.

"We should keep moving," he said. "The high passes are still open. Two days of hard walking puts us past anything they can reach before winter."

Xanthe glanced at him sideways. "Past it," she repeated. "Not away from it."

"There's a difference?"

"No," she said quietly. "I suppose there isn't."

* * *

They came down off the ridge at midday and found the world already changed.

The shepherd hamlet of Brant's Hollow sat in the fold of the mountain like a handful of gray stones, twelve houses and a shrine and a well that never froze. Elodie had watered stock there a dozen times over the years, had traded salt for cheese with old Marta who ran the place with three grandsons and a bad hip. He walked in expecting bread and gossip and maybe a bed of straw in the byre if the weather turned.

What he found was a village packing itself onto carts.

The whole population of Brant's Hollow — nine souls now, he counted; someone had already gone ahead — was loading mattresses, goats, a loom, a cradle, sacks of flour stamped with the Marrowgate assay mark. The shrine door stood open and empty. Someone had carried the god out too, a little painted figure of the Shepherd swaddled in cloth like a sick child.

Marta's eldest grandson saw him first. "Eriksen." The boy's voice cracked on the name. He was sixteen and trying to be a man about it and mostly failing. "You heard, then."

"I heard smoke," Elodie said. "Tell me the rest."

"Outriders came through yesterday." The boy kept glancing south, as though the road itself might grow teeth. "Wearing the closed eye. Polite ones, if you can believe it. Said the army's coming up the Thorn road and honest folk should move along. Said there'd be a census after." He spat. His hands were shaking while he did it. "A *census*, Eriksen. My gran says that's what they did in Cindral. Counted everyone first. Wrote everyone down. And then—"

"And then the names went out like candles," Marta finished. She came around the cart, small and iron-backed, and looked Elodei up and down the way she'd looked at him when he was nineteen and lying about his age to get work on the caravan roads. "You're the one they're saying has the map."

The words went through him colder than the wind off the glacier.

"Am I," he said.

"Don't." Marta's voice dropped. "Don't do that with me. A peddler came through two days ago with his ears full of every rumor between here and Marrowgate. Says there's a smuggler and a wolf-woman carrying something the usurper wants badly enough to burn half the range for." She gripped his sleeve with fingers like knotted rope. "I don't care what it is. I'm telling you because my boys will take the lake road east, and east is where the army isn't, yet. If you're going west, don't bring whatever you're carrying through my village again. I've buried enough. I won't bury names too."

Elodie held her gaze and felt the map's weight inside his coat like a second heart. "Thank you, Marta."

"Thank me by being somewhere else when they get here." She released him, softened by a degree. "You were a rude child and you've become a rude man, but you never once stole from us, and in these mountains that's near sainthood." She turned back to her cart. "Go on, then. Both of you. Whatever you are to each other."

Xanthe, who had hung back at the edge of the square, inclined her head to the old woman — the deep, formal inclination of Cindral, elbow to palm — and Marta stared at the gesture as though a ghost had bowed to her. Then they walked out of Brant's Hollow, and behind them the last cart groaned under its load, and the village began to unmake itself voluntarily, house by house, before the usurper could do it worse.

* * *

*They counted them first,* Xanthe thought, as the trail climbed away from the hollow. *That's how it began with us, too. Not with fire. With ink.*

She remembered — in fragments, always fragments, her memories of Cindral like shards of stained glass held up to a light that kept shifting — men with ledgers going door to door in the capital's outskirts. Her mother's steward letting them in with the smooth courtesy due to any royal functionary. The scratch of pens. Her father laughing afterward, saying let them write what they like, paper holds nothing that blood doesn't hold better.

Paper had held everything. Paper had taken everything. Every name written in those ledgers became a name that could be struck out, and when the striking came, it came not as slaughter but as absence — streets she could no longer picture, a nurse whose face slid out of her mind like water through fingers, her own language going strange in her mouth for whole minutes at a time until she bit her tongue hard enough to bleed and clawed it back.

Now the ledgers were coming to the Thornfell, and the free cities were arguing about procedure.

She caught herself baring her teeth at the trail and made herself stop. Elodie was twenty paces ahead, moving with that economy she both envied and distrusted, reading the ground like a priest read scripture. He had not asked her what she was thinking. He rarely did anymore. Somewhere in the last hundred miles he had stopped treating her silences as problems to be solved and started simply leaving room for them, and she found she hated how much that mattered to her.

Below them, the valley smoked. Above them, the pass waited, white and indifferent.

Two more days, and they would stand at the Door. Two more days, and she would ask him for the thing she had no right to ask — the truth of what opening it required, which she had carried alone since the cave, since the snow, since she had told him *only her blood* and watched him absorb it the way stone absorbs rain, showing nothing, holding everything.

She had told him part of it. That had been necessary; he'd earned it, dragging her wounded body through three miles of drifts when her own shape betrayed her mid-shift. But she had not told him the rest — that the Door took a life freely given, that her plan had always ended with her own throat, and that every mile since the cave had been her quietly arranging the ending so he couldn't interfere.

And lately — this was the treacherous part, the part that kept her awake at the fire's edge while he slept — lately she had begun to wonder whether she wanted to live more than she wanted her country back, and whether wanting that made her a coward or just a woman who had finally found one person worth staying for.

The trail switchbacked. She followed him up.

* * *

They smelled woodsmoke again at dusk, but this time it was one fire, deliberate, built in the lee of a waystone where the old smuggler road crossed the shoulder of Mount Karrick. Elodie stopped fifty yards out and put his arm across Xanthe's chest without thinking about it, and she let him, and he noted distantly that she let him.

A voice floated up from the fire. "If that's Eriksen, he's standing downwind like a guilty man. If it's not Eriksen, I've got a crossbow and poor manners."

Elodie exhaled. "Doss."

A laugh rolled up the slope. "Get down here before your supper gets cold. I cooked for four, but wolves eat like wolves, don't they?"

The man by the fire was broad as a doorway and going gray at the temples, sitting on his pack like a king on a throne too small for him. Dov "Doss" Ferren — once the best wheelman on the western routes, once Elodie's partner for three years that had felt like thirty, now grown prosperous running goods through the free cities and grown careful running anything at all. He looked older than his years. Everyone did lately.

"Doss Ferren," Elodie said, "in the high passes in autumn, cooking for four."

"For six, actually." Doss jerked his chin at the shadows beyond the fire, where two shapes resolved — hard-faced women with short swords and the flat stare of hired steel. "But the other two are watching the trail, so mind your manners." His eyes flicked to Xanthe, widened fractionally, and came back to Elodie with a new wariness in them. "So it's true, then. The rumors. There really is a wolf of Cindral walking the Thornfell with the last honest smuggler in the mountains."

"Rumors are getting specific."

"Rumors are getting expensive." Doss leaned forward. "There's a price on this road, Eriksen. On the pair of you. Enough gold to buy a ship. Enough to buy a *life*. And enough inquisitors crawling these slopes to make collection a real possibility." He spread his hands. "Which brings me to why I'm here, burning good lamp oil on a mountaintop waiting for a man who may or may not want to see me."

"You knew I'd come this way."

"I hoped. You always were sentimental about the Karrick crossing." Doss reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, oilskin-wrapped, and laid it on the flat stone between them with the reverence of a man setting down a relic. "Passage. Real passage, not smuggler's promises. The *Kindly Tide*, out of Vell Harbor on the coast — Captain Ostra owes me more than she admits and hates the usurper more than she owes me. She'll take two passengers, no questions, no manifest, no names written anywhere. South along the shore, then west, past every port the closed eye flies over, to the Free Isles." He tapped the paper once. "Once you're there, no writ reaches you. No ledger touches you. No inquisitor can find you, because there'll be nowhere left that remembers you were ever worth finding." He smiled, and for a moment it was the old smile, the one from the road years, warm as brandy. "It's a good offer, Elodie. It's the best offer either of you will ever get. I staked my own cut of the season to buy it, so I'd take it personally if you laughed at me."

The wind moved through the pass above them, keening faintly against the waystone, and Elodie found he couldn't speak.

He looked at the paper. He thought about what it was: a door. A real one, this time — not the sealed heart of a dead country, not a threshold that drank oaths and lives, but a plain human door that opened onto sea air and anonymity and mornings without anyone hunting him. He thought about waking in the Free Isles with salt on the wind and no god's silence pressing on his chest like a hand. He thought about Xanthe alive. That was the thing that undid him — the image of her alive, old, her hair going silver, her amber eyes still sharp, walking a market street where no inquisitor would ever look twice at her.

And underneath it, quieter, the other image: the villagers of the nameless places, the old woman who had wept at the seal, Marta's grandson with his shaking hands, the whole soft world of the free cities waiting for the ledgers to arrive.

"How long," he managed, "do the free cities have?"

Doss's smile faded. "A season. Maybe less. Marrowgate's walls are good and its people are stubborn, but stubbornness starves like anything else does. The army down there—" he nodded southward, toward the distant smudge of the vanguard's fires, visible now in the blue dusk like fallen stars— "that's just the first finger of the fist. There are forty thousand more coming up the Thorn road behind it." He paused. "Unless something changes. Unless somebody does whatever it is you two are climbing this mountain to do." He held up a hand. "I don't want to know. Knowing is how you end up in a cellar in Marrowgate answering questions. But I can count, Eriksen. I can see which way the season's blowing. So here's my offer, and it comes with no strings, because you taught me that much decency back when we ran the salt together: the *Kindly Tide* sails from Vell Harbor in nine days. Be there, and it's yours. Don't be there, and I'll drink to you and mean it." He pushed the paper across the stone. "Take it. Even if you never use it. Take it anyway."

Elodie took it. The oilskin was cold and slick in his fingers.

Beside him, Xanthe had not moved. She stood with the firelight carving her profile out of the dark, watching him with an expression he couldn't read — or wouldn't let himself read, because if he read it wrong it would break something in him that was already cracked.

"Nine days," she said softly.

"Nine days," Doss confirmed. He looked between them, and something kind and tired moved across his heavy face. "I'm going to walk down to check my watchers now. Very slowly. For quite a while." He rose, gathered his coat, and clapped Elodie on the shoulder with a hand like a paving stone. "Whatever you decide," he said quietly, close to Elodie's ear, "decide it looking at her, not at the map. That's free advice from an old partner, and it's worth more than the passage." Then he was gone down the darkening trail, whistling an old road song, his footsteps fading into the vast hush of the mountain.

The fire crackled. Far to the south, the enemy's thousand cookfires burned their patient, terrible stars into the valley floor.

Xanthe spoke without turning her head. "He's right, you know. About the tide. If you take it — if *we* take it — no one could touch us. We could be nobody. I've spent my whole life being somebody the world wanted erased. Being nobody sounds almost like paradise." Her jaw tightened. "And every village between here and the coast would go out like candles while we sailed away warm and safe and *nobody*."

"You don't have to argue me into it," Elodie said. "Or out of it."

"Don't I?" Now she turned, and the firelight showed him her eyes, and what was in them was not the wolf and not the exile but something younger and more frightened than either. "Then what are you waiting for, Elodie Eriksen? Tell me what you're going to do. Because I've been lying to you since the cave — not about the Door, not about what it needs, but about who pays it. I made my choice a long time ago and I've spent every mile since making sure you couldn't unmake it. And now this—" her hand trembled toward the oilskin packet— "now *this*, a real escape, a real life, dangled in front of us like the last kindness of a cruel world, and I find I can't think straight, because for the first time since Cindral fell I *want* something for myself, and I don't know if that makes me a traitor to my country or finally honest with myself."

The wind gusted, scattering sparks. Above them the waystone hummed faintly, the way old things hum when the weather is about to turn.

Elodie looked at the passage paper in his hand. Then, as Doss had told him to, he looked at her.

"Come with me tomorrow," he said. "Not to Vell Harbor. Up. To the last page of the map. There's something I have to show you, something I found in the frost at the cold altar, something I haven't told you because I needed to be sure I wasn't mad." His hand closed around the oilskin, not pocketing it, not burning it — holding it, the way a man holds a coin he hasn't yet decided to spend. "And then, Xanthe — when you know what I know — we decide together. Both doors. All the way open. No more lies between us. Agreed?"

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she stepped closer to the fire, into the warmth, and nodded once — the smallest movement, but it carried the weight of an oath.

"Agreed," she said. "But Elodie — whatever's written in that frost of yours—" her voice dropped to almost nothing— "it had better be worth what it's about to cost us. Because I felt it too, tonight. The mountain's changed. Something's coming up behind us faster than any army."

Far down the dark trail, where Doss and his watchers kept their vigil, a single whistle sounded — two notes, sharp and urgent, the old smuggler signal for riders on the road.

Inquisitors rode horses. These were not horses.
