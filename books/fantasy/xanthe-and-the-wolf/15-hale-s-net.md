## 15. Hale's Net

The smugglers sang when they were drunk, and Inquisitor Hale had learned that a man's songs told you more than his ledgers ever would.

He sat in the back room of the Drowned Lantern, a tavern in Marrowgate's river quarter where the ceiling sweated and the lamps burned tallow that smelled of old funerals, and he listened to the last of Corvess's crew sing a ballad about a girl who married the sea. His hands were folded on the table before him, as they always were. His face was arranged into an expression of mild, patient sorrow — the expression he wore for interrogations, for executions, for the reading of sentences to weeping families. It was not cruelty. It was never cruelty. It was the look of a physician delivering a diagnosis he could do nothing about.

Around him, four of his acolytes stood with their hoods back, their faces bare, because Hale believed the erased deserved to see who unmade them. *If we must be the knife,* he had told them once, *we will at least be an honest knife.* Two of them had wept after that sermon. He had counted it a good sign. Men who wept were men who understood what they carried.

Corvess himself was bound to a chair in the center of the room, one eye swollen shut from the taking of him — his men had fought, briefly, stupidly, and two of them would not sing anything again. The rest had been gathered like wet leaves. The map itself was not among Corvess's effects. That fact sat in Hale's stomach like a swallowed stone.

"The Eriksen man," Hale said quietly. "Tell me about him."

Corvess spat blood onto the floorboards. "You'll get nothing—"

"Matthias." Hale said the name gently, and watched the smuggler flinch, because no one had used his given name in eleven years, since the raid on the salt piers, since the ledger of his debts had passed into Hale's keeping. "You have a daughter in Lowmark. Ilsa. She is seven. She draws horses badly and loves them completely. When this is finished — whatever we find here tonight — she will grow up in a country that remembers her name. That is what I am offering you. Not your life. Your *name*, kept."

The silence stretched. One of the acolytes shifted by the door.

"There was a buyer," Corvess said finally, hoarse. "A fence called Ombrey. The map went through his hands and came back out again inside a day. Everyone who touched it got scared off. But the Eriksen man — he didn't sell it. He walked north out of the city with a woman. Dark-haired. Walked like—" Corvess hesitated, searching. "Walked like the ground belonged to her."

Hale felt something turn over behind his ribs.

"A woman," he repeated.

"Foreign accent. Old accent. My man Vetch heard her speak — said it sounded like words that had been *buried*." Corvess laughed, a wet, broken sound. "Buried words. What does that even mean?"

It meant Cindral. Hale did not say so. He stood, smoothed the front of his grey coat, and inclined his head to Corvess with something very close to respect.

"Thank you, Matthias. Your daughter will be remembered."

He meant it as mercy. He knew, walking up the stairs into the cold river air, that it would land as a curse.

* * *

They rode out of Marrowgate before dawn, nine riders under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and Hale spent the first hours of the road composing his report in his head the way other men composed prayers.

*The map survives. The bearer moves north along the Thornfell spine, toward the pass the surveyors cannot chart. With him travels a woman of Cindral blood — the heir, if my reading of the old genealogies holds. She means to reach the Door.*

And then the part he could not yet write, because writing it made it real: *and I am no longer certain that stopping her is mercy.*

Doctrine was simple. Doctrine said: twenty-two years ago, the Concordat of Ash unmade Cindral — its name from every treaty, its shape from every map, its dead from every memory but the ones too stubborn or too guilty to forget. Doctrine said the erasure was necessary, because Cindral's last king had opened something that should have stayed shut, and a country that births such things must be amputated like a limb. Doctrine said the forgetting was not punishment but *surgery*. You did not hate the flesh you cut away. You pitied it.

Hale had recited doctrine his whole life. He had held the hands of people being unmade — villagers in the borderlands whose names dissolved even as he spoke comfort to them — and he had whispered the liturgy of oblivion and believed, truly believed, that he was sparing them worse.

But three nights ago, in the village of Harrow's Bend, he had found an old woman sitting in the road, rocking, saying over and over: *I know I loved someone. I can feel the shape of it. Why won't the shape tell me its name?*

He had knelt beside her. He had taken her hands. And for the first time in nineteen years of service, Inquisitor Hale had not known what to say.

* * *

The mountains received them the way a jaw receives a fist.

By the fourth day the road had narrowed to a shelf above a valley full of cloud, and the pines grew sideways out of rock, and the cold found the gaps in everything. Hale's acolyte Brannic, the youngest, rode up alongside him as they climbed.

"Father," Brannic said — they called him Father though Hale had never been ordained to anything but the work — "the scouts ahead report smoke. A camp. Not military."

"Eriksen's?"

"Possibly. Or the nameless. There are ferals in these heights now. Whole villages' worth, living like animals."

Hale reined in. Below them, through a tear in the cloud, the valley opened like a bowl of milk, and far across it he could see the vanguard of the Ninth Column — ten thousand men in the usurper's black-and-gold, supply wagons strung along the valley floor like beads, siege engines wrapped in oilcloth against the snow. An army preparing to hold a pass against enemies that did not officially exist, in a country that did not officially exist, on behalf of a throne that had written the whole question out of history.

*What are we defending?* he thought. *A blank page.*

"Send word to the Column," he said. "I will need authority for what comes next."

Brannic looked at him. Brannic was twenty-three, devout, kind to animals, and had once asked Hale whether the forgotten dreamed. Hale had answered honestly: *no one knows.* The boy had not slept well since.

"What comes next, Father?"

Hale looked north, to where the pass cut through the peaks like a wound that had half-healed. Somewhere beyond it, a woman with buried words in her mouth was walking toward a Door, and a mercenary who had lied for her was walking beside her, and between them and the world stood the thinnest possible margin of Hale's own conviction.

"I am going to request the Loom," he said.

Brannic went white. Every acolyte knew the word; none of them had ever heard it requested outside the vaults of the capital. The Loom of Unmaking — the engine the Concordat had built to erase Cindral, disassembled afterward into three parts and hidden, because even its makers feared it. It did not burn cities. Burning was honest; burning left ash, and ash left grief, and grief kept names alive. The Loom simply *retracted*. Aimed at a mountain pass, it would return the world to a time before the pass existed — and everything standing on it would go with it, gently, into the never-was.

"Father," Brannic whispered, "there are people up there. Nameless ones, maybe. Villagers."

"Yes," Hale said.

"And the Eriksen man. And the woman."

"Yes."

"They'd be—" The boy could not finish it. *Unmade* was the word, and even the faithful flinched from it.

Hale reached out and gripped the young man's shoulder, feeling the fine tremor in it, and hated himself with a clean and total thoroughness that felt almost like devotion.

"They are already lost," he said. "The woman especially. If she reaches that Door, everything the Concordat bought with twenty years of forgetting is forfeit — every treaty, every peace, every grave we emptied to make the world safe. Do you understand? She carries a country in her blood, and countries are heavier than people. I am sorry. I am so sorry. But the arithmetic does not care that we are sorry."

Brannic saluted, eyes wet, and turned his horse down toward the valley, toward the Column, toward the capital and its hidden vaults.

Hale watched him go and thought of the old woman in the road, rocking, mourning a love whose name had been taken before she could bury it properly.

*This is kindness,* he told himself. *This is the kindest thing anyone has ever done to those mountains.*

He did not believe it. That was the worst part. He gave the order anyway.

* * *

The reply came back in three days, sealed with the usurper's sunburst, and Hale read it alone in his tent while the wind worried the guy-lines like a dog at a bone.

*Permission granted. The Second Fragment travels with the Column's engineers. Coordinate timing with the assault on the pass. Let it be recorded that the Inquisitor Hale acted throughout from compassion.*

*Let it be recorded.* He set the letter down and laughed — one short, ugly sound that startled him, because he could not remember the last time he had laughed. Compassion, recorded. Somewhere a clerk would file his pity in triplicate.

That night he walked the perimeter alone. Snow had begun to fall, fat and slow, filling the tracks of the day, erasing in miniature what the Loom would erase in whole. Ahead of him the pass stood black against the stars, and he thought he could see — though he told himself it was only starlight playing tricks — a faint warmth in the stone of the far heights, a glow like embers banked behind a shuttered door.

Like altars. Like altars still warm under the mountain, waiting.

He thought of the woman. He had never seen her face, only heard her described — *walked like the ground belonged to her* — and he found himself hoping, blasphemously, that she would feel the Loom coming. That some instinct older than maps would lift her head and let her run.

Then he thought of Harrow's Bend, and the old woman rocking in the road, and he straightened his coat and went back to his tent to write the orders that would aim the weapon.

*A good man arming an unforgivable act,* some voice in him observed — dry, distant, perhaps his own, perhaps the mountain's.

*Yes,* Hale answered it. *And when the pass is gone, and the blank space is blanker, I will stand on the edge of the nothing I have made and I will remember every name I took. That will have to be enough. There is nothing else.*

Snow fell. The orders went south by fast rider. And high in the Thornfells, in a camp he could not see, a fire burned around two figures he could not name, and the mountain waited, warm-hearted and doomed, for what was coming down upon it.
