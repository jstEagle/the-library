## 16. The Erasure Comes

*Elodie*

He felt it before he heard it — a wrongness in the air, like the held breath before a landslide.

They had made camp in a hollow below the pass, the last of the nameless villagers gathered around a fire built low and smokeless. Old Sera had remembered her name three days ago, weeping it into Xanthe's cupped hands like water from a cracked vessel. The boy called Finch — no, *Fennick*, he'd remembered too, shouting it at the stars until his voice broke — slept against his mother's side. Eleven villagers now. Eleven who could say their own names when asked.

The rest were gone ahead of them into the mountains, or gone altogether, flickering out like candles in a draft while Elodie watched, unable to do anything but hold their hands as they thinned into nothing.

Now the wrongness came again, heavier.

"Get up," he said. "Everyone up. Now."

Xanthe's head lifted from where she'd been teaching Fennick the shapes of Cindral letters traced in ash. She looked at him — really looked — and whatever she saw in his face made her rise without question.

"What is it?"

"I don't know." His hand found the map at his chest, and beneath it, the cold ache that had lived there since Vael's frost-word. "The mountain doesn't like something."

Then the sound came.

Not thunder. Thunder has weight, has echo, rolls down valleys and shakes snow from branches. This was the opposite of thunder — a silence so vast and sudden it pressed against his eardrums like deep water, a silence with *edges*, and Elodie understood, in one lurching instant, that he was hearing a piece of the world stop having ever existed.

The peak to the north — Greytooth, the smugglers called it, the one with the split crown he'd used for navigation since he was sixteen years old — was simply not there.

Not destroyed. Not collapsed into rubble and screaming rockfall. *Absent.* Where its bulk should have risen black against the stars there was only sky, unbroken sky, and a valley floor that continued smoothly to the horizon as though the mountain had never interrupted it. His memory screamed at the impossibility even as his eyes reported it: he knew that skyline. He had drawn that skyline from memory a hundred times for clients. And now the line ran clean where it should have broken, and some part of his mind was already smoothing over the gap, already deciding that the horizon had always looked this way—

"No," he said aloud, to himself, to his own traitorous mind. "Greytooth. There was a mountain called Greytooth." He gripped the words like handholds over a void. "Say it. All of you. Say what you remember."

And the villagers said it — haltingly, Sera loudest, her cracked voice naming peaks and paths and the shrine at Cold Altar, the river with the copper taste, the festival of first thaw — and Elodie felt the words hold, felt them catch like hooks in the fabric of the vanishing, keeping small pockets of the world real through sheer stubborn recitation.

But the mountain stayed gone.

* * *

*Xanthe*

She stood at the lip of the hollow and looked north into the space where Greytooth should have been, and inside her chest something tore along an old seam.

She knew that mountain. Not from maps — from her mother's voice, from lullabies about the shepherd-king who sheltered his flocks in Greytooth's split crown during the Long Winter, from the shape of it she'd traced in spilled sugar on her grandmother's table when she was small enough to sit on that woman's knee. It was in her blood the way rivers are in a valley's. And now it was nowhere, and worse than nowhere: it had never been anywhere, and the world wore the absence like it had always fit.

This was what they had done to Cindral. Not war. Not conquest. *This.* A country peeled out of existence, and every mountain in it, every hearth, every grave, every child — smoothed over, forgotten by the very stones.

Her legs went out from under her.

She didn't remember falling. She was on her knees in the frost-hardened grass and someone was saying her name — her true name, the long one, the Cindral one that only Elodie used now, syllables she'd taught him in the cave during the snowbound weeks because she'd wanted, desperately, to be *known* — and his hands were on her shoulders, solid, warm, human, and she clung to them like a drowning woman.

"It took my mountain," she heard herself say, and her voice came out small and young and seven years old. "It took — Elodie, that's *my* mountain, I climbed it, I — my mother showed me the eagle nests—"

"I know." His voice was rough. "I know. Say it anyway. Say everything you remember. Don't let go of a single piece."

So she said it. Kneeling in the dark with the impossible smooth horizon before her, she poured out Cindral like a woman bailing a flooding boat — the eagle nests, the blue glaze on the temple roofs, her grandmother's kitchen smelling of juniper and scorched honey, the coronation road, the names of her dead, all nine generations back to the founding, spoken into the night like a spell, like a plea, like a war cry. The villagers joined her. Sera's voice, Fennick's thin treble, the others adding fragments — a well that sang in drought, a dog named Bracken, a bread baked only at funerals. They built Cindral out of memory between them, a fragile lantern-country held up against the erasing dark.

And somewhere in the middle of it, the tearing in her chest stopped being grief and became something with edges. Something you could hold. Something you could *use*.

She rose. Her knees steadied. Her voice, when it came, did not shake.

"They think forgetting is mercy," she said. "Hale thinks it. Maybe the usurper believes it too, in whatever rotted place he keeps his heart. They erase us and call it kindness, and the world lets them, because oblivion hurts less than mourning." She turned to face them all, and the wolf rose in her eyes, gold and cold and ancient. "But we *remembered*. Sera remembered. Fennick remembered. Every name we've given back is a wall they couldn't burn down. So we keep walking. We carry every name to the Door, and we open it, and we make this whole cursed world say *Cindral* again until the word wears grooves in their tongues."

Old Sera reached up and gripped Xanthe's wrist with a hand like a bird's claw, surprisingly strong. "First witness," the old woman said, using the title from the old rites, the one that meant the one who carries memory for the people. "That's what you are, girl. Walk."

* * *

*Elodie*

They moved through the remainder of the night, and the world kept dying around them.

It came in waves now, the weapon — if it was a weapon; Elodie had no other word for a thing that unmade geography. Each pulse arrived as that terrible edged silence, and each time, another stretch of the range went smooth. The pass they'd meant to take: gone. The cairns marking the high route: gone, and the ground under them reshaped into meadowland that had never known boots. Twice Elodie's hard-won knowledge of the Thornfells simply lied to him — a ridge he'd have sworn by led nowhere, because the ridge was younger than his memory of it, because the world had been rewritten underneath the map in his head.

Only one thing anchored the terrain now. Wherever the villagers walked, the land held. Their remembered names stitched the ground beneath their feet like guy-ropes holding a tent against wind. Elodie watched it happen with the awe of a man watching scripture occur: Sera walking, and the path continuing behind her; Fennick singing a nonsense rhyme his mother had retaught him, and birdsong persisting in the trees overhead instead of stopping mid-phrase. Memory, he understood at last, was not sentiment. It was load-bearing.

Dawn found them at the mouth of the descent — the hidden way, the last page of the map's road, leading down toward the sunken capital. Below them the foothills fell away into cloud, and beyond the cloud, faint and far, the usurper's siege-camps must have been waking. Above them, the northern heights where Greytooth had been were still and smooth and wrong.

Elodie counted heads twice. Eleven villagers. One wolf-blooded heir. Himself. All present. All *real* — he checked, absurdly, touching each shoulder, needing to feel warmth and substance, terrified of finding his hand close on empty air.

When he counted the third time, Xanthe caught his wrist.

"We're all here," she said quietly. "Because of you. You've been carrying half these names yourself since the shrine — I've heard you, in the dark, reciting them like a rosary." Her grip tightened. "You prayed to a silent god for years, Elodie Eriksen. Look what your stubbornness saved."

He wanted to answer and found his throat closed. In the end he just nodded, and pressed her hand once, and turned to lead them down.

At the threshold of the hidden road, the frost on the stones caught the early light — and across it, in letters none of them had written, one word gleamed:

**CINDRAL**

Below it, fainter, as if the writer's strength was failing after twenty years of holding a door:

*Come. Quickly.*

Vael's voice. Vael's handwriting. The silent god, speaking at last through the only medium left to him — a god imprisoned, holding shut a door from inside, spending his last strength on two words of frost.

Xanthe read it and made a sound Elodie had never heard from her, low and animal, grief and hope in the same breath.

"He's alive," she whispered. "Our god is still *in there*. Still fighting."

Elodie looked at the fading letters, then north at the smooth horizon where a mountain used to be, and felt the choice that had been forming in him since the shrine settle into its final shape — heavy, inevitable, and almost calm.

"Then we don't stop," he said. "We walk day and night. Whatever's left of this road, we take it *now* — before they fire that thing again, and before whatever's holding that door runs out of words."

He stepped onto the descending stair, and eleven villagers and a queen-in-exile followed him down into the dark, each of them whispering names into the cold like a litany, like a lamp, like a country refusing to die.
