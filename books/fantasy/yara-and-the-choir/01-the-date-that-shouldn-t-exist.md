## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The bone was small enough to cup in one hand—thigh bone of a child of six years, pulled gentle from the river silt three days past—and Marguerite Okonkwo held it to the window light the way a jeweler holds an uncut stone. Outside her workshop above the chandlery, Thornfell village was closing itself against the evening. Shutters clattered. A dog complained about the cold. Somewhere down the lane, Old Marta was scolding her goats in the particular voice of a woman who had been scolding them for forty years and would scold them forty more.

Marguerite listened to all of it without hearing any of it. Her attention was on the bone.

*Little Wren Halloway*, she thought, fitting the name into her mind like a key into a lock. *Drowned at the millrace. Loved frogs. Bit her sister once over a honeycake and cried about it after.*

She had gathered these things from the mother that morning, in the hushed parlor where grieving women always seemed smaller than they'd been before, folded down into chairs as though grief were a weight pressing them flatter. The mother had wept and talked and wrung her apron, and Marguerite had done what she always did: nodded, asked soft questions, carved nothing yet. You couldn't carve a memorial bone from secondhand grief. You had to wait until night, until the workshop was empty, until she could hold the bone and listen to what it carried.

Because bones remembered. Not thoughts—nothing so tidy as thoughts—but impressions. Warmth and terror and the green smell of riverbank mud. The last thing Wren Halloway had felt with her whole small body was water closing over her like a door.

And voices. Bones held voices the way amber held insects.

Marguerite took up her finest burin, the one with the whale-ivory handle worn smooth by nine years of her grip, and set its tip against the bone's pale curve. She breathed out slowly. She let herself listen.

At first there was only the ordinary murmur every bone gave off when she touched it—a sound less heard than felt, like wind moving through a house you weren't quite asleep in. Then, as her blade found its first groove, the murmur resolved.

*A frog,* said a voice, high and delighted and terribly small. *Mama, look, I caught a frog and it PEED on me—*

Marguerite's throat tightened. She kept carving. This was the work: not to flinch, not to turn away, but to hold the dead's last bright fragments steady while she set them into the bone in spiral script older than the village, older than the kingdom, older than anyone living knew how to read except her. When the bone was finished and buried with the body, the voice would quiet. The child would rest. It was the only part of her gift she allowed herself to use openly, disguised as craft, disguised as tradition—the eccentric bone-carver's ritual that no one in Thornfell thought twice about because no one thought about Marguerite Okonkwo much at all.

That was the arrangement she'd made with the world, though she'd never said it aloud even to herself. *I will be useful and strange and slightly dull, and in exchange you will not look too closely.*

She was three spirals deep, the frog story curling around the bone's neck in silver-bright script, when the whisper came.

Not Wren's voice. Not any child's voice. This was many voices braided into one, dry as pages turning, and it did not come from the bone in her hands. It came from everywhere—from the walls, from the floorboards, from the drawer beneath her bench where she kept the book.

*Maaar-gue-riiite.*

Her burin stopped mid-stroke.

*Marguerite Okonkwo.* The whisper tasted her name like a coin bitten for gold. *Bone-Singer. Tonight. Tonight tonight tonight—*

"Who's there?" she said, and hated how thin her voice sounded in her own workshop.

No answer. Only the ordinary sounds of Thornfell settling into dark: the dog, the goats, the wind worrying the eaves. But the whisper had left something behind, the way a bell leaves its hum hanging in the air after the strike. And underneath the hum, faint but unmistakable, Wren's little voice was still going—*frog, frog, mama, look*—as if nothing else in the world had happened.

Marguerite sat very still for a long moment. Then she set down the burin, wiped her palms on her leather apron, and unlocked the bottom drawer.

* *

The Vellum Choir had come to her eleven years ago, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked inside a crate of cow femurs she'd bought at the estate sale of a dead physician in Lowmarsh. She'd told herself it was scholarly curiosity. She'd told herself lots of things about that book over the years—that she kept it locked away because selling it would be dishonest since she didn't know its provenance; that reading it might be dangerous so best not to; that the passages written in a hand identical to her own were coincidence, or forgery, or some joke of fate she simply hadn't unraveled yet.

It was a slim volume bound in vellum gone the color of old teeth. Its pages held prophecy—not the carnival kind, but the kind that made your skin crawl, verses arranged like musical notation, each line attributed to a name. Ninety-nine names. A choir of the dead, singing forward through time. The last entry was dated ninety-eight years ago, and the name signed beneath it was *Sister Alba of the Hollow Watch*, and below Sister Alba's signature the page went blank and stayed blank, year after year, decade after decade, no matter how often Marguerite checked.

Until now.

She set the book on her bench beside the half-carved memorial bone and did not touch it. The lamp guttered. Her heart was doing something embarrassing and arrhythmic against her ribs.

*You are being ridiculous,* she told herself. *Books don't write themselves. You dreamed this. You've been eating cheese too late at night and dreaming nonsense and now you'll open the book and find exactly what it has always contained, which is nothing, which is blankness, which is safety.*

She opened the book.

The blank page after Sister Alba's entry was blank no longer.

New ink lay across it in that impossible familiar hand—her hand, the way her letters leaned eastward, the way her *g*'s looped too low—as though someone had dipped a pen in her wrist and written while she slept. Two lines only:

*When the thirteenth moon rises over Thornfell, the Bone-Singer hears her own name in the mouths of the dead.*

Beneath it, in the same ink, smaller:

*Tonight.*

Marguerite read it four times. Then she looked up at the almanac nailed to her wall, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and confirmed what she already knew in her marrow: the thirteenth moon—the hidden moon, the moon the cults whispered about, the moon that rose only once a century and wasn't supposed to rise at all—waxed toward full this very week. Full in five nights.

Tonight was the first night it showed its face above the fells.

"No," she said aloud, to the book, to the whisper, to whoever had written her future in her own handwriting. "No, I don't know who you think I am, but I carve bones for drowned children and cheat widows' memories out of their grief-money, and that is *all* I am."

The book said nothing. Books are good at that.

But the whisper hadn't been only in the workshop. She understood that now, with a cold spreading slow through her chest like frost across a windowpane. The whisper had been in Wren's bone. The dead themselves—little Wren with her frog, and whatever vast congregation pressed close behind her—had spoken Marguerite's name and tonight's date, had sung them, had *known* them. Which meant the invisibility she had built her life upon, the careful dull ordinariness of Marguerite Okonkwo the bone-carver, had never been real. You could not hide from the dead. They had known where she was all along.

They had been waiting for her all along.

* *

She worked until the candle burned low, because work was the only prayer she trusted. She finished the frog spiral, and the millrace spiral where terror turned to cold and then to nothing, and the final spiral where she wrote *rest now, little one, rest* in the old script, and as she carved the last stroke she felt the bone go quiet in her hands—the specific silence of a voice laid down at last—and she wept a little, quietly, the way she always did, and pretended it was for Wren alone.

Then she banked the fire and stood in the middle of her workshop and made herself think.

The whisper had said *Bone-Singer*. The book said *Bone-Singer*. She had never called herself that; she had never heard the phrase anywhere in thirty-one years of life. Whoever—or whatever—had written the prophecy had named her with a word that belonged to her the way a scar belongs to skin.

And there was more. Now that she let herself look, really look, at the new page, she saw fainter marks beneath the two lines, ghost-ink, half-formed, like words trying to surface through deep water. She tilted the page toward the lamp. Letters swam there—*covenant*, maybe. *Hollow*. A word that might have been *massacre*. And one line almost legible, almost risen: *the choir was murdered and the bargain broke and only the Singer can—*

The rest sank back under the ink like a body slipping beneath a lake's skin.

Marguerite closed the book with fingers that were steadier than she deserved.

Outside, the sky above Thornfell had cleared, and there, low over the black teeth of the fells, hung a sliver of light no almanac charted, no farmer named, no priest blessed. The thirteenth moon, rising for the first time in a hundred years, thin and white and patient as a fingernail clipping.

As she watched, hoofbeats sounded on the lane below—more than one horse, moving fast at an hour when honest travelers slept. Through the gap in her shutter she caught lantern-light swinging, and figures in long dark robes, faces shadowed, reining up before the chandlery. One of them raised a knocker.

A voice drifted up through the floorboards, muffled but clear enough. The chandler's wife, bleary, answering her door at midnight.

*"The bone-carver?" the woman said. "What would robed strangers want with the bone-carver?"*

Marguerite blew out her lamp. In the dark, the Vellum Choir sat on her bench like a sleeping animal, and somewhere beneath the village, beneath the river, beneath the world, a hundred dead voices began—softly, softly—to sing her name.
