## 2. Smuggler's Road

Marguerite left Thornfell the way she had entered it eleven years ago: before dawn, with everything she owned wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to her back.

She had rehearsed this departure a hundred times in her head—some part of her always knew that invisibility was a loan, not a gift, and that one day the collector would come. She had simply imagined more time. She had imagined she'd have the decency to say goodbye to Widow Anselm's goats, at least.

The robed strangers had arrived at dusk, three of them, moving through the village like water finding cracks. She'd watched them from her workshop window as they stopped at the tavern, and even across the square, through glass and lamplight and the ordinary noise of an ordinary evening, she had heard them.

Not their voices. Their *cargo*.

Every one of them carried voices that did not belong to them—a dozen whispers each, pressed flat and thin, the way a flower is pressed until it forgets how to open. The dead, but dead wrong. Bottled. Harvested. And beneath the whispers, threaded through them like wire through wax, a single repeated phrase, patient as erosion:

*the bone-carver. the bone-carver. find the bone-carver.*

She had blown out her lamp, gathered the Vellum Choir from its hiding place beneath her floorboards, and been out the back door before the tavern keeper finished pouring their third cup.

Now the mountain road unspooled ahead of her, grey in the pre-dawn, and Marguerite Okonkwo walked the way she carved: steadily, precisely, without looking back. Grief could wait. Terror could wait. Everything could wait except distance.

By full light she was above the village, on the shepherd track that clung to the western shoulder of Mount Ceren like a scar. By midday she had left even that behind, turning onto the passes—the hidden ones, stitched between cliff and scree, known only to her and the bones of the people who had once used them.

Her mother had shown her the first when Marguerite was nine: a crack in the rockface no wider than a coffin, masked by hanging ivy, opening into a corridor of stone that smelled of cold and old rain. "Smugglers cut these," her mother said, "back when the crown taxed salt. Then the smugglers died or went honest, and the mountain kept their roads for itself." Her mother had smiled then, the rare smile, the one she spent like coin. "You remember them, little bird. You're the only one who listens to anything."

Marguerite listened now. The mountain murmured around her in its sleep—water ticking through fissures, stone settling by fractions, and here and there the small dry voices of things buried long ago, saying nothing in particular, just glad, in their dim way, of company. She had always found it comforting. Today every whisper made her flinch.

Because the Vellum Choir rode against her spine, wrapped in oilcloth, and she could feel it the way you feel a bruise: constantly, and worse when you thought about it. *Marguerite Okonkwo,* the ink said. Tonight's date, in a book completed a hundred years ago. *The Bone-Singer.* And below all of it, in a hand different from the rest, cramped and urgent as if written by someone running:

*she will take the smugglers' road.*

Which meant the robed strangers would know too. Which meant the hidden passes were only hidden from people who hadn't read the same book.

She climbed faster.

* * *

The pass narrowed toward evening into a defile the locals called the Throat—two walls of black granite leaning so close overhead that the sky became a river of fading blue. Marguerite moved through it half by memory, half by touch, her boots finding ledges her eyes had forgotten but her feet had not.

That was why she heard the humming.

Someone was singing in the dark ahead of her—not a song she knew, or perhaps a song with no shape yet, notes wandering like smoke. And threaded under it, faint as breath, she caught something that stopped her heart mid-beat: the hummer's voice carried an echo. Not the harvested flatness of the robed men's cargo. Something else. Something *layered*, as though the man singing were two men, or one man sung through by the mountain itself.

She pressed into an alcove, hand going to the boning knife at her belt. It was a poor weapon for a person and a fine one for bone, but it would have to do.

He rounded the bend walking backward.

That was her first absurd impression of him: a lean man in a patched courier's coat, dark-skinned, quick-eyed, strolling *backward* up a lethal mountain path while talking over his shoulder to empty air. "No, no, I heard you the first time," he was saying. "Left at the split, mind the ice, very good, thank you, you're a terrible conversationalist and I mean that affectionately."

Then he turned forward, saw her, and stopped so abruptly he nearly went down on the scree.

For a moment they regarded each other across ten yards of frozen twilight. He looked exactly like what he was about to prove himself to be: a smuggler, road-worn and amused, with a face built for grinning and a grin currently held in reserve. A coil of rope hung at his hip beside a knife that had seen honest use. His eyes, though—his eyes were doing none of the smiling. They went from her face to the oilcloth bundle on her back and stayed there, and something in them flickered like recognition, or grief, or both.

"You're real," he said. It did not sound like a compliment. It sounded like relief wearing fear's coat.

"You're lost," Marguerite said.

"I'm never lost." He spread his hands. "Lost requires a destination you chose. I have a destination that chose me." He tipped his head back toward the walls of the Throat. "These told me where to go. Three days ago, near Hollowmere, the mountain started talking. Not to everyone—to me. Told me to walk north until I met a woman carrying a book of dead men's handwriting, and to not let her die."

The knife came out of her belt before she had decided to draw it. "Turn around. Walk back the way you came."

"I genuinely don't know which way that is. The mountain only gave me directions one way." He hadn't moved. That was the thing she noticed second: he wasn't afraid of the knife, and it wasn't bravado holding him still. It was the stillness of someone who has already been afraid of worse. "Name's Tam. And before you carve me, consider: if I wanted to hurt you, would I announce myself? Would I stand here chatting while you get comfortable?"

"Robed strangers are asking after me in Thornfell. You'll forgive me if my hospitality is thin."

Something shifted in his face. The grin finally arrived, but it arrived wrong—tight, humorless, a door slammed rather than opened. "Grey robes? Hoods sewn with a crescent that looks like a closed eye?" He spat, deliberately, onto the stone. "Then we're running from the same moon-cult, bone-carver, and mine has been hunting me longer than yours has been hunting you."

"They're not mine."

"No?" He took one step closer. She raised the knife; he ignored it with the ease of long practice. "They followed you out of a village you've lived in quiet for years. They're climbing your mountain tonight. And you're standing in a pass only smugglers and ghosts know about—which means either you've got secrets worth killing for, or the mountain's been talking to you too." His voice dropped, and underneath it she heard it again, that doubled quality, echo folded into sound. "It does talk, doesn't it. To you."

Marguerite said nothing. Silence was the oldest tool in her kit; she had built a life inside it.

But Tam only nodded slowly, as if her silence were an answer read aloud. "Right. Fine. Keep it. Everyone should own one thing the world can't take." He shrugged his pack higher on his shoulders. "I'm camping at the fork ahead. There's a hollow behind the fallen pillar, out of the wind, big enough for two fires if nobody likes anybody. You're welcome to freeze alone somewhere more scenic, or—" he turned and began walking backward again, infuriatingly, "—you can camp where the mountain can watch both of us, since apparently it's invested."

"I don't travel with strangers."

"Wonderful," he called back, already retreating into the blue dusk. "Neither do I. We'll be miserable together."

* * *

She did not go to the fork. Of course she didn't. She climbed another hour past it, found a ledge with a single approach, wedged herself into the stone with her back to granite and the knife loose in her hand, and slept in shallow slices, waking at every shift of wind.

Around midnight, she admitted three things to herself, in order of increasing unpleasantness.

First: the robed strangers would reach Thornfell by morning, learn she had fled, and follow. Alone, on ground they might also know from the Vellum Choir, she was a hare with excellent hearing and nowhere to run that wasn't already written down.

Second: the man at the fork had not lied. She had listened to his heartbeat while he spoke—it was a habit, listening, she couldn't stop—and hearts lie badly. It had beaten steady and quick and true, and when he'd spoken of the cult, it had stumbled like a man remembering a wound.

Third, worst of all: when he talked to the mountain, the mountain had answered. She had heard it. Faint, wordless, but unmistakably a reply—the deep slow voice of Ceren itself, bending around him the way it bent around nothing else she had ever met. Whatever Tam was, the mountain had sent him. And the last time the mountain had wanted something from her, it had written it in a century-old book with her name in the margin.

Dawn found her striking camp.

She descended to the fork with her jaw set, telling herself this was tactics, not trust. Tactics: a smuggler knew the roads, the borderlands, the ways in and out of places with names she'd need soon. Tactics: two backs watched better than one. Tactics had nothing to do with the fact that she had spent one night alone on the mountain and discovered that silence, her lifelong companion, had begun to feel less like safety and more like the inside of a grave.

Tam was awake, of course, sitting cross-legged on the fallen pillar with a tin kettle steaming over a small flame. He didn't gloat. She marked that in his favor, grudgingly, the way a miser marks a coin.

"Tea," he offered, pouring a second cup without being asked. "Nettle and pine. It tastes like an apology from a tree."

She took the cup because refusing warmth twice was a habit she couldn't afford. It tasted awful. She drank it anyway.

"Rules," she said.

His eyebrows rose, delighted. "Go on."

"You walk behind me where the path forks. You don't touch the bundle on my back. If I say run, you run, and if I say hide, you stop breathing until I say otherwise." She fixed him with the look she used on grieving families who wanted to argue about epitaphs. "And you tell me what the mountain says. All of it. Every word."

For a moment the charm fell away entirely, and what looked out at her was tired and old and strangely young at once, like something that had been walking a long time without ever learning how. "Every word," he agreed quietly. "Even the parts that scare you."

"*Especially* those."

He raised his tin cup. "To misery together, then."

She touched her cup to his because it was easier than admitting she'd already drunk from his kettle, and because—for the first time since she'd read her name in the Vellum Choir—the dread in her chest had loosened by a single degree. Not gone. Just shared, which was a kind of arithmetic she hadn't done in years.

Below them, far down the valley, a thread of smoke rose from Thornfell. Too much smoke. Too black.

Tam saw it too. He stood slowly, and the mountain around them shivered—or seemed to—and in the space where sound should have been, Marguerite heard it speak, clear as a bell rung underwater:

*Hurry.*

She was moving before the echo faded.
