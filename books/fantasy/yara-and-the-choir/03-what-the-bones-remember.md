## 3. What the Bones Remember

The waystation squatted at the shoulder of the pass like something that had died standing up and never been told. Its roof had half-collapsed into a fan of rotten shingles, its door hung from one hinge, and the wind came through the gaps in the walls with a sound like a man whistling through his teeth. Tam Vess loved it immediately, in the way he loved all ruined places: they asked nothing of him.

"Cozy," he said, ducking through the doorway and spreading his arms as though presenting a palace. "Mind the draft, mind the rats, and if anything bites you, bite it back. House rules."

Marguerite didn't answer. She hadn't answered much since noon.

He'd noticed it on the road—how could he not, when noticing people was the one craft he'd never had to fake? She walked differently now than she had that first morning, when she'd come down out of the high passes like a blade wrapped in wool: wary, sharp, entirely certain of her own edges. Now there was a looseness to her, a woman carrying something heavier than her pack and pretending it was nothing. Twice she'd stopped dead on the trail for no reason he could see. Three times she'd turned her head as if someone had spoken—and no one had. No one he could hear.

He built a fire anyway. Small, shielded by stones, smoke thin enough to lose itself against the clouds. Old habits. He couldn't have said whose old habits; that was rather the trouble.

That was the thing about Tam Vess, and he knew it the way a gambler knows a marked deck: there was a hole where his past should be. He'd woken three years ago—four?—in the hold of a grain barge off Saltmere, with a knife, forty coppers sewn into his coat lining, and a name stitched inside the collar that fit him like a coat tailored by someone who'd only ever heard of men. *Tam Vess.* It sounded right. It sounded like a lie told well enough to be true. Everything before the barge was a smear of grey, like ink gone wet. He'd learned not to look at the smear. You didn't win at cards by staring at the bottom of the deck.

But lately the smear had started moving.

Marguerite sat across the fire, cross-legged, a bone in her lap. One of her own carvings—a memorial piece, he'd gathered, though he'd been careful not to ask too much about the trade. Carvers for the dead made most folk nervous, and Marguerite Okonkwo made most folk nervous twice over. But tonight she wasn't carving. Tonight she was holding the bone the way you'd hold a sleeping bird, and her lips were moving, and—

"You know," Tam said lightly, "most people talk to themselves facing away from the fire. More dramatic shadows that way."

She looked up. For a heartbeat her eyes went straight through him—not past him, *through* him, the way a key goes through a lock—and something cold walked up his spine wearing very small boots.

"He's here," she said.

Tam glanced around the ruined room. Wind, rot, rats. "Who's here?"

"The maiden." Her voice had gone quiet and level, the voice of someone reporting weather. "From the choir. The one I heard at Thornfell—the one who whispered my name. She followed us." A pause. "She says—" Marguerite's brow furrowed, listening to something in a register the world kept below hearing. "She says you shouldn't sit with your back to the north wall."

"Why?"

"Because a hundred years ago, someone died against it."

The fire popped. Tam found, to his mild surprise, that he did not move. Charm, he'd long ago decided, was simply fear wearing good clothes, and he owned excellent clothes.

"Well," he said. "I'm sure she's very kind, but I'll take my chances with the wall. Dead women don't own the real estate yet."

Something crossed Marguerite's face that might have been the ghost of a smile. Then it was gone, and she leaned forward, firelight carving hollows under her cheekbones, and said, "Ask me what else she says."

And there it was—the trap laid gently in the middle of the floor, baited with his own curiosity. He could walk around it. He was very good at walking around things; it was practically his profession. But he thought of the barge, the grey smear, the name in his collar, and heard himself say:

"What else does she say?"

Marguerite held the bone out flat between them, an offering or an accusation. "She remembers the massacre. The night the choir was murdered at Thornfell's shrine. She remembers eleven of them dying." Her eyes came up. "She remembers twelve running."

The wind filled the pause with its whistling. Tam laughed—one short bark, automatic, the sound he used to buy time in negotiations gone sideways. "Running. That's flattering. I wasn't alive a hundred years ago, love. I've got the face for it, granted, but the arithmetic fails."

"I haven't told you the rest."

"No," he agreed. "You haven't."

"She doesn't remember his face." Marguerite turned the bone slowly in her fingers. "A hundred years is a long time, even for the dead. Faces go first, then names, then everything but the shape of the wound. But she remembers this: he came back to warn them, and he was too late, and he ran because the moon-priests marked him, and the mark ate his memory before it finished eating him. She remembers that the last anyone saw of him, he was walking east toward the coast with nothing in his head but fog." Her voice dropped. "And she remembers that he was laughing when he left. Laughing so he wouldn't scream. Because laughter was the only thing the mark couldn't take."

The fire had gotten small. Tam didn't remember feeding it less wood. His hands were steady—he made them be steady—but somewhere beneath his ribs, in the place where a history should have been anchored, something shifted like ballast coming loose in a storm.

"Three years ago," he heard himself say. Or four. "I woke on a grain barge off Saltmere. East coast."

"Yes."

"With nothing in my head but fog."

"Yes."

"And a knife and forty coppers and a name that fits like a borrowed coat."

"Yes," Marguerite said again, softly, and there was no triumph in it at all. Only a terrible gentleness, which was somehow worse. "I'm sorry, Tam."

"Don't be sorry." He stood, because sitting felt suddenly like drowning upright. He paced to the north wall—the north wall, of course, always the north wall—and pressed his palm against the old timber, and the cold of it climbed his arm like water finding its level. "Be useful instead. Tell me what happens to a man the moon-priests mark. Tell me what I am."

"I don't know."

"You carved bones for the dead your whole life. You hear them. You're the closest thing this century has produced to an expert, so make an educated guess."

She was silent a moment. When she spoke, each word came out placed carefully, like stones across a river. "I think the mark was meant to kill you. I think it failed—or was interrupted—or was *paid for*, somehow, and what was left over crawled out of the fog with a knife and a sense of humor and started walking west, back toward the mountains. Back toward the thing you died failing to do." She looked at the bone in her lap. "She thinks you came back for us. For the choir. She thinks some part of you has been walking home for a hundred years."

Tam stared at her. And beneath the charm, beneath the quip already forming behind his teeth—something about ghosts having poor taste in real estate—there was a silence. Not the comfortable silence of a man with nothing to say, but the vast, howling silence of a room in a house he'd never entered that nonetheless contained his furniture.

Because it explained things. God, it explained *everything*. The dreams he'd never mentioned—fire seen from a distance, bells rung backwards, a woman's voice singing a song he always woke up humming and never remembered learning. His unerring instinct for mountain passes he'd never walked, for shrines he'd never visited, for the exact weight of a choir-maiden's grief. The way the moon-cult's sigil, glimpsed once on a smuggled crate, had filled him with a hatred so old and total it felt geological.

He'd thought he was a man without a past.

He was a man whose past had been *eaten*, and the eater was still hungry, and the meal had been him.

"TAM."

Marguerite's voice cracked like a whip, and he realized two things at once: he had drawn his knife without deciding to, and the ghost was standing in the corner of the room.

Not a shimmer, not a rumor. Standing. A young woman in the rotted remnants of a white choir-robe, translucent as breath on glass, her throat bearing a bruise-dark line that a hundred years had not faded. She was looking at him with an expression he had no name for—grief and recognition tangled together, hope and horror wearing the same face.

Her mouth moved.

"I can't hear her," Marguerite said quickly. "Not clearly, not without—I need a few minutes, I need to carve, give me—" She was already pulling her tools from her pack, hands shaking, and Tam understood dimly that she was terrified, that this was more than she'd ever channeled before, that she was doing it anyway. For him. The woman who trusted no one, doing the one thing that cost her most, for the man she'd known three days.

The ghost drifted closer. Tam did not move. Every instinct honed on a hundred roads screamed at him to run, and every one of those instincts, he now understood, belonged to a coward who had run once already and hated himself for a century.

The ghost raised her hand. Trembling. And touched his cheek.

Cold. Cold like the far side of dawn. And into that cold poured—fragments. Not memories; he had no shelf to put them on. Just *shapes*: candlelight on stone. Eleven voices rising in harmony. A bell tolling wrong. His own hand, younger, reaching for a door he would not reach in time. Laughter—his laughter—raging against the dark like a struck match.

Then it was gone, and the ghost stepped back, and her expression broke into something that was unmistakably, devastatingly, *relief*. As if she'd spent a hundred years wondering whether the twelfth had made it out alive, and now, at last, she knew: yes. Yes, he had. Broken, emptied, remade as a stranger with a stolen name—but alive, still walking, still *laughing*.

"Oh," Tam said. Very quietly. All the charm gone out of him like air from a punctured lung, and what remained underneath was just a man, standing in a ruin, holding a knife he didn't need. "Oh, hell."

Marguerite looked up from her carving, eyes wide. "What did she—what happened? What did you see?"

"Nothing." He slid the knife home. His voice came out rough, scraped raw, and honest for perhaps the first time since Saltmere. "I saw nothing. That's rather the whole problem."

* *

He didn't sleep. He sat by the fire turning the revelation over like a coin of unknown currency, trying to find the edge of it, while Marguerite worked beside him—actually worked, carving quick and fierce into a fresh length of bone, her lips moving as the maiden poured a century of memory through her hands.

Near midnight—or what he guessed was midnight; the sky stayed stubbornly clouded—she set the bone down and rubbed her eyes.

"She wants to tell you herself. Properly. Through the carving." Marguerite hesitated. "It will cost me. Hearing that clearly, that long—it's like standing in a river. You come out changed."

"Then don't." The words were out before he could weigh them. "Marguerite. Whatever you're planning to do on this mountain—whatever the book says you are—you can't spend yourself on every ghost with a grievance. Save it for the heartstone."

"This isn't a grievance." She picked the bone back up. "It's a message. There's a difference." Her jaw set in that way he was beginning to catalog the way other men studied tide tables. "Besides. If you're really the choir's twelfth—if you've been walking home for a hundred years—then someone should tell you what you're walking home *to*."

She closed her eyes. Set the bone against her brow. Began to sing—low, wordless, a sound that wasn't quite sound, a sound like the memory of sound.

The temperature fell. The fire burned blue at its edges. And Tam Vess, smuggler, liar, ghost of a ghost, watched the dead speak through the living and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever he had been running from across three years and four provinces, it was behind him no longer.

The maiden's voice rose through Marguerite's throat, and the first words it shaped were these:

"*Brother, they are coming. The thirteenth moon rises early, and the priests of the pale tithe have smelled the Bone-Singer on the wind. You must reach the heartstone before it crests the ridge—or she will be taken, and the Hollow will open, and this time there will be no choir left to close it.*"

The blue light guttered. Marguerite sagged, gasping, catching herself on one hand.

Tam was already on his feet, kicking earth over the fire, throwing her pack at her, every lazy ounce of charm burned away and replaced by something older and harder that fit his shoulders like it had been waiting a century for the chance.

"How long?" he demanded. "How long until the moon crests the ridge?"

Marguerite blinked up at him, ash-smeared and shaking. "Two days. Maybe less."

"Then we walk tonight." He pulled the ruined door open, and the wind rushed in, smelling of snow and something farther off—something pale and patient and hungry. Somewhere down the valley, faint as a struck bell, a note of singing rose that had no living throat behind it.

Tam smiled. It was not a pleasant smile, but it was, at last, entirely his own.

"Come on, Bone-Singer," he said. "Let's go introduce the priests to the man they failed to kill."
