## 4. The Moon-Cult's Tithe

They took her at the ford.

She should have known better—Tam had warned her that the low crossings were watched, that the cult's reach ran along every river like rot through timber. But the high passes had turned to ice overnight, and the waystation was two days behind them, and the drowned child's bone still hung at her throat, and she had thought, foolishly, that a single woman leading a mule through grey morning mist would be beneath their notice.

The nets came out of the willows on both banks at once. Weighted cords, silvered with something that hummed against her teeth—a sound like a struck tuning fork, like a hymn sung wrong. The moment it touched her skin, the whispers of the dead went silent. All of them. The whole murmuring world she had carried since childhood simply *stopped*, and the silence was so vast and so sudden that she cried out as if struck.

Then hands. Many hands. She bit one, kicked another, and got her elbow into a soft throat before they bore her down into the shallows. Water in her mouth, her ears, the cold pressing her flat—and above her, distorted through the current, faces in pale robes looking down with an expression she would remember long after: not cruelty. *Interest.* The look of merchants appraising goods.

When they hauled her up she was bound wrist and ankle and dumped across the back of a horse like a sack of meal. Tam was nowhere. Either he'd escaped or they hadn't found him, and she clung to that uncertainty the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood. If he was free, he might follow. If he was free, he might do something reckless and brave and stupid, because that was what Tam did, charm first and sense after.

*Don't,* she told him silently, as the horses picked their way upstream. *Don't come.*

The dead, of course, could not tell her whether he heard.

* * *

The place they brought her had been a monastery once. She could read it in the bones of the buildings—the cloister walk with its broken arches, the chapel roof fallen inward, the cells with their iron rings still bolted to the walls. Now the courtyards were hung with lanterns of smoked glass that burned a color she had no name for, a grey-gold light that made everyone's face look like something dredged from a river.

And everywhere, everywhere, were the jars.

Rows of them. Shelf upon shelf in what had been the refectory, in the scriptorium, along the cloister walls under little wooden eaves to keep off the rain. Glass vessels of every size, each one filled with a slow-swirling vapor that caught the lantern light and held it. And each jar *sang*. Not aloud—not with anything her ears could find—but in that deeper register she had carried alone all her life. A thousand voices, thin as threads, humming inside their glass prisons.

Marguerite stopped walking so abruptly that the guard behind her walked into her back.

"You can hear them." The voice came from ahead, dry and pleased. "Of course you can. That's rather the point."

The woman who emerged from between the shelves was old in a way that didn't add up. Her robes were fine, her bearing straight, her skin smooth as a girl's—but her eyes sat in her face like stones in fruit too ripe, ancient and sunken and *tired* in a manner no amount of harvested youth could touch. She looked at Marguerite the way Marguerite had once seen a starving man look at bread.

"Mother Vey," one of the guards said, and knelt. The others followed. Marguerite stayed standing, mostly because her ankles were bound and kneeling would have meant falling.

"The bone-carver," Mother Vey said. She circled slowly, and Marguerite felt the appraisal like a hand moving over her. "Do you know how long we have searched for your kind? A hundred years. A full century since the last choir burned at Thornfell, and every medium since has been a guttering candle—a whisper here, a half-hearing there. But you." She stopped in front of Marguerite, close enough that Marguerite could smell something underneath her perfume, something sweet and rotten, like flowers left too long in a sealed room. "You hear them *whole*. You speak with them. You carve their names and they answer. Do you have any idea what you are worth?"

"I know what these are," Marguerite said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The jars. They're people."

"They're *voices*," Mother Vey corrected gently, as if instructing a slow child. "The rest was dross. Flesh tires, dear. Flesh fails. But a voice—a voice properly taken at the moment of death, before it scatters into the Hollow—can be kept. Can be *used*." She lifted one jar from its shelf with tender reverence. Inside, the vapor coiled and pressed against the glass, and Marguerite heard it whimper. "This one sang lullabies for forty years. Two hundred children slept to that voice. And now—" Vey held it to her own lips, tilted her head back, and drank.

It was not drinking. It was worse than drinking. The vapor poured into her open mouth and the woman's throat worked and for a moment her sunken eyes brightened, her posture freshened, some deep weariness smoothing away—and the whimpering in the jar fell silent forever. Then she set the empty glass down and sighed like a woman finishing a fine wine.

"Every tithe keeps me another season," she said. "You understand now why the covenant had to break. The Hollow hoards the dead's voices behind its veil, lets them fade into nothing, wastes them. We merely... redistribute." Her smile was kind, which made it monstrous. "But tithes are thin fare. A voice taken at the edge of death is already frayed. What I need—what my brothers and sisters need—is a door held *open*. A medium strong enough to stand at the threshold and unbar it, so that we may harvest as the dying die, fresh and whole, without end."

"And you think that's me."

"I know it is you. The book says so." Mother Vey's eyes glittered. "Oh yes, child. We have our own copy of the Vellum Choir. Did you think you were the only one reading prophecy in the dark? *The Bone-Singer comes when the thirteenth moon rises, and the threshold opens at her song.*" She reached out and touched the memorial bone at Marguerite's throat, and Marguerite flinched hard enough that the guards seized her arms. "We don't want to hurt you. We want to *crown* you. You would be the first of the new choir. Revered. Fed on the finest tithes. Immortal in everything but name."

"For how long?" Marguerite asked.

"Forgive me?"

"How long does the door stay open?"

Mother Vey laughed, a sound like dry leaves. "As long as I live. Which, with you to serve it, will be a very long time indeed."

* * *

They put her in a cell that had been a monk's cell, iron-ringed walls and a straw pallet and a window too narrow for anything but air. The silvered netting had been replaced with plain rope, and the moment it was, the whispers came flooding back—distant here, muffled, but present, a tide returning to a drained shore. She sat on the pallet and listened until she found them: the jarred voices, faint through stone and glass, singing to themselves in the dark to keep from going mad.

One of them was close. Directly below her, if she judged right. A young voice, a boy's, worn down to almost nothing.

*Hello,* she thought toward it, the way she'd spoken to ghosts all her life. *I hear you.*

The silence went taut. Then, thread-thin, disbelieving: *You hear me? Truly?*

*Truly. What's your name?*

A long pause. *I... I had one. It's been so long since anyone said it.* Another pause, and then, small and frightened: *Are you going to drink us?*

*No.* She wrapped both hands around the bars of the window and gripped until her knuckles ached, as if she could hold the whole rotten edifice shut by force. *No, listen to me—all of you down there, all of you in the glass. My name is Marguerite Okonkwo. I am a bone-singer, and I hear every one of you, and I swear on the bones I carve that this ends. One way or another, this ends.*

The whispering below her changed. She couldn't have said how—it wasn't louder, exactly, but it *gathered*, a hundred frayed voices turning toward her like flowers toward a window. And beneath the fear in them she felt something else take root. Hope, maybe. Or just the simple animal relief of being witnessed.

That was when she understood the shape of the thing they wanted from her, truly understood it, and horror curdled into something harder in her chest. It wasn't her death they needed. Death she could have spat at. They needed her *consent*—a medium's willing song at the threshold, because the covenant, even broken, even bleeding, still demanded that the door be opened freely. They would crown her and flatter her and starve her gently until she sang. And if she refused—

If she refused, they would simply wait. They had waited a hundred years. They had jars enough to outlast her lifetime ten times over, and somewhere out there Tam was either running or captured, and Isolde's maps were half a mountain range away, and Sefu—

*Sefu.* The mute acolyte who had cut out his own voice rather than let them take it. He had known. He had stood in this place and seen the shelves and chosen mutilation over complicity. She pressed her forehead against the cold stone wall and breathed, and let the horror finish sharpening itself into resolve, the way a blade finishes itself on a whetstone.

They would not get her song. Not freely given, not coerced, not traded for comfort or youth or crowns. She would go silent before she opened that door—she would bite through her own tongue, she would become another Sefu, she would—

The lock turned.

She was on her feet before the door finished opening, weight forward, ready to spend herself on whatever came through. But it was only a novice, young and hollow-eyed, carrying a tray of bread and water. The girl set it down without meeting her eyes and turned to go, and paused, one hand braced on the doorframe.

"There are others asking for you," the girl said, barely above a breath. "Since yesterday. At the treeline. A cartographer, they're saying, with stolen maps, and—" her gaze flicked up, terrified and fierce all at once, "—and a smuggler who talks too much. They're saying the mountains themselves sent them."

Marguerite's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs.

"They're coming for you," the novice whispered, and fled, pulling the door shut behind her, leaving Marguerite alone in the dark with the bread, the whispered chorus of the imprisoned dead, and—for the first time since the nets closed over her at the ford—a reason to count the hours until nightfall.
