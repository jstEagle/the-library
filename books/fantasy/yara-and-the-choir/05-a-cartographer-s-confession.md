## 5. A Cartographer's Confession

Twenty years of exile had taught Isolde Vey three things: how to read weather in the color of a crow's wing, how to sleep with one hand on her knife, and how to recognize the sound of people about to die badly.

The last one came down the mountain at dusk—a stumble and a gasp, boots slipping on scree, followed by the particular silence of two people who had heard something behind them and were trying not to breathe. She was mending a net over her smoke-fire when she heard it, and she did not move for a full minute. She counted her heartbeats instead, the way she'd once counted contour lines, until the sounds resolved: two travelers, not soldiers. One breathing too fast. One not breathing much at all.

Then, fainter, threaded beneath the wind off the glacier like wire under cloth—voices. Many voices. Singing something that was not a song.

Isolde put down the net.

She knew that sound. She had spent twenty years pretending she didn't, the way an amputee pretends the hand still itches. The Hollow spoke in harmonies that had no throats behind them, and every mapmaker who'd ever charted its borders had gone mad or missing or both, because you cannot draw a line around a thing that sings to you from inside your own skull. She had tried anyway. She'd been very good at it, right up until the Crown burned her maps and struck her name from the rolls of the Royal Cartographic Guild and sent her into these mountains to rot.

She had been rotting productively ever since.

* * *

She found them at the mouth of the ravine where the old pilgrim trail collapsed into rockfall, which meant they'd come over the high passes—the hidden ones, the ones only marked on maps that officially did not exist. A woman with bone-carver's hands and a face like someone holding a door shut against a flood. A man beside her, charming-looking even while bleeding, with a wound along his ribs that was far too neat to be an accident. Blade work. Cult work.

And behind them, down the ravine, six figures in moon-pale robes moving with the unhurried confidence of wolves who know the prey is tired.

Isolde had spent twenty years avoiding other people's emergencies. She told herself this one wasn't hers either as she pulled the pin on the rope rig she'd spent four years building across the ravine head and let forty tons of loosened talus come down between the cultists and the trail.

The mountain roared. Dust swallowed the world. When it cleared, six white-robed figures stood regarding the fresh wall of rubble with what looked less like frustration and more like patience, which frightened her more than pursuit would have. Then, as one, they turned and walked back the way they'd come.

They'd learn another route by morning. They always did.

"Inside," Isolde said. "Quickly, before I remember I hate company."

* * *

Her shelter was a surveyor's station once, before the Crown decided the mountains' secrets were better unkept. Now it was shelves of instruments, bundles of maps drawn on anything that would hold ink—bark, vellum, hide, the backs of old proclamations declaring her a traitor—and a stove that smoked when the wind came wrong. The man with the cut ribs sat down heavily on her cot without being invited, which she noted as either rudeness or blood loss. The woman stood in the doorway, taking inventory the way Isolde herself took inventory, and stopped dead at the wall of maps.

"You've mapped the passes," the woman said.

"I've mapped everything." Isolde stoked the stove. "It's my one redeeming feature."

"No. You don't understand." The woman crossed to the wall and touched a sheet showing the Thornfell massif with annotations in Isolde's own cramped hand—elevations, water sources, and along the eastern face, in red, a winding line labeled in a language Isolde hadn't spoken aloud in twenty years. "This is the route to the heartstone. This exact route. How do you have this?"

"Because I drew it before your grandmother learned to walk, most likely."

"The heartstone isn't real," the man said from the cot. He said it lightly, the way people say things they're desperate to believe. "Fairy stories. Old wives with too much time."

"Everything is real," Isolde said, "until it kills you. Ask me how I know."

The woman turned. In the stove light her eyes were the color of wet slate, and Isolde saw something in them that made her chest ache with recognition: the look of a person who has heard the Hollow sing and has not yet learned to stop listening. Twenty years ago, Isolde had seen that look in a mirror.

"What's your name?" the woman asked.

"Vey. Isolde Vey. Formerly Royal Cartographer, Third Rank. Currently nobody, by royal decree." She nodded at the man's ribs. "That needs stitching. Take off your shirt, smuggler, unless you'd rather be dramatic about it."

"How did you know I'm a—"

"You have rope burns in patterns no honest person gets, your boots are resoled with sailcloth, and you flinched when I said *smuggler*, which means you're a good one." She threaded the needle, held it in the flame. "Hold still."

He held still. Braver than most.

While she worked, the bone-carver studied the maps in silence, moving along the wall like a pilgrim along a frieze, and Isolde watched her out of the corner of her eye and felt the thing she'd been carrying for two decades shift inside her, heavy cargo changing hands.

* * *

She stitched him closed, gave them barley soup that tasted of smoke and nothing else, and then, because there was no way around it anymore, she went to the iron-bound chest beneath her cot and took out the map.

Not the copies. Not the fragments she showed traders to buy goodwill. The original. The one that had ended her life.

She unrolled it across the table between them and weighted the corners with stones, and for a moment none of them breathed.

It was beautiful, even now. Even knowing what it cost. The Hollow rendered in her finest hand—not as a blank space with the cartographer's traditional formula, *here be nothing*, but as a country. Rivers that ran silver and uphill. Cities whose streets rearranged themselves nightly, each layout recorded in overlapping ghost-lines of ink. And at the center, drawn and redrawn so many times the vellum had gone soft as cloth, a single structure like a bell made of thorns, ringed by thirteen small moons.

Around its border ran the covenant, transcribed from the last surviving choir record, in the old tongue:

*A voice freely given keeps the door. A door unkept lets the dead pour through.*

"The Crown didn't exile me for making it up," Isolde said quietly. "They exiled me for making it *accurate*. There are people in high places who prefer the Hollow unmapped, because an unmapped door can't be guarded—or sold." She met the bone-carver's slate eyes. "You're Marguerite Okonkwo. The bone-carver from Thornfell. And three days ago, strangers in robes started asking after you in every village south of the pass, which means whatever's in that book of yours has woken up. Which means you're her. The Bone-Singer."

Marguerite's jaw tightened. "I haven't agreed to be anything."

"No one agrees. That's rather the point of prophecy." Isolde sat down heavily, and twenty years fell onto her shoulders all at once. "I found the choir records thirty years ago, in the sealed archive beneath the Guildhall. Records of the massacre—a hundred years past, the whole choir murdered mid-song, the covenant broken, the Hollow left swinging open on rusted hinges. And in the margin of the last page, one line in a different hand. A prediction. *A Bone-Singer will rise in Thornfell, when the thirteenth moon returns.*"

She laughed, and it came out wrong, rusty.

"I was young. I thought if I mapped the Hollow properly—every border, every breach—I could find where the next Bone-Singer would stand. I thought knowledge was a kind of protection. The Guild thought I was mad. The Crown thought I was dangerous. Both burned my work." Her hand flattened against the vellum, over the thorn-bell at its heart. "I kept drawing anyway. Twenty years, alone in these mountains, drawing the same mountain range over and over, waiting for a date to arrive in a book I'd never see. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I have been walking toward you my entire ruined life, Marguerite Okonkwo. I just didn't know your face until you walked into my firelight."

The silence stretched. Outside, wind worried the rocks, and beneath the wind—faint, patient, many-voiced—the singing continued.

"And you?" Marguerite said at last, turning to the smuggler. "Why does a man with no name and a knife-wound follow a stranger over the high passes?"

Tam smiled his easy smile, but it flickered, and Isolde—professional observer of terrain, human included—saw the crack in it plain as a fault line.

"The mountains told me to find her," he said. "I wish I were joking."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." He looked down at his stitched ribs, and for a moment the charm dropped away entirely, leaving something hollow and young. "There are twenty years of my life I can't account for. Before that, nothing. I woke up on a road outside Thornfell with coin in my pocket and skills I don't remember learning, and every time I try to look backward, it's like—" His hand closed. "Like looking at one of her maps where someone's cut out the middle."

Isolde felt the hair rise on her arms. A man with a hole where his history should be, walking toward the Hollow as though summoned. She had read enough choir marginalia to know what the old mediums wrote about such souls, and she kept it behind her teeth, because some doors are kinder locked.

"So," she said instead, standing, brisk again, because feeling was a country she'd lost her map to long ago. "The moon-cult wants you, the Hollow is opening, and we have perhaps nine days before the thirteenth moon reaches full. I have the only complete maps of the ascent. You have whatever it is you have." She began rolling the great map closed, and her hands, she noticed, were not quite steady. "I am not offering friendship. I am offering logistics. Let's be clear about that."

"Naturally," Tam said.

"I wouldn't dream of anything else," said Marguerite—but she smiled, small and tired and real, and Isolde caught herself smiling back before she could stop it, and hated how easy it was, how the three of them already fit together like survey points defining a shape none of them had agreed to draw.

Three broken instruments, she thought. God help us, maybe we still triangulate.

She banked the stove and reached for her pack, and that was when the knock came at the shutter—three slow raps, spaced like footsteps, like a heartbeat counting down.

Through the gap in the boards, moonlight fell on a figure standing in the snow. Young. Robed in white. Hands cupped around a mouth that opened and produced no sound at all.

On a cord around its neck, catching the light, hung a small carved token shaped like a tongue.
