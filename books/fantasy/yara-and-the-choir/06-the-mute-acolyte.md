## 6. The Mute Acolyte

The shrine announced itself before we saw it.

Isolde noticed it first—a wrongness in the wind, she said, a place where the air moved around something instead of through it. Tam called it luck when the pass narrowed and spat us into a hollow cupped between two shoulders of granite, but I knew better than to call anything luck anymore. The dead had been murmuring since dawn, not words yet, just that low tide-sound they made when something important was near. Like an audience settling into their seats.

The shrine sat at the bottom of the hollow like a thing that had been dropped there by a careless god. It was older than the cult, older maybe than Thornfell itself—rough-hewn walls furred with lichen, a doorway without a door, and above the lintel a carving so weathered it took me a long moment to read it: a row of small figures, hand in hand, singing. Their mouths were open. Their throats were lines of stone running down into the rock itself, as if the song had become part of the mountain.

"The old faith," Isolde said quietly. She had her map case clutched against her chest the way other people held children. "Before the Choir formalized. Before anyone thought the dead needed *managing*."

"Everything needs managing," Tam said. "That's what my mother used to say about me." But he said it lightly, the way he said everything, and I'd learned by now that the lightness was a door he kept closed. Behind it was that hole where his past should have been. He touched the doorframe as we passed, and I saw his fingers linger on one of the carved singers longer than chance would explain.

Inside, the shrine was one round chamber open to the sky, snow sifting down through the broken roof and melting on a floor of polished black stone. Candles had burned here recently—I could smell the tallow, see the wax pooled in the cracks. Someone lived here. Or had, until very recently.

And against the far wall, half-hidden in shadow, someone was watching us.

I felt him before I saw him—the way you feel a held breath in a quiet room. He was young, younger than me, wrapped in a robe the grey-white of bone, hood drawn low. His hands were raised slightly from his sides, palms out. Not surrender. *Caution.* The gesture of someone who has learned that sudden movement gets people hurt.

"We're not going to hurt you," I said, and then heard how many times those words must have been said in this room by people who meant the opposite.

He didn't answer. Of course he didn't. Because when the light shifted and the hood fell back, I saw his throat—and the scar.

It was old, that scar. A clean, deliberate line, made by someone who knew anatomy, made on themselves or by a surgeon's careful hand. Where his voice should have been, there was only smooth, sealed skin. He had no Adam's apple to speak of. They had taken it. Or—

Or he had.

Tam's hand went to his knife. "Cult colors," he said softly. "Marguerite."

"I see them." The grey-white wool, the moon-phase stitching at the hem. This was one of theirs. One of the people who had sent robed strangers through Thornfell asking after the bone-carver. One of the people who wanted to open the Hollow and keep it open, and harvest the dead like wheat.

But he wasn't reaching for a weapon. He wasn't running. He was watching me with an expression I recognized from the inside—hope fighting terror for control of a face, and neither one winning.

Slowly, deliberately, keeping his palms visible, he knelt and drew something from a fold of his robe. Small objects, white as teeth. He laid them out on the black floor between us, arranging them with quick, precise movements, and stepped back.

I looked down.

They were bones. Carved bones—slivers of rib and finger-bone and bird-bone, each one etched with marks. Not letters. Pictures, almost. A knife. A mouth with a line struck through it. A figure kneeling. A moon with thirteen points.

"He can't speak," I said. My own voice came out strange, thin. "They didn't take his voice. Look at the scar—it healed clean. Too clean. This was done carefully. Willingly."

"Why would anyone—" Tam started.

"*Why would anyone*," I repeated, and laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "Because the tithe, Tam. You remember what the ghosts told us. What the cult does. They don't just take voices from the dead. Every acolyte gives their own voice when they take the grey robe. That's the entry price. Your living voice, added to the choir that keeps their master young."

I crouched beside the tokens, not touching them. "And this one refused."

The acolyte watched me kneel. His breath had gone shallow. In the candle-smell and the falling snow, he looked less like an enemy and more like what he was: a boy who had stood in front of his god's representatives and chosen mutilation over complicity. Who had cut his own voice out rather than let it be *used*.

I knew that arithmetic. God, I knew it. Twenty years of making myself small in Thornfell. Twenty years of never humming while I worked, never singing at funerals, never letting the gift show in my face when the dead spoke. I hadn't cut anything out. I'd just buried it so deep and so long that some mornings I woke up not sure which parts of me were real and which were the disguise. Self-erasure doesn't need a knife. It only needs time.

"You stayed," I said to him. "You stayed in their house and you couldn't leave and you couldn't speak, so you learned to carve. How long?"

He held up ten fingers. Then folded four down.

Six years. Six years of silence in the house of the people who murdered the last choir. My chest ached with it.

"Can you read these?" Isolde asked, easing forward. The cartographer in her had already catalogued the token system—she was crouching now too, eyes bright behind her exhaustion. "This is a language. A real one. You built a language out of bone."

His cheeks colored. He nodded once, quickly, as if the nod embarrassed him.

Then his gaze came back to me, and everything else fell away.

He pointed—one finger, trembling slightly—at my chest. At the space over my heart. Then he picked up one of his tokens, the one carved with an open mouth, and pressed it flat against his own breastbone. And his eyes were saying what his ruined throat could not:

*I know what you are.*

The dead in the walls went very quiet. Listening.

"How," I whispered.

He began laying out tokens again, fast now, urgent. A mountain. A fire. A circle of robed figures. A bell—or a moon, I couldn't tell. His hands shook and the story came out jumbled and he made a sound of frustration, a small wordless click in his throat that must have cost him, and pressed both fists against his temples.

"Slowly," I said. "We have time. We—"

We didn't have time. The thirteenth moon was waxing somewhere beyond the peaks; the ghosts had told me we had days, maybe hours. But I said it anyway, because he needed to hear it, because six years is a long time to go unheard.

"Slowly," I said again. "Start anywhere. I'll listen."

Something broke in his face. Not dramatically—just a loosening, like ice shifting on a river. He sat back on his heels and began again, one token at a time, and this time I followed.

A mountain: *this place*. A path winding up: *the way to the heartstone*. A figure with a line through its throat: *me*. Then a cluster of tokens arranged in a ring, each marked with a different symbol—a chalice, a chain, an eye, a flame—and his finger tapped each one twice.

"Names?" Isolde guessed. "Ranks? The inner circle of the cult?"

He nodded hard. Seven tokens. Seven masters. Then he swept all but one away—the eye—and placed it alone at the center of the ring, and around it he arranged smaller tokens: dozens of them, each carved with a tiny open mouth.

"The Eye keeps the voices," Tam said quietly. All his charm had drained out of his face, leaving something older and harder underneath. "All those mouths. All those stolen voices, feeding one man."

The acolyte looked at Tam for a long moment. Then he did something extraordinary. He picked up a blank token—an uncarved sliver of bone—and held it out to Tam across the space between them, his expression unreadable.

Tam didn't take it. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"He's offering you a voice," I said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "A blank one. He's asking—" I turned to the acolyte. "You want to hear him speak. Truly speak. Don't you. You think if he holds your bone, you'll be able to... what? Borrow his voice? Speak through it?"

The acolyte's eyes were wet. He nodded.

And Tam—clever, guarded Tam, who kept his past locked behind charm like a man boarding up windows—took the token. Held it. And when he spoke next, testing it, saying nothing more than *can you hear me*, the acolyte's whole body shuddered, and tears slid silently down his face, and I understood that for six years no one had ever given him their voice. Everyone he'd ever served had demanded his.

"We're taking him with us," I said.

Tam's head came up. "Marguerite. He's one of *them*."

"He's the reason they'll fail." I stood, knees cracking in the cold. "Think about it. He knows the shrine paths, the watch rotations, the seven masters' signs. He knows where the Eye keeps the harvested voices. He's spent six years memorizing everything he couldn't say." I looked down at the little white bones scattered on the black floor, all those careful pictures, six years of a silenced man teaching himself to sing in a language no one had bothered to learn. "They took his voice and he built another one out of the only material they let him touch. He's been preparing for this longer than any of us."

The acolyte was gathering his tokens with shaking hands, wrapping them in cloth, and I could see he still didn't quite believe it—that we were real, that the offer was real. So I did the only thing I knew how to do. I knelt, took one of his blank tokens from the cloth, and carved into it with my thumbnail, quick and rough, the only mark that mattered: a figure standing, arms open. Not kneeling. Standing.

I pressed it into his palm and closed his fingers around it.

"What's your name?" I asked. "Your true one. Not your cult name."

He hesitated. Then he traced letters on the floor with one fingertip, slow and deliberate, in the old alphabet every village child learns from gravestones.

*S-e-f-u.*

"Sefu," I said, and the dead in the walls sighed all together, a sound like wind through a reed bed, and I swear the candles guttered toward him as if bowing. "Sefu. We have a long climb ahead of us and worse waiting at the top. Can you lead us past their watchers?"

Sefu rose. He looked at each of us in turn—Isolde with her maps, Tam with his borrowed voice and his missing past, me with my name written in a dead woman's book—and then he lifted one last token from his bundle and held it high so the grey light caught it.

It showed a mountain peak. And above the peak, a moon.

Thirteen points around its rim.

And every one of the points had been freshly scored deep, as if he'd been counting down. As if he'd been counting down for years, waiting for someone to come who could read it.

There were three points left.

Three nights until the thirteenth moon rose full over the heartstone—and Sefu turned toward the upper passes and began to climb, expecting us to follow.
