## 7. Thirteenth Moon Rising

The thirteenth moon rose the color of a wound.

Sefu saw it first, because he had learned to watch the sky the way other people watched doors—for what came through them. It climbed above the eastern ridge, swollen and wrong, a moon that belonged to no calendar Isolde had ever drawn. The old maps called it the Tithe-Moon. The cult called it the Opening. Sefu called it nothing, because he had no voice to call it anything, and because some things did not deserve a name.

He tapped Marguerite's shoulder twice and held up the bone token he had carved in the night: a thin crescent, its underside scored with eleven parallel lines. *It is early. We are late.*

She read it in a glance, the way she read all his tokens now, faster than Tam ever managed, as if the dead had taught her a shorthand the living hadn't earned. Her face did the thing it always did when the arithmetic went bad—went still, went hard, went onward.

"Then we stop sleeping," she said.

They had been climbing for six days. Thornfell was a smudge of memory below them, and above them the Thornfell heartstone waited in its crown of black granite, close enough now that Sefu could feel it in his teeth—a pressure, a hum, like a struck bell that had never been allowed to finish ringing. His chest ached with it. His chest ached with everything these days. He had cut his voice out rather than give it to the cult, and in the four years since, he had learned that silence was not an empty thing. Silence was full. Silence pressed. And up here, beneath the thirteenth moon, the silence was pressing *back*.

Tam ranged ahead the way he always did, all loose limbs and easy swagger, but Sefu had watched him long enough to see the truth under the charm: Tam moved like a man walking a road he had already walked. He never hesitated at a fork. He never stumbled on scree. Once, when the path split around a fallen pine, Tam had said, "Left," before Isolde's compass had finished settling, and then looked faintly surprised at himself.

*You remember this mountain,* Sefu had carved to him that night by the fire.

*Everyone remembers mountains,* Tam had said, grinning. *They're very memorable.*

But his grin had not reached his eyes, and Sefu had seen the dead wear that same expression—the look of souls who knew more than they could say. Sefu did not press. He knew what it was to have a hole where your history should be. He had dug his own.

* * *

The ghosts came at dusk on the seventh day, at the treeline, where the pines gave out and the bare shoulders of the mountain began.

Sefu felt them before anyone—his scars went cold, both sides of his throat, a frost that had nothing to do with altitude—and he stopped so abruptly that Marguerite walked into his back. He raised his hand. Waited. The wind moved through the last of the pines, and the wind was *singing*.

It was not a melody. It was voices—dozens of them, braided, the way a choir braids, each thread distinct and none of them alone. Sefu had spent four years as a mute among the loud, and he knew voices the way Isolde knew coastlines. These were old. These were *murdered*. He could hear the cut in them, the same cut that lived in his own scars.

Marguerite heard them too. He saw it hit her like a physical blow—her hand flew to her sternum, her breath went ragged—and then she did the thing that made Sefu certain, in the marrow-deep way he was certain of very few things, that the Vellum Choir had chosen rightly.

She stepped *toward* it.

They came out of the treeline like mist off a lake. Figures. Eleven of them, though they shifted when you tried to count, robed in the grey of old ritual vestments, their faces the soft unfinished faces of the drowned and the burned. The choir. The last choir, massacred a century ago on this very mountain, the covenant broken over their bodies. Sefu had served their murderers. He had carried their tithe-jars. He had thought, in his worst nights, that he could smell their voices on his own hands.

One of them came forward. A young woman, her vestment torn at the shoulder, and Sefu's blood went to ice because he *knew* her—he had seen her face in the cult's reliquary, painted in a martyr's icon they kept not out of reverence but out of ownership. Sister Veyra. The first to die.

She looked at Marguerite, and her mouth moved, and though Sefu could not hear words—only Marguerite could—he could read the shape of them well enough. *Bone-Singer. Come. Quickly. They are coming too.*

Then the ghosts turned as one, all eleven, and began to climb, and the mountain lit them faintly from within, lanterns made of grief.

Tam had gone white. Not pale—*white*, the white of bone, and for one heartbeat Sefu could see through him, see the granite scree behind his shoulder where his shoulder should have been. Then it was gone, and Tam was rubbing his arms and saying, too lightly, "Well. That's a new one," and Sefu carved nothing, because some truths should not be forced into daylight before their owner was ready to carry them.

* * *

They climbed through the night.

Isolde set the pace, and it was a brutal one, the pace of a woman who had spent twenty years being told her life's work was heresy and had decided to prove the empire wrong with her boots. She had the maps strapped to her back in their oilskin case—every one of them, the originals, the disgraced surveys of the Hollow's borders that had cost her her name, her post, her country. She had confessed it all to them weeks ago, by firelight, in the flat voice of a woman reciting her own sentence: *I mapped the Hollow. I saw what the covenant was holding back. They stripped my commission and burned my charts, and I redrew every one from memory, and I have been looking for the Bone-Singer ever since.*

Now those maps rode between her shoulder blades like a second spine, and Sefu understood—they all understood—that Isolde would as soon surrender her hands.

The thirteenth moon climbed with them. It should not have. It hung in the west when they were in the east-facing couloir, and it hung in the east when they turned, and by the small hours it was simply *above*, directly overhead, following them like an eye. The light it cast was wrong—not silver but the pale yellow of old ivory, of the bones Sefu had carved all his life in the cult's ossuaries. Under that light, his shadow did not fall where it should. It fell *behind* him, stretched toward the summit, as if the mountain were pulling at him.

He tapped Marguerite and showed her his shadow. She looked at it a long moment.

"It's pulling all of us," she said. "That's the covenant. It knows we're coming. Or it knows *something* is."

Sefu carved quickly: *Or the cult. The moon is theirs.*

"Then we climb faster than their moon," Marguerite said, and Isolde, three steps above, snorted—a dry, bitter sound that was the closest she came to laughter—and lengthened her stride.

They climbed faster than their moon. For a while, it almost worked.

* * *

The ambush came at the treeline.

Not the treeline they had passed—the upper one, the last ragged fringe of pine that clung to the mountain's throat before the bare granite of the summit fields, and Sefu would understand later, turning it over in the wordless way he turned everything over, that the cult had chosen it deliberately. The trees gave cover to the hunters and none to the hunted. The narrow path gave no room to fight. And the ghosts—Sefu realized with a lurch—the ghosts had gone quiet, had thinned, had been *herded*, the way you herd sheep toward a pen.

The first crossbow bolt took a pine an inch from Tam's ear. The second took Marguerite's pack off her shoulder and pinned it to a trunk, prophecy-book and all, and Sefu saw her face as she looked at the Vellum Choir hanging there, skewered, and saw her choose—*saw her choose*—to leave it and run.

Robes came out of the trees. A dozen of them, faces hidden, moon-sigils sewn in silver thread, and at their center a tall figure Sefu knew by the shape of his stillness: Father Aurel, the Tithe-Keeper, the man who had stood at the altar while Sefu's blood ran down his chin, who had said, gently, *You may keep the silence, child. We only ever wanted the voice.* He had not aged a day in four years. He had not aged a day in a century, if the ghosts' faces were anything to go by.

"Bone-Singer," Aurel said, and his voice was kind, and that was the worst thing about it. "You are expected. All of you are expected. Come down, and no one needs to be harvested before their time."

"Isolde," Marguerite breathed. "*Run.*"

But Isolde had already stopped running. She had already turned. Sefu saw it happen the way he saw everything—too fast, too whole, in a single terrible instant: Isolde unshouldering the oilskin case, Isolde looking at the narrow path, at Tam and Marguerite and himself, at the summit above and the robes below, and making the arithmetic that cartographers make, the arithmetic of terrain and time and what can be carried and what must be left.

"Take the east chute," she said. "It's on the maps. It's the only way past the scarp before moonset." She was already pulling the case from her shoulders. "They want the charts more than they want me. A dead cartographer's maps are worth more than a live one's silence. I can *trade*—"

"You can trade nothing," Tam snarled, "because you're coming *with*—"

A bolt took Isolde through the chest.

Sefu did not hear it. He felt it, in his scars, in the frost of them, the way he felt every violence done near him now, as if his cut-out voice had left a wound in the world that bled whenever the world was cut. Isolde went down against the pine, and the oilskin case went down with her, and her hand—her hand found it, dragged it under her, spread her arms over it the way a bird spreads over eggs, over flame.

"Run," Isolde said, and her voice was the flat voice, the sentence-reciting voice, and her blood was spreading across the maps' case in a dark bloom. "The east chute. *Go.* I bought you twenty years of searching. Don't waste it on four minutes of grief."

The ghosts came then. All eleven, bursting from the treeline like a wind given faces, and the robes recoiled, and Father Aurel's kind voice rose into something that was not kind at all, and Sefu did not see the rest, because Tam had Marguerite by the arm and Marguerite had Sefu by the wrist, and they ran.

They ran up the east chute with the thirteenth moon burning overhead and Isolde's blood on the mountain below them, and Sefu, who had no voice, discovered that grief does not require one. It came out of him anyway—a sound, a raw tearing sound from the ruin of his throat, the first noise he had made in four years, ugly and broken and *loud*, and Marguerite heard it and did not flinch. She gripped his wrist harder.

"She's alive," Marguerite said. "I'd hear it if she weren't. The dead talk to me, Sefu. She's *not talking*." Her voice cracked, and hardened. "Which means she's fighting. So we climb. We climb until we can make her fight worth something."

Above them the summit fields opened, black granite under an ivory moon, and at their heart, faint as a pulse, the heartstone's hum rose to meet them.

And below, growing fainter and then not faint at all, Sefu heard the choir singing—eleven voices and, impossibly, a twelfth, low and dry and stubborn, a cartographer's voice joining the song of the murdered dead.

They climbed.
