## 8. The Heartstone Bargain

The heartstone was not what the prophecies had promised.

Marguerite had imagined a jewel, perhaps, or a fist of black glass veined with light—something that announced its own importance. Instead it was a bone. A single vertebra the size of a chapel dome, half-buried in the granite of the summit like the fossil of some god that had knelt down here to die and never gotten up. Moonlight slid along its curves. It did not glow. It *hummed*, low and patient, the way an empty church hums after the last hymn has died and the candles are guttering.

She had heard that hum her whole life without knowing it. It had been underneath every whisper from every corpse she'd ever carved for—the bass note beneath all the small voices. She understood now why the dead sounded less afraid near Thornfell than anywhere else she had worked. They were close to home.

"It's cold," Sefu signed, his hands pale in the moonlight, breath ragged from the climb. Blood still crusted at his temple where a cultist's stone had grazed him below the treeline. "The whole mountain is holding its breath."

"The mountain isn't breathing," Tam said quietly. He stood at the edge of the summit field with his back to them, and there was something wrong about the set of his shoulders that made Marguerite's chest ache before he even turned around. "Look."

Below, far below, the treeline burned. Not with fire—with lanterns. Dozens of them, moving up the passes they had climbed through the night, a slow white constellation ascending. The moon-cult had lost them once. They would not lose them twice.

"An hour," Marguerite said. "Maybe two."

"Then we don't waste it." Tam turned, and he was smiling—that smile, the one that had disarmed her on the smuggler's road, crooked and warm and utterly unreliable—and she realized with a lurch that she had learned to read him well enough to know this smile was armor. "Come on then, Bone-Singer. Introduce yourself."

* * *

The stone accepted her hands the way a sleeping animal accepts touch: a shiver, a settling, a deepening of the hum until she felt it in her teeth, in the hollows behind her eyes. When she laid both palms flat against the vertebra's surface, the voices rose.

Not whispers this time. The dead spoke clearly here, as if distance were simply another kind of deafness and the stone had cured it. Eleven voices, braided. The murdered choir of Thornfell, dead a hundred years, singing their names into her bones.

*I am Wren, I kept the eastern door.*

*I am Odile. I sang my mother across.*

*I am Halvard, and I am sorry, I am sorry, I was the first to run—*

And beneath the eleven, older and vaster, a voice that was not a voice but a *pressure*, the covenant itself speaking through the stone the way wind speaks through a crack in a wall:

*A life, freely given. A voice, freely surrendered. The door is held shut by sacrifice alone, and the sacrifice must be renewed each century by the Choir. The Choir is broken. The tithe comes due. The Hollow opens unless—*

"Unless what?" Marguerite whispered.

*Unless a Bone-Singer stands where you stand, and pays.*

The word *pays* went through her like a hook through cartilage. She knew—I have always known, she thought; I knew the moment I saw my name in the Vellum Choir—knew what freely given meant when spoken by something this old. Old bargains did not accept coin. They did not accept labor or love or good intentions. They took lives.

She pulled her hands back. The hum dimmed to a pulse. Behind her, Sefu had arranged his bone tokens on the granite: **WHAT DOES IT WANT**.

"What they all want," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Everything. And only one price on the list."

"No." Tam's voice, sharp, behind her.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to hear the terms." He crossed the summit field toward her, fast, and for once there was no charm anywhere on him, nothing smooth, just raw fear wearing his face like a borrowed coat. "I've known since the waystation. Maybe since the road. Maybe—" he laughed, a terrible sound, "—maybe since before I could tell you my own name, which, incidentally, I couldn't."

Marguerite went very still. "Tam."

"My name isn't Tam." He said it quickly, the way you tear off a bandage. "Or it is now. It's the only one I've got. But I remember things, Marguerite. I remember a choir loft. I remember running. I remember being the last one out of the shrine at Thornfell while eleven people who loved me screamed behind me, and I remember dying in the snow with their song still in my mouth, and I remember *waking up*, forty years ago, in a smuggler's camp, with no past and no scars and a hole in my chest shaped exactly like everything I'd lost." His voice cracked and he let it. "The mountain told me to find you because the mountain is the stone and the stone has been waiting a hundred years for someone to come pay its debt. I'm not your guide. I'm the last survivor of the choir you're meant to replace. I'm the ghost they gave flesh so the bargain could be kept. I was always going to end up here, offering myself. That's the whole of what I am."

The silence afterward was enormous. Below them the lantern constellation climbed. Above them the thirteenth moon sat fat and ivory on the crown of the sky, hours from full.

Sefu's hands moved slowly, deliberately: **IS IT TRUE**.

"I think," Marguerite said, and her throat hurt, "I think he's been trying to die since the day I met him. Every risk he took. Every joke. Charm is what people wear when they've already said goodbye to everyone." She looked at Tam. "Aren't I right?"

He didn't answer. Which was an answer.

"Then hear me," she said, and stepped between him and the stone. "I have spent my whole life invisible. Carving bones for the dead, hearing them, saying nothing. I built myself into a shadow because shadows don't get hunted, don't get named in prophecy books, don't get *taken*. And it didn't work. The world found my name anyway. So I'm done with disappearing—mine, and yours." Her hands were shaking. She let them. "You don't get to give yourself, Tam. You spent a hundred years—however long you had—as a ghost carrying guilt for surviving. You finally got a body. You finally got *friends*. And your grand plan is to hand it all back? No. I refuse."

"The covenant doesn't care what you refuse—"

"Then listen to the terms again." She turned back to the stone and pressed her palms to it, hard, hard enough to bruise. "*A life, freely given. A voice, freely surrendered.* Two clauses. Two prices. Everyone reads the first and stops." The pressure of the old voice gathered beneath her hands, curious, attentive. "I'm a bone-carver. I carve names into bone so the dead aren't forgotten. My whole gift is *holding voices*. Every voice I ever heard is still in me—the drowned child I carved last month, the grandmother whose ribs I etched when I was nine, the choir, all eleven of them, singing in my skull since the waystation. What if the life given isn't blood? What if the life given is the life of memory—the self I've spent forty years building out of other people's voices?"

"That's not how bargains work," Tam said hoarsely.

"You don't know how bargains work. You ran away from the last one." But she said it gently, and she reached back without looking and found his hand, cold in the moonlight, and gripped it. "Old thing," she said to the stone, to the covenant coiled inside it, "hear my offer. I will give you my memory of every voice I have ever carried. All of them. The dead and the living alike. I will forget the child's laugh and the choir's hymn. I will forget Isolde swearing at her maps. I will forget Sefu's hands talking in the dark. I will forget—" her voice broke, and she pushed through it, "—I will forget the smuggler who met me on the road and lied beautifully and turned out to be the truest man I ever knew. That is a life. It's mine and it's freely given, every piece of it, and it will seal your door for another hundred years better than one more ghost rattling around your mountain. Take it. Or take nothing and watch the cult try to open you themselves, badly, and curse you to leak forever."

The stone was silent.

The mountain was silent.

Even the lanterns below seemed to pause.

Then the hum changed—not louder, but *fuller*, the way a chord changes when the final note arrives—and Marguerite felt the covenant turn its attention on her fully for the first time, vast and ancient and, impossibly, almost gentle, like something that had waited a very long time to be offered anything other than death.

*Accepted,* said the hollow places of the world. *When the thirteenth moon crowns, sing what you carry, and be emptied. Be the Choir. One voice containing hundreds.*

"Wait," Tam said. "Wait—Marguerite, you'll forget *us*. You'll stand up from that stone and look at us like strangers—"

"Yes."

"There has to be another way, there's always another way, give me an hour—"

"We don't have an hour." She pointed down the mountain with her chin. The lanterns had reached the high scree. "And even if we did, I wouldn't look for one. This is my choice, Tam. Mine. On my terms. You of all people should understand wanting to choose the shape of what you lose." She smiled at him, and it cost her everything, and she paid it gladly, because that was the point, wasn't it—freely given. "Besides. Isolde told me once that a map isn't the territory. It's just how you find your way back." Her eyes stung. "They'll find their way back to me. Someone always remembers. That's what I do for the dead. Let them do it for me, just this once."

Sefu signed something fast, furious, grief-struck—then folded his hands, and pressed them flat over his heart, and bowed to her the way acolytes bow to altars.

Tam pulled her against his chest and held her, and above the treeline, faint and rising, Marguerite heard the ghosts begin to sing—eleven voices and a twelfth, the stubborn cartographer's, climbing toward them through the dark.

Above the summit, the thirteenth moon touched the edge of fullness, and the heartstone began, softly, to open.
