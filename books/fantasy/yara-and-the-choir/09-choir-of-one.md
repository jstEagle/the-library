## 9. Choir of One

The heartstone did not look like a heart. It looked like a wound that had healed wrong—a mass of black granite thrust up through the mountain's spine, veined with seams of pale quartz that pulsed in rhythm with something far below. Or perhaps above. At Thornfell's height it was difficult to say where the world ended and the Hollow began.

The thirteenth moon hung swollen and silver over the ridge, close enough that Marguerite could see its craters like fingerprints pressed into wax.

She stood before the stone with Tam's hand still warm on her arm, his confession still ringing in the hollow place behind her ribs. *I am the last survivor. I have been dead for a hundred years. I was given flesh to find you.* She had not let go of him yet. She suspected that if she let go now, she never would.

"The covenant wants a life," he said again, quieter this time, as if repetition could make the terrible arithmetic gentler. "Freely given. That was always the price, Marguerite. A hundred years ago the choir gave themselves all at once, and the Hollow closed. The cult broke it open. Someone has to pay to shut it again." He smiled—the smile that had charmed her on a smuggler's road a lifetime of weeks ago—and it cracked her heart clean down its oldest seam. "Let it be me. I've already had my death. This one is borrowed anyway."

"No."

"Marguerite—"

"No." She turned to face him fully. Behind him, Sefu stood at the stone's base with his carved tokens fanned in both hands, and Isolde lay propped against a boulder, gray-faced, one hand pressed to the wound in her side, her maps spread around her like shed feathers. The ghosts of the choir ringed the summit—nine shapes woven from moonlight and grievance, watching with faces she had come to know as well as any living souls'. The maiden Wren, who had spoken to her in waystations and graveyards across three provinces. The old cantor, Hollis, whose voice rumbled like stones settling. All of them waiting. All of them hoping. None of them able to tell her what she'd already read in their own Vellum book: *the Bone-Singer gives what cannot be taken.*

"You've been dead a hundred years," she said to Tam. "You spent them hollow, carrying a hole where your life should be. I know exactly what that costs, because I've heard it in every bone I ever carved. I will not send you back into it." Her voice shook, and she let it. "And I will not leave Sefu without a tongue to speak for him, or Isolde bleeding out on a mountain because I chose the easy grief."

"So you'll choose the hard one," Tam said. "Dying."

"I'll choose neither." She stepped toward the heartstone. Its surface hummed against her palms when she laid them flat—cold and hot at once, like holding a scream. "I'm a bone-singer. I don't give lives. I give voices."

* * *

She had known, in the way she had known since the drowned child's femur whispered her name, that this moment would ask everything of her. But knowing and standing inside it were different countries entirely, and the border between them was the stone singing against her skin.

*Covenant,* said the heartstone, and the word was not sound but pressure, a tide pulling through the marrow of the mountain. *A life freely given.*

"A life," Marguerite agreed, "lived in voices." She closed her eyes. "Take the ledger. Every voice I have ever carried."

The stone went still. Even the thirteenth moon seemed to lean closer.

She began, as she had begun every carving, every séance, every midnight whisper to the grieving, with breath. But this time she did not shape the breath into another's voice. This time she opened herself completely—every door, every locked room, every secret shelf where she had stored the dead so they would not be lost.

The first voice out of her was her mother's, humming over a cradle twenty years gone, and the losing of it felt like tearing off a finger. The second was her father's, laughing once, only once, before the fever took him, and by then she was weeping without pausing, because the covenant did not accept halting gifts.

She sang the drowned child whose small bones had started all of this, sang the gurgle of water in lungs and the mother's wail on the riverbank. She sang the murdered choir—all nine of them, their dying cries and their living laughter, Wren's bright irreverence and Hollis's patient gravity—and felt each memory loosen from its mooring as it passed through her throat into the stone. She sang the cult's victims, hundreds of harvested voices the tithe-masters had bottled in glass and stolen marrow, and the heartstone drank them and shuddered, and somewhere below the quartz seams flared white.

She sang Tam. His voice on the smuggler's road—*"the mountains told me to find you"*—his laugh at her fire, his low confession minutes ago. She felt that memory go and grabbed for it and it slid through her fingers like river water, and she sang harder, faster, giving everything before it could be taken piecemeal.

She sang Isolde saying *I've been searching for you for twenty years.* She sang Sefu's silence, which had its own voice, its own eloquence—the click of bone tokens, the fierce set of his jaw.

The Hollow answered.

Below the heartstone, below the mountain, below the world's floor, the breach yawned wide—she felt it the way you feel a missing tooth, an absence with edges. And now the covenant poured through her like molten light down the quartz seams, following her song, stitching the tear closed thread by golden thread. She saw it even through closed eyelids: the map of the wound, and the seamstress working it, and the seamstress was herself, was her voice, was every dead soul she had ever sheltered lending their weight to the closing.

On the treeline below, the cult was screaming. She heard them distantly, their stolen youth curdling, their master's century of borrowed years collapsing all at once like a tent with its poles cut. The Hollow sealed against them with a sound like a cathedral door, and the thirteenth moon crossed the summit's peak, and the covenant spoke its final word through her own mouth in nine voices at once:

*Paid. Freely. In full.*

* * *

What remains, after?

She came back to herself kneeling on cold granite, her throat raw, her hands still pressed to the heartstone—which was only stone now, dark and quiet, its quartz seams dim as old scars.

The thirteenth moon was setting. That seemed wrong; it should have been dawn, shouldn't it? Some part of her insisted that after such a night the sun must rise. But no. The moon slid behind the western ridge like a coin into a pocket, and the stars wheeled indifferent overhead, and Marguerite Okonkwo knelt on a mountaintop trying to remember why she was crying.

She was crying very hard. There was a reason. There had been a reason. It sat just beyond the wall of her skull like a voice in another room—familiar, urgent, gone.

"Marguerite?"

A man crouched in front of her. Handsome, weathered, eyes red-rimmed. He was looking at her the way people look at things they have been warned they might lose. When she didn't answer, his face did something complicated—grief and relief fighting for the same ground.

"Do I—" Her voice came out ruined, sanded raw. "Do I know you?"

He flinched. Then, visibly, deliberately, he gathered himself, and smiled, and it was the bravest thing she had ever seen anyone do. "You're going to," he said. "My name is Tam. We're friends. We traveled a long way together, you and I. And you did something last night that saved everyone, and you don't remember it, and that's—" his voice caught, mended itself— "that's all right. Because I remember. We all remember. We'll carry it for you."

Behind him, others: a woman with a bandaged side clutching rolled maps like a lifeline, tears tracking through trail dust. A young man holding up a smooth bone token carved with an open hand, and then flipping it, quickly, to reveal its other side—a heart, roughly cut, offered like a sentence.

Marguerite looked at them. Strangers. Strangers who knew her name, who were looking at her with love so naked it embarrassed her, though she could not have said why.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I don't—there was something I was supposed to keep. Something important. Everyone I ever..." She touched her own throat, wondering at the rawness there, the ghost of ten thousand songs. "It's all gone. It's all just... quiet."

The woman with the maps knelt beside her, wincing, and took her hand. "Then we'll have to make some noise," she said.

Far below, in the valley, the first true dawn of the new covenant was breaking over Thornfell—and Marguerite watched it with empty hands and a full company, not knowing yet what she had paid, or that the story of what she'd given was only beginning to be told.
