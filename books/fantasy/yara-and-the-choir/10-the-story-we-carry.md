## 10. The Story We Carry

The first snow of the year came down on Thornfell like a benediction, slow and thick, settling on the new rooftops and the old graveyard alike. Tam watched it from the doorway of the bone-carver's shop, a mug of mulled cider warming his hands, and thought—as he thought every day now, in some form or another—*she doesn't know who I am.*

She knew, of course. That was the strange arithmetic of it. Marguerite Okonkwo knew that Tam was Tam, that he was hers, that he had walked beside her up a mountain and offered her his life and wept like a boy when she refused to take it. She had all the facts, written out in Isolde's careful hand and bound into a book that lived on her workbench between the chisels and the bone dust. What she didn't have was the weight of them. The facts sat in her memory like stones in an empty room—present, accounted for, carrying no echo.

Inside, she worked. Through the frost-furred window he could see her bent over a memorial rib, her knife moving in long sure strokes, carving a child's name into bone for a family who had lost their son to the river in autumn. Her hands remembered everything even when her mind did not. He found that both beautiful and unbearable, and had stopped trying to separate the two feelings, because they arrived together now, always, like weather.

He went inside. The bell above the door—the one Sefu had hung there with great ceremony, using three bone tokens and a look of profound disapproval until Tam understood it was meant as a gift—chimed softly.

Marguerite looked up. And smiled. And for one heartbeat, before the smile could settle into its familiar shape—the shape that held him at arm's length from the deepest rooms of her, not from coldness but from honesty—he let himself imagine it was the old smile, the one that had once said *I hear you, smuggler, all of you, every buried thing.*

"Tam." Her voice was warm. Warmth was not the same as recognition. "You're dripping snow on my floor."

"I'm distributing it evenly," he said. "It's called craftsmanship."

"It's called making work." But she was laughing as she said it, setting down her knife, wiping bone dust from her fingers. "Sit. I've been waiting for you."

His heart did the small treacherous leap it still did, every time, though he'd learned to cage it. "Waiting?"

"Sefu brought tokens this morning." She nodded toward the corner of the bench, where a small carved bone lay: a spiral, wound tight, opening outward. "I know what it means because you told me. Story night. You tell me things after dark, when the shop is closed, so I don't have to read them alone." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes—frustration, maybe, or grief wearing frustration's coat. "I hate that I have to be told."

"You remember being told," he offered gently. "That counts for something."

"It counts," she agreed, "for something." She turned the spiral token over in her palm. "Tell me tonight's story. Whichever one you want. You always pick."

He always picked the same one, lately. He wondered if she noticed. He wondered if she minded. He sat down on the stool across from her bench, the one with the worn seat that had become his by attrition, and began.

* * *

"There was a woman," he said, in the voice he used for this—a storyteller's voice, rounded and warm, the smuggler's charm repurposed into something honest, "who could hear the dead. Not ghosts rattling chains—the dead themselves, their voices, carried in the bones of the world. She spent her whole life pretending she couldn't, because being invisible felt safer than being seen. Do you remember her?"

"She sounds foolish," Marguerite said, but her mouth curved.

"She sounds careful. There's a difference. One night she found her own name in an old book, inked beside a date, and the date was that very night, and everything she'd built her invisibility out of came apart in her hands."

"The Vellum Choir." Isolde spoke from the doorway, where she had appeared with the silence of a woman who had spent twenty years learning not to be caught. She was older than she'd been a year ago—or perhaps just less hidden about it, her grey hair loose, her ruined reputation worn like a medal now instead of a wound. In her arms she carried a leather tube, and from the tube she drew a map, unrolling it across the cleared half of the workbench with the reverence other people reserved for scripture.

It was the map of the Hollow. Her masterpiece, the one that had earned her exile: borders drawn where no border should be, the thirteenth moon's path inked in silver, the Thornfell heartstone marked with a symbol no cartographer's guild would recognize and every ghost in the mountains would. Along the margins, in her small precise hand, ran annotations—and among them, Tam knew without looking, were the words *M. Okonkwo, the Bone-Singer, who gave more than a life; she gave the remembering.*

"You're doing the map again," Marguerite said.

"I'm doing the map again." Isolde smoothed a wrinkle with two fingers. "Because you asked me last week where the shrine was. The one where we found Sefu. And I would rather show you a hundred times than have you not know your own road." She tapped the mountain's shoulder, the treeline, the pass. "We came up here. The cult was waiting below. And Isolde—" her voice did not waver; she had practiced this, Tam realized, practiced telling her own death in advance until it obeyed her, "—Isolde fell here, at the trees, keeping the maps against her body so the cult couldn't burn them. And then she got back up. Because the maps mattered, and because falling wasn't finished with her yet."

"And I climbed past her," Marguerite said quietly. It was not a question. She had heard this part many times. Her hand had gone, unconsciously, to her own collarbone—to the place where, Tam knew, a scar sat that she had no story for.

"You climbed past her. With Sefu holding your sleeve. With me holding yours. With the whole murdered choir walking beside us like lanterns, showing us the way up." He leaned forward. "Do you want the heartstone part tonight? Or shall I save it?"

Marguerite was quiet a moment. Snow ticked against the window. Then: "The heartstone part. Always the heartstone part."

* * *

So he told her. A man made of a ghost given flesh, who had loved her across a century without knowing why; who had stood at the stone and offered himself because it was all he had to give; and a woman who looked at that offering, at the one gift anyone had ever laid at her feet that cost its giver everything, and said *no.* Who said: *I will not pay with a body. I will pay with a self. I will give the stone every voice I ever heard—every ghost, every whisper, every name the dead ever trusted me with—and I will give it the living ones too. Yours. Isolde's. Sefu's. Mine.*

And the stone had taken it. And the covenant had reforged, and the Hollow had sealed like a wound closing, and the moon-cult had come up the mountain howling for the door they needed her to open and found it shut forever, and their young-faced master had aged a hundred years in the space of a scream.

And Marguerite Okonkwo had walked back down the mountain alive, and free, and empty as a bell.

"—and she woke in the waystation," Tam finished, in the hushed voice the ending deserved, "and the first face she saw was mine, and she didn't know it. And the first thing she said was—"

"'Who are you people,'" Marguerite supplied, dry, "and 'why is the mute one crying.'"

Sefu, who had let himself in through the back some minutes ago as he always did, unhooded his lantern and set out the evening's bread and honey without a sound. At the joke he touched two fingers to his chest and flicked them outward—his sign, the one Tam had taught him, the one that meant *laughter, small.*

"He wasn't crying," Tam said.

"He was absolutely crying. Isolde told me."

Isolde, rolling up her map, did not deny it. "He was. We all were. You'd just saved the world and you were asking us our names."

"And you gave them." Marguerite looked around the lamplit shop—at the map, at the bread, at Sefu's steady hands, at Tam—and her voice gentled into something almost too quiet to hold. "Every night since. You keep giving them."

"That's the arrangement," Tam said lightly, because lightness was the only way he could carry it. "Found family means someone remembers for you. You do the carving. We do the keeping."

She was silent for a while. Outside, the snow kept falling, filling the tracks of the day, erasing nothing that mattered. When she spoke again, she had turned the spiral token over and over in her fingers, and she did not look at any of them, which meant she was going to say the thing she had been turning over for days.

"I heard something today," she said.

The room stilled. Sefu's hands stopped mid-motion. Isolde set down the map tube with a click that sounded enormous.

"Not a voice," Marguerite hurried on. "Nothing like—before. Just... when I was carving the Ashford boy's name, the bone hummed. Under the knife. Like a string someone plucked once, far away. And for half a second I thought I heard—" She stopped. Started again, steadier. "I thought I heard singing. A choir. Very far off. And it sounded like it was singing *my* name."

Tam's chest tightened. He glanced at Isolde, whose face had gone carefully, professionally blank—the cartographer's face, the one she wore when new terrain appeared at the edge of the known map.

"The covenant took what you gave," Isolde said slowly. "What you gave was the hearing. But hearts regrow around scars, Marguerite. Perhaps bones do too."

"Or perhaps," Tam said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, "the stone left you a door. Small. Cracked. Because covenants are bargains, and bargains have terms, and somewhere in that century-old book there may be a line none of us thought mattered." He met Marguerite's eyes—eyes that did not hold their history, but held, unmistakably, *her*, the self that had survived the losing of everything else. "Shall I fetch the Vellum Choir?"

Marguerite looked down at the spiral token in her palm. Wound tight, opening outward. She smiled—not the old smile, not yet, but one that might, someday, grow into its shape.

"No," she said. "Tonight we eat bread, and tomorrow you'll tell me the heartstone story again. But the day after—" her fingers closed around the token, "—the day after, we open the book. All of us. Together."

Outside, far off in the white dark of the mountains, something that might have been wind—or might have been a choir, remembering—passed over rebuilt Thornfell, and did not stop.
