## 1. The Date That Wasn't There

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Bram Almeida heard the sound that no ledger should make: the soft, wet whisper of a page turning itself.

He set down his pen. In forty years of keeping accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things, he had learned that the difference between a good keeper and a dead one was precisely this — the willingness to stop writing when the room began to write back.

"Yara," he said to the empty counting-room above him, though he did not raise his voice. She would hear. She always heard. "How many entries tonight?"

"Eleven so far." Her voice drifted down through the floorboards, thin and clear as a needle drawn through cloth. "A debt of one remembered name, paid in full by a widow on Coil Street. A promise of rain owed to the eastern farms since spring — collected at dusk, apparently. And someone in the Low Quarter has been dreaming a house into existence for six years without declaring it, so I've opened an account."

Bram allowed himself the smallest of smiles. Eleven entries was a quiet night. Quiet nights were the only kind he trusted, and even then only provisionally, the way a man trusts a river he has never seen in flood.

"And the gate?" she called down.

"The gate is the gate." He said it the way he said it every night, and every night it was true, and every night the truth of it cost him something small and unrecoverable, like a coin worn smooth between finger and thumb.

He took up his lantern and went down.

* * *

The stair beneath the counting-house was older than the Guild, older than the city that had grown around the Guild like ivy around a nail. Its steps were stone the color of dried blood, and they were not worn in the middle as stairs should be, because in three hundred years almost no one had climbed them more than a handful of times. Bram's own boots had made the deepest hollows, and he found a grim comfort in that — that the most-worn thing in this place was the path of a single ordinary man, walking down every night to look at a door and confirm that it was still closed.

The gate filled the end of the vault the way grief fills a house: completely, and mostly by being unremarked upon.

It was iron, or it had been iron once, before the forging of it had passed through hands that understood what they were making. Now it was something else — a dark so dense it seemed to drink the lantern light rather than reflect it, worked over with bands and sigils and nine hundred locks, none of which were locks in any sense a locksmith would recognize. They were sentences. Each one a clause of refusal, hammered into metal, and the whole of them together said *no* in a language older than Vessany's first king.

Bram walked the gate's width slowly, as he did every night, touching each of the seven anchor-sigils with two fingers. Warm. Warm. Warm. Cool — he paused at the fourth, held his palm flat against it, counted to thirty. It warmed under his hand, grudgingly, like a cat remembering it liked someone. Fine. Fine. The fifth, sixth, seventh: warm, warm, warm.

He exhaled. Somewhere behind his ribs, the knot he carried there loosened by one thread.

It was on the walk back — lantern low, mind already climbing the stairs ahead of his body toward the small pleasure of cold tea and a finished column of figures — that he saw the date.

There was no calendar in the vault. There had never been a need for one. But the wall beside the gate bore the tally-marks of keepers going back two centuries, each one carving the day's date beside their name in the old guild-fashion, a chain of witness running back through dead men's handwriting. Bram carved his own every night with the point of his knife. Tonight he raised the knife, and stopped.

Someone had already written today's date.

Not carved. *Inked*, in fine black script, low on the wall where the tally-marks gave way to bare stone, where nothing should have been able to hold ink at all. He crouched, brought the lantern close. The letters were small and precise and slanted slightly rightward, and they read:

*Tenth night of the Drowned Moon.*

Tonight's date. Written in ink that could not have been applied by any hand, because the vault had been sealed since morning and Bram had the only key, and because the stone drank water the way sand does, and yet the ink sat upon it glistening, wet, patient.

And the handwriting —

Bram was an auditor. Handwriting was his trade the way blades are a soldier's trade; he knew the twelve clerks of the Guild by their loops and crossings the way other men know faces. He knew Yara's hand better than his own, having corrected it for seven years — the tall ascenders, the way her *t*s crossed with a single decisive stroke, the little backward lean of her numerals, as if even her numbers were wary of leaning too far forward.

The date on the wall was written in Yara's hand.

Exactly. Not similar. Exact, down to the wary numerals, down to the ink-bleed pattern of the particular cheap tallow-smoke ink the Guild issued to its junior clerks because it was all the budget allowed.

Bram stood very still in the cold of the vault, and the lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft, and the gate said nothing, which was somehow worse than if it had said anything at all.

* * *

Above, the counting-room glowed amber against the dark, and Yara sat exactly where he had left her, sleeves pushed up, three ledgers open around her like the petals of some paper flower. She was twenty-three years old and had been the Guild's since she was found on its steps one winter night with no name, no blanket worth the word, and no record anywhere in the city of her having been born. The Guild had named her themselves — *Yara*, from the old word for *what remains*. Bram had argued for something warmer. He had lost. He often thought, watching her work, that the loss had shaped them both.

She looked up when he came in, and whatever she saw in his face made her put down her pen.

"Bram?"

"There's ink on the vault wall," he said. "Today's date. In your handwriting."

He had rehearsed a gentler version on the stairs. It abandoned him entirely, and he watched the color leave her face in the honest way it leaves the young, all at once, like a tide going out.

"That's not possible," she said. "I haven't been down there since — I've never touched that wall. I've never—" She stopped. Looked at her own fingers as if they belonged to a stranger who might have been busy while she slept. "You think I wrote it?"

"I think no one wrote it," Bram said. "That's what frightens me."

He pulled a chair to her side of the table — an intimacy he permitted himself perhaps twice a year — and laid out the problem the way he'd lay out a discrepancy in an account, because discrepancies were the one language in which he could say frightening things steadily.

"A ledger records what has happened," he said. "That is its entire virtue. When a ledger begins to record what *will* happen, it stops being a ledger and becomes something we burn. Do you understand me?"

"You think it's a prophecy."

"I think the word *prophecy* is a comfort people use for documents that should terrify them." He rubbed his eyes. "There's a book in the deep archive. Restricted. I've read it once, the year I took the keys, and I have spent eleven years wishing I hadn't. It's a hundred years old. It's bound in grey leather, and it doesn't have a title, and it describes — among other things — a gate, and a Crown, and a person with no bloodline and no past, and a specific date on which all of those things would meet."

Yara had gone very still. Stillness was her gift; he had seen her sit unmoving through audits that made grown merchants weep. But this was a different stillness, the stillness of prey deciding whether the shape in the grass is wind.

"What date?" she asked.

"You already know," Bram said gently. "That's why your voice sounds like that."

* * *

They went down together, which broke a rule so old it had no number, and neither of them remarked on it, because rules are for nights that intend to continue being ordinary.

The lantern light found the inked date exactly as he'd left it, still wet, still patient. Yara knelt before it. She did not touch it. She held her hand above the letters, close enough that he could see the tremor she was refusing to let reach her face, and compared them against her own hand as though the air itself were a page.

"It's mine," she whispered. "It's — Bram, that's my *hand*. That's how I cross things out when I'm copying, that little hook, I do it without thinking, nobody taught me, I just—"

"I know."

"How can it be mine? I didn't write it. I *swear* I didn't write it."

"I believe you." And he did, wholly, which was the most frightening part, because Bram Almeida believed almost nothing, professionally speaking. Belief was for people whose ledgers balanced.

Yara stayed kneeling. The lantern guttered. And then, in a voice gone small and careful, the voice of a clerk reading aloud a figure she hopes will turn out to be a mistake:

"The restricted book. The hundred-year-old one. The date it names — is it this one? Is it *tonight*?"

Bram looked at the gate. Nine hundred sentences of *no*, silent as they had ever been. But the fourth anchor-sigil, the cool one, the grudging one — he could see from here that it had begun, very faintly, to glow. Not warming. *Answering.* Like a lock that has heard, somewhere far off in the dark, the footsteps of the key coming home.

"Yes," he said.

Yara rose. She wiped her palms on her apron, a small domestic gesture, absurd and heartbreaking in the cold of the vault, and looked at the gate the way one looks at a sleeping animal whose breathing has changed.

"Bram," she said. "Why is it looking at me?"

And the gate — which had never spoken, which had never done anything in three hundred years but refuse — grew warm along its whole dark length, sigil after sigil after sigil, like a body waking, and somewhere beneath the iron, beneath the stone, beneath the city and its history, something turned over in its long sleep and said her name.
