## 2. What the Gate Keeps

Her name came again.

It did not come as sound. Sound has edges, and this had none — it arrived instead the way warmth arrives through a wall, or the way you know, without turning, that someone in a crowded room is speaking about you. It moved up through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her ankles, and settled somewhere behind her sternum like a coin dropped into a well she had not known she was carrying.

*Yara.*

"Step back." Bram's voice was steady, which frightened her more than shouting would have. Bram did not do steady. Bram did hands that shook and ledgers that trembled and a voice that frayed at every seam. Steady was new. Steady was wrong. "Yara. Step back from it. Slowly."

She stepped back. The gate followed — not physically, not yet, but with its attention, its heat, that slow rolling wakefulness passing sigil to sigil along its length like lamplight moving down a corridor of windows. She could see them now, the sigils, though an hour ago they had been only worn scratches in black iron: each one brightening as it woke, each one shaped — and this thought arrived whole and terrible, fully formed, the way her best arithmetic sometimes did — *each one shaped like a letter.*

Not letters of any alphabet she knew. But letters. A script. A sentence three hundred years long, and the gate was reading itself aloud, and the last word of the sentence was her name.

"Bram," she whispered. "What does it say?"

"It doesn't say anything." He had come around the counting-table to stand between her and the gate, and she realized he had put his body there deliberately, the old man interposing himself between his clerk and a thing sealed behind iron before either of their grandmothers was born. His hands were shaking again. Good, some small sane part of her said. Good. That's Bram. "It's never said anything. It doesn't know how. Whatever this is—"

*Yara.*

The second time was worse, because the second time she recognized something in it. Not menace. Not hunger, or not only hunger. There was a quality beneath the soundless sound that she had spent her whole orphaned life learning to hear across a room, at market stalls, outside lit windows on winter nights:

Relief.

The way someone says a name when they have been looking for its owner for a very long time.

"I'm going to take you upstairs," Bram said. He reached for her arm and she let him take it, his fingers cold through her sleeve, and together they turned toward the stair —

And the gate opened.

There was no groan of hinges. There was no hinge. The iron simply became a doorway the way a held breath becomes speech, the sigils flaring white-hot and then going dark, all of them at once, and where the gate had been there was now a passage down — deeper than the vault, deeper than any cellar the guildhouse had ever owned, down into a dark that was not empty but *attentive*. Air moved out of it against the natural direction of things, air that smelled of ink and cold copper and, underneath, faintly, unbearably, of candlewax. Of home. Of her own desk upstairs with its guttering tallow stub.

It smelled like her handwriting.

"No," Bram said, and his grip tightened. "No, no, no — it's not supposed to — it opens for no one, Yara, it has never—"

But her feet were already moving.

She would spend the rest of her life trying to explain this to herself, and she would never quite manage it, because the truth refused all comfortable shapes: she walked because she wanted to. Not compelled. Called. Every step toward that dark was a step she chose with both feet, and the shameful part — the part she would hide even from herself, folding it away like a love letter in a ledger nobody audits — was how *right* it felt. Three-and-twenty years of being nobody's daughter, no village's child, a foundling with a name given by a guild clerk's pen and nothing else, and here, finally, at the bottom of the world, something knew her. Something had been waiting for her specifically, the way a lock waits for its key, the way a blank page waits.

She was two steps into the dark when Bram caught her around the waist.

He was sixty-one years old and built like a filing cabinet, and he hauled her back with the desperate strength of a man who has buried too many friends, and she fought him — she was mortified to discover she fought him, heels scrabbling on stone, hands clawing at his forearms — while below them, in the deep attentive dark, something laughed. Not cruelly. That was the horror of it. It laughed the way family laughs, warm and low, at the sight of children struggling against bedtime.

"Let me go," she heard herself say, in a voice she didn't recognize. "Bram. *Bram.* It knows my name. Nobody knows my name. I don't even know my name, not really, it was just written on a form—"

"It knows what it wants you for," Bram gasped, dragging her backward, one agonizing inch at a time. "That's not the same thing. That has never once been the same thing."

And then the dark reached for her.

It did not touch her body. It went past her body entirely, the way an auditor goes past the cover page straight to the figures, and it read her. She felt it turn her over — her name, her non-existent mother, the orphanage at Vessany-harbor with its boiled cabbage smell, the first ledger she ever balanced at age nine, the specific loneliness of eating supper alone among other people's families — and it did all this not with cruelty but with *care*, with the horrible tenderness of a thing that intends to keep you. And at the center of the reading, at the place where a self should be, it paused.

She felt it pause. She felt it find the blankness there, the place where other people had bloodlines and birthrights and mothers' faces, the place in her that had always felt less like emptiness than like *margin* — clean, ruled, waiting — and she felt something in the dark go still with an attention so total that the whole vault seemed to lean toward it.

Then it spoke, once, directly into the marrow of her, in the voice of every voice she had ever longed to hear:

*Found you.*

Bram got her over the threshold. He got her up the stairs somehow, half-carrying her, her legs gone strange and far away, and behind them the gate closed with a sound like a book shutting, soft and final, the sigils dimming one by one until it was only iron again — worn, scratched, innocent, lying.

He sat her down at the counting-table and put a candle in front of her and a cup of the terrible chicory he drank, and he stood over her breathing hard, and for a long time neither of them said anything. Upstairs, faintly, the guildhouse went on being ordinary: someone laughing in the map-room, rain starting against the high windows, the clock in the hall striking the quarter-hour with its usual mild incompetence.

"Show me your hands," Bram said at last.

She showed him. Her palms looked ordinary. Then he tilted them toward the candlelight and made a sound low in his throat, and she turned her own hands over and saw it: a mark on each palm, fine as a hairline crack in glaze, a single character in that unknown script — the last letter of the gate's long sentence, the word it had ended on. Her name, written where no ink could reach it.

"Does it wash off?" she asked. Her voice sounded like someone else doing an impression of her.

"There's a keeper's manual in the restricted stacks," Bram said. "Third shelf. I've read every page of it eleven times, and nowhere — *nowhere* — does it say what to do if the gate takes notice of a person." He pulled out a chair and lowered himself into it across from her, and suddenly he looked every year of his age and several years beyond, a man watching a fire start in a building full of paper. "Yara. Listen to me. What happened down there — the way it spoke, the way it *knew* — that is not supposed to be possible. The gate keeps something. Something the first king bound at terrible cost, something that wore crowns before the binding, something that has spent three centuries pressing its face against the inside of that door. And tonight it looked through the keyhole of the world and saw you, and it said your name like a man saying the name of the woman he means to marry."

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere above them the rain thickened.

"And the worst of it," Bram went on quietly, "the very worst of it, is that you *walked*. I had to drag you, girl. You walked toward it glad."

She looked down at her marked palms, turned up in her lap like two small open books.

"Yes," she said. Because it was true, and because she had learned from ledgers that a lie anywhere poisons everything downstream of it, and because — this was the part she kept folded away, the love-letter in the margin — some cold clear clerk-part of her had already begun asking the question that would not stop asking itself: *if a thing with no name of its own, three hundred years old and hungry for belonging, looked at me and said found you — which of us was calling to which?*

"Bram," she said. "I need to know what I am. Before it comes back. Before it finds another way. I need to know why a gate that opens for no one opened for *me*."

He was quiet a long moment. When he answered, he would not meet her eyes; he looked instead at the sealed door below, at the iron that lay and lied, and his face was the face of a man about to break a rule he had kept for forty years.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we go and see Sef."

* * *

She did not sleep.

She told herself she would — she banked her fire, she lay down in her narrow room under the eaves where the guildhouse kept its clerks, she performed the whole liturgy of sleep — but her body lay there humming like a struck bell, and every time she closed her eyes she saw the dark at the bottom of the stairs, attentive, patient, fond.

Near midnight she gave up and sat at her little window with her palms open in her lap, watching the rain come down on Vessany. The mark on each hand had faded to almost nothing by candlelight, but she could feel it, the way you feel a word written on skin in invisible ink — present, waiting, hers. Twice, testing, she traced it with one fingertip, and twice the trace answered: a faint warmth, a pulse, like a knock returned from the far side of a door.

On the third try she stopped herself, hands pressed flat against her knees, heart going hard.

Because here was the thing she could not say to Bram, the thing with no honest column in any ledger: down in the dark, in the moment before he dragged her out, she had not felt afraid. Or rather — she had felt fear, real fear, cold and animal — but underneath the fear, coiled tight as a spring, there had been something else, something she recognized now as she sat alone in the smallest hours with the whole sleeping city below her.

Joy.

Someone had said her name. Someone vast and ancient and imprisoned had reached up through three hundred years of stone and iron and said *found you*, and some starving unguarded part of her — the part that had waited her whole life at windows like this one, watching other people be claimed, be kept, belong — had sung back.

She wrapped her arms around her knees and pressed her forehead to the cold glass and watched the rain, and far across the sleeping city, up on its hill where the palace lamps burned late, she had the sudden unaccountable certainty — clerk's certainty, arithmetic certainty — that somewhere up there, in a bed he might never leave alive, a dying king had just dreamed her face.

And that he had woken saying her name.
