## 3. A Clerk of No Bloodline

The rain found Yara before the streets did.

She had left the guildhouse without a cloak, without her good boots, without the little tin of peppermints she kept in the third drawer of her desk for the long nights of balancing — she had left, in truth, without deciding to leave at all. One moment she had been standing over the prophecy ledger with Bram's lamp guttering beside her, and the next she was running down the counting-house stairs, past the door that should not have opened, out into the wet dark of Copperlane with her heart going like a stamp-press.

*It's my handwriting.*

The words kept pace with her, step for step, down every alley. A hundred years old, that ledger. She had checked the vellum herself, run her thumb along its foxed edge, smelled the iron-gall ink gone brown as old blood. And across the page, in a line as neat and unremarkable as any entry she'd ever made: *On this night the gate will know its keeper, and the Unwritten will come home.*

Tonight's date. Her handwriting. Her name — no, not her name; worse than her name. The Unwritten.

Yara turned off Tallow Street into the warren behind the chandlers' row, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the rain came down in ropes rather than drops. She knew where she was going even before she admitted it. There was only one person in Vessany who could tell her what she was, and he lived above a shop full of dead people's paper.

Sef's archive occupied what had once been a granary, three floors of pigeonholes and plan-chests and ledgers stacked to the ceiling beams on planks laid across barrels. The sign outside read SEF: RECORDS KEPT, RECORDS FOUND, RECORDS FORGIVEN, which had always struck Yara as either a joke or a confession. She hammered on the door until her knuckles sang.

Light bloomed behind the shutters. Bolts — four of them, then a fifth she'd never heard before — drew back one by one.

Sef looked at her through the gap the way a man looks at weather he doesn't trust. He was small and grey and precise, with ink-stains up both forearms like old tattoos, and his eyes went from her soaked hair to the street behind her and back again.

"You're bleeding," he said.

"I'm what?" She touched her cheek and her fingers came away red — not hers. Lamp-oil and rust from the vault door, she thought wildly. Or something else. Something the gate had left on her.

"Come in before the whole street sees you." He shut the door and shot the bolts home with the tenderness other men spent on children.

The archive smelled of dust and camphor and, faintly, of oranges — Sef ate one every evening and buried the peel in a pot of dried lavender, an eccentricity Yara had always found comforting. Tonight it felt like a smell from another life, some ordinary life, where a girl balanced accounts by candlelight and the strangest thing in her day was a column that wouldn't sum.

"Tea," Sef said, already putting the kettle on a spirit-lamp among the record boxes. "And then you'll tell me why a Guild clerk is running through Copperlane at midnight looking like something dug up."

"I can't," Yara said. "I mean I can, but you won't—" She stopped. Pressed her hands flat against her knees to stop them shaking. "Sef. I need you to find my birth record."

The kettle ticked as it warmed. Somewhere above them, rain worked at the roof tiles with patient fingers.

Sef did not laugh, which was the first frightening thing. He poured tea into two cups chipped at the lip and sat down across from her at the long table where he authenticated deeds, and he looked at her for a long time with those grey, exact eyes.

"Do you understand what you're asking?"

"No," she said honestly. "That's why I'm asking."

"A birth record isn't one document, girl. It's a thread. The parish register where the midwife filed her fee. The census page. The tithe rolls, if there was land. The guild indenture books, if there was trade. A person leaves a trail of ink from the hour they're born, and I have followed such trails through forty years of paper." He set his cup down without drinking. "So you'll give me everything. Mother's name or district of birth, father's trade or absence of same, the parish, the year, whatever fragment you have, and we'll pull the thread."

"That's the thing." Yara heard her own voice go small. "There isn't a thread. There never has been. I was left at the guildhouse gate — you know that, everyone knows that — winter, twenty-two years ago, wrapped in a wool blanket with no mark on it. No token, no note. Bram says the Foundling Office searched for a year. But nobody ever actually *looked*, did they? Not properly. Not in paper."

Sef was very still.

"Give me the date," he said. "The exact date they took you in."

"Ninth of Frostwane." She didn't hesitate; it was the only birthday she had, the day the guild-book first held her name. "Twenty-two years ago this winter."

He got up. He did not ask if she wanted more tea. For the next three hours — and she counted them by the candles he lit and snuffed, because Sef wasted neither wax nor words — she watched him work, and the archive around her stopped feeling like a refuge and started feeling like a lung slowly filling with cold water.

He started with the Foundling Office rolls, naturally. Then the parish registers of all nine parishes within a night's walk of the guildhouse, cross-referenced against midwives' fee-books, because a child born in Frostwane would have been born indoors or died of it. Then the coroner's returns — stillbirths, abandonments, exposures — for a month on either side. Then, when that yielded nothing, he widened. The workhouse intakes. The charity hospital logs. The carter's manifests, on the chance a child had been brought in from the country. The border passes, in case someone had carried her across from Callowmere or the Salt Marches and failed to declare her.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

It wasn't that the records contradicted her existence. It was subtler and much worse than that. It was as though the ink itself had agreed, unanimously, to say nothing.

"Everyone leaves a trail," Sef murmured, somewhere past the second candle, not to her, maybe not to himself either. "The dead leave trails. The unborn leave trails. I have traced children who lived six hours. I have traced children who were never born at all — the fees were paid regardless." He pulled another register toward him, licked his thumb, turned pages with a sound like falling leaves. "You are not hidden, Yara. Hidden leaves shadows. You are—"

"Don't say it," she whispered.

"—*absent*," he finished anyway, because archivists are cruel the way surgeons are kind. "The paper does not know you. Twenty-two years, nine parishes, four jurisdictions. Either every clerk in Vessany conspired across a century to erase you—" he closed the last book, gently, the way one closes a coffin, "—or you were never written in the first place."

The candleflame stood perfectly straight, as if afraid to move.

"*Unwritten,*" Yara said. The word came out flat, tasting of the prophecy ledger, of her own impossible handwriting. "That's what it called me. The Unwritten will come home."

Sef's head came up sharply. "What ledger? What did you read?"

But before she could answer — before she could decide how much of the gate and the whispering vault and Bram's grey face she dared hand over to a man who traded in secrets — the kettle, forgotten hours ago on its little flame, began to scream.

Neither of them moved to take it off.

"The Crown," Sef said quietly. "You read something about the Crown."

"You know about it." It wasn't a question. The night had passed the point of questions. "Everyone knows but me."

"I know that the kings of Vessany die strange," Sef said. "I know that our archives hold three hundred years of royal decrees in which the handwriting changes overnight — not the style, mind, the *hand* — and no historian has ever satisfactorily explained it. I know that the Guild of Impossible Things pays me, quarterly, in coin minted before the last dynasty, to keep certain records exactly where they are and never, ever read them." He rubbed his ink-stained wrists, a gesture she had seen a thousand times and never once wondered at. Now she saw it for what it was: a man holding himself by the arms. "And I know that tonight, for the first time, I am glad of the bolts on my door, and sorry they were bought too late."

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell began to ring. Then a second bell, answering it. Then all of them, every tower in the city, ragged and urgent, the way bells ring when wards fail or kings fall ill or gates open that must not.

Yara stood. Her legs felt borrowed.

"Sef," she said. "What am I?"

He looked at her a long moment — at the rain-soaked clerk with no mother, no parish, no thread of ink anywhere in the world tying her to it — and she watched him choose, visibly, between mercy and truth.

"You're a blank page," he said. "And something very old has been waiting a hundred years for exactly that."

* * *

Three miles away and half a lifetime deeper into the night, King Aldous dreamed.

He dreamed the way drowning men breathe water: completely, and against every instinct. The Crown had taken most of his sleep years ago — it did not need rest, and it grudged him any — but lately it grew careless in the small hours, sated on its own scheming, and in those stolen minutes Aldous sank down through the layers of himself like a stone through pond-water, past the voices, past the cold golden pressure behind his eyes, down to the last unflooded room.

In that room, he dreamed of her face.

He had never seen her. That was what made it unbearable. The face came to him without context — no hall, no crowd, no crown — just a young woman with rain-dark hair and a smudge of ink along her jaw, looking at him with an expression he recognized because he wore it every day in his mirror: the look of someone asking *is anyone in there?*

"She's coming," the room whispered. Or perhaps he whispered it. In here the walls and the king had long since exchanged voices.

Aldous tried to hold the face. It was like holding water. Around him, in the flooded upper layers of his own mind, he could feel the Crown moving — vast, patient, pleased — arranging things. An early coronation. A boy heir with a soft skull. Wards failing across the realm like lamps going out one by one along a road. And beneath everything, humming through the stones of the palace like a second heartbeat, the gate.

*Soon,* said the Crown, in the voice it used for him alone, the tender one, the one that had talked him to sleep for thirty years. *Soon we won't need you at all. Soon there will be someone with no bloodline to fight us. A clean page, my love. We waited so patiently for a clean page.*

Aldous clawed his way up toward the surface, toward one hour — the physicians gave him one hour, sometimes, near dawn, when the Crown's attention wandered — one hour in which his mouth would be his own.

He had something to do with it.

He had an apology to write, and a rite to send, and not much time left to be Aldous at all.

In the archive above the chandlers' row, Yara heard the bells and did not yet know they were ringing for her.
