## 5. An Audience with the Worn King

They dressed her in silk the color of river fog.

Yara stood on a stool in a chamber that smelled of cedar and old incense while three palace dressmakers moved around her with pins in their lips and reverence in their eyes — or what she thought was reverence until she caught one of them looking at her the way clerks look at an account that won't balance. Not admiration. Apprehension.

"Hold still, miss," said the eldest, kneeling to hem the train. "It's not every day the palace sends for a guild clerk."

*Sent for.* As if it were an invitation. As if there had been a card.

The Guild's vote had been four hands to two. Bram had raised his for her freedom; Master Corvelin had raised his for the king; and when the tally was read aloud, Yara had understood that the word *tribute* had been chosen deliberately, by people who kept ledgers of their own and knew exactly what each entry cost. She was being paid out of the Guild's accounts like a debt. A clerk of no bloodline, sent south to Vessany's capital in a closed carriage with two silent guards and a letter under seal she was forbidden to read.

"You'll want the circlet," the eldest dressmaker said, lifting a thin band of silver from its velvet cradle. "By order."

"I'm not royalty," Yara said.

"No, miss." The woman set the circlet in Yara's hair with hands that trembled very slightly. "That's rather the point, isn't it."

Yara looked at herself in the tall glass afterward and did not recognize the girl who looked back. The silk made her seem taller. The circlet made her seem *claimed*. Beneath the borrowed finery she wore, hidden against her ribs where no dressmaker's pin would find it, the scrap of paper Bram had pressed into her palm at the last moment before the guards took her arm — *If he is lucid, say the word "ledger." If he is not, run.* — and beneath that, against her skin, the faint cold ache of the mark the gate had left on her, which had not faded in nine days and which throbbed now like a second pulse as the carriage rolled through the palace gates.

* * *

The Palace of Vessany had been built by men who wanted history to look at them and weep.

Yara walked corridors floored in marble quarried from three extinct mountains, past windows of glass so old and thick that the world outside swam green and slow as pond water. Servants bowed to her without meeting her eyes. Somewhere above, bells rang the hour, and the sound went through her teeth.

She counted things. It was what she did when fear came — she had done it since the foundling-house, counting stairs, counting spoons, counting the seconds between thunder and lightning, because numbers could be trusted to stay where you put them. So she counted: forty-one steps down the Processional Gallery. Twelve guards. Three doors. One throne room ahead, its doors twice her height and banded in bronze, carved — she stopped, breath catching — carved with a gate.

Not a decorative gate. *The* gate. The arch, the seven rivets, the seam down the middle. It covered both doors entire, rendered in bronze by some artist a thousand years dead, and whoever had carved it had gotten every detail right, including the small worn hollow at its base where a keeper's hand would rest.

"They say the doors were cast from drawings of the real thing," said the chamberlain, a dry stick of a man whose voice suggested he recited this fact eleven times a day. "His Majesty will receive you in the Hour Chamber. He receives few there. You should feel honored."

"Should I?" Yara asked.

The chamberlain's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You should feel *careful*, miss. Honored is optional."

* * *

The Hour Chamber was small — shockingly small after the gallery, a round room no wider than a miller's kitchen, lit by a single high window and a brazier gone grey with ash. There was no throne. There was a plain chair, a table bearing a decanter of water and one cup, and a man.

King Aldous sat with his hands flat on his knees, the way a penitent sits, or a man waiting for a verdict. He was younger than his coins. That was Yara's first thought, absurdly clerical: she had seen his profile on the realm's currency all her life, stern and golden, and the man before her was maybe fifty and looked carved out of driftwood, sanded down by weather no sea produced. His eyes were the worst part. They were clear. Terribly, desperately clear, the way a drowning man's are clear when he looks up through the last foot of water.

"Close the door," he said. His voice was rough, like cloth pulled over gravel. "And then come stand where I can see your face properly. I have dreamed it, you know. For six nights. I would like to check my work."

Yara did not close the door. "Your Majesty sent for me."

"The Crown sent for you." He said it fast, spitting the words out before something else could take his mouth. "I merely signed the letter. Sit. Stand. Whatever you like, but listen, because I get—" his hand made a wavering gesture, "—I get perhaps an hour. Some mornings less. It comes back to me the way tide comes into a rock pool, and then it goes out again, and when it goes out *he* uses the beach. Do you understand me, girl? Say yes or no."

"Yes," Yara said. Her heart was slamming against the hidden note in her bodice, against Bram's handwriting, against the word *run*.

"Yes, good." Aldous exhaled, long and shaking. "Then hear the whole of it. Three hundred years ago my ancestor Emeric put a crown upon his head at this city's founding, and the crown was not empty. Something lived in the gold — something older than the gold, something that had been waiting in the mountain the metal came out of, and it poured itself into him the moment the band touched his brow. It has poured itself into every king since. It wears us. It thinks with our thoughts and spends our years and it has eaten thirty generations of Vessany's kings from the inside, and do you know the joke of it?" He laughed, and the laugh was terrible because it was genuinely amused. "The joke is that it governs *well*. Roads. Granaries. Peace. It wants the kingdom healthy the way a farmer wants his oxen healthy. We have never been kings. We have been *livestock*."

The mark beneath Yara's ribs went cold enough to burn.

"It is tired of us," Aldous said. "We are worn out, all of us, inbred and half-hollowed, our blood too full of it to hold anything new. It wants a vessel with no bloodline at all. No inheritance of resistance. Blank parchment." His clear, drowning eyes fixed on hers. "It found you."

"How do you know what I am?"

"Because it talks about you." He leaned forward, and up close she could see the fine tremor that never left his hands, could smell the sourness of a body fighting its own occupant. "When it rides me, it hums. It hasn't hummed in a century. Lately it hums *your name*. Yara. Yara. Like a man humming over a key that finally fits a lock he's been picking for three hundred years."

The word was in her throat before she chose it — Bram's word, the test, the lifeline. "*Ledger,*" she said.

Something happened to the king's face. All the desperate clarity of it gathered, focused, and broke — not into madness but into relief, the relief of a man handed proof.

"A ledger," Aldous whispered. "Yes. Oh, you clever, clever keeper. Yes — that is exactly what you must become. Listen to me now, quickly, quickly, because the hour turns." He gripped the arms of the chair. "There is a rite. The first binding, the one that put it into Emeric's crown, can be reversed — unbound, unwritten, returned to the dark it came from. I have spent twenty years finding the pieces of it. Twenty years pretending to be an empty crown so it would grow careless. I have them all, the words, the order, the—"

His jaw snapped shut.

Yara watched it happen. Watched the man go out of his own face like a lamp blown out, watch the features rearrange themselves around something else, something that used the same bones but wore them differently — the way a house is the same house whether it is loved or occupied by soldiers. When King Aldous spoke again, the voice was the same voice and utterly unlike it: smooth, unhurried, warm as a banked fire.

"There he goes," the Crown said pleasantly, through the royal mouth. "My dear tired tenant. Always scribbling, always hiding little scraps of paper in the walls." The king's head turned, smooth as a weathervane, and those clear eyes — not clear anymore, *luminous* — settled on Yara with an attention that made her skin try to leave her body. "But you and I don't need to listen to him, do we. You and I have better things to discuss."

Yara's legs would not move. The door was twelve feet away and might as well have been across the sea.

"You're frightened," the Crown observed, and the king's ruined face arranged itself into gentleness. "Of course you are. No one has ever been gentle with you, so gentleness frightens you more than threats. I know you, Yara. Do you know how well I know you? I wrote the prophecy that caught you before your grandmother was born. I chose the century. I chose the date. I chose the guildhouse that would take in a nameless child, because nameless children raised among strangers learn to make themselves useful, quiet, indispensable — learn to keep other people's accounts because they have none of their own." The king rose from the chair, slowly, joints cracking, and even that seemed graceful in the wrong body. "You think you survived your life. You didn't. You were *cultivated*. And everything cultivated belongs to the gardener."

"That's not belonging," Yara heard herself say. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "That's ownership."

"Is it?" The Crown smiled with the king's mouth, and it was a kind smile, and it was the most horrible thing Yara had ever seen. "Then tell me what you call it when the foundling-house feeds you and forgets you in the same hour. What you call it when the Guild votes four to two and puts you in a carriage like coin in a purse. What you call it when the only man who ever fought for you does it from behind a locked door." It took a step toward her. The brazier ash stirred though there was no wind. "I am offering you a family, Yara. The oldest family in the world. Every king of Vessany for three hundred years — my sons, my sons, my sons — and you would be first among them. Not livestock. *Heir*. A mind with nothing written on it, given everything: the realm, the centuries, the deep gold thoughts under the mountain. You have spent your whole life balancing other people's books. I am offering you the only account that will never close."

The mark under her ribs flared white-hot, and — this was the shameful part, the part she would carry like a stone — some starved creature far down inside her *leaned toward it*. Warmth. Purpose. A name spoken by something vast. She felt the pull in her chest like a hook, sweet as the memory of anyone, *anyone*, saying *you're ours*.

"You dreamed of me," Yara said. "The king said. Six nights."

"He dreams what I show him," the Crown said. "Though lately I confess I dream of you myself. You are going to be magnificent."

"Then you've made one mistake."

"Oh?"

"The ledger." Her hand found the scrap of paper against her ribs; her other hand found, folded inside her sleeve where Sef's frantic midnight message had told her to keep it, the torn corner of the prophecy page — the date inked a hundred years ago in handwriting identical to hers. She held it up between them. "Ledgers record what *was*. You wrote my future in ink a century before I was born. But I'm a clerk, Your Majesty. And the first rule of the count-house is—" her voice shook and she let it, "—no entry stands that wasn't witnessed by two hands. You wrote alone. Which means every line of me is *provisional*."

For one heartbeat the luminous eyes flickered, and behind them, far down, something drowned and desperate surfaced — the king, watching, *memorizing* — and then the Crown's smile widened, and the doors of the Hour Chamber opened on their own, and the chamberlain's voice said the audience was concluded, and the last thing the Crown said, softly, through the mouth of the dying king of Vessany, was:

*"Provisional entries, little clerk, still get posted to the book. Ask your friend Sef what he found in my handwriting — if he lives long enough to tell you."*

* * *

The carriage back through the darkening city smelled of the dressmakers' cedar, and Yara sat rigid in her river-fog silk with the prophecy fragment cutting her fingers where she clenched it, running the Crown's parting words over and over like a coin she could not stop biting.

*Ask your friend Sef.*

Nine days ago Sef had been arrested by the Guild's own wardens for tampering with sealed archives. She had assumed it was finished. Assumed he was in a cell, or worse.

But the Crown knew he had *found* something. Knew it and feared it enough to warn her away — and power that warned instead of silenced was power that was afraid.

Yara pressed her forehead to the cold glass and watched the lamps of the capital slide past, each one a small gold eye, and thought: *the Crown writes in ink. I will write in pencil. Everything provisional. Everything revisable.*

The carriage turned off the processional road — not toward the Guildhouse, not toward any cell she knew — and the guards facing her did not meet her eyes, and somewhere behind them, in the dark beyond the lamps, hoofbeats fell in alongside the carriage at a gallop, closing fast.
