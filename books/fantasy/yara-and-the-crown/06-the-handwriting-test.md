## 6. The Handwriting Test

Sef had spent forty years among the Guild's archives and had learned that paper remembers what people would rather forget. Ink held grudges. Vellum kept score. The archive beneath the counting-house breathed dust and tallow and the faint iron smell of old corrections — places where a clerk had scraped away a mistake and written over the wound. He loved it here the way other men loved their mothers: unreasonably, and with a certain dread of being found out.

He had not slept since Yara was taken into custody. He told himself it was the work.

The prophecy ledger lay open before him under a lens of ground glass, its pages the color of weak tea. *The Vessany Codex*, catalogued a hundred years ago as an anonymous prophetic text, accessioned with a shrug by some long-dead archivist whose initials — T.M. — were stamped in fading violet on the flyleaf. Sef had read it a dozen times since the girl's arrest. Tonight he read it differently. Tonight he read it like a ledger.

Because that was what Bram had said, standing disgraced in the doorway of the archive at the hour when even the candles seemed ashamed to burn. Not *she is doomed*. Not *the gate stirred*. He had said, with the flat voice of a man reporting a discrepancy he could no longer deny:

"Sef. It's written like accounts. Look at it like accounts."

So Sef looked at it like accounts.

*When the moon stands twice-borrowed on the ninth of Deepfrost,* the codex promised, *a child unwritten shall come down to the gate, and what waits below shall rise into her as water rises into an empty cup.*

He turned to his working table, where he had assembled everything the Guild possessed touching Yara Hartwell. It was a thin stack. That was the first thing. He had pulled her file expecting the usual sediment — birth notice, apprenticeship bond, letters of reference, the small bureaucratic moss that accumulates on every life. Instead: one intake slip, undated in the column where the intake officer should have recorded where she came from, signed by a keeper whose name Sef had to look up twice because it appeared nowhere else in forty years of records. One guild-book entry. One set of ledgers, surrendered as evidence.

Her ledgers. He smoothed the first of them flat and set it beside the codex, and he began, clerk against prophet, to compare hands.

At first there was nothing. Of course there was nothing; a century separated the two scripts, and scripts drifted like riverbanks. The codex wrote its *th* with a crossed ascender; Yara did not cross hers. The codex leaned rightward, urgent, while Yara's hand stood upright as a fencepost, each figure placed in its column with the modest perfection of a woman who had never once believed anyone was reading over her shoulder.

It was the figures that betrayed them.

Every clerk develops habits in numerals — small corruptions, private sins. Sef himself hooked his sevens like a man afraid they'd run away. He had catalogued these habits for decades; it was how he caught forged entries, because forgers could copy letters but always forgot that numbers have fingerprints too.

Yara wrote her fours with an open top. A rare habit. Rarer than she knew.

Sef went back through the codex slowly, his lens low, his breath held so as not to fog the glass. On the eleventh leaf, in the middle of a passage about the rising of what waits below, the prophet had set down a number — *three hundred years and one* — and Sef put the lens down very carefully, because his hand had begun to shake.

Open-topped fours. Three of them, buried in the numerals like needles in wool.

He told himself it was coincidence. He was an archivist; coincidence was his daily bread. But then he found the nines. Yara wrote her nines with a tail that curled back beneath the body, a habit she must have learned from no one, because Sef had searched the guild-book and found no clerk who taught her, no master whose hand matched, no lineage at all — and there in the codex, on the thirty-first leaf, in the phrase *nine gates below the water*, the same backward-curled tail, unmistakable, idiosyncratic, *hers*.

A hand does not repeat itself across a hundred years unless it is the same hand.

Sef sat back. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were waiting.

He worked until the third bell past midnight, and what he found made him wish devoutly for ignorance. The codex was not merely in Yara's hand. It was in her hand *at different ages*. The early leaves were looser, younger — a hand still finding itself, the way a child's does. The later pages tightened, matured, acquired her exact and current discipline, the discipline of a grown clerk balancing impossible accounts by candlelight. The prophecy had been written forward, across decades, in a script that grew up alongside her — or rather, that she would grow up *into*. Whoever had written it had known her hand before she existed. Had practiced it. Had rehearsed her the way an actor rehearses a role, leaving the final page blank for the night she finally sat down at the desk of herself and took up the pen.

And the last page — the page naming tonight's date, inked in her own handwriting before she was born — that page was fresh. The ink sat proud of the surface. It had not been written a hundred years ago at all. It had been written recently. Perhaps within the year. Perhaps within the month.

The codex was not a prophecy. It was a trap laid in advance and baited with her own future hand, and someone — something — had been keeping her fed, housed, and blank for exactly as long as it took to write her.

Sef blew out the candle. In the dark, the archive smelled of iron and old corrections, and he thought: we did not find her. We were given her. The Guild raised a vessel and called it charity.

* * *

They convened at dawn, which was itself an omen; the Guild of Impossible Things preferred to do its impossible business after dark, when the accounts could not see them. Sef stood in the corner of the tally-hall with his findings sewn inside his coat and watched the vote go exactly the way he knew it would.

Bram Almeida stood accused — not formally, the Guild was too cowardly for formality, but in the sideways manner of institutions, with murmurs and withdrawn privileges and the removal of his keys. He had opened the vault door's outer ward to admit the king's men. He had let the girl be seen. And when the Guild voted, nineteen to four, to deliver Yara Hartwell to the palace as tribute — to give the Crown-worn king precisely the vessel his parasite had spent a century grooming, on the theory that a trap sprung deliberately might be survived more easily than one sprung by accident — Bram spoke the only honest sentence spoken in that room all morning.

"We are not springing the trap," he said. "We are the trap. We built her cell and called it a home."

Nineteen voices called the question. Four did not. Sef's silence counted among the four, though his tongue never moved; he simply could not make it agree, and shame has many dialects.

He watched them take Bram's remaining keys. He watched the old keeper walk out of the tally-hall with his shoulders square and his eyes fixed on nothing, a man carrying the only clean thing left in the building, which was his disgrace.

Sef went back down into the archive and locked the door behind him and took out the codex, and read the last page again, and understood at last what he had to do. Because there was one more thing in the margins — a single marginal note in violet ink, the same violet as the accession stamp, the dead archivist T.M.'s color. T.M., who had catalogued this book a hundred years ago. Who had stamped it and shelved it and apparently noticed nothing.

Except that the note read: *Hand matches no living clerk. Keep separate from guild records. B. will understand.*

*B.* Bram. Or Bram's grandmother, or some earlier keeper of the gate — but the Almeidas had kept the gate for generations, hadn't they, and Bram had once mentioned, in the careless way he mentioned everything that mattered, that his family had been "paper-people" before they were door-people.

The prophecy had been flagged a century ago by someone who saw the same thing Sef now saw, and the Guild had shelved the warning and forgotten it. Institutions forget on purpose. Forgetting is cheaper than grief.

Sef unlocked the door again. There was a girl in custody on the third floor who deserved to know what she was before the palace taught her.

* * *

He never learned how Bram got in. Later he suspected the old keeper had never truly surrendered all his keys — that some locks answer to blood and stubbornness rather than iron — but at the time it hardly mattered. What mattered was the sound, just past the fourth bell, of Bram Almeida's voice outside the custody room saying, in the tone of a man requesting a routine audit, "Guild business, brother, open the door," and the door opening, and the two guards on the other side folding up like bad arithmetic.

Sef had brought the documents. He had spent the intervening hours copying the crucial leaves — the fours, the nines, the fresh ink of the final page — because he would not hand over the codex itself; some books should stay in the dark where they were raised.

Yara stood in the ruined doorway with her wrists still ringed in ward-cord, pale as an unpaid debt, and looked from Bram to Sef and back.

"You're breaking me out," she said. "Why? You voted. I heard the count through the floor."

"Because we were wrong," Sef said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a spine, "in the worst way it is possible to be wrong." He pushed the copied leaves into her bound hands. "Read your own handwriting. All of it. The prophecy isn't about you, child. It's *by* you. Written across a hundred years, in your hand, growing older as you grow older. Something below that gate didn't find you. It ordered you. The way you'd order paper and ink." He swallowed. "Your blankness isn't an accident of orphanhood. It's specifications."

The lamplight in the corridor guttered. Yara looked down at the leaves, and Sef watched her do what she always did — what had made her the finest clerk the Guild never celebrated — trace each figure with her fingertip, checking, verifying, refusing to believe an account until she had balanced it herself.

He watched her arrive at the total.

"That's my four," she whispered. "That's my nine. That's—" Her voice failed and reassembled itself somewhere colder. "I didn't write this."

"No," Bram said quietly. "You haven't yet. That's the horror of it, girl. Somewhere ahead of you, you do."

Yara closed her hands around the pages until the copies crumpled, and when she lifted her face she was not crying, which frightened Sef more than tears would have. She looked like a ledger that had discovered it had been kept by someone else.

"Then I'll write something else," she said. "Where does it end? Where does the hand stop?"

"The river," Bram said. "Where the first king was bound. It's the only geography in the codex that isn't about you." He glanced at Sef, and something passed between the two old men — an apology, an absolution, a debt acknowledged. "We leave now, before the Guild counts its guards."

Sef pressed one last thing into Yara's crumpled fist: the violet-inked marginal note, copied fair. "T.M. saw it first, a hundred years ago. He trusted a keeper named B. to understand. Whatever your hand was written to do — someone has been fighting it since before you were born. You are not alone in the margin."

Down the stair, past the sleeping counting-house, toward the door that led out of everything ordinary — Yara paused on the threshold, the ward-cord still on her wrists, and asked the question Sef would carry for the rest of his life:

"If the Crown wrote me to be its cup," she said, "who taught me to check the sums?"

No one answered. She stepped into the dark, and behind them, far below the counting-house, something beneath the gate began — softly, patiently, in a handwriting Sef recognized — to practice hers.
