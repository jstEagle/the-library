## 7. Where the River Forgets Its Name

They traveled north for nine days, and on the ninth the road ran out of names.

Yara felt it happen beneath her, in the soles of her feet, the way she felt errors in a ledger — a small wrongness where a figure should have stood. The milestones had stopped three villages back. The map Sef had pressed into her hands, folded soft as cloth from a century of handling, showed a river here, but no bridge, no ford, no town, only a blank space the cartographer had filled with a single word in faded ink: *here the water forgets*.

"Prophecy geography," Bram said, when she showed him. He said it the way he said *audit* and *reconciliation*, as if dread were a line item to be entered and carried forward. "The Crown wrote the places into the verse so we couldn't mistake them. It wants us to arrive. That's the part I keep tripping over, girl. We're walking into a trap and I'm the one holding the map."

"Then why are you walking?"

He was quiet for a while. The pines came down close to the road, and the light through them went the color of old brass. "Because every keeper before me died behind a sealed door," he said at last, "and I have begun to suspect that sealing a thing is not the same as keeping it. Sealing is just binding with the door shut. Your Sef thinks the first king was unbound at this river — truly unbound, the knot cut rather than hidden. If the craft can do that, it can do the reverse. It can be *learned*." He glanced at her sidelong. "And you are the only unbound variable in three hundred years of Vessany's arithmetic. If anyone can learn it, it's the woman the prophecy couldn't predict."

She wanted to say something brave. What came out was smaller and truer: "I've never been good at anything except balancing accounts that shouldn't balance."

"That," said Bram, "is the entire job."

* * *

The river found them before they found it.

They smelled it first — cold stone and wet iron, the smell of the counting-house cellar, of the gate, and Yara's skin prickled with the wrongness of it, that the water this far from the guildhouse should carry the same scent as the thing beneath it. Then the trees opened, and there it lay: a river wide as a street and moving strangely, without sound. Not silent — soundless. The water poured over its stones and made no noise at all, as though the river had forgotten that noise was part of its nature, and the silence pressed on Yara's ears like deep water.

"Here the water forgets its name," she recited, and her breath came out white though the day was mild.

The banks were littered with stones, and the stones were carved. She knelt at the first one and brushed away the moss with her sleeve. Letters, worn to shallow ghosts — not Vessani script but something older, angular, the letters cut deep and then deliberately filled with lead, as if someone had wanted the words both said and silenced. She cleared a second stone, and a third. A ring of them, thirty or forty, standing in the shallows like a congregation.

"It's the binding," Bram breathed. He had gone gray around the mouth. "The first binding. This is where they — Aldous said the first king. He didn't say they'd written it *down*. Standing stones. A ledger in stone."

"Every binding is a ledger," Yara said, and heard the words come out of her as if someone else had balanced them first. "That's what you taught me. A binding is just a record of what was taken and what was given and who witnessed it. You said the gate has a ledger. You said the Crown keeps accounts." She looked up at him, and the river's silence seemed to pour through her as she spoke. "This is the Crown's first entry. This is where it learned bookkeeping."

Bram sat down heavily on a stone that was not, she noticed, carved. For a long moment he only looked at the water moving without sound, and she watched an old man's certainty settle into the shape of something humbler and more durable.

"Then teach me," she said.

"I don't know it."

"You know the *shape* of it. You've kept the gate for thirty years. You know what a binding feels like from the outside, the way a clerk who's never signed a contract still knows what one is." She stood, and her knees were shaking, and she made them hold. "And I know ledgers, Bram. Better than anyone in the Guild. If unbinding is just reading the entry and reversing it — debits and credits, what was taken and what was given — then I can learn. I have to learn. In three days the Crown crowns its puppet, and after that there won't be a realm left to save."

He looked at her a long time. Then he took off his coat, folded it with the precise economy of a man who had folded that coat ten thousand times, laid it on the uncarved stone, and said, "Then we begin with the first lesson every keeper learns, and I never thought I'd teach it standing above water instead of below it." He held out his hand. "A binding is a promise. Before you touch anyone's knot — before you so much as look at it — you must be able to keep your own. Give me your hand."

* * *

The keeper's craft, it turned out, was bookkeeping all the way down.

Bram could not teach her the deep work — no living keeper could, he said; the last one who'd known it had died with the gate's first seal — but he could teach her the grammar of it, the way a child learns letters before poems. That evening, in the lee of the standing stones, with a driftwood fire that burned strangely bright against the river's silence, he made her recite the keeper's catechism until her voice went hoarse.

*What is a binding?* A record.

*What is a record?* A promise made visible.

*What is an unbinding?* Not destruction. Forgiveness of the debt — with full knowledge of what the debt was.

"That last one," Bram said, poking the fire, "is the one that kills keepers. Anyone can break a binding, girl. Smash the stone, burn the ledger, tear the seal. The Crown would love you forever if that's all it took. But an unbinding — a true one — means you read the whole entry first. Every cruelty, every theft, every year of it. You hold the whole account in your mind and you say: *I see what this is, all of it, and I release it anyway.*" He looked up. "Most people can't do that. Most people either can't look, or they look and then they can't forgive. The Crown counts on it. That's why it's lasted three hundred years. Nobody's ever been able to read it all the way through and still want to let it go."

"Then I'll practice," Yara said. "On small things."

"On small things," he agreed. And then, because he was Bram, he produced from his pack a battered pocket-ledger, one of his own, thirty years of daily entries in his cramped hand, and put it in her lap. "Start with mine. If you can read a man's thirty years and forgive them, you may be ready to read a monster's."

She read all night.

It was not what she expected. She had imagined his ledgers would be like him at the counting-house — dry, precise, impenetrable. Instead they were a man laid open. The first entry, dated the day he took his oath: *Sealed the gate. Hands shook for an hour after. The thing below said my mother's name.* Then decades of the same small courage, entered in figures: ward-strengths, tremors in the stone, the gate's hungers logged like weather. And between the columns, in the margins, in ink gone brown, the things he never said aloud: *Heard it weeping tonight. Hated that it sounded like a child.* *Keeper Vess died today. I inherited her chair and her nightmares.* *If I die before I train a successor, the gate opens and it will be my fault. Pray the Guild sends someone. Pray they send no one at all.*

Yara read until the fire burned to embers, and when she looked up Bram was watching her across the coals with an expression she had never seen on him — undefended, an old man waiting to find out what his own account said.

"I see what this is," she said quietly, "all of it. And I release it anyway."

The words went through the air like a stone through still water. She felt something shift — not in the river, not in the stones, but in herself, some hinge turning that had been rusted shut since the night the gate had whispered her name. For the first time since she'd found her own handwriting in a century-old prophecy, she was not following a script someone else had written. This — sitting by a dead fire, forgiving an old man his fears — this was *hers*, chosen, unwritten anywhere until she wrote it now.

Bram let out a breath that seemed to have been held for thirty years. "Well," he said, gruffly, blinking at the embers. "First entry balanced."

She laughed — actually laughed, a sound the silent river seemed to lean in to hear — and something loosened between them that had been formal since the guildhouse. He showed her how to hold her hands over the water to feel the old binding's weight; she showed him a margin-note convention from the Guild's double-entry system that made his eyes go wide and hungry. They argued about notation like children. They slept in shifts, and in the morning the river was still soundless, and the stones still stood, and Yara stood among them practicing reading the first king's binding — just the edges of it, just the outermost figures, the way you read the spine of a book before opening it — and for whole minutes at a time she forgot to be afraid.

It was Bram who found the message.

She heard him call her name — her actual name, the one he'd only ever said twice, carefully, as if handling something breakable — and came down the bank at a run. He was standing at the water's edge holding a dead gull, or what had been a gull; its feathers were stiff with frost though the morning was warm, and bound to its leg with wire was a scrap of paper no larger than a playing card.

The handwriting on it was shaking. It had been written by a man fighting for every letter, and it said:

*They crown the boy tomorrow at dawn, not at noon as planned. I have perhaps one lucid hour left in me and I am spending it on you. The unbinding rite is not in the stones. The Crown removed it from the first binding — that is why the river is silent; the river's name was the last word of the rite. I remember it. I am the only one alive who remembers it. Come to the coronation, Yara. I will give you the words with my dying breath, and my apology, which is longer. Trust the keeper. Trust yourself. — A.*

Yara read it twice. The river said nothing, but all around her the standing stones seemed, for one heartbeat, to lean inward — listening, the way the gate had listened.

"Tomorrow at dawn," Bram said. His voice was very steady, the way it went steady before disasters. "That's a day's hard ride, and the roads to the capital will be full of coronation traffic." He looked at her, and she looked at him, and she saw that neither of them was afraid anymore — or rather, that both of them were, and had decided, jointly, in the manner of a balanced ledger, what to do about it.

"Then we'd better ride," Yara said, "like the impossible thing we are."
