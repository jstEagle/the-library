## 8. The Crown's Coronation

The bells woke him before the Crown did.

That was new. For thirty years — since he was a boy of nine with a father already hollowed out beside him on a bier — Aldous of Vessany had woken to the Crown's voice first, before birdsong, before his own heartbeat, before the small human fact of his own name. The Crown was always there in the dark behind his eyes like a lamp lit too close, saying *rise*, saying *reign*, saying *remember what you are for*.

But this morning the bells came first. The great bronze throats of the Cathedral of the Founding, ringing out of season, ringing at four in the morning, and beneath them the Crown's voice was thin as a coin worn smooth.

*Aldous.*

"I'm here," he said aloud, to the empty bedchamber, because it mattered to him — it had always mattered to him — that no one mistake who was speaking.

*The coronation is moved forward. Dawn. You will attend. You will stand. You will not embarrass us.*

"Us," Aldous said, and almost laughed. He lay still under the furs and listened to his own body the way an old clerk listens to a failing roof: cataloguing the leaks. His heart stumbled every fourth beat now. His hands, when he raised them into the candlelight, were translucent as parchment held to a window, blue veins branching through them like ink gone wrong. Three hundred years the Crown had worn Vessany's kings, and it wore them out like gloves. His grandfather had lasted forty years. His father twenty-six. He had given it thirty-one, and he could feel it now, the way one feels a ring grown too tight — the Crown pressing against the inside of his skull, testing the walls, finding them thin.

It had found something better. He'd known since the river country went quiet on the Crown's tongue. Since the dreams.

Since *her*.

He sat up. The chamber was cold; the palace wards had been failing for weeks, the old protections fraying like hempen rope, and the hearth-fire burned sullen and low as if it too were tired of being commanded. In the mirror across the room a man sat up in the furs who looked like Aldous's own funeral portrait. Behind that man's eyes, faintly, a light moved. Not his.

"Dawn," Aldous said. "You're rushing."

*The vessel approaches,* said the Crown, and for one unguarded moment its voice lost its imperial smoothness and sounded — this was the only word Aldous had ever found for it, in all his long captivity — *hungry*. *We will be ready.*

"She's not a vessel." His voice cracked on it, dry as autumn leaf. "She's a girl. She's a ledger-clerk. I've seen her in my dreams for a month and she's just — she's a person who bites her thumbnail when she's counting."

Silence, then, from the thing behind his eyes. A silence with weight to it, like deep water. And then, gently, in the tone it used when it wanted to remind him how little his opinions weighed:

*You will attend the coronation. You will name Prince Corvel heir before the assembled court. You will speak clearly. Your body is failing us faster than expected, and we do not intend to explain your collapse mid-ceremony. Stand straight, Aldous. You have been a good coat. We would not have you die crooked.*

Aldous sat alone after the voice withdrew to whatever cold place it kept itself, and he looked at his hands, and he thought: *a day*. Perhaps two. He had perhaps a day left in which he was Aldous, king of nothing much, husband to a dead queen, father to no one, keeper of one secret large enough to damn him.

And somewhere between here and the capital, riding hard along roads full of coronation traffic, was a girl with no family and no past, carrying — if the river-country reports were true, if Bram Almeida's smuggled letters were true — a way out.

Not for the realm. He was past pretending the realm was his to save; the realm had been governed by the Crown's patience for three centuries, and kings were only the face it showed itself in the mirror. No. What Aldous wanted, with the small fierce greed of a dying man, was simpler than salvation.

He wanted to die as himself. Even for a minute. Even alone, in the dark, with no lamp behind his eyes.

There was a way. There had always been a way; the keepers of the Guild of Impossible Things had died keeping fragments of it, and Bram — poor steady Bram, whom the Crown had ordered him to betray twice and whom he had betrayed once, and never forgiven himself — had the rest. But the rite needed words. Exact words. Words that could not be spoken aloud in any room the Crown might listen through, and the Crown listened through everything: the ward-stones, the mirrors, the mouths of sleeping servants. Aldous had learned to write letters that meant other things. He had learned it the way prisoners learn the grammar of their walls.

He had one letter left to write.

* * *

They dressed him at dawn by candlelight, though dawn light was coming grey through the shutters, because the Crown disliked the sun on ceremony days — sunlight made the regalia look cheap, it said, and the regalia must never look cheap. The Crown had opinions about cloth. It had ruled an empire for three hundred years and its passions had narrowed, over the centuries, to velvet weights and the precise angle of a scepter and the correct depth of a bow. Kings were interchangeable. Ceremony was eternal.

His chamberlain, Master Ollen, knelt to fasten the king's boots, and Aldous saw the boy flinch at the smell of him. Rot, faintly. The body going ahead of schedule.

"How long has it been since you slept, Majesty?" Ollen asked, not looking up.

"I sleep," Aldous said. "I dream of a girl I've never met, and then I wake up wearing myself. It's very restful."

Ollen's hands stilled. "Majesty—"

"It's a joke, Master Ollen. Laugh or don't. The morning is short." He flexed his fingers inside the ceremonial gloves, feeling how little answered him. His right hand had begun to lag, half a heartbeat behind his intention, like a servant who had heard rumors the household was ending. "Tell me about the roads. The northern roads."

"The roads, Majesty?" Ollen rose, arranging the ermine. "Full of pilgrims. Everyone wants to see young Corvel crowned — the people are saying it's a blessing, a fresh king after so long—"

"After three hundred years of the same king wearing different faces, you mean."

"Majesty." Ollen's voice dropped. There was a strange quality in it, and Aldous looked up sharply and saw the man was afraid — not of him, but *for* him, which was rarer and more painful. "Forgive me. But you look like a man reading his own accounts."

"I am a man reading my own accounts," Aldous said. "I find several entries I don't recall making, and a debt come due." He gripped the chamberlain's wrist — his left hand, which still obeyed — and leaned close, and spoke quickly, quietly, in the space of a single breath, because the Crown grew bored with dressing and boredom made it wander. "The Guild's counting-house. If anyone comes asking, from the river country, with ledgers — let them in. Whatever they carry. Let them in and ask nothing."

Ollen stared. "Majesty, the Crown has ordered the Guild's clerks seized on sight—"

"The Crown," Aldous said, "is not listening to this particular sentence, because it thinks I am only complaining, and I have complained for thirty years, and complaint is the sound a coat makes when it wears thin." He released him. "Let them in, Ollen. It's the last order I'll ever give you, and it's the only one I've ever given as myself."

The chamberlain's face did something complicated. Then he bowed — lower, Aldous noticed, than protocol required — and said, "Yes, Majesty," and Aldous chose to believe, in the way of dying men choosing beliefs, that the words had been understood all the way down.

* * *

The throne hall smelled of lilies and cold stone.

They had filled it by force of habit rather than love: the great houses in their colors, the Guild's delegation gray and watchful at the back (Bram Almeida absent — arrested, fled, or dead; Aldous couldn't tell which possibility hurt most), the foreign envoys with their diplomatic smiles, and everywhere the ward-stones humming in their settings like struck glasses that would not stop ringing. The realm's protections had been failing for a month. Aldous could feel each one go the way a man feels teeth loosen — the western march wards gone silent three nights ago, the cathedral stones singing flat this morning, and beneath the palace, far beneath, past foundations and forgotten stairs, the gate.

He felt it strain the way the Crown felt it: as pressure, as appetite frustrated. Something down there was pushing against a door that had held for three hundred years, and the door was held by keepers, and the keepers kept dying, and today the Crown had decided it could wait no longer for its perfect vessel and would simply take the realm through the front entrance while it groomed the girl for the inner rooms.

Prince Corvel stood beside the throne, fifteen years old, wearing his coronation whites, looking — Aldous observed with distant grief — exactly as Aldous himself had looked at nine: well-fed, well-schooled, and utterly unaware that the fitting had already begun. The Crown had been leaking into the boy for a year. Small things. A new formality in his letters. A fondness for the royal *we*. A way of looking at servants as furniture.

"Uncle." Corvel's voice, bright with nerves. "Am I — do I bow to you first, or—"

"To the altar first," Aldous said. "To me second. To the Crown—" he stopped. Swallowed. "To the Crown last. It always comes last, Corvel. Remember that, whatever else you forget."

The boy blinked at him. Somewhere far back behind those adolescent eyes, something adjusted its grip.

The horns sounded. The doors opened. And Aldous, rising on legs that shook, walking the long nave on the Crown's borrowed strength — it fed him strength for ceremonies the way one feeds a horse before slaughter — took his place beneath the baldachin and performed, for the last time, the oldest lie in Vessany: the king, whole and sovereign, bestowing a kingdom.

He watched the crown descend toward Corvel's head and felt the Crown inside him lean forward like a man watching his reflection walk into a new house.

And in that moment — the moment of transfer, when three centuries of ritual pulled taut like a rope between two bodies — Aldous felt the Crown *leave him a little*. Not entirely. Never entirely; it kept a hand in every pocket. But the greater weight shifted toward the boy, drawn by the ceremony's ancient channels, and for the first time in thirty-one years Aldous's skull was his own, mostly, briefly — a dim, quiet room instead of a lamplit cell.

One hour. Perhaps less. He could feel the borders of the lucidity already narrowing, the Crown's attention split between host and heir, and he walked — slowly, an old king taking air — back through the antechambers to his study, and barred the door, and sat down at the desk where he had signed away a thousand laws he had never read.

Paper. Ink. His left hand, which still obeyed.

He wrote fast, and he wrote in the cipher Bram had taught him thirty years ago over wine and treason — the cipher of the Guild of Impossible Things, ledger-marks and balance-lines, numbers that meant words and words that meant the end of the world. The unbinding rite, as the last free keeper of the river stones had whispered it to his grandfather's grandfather and passed it down the royal line like a hereditary disease, waiting for a king strong enough to use it and weak enough to want to. Nine phrases. Each one a hinge. Each one requiring a speaker who was *present* — truly present, self-possessed, unworn — which was why no Crown-worn king had ever managed it, and why the Crown had never feared it, and why it had spent a century writing a prophecy to manufacture someone who was.

Someone blank. Someone unwritten. Someone who could hold the words without holding anything else.

*I'm sorry,* he wrote at the end, and his hand was shaking now, and the pressure behind his eyes was building again, the Crown noticing the length of his absence, turning its attention homeward like a searchlight. *I'm sorry for my grandfather's sins and my father's silence and my thirty-one years of standing straight while you were worn. I'm sorry I knew about the gate and said nothing until saying nothing became the shape of my life. If you are reading this, Yara-of-no-family, then you were designed to be a door, and I am giving you the one key that lets you refuse to open.*

He sealed it with wax and pressed into the soft red circle not the royal seal — the Crown watched the royal seal — but the signet of his mother, dead forty years, a plain ring stamped with a sparrow: the mark of the one person who had loved Aldous before the fitting began. Bram would know it. Bram had seen it once, long ago, on the night they had planned a betrayal neither of them had survived intact.

He rang for Master Ollen.

"One letter," Aldous said, when the chamberlain came. "South, tonight, by the fastest rider. To the counting-house of the Guild of Impossible Things, or to whoever carries their books on the road. Give it to no one else. Lose it and I will haunt you competently."

Ollen took it with both hands, the way one takes a live coal.

"And, Ollen." Aldous looked out the window at the city below — the real city, smoke and laundry and children shouting in the gutters, none of whom had ever once been ruled by anyone but the thing behind his eyes. His vision was doubling. His hour was closing. "When the bells next ring out of season — don't run. Stand in the street and watch the sky. I think you'll finally get to see what we've all been working on."

Behind his eyes, the lamp flared white.

*Enough,* said the Crown, returning like winter. *Return to the hall. Smile for the realm, Aldous. Smile while you still have a mouth I can move.*

Aldous smiled. It was, by every account, a kingly smile.

And in the antechamber beyond, Prince Corvel — crowned an hour early, pale under the gold, already standing a little too straight — turned to greet his uncle with eyes that were not entirely a boy's, and said in a voice with three centuries underneath it:

"We have been expecting the courier, Uncle. Shall we discuss what happens to messengers?"

Far to the south, on a road black with coronation traffic, a rider with a sparrow-sealed letter spurred his horse through the dark, and the gate beneath the counting-house began, softly, to sing.
