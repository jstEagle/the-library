## 9. The Unbinding of Aldous

The coronation hall of Vessany had been built to make a person feel small, and it did its work well.

Yara stood at the base of the dais steps with the rite folded against her ribs, pressed there by the sash of a borrowed dress the Guild had dressed her in — white, because they wanted her to look like an offering, and she supposed that was what she was. The hall rose around her in pillars of green-veined marble, each one carved with the face of a dead king, thirty generations of them staring down with the same mild, patient expression, as though patience were something you could chisel into stone and have it be true. Banners hung from the vaulted dark like drowned things. Ten thousand candles burned, and none of their light reached the ceiling.

The court filled the benches on either side: lords in winter furs, ladies with powdered throats, soldiers whose spears knocked softly together when they shifted. They had all been told she was a gift from the provinces. A clerk sent to present tribute accounts to the new reign. Nobody looked at her twice, except the ones who did — the ones whose eyes slid over her face and then jerked away as though they'd touched a stove.

*She's here,* those eyes said. *It said she would come.*

Aldous stood at the top of the steps beneath the empty canopy, waiting to crown his nephew. He was a tall man made stooped by something heavier than years. His robes fit him the way a coat fits a scarecrow. And his hands — Yara watched them where they gripped the arms of the throne, knuckles pale as candlewax — his hands were shaking.

*Hold on,* she thought at him, uselessly, across forty feet of marble and three hundred years of occupation. *Hold on until I get there.*

Bram was somewhere behind her, among the servants and under-clerks crowding the western doors, carrying the gate-key wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart. She could not see him. She could feel him, or she let herself believe she could — a fixed point at her back, the way a lighthouse is a point for ships that hope never to need it.

The herald cried the beginning of the rites. Trumpets. The sound went up into the dark and did not come back down.

Yara began to walk up the stairs.

No one stopped her. That was the horror of it, the thing she would remember afterward in the long quiet of the gate-room: no one stopped her. The Crown had written this hall's choreography a century before any of these people were born. The prophecy ledger had named tonight, had named the vessel, had named the hour between the old king's failing and the new king's crowning when the wards of Vessany ran thinnest — and everything now proceeding was simply the machinery of that page turning itself. The guards saw a clerk delivering tribute rolls. The lords saw a servant. The Crown, wherever it sat inside Aldous's failing skull, saw exactly what it had ordered.

She reached the dais. Up close, the king smelled of myrrh and something underneath it, older — cold stone, river water, the smell of the vault beneath the counting-house. His eyes found hers.

For one moment, they were only a dying man's eyes. Wet. Frightened. Enormously tired.

*"You came,"* he breathed. It was barely sound. *"Gods forgive me, girl, whatever it cost you to come."*

"Your message was very clear," Yara whispered back. "You wrote the unbinding in the margin of a psalter. Sef nearly wept at the penmanship."

Something moved behind the king's mouth. Not a smile. A struggle — the way a drowning man's face looks when he tries to surface one last time, and the water closes. When Aldous spoke again his voice had changed, still his voice but worn smooth on one side, like a coin rubbed too long in a pocket.

*"There she is."* The court hushed; the voice carried strangely, too clearly, as if the hall itself had been built to amplify it. *"My unwritten daughter. Do you know how long I waited? Do you know what it is, little clerk, to keep accounts for three hundred years?"*

"I keep accounts," Yara said. Her own voice sounded far away, thin, but steady. "That's rather the point."

The Crown laughed through the king's mouth, and the lords laughed too, uncertainly, because their king had laughed, and laughter in that hall had always been a command.

*"Come closer. Let me see what a hundred years of careful writing made."*

"You wrote the prophecy," Yara said. "Sef proved it. The ink, the hand, the paper stock — all of it yours, fed through a hundred willing scribes who thought they were transcribing heaven. You groomed a blank page and called it destiny."

*"I prefer 'authored,'"* the Crown said, mild as a tutor. *"Destiny is only authorship that worked. Come. Take your place. You want this as much as I do — don't pretend otherwise. I have read your ledgers, Yara. Every impossible entry, balanced to the copper. You think I didn't recognize the hunger in them? A self with no past, keeping perfect records of other people's lives, begging the world to write her in."* The king's ruined lips curved. *"I am offering you the only family that has ever held this realm together. Three centuries of continuity. One body, endlessly renewed. You will never be alone again. You will never be no one again."*

And there it was — the thing she had been afraid to name since the night the gate first whispered her name. The shameful thrill. *Chosen.* She felt it even now, rising in her like heat, and she did not fight it. She let it stand beside her, acknowledged, accounted for.

"Yes," she said aloud, and the court stirred. "I do want it. Write that down in whatever book you keep. Entry the first: *the vessel consents.*"

She drew the folded rite from her sash and opened it, and the candles guttered all at once, ten thousand flames bending toward the dais like grass under wind.

The words of the unbinding were not grand. Sef had warned her of that. They were bookkeeping — the oldest kind, the kind Bram taught her her first week at the guildhouse, bent over her shoulder smelling of pipe smoke: *every entry must name what leaves, what remains, and who bears the loss.* She spoke them in the old tongue, the tongue the first king had been bound in, and with each line she laid her palm flat against the king's breastbone, over the heart, where the Crown sat like a rider who had forgotten there had ever been a horse.

*What leaves:* the intelligence called Crown, three hundred years of tenancy.

*What remains:* Aldous of Vessany, whatever was left of him, however little, however brief.

*Who bears the loss:* she almost stumbled here. The rite required a name, a bearer, someone to hold the debt. She had meant to say her own name and found — standing there, palm to the king's chest, the whole watching hall holding its breath — that she could not. Not because she was afraid. Because the name wouldn't come. As if some part of her, the part the Crown had spent a century writing toward, had already been left blank on purpose.

*"Yes,"* the Crown whispered, and the triumph in it was gentle, which made it worse. *"You see? There is nothing there to spend. Give me the page, child, and I will write us both."*

It abandoned the king the way a tide abandons a beach.

Yara felt it leave — a rush of cold going out of the body under her hand, Aldous sagging like a puppet with cut strings even as guards rushed forward — and then it was everywhere, in the candle-smoke, in the banners, in the space between her heartbeat and the next. The hall went dark at the edges. The court screamed. Somewhere below, impossibly far below, through stone and earth and the roots of the city, she felt Bram open the gate, and the world tilted around that opening like water finding a drain.

The Crown poured into her.

She had braced for pain. What came instead was reading. It read her — fast, vast, patient, turning her over like a buyer thumbing a manuscript — searching for the seam of her, the place where a self begins, the handle by which a parasite takes hold. It searched her childhood: not there. Her mother's face: not there. Her fears, her wants, her secret shames, the warm animal core every soul has that a thing like the Crown needs in order to grip —

Not there. Not there. Not there.

*Nothing,* it thought, and for the first time in three hundred years, the Crown knew fear. *There is nothing here to wear.*

"That's right," Yara said, inside herself, inside both of them, in the dark where it writhed looking for walls that weren't. "You wrote me blank. Blank was the specification. But you made one mistake, and Sef found it, and Bram taught me the rest."

She opened the ledger.

Not a metaphor — or not only. In the place where a self should have been, she had spent seven months building the thing the Crown had never imagined anyone would build: a structure. Entries. Columns ruled in her own hand, dated, cross-referenced, balanced to the copper. *Entry the second: the vessel contains a ledger.* She took hold of the Crown the way she took hold of a misfiled account — without anger, without mercy, with the terrible calm of someone who has done the work — and she began to write it in.

It fought. Of course it fought. It had worn kings; it had never been *clerked*. It threw three centuries of memory at her — coronations, betrayals, the first king screaming as it entered him, wars conducted from inside a skull — trying to bury her in its past, to give her a history so vast she'd drown in it and become, at last, something with a shape it could seize.

She filed it. All of it. Line by line. *Received, one reign, damaged. Received, one war, cause: us. Received, one apology, unsent, weight: considerable.* Each entry she wrote bound a piece of it; each piece bound made her ledger heavier, realer, more herself — and this was the sacrifice no one had warned her of, the cost arriving exactly on schedule: every entry cost her room. She could feel herself being spent, column by column, the way the rite demanded. *Who bears the loss.* She bore it. She bore it gladly. A self that was only ever going to be a doorway might as well be a good one.

Far above the dark, in a hall full of screaming candles, her body stood with its palm on a dying king's chest, and her mouth was moving, and Bram Almeida was holding the gate open with both hands and both names, hers and his, shouted down three hundred feet of stairwell into the roaring dark.

*What leaves:* the Crown.

*What remains:* Aldous, breathing, free, weeping without knowing why.

*Who bears the loss:* — and here, at the final line, with the last entry half-written and the last of her trembling like a candle in a draft, a hand closed over hers. Not in the dark. In the hall. Bram had come up the stairs through the chaos, an old man moving like a young one, and his fingers pressed down over hers on the king's breastbone, and his voice said, rough and certain:

"*Bram Almeida bears it. Jointly. Put it in the book, girl. Both our names. That's an order from your keeper.*"

She laughed — or the dark laughed, or both — and wrote it down.

*Entry the last: received, one Crown, three hundred years outstanding. Bound. Balanced. Signed,*

She signed it. Both names.

* * *

The candles came back one by one, like a congregation remembering how to breathe.

Yara woke on the dais steps with Bram's arm under her shoulders and the taste of ink in her mouth, actual ink, black at the corners of her lips. Above her, King Aldous sat slumped against the throne he had occupied for forty years, and his face — his own face, unoccupied, astonished, sixty seconds from death and knowing it and smiling anyway — looked down at her.

"Keeper," he said. His voice was only his voice now, small and human and ending. "Vessany thanks its keeper."

Then the bells began — not the coronation bells, the others, the alarm bells, ringing down from every tower in the city, and Bram lifted his head toward the western doors where the heralds were shouting something about the river, about the river running backward, about the gate.

Yara sat up. Inside her, quiet as a closed book on a high shelf, something waited to be catalogued.

"The gate's open," Bram said grimly, pulling her to her feet. "We held it too wide, and now, my dear, we're going to have to close it properly — and I suspect you already know there's only one way that ends."

She did know. She picked up her ledger, and she went.
