## 10. Keeper of the Gate

The king died on the third morning after his unbinding, in a narrow bed with clean linen and no crown upon it.

I was there. I had earned the right to be there, though it did not feel like a right; it felt like a debt I would be paying for the rest of my life. The room smelled of beeswax and feverfew and the particular sweetness of a body that has stopped fighting. Aldous of Vessany, who had worn a parasite for thirty-one years and called it duty, lay with his hands folded on his breast like a man waiting to be called upon in council, and his eyes — gods, his eyes were *his own*, brown and tired and faintly amused, with no cold light swimming behind them at all.

"Ledger-clerk," he said. His voice was a dry leaf. "Come closer. My eyes have gone soft as a boy's."

I came closer. Bram stood at the foot of the bed, hat in his hands, which was how he stood everywhere now that his hat had lost its purpose of hiding him from what he'd helped make.

"I never learned your name," the king said. "They brought you to me hooded, like contraband. Tell me before I go, so I can carry it where I'm going."

"Yara," I said. "Just Yara. There's no family name. Sef looked."

Aldous smiled. It transformed his whole ruined face into something young. "Just Yara," he repeated, tasting it. "Do you know, in three hundred years, that thing below never once asked anyone's name? It knew them all already. Names meant nothing to it." His fingers twitched against mine — light as moth-wings. "You gave it one anyway. In the end. You made it *Yara's*. That's the only victory over such a creature, girl. Not to destroy it. To own it."

He died an hour past noon, while rain moved softly across the palace gardens and a sparrow argued with the gutter outside his window. No wards failed when he went. No gate groaned. The realm simply continued, the way a held breath finally lets itself out, and somewhere in Vessany three hundred years of dread quietly became history.

Bram closed the king's eyes. I wrote the time in my ledger, because someone had to, because writing things down is the only immortality I believe in.

* * *

They buried Aldous under a rowan tree rather than in the crypts, at his own instruction, delivered in that last lucid hour: *No stone with my name on it. Let me be a tree. Trees don't get possessed.* The court thought it eccentric. The court had thought a great many things eccentric lately, including the coronation that hadn't happened, the puppet heir who had been quietly returned to his mother's estate with a pension and a warning, and the hooded clerk who had walked into the throne hall and unmade the oldest power in the kingdom with a pen.

The Guild came to me a week later, all nine of them, in the long room above the counting-house where they had voted to hand me to the king like a sack of grain. They sat along the table the way penitents sit, which is to say badly, backs not touching chairs. First Chair Merrow had aged ten years in ten days. Her hands, always so certain on the gavel, kept folding and unfolding in her lap.

"Yara," she said. "The Guild offers you—"

"Anything," I said. "I know. You've sent the letter twice. I read it by candlelight, both times, and I noticed the word *anything* was written very carefully, the way one writes a word one hopes won't be taken literally."

Merrow flushed. Old Corvane, who had voted loudest for my surrender, looked at the table. Bram, seated apart from them — they still hadn't given him back his chair — watched me with that steady keeper's patience that I had once mistaken for indifference and now knew for love wearing its work clothes.

"We cannot undo the vote," Merrow said. "We can only tell you what we should have told you from the beginning: that the Guild of Impossible Things exists because impossible things must be tended, and tenders must be honored, and we failed at the second so thoroughly that we nearly destroyed the first. Ask us for anything. A house. A title. The archives. The Guild-book will record it as debt paid in perpetuity."

I had thought about this for seven days, in the intervals between sleeping and not sleeping, between the dreams where the Crown whispered through its bindings and the mornings when I checked the seals on its pages the way other people check the weather.

What I wanted could not be granted. That was the truth of it. Belonging isn't a gift; it's a post you take up and hold until your hands learn the shape of it. And I knew exactly which post.

"The gate," I said.

The room went very still.

"Beneath the counting-house," I said, "there is a door that must never open, behind which sits a hundred-year prophecy written by the thing it prophesied, and beneath *that* — since nine nights ago — sit forty-one ledgers in my handwriting containing a parasitic intelligence bound entry by entry into paper and ink and salt. Someone has to keep them. Someone has to audit the seals every night, balance the account, add a page when it strains, and stand in the dark listening to it try out my voice until it remembers that its voice belongs to me now." I looked around the table, at each of their faces, and found — to my astonishment — that I wasn't afraid of them anymore. "I'm asking for the worst job in the Guild. I'm asking for the one nobody wants. Because it's the only one that was ever really mine."

Old Corvane spoke first, voice cracking like ice on a pond. "Child, that watch killed every keeper before Almeida. It will eat your years. It will eat your sleep. It will—"

"Yes," I said. "I've read the ledgers. All of them. Including the ones about the keepers." I let that settle. "It ate their years because they fought it alone and lied about it. I intend neither."

Merrow closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had shifted in her face — grief and relief tangled together, the look of a woman setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. "Then the Guild grants it," she said. "Keeper of the Gate, Yara, of no bloodline and no past, chosen freely." She hesitated. "And Yara — the Guild asks nothing in return except that you let us do better. Give us a chance to do better."

"Ledgers forgive debts when they're paid," I said. "Start paying."

* * *

The counting-house at night is a different country. The candles burn low and gold, and the great ledgers of the Guild sleep in their shelves with the soft sighing sound old paper makes when it settles, and if you stand very still on the third stair you can hear the building thinking — timbers ticking, pipes murmuring, the vast slow respiration of a place that has kept secrets for four centuries and intends to keep more.

Below all of it, down the spiral stairs, past the door that must never open, is my office now. Bram built the desk himself. He refused to let anyone help, which took him eleven days, and when it was done it was slightly crooked and utterly perfect, and he pretended not to watch my face when I touched the wood.

The Crown's ledgers live in the iron cabinet against the far wall. Forty-one volumes, each sealed with wax and salt and a line of my blood dried brown as autumn. At night, sometimes, if the wind is right, the cabinet hums. Not words. Not anymore. Just a hum, like a hive dreaming, like a choir that has been told to sing *pianissimo* for the next hundred years. When it hums too loudly, I open the current volume and write. It doesn't matter what — inventory, weather, the price of tallow. The act is the anchor. Every entry says, in effect: *someone is keeping accounts here, and you are on the wrong side of them.*

Tonight the hum is quiet. Tonight the seals are sound. Tonight I sit at my crooked desk by lamplight and hear Bram's boots on the spiral stair, unhurried, familiar, and I feel something I am only now learning to name without flinching.

Home. The ordinary word. The impossible one.

"You're late," I say as he comes down. "Audit started a quarter hour ago. I'll note it in the margin."

"Noted with pleasure," Bram says, and hangs his hat — his old hat, the one with the secret brim, the one he wore to hide — on the peg I put there for him without asking. He doesn't comment on the peg. He doesn't need to. Some things are entered in the book without being spoken aloud.

We work. He checks the wax seals; I check the salt lines; we compare figures the way we did on my first night, a hundred years ago or six months, I've honestly lost track. Then he sets down his lamp, and takes from inside his coat a slim volume I know by heart and have never once been allowed to touch.

The guild-book. The true one. The register of names, begun four hundred years ago, in which every member of the Guild of Impossible Things is entered in their own hand — or, failing that, in the hand of the member who vouches for them. I have seen it twice. Both times I looked for my name and both times the page turned blankly past, because I was staff. Because I was a clerk. Because I was nobody, and the book only knows somebodies.

"Bram," I say carefully. "What are you doing?"

"My last official act as Keeper of the Gate," he says. His voice is steady but his hands aren't, and seeing Bram Almeida's hands tremble undoes something in my chest I'd been holding shut for weeks. "Which position, as of tonight, is vacant." He opens the book to a fresh page. He uncaps the ink. "I'm told the new keeper requires a second. Someone to share the watch. Two voices in the dark are harder to lie with than one."

"You hate the dark," I say. "You told me. First week. You said the dark down here sounds like breathing."

"It does," he says. "That's rather the point. A gate that breathes deserves two listeners." He holds out the pen. "But there's a formality first. The book requires the name. Full name, in the entrant's own hand, witnessed by a member in good standing." He turns the book toward me. The page waits, white as snowfall. "So. Yara. Of no bloodline, of no past, of no family anywhere in any archive in Vessany." His eyes are bright. "Tell the book who you are. Whatever you decide that means. It's yours to write. It was always yours to write — that's the whole point of you, girl. That's what the Crown never understood and what the prophecy got wrong. Blankness isn't emptiness. It's *room*."

I take the pen. My hand does not shake, which surprises me, because everything else in me is shaking like a struck bell.

I think about names. About the ones I was never given and the ones I've earned. I think about a girl who balanced impossible accounts by candlelight because the numbers couldn't hurt her and the numbers never left. I think about a king who wanted to be a tree, and an archivist who taught me that a missing record is still a kind of testimony, and a man in a hiding-hat who broke me out of custody with a stolen cart and a forged writ and no plan beyond *not letting them have you.*

I dip the pen. I write:

*Yara Almeida. Keeper of the Gate. Of no bloodline, and therefore of every one.*

Bram reads it. Bram goes very still. Then he laughs — a rough, cracked, wonderful sound, the laugh of a man who has guarded a door alone for twenty years and just heard footsteps coming to share the watch — and blots the page with the care of a priest handling relics.

"Witnessed," he says thickly, and signs beneath my name. "Entered into the guild-book this night, in her own hand. Binding." He looks up. "*Binding.* Do you see what you did there? You even made the paperwork pun."

"I learned from a master," I say.

Below us, faint as a heartbeat, the cabinet hums. Above us, the guildhouse settles into its centuries. Between the two, at a crooked desk, two keepers bend over a lamp and begin the night's accounts — and for the first time since the date that wasn't there appeared in a century-old ledger, the numbers balance perfectly, and the dark sounds like breathing, and the breathing is friendly, and I am not alone in it, and I never will be again.

Somewhere in the iron cabinet, something that wore thirty kings tries my name against the silence and finds it doesn't fit anymore.

It's mine. I checked the entry myself.
