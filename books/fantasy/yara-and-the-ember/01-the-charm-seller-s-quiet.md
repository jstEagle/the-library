## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet

The night market of Grayhollow smelled of wet wool, fried onions, and fear kept carefully under the floorboards.

Wendell Brightwater had learned to read all three before she'd learned the city's streets. Fear was the one you had to track most closely—fear changed its scent with the seasons, thickened in autumn when the Purge's anniversary came around like a bad tooth, thinned in midsummer when the Inquisition's patrols grew lazy and fat on festival bribes. Tonight it sat heavy over the market stalls like fog off the river, and Wendell arranged her charms beneath her awning with the particular care of a woman handling something that could detonate.

Which, technically, everything on her table could.

"Love knots," she called softly, because shouting drew eyes and quiet drew only ears, and ears were easier to manage. "Charms against forgetting. Charms against bad dreams. Blessings for safe roads."

All of it harmless. All of it *meant* to be harmless—the knot-work braided from river reeds with no more magic in it than a fisherman's patience, the dream-charms stuffed with lavender and chamomile that worked because herbs worked, not because anything whispered to them. Seven years she had sold nothing but the ordinary dressed up in pretty words, and in seven years not one item on her table had ever so much as flickered.

That was the trick of survival in Grayhollow. Not hiding your power—that was impossible; power leaked out of you the way heat leaked from a stove. The trick was making yourself so small, so mundane, so thoroughly uninteresting that no one thought to look at what leaked.

*Invisibility is a discipline,* her master had told her once, sharpening a blade by candlelight in a house that no longer existed except as ash and a scar on the city's memory. *Anyone can swing a sword, Brightwater. Few can stand in a room full of people who want them dead and be forgotten by morning.*

He'd been forgotten by morning too. That hadn't saved him.

A woman in a fishmonger's apron stopped at the table, turning a charm against forgetting between chapped fingers. "Does this work?"

"Better than most prayers," Wendell said, and smiled her merchant's smile—the one that lived on her face the way a coat hung on a hook, easy to put on, easy to take off, never quite warm enough to reach her eyes. "My grandmother's recipe. Rosemary for memory, iron for grounding, and a word or two my family's kept close for generations."

Every word true. None of it honest. She was getting very good at sentences like that.

The woman bought three—one for herself, one for her mother whose mind was going soft as overripe fruit, one for a son who'd gone to the mines and come back quieter than he left. Wendell wrapped each in brown paper and string, and when their hands touched across the coins she felt the small cold ache she always felt: the ache of a gift she couldn't give. Because she *could* have made that woman's son sleep without nightmares. Could have eased the mother's forgetting with a breath and a spoken syllable and the old, sweet weight of her oaths rising through her arm like sap through wood.

She could have done it in four seconds. It would have cost her everything, and probably gotten the fishmonger burned alongside her.

"Thank you kindly," the woman said, and moved on into the fog of lanterns and voices.

Wendell exhaled slowly and counted her coins. Eleven coppers and a bent silver bit. Enough for bread, lamp oil, and the good honey Yara liked stirred into porridge on cold mornings. Not enough for much else, but then, wanting little was another kind of armor.

Above the market's canvas roofs, Grayhollow's sky was doing what it always did this time of year: sitting low and starless, smothered by cloud and coal smoke and the general civic determination not to look upward. Tomorrow would be the ninth anniversary of the Purge. The city didn't mark it officially—officially there was no Purge, only *the Correction*, commemorated with a municipal holiday and a parade of white-cloaked inquisitors whose boots struck the cobblestones in time like a metronome counting down somebody's life. Unofficially, every mage still breathing in Grayhollow counted the days themselves, the way prisoners scratched tallies into cell walls.

Eight years ago, Wendell had stood in the market square with forty other swordspeakers while the Inquisition read names aloud. She remembered the sound the fire made. Everyone assumed it was screaming they heard, afterward. It wasn't. Screaming ended quickly. The fire itself went on for hours, a low greedy roar, and the crowd stood silent in the light of it, and Wendell had stood among them with her oath sealed shut behind her teeth like a swallowed coal, and she had done nothing.

That was the price. That was always the price. You could keep your head down and keep breathing, or you could speak, and speaking meant burning—and burning meant everyone who loved you burned in the same wind. She had chosen silence. She chose it again every morning, and every morning it cost a little more, like rent paid to a landlord who raised the rate whenever he pleased.

*One more year,* she told herself, the way she did every autumn. *One more year of being nobody, and then—*

Then what? She'd been promising herself an "and then" for seven years, and the promise had worn thin as an old coin. There was no and-then. There was only the table, the charms, the smile, the slow erosion of everything she'd been until even she sometimes doubted there'd ever been a woman named Wendell Brightwater who could make steel sing.

"You're scowling," said a voice at her elbow. "You only scowl when you're counting money or remembering things, and I can see your ledger from here."

Pip dropped onto the stool beside her stall as if he owned it, which in fairness he might have, since Wendell had never actually seen him acquire the stool. He was fourteen, sharp-faced, quick-fingered, with the permanent expression of a boy who knew where every lock in the city kept its weaknesses. Officially, Pip was her stock-boy. Unofficially, Wendell had stopped asking how merchandise appeared in her back room at prices no legitimate wholesaler would offer, because the answers would require paperwork, and paperwork required existing.

"I'm remembering things," she admitted.

"Ah." Pip plucked an apple from his pocket and buffed it on his sleeve. "The anniversary. You know what helps? Not thinking about it." He took an enormous bite. "Works for me. I don't think about my whole childhood and I'm thriving."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's said to me all week, and a man wept over the price of dream-charms yesterday."

"Weeping's free. Apples aren't." He nodded toward the far end of the lane. "Speaking of which. Your girl's coming. Been standing behind the candle-seller's stall for five minutes working up the nerve to sneak up on you, which is pointless, because I heard her boots from the bridge."

Wendell turned, and despite everything—the fog, the fear, the tally marks scratching themselves in her chest—something in her loosened.

Yara came down the lane the way she did everything, which was like someone who had decided the world should move out of her way and was mostly right. Sixteen next month, all elbows and certainty, dark hair escaping its braid, carrying a basket of mending she was ostensibly delivering to save Wendell the trip home and was actually an excuse. She'd been raised in this market the way a vine is raised on a wall—shaped by it, clinging to it, taller than anyone expected.

"Mama Brightwater," Yara said, using the name she'd invented at age six and refused to surrender, though Wendell had never married and certainly wasn't anybody's mother, and both of them knew it, and neither of them wanted it any other way. "You look terrible."

"Thank you, dear. Years of practice."

"Pip says you're brooding about tomorrow."

"Pip talks too much."

"Pip," Pip said, "is going to go count the takings, which is a job I have generously given myself," and he vanished into the crowd with the apple and, Wendell noticed a moment later, the bent silver bit from her coin box. She let him. Consider it wages.

Yara set down the basket and started restocking the display with the brisk familiarity of someone who'd been doing it since she could carry a bundle of knot-charms. This was the part of the evening Wendell guarded like a flame cupped in her palms. Not the selling. This. The ordinary miracle of a girl humming off-key beside her, complaining that the new ribbon stock was ugly, stealing a honey-drop from the jar Wendell kept for exactly this purpose and pretending she hadn't.

Seven years ago, Wendell had found a baby on the steps of the Lanternfold orphanage with no name but the one pinned to her blanket—*Yara*, in a hand that shook—and had told herself she was only checking on the child. Once a week. Then daily. Then the matron had taken ill, and Wendell had taken the baby home "temporarily," and temporary had become seven years of scraped knees and fever-nights and teaching a small fierce person to read, and somewhere in the middle of it Wendell had realized she would burn down the world for this child, which was a problem, because the world was already quite flammable and she was trying very hard not to be noticed.

"What are you staring at?" Yara asked, not looking up.

"You've got honey on your chin."

"I do not—" She did. She wiped it with her sleeve, glaring. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The looking-at-me-like-I'm-going-to-vanish thing." Yara's voice was light, but her eyes weren't. They were sharp and dark and far too perceptive, and lately—these past few months—they'd lingered on Wendell a beat too long after jokes, like a reader who suspected the last page had been torn out. "I'm sixteen in a month, Mama B. I'm not going anywhere."

*No,* Wendell almost said. *You're not. Nothing will take you from me. I have buried one family already and I will lie to God and the Inquisition and my own dead master before I let anything touch you.*

What she said was: "Good. Because you owe me a week of mending deliveries, and I intend to collect."

Yara laughed, and the laugh did what it always did—reached into Wendell's chest and rearranged the furniture. This was the warmth. This was the whole point. Whatever the silence cost, whatever small deaths invisibility died daily, there was this: a stall in a fog-bound market, a stolen apple somewhere in the crowd, a girl with honey on her chin calling her *Mama B* like it was the simplest truth in the world.

It was enough. She told herself it was enough.

* * *

They walked home together past midnight, lanterns guttering along the Drowned Quarter's leaning streets, and Wendell felt the fear rise again as they crossed into the shadow of the Inquisitorial Hall—white stone, white cloaks drying on lines inside the courtyard like shed skins. A patrol passed them at the corner: three inquisitors, young, bored, hands resting on iron-tipped staves. One glanced at Yara. One glanced at Wendell's charm-bag. Neither looked twice.

Nobody ever looked twice. That was the discipline. That was the whole art of her life, performed nightly for an audience that must never learn it was watching a performance at all.

But tonight, passing beneath the Hall's eaves, Wendell caught something she hadn't caught in seven years—a smell underneath the wet stone and smoke. Faint. Old. Like a hearth raked out and still breathing embers somewhere in the walls.

Her skin prickled. Her sword-hand, hanging empty at her side, ached the way old wounds ache before rain.

"You've gone stiff again," Yara said quietly. She'd noticed. Of course she'd noticed. "What is it?"

"Nothing. Old memories." Wendell forced her shoulders loose, her stride easy, her face into the merchant's smile. "Come on. Honey-porridge before bed, and tomorrow we keep the shuttered stall closed and stay in. Anniversary rule."

"The boring rule."

"The rule that keeps us alive, dear."

Yara rolled her eyes and went on ahead, and Wendell followed, and if she looked back once—at the Hall, at the white cloaks swaying on their lines, at the sky pressing black and starless over the rooftops—well. Looking back was allowed. It was the only luxury invisibility permitted.

Behind them, deep in the Inquisitorial Hall, in an office lit by a single candle, someone opened a ledger and wrote down a name.

Not hers. Not yet.
