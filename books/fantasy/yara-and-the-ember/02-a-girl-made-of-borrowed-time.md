## 2. A Girl Made of Borrowed Time

The morning of Yara's sixteenth birthday, Wendell Brightwater burned the honey cakes.

Not badly—only the bottoms, only enough that she could scrape each one with a knife and dust the scars with cinnamon and pretend nothing had happened. But she stood over the iron pan in the kitchen behind the charm shop and felt the failure of it all the way down into her hands, which had never once trembled around a blade and shook now over something as simple as sugar.

*You can kill a man at forty paces,* she told herself, scraping. *You cannot keep a cake from burning.*

It was not really about the cake. She knew that. It was about the number sixteen, which sat on her chest this morning like a stone, like a debt coming due. Sixteen years since a dying woman had pressed a bundle into Wendell's arms in the reek of a border waystation and made her promise things no one should promise. Sixteen years since Wendell had looked down at a face still blue with birth and thought: *I will lie so well you will never know there was anything to lie about.*

Sixteen years, and the lying had gone perfectly. That was the worst of it. The lie had held like good mortar, and mortar was what it was made of, if Brannoc ever spoke true—and every year it set a little harder, until now there was a girl upstairs sleeping off a headache from stolen plum wine, and there was a truth beneath the mountain, and between them stood Wendell with a knife and a pan of scarred honey cakes, pretending to be only a woman who sold charms that did nothing.

She heard Yara moving above her. The old floorboards of the shop announced everyone who walked on them—Pip claimed they were gossiping, and paid them in coppers to stay quiet when he crept in through the window at odd hours—and Wendell knew Yara's particular tread by heart: light, quick, always slightly impatient with the ground for being where it was. The tread crossed to the washstand. Paused. Crossed back.

Then the boards groaned, and Wendell had just enough time to slide the cakes onto a chipped blue plate before Yara came down the stairs two at a time, hair unbound and rioting, wearing the good shawl Marisol had dyed with madder root last spring.

"Happy birthday," Wendell said. "I ruined the cakes."

"You ruin the cakes every year." Yara dropped into a chair at the little table by the window and pulled the plate toward herself with the shameless gravity of the young. "It's tradition. If they came out right I'd think you'd been replaced."

"By whom? Who would take this job?"

"Someone who can cook. Someone boring. Pip says you were a soldier before you were a baker of failures."

"Pip," Wendell said, "says many things."

Yara ate a cake with the bottom scraped thin, cinnamon dusting her chin, and looked out the window at Grayhollow waking up—the chimney smoke standing straight in the windless morning, the gulls arguing over the fish market, the gray stone going gold where the sun found it. A city built of secrets, every one of them load-bearing. Wendell watched her watch it and felt the stone in her chest shift.

"I dreamed about the mountain," Yara said.

Wendell kept her hands busy with the kettle. "Did you."

"Not the mountain exactly. More like—" Yara frowned, chasing it. "Warmth. Like standing near a forge but inside the warmth somehow, and someone was counting. Just counting, very slowly, very kindly, like a lullaby. And every number they counted, somewhere far off a star went out." She laughed, embarrassed, and waved a hand. "Stupid. Plum wine dreams."

"Plum wine dreams," Wendell agreed, and poured the water, and did not let her hands shake where the girl could see them.

Because Wendell knew that dream. Had known it once, years ago, secondhand—a woman in a waystation bed, fevered, whispering about counting and coals and the price of passage. *They count kindly,* the mother had said. *That's how you know they're old. Nothing young counts that gently.*

She had buried those words with the woman. Apparently some words refused burial. Apparently they traveled, patient as seeds, from a dying mother's mouth to a daughter's sleep, across sixteen years and a city's worth of silence.

"More tea?" Wendell asked.

"Always more tea." Yara wrapped both hands around the cup when it came, and for a while they simply sat, the two of them in the morning light, and Wendell committed it to memory the way she committed everything now—greedily, suspiciously, like a miser counting coins she feared were counterfeit. The steam. The cinnamon. The gulls. *If today is all you get,* she told herself, as she told herself every day, *let it be enough.*

It was never enough. That was the flaw in the arithmetic, and she'd known it from the start.

* * *

The shop opened at noon, as it always did on festival days, and by mid-afternoon the counter was crowded with the small commerce of Grayhollow's superstitions. Wendell sold charms the way other shops sold bread: quickly, cheaply, with a smile calibrated to be warm without being memorable.

A knot-charm for a boy whose hands sweated before examinations. A salt-ring pendant for an old woman afraid of the dark in her stairwell. A love-token, bought by a young man who blushed so hard Wendell took pity and told him it worked best if he also spoke to the girl, which seemed to genuinely surprise him.

Every charm was honest in its dishonesty. Thread, salt, river-stone, a word or two of nonsense Latin left over from some dead scholar's grimoire. None of it worked. None of it *could* work—that was the point, that was the whole architecture of her invisibility. In a city that burned spellcasters, the safest magic was none at all, and the safest place to hide a swordspeaker was behind a counter selling knotted thread to the frightened.

And yet. And yet there was Mrs. Aldery, who had worn her salt-ring for nine years and swore she'd not had a single bad night since. There was the ferryman who wouldn't cross without his knot-charm. There was Yara herself, who had grown up believing her guardian sold real protection and had never once been disillusioned, because the protection was real—it just didn't come from the charms.

It came from a woman who slept with a sword under her floorboards and woke at every sound.

"Business is good," said a voice at her elbow, and Wendell turned to find Pip materializing out of the crowd the way he did, small and quick and dressed in a coat that had been expensive before someone else owned it. He set three apples on the counter in payment for nothing, because Pip's economy operated on principles known only to Pip.

"It's a birthday," Wendell said. "Birthdays are good for business. People feel mortal."

"People feel mortal every day. They just admit it on birthdays." He stole an apple back off his own payment, considered it, put it down again. "The girl's sixteen. That's a big one."

"It is."

"You're going to give her the thing."

Wendell's hands went still over the counter. "What thing?"

"The thing in your pocket that you've been touching every hour since dawn like a rosary." Pip smiled with all the warmth of a knife being sharpened. "I steal for a living, Brightwater. Noticing pockets is the whole trade."

Wendell took the locket out and set it on the counter between them, and for a moment neither of them touched it.

It was silver, old work, the kind of craftsmanship nobody did anymore—too fine, too deliberate. On its face was engraved a mountain seen from below, its peak lost in cloud. Inside, on one side, a space for a portrait that had never been filled; on the other, a chamber just large enough for something small and precious. Wendell had spent four nights this week on the charm alone, working by candlelight after Yara slept, whispering the binding words in the old tongue, the forbidden tongue, the language of her order that lived now only in her own mouth and in whatever rust had not yet eaten it.

It was a forgetting-charm inverted. Where most such magic preserved memory against assault—against the mind-eaters of the deep roads, against the Inquisition's questioning draughts—this one did something subtler. It held a person's memories *to them*. Anchored them. Made them harder to lose, harder to have taken, harder, perhaps—

Harder, perhaps, to have sold away.

"That's not a birthday gift," Pip said quietly. "That's armor."

"She'll think it's pretty."

"She'll think it's pretty," Pip agreed, "and then she'll wear it every day for the rest of her life, and one day she'll notice it hums when she's near certain doors, and she'll ask you why, and you'll have to decide whether to lie to her face for the thousandth time or the millionth." He pushed the locket back toward Wendell with one finger. "I'm not judging. I've lied to better people than both of us for worse reasons. I'm just saying—I've seen what's in your pocket, and I know what it costs to carry."

"You don't know what it costs."

"No," Pip said, and for once his voice had no performance in it at all. "But I know what it costs *you*. I watch you the way you watch her."

He stole two apples this time and vanished into the crowd, and Wendell stood alone at her counter holding sixteen years of silver and guilt, and the afternoon went on around her, ordinary as bread.

* * *

The party was small, because everything in their lives was small by design. Marisol came early with a jar of plum brandy and a scolding for the cakes' condition, then stayed to help eat them anyway. Brannoc arrived late and silent as always, huge and gray-bearded, carrying a box of tools wrapped in oilcloth that turned out to contain a hairpin he'd forged himself—iron, simple, beautiful in the way of things made by hands that understood metal the way priests understood prayer. He gave it to Yara without meeting anyone's eyes, mumbled something about it being nothing, and retreated to the corner with his ale, where he remained like furniture that occasionally drank.

Pip produced a silk scarf that everyone privately assessed as stolen and publicly admired as charming. Serah Vane did not come, because Serah Vane was Inquisition now, and there were doors she could no longer walk through even if her heart still knocked on them. But a parcel had arrived at the shop that morning with no note: a book of folk tales, old edition, the spine repaired with careful stitching. Wendell recognized the stitchwork. Some people showed loyalty the only way the law left them room to.

They ate. They drank. Marisol told the story of the time she'd cursed a tax collector with boils—not real boils, she hastened to add for the benefit of any listening walls, purely cosmetic ones—and Brannoc, to universal astonishment, laughed, a sound like boulders settling. Yara glowed. And through it all Wendell watched her daughter—because that was the word, whatever the paperwork said, whatever the contract said, *daughter*, chosen and carried in the heart if nowhere else—and waited for the moment she dreaded and had rehearsed for.

It came when the candles were lit.

Sixteen candles on a honey cake with its scars hidden under cinnamon, and Yara leaning forward to blow them out, and Marisol calling out "Make it a good wish!" and Yara pausing, eyes closed, lips moving around something silent and serious. Then breath, darkness, cheers, and the cake cut and passed around, and Yara turning to Wendell with candlelight still caught in her eyes and asking, casually, terribly:

"What did you wish for me? When I was born. Did you make a wish?"

The question was nothing. The question was everything. Wendell smiled and said, "I wished you'd sleep through the night," and got the laugh she was aiming for, and reached into her pocket, and brought out the locket.

The table quieted. Even Pip stopped eating.

"For your birthday," Wendell said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, too formal, like a speech rehearsed. "It was your mother's."

It was not. It had belonged to a pawnbroker in the Sallow Quarter who'd sold it for drink money, and Wendell had bought it eleven years ago specifically for this day, and the lie slid out smooth as oiled steel because she had practiced it, because she practiced all of them, because that was what her life was now, a performance running sixteen years without an intermission.

Yara took it carefully, turning it in the light. "My mother's?"

"Mm. She wanted you to have it when you were grown. Sixteen is grown, more or less. Don't tell Marisol I said so."

Inside, Wendell knew, the charm lay coiled and waiting, keyed to Yara's heartbeat, woven through with words in a dead language. *Hold what is yours. Keep what is yours. Let nothing take what is yours.* She had written the binding herself and rewritten it twice, unsatisfied, and finally understood on the third night that what she was really trying to bind was not memory at all but a soul—to anchor a girl's self to her body against a claim already filed, already signed, already sealed in a court beneath a mountain. She was building a lock without admitting there was a door.

Yara opened the locket. The empty portrait side stared up at her, and Wendell saw her frown at it, just slightly—the smallest crease, the first crack in the ice.

"There's no picture."

"No."

"But there's a space for one. Someone meant to put a picture here and didn't." Yara looked up. "Was that her? Did she mean to?"

"She ran out of time," Wendell said, which was true, though not in the way Yara would hear it. "People do."

Yara closed the locket. Her fist closed around it after, tight, and Wendell watched the girl hold her own inheritance—half counterfeit, half true, all of it heavier than silver—and something in her expression shifted, settled, became the look Wendell had learned to fear over sixteen years: the look of a question being filed away rather than answered.

Then it passed, and Yara was smiling again, and putting the chain over her head, and letting Marisol fasten it, and the party rolled on. But Wendell had spent a lifetime reading faces across blades, and she had seen the filing. The question wasn't dead. Questions like that never died. They went into the ground and waited, patient as seeds.

* * *

Later—much later, the dishes done, Marisol snoring in the spare room, Brannoc gone home through streets he knew better than their own mothers knew them, Pip having left by the window with the grace of a cat who considers doors a personal insult—Wendell climbed the stairs to check on Yara, as she did every night, as she would do every night until she died or the world ended, whichever proved more punctual.

The girl was asleep, sprawled and graceless, one arm hanging off the bed, the locket rising and falling on her chest with her breathing. Moonlight through the window laid a pale stripe across the floor, across the heap of books, across the new-forged hairpin glinting on the nightstand where she'd placed it like a treasure.

Wendell stood in the doorway a long time.

*Tell her,* said a voice in her that sounded like her dead master, like her dead friend Serah-before-the-Purge, like every version of herself she might have been. *Tell her what she is. Tell her what's coming. Give her the choice you never had.*

*And then what?* answered the voice she actually lived by, the tired one, the practical one. *She runs to the mountain alone. Or she stays and hates me. Or the Inquisition hears her asking questions and we both burn. Protection is a lie I can live inside. Truth is a fire I can't.*

*Is it?*

She had no answer. She only had the moonlight and the sleeping girl and the locket doing its silent work—*hold what is yours, keep what is yours*—a lock bolted from Wendell's side of the door, never asking the person locked in whether she wanted locking.

Wendell crossed the room, straightened the blanket, and brushed the hair back from Yara's forehead the way she had done since the girl was small enough to hold in two arms. Yara murmured something in her sleep and turned toward the touch, toward the warmth, trusting completely, and the trust went through Wendell like a spear finding the gap in armor she hadn't known she was wearing.

"My girl," she whispered. "My girl made of borrowed time."

Below her, in the city, Grayhollow slept. Above her, through the window, the stars burned on in their courses—all of them, tonight, every one, indifferent and whole.

She did not know, could not have known, that the ledger was open. That somewhere beneath the mountain something old was counting, slowly, kindly, patiently, and had just finished counting a star.

She kissed her daughter's forehead, took up her candle, and went downstairs to sit awake with her sword and her lies until morning.

Behind her, in the dark, the locket hummed—so faintly that no one sleeping heard it. So faintly that even Wendell, two floors down, missed it entirely.

But it hummed. And outside the window, high over Grayhollow, a star flickered—once, briefly, like a candle catching a draft—and steadied again.

For now.
