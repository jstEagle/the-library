## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Wendell Brightwater had learned to read a crowd the way other people read weather, and tonight the night market of Grayhollow smelled like rain before a storm.

Not literal rain. The autumn sky was dry as parchment, clear and cold, the kind of sky that should have been thick with stars. She glanced up out of habit—she always did on this week of the year, the anniversary of the Purge, when her skin remembered fire that her face refused to show—and counted them the way she counted everything precious: quickly, secretly, and with the knowledge that counting wouldn't keep them.

Seven years since the order fell. Seven years since she'd watched the last swordspeaker burn in Ashmarket Square with his oath still locked behind his teeth, because even at the end he hadn't given the Inquisition the gift of hearing it. Seven years of selling luck-charms and traveler's wards made of nothing but knotted thread and honest suggestion.

"Charms for the road," she called now, her merchant's voice bright and hollow as a tin bell. "Wards against ill winds. Knots for safe passage over the pass."

The lie had worn smooth from use. That was the cruelest part—not that she lied, but that the lying had become easy, comfortable, a coat that fit so well she sometimes forgot she'd never owned it honestly.

A fishwife bought three knots for her sons going downriver. A clerk's wife fingered a charm against forgetting and put it back, laughing at herself. Wendell smiled at all of them with the same measured warmth, and beneath the smile her oaths sat coiled in her chest like sleeping iron, seven years silent, growing heavier every season.

*They rust,* her master had told her once, when she was young enough to ask dangerous questions. *An oath unspoken is an oath unfed. Speak it or lose it.* She had chosen to lose it. Every day, she chose it again. Some choices you make once; hers she remade each morning, and each morning it cost more.

She was packing up early—the market felt wrong tonight, too quiet, the lamplighters hurrying their rounds—when she saw Yara cutting through the stalls with Pip at her elbow, both of them flushed with some scheme.

"You're closing?" Yara said. "You never close early."

"The air's strange." Wendell folded the last of the thread-knots into her case. "Go home ahead of me. Both of you."

Pip grinned, gap-toothed and quick-eyed. "We were going to climb the bell tower. Brannoc says on a clear night you can see the border lights from up there, way out past the ash fields."

"Brannoc says a lot of things," Wendell said, and something cold slid along her spine that had nothing to do with the wind. "Home, Yara."

Yara rolled her eyes the way only sixteen-year-olds can, a full-body performance of grievance, but she went. Wendell watched her go—that girl, her girl, borrowed time walking away in secondhand boots—and did not know yet that the time was nearly due.

The first star went out while she was locking her stall.

It didn't fall. Falling stars have grace, have motion, have somewhere to be. This one simply ceased, the way a candle ceases when pinched, and the darkness where it had hung seemed deeper than ordinary dark, a hole cut in the cloth of things.

Wendell stood with her key half-turned in the lock and stared up.

The second star died before she could draw breath.

Then a third, and a fourth, and the market began to notice—not all at once, but in ripples, a woman stopping mid-haggle, a child pointing upward with a kebab still in hand, the lamplighter on his ladder turning his head slowly like a man watching a ship sink.

"No," Wendell whispered, because she knew what extinguishing stars meant. Every hedge-witch's grandmother knew. Stars did not die by accident. Stars died *collected*, harvested, drawn down into vessels and vaults, and there was exactly one power in the world that fed on starlight, and it slept beneath a mountain that the border wards had been built to seal.

The wards. Her stomach turned to stone. The border wards—

They shattered like glass across the whole horizon at once.

There was no sound at first. Then the sound arrived, rolling in from beyond the hills, a vast crystalline groan, a cathedral's worth of windows breaking in slow motion, and above the eastern ridge where the ward-lights had glowed soft blue for two hundred years, Wendell saw the light gutter, fracture, and pour down the far side of the mountains like water finding a crack in a dam.

Every hair on her arms rose. Deep in her chest, the sleeping oaths shivered—not waking, not yet, but stirring the way a dog stirs when it smells fire.

The market broke apart around her. Screaming started small—a child first, then the fishwife, then everyone—and Grayhollow, which prided itself on being too gray and too stubborn to panic, panicked beautifully and completely. Stalls overturned. Someone's cart bolted. The temple bells began ringing without any order, all of them at once, a chaos of bronze.

Wendell ran home through streets full of running people, and the whole way she thought one thought, over and over, like a prayer worn down to the wood: *Yara. Yara. Yara.*

* * *

Yara had not gone home.

She had gone up the bell tower with Pip after all, because Yara Vane-no-name had never once in her life done the sensible thing the first time it was asked of her, and because Pip knew which stones made handholds.

So they were forty feet above the rooftops, wind whipping Yara's dark hair loose from its braid, when the stars began to go out.

"Are you seeing this," Pip breathed.

"I'm seeing it."

"It's like someone's—" Pip squinted, head cocked, and Yara knew that look; Pip listened to the world the way others looked at it. "Someone's *blowing* them out. Like candles. You can almost hear it, a little—"

"*Whuff*," Yara finished, and then hated how small her voice sounded against the wind.

Star by star, the sky emptied. It wasn't frightening at first. It was beautiful, terrible the way avalanches are terrible from a distance, a slow black tide rising across heaven. Then the horizon lit blue-white, and the sound came—the breaking, the enormous glassy grief of it—and Yara felt it in her teeth, in her sternum, in the locket against her breastbone.

The locket hummed.

She'd worn it three days, ever since her birthday, ever since Wendell had pressed it into her palm with that look Yara couldn't name—tenderness stretched over guilt, like paint over a crack. The locket had never done anything before except sit warm against her skin. Now it vibrated like a struck tuning fork, humming in sympathy with the dying wards, and Yara pressed her hand flat over it and felt something answer from very far away. From the mountains. From under the mountains.

"Yara." Pip had gone white. "Your necklace—it's doing something—"

"I know."

"We should get down. We should get down and go home and bar the door, my ma will kill us, the Inquisition's going to—"

The Inquisition. Even here, even now. Down in the streets below, torches were already gathering at the chapterhouse, red-and-gray figures moving with the horrible efficiency of people who had rehearsed for disaster their whole careers. Horns blew three long notes: curfew. Assembly. Suspicion.

Yara took one last look east, at the broken light bleeding off the mountains, and felt the locket pull toward it like a hooked fish feeling the line.

Something under the world had woken up. And somehow, impossibly, some piece of it knew her name.

* * *

Serah Vane heard the wards break while she was signing execution orders.

That was the truth of her evening, and she would carry it a long time: nine names on her desk, nine unregistered mages condemned in routine batches, her pen scratching through the ninth signature when the window behind her filled with blue-white light and the sound came through the glass like the sky clearing its throat to weep.

Her pen stopped mid-stroke. A blot of ink spread across the page like a wound.

For eleven years Serah Vane had served the Inquisition of Grayhollow, and in eleven years she had learned that the institution had two faces, and that her work was to wear the presentable one. She had burned people whose worst crime was selling fever-charms to mothers. She had told herself it kept worse things contained. She had gotten very good at not finishing certain thoughts.

Now, watching the border wards—the wards her own order maintained, funded, *worshipped*—pour their light down the mountainside like spilled milk, she found one of those unfinished thoughts completing itself unbidden:

*What were they actually keeping out?*

And its twin, colder: *Or keeping in?*

The door banged open. Junior inquisitor Ostell, young, breathless, already reaching for the comfort of doctrine. "Inquisitor Vane—the wards—the Prelate wants every registered mage accounted for, and sweeps of the unlicensed quarters begun within the hour. He says—his words—he says *someone did this*."

"Someone did this," Serah repeated.

"The stars, ma'am. Stars don't just go out. That's spellcraft. High spellcraft. Somebody in this city is working forbidden arts right under us, and the Prelate wants them found tonight."

Serah looked past him, out the window, at the black holes punched in the sky. Nine stars dead that she could count. Maybe more. And beneath her fear, beneath the old trained reflexes, something in her that had been quietly starving for years lifted its head.

Because she had seen the ward-registry. She had filed the reports. The wards drew their strength from somewhere, and no report she'd ever signed had ever said plainly from where, and tonight the somewhere had come calling, and the Prelate wanted to blame the ragged hedge-crafters of the night market for the death of heaven.

"Ostell," she said slowly. "Who benefits?"

"Ma'am?"

"When something falls, who picks up what it drops? Find me the ledger of ward-maintenance. All of it. The old ledgers, before my time." She picked up her cloak. "I want to see what we've been paying."

"That can wait until—"

"It can't." She was already moving, and if her voice shook slightly, Ostell was too young to recognize why. "Sweep the quarters if you must. Gently. But I'm going to find out what actually broke tonight, and Prelate Corvan can hang me for insubordination later."

Down in the city, the bells were still ringing. Somewhere in Grayhollow, she knew—had known for eleven years and never let herself act on knowing—lived the last person alive who might understand what those broken lights meant. A woman who sold charms that were slightly too well-made. A woman Serah had loved like a sister before the law had made love into evidence.

Serah Vane pulled her hood up and went out into a city with no stars, toward the one door she most dreaded knocking on.

* * *

Wendell got home to find Yara already there, breathless, wild-eyed, Pip hovering in the doorway behind her.

"You're hurt—" Wendell started, hands already checking her, arms, shoulders, that quick inventory of love disguised as scolding—

"I'm fine. Wendell, the wards are *broken*. We saw it from the tower. And the stars—" Yara's voice caught. "They're going out. One by one. Like they're being *taken*."

"I know." Wendell's hands were steady. Her voice was steady. Inside, everything screamed. "Bar the shutters. Light no charms—I mean it, Yara, nothing that glows, nothing that hums. If the Inquisition comes down our street, we are a charm-seller's family and nothing else."

"But what's happening? What does it *mean*?"

And there it was: the question, asked straight and open, the way Yara asked everything, and Wendell felt the whole buried architecture of seven years of silence groan under the weight of the moment. *Tell her,* said something deep and long-starved. *Tell her about the mountain. About the contract. About what's coming due.*

Instead she said, "I don't know," and heard the lie land between them like a dropped plate.

Yara stared at her. Something flickered across the girl's face—not belief, not yet disbelief, but a first small splinter of doubt, sliding in where trust had been seamless. Wendell watched it happen and could do nothing, because the alternative was a truth that would either save her daughter or send her running straight toward the thing that wanted her.

"Hornets' nest," Pip said wisely from the doorway. "My gran always said, when heaven goes quiet, hide under the table till it stops buzzing."

"Sound advice," Wendell managed. "Go home, Pip. Bar your own door. Yara—bed. We'll talk in the morning."

"There won't be a morning like any other morning," Yara said quietly, and went, and the stairs creaked under her like accusation.

Wendell barred the shutters alone, then sat in the dark with her sword wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards, her palm flat against the boards, feeling the faint tremor in the iron. The oaths beneath her breastbone had stopped stirring. Now they strained—toward the mountain, toward the broken light, like compass needles finding north for the first time in seven years.

A knock at the door. Three measured raps. Official.

Wendell's blood went to ice water. She knew that knock. She had hoped, these seven years, never to hear it again, and had known, in the dishonest corner of her heart, that someday she would.

She opened the door on a figure in red-trimmed gray, hood back, candle-guttered lantern held low. Eleven years of office had carved lines around Serah Vane's mouth, but the eyes above them were the same eyes that had shared stolen wine with Wendell on a dormitory roof twenty years ago, before either of them knew what the world would cost them.

"Wendell," Serah said. "We need to talk. And for once in your life, don't lie to me before I've even crossed your threshold."

Behind Wendell, upstairs, a floorboard creaked. The locket's hum drifted down the stairwell, faint and sweet and damning, and Serah's gaze flicked upward, then back, and Wendell saw the exact moment her old friend understood that whatever had come due tonight under the broken sky had been living in this house all along.
