## 4. What the Locket Remembers

The locket hummed the night the stars began to die.

Yara felt it first as a warmth against her collarbone, small and insistent, like a bird pressing its heart against her skin. She had been sitting on the roof of Wendell's shop with her legs dangling over the gutter, watching the sky do the impossible thing—watching stars go out one by one, not falling, not streaking away in bright arcs, but simply *ceasing*, as though someone were pinching out candles in a room too vast to finish darkening.

Below her, Grayhollow was screaming.

She could hear it through the floorboards and the shutters: bells, dogs, voices cracking with fear, the particular wail of a city that had forgotten how to be afraid and was remembering all at once. Somewhere down the street, a woman was shouting about wards. Somewhere farther, someone was shouting about mages. The two shouts tangled together until they sounded like one word, one accusation, and Yara shivered even though the summer night was warm.

*Unregistered.* That was the word the Inquisition used. *Unregistered practitioners.* People who carried magic in their bodies the way other people carried scars. If you were caught, you burned. Everyone in Grayhollow knew what happened at the ash-pits behind the Inquisitor's Hall. Everyone had learned, over seven years, to look at their neighbors carefully and say nothing.

The locket hummed again, harder this time, and Yara pulled it out from under her shirt.

Wendell had given it to her that morning—for her sixteenth birthday, wrapped in brown paper and tied with butcher's string, because Wendell Brightwater did everything practical, even love. It was a small oval of tarnished silver on a thin chain, and inside, where a portrait should have been, there was only a smooth disc of dark glass that reflected nothing. *Charmed against forgetting,* Wendell had said, and her voice had done something strange when she said it, gone flat and careful, like a woman walking on ice. *So you never lose the things that matter.*

Yara had thought it was an odd gift. Sweet, but odd. She'd thanked her anyway, because Wendell's face had been doing that thing it did—the thing where she smiled with her mouth and grieved somewhere else entirely—and Yara had learned, over sixteen years, not to poke at that particular bruise.

But now the locket was humming, and the dark glass disc was no longer reflecting nothing.

It was glowing.

* * *

Light moved beneath the glass like something swimming under lake ice. Yara held the locket up, turning it, and the glow gathered itself into shapes—no, not shapes. *Images.* Tiny ones, pressed flat into the black surface as if the locket were a window into a room much deeper than its size allowed.

She saw a gate.

It stood at the mouth of a mountain—a mountain she somehow knew without knowing how, the way you know the shape of your own hand in the dark. The gate was older than any architecture she'd ever seen in Grayhollow, carved from stone the color of cooling coals, veined with lines of dull orange light that pulsed slowly, patiently, like breathing. Figures stood before it. They were tall and wrong in a way her eyes kept sliding off of, the way you can't look directly at the sun or at grief, and they wore robes stitched with patterns of embers.

And there was a voice. Not heard so much as *remembered*—rising up out of the locket like heat out of a banked fire, low and crackling and almost kind.

*"The payment is received."*

Yara's breath stopped.

The image shifted. Now she saw a table, or something like a table, and a document spread across it—parchment that wasn't parchment, a surface that seemed faintly warm even through the locket's glass, written over in characters that crawled like ants of light. And at the bottom of the document, a signature. A name in a fine, shaking hand, pressed into the burning paper by a woman whose fingers Yara could see trembling at the edge of the vision—a woman with dark hair going gray at the temples, a woman with a cheekbone that Yara saw every single morning in her own reflection.

Her mother's hand. Her mother's signature. Her mother selling—

The vision snapped shut like a door.

The locket went still against her palm, warm as a living thing, and Yara sat on the cold rooftop with the whole dying sky above her and did not move for a long time.

*The payment is received.*

Payment for what? Payment *of* what?

And why—why, why, *why*—did the woman in the vision wear her mother's face?

* * *

She didn't sleep. She tried. She lay in her narrow bed above the charm shop with the locket clutched in one fist and stared at the ceiling, listening to Grayhollow tear itself apart below. Every hour or so another bell would start, and every hour or so the shouting would change key—fear curdling into blame, blame sharpening toward the old familiar target. She'd grown up on stories of the Purge, told in whispers, told in warnings: *keep your head down, don't ask questions, if you see something strange in your neighbor, you never saw it.* Seven years of quiet. Seven years of a city holding its breath.

And now the stars were going out, and everyone knew who would pay for it.

Near dawn, Yara heard Wendell's footsteps on the stairs—heavy, deliberate, the tread of a woman who wanted to be heard coming. Wendell always announced herself that way. Yara had thought, for years, that it was politeness.

Now she wondered if it was performance.

The door opened. Wendell stood in the frame with a candle in one hand and her face arranged into calm, which was its own kind of tell, because Wendell's face was never *arranged* unless something underneath needed hiding.

"You're awake," Wendell said.

"The sky's missing forty stars," Yara said. "Hardly anyone's asleep."

Wendell crossed the room and sat on the edge of the bed, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The candle flame leaned between them. Up close, Yara could see how tired Wendell looked—not just tonight-tired, but a tiredness with years packed into it, sedimented, geological.

"Yara." Wendell set the candle on the sill. "Whatever happens in the next few days—and things are going to happen, I won't lie to you about that—I need you to stay close to the shop. Don't go to the square. Don't answer questions from anyone wearing gray."

"Because they'll be asking about charms." Yara kept her voice level. "You sell charms."

"I sell *harmless* charms," Wendell said, too quickly. "Knots against nightmares. Salt for luck. Nothing that needs registering, nothing that—" She stopped herself. The silence filled the room like water rising.

And Yara opened her fist and showed her the locket.

"It hummed," she said. "Last night. While the stars went out. It showed me things."

Something happened to Wendell's face then—something fast and terrible, a whole war fought and lost in the space between two heartbeats. Yara watched it happen and understood, with the awful clarity that comes only once in a life, that the woman sitting on the edge of her bed had known. Had always known. Had known whatever lived inside the locket, whatever the gate and the voice and the signature meant, for longer than Yara had been alive.

"That's not possible," Wendell said quietly. "I warded it. I warded it *myself.*"

"So you admit there's something to ward against."

"Yara—"

"What is it?" Yara was sitting up now, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated that, hated being sixteen and scared and small in front of the one person she'd never once been afraid of. "What did you put in it? What does it know?"

"It doesn't matter right now—"

"*What does it know?*"

The shout bounced off the low ceiling. Below them, faintly, a door slammed; Mrs. Aldery in the ground floor flat complaining about noise, probably, or maybe not complaining at all, maybe listening. Wendell glanced toward the floor and back, and when she looked at Yara again her eyes had gone wet and furious at the same time, which was such a Wendell expression, so exactly the face of the woman who had bandaged her knees and taught her letters and lied about where babies came from and never once, not ever, let her be hurt—that it nearly undid Yara completely.

"Listen to me," Wendell said, low and fierce. "There are men and women in this city who would burn this building down around us if they heard half of what you're shouting. So you will keep your voice down, and you will keep that locket against your skin, and you will trust me when I tell you that the less you know right now, the safer you are. Do you understand me?"

"Do I understand you?" Yara laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "You gave me a locket that remembers things. You said—this morning—you said *so you never lose the things that matter.* What things, Wendell? Whose things?" She held the locket up between them, and the dark glass caught the candlelight and threw it back in a long orange line, like a coal waking. "I saw a gate in a mountain. I saw a contract. I saw my mother sign it. And the voice called me—"

She couldn't say it. Saying it would make it real.

"*The payment,*" she finished anyway, in a whisper. "It called me the payment. Payment for what?"

For a long moment Wendell said nothing at all. The candle guttered. Down in the streets, the bells kept ringing, and somewhere a crowd was chanting something Yara didn't want to hear clearly.

Then Wendell reached out and took Yara's hand—the hand holding the locket—and closed Yara's fingers back over it, gentle as tucking in a blanket.

"Not tonight," she said. "Please. Not tonight."

And she kissed Yara's forehead, the way she had since Yara was small enough to carry, and left the room, taking the candle with her, closing the door soft as snowfall.

* * *

Yara lay in the dark and did the arithmetic of her whole life.

Sixteen years. Sixteen years of a woman who appeared in Grayhollow the same season a foundling did, who took her in, who raised her with a ferocity of tenderness that Yara had never once questioned because questioning it would have felt like biting a hand that fed her. Sixteen years of *where did I come from?* asked at bedtime, at birthdays, at funerals, whenever some door in her chest rattled—and sixteen years of the same answer, delivered in that flat careful voice: *a bad winter, a mother who couldn't keep you, a story that isn't yours to carry yet.*

*A story that isn't yours to carry yet.*

*Yet.*

The word had always been there. Yara had just never listened for it. And now, lying in the dark with a humming locket against her sternum and a dead sky outside the window, she finally heard it for what it was: a promise. Wendell had promised her the truth someday, in the same breath she'd withheld it, and Yara had accepted the bargain because she'd trusted the woman making it.

She still trusted her. That was the worst part. Some deep, stubborn root of her loved Wendell Brightwater past all sense and all evidence, and no amount of lying would pull that root up. But trust, she was learning tonight, was not the same thing as belief. She believed Wendell loved her. She no longer believed Wendell would tell her the truth unprompted.

Which meant the locket was hers now. Whatever it remembered, whoever's memories those were—her mother's, most likely, sealed into silver by hands that must have touched it last sixteen years ago—it was the only honest witness she had.

The locket pulsed once, softly, as if it heard her thinking.

Yara sat up in the dark. Through the window she could see the mountain—everyone in Grayhollow could see the mountain, the vast sleeping shape on the northern horizon that the old maps named only *the Warden's Rest* and the newer maps didn't name at all. Tonight it stood against a sky with holes punched in it, blacker than the sky around it, and Yara felt the locket lean toward it against her breastbone like a compass needle finding north.

*The payment is received.*

No, she thought. Not yet it hasn't been.

She didn't have a plan. She was sixteen years old, untrained, unknown, armed with a charm against forgetting and a stubbornness her mother had apparently passed down along with her cheekbones. But downstairs, Wendell was awake—Yara could hear the floorboards creaking in the shop below, pacing, pacing, the rhythm of a woman deciding something enormous—and Yara knew, with the same certainty the locket seemed to carry, that whatever Wendell decided, it would involve protecting Yara by keeping things from her. Another winter. Another bedtime answer. Another *yet.*

The bells rang out across the city, three strokes, urgent, and beneath them she heard the crowd in the square take up a new chant, uglier than before, and somewhere in the chant was the word *burning.*

Yara closed her hand around the locket, and made herself a promise that felt older than she was:

*You'll tell me the truth, or I'll find it myself. Either way, I'm done waiting for yet.*

Outside, another star went out.
