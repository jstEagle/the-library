## 5. The Inquisitor at the Door

The knock came at dawn, three measured raps, each one placed like a coin on a dead man's eyes.

Wendell knew before she opened it. There were only two kinds of people who knocked that way in Grayhollow: debt collectors and the Inquisition, and she owed no one money. She stood in the narrow hallway of her shop with her hand flat against the door and breathed once, slow, the way she'd been taught to breathe before a duel a lifetime ago, in an order that no longer existed except in ash and wanted-posters.

Through the wood she could smell cold iron and juniper smoke. The Inquisition's scent. They burned juniper in their censers because it was said to make concealment magic curdle, to make liars' tongues swell. It was nonsense, mostly. Mostly.

"Open the door, Brightwater." The voice was hoarse, like gravel dragged over gravel. "I can hear you standing there."

Seven years since she'd heard that voice say her name. Seven years of rehearsing for this exact moment, and every rehearsal fled her now like sparrows off a wire.

She opened the door.

Serah Vane stood on the step with the gray light behind her, and for one absurd heartbeat Wendell's memory overlaid the woman she'd known onto the woman she saw—the young inquisitor-candidate who used to steal honeycakes from the temple kitchens, who laughed until she snorted, who had sworn beside Wendell beneath the same banner that later burned. That girl had been broad-shouldered and bright-eyed and certain the law could be a kind thing.

The woman on the step was still broad-shouldered. Everything else had gone to iron and fatigue. Her gray coat fit her like armor that had stopped being comfortable years ago and was now simply worn. A censer hung from her belt, unlit. At her hip, instead of the old ceremonial blade, hung the short black baton of the modern Order—a tool for breaking fingers, not blessing them. There were new lines around her mouth, carved deep, as if she'd spent seven years practicing not saying things.

"You look terrible," Wendell said, because her mouth had always outrun her sense.

"And you look like a charm-seller," Serah said. "Which is what worries me."

* * *

The shop was small and deliberately dull: jars of dried lavender, bundles of river-stone, copper charms stamped with harmless sigils—luck-knots, sleep-blessings, little hammered suns for colicky babies. Every piece in the place would pass inspection under a magnifying lens. Wendell had made sure of it the way other merchants made sure their scales balanced. The real work happened upstairs, in a room with no name, behind a wall that was not a wall, where she kept the last of what she was wrapped in oilcloth like a corpse she couldn't bring herself to bury.

Serah walked the aisles slowly, gloved fingers trailing along the shelves, and Wendell followed half a step behind, heart hammering a rhythm she prayed didn't show in her face. Above them, through the floorboards, came the soft thump of Yara moving about in the rooms above—getting dressed, probably arguing silently with her hair. Sixteen today. Sixteen, and the whole city wearing a black sky like mourning clothes, because four nights ago the stars had begun to go out.

That was why the Inquisition was sweeping the market districts. That was why there were bonfires in the square again—not celebratory ones, not yet, but the kindling was stacked and dry and everyone could do the arithmetic.

"Fourteen stars," Serah said, without turning. "Gone in four nights. The astronomers say they didn't fall. They say they were *taken*. Like coins out of a purse." She picked up a luck-knot, turned it over, set it down. "You know what the Grand Inquisitor said from the cathedral steps yesterday?"

"I don't follow the sermons."

"He said the heavens are being stolen by unregistered mages. He said every hidden charm, every secret oath, every coward who's spent years pretending to be ordinary—they're all threads of the same theft." Serah finally turned, and her eyes were the same gray-green they'd always been, which was somehow the worst part. "He said the burning pits will be reopened by month's end."

"The pits never closed," Wendell said quietly. "They just got polite about scheduling."

Something flickered across Serah's face—agreement, maybe, or its ghost—and then was shuttered away. She lifted her chin toward the stairs. "Who lives up there?"

"My daughter."

"You don't have a daughter. You have a lodger. A foundling you took in, per your registration papers, out of charity." Serah recited it flatly, but Wendell caught the question underneath, coiled inside the words like a wire inside a rope. *Why did you take her in, Brightwater? You were never charitable. You were never anything but careful.*

"A person can change," Wendell said.

"Yes," Serah said. "I've heard that happens."

* * *

The inspection should have taken ten minutes. It took two hours, because Serah inspected the way she did everything now—thoroughly, joylessly, leaving nothing unexamined and nothing remarked upon. She checked the seams of the lavender sacks. She held the copper suns up to the window light and looked for the shimmer that betrayed living spellwork beneath dead metal. She asked Wendell to recite the wording on her market license twice, listening not for the words but for the pauses between them.

Wendell lied throughout. She lied about when she'd last traveled outside the city walls. She lied about the source of her river-stones. She lied about knowing anything of swordspeakers, about ever having held an oath-blade, about the scar on her left palm being a kitchen accident rather than the mark of a vow spoken in firelight before forty witnesses, all of whom were dead.

And Serah watched her lie, and Wendell could see her watching, and neither of them said a word about it, which was its own kind of conversation—the oldest one they had.

"You've gotten bad at this," Serah said at last, setting down a jar of salt she'd been weighing in her hand for reasons that had nothing to do with weight.

"At selling charms?"

"At standing still while someone searches you." Serah's voice dropped. "You used to be able to stand in a room full of people hunting your kind and be furniture. I remember. You were the best I ever saw at it. Now you flinch when I touch the third shelf."

"The third shelf is where I keep the expensive stock."

"The third shelf is empty, Wendell. It's been dusted recently and it holds nothing, and you watched my hands the entire time I was near it." Serah exhaled through her nose, long and controlled, a sound like a banked fire deciding not to become a blaze. "I'm not here for the shelf."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because the list I was given has forty names on it, and yours is ninth, and I requested it personally." She said it the way a person admits to a disease. "Because when the wards on the border shattered—did you feel it? Of course you felt it; the whole city felt its teeth click together—when they shattered, the resonance pattern matched something in the old archives. Something from before the Purge. And the archive entry was signed by an order that officially never existed, and one of the names in its roster is *Wendell Brightwater*, oath-bound, rank of speaker, presumed dead."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship tilts. Upstairs, Yara's footsteps crossed the ceiling—unhurried, humming, sixteen years old and alive, and Wendell thought: *not her, whatever comes, not her.*

"I don't know what you're talking about," Wendell said.

"There it is," Serah said softly. "There's the lie. You know what's strange? It's not even a good lie. You used to be magnificent." She looked at Wendell with something that wasn't anger. Anger would have been easier. It was grief, worn smooth as sea glass from years of handling. "What happened to you?"

"You know what happened." The words came out of Wendell before she could stop them, low and rough. "Forty-one pyres in the temple square. I counted. Did you? Or were you looking away like everyone told us to?"

"I was lighting some of them." Serah said it without flinching, and Wendell realized that was its own kind of scar tissue—that she'd made herself able to say it. "That's why I'm telling you this instead of arresting you. Call it interest paid on a debt."

* * *

They ended up in the back room, sitting across from each other at the scarred worktable where Wendell soldered her harmless charms, because Serah had said, *I'm not discussing this on your doorstep*, and because some habits of trust survive even when everything they were built on has burned.

Serah unrolled a leather folio on the table. Inside were papers, and Wendell's stomach dropped when she saw the topmost one, because it was a charcoal rubbing of a seal—a mountain split by a vertical line, crowned in flame. The mark of the Ember Court. The court beneath the mountain, from the oldest stories, the ones mothers told badly-behaved children. The court that had supposedly starved into legend centuries ago.

"This turned up in the wreckage of the border ward-stations," Serah said. "Three days ago. My superiors confiscated all copies within the hour. All copies but mine." She tapped the rubbing. "Do you know what it is?"

Wendell looked at the seal and felt, beneath seven years of practiced calm, something ancient shift its weight—like a door in a deep cellar swinging open on a room she'd nailed shut.

"No," she said, and it was the truest lie she'd told all morning, because she didn't *know*, she only feared, and fear was a different animal.

"Mm." Serah studied her for a long moment. Then, instead of pressing, she drew out a second paper—a list, handwritten, names in columns—and Wendell's blood went to ice water, because halfway down the second column, written in a clerk's careful hand, was a name she knew better than her own.

*Yara. Foundling. Age sixteen. Provenance: unknown. Status: pending retrieval.*

"What is this?" Wendell's voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were using her throat.

"It's a retrieval ledger," Serah said slowly, watching her face. "It appeared in the same file as the seal. It lists thirty names, all foundlings, all born in the year the border wards were raised—all marked *collected* except one." She turned the paper toward herself, and her brow furrowed at whatever she saw in Wendell's expression, because Wendell could feel her own face doing something it had never done in front of another soul: coming apart.

"Wendell." Serah's voice changed—sharper now, the investigator surfacing through the friend. "Look at me. Why does that name matter to you?"

"It doesn't."

"That's the worst lie yet. Your hands are shaking."

Wendell looked down. Her hands were shaking. Seventeen years of discipline, seven years of silence, and a single line of clerk's ink had undone them both.

Upstairs, faintly, Yara laughed at something—probably at her own reflection, probably at nothing, the way sixteen-year-olds laugh on their birthdays when they don't yet know the sky owes a debt against them. The sound came down through the floorboards like water into a drowning room.

"She's my daughter," Wendell said.

The silence stretched. Somewhere out in the street, a crier was shouting the morning edict—curfew moved to sundown, all charm-sellers to present licenses for review, all citizens to report signs of unlawful craft. The city tightening like a fist around its own throat.

"I took her in when she was a day old," Wendell went on, and once the dam cracked the words came muddy and fast. "Her mother was dying. She made me promise. There were—arrangements. Debts. Things I didn't understand then, that I—" She stopped herself with visible effort, jaw working, and dragged a breath in through her teeth. "She's mine. Whatever the ledger says, whoever wrote it, she's *mine*."

Serah sat very still. When she spoke, her voice was quiet enough that Wendell had to lean in to catch it.

"Do you hear yourself? Do you hear what you just admitted? To *me*?" She pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. "Seven years I've wondered if you were alive. Seven years I've told myself that if you were, you'd have been smart enough to stay buried. And now, in one morning, you've confessed to a false registration, harbored an undocumented ward, concealed an oath-identity, and reacted to a classified document like a mother whose child is on a butcher's bill." She looked up, eyes wet and furious and frightened. "I should arrest you. If any other inquisitor had sat in that chair, you'd already be in irons. You know that. You *know* that, and you talked anyway, because you have always, always believed that the truth is a shield. It isn't, Wendell. Here, truth is just the shape of the knife they use."

"Then why haven't you drawn it?"

"Because—" Serah stopped. Her hand went to the folio, to the ledger, to the charcoal seal of a court that should not exist, and Wendell watched her wrestle with something she'd clearly been wrestling alone for a long time. "Because fourteen stars went out and my superiors aren't afraid. Did you notice that? Pyres for the small mages, yes, theater for the crowds—but the men who sign the orders aren't *afraid*. When the sky itself goes dark, honest men are afraid. These men are *busy*." She closed the folio with a snap. "I've been telling myself it's nothing. Discipline. Faith. And then I walked into this shop and watched the best liar I ever knew lie worse than a child, over a name in a ledger, and I thought: *she knows something. And whatever she knows, the Order wants it buried.*"

"Serah—"

"Don't thank me." She stood, fastening the folio under her arm, and for a moment the lamplight caught the lines of her face and Wendell saw how exhausted she truly was—bone-deep, years-deep, the exhaustion of a woman holding up a building she no longer believed in. "I logged this address as cleared. No irregular charms, no violations. It buys you time, not safety. A week, maybe less, before someone cross-checks the ninth name on my list against the census rolls and asks why an inquisitor spent two hours in a charm shop and left empty-handed."

"Why?" Wendell asked. "After everything—why?"

Serah paused at the doorway to the front room. She didn't turn around.

"When we were candidates," she said, "you told me that oaths weren't chains. You said an oath was a rope thrown across a chasm—you hold your end, someone holds theirs, and that's the only way anyone crosses." Her knuckles whitened on the doorframe. "I've been holding my end of a rope attached to nothing for seven years. Let me pretend, for one week, that someone's still holding the other side."

Then she was gone, boots ringing on the cobblestones, and the juniper-and-iron scent of her faded from the doorway like a tide going out.

* * *

Wendell stood in the empty shop for a long time. The morning light crept across the floorboards, touching the shelves, the harmless copper suns, the third shelf with its recent dusting and its nothing-at-all. Out in the square, the kindling stacks stood ready. Fourteen stars missing from a sky that used to hold thousands.

*Pending retrieval.* The words circled her like crows.

She climbed the stairs on legs that felt borrowed. Through Yara's half-open door she could see her—the birthday girl, hair half-pinned and half-abandoned, turning the silver locket over in her fingers, the one Wendell would give her tonight after the cake, the one charmed against forgetting. Against forgetting. Gods. What had she been thinking? Some things deserved forgetting, and some things refused it, and the locket had been drinking in secrets for sixteen years like rain into dry ground.

"Morning," Yara said, grinning. "Is it true the inquisitors searched Old Tam's stall yesterday? Pip says they took his whole basket of dream-herbs and he cried."

"They questioned him and let him go," Wendell said, aiming for lightness and hearing how far short it fell. "Eat something."

Yara looked at her then—really looked, the way she sometimes did, with those eyes the color of banked coals, eyes that had come from somewhere, from someone, from a dying woman's bargain that Wendell had carried alone for sixteen years. Sixteen years of silence, purchased with love, priced daily in guilt. And now a ledger with her daughter's name in it, and an old friend holding her end of a rope across a chasm, and a seal of flame and mountain burned into the backs of Wendell's eyelids every time she blinked.

"You're doing the thing," Yara said. "Where you smile but you're somewhere else. Where is it you go?"

*To the night your mother died,* Wendell thought. *To the road beneath the broken wards. To the contract I helped carry and never read to the end.*

"Nowhere," she said, and heard, in her own voice, exactly what Serah had heard all morning.

That night, after the cake and the locket and Yara's laughter fading into sleep, Wendell went down into the nameless room behind the wall that was not a wall. She unwrapped the oilcloth from the last of what she was, and she sat in the dark holding it, and she began—at last, alone, aloud—to tell herself the truth she'd buried with Yara's mother on the night the wards first sang.

It started with a dying woman's plea, and a gate in the mountain, and a price Wendell had promised never to speak of.

And it ended, she already knew, with a signature she should never have let stand.
