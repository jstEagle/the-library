## 6. Sold Before She Could Speak

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow, and Wendell Brightwater sat in the dark of her own kitchen and let herself remember.

She had spent seven years becoming very good at not remembering. It was a discipline like any other—swordwork, warding, the merchant's smile she wore at the night market. You practiced a thing until it lived in your hands without your permission. Forgetting Yara's mother had been the hardest discipline of all, because forgetting was not a skill but a surrender, and Wendell had never once in her life surrendered gracefully.

But the stars were going out.

She could see it through the window over the sink: the sky above Grayhollow's rooftops, blacker than it should have been, pocked with gaps where last week there had been constellations old enough to have names in three dead languages. The fishwives said it was weather. The priests said it was punishment. The criers in Copperlane Square said, with the flat rehearsed confidence of men who had been told what to say, that the Inquisition had the matter well in hand and that any citizen with knowledge of unregistered spellcraft should come forward immediately.

Wendell knew what it was.

She had known since the first star guttered, because she had watched this happen before—not in the sky, but on paper, in ink gone brown with age, in a room that smelled of blood and mountain cold. And now the thing she had buried was clawing its way up through seven years of careful soil, and it had Yara's face.

So she lit no new candle. She sat in the dark, and she remembered.

* * *

It had been autumn, eight years ago—the autumn after the Purge, when every swordspeaker's oath in the borderlands had been declared heresy and every swordspeaker who hadn't run had burned. Wendell had run. She told herself, then and for years afterward, that running had been strategy. Live, hide, wait for the order to be restored. A sensible lie. She had been good at those too.

She'd been working the far villages then, selling charms so harmless they were barely charms—a knot of red thread against headaches, a sprig of dried rowan hung over doors because people liked the look of it. Nothing the Inquisition's sniffers could catch. She had come to a village called Thistlefen, four houses deep in the marsh country near the old border wards, and there she had found a woman dying in the last house, alone except for a newborn who wouldn't stop crying.

Her name had been Sera Voss. Not Serah—that was the inquisitor, a different woman entirely, though Wendell would spend years flinching at the shared sound—but Sera Voss, a hedge-witch of no particular talent and enormous stubbornness, who had gotten herself with child by a ward-smith who died building the very walls he'd broken his back on, and who had then done something desperate and unforgivable and utterly understandable.

Wendell had knelt beside her pallet and known within thirty seconds what she was looking at. Not the fever. Not the birthing-wound gone bad, though both of those were present. It was the *drain*—the grayish pallor under the skin, the way the woman's shadow seemed thinner than her body, as if something had already begun drinking her down. Soul-debt. Wendell had seen it exactly twice before, on the bodies of gamblers who'd wagered more than they owned in the wrong parlors.

"You've sold something," Wendell said. No preamble. The woman was past the point where gentleness did anything but waste time.

Sera Voss laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up blood-flecked froth that she wiped away with the back of her hand as casually as wiping rain from her cheek. "You're one of them," she said. "A speaker. I can smell iron on you."

"I sell thread knots for headaches."

"You're one of them." Her eyes—dark, fever-bright, absolutely clear beneath the fever—held Wendell's and did not let go. "Good. Then maybe you'll understand what I'm about to tell you instead of calling a priest."

Wendell should have left. That was the discipline, the whole architecture of her survival: see nothing, hear nothing, owe nothing. Every oath-bound ear that heard a confession became evidence; every kindness became a debt that could be tortured out of her later. She had drilled it into herself for two years by then. *Invisible, invisible, invisible.*

Instead she sat down on the stool beside the bed, and the baby screamed, and Sera Voss began to talk.

* * *

"They came in spring," the dying woman said. "Two of them. Grey coats, grey gloves. Inquisition, but not the burning kind—they were the other kind, the polite kind, the ones who carry ledgers instead of torches." She turned her head on the pillow to look at the cradle by the window, where the newborn had finally exhausted itself into red-faced sleep. "They said my husband's work on the western wards had a flaw. His flaw. They said the flaw had cost the crown eleven days of ward-strength during the last storm-tide, and that debts of that size didn't die with the debtor. They passed to family. To blood."

"That's not law," Wendell said quietly. "Ward-work debt isn't inheritable. Even under the new edicts—"

"Even under the new edicts." Sera smiled, and it was the worst smile Wendell had ever seen, because it held no bitterness at all, only a terrible adult understanding of how the world worked. "I know. I read the edicts myself, hoping I'd misread them. But they didn't need law, speaker. They needed me frightened, and I was frightened, and when a woman is frightened enough, law is just a word for whatever the man with the ledger says." She drew a breath that whistled. "They gave me a choice. Well. They called it a choice."

Wendell's hands had closed into fists on her knees without her noticing. Even then—even eight years ago, even before everything—some part of her had understood she was hearing the shape of her own damnation being cut.

"What did they offer?"

"Not offer. Menu." Sera's voice went flat, reciting, and Wendell understood she had recited this before, perhaps to herself in the dark hours, over and over, trying to make it make sense. "Choice one: I sign over my own soul-service. Twenty years bound to the crown's ward-works, my labor and my little magics, until they wear me out. But I'm dying anyway, speaker, so twenty years is worth nothing to them and they know it. Choice two—" her eyes went again to the cradle, and her voice cracked clean through, "—choice two, they take the debt from the child. At birth. Before she can even cry properly. They sell it on. There are buyers, they said, for a soul that's never been used. Clean stock. Unspent."

The kitchen fire popped. Eight years later, in a dark kitchen in Grayhollow, the memory made Wendell's hands shake hard enough that she pressed them flat against the table.

"And you signed," she whispered to the empty room, to the ghost, to the girl asleep upstairs who was sixteen now and asked questions with her mother's dark stubborn eyes.

"I signed," Wendell answered herself, in Sera Voss's voice, because she had heard it enough times in dreams that it lived in her throat now alongside her own. "God forgive me, I signed."

* * *

Because here was the part Wendell had buried deepest, the coal at the center of all that careful soil:

It wasn't just the coercion. Coercion was almost bearable—it had a villain, grey coats and grey gloves, men with ledgers, an institution she could hate cleanly. What Wendell had buried was what came after the signing. What Sera Voss had said next, with her last hour of strength, while the baby slept and the marsh wind moaned through the shutters.

"The buyer wasn't the crown," Sera had said. "I thought it would be the crown. Ward-debt, crown-ledgers—I thought my daughter would grow up in some government nursery, sewing ward-sigils with her soul stitched to the work. That's what they let me think. But the paper they brought me the second day—" she fumbled weakly at the blanket, as if she could still pull the document from its folds, "—it wasn't crown vellum. It was ember-red. And the seal wasn't the king's tower. It was a mountain."

Wendell had gone cold. Everyone in the borderlands knew the mountain stories. Children's tales, mostly—the court beneath the world, the Ember Queen who bought what grief put up for sale. Old wives' warnings: *don't bargain on the mountain road, don't sing where the stones are warm.* Nobody believed. Believing was for grandmothers.

"It's real," Sera Voss had said, watching Wendell's face drain. "You know it's real. I saw your face change. Good. Then hear the rest, because I don't have long and I need someone to know. They wanted passage. That's what the grey-coats wanted—for themselves, or their masters, I couldn't tell. Passage through the western wards, unmarked, unmetered, for something coming south. And the mountain holds the old keys. Older than the wards themselves. So the grey-coats brokered. They took my daughter's contract to the mountain, and the mountain paid them in what they actually wanted—a door opened—and the mountain keeps the payment." Her breath caught, held, released. "My baby isn't debt anymore, speaker. She's *bought*. There's a signature under mine on that paper, and it isn't human handwriting, and someday—someday when she's grown, because that's how those things work, they always wait until the soul is ripe—the mountain will come to collect."

"Why are you telling me?" Wendell had asked, and hated the question even as it left her mouth, because she already knew the answer, and knowing it would ruin her life.

"Because I'm dying, and she needs someone." Sera Voss reached out and caught Wendell's wrist with fingers light as ash. "Not a guardian. Not a hero. I know what you speakers are—you burn bright and get everyone around you killed, and the order's dead anyway. I just need someone ordinary. Someone who can hide. Hide her, hide yourself, keep her small and quiet and *unripe*, if hiding slows them. Buy her years. However many you can steal." The grip tightened, the last of her strength flowing into it. "And when she's old enough to ask—and she will ask, she has my jaw and my stubbornness—tell her the truth. All of it. Swear it."

"I don't swear anything anymore," Wendell had said. "Swearing is what got us all burned."

Sera Voss had smiled that terrible smile one last time. "Then don't swear it. Just *do* it. That's harder anyway."

She was dead before dawn. The baby cried once in the night and Wendell, who had not slept, who had sat all night with her back against the door like a sentry at a gate, picked her up and walked out of Thistlefen before sunrise and did not stop walking for nine days.

* * *

That was the confession. That was the coal. Wendell sat in her kitchen in Grayhollow, eight years and one catastrophe later, and admitted it to herself in plain words because plain words were the only coin the situation accepted anymore:

*I knew. From the first night. I knew Yara was sold—not apprenticed, not fostered, not orphaned into my care by luck. Sold. Her soul signed over to the Ember Court by men with ledgers, to buy a door through the walls we all huddle behind. And I took her anyway, and I hid her, and I told myself for seven years that hiding her was saving her.*

*And it was a lie.*

Not the whole lie. That was the poison of it—the best lies are mostly true. Hiding had kept her alive. If Wendell had marched to the Inquisition with Sera Voss's testimony, Yara would have become evidence, and evidence burned. If Wendell had hunted the grey-coated brokers, she'd have died in an alley with her sword rusted in its sheath and Yara collected regardless. Every year of silence had purchased real safety. She could stack the reasons like cordwood and they'd reach the ceiling.

But underneath the cordwood, at the bottom, was the truth she'd never let herself touch: hiding Yara had also hidden *Wendell*. Keeping the secret had meant never having to look at it. Never having to kneel in front of a dark-eyed girl and say *you are property, and I helped make you invisible to protect myself as much as you.* Silence was comfortable. Silence was a house you could live in. And Wendell Brightwater, who had once sworn oaths in iron and firelight, had chosen the comfortable house for seven years and called it love.

The locket. God help her, the locket. Last night—Yara's sixteenth birthday, the exact anniversary of the sale's ripening, because of course Wendell had never once marked the true date aloud—she had fastened that charm against forgetting around the girl's neck and called it a gift. A protection. And all the while her hands had known what her mouth refused to say: that the locket was a lock on a door Wendell was too cowardly to open herself. That she had handed her daughter the key to her own history and prayed she'd never turn it.

The stars were going out because the ripening was done. Nineteen years of interest on a soul, come due. Somewhere beneath the mountain, something had finished counting, and the ledger was balancing itself across the sky, star by star, and every extinguished light was a page turning toward Yara's name.

Wendell stood up so fast the stool went over behind her.

She stood in the middle of her dark kitchen, in the city that burned people like her, and felt shame transmute—not into peace, she was far too angry for peace, and the anger was aimed squarest at herself—but into something with edges. Resolve. The old iron feeling, the one she'd thought rusted out of her forever. It moved through her chest like a blade being drawn slowly, painfully, from a scabbard sealed with seven years of tar and silence, and it hurt, and she welcomed the hurt, because the hurt meant the blade was still steel underneath.

No more hiding. No more cordwood of reasons. No more comfortable house.

Tomorrow—no, tonight, tonight, before the sky lost another star—she would wake Yara and tell her everything. The grey coats. The ember-red paper. The mountain. The whole ugly architecture of it, kneeling if she had to, taking whatever the girl's face did when she heard it, because Sera Voss had asked one thing with her dying grip and Wendell had owed that debt longer than any other she carried. *Tell her the truth. All of it.*

She was halfway to the stairs when she heard the front door below swing open—softly, expertly, the way it only did when someone didn't want to wake the house—and the bottom dropped out of her stomach.

She took the stairs three at a time.

Yara's bed was empty, covers thrown back, the window latched open to the starless dark. On the pillow lay a scrap of paper in her quick slanting hand, and beside the note, the space where the locket had lain was bare.

*Don't follow me,* the note said. *If they trace it, it should lead to me, not you. You've hidden long enough. I read the locket. I know about the mountain. I'm going to give it back.*

Wendell stood holding the paper with the wind off the starless sky moving through the open window, and somewhere north beneath the black horizon, a mountain waited with a door that had already been paid for.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the dark, to the mountain itself. "Absolutely not."

She was already reaching for her boots.
