## 7. Running Toward the Mountain

The locket woke before I did.

I felt it against my sternum like a second heartbeat—warm, insistent, pulling. Not the way a compass pulls, which is patient and indifferent. This was the way a hook pulls, the way a fish feels the line go taut and understands, in its small doomed way, that something on the other end has decided.

I sat up in the dark of my room above the charm shop and pressed my palm flat over it. Through the wall I could hear Wendell moving around downstairs. She thought I couldn't hear her at this hour. She thought a lot of things she was wrong about.

She'd been crying. Not loudly—Wendell never did anything loudly—but the floorboards told me everything. The particular drag of her steps when she carried something heavy that wasn't made of anything you could put down. She'd been crying since the night the stars went out, and last night she had looked at me across the supper table like I was already a grave she was standing beside, and she'd said, *Eat your stew, love,* in a voice cracked clean through the middle.

She knew what was happening to me. She had known for sixteen years.

I dressed in the dark. Boots, cloak, the knife Wendell gave me last spring with instructions never to use it unless I meant it. I took the bread she'd baked two days ago, the cheese, the little flask of plum brandy she kept for customers who needed their hands steadied before they bought grief-charms. I left coin on the counter—not all of it, but enough that she'd know I wasn't stealing, that this was different from running away, that this was *paying my way* toward something.

Then I stood in the doorway of her room and watched her sleep.

She slept badly these days, one hand always curled near the headboard where her old sword used to hang, back before she melted it down and sold the hilt and told me the story about how swordspeakers were just a fable soldiers invented to make long marches interesting. Her face in the lamplight from the street was older than it had been a week ago. Older than it had been yesterday. Whatever the sky's going dark had done to Grayhollow, it had done something worse to her.

*I'm doing this for you,* I told her silently, and hated how much it sounded like the things people say right before they do something unforgivable. *You lied to keep me safe. I'm leaving so your lie doesn't get us both burned.*

The locket pulled. I went.

* * *

Grayhollow at three in the morning is a city pretending to be asleep, which is not the same as sleeping. I kept to the tanner's row where the smell covers everything, including footsteps, and slipped out through the corpse gate—the little postern by the rendering yard that the watch never locks because no one wants to be the one assigned to guard it.

The wards were still broken. That was the thing nobody in the city could stop talking about: the border wards, the great invisible fence that had stood since before anyone's grandmother, shattered the same night the stars began dying, and now the countryside beyond Grayhollow was just—open. Like a house with its doors torn off. Like a mouth.

I'd never been outside the walls. Sixteen years inside a city that outlawed magic, and the farthest I'd ever walked was the orchards at Midsummer, holding Wendell's hand, both of us pretending we weren't scanning the tree lines for Inquisition riders.

Now there were no riders. There was nothing. The road north out of Grayhollow unspooled through dead wheat fields under a sky with four stars left in it—four, down from five when I'd fallen asleep—and every one of them flickered like a candle in a draft.

The locket pulled north. Of course it did. Everything in the stories pulled north, toward the mountain, toward the Ember Court that Wendell swore up and down was another fable, another soldiers' invention. Except the locket didn't swear anything. The locket just hummed against my skin and showed me things when I slept: a gate of black stone veined with red, a voice like coals settling in a grate, a line of ink in a language I somehow understood anyway—*the bearer of this debt being the issue of one Marisol Vey, sold in her first hour, payment due upon her sixteenth year.*

My sixteenth birthday was eleven days ago.

Four stars. Nineteen days, the locket whispered, though lockets don't whisper, so I suppose what I mean is that I knew it the way you know your own name, without anyone having to say it. Nineteen days until the last star died, and whatever happened to the last star happened to me.

So I walked. And because walking alone at night with your whole life in a satchel leaves too much room for thinking, I made myself a plan, and then I made the plan better, and by the time the sky went grey I had convinced myself completely.

Here was the plan: I would reach the mountain. I would present myself. Whatever bargain had been struck with my mother—whoever she was, wherever she was, if she was even alive—I would renegotiate it. Courts loved ceremony; everyone knew that. Courts loved a debtor who came willingly, head high, terms in hand. I would offer them something else. I didn't know what yet. Time, maybe. Service. A story worth more than a soul. I was good at stories; Wendell had raised me on them, had filled my head with them every night of my childhood, heroes and tricksters and girls who talked their way out of devils' bargains.

And Wendell—this was the important part, this was the part I turned over and over like a stone in my pocket—Wendell would be safe. Because the Inquisitor had come to our door. I'd seen her through the curtain, the tall woman with the tired eyes and the ledger, and I'd heard Wendell lie to her, badly, terribly, the worst liar in Grayhollow trying to explain away charms that any fool could feel humming. The only reason Serah Vane had walked out without irons was that they'd been friends once, before. Before what, Wendell wouldn't say. But friendships rot, and ledgers fill, and next time there would be no warning, just fire.

They wanted an unregistered mage. They wanted someone to burn for the falling stars, because cities are like that—when the sky breaks, they need a body for it. If I was gone, Wendell's shop would just be a shop. Her charms would be trinkets. The girl with the strange locket and the stranger eyes would be a rumor that walked north and out of everyone's jurisdiction.

I was saving her. That was the shape I gave it, and I held it up to the light of the rising sun and found that it looked almost true.

Almost. There was a splinter in it I tried not to touch: the fact that she'd lied. Sixteen years of lies. *You were a foundling left on the temple steps.* *Your mother's name is lost, love; foundlings' names usually are.* *No one comes looking for foundlings, that's the mercy of it.* Every answer smooth as river stone, worn perfect by repetition. And the whole time there'd been a contract with my name on it buried somewhere in her chest like a swallowed coal.

I touched the splinter anyway, walking, because you always do.

Maybe she'd been protecting me. Probably she'd been protecting herself. Both things could be true at once—that was the lesson of my sixteenth year, arriving early, arriving hard. She'd hidden her own magic for seven years behind a merchant's smile, so hiding was what she knew. Hiding was her native language. Of course she'd raised me fluent in it.

Well. I was done speaking it.

* * *

I made thirty miles the first day, which I know because Brannoc—the ward-smith who sometimes drank with Wendell and once let me hold his measuring chain while he grumbled about the city walls—once told me a man walks twenty and a fool walks thirty, and I have never once in my life been mistaken for a man.

The land changed fast once Grayhollow fell behind. The wheat gave way to moor, the moor to stands of ash trees with bark the color of old bone. Bell-flowers grew along the roadside—the ash bells, Wendell called them once, in a voice that had gone somewhere else entirely—and they didn't ring in the wind. They rang when something magical passed. I learned this at dusk on the first day, when a hare crossed my path and the nearest bell gave a single clear note like a dropped spoon.

When *I* passed, they rang in chorus.

I stood very still among them, listening to forty bells singing my arrival to the empty hills, and understood two things at once. First: whatever I was, whatever the contract had made of me before I drew my first breath, it was loud. Louder than the hare. Louder, probably, than Wendell with all her buried oaths. Second: I could not hide. Not from the bells, not from the mountain, not from whatever listened on the wind between them.

Good, I decided. Let them hear me coming. Debtors who sneak are debtors who doubt.

That night I climbed into the hollow of a lightning-split oak, ate half the bread, wrapped myself in the cloak, and held the locket up to the four remaining stars.

It showed me the gate again. Closer this time—or I was closer, which amounted to the same thing. Black stone, red veins, and carved above the lintel in letters that glowed like banked coals: words I could almost read, almost, the way you can almost remember a dream. And beneath the gate, faint as breath on glass, a figure waiting. Tall. Patient. Its face was a kindness, and the kindness was the most frightening thing I had ever seen, because it was exactly the wrong shape—it fit like a mask fits, covering something underneath.

I woke with my heart slamming and my hand full of locket and dawn coming up thin and cold through the branches.

Two hundred miles to the mountain, if the map stitched inside my satchel flap was honest, and I'd stolen it from Pip's stall fair and square, or at least fairly enough for a thief's map. Four days' walk for a fool. Three, if I didn't sleep.

Behind me, somewhere past the horizon, Wendell was waking up to an empty bed and a counter full of coins, and I made myself imagine her face, made myself carry it like a lantern. Anger first. Then fear. Then—the part I clung to—the slow dawning understanding. *She'll know why,* I told the ash trees. *She'll know I did it to save her. It's what she taught me. You protect the people you love, even from themselves. Even from yourself.*

Even, said a small cold voice that sounded unpleasantly like mine, by lying to them. By choosing for them. By deciding what they can bear without asking.

I shut the voice up with walking.

* * *

On the third day I met the first empty village.

It wasn't destroyed. That was what unsettled me—there was no ruin in it, no burn marks, no broken doors. The village of Harrow's Bend, according to the leaning signpost, simply had no one in it. Doors stood open. A cradle rocked in a draft with no baby in it. Soup had gone moldy in a pot on a stove whose fire had long since died. On the well-house door, someone had nailed a paper that read, in a shaking hand: GONE TO THE CITY. THE BELLS WON'T STOP RINGING. SOMETHING IS COMING NORTH AND THE WARDS ARE DOWN AND WE WON'T BE HERE WHEN IT ARRIVES.

Something is coming north. I looked at the paper a long time. Then I looked at my own hands.

I hadn't thought of myself as the something. I'd thought of myself as the answer, the negotiator, the girl who walks into the devil's parlor and talks everyone home. But the bells sang for me across forty miles of moorland, and villages emptied themselves ahead of me like water ahead of a plow, and the locket pulled, and the fourth star from the east guttered while I watched it, right there at midday, dimming from silver to grey to a smudge like breath wiped off glass.

Three left. Eighteen days.

I sat down on the village well and let myself, for exactly one minute, be afraid. I timed it by the drip of the bucket-chain. One minute of shaking hands and burning eyes and the wild animal certainty that I had made a catastrophic mistake and should turn around, run home, throw myself at Wendell's feet, confess everything, let her decide, because she was the adult, she was the swordspeaker, she was—

She was a woman who had spent seven years teaching her own face to lie, and who had looked at me over supper and called me *love* like a farewell. She wasn't coming after me. I'd known that since the corpse gate. People who spend their lives hiding don't chase; chasing is exposure, exposure is fire. She would grieve. She would light candles. She would tell herself I was saving her, and hate me a little for making her believe it, and hate herself more for believing it.

Unless.

Unless the lying stopped working on both of us at the same moment. Unless she was, right now, at this very hour, doing the one thing I had never once seen her do—choosing someone over her own safety. Coming north. Breaking her beautiful invisibility wide open for me.

I didn't believe it. But I wanted to believe it so badly that the wanting felt like a kind of proof, and I have learned since that this is exactly the arithmetic fools run on, wanting divided by fear equaling hope, and it always comes out wrong.

One minute ended. I stood up. I filled my flask at the well, took nothing else from Harrow's Bend because the village felt like a held breath I had no right to exhale, and walked on north, and the ash bells rang me through it, mile after mile, announcing me to the mountains.

* * *

The mountain appeared at sunset on the fifth day, and it was wrong in a way I couldn't immediately name.

It wasn't that it was large, though it was—a black shoulder rising out of the moor like something surfacing. It was that the land bent toward it. Rivers ran uphill at its base. Clouds circled it instead of passing over. The ash bells grew denser as I climbed the last ridge, thousands of them, tens of thousands, ringing continuously now, one long chord that wasn't a sound so much as a pressure, and I realized with a lurch that they weren't announcing me anymore.

They'd been announcing me for five days. Now they were welcoming me home.

*Home.* The word arrived in my head wearing someone else's voice—warm, coal-edged, patient—and I knew, the way I'd known the nineteen days, that it belonged to whatever waited behind the gate of my visions. The court beneath. The thing the contract fed. It knew I was close. It had known since the corpse gate, probably since the night I was born, since my mother's trembling signature dried on the page.

And here is what frightens me most, looking back: part of me leaned into that voice. Part of me heard *home* and felt the pull double, treble, felt my feet quicken on the scree like a dog hearing its name. Sixteen years of borrowed time in a borrowed city with a borrowed name—Yara Brightwater, foundling, daughter-of-no-one—and here, finally, was a door with my true name carved over it. Whatever it cost. Whoever I'd been sold by. Sold *to* felt, in that moment, horribly like belonging.

That's how they get you. I understand that now. Not with chains—with welcome.

I crested the ridge as the last light died and there it was: the gate. Black stone, red-veined, exactly as the locket had shown me, except that the locket had flattered it. It was vast. The lintel stood fifty feet high, and the coalscript above it burned steady and bright, and I could read it now, every word, in a language I had never been taught:

*ALL DEBTS COME HOME.*

Below the gate, in the blue hour between sunset and dark, stood the herald. Tall. Patient. Its face was a kindness shaped like a mask, and when it saw me it smiled, and the smile reached all the way to its eyes, which was worse than if it hadn't.

"Yara," it said, and my mother's name for me—my only inheritance—sounded in its mouth like a key turning. "Sixteen years, and you came the last hundred miles on your own feet. The Court is so pleased. We do love a payment that arrives *willingly*."

The word landed like ice water. Payment. Not guest. Not daughter. Not home.

But the gate was opening, and the warmth pouring out of it smelled like woodsmoke and bread and every winter evening I'd ever loved, and the herald extended its hand, and I—fool, thirty-mile fool, girl made of other people's bargains—took a step forward.

Far behind me, down the long dark road I'd walked, the ash bells began to ring in a new rhythm. Not my rhythm. Faster. Urgent. Many-footed, many-horsed, coming north at a pace no lone walker could match.

Someone was following me after all.

The herald heard it too. Its smile thinned by a single degree. "Ah," it said softly, into the ringing dark. "It seems the seller has come to inspect the goods."
