## 8. The Crew Nobody Wants

The morning after the stars went out, Wendell Brightwater stood in the ruins of her night market stall and took inventory of what was left of her life.

The charm table had been overturned—by looters or by fear, she couldn't tell which, and it didn't matter. Her little glass vials lay shattered in their straw, the harmless tinctures bleeding color into the mud: rose for calm, salt for sleep, iron filings that were only ever just iron filings. Seven years of careful, deliberate harmlessness scattered in a puddle. Above the market square, Grayhollow's bell tower tolled the alarm hour over and over, as if repetition could ring the sky back into shape.

Yara had been gone since dawn.

Not gone gone. Wendell checked the thought like checking a wound for depth. Gone to the loft above the dye-shop where they lived, packing a bag with the grim efficiency Wendell had pretended not to notice through the wall all night. Gone because Yara had looked at her across the wreckage of last evening—the black sky, the screaming, the Inquisition's white hoods already moving through the crowd—and said, in a voice Wendell didn't recognize, *You know something. Don't lie to me.*

And Wendell had lied to her. Badly. Again.

So now there was nothing left but the plan she'd sworn she'd never need: find people willing to walk into the Ember Court's mouth, and pray that the kind of person willing to do that was also the kind who wouldn't sell them both to the Inquisition by nightfall.

She pulled her coat tight and went looking for Brannoc Hale.

* * *

He still worked the old foundry district, past the tanners' row where the smell alone kept the Inquisition's patrols scarce. The forge at the end of Cinder Lane hadn't held a license in eleven years; its chimney smoked anyway, thin and gray, on the days when a man needed his hands to remember what they used to make.

Wendell knocked the way you knock on a door that might not open: twice, then stopped.

"Go away," said a voice like gravel settling. "Whatever it is, I don't fix it anymore."

"It's a ward, Brannoc."

Silence. Then the scrape of a bar, and the door swung wide enough to show half a face—one eye brown and sharp, the rest of him hidden behind a beard gone more ash than black. Brannoc Hale had been the finest ward-smith in the border provinces before the Purge, before the tribunal, before whatever happened in that courtroom that no one would speak about. Now he made hinges. Cheap ones, badly.

"Wendell Brightwater." He said her name like he was testing whether it was still load-bearing. "I heard you died."

"I let people think so."

"Mm." His eye flicked past her shoulder, down the empty lane, cataloguing threats out of habit. "You're here about the sky."

"I'm here about the wards."

"They're the same question." He opened the door wider—a surrender disguised as hospitality—and she stepped into heat and shadow. The forge was cold, but the walls weren't. She felt it the moment she crossed the threshold: old wardwork humming in the stone, dead circuits and living ones tangled together like roots around a coffin. A man didn't stop being a smith just because they took his name off the rolls.

Brannoc poured her tea from a kettle crusted with years. It tasted like iron and patience.

"The border wards fell last night," Wendell said. "All of them. Not breached—fell. Like someone cut the threads."

"No one cut anything." He set his cup down without drinking. "I've been listening to the stones all night. The wards didn't fail, Brightwater. They were *spent*. There was power banked in those foundations—old power, laid down when the wards were first raised, before either of us could walk. Something's been drawing on it. Drawing hard. Last night it drew the last of it."

"Drawing for what?"

"That," Brannoc said, "depends on what's under the mountain. And I'll tell you right now, woman, I know exactly what's under the mountain, and I have spent eleven years praying I was wrong."

The fire pit sat between them, unlit. Wendell watched the cold coals and made herself say the next part plainly, because Brannoc had never once in his life tolerated anything else.

"My daughter was sold to the Ember Court. At birth. The contract comes due while the stars are falling. When the last one goes out—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through it. "When the last one goes out, they take her soul. Nineteen days, maybe fewer. I'm going into the mountain after her, and I need someone who knows how the wards were built, because I think the way in runs through their bones."

Brannoc laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry and cracked, like a door opening on an empty house.

"You want to go *down*." He shook his head. "I helped build the outer lattice when I was young enough to believe it was holy work. You know what we mortared those foundations with? What the old guilds fed into the deep anchors?" His eye fixed on her, and behind the weariness in it was something older and much worse. "I know what's under there because I helped pay for it. And I swore I'd never go back."

"Then don't come for me," Wendell said. "Come for the ledger. Every contract has one. If your brother—if what happened to your brother is written anywhere, it's written down there."

She saw it land. She hated herself a little for aiming there, and did it anyway, because nineteen days was not a generous budget for decency.

Brannoc stood. Walked to the window. The gray light came down Cinder Lane and made him look carved from something that had been left out too long.

"If I do this," he said finally, "we do it my way through the deep passages. No oaths spoken aloud—not yours, especially not yours. Swordspeaker words echo strange underground. And if at any point I tell you to run, you run, and you leave me, and you don't look back, because I am not going to be anyone's reason to die twice."

"Agreed," Wendell said.

"And Brightwater." He turned. The eye was wet, and furious about it. "If the girl dies anyway—all this, the whole ugly descent, every debt we drag up out of that dark—I will spend what's left of my life making sure the world knows whose fault it was. Including yours."

"Fair," said Wendell, and meant it, and picked up her cup with a hand that almost didn't shake.

* * *

Pip was easier to find and harder to convince, mostly because Pip refused to stay found.

Wendell tracked the thief through three false names and two borrowed coats across the lower city, following rumors the way you follow smoke—always knowing the source had already moved. She finally cornered her in the chandlery off Rope Walk at midday, where a small figure in a boy's cap was lifting candle-wicks with the serene entitlement of a tax collector.

"You're slow," Pip said, without turning around. "Been watching you blunder up and down Rope Walk since the fish stalls. Thought I'd give you another quarter hour out of pity."

"Pip."

"Thief-taker's getting better," Pip conceded, turning. Under the cap, the face was younger than the eyes—nineteen, maybe twenty, sharp-cheeked and freckled, with the particular stillness of someone who listened to everything. Everyone in Grayhollow's underworld knew Pip could hear locks: the tumblers whispering, the bolts muttering, the whole secret grammar of doors. No one knew how. Pip wasn't saying, and the Inquisition had never managed to make anyone say.

"I need a door opened," Wendell said. "Under the mountain."

"Everyone needs a door opened. That's why doors exist—to be needed against." Pip bit the end off an apple she absolutely had not paid for. "Why would I go near that place? Sky goes dark and suddenly everyone remembers the mountain exists. Bad time to be interesting."

"The pay is whatever we find."

"The pay is always whatever we find. That's thief-talk for *nothing*." But Pip had stopped chewing. "Say the true thing, merchant. You've got a liar's face today and I don't work for liars."

Wendell breathed out. "My daughter's soul is collateral on a contract with the Ember Court. I have nineteen days to steal it back."

The apple lowered slowly. For a long moment Pip just looked at her, and Wendell had the disorienting sense of being *listened to*—not her words, but the lock of her, clicked through and tested.

"Huh," Pip said at last. "That's a real thing. That's a real, actual, horrible thing." The apple went into a pocket. "Right. Here's my problem, Brightwater. I hear things. Doors, locks, chains—all of it talks, if you've got ears for it. And last night? After the stars went out?" Pip leaned close, voice dropping to almost nothing. "Everything in this city started talking at once. Every vault, every strongbox, every sealed archive in Grayhollow woke up like a kicked hive. Something *down there* called, and every lock in the city answered. I couldn't sleep for the noise of it."

"So you'll come?"

"I'll come," Pip said, "because I want to know what said hello. Also because you'll die without me, obviously. Also—" and here Pip's grin surfaced, quick and crooked and entirely without comfort, "—because if we're robbing the Ember Court, I want it known that Pip Quillfinger robbed the Ember Court. Write that on my stone. Small stone. I'm not fussy."

* * *

Marisol Vey was the hardest ask, and Wendell saved her for last because of it.

The hedge-witch kept a room above a dyer's shed in Tallow Quarter, behind three locks and a curtain of dried rue, and she answered Wendell's knock with a crossbow bolt through the doorframe—deliberately wide, deliberately a warning.

"It's Wendell."

"I know who it is." Another voice, closer now, through the wood. "I heard you coming up three flights. Your boots are lying about your weight, Brightwater, and your charms are lying about everything else. Come in before the neighbors start counting knocks."

Inside, the room was a storm of hanging bundles and copper pots, and Marisol herself was a storm to match—sixty-odd years of exile worn like armor, gray hair hacked short, forearms bare and inked with sigils that predated the Purge by a generation. She'd been a healer of the old school, the kind that mixed prayer and poultice and never saw a difference, until the Inquisition burned her sister at the Ash Rite and exiled her from three provinces in a single season. She'd settled in Grayhollow the way a knife settles in a wound.

"You've got the look," Marisol said, pouring something amber into two cups without asking. "The look of a woman about to say a stupid thing bravely."

"The border wards are dead. My daughter belongs to the court beneath the mountain. I'm going down after her."

Marisol drank. Considered. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"How many fools have you collected so far?"

"A disgraced ward-smith and a lock-hearing thief."

"A *ward-smith*." Marisol's laugh had no humor in it, only history. "Half the reason my sister burned is that the ward-smiths built the registry that named her. And now you bring me one of them." She set the cup down. "No."

"Marisol—"

"No. Listen to me, Brightwater, because I like you, which is rare and expensive. You're asking me to crawl toward the Inquisition's favorite horror story wearing a team I don't trust, to fight a court I can't see, for a girl I've met twice." Her eyes were flint. "Give me one reason."

Wendell had prepared arguments on the walk over. Contracts, leverage, Marisol's grudge, the promise of records that would burn the Inquisition's name off every province map. She opened her mouth to deploy them.

What came out instead was the truth.

"Because I've been hiding for seven years," Wendell said quietly. "Seven years of harmless charms and a merchant's smile and pretending the order I served never existed. And last night the sky went black and my daughter asked me if I was lying to her, and I lied to her, and I watched her decide to stop trusting me. That's what hiding buys, Marisol. It doesn't buy safety. It buys a smaller and smaller room until one day you look around and realize you built the cell yourself." She met the old woman's stare. "I'm done hiding. I'd rather be ruined loudly than safe silently. And I think—maybe wrongly, but I think—you've been waiting seven years for someone to say that out loud where you could hear it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the dyer's shed coughed steam, and the rue bundles stirred in a draft that had no business existing indoors.

"You're a dangerous woman, Wendell Brightwater," Marisol said at last. "Not because of the sword-magic everyone whispers about. Because you talk like that and mean it." She reached up and pulled a traveling cloak from a peg, slinging it over her shoulders with the finality of a verdict. "Fine. I'm in. But understand the terms. If we get within arm's reach of any Inquisitor—any at all—I get to be the one who decides whether we run or we burn. I've earned that. I've been running for thirty years and I am *tired* of it being the only verb I own."

"Agreed."

"And if your girl makes it home," Marisol added, shouldering past her to the door, "she learns proper hedge-craft. None of this swordspeak mysticism. A child should know how to mend a fever and curse a bad harvest. Useful magic. Kind magic."

"She'd like that," Wendell said, and found, to her surprise, that it was true.

* * *

They met at midnight in the shell of the old ferry-house by the Gray, where the river ran shallow and the Inquisition's patrols thinned to nothing. Four people around a broken table: a disgraced smith sharpening tools that hadn't seen honest work in a decade, a thief eating someone else's bread, a hedge-witch laying out dried herbs with surgical contempt, and Wendell, holding a stub of charcoal over a scrap of sailcloth, trying to draw the mountain's approaches from a memory seven years stale.

It should have been a war council. It felt, absurdly, like a kitchen.

"—all I'm saying," Pip was insisting, mouth full, "is that if the Ember Queen has a treasure room, and statistically she must, then the mission has *two* objectives and I'm only contracted for the honorable one—"

"You're not contracted at all," Brannoc rumbled. "You volunteered. I watched you do it. There may be witnesses."

"Volunteering is just theft with extra steps."

"Children," Marisol said, without looking up from her herbs, "both of you are going to die in the dark unless you learn to be quiet in it. Practice now."

And Wendell looked around the broken table—at the man building her a road through the bones of his own guilt, at the kid who heard the world's locked doors talking, at the woman who'd chosen fury over fear for thirty years—and felt something crack open in her chest that she didn't have a name for. Not hope exactly. Hope was clean, and this was jagged, reluctant, embarrassed by itself. It was the feeling of a family assembling out of spare parts: no blood shared, no oath binding, nothing held together except the decision to keep showing up.

She'd had a family like that once. The order had called it a brotherhood and buried it in ash.

This one she would keep.

"Here," she said, tapping the sailcloth. "The mountain's western flank. The original ward-lattice ran through the rock itself—Brannoc, the anchor-points, are they climbable?"

"Some of them. The third and seventh. The others are—" he paused, choosing the word with a craftsman's care, "—occupied."

"Occupied," Pip repeated. "By what?"

"By what the contracts bought," Marisol said flatly, and the table went quiet, because they all understood her, and none of them wanted to.

Wendell rolled up the sailcloth. Nineteen days, maybe fewer. One disgraced smith, one thief, one hedge-witch, one rusted oath, and a lie she owed her daughter that was growing heavier with every mile.

"We leave before the bell," she said. "Travel dark, move in pairs, and nobody speaks my name past the tree line. From tonight I'm just a merchant with a very bad delivery route."

Pip snorted. Brannoc snuffed the lamp. Marisol pocketed her herbs like a woman loading a weapon.

And as they slipped out of the ferry-house into a starless dark that pressed close as water, Wendell felt it—a faint, insistent pull, low in her chest, like a hook set behind the breastbone. The same direction. Always the same direction.

West. Toward the mountain.

Toward Yara, walking the same road ahead of her, alone, believing she was saving them both.

"Faster," Wendell whispered to the dark, and broke into a run.
