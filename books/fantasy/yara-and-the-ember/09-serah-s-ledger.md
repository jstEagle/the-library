## 9. Serah's Ledger

The ledgers of the Inquisition were kept in a room with no windows, on the theory that paper was a thing that preferred the dark.

Serah Vane had believed that once. She had believed it for nineteen years, through her novitiate and her trials and the long gray years after, when she'd learned to write *condemned* in a hand so steady it looked like printing. The dark was honest, she'd told herself. The dark kept no favorites. A record written without light could not flatter anyone.

She did not believe it anymore. She wasn't sure when she'd stopped—perhaps the night the stars went out, perhaps before—but tonight, standing in the archive beneath the Bastion with a lamp turned up as high as it would burn, she felt the old faith in ink and order cracking along seams she hadn't known were there.

The archive smelled of tallow, dust, and cold iron. Shelves rose into shadow like the ribs of some enormous drowned ship, each one labeled by year and province, each one locked behind a grate that required two keys: hers, and the duty archivist's. Tonight the duty archivist was Brother Callow, a soft-spoken man with ink-stained cuffs who had been cataloguing confiscations since before Serah took her first oath. He had let her in without question, because she was Inquisitor Vane, and Inquisitors did not steal from their own house.

They only buried things in it.

"Third sub-vault," Callow said, hobbling ahead with his ring of keys. "You wanted the ward correspondence? That's provincial holdings, mostly. Duplicates of what the Bastion already has."

"I want the originals."

He glanced back at her, and in the lamplight his face was a map of small kindnesses and old tiredness. "Originals don't leave this floor, Inquisitor."

"I know." Her voice came out flat as a blade laid on a table. "I'm not taking them anywhere. I just need to read them."

*Read them.* As if that were a simple thing. As if reading hadn't become, in the last six days, the most dangerous act in Grayhollow.

Six days since the sky had gone black star by star, like candles being pinched out by an unseen hand moving west to east across the heavens. Six days since the border wards had shattered with a sound every soul in the city had heard in their teeth. Six days since the Lord-Inquisitor had stood on the Bastion steps and declared, in a voice pitched perfectly between grief and fury, that unregistered mages had committed an atrocity against the realm—and that the Inquisition would find them, and the pyres would be lit by week's end.

Serah had stood in the crowd and listened to him, and something in her chest had gone quietly, terribly still.

Because she had seen the wards fail before. Not shatter—*fail*, sagging like an old man lowering himself into a chair, the way wards failed when their power source guttered out. And she had spent those six days doing what she did best: reading. Reports, manifests, seizure inventories, the endless bureaucratic sediment of nineteen years of service. And in all that sediment, one phrase kept surfacing, in different hands, different years, always redacted:

*Contractual obligation, see Ember file.*

She'd asked the Lord-Inquisitor's secretary about the Ember file. The secretary had smiled at her with genuine warmth and told her there was no Ember file.

So here she was, at midnight, in a windowless room, looking for a file that did not exist.

Callow unlocked the third sub-vault and left her to it, with a promise of tea that neither of them believed in. The vault was narrow, cold enough that her breath ghosted, and lit by her single lamp. She worked methodically, the way she'd been trained—by year, by seal, by weight. Ward correspondence, 601 through 619. Most of it was exactly what it appeared: requisitions, complaints from ward-smiths about material shortages, the usual grumbling machinery of empire.

Then she found the box marked **E-7**, and her heart, which had been steady through worse nights than this, began to beat too loudly in her own ears.

The box was not locked. It was simply misfiled—or rather, filed under a designation that meant nothing, which was its own kind of lock. Inside were perhaps forty letters, bound in cord gone brittle with age, and the topmost letter bore a seal she knew better than her own signature: the Ember Court's, a coiled flame pressed deep in black wax.

She untied the cord with fingers that refused to hurry.

---

The first letter was dated twenty-two years ago. It was addressed to Lord-Inquisitor Aldric Vane—her father—and it began, with terrible courtesy:

*My lord, we are pleased to confirm receipt of the seventh installment, and to note that the terms remain satisfactory to both parties...*

Installment. She read the word four times before it would resolve into meaning. Then she read the letter entire, twice, her lips moving though no sound came.

It was a receipt. The Ember Court—the court beneath the mountain, the court that existed in Grayhollow chiefly as a story mothers told to frighten children away from the passes—had been sending receipts to the Inquisition for twenty-two years. Installments of *what*, the early letters did not say directly. But the later ones did, in the way that institutions eventually stop bothering to hide what they have decided no one will ever punish them for.

*Soul-weight received: three units. Balance remaining under Contract Ashfall: eleven units. We remind your office that timely collection preserves the dormancy, and that dormancy preserves, as ever, our mutual quiet.*

Three units. Eleven units. Dormancy. Mutual quiet.

Serah set the letter down on the shelf-edge with great care, the way one handles a thing that might go off. Her hands were not shaking. She noted that distantly, clinically, the way she noted everything now—as evidence. Nineteen years of training did not simply evaporate; it curdled. Every skill they had given her was suddenly aimed inward, at them.

She read on.

The correspondence told a story in the flat voice of bureaucracy, which was somehow worse than any confession could have been. Twenty-two years ago, the border wards—the great wards, the ones every schoolchild learned had been raised by heroic sacrifice to keep the wild magic out—had begun to fail. Not from enemy action. From *malnutrition*. They had been built, the letters made clear in the euphemism of engineers, on a foundation of bound souls, and the foundation was being consumed.

And the Ember Court had offered a refinancing.

*Contract Ashfall*: a schedule of payments, souls delivered at intervals, in exchange for which the Court would maintain the dormancy of whatever slept beneath the mountain—the thing the wards had truly been built to hold down, not keep out, and the realization of that landed in Serah's stomach like swallowed ice—and in exchange for which the Inquisition would do what it did best.

Keep the supply coming.

That was the part that broke something loose in her chest, some load-bearing wall of belief she heard crack but didn't yet hear fall. Because the letters didn't say *we will hunt mages*. They said things like *your recent condemnations have been processed efficiently* and *the quality of the last installment was notably consistent*. The Purge—the Purge she had served in, the Purge that had taken her friends and her faith and Wendell Brightwater's whole world—had not been a crusade against dangerous magic.

It had been a harvest schedule.

She thought of the condemned lists she had signed. Forty-one names over nineteen years, most of them small magics, harmless magics, hedge-craft and charm-sellers and one poor woman in the Fenrows who could make bread rise by singing to it. She had signed them believing each one was a nail holding shut a door against the dark.

They had been deliveries.

Her father's handwriting appeared throughout the early letters—not as author but as annotator, marginal notes in his cramped, precise script. *Balance confirmed.* *Schedule acceptable.* Once, chillingly, beside a passage about the dormancy: *The alternative is unacceptable. Proceed.*

The alternative. She wondered if he had ever named it aloud, even to himself. She wondered if he had known that his daughter would one day wear his title and sign his lists.

The final letter in the bundle was the newest—dated nine months ago, under the current Lord-Inquisitor's seal. It was brief, and it was the worst of them, because it was calm:

*The Court advises that the balance of Contract Ashfall falls due within the year, and that the final installment must be of particular weight—a foundling, soul-unencumbered, sold into the contract at birth. Your office is asked to confirm that no interference with collection will occur, and to prepare the city's understanding accordingly.*

*A foundling, soul-unencumbered, sold into the contract at birth.*

Yara. The name arrived in Serah's mind unbidden—Wendell's girl, the child she had seen grow up at the edge of the night market, the girl who used to run messages between the charm stalls with coins clenched in her fist. Sold at birth. The stars going out one by one were not an atrocity committed by hidden mages.

They were a countdown. And the city's understanding was being *prepared*—the arrests, the accusations, the pyres being stacked even now—all of it theater, all of it smoke thrown over a debt collection, and she, Serah Vane, had spent six days hunting the innocent on behalf of the creditors.

She sat down on the cold stone floor of the vault, because her legs had opinions her dignity could not override, and she laughed—one short, awful sound that no one heard.

*Prepare the city's understanding accordingly.*

She had prepared it. She was preparing it still. There was a list on her desk upstairs with nine names on it, and by dawn there would be warrants, and by week's end there would be fire, and none of it—not one torch, not one trial—would have anything to do with justice.

---

She did not know how long she sat there. Long enough that the lamp burned low and Callow came looking for her, and long enough for the cold of the floor to climb into her bones and make itself comfortable.

When he found her, he looked at the letters spread around her, and then at her face, and something in his expression suggested he had known what lived in box E-7 for a very long time. Twenty-two years, maybe.

"You're the fourth person to find that box," he said quietly. "The other three requested transfers within the month. Two of them are dead now—accidents. The third stopped speaking altogether."

"Why tell me that?"

He lowered himself onto a crate with the care of a man whose joints had outlived his courage, or perhaps the reverse. "Because you asked for the originals. The others asked for copies first. Copies can be denied. Originals mean you already know, and you want to know *how much*." He folded his ink-stained hands. "What will you do, Inquisitor?"

The honest answer was *I don't know*, and she almost gave it, because lying had come easily to her for nineteen years and it suddenly, violently, did not. But she looked at the letter with the foundling clause, and she thought of a girl with coins clenched in her fist, and she said:

"There's a warrant list on my desk. Nine names. I'm going to lose it."

"Lose it," Callow repeated. "Not burn it."

"Burning it looks like treason. Losing it looks like incompetence." She stood, and her knees cracked, and she found she didn't care. "I've been competent for nineteen years, Brother. Look where it got everyone."

"And after the list is lost?"

After the list was lost, there was a charm-seller in Grayhollow who lied badly and had once been her closest friend, and a girl sold before she could speak, and a contract with nineteen days—no. Fewer than that now. Days draining away like the stars had drained, one by one, while she signed her lists.

"After," Serah said, picking up her lamp, "I think I'm finally going to do my job. The one I thought I was doing all along."

Callow watched her gather the letters, all forty of them, and bind them back in their brittle cord, and he did not stop her, and he did not help her, and both of those things were choices too.

At the vault door she paused. "Why did you keep them? All these years. You could have lost the box."

The old archivist smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had seen in nineteen years of professional sadness. "Inquisition law, Inquisitor. We destroy nothing. Someday, someone with clean hands would need proof." He nodded at her hands. "I hoped they'd be yours."

She carried the ledger-correspondence up through the sleeping Bastion, past the duty desks where junior inquisitors sharpened questions instead of swords, past the wall of condemned portraits that she had walked proudly past a thousand times and now could not look at. On her desk, the warrant list waited under a paperweight of gray glass. Nine names. One of them was Marisol Ferro, exile hedge-witch, threat level negligible.

One of them, she noticed for the first time, was annotated in the Lord-Inquisitor's own hand: *Priority. Connected to collection.*

Connected to collection. Even the girl's neighbors were just logistics now.

Serah took the list, opened the window, and let the wind take it. It was not enough—it was nowhere near enough, it was one list against an institution—but watching those nine names scatter over the rooftops of Grayhollow, she understood that she had crossed a line that did not appear on any map, and that there was no walking back across it.

Then she took the letters from box E-7, wrapped them in oilcloth, and went out into the starless night to find Wendell Brightwater—because if the Inquisition had helped broker the sale of a child's soul, then somewhere in that tangle of paperwork was proof, and proof was the only weapon she had left that wasn't a lie.

She did not yet know that the road to the mountain ran through the same door she was knocking on. She did not yet know that giving the truth away freely would buy more than nineteen years of force ever had.

She only knew that the stars were going out, and her church had lit the fuses, and she was done pretending otherwise.
