## 10. The Road of Ash Bells

The borderlands began where the paved roads ended, and the paved roads ended where the Inquisition's writ grew thin.

We rode out of Grayhollow before dawn on the third day, four riders and a stolen cart, because Pip had insisted that a crew traveling openly was a crew asking to hang. The cart carried our supplies, Marisol's bundled herbs, and Brannoc's tools—hammers and chisels and a folding frame of ward-smith's brass that he handled the way a priest handles relics. Yara was not with us. That absence rode in the cart too, heavier than everything else.

The road north was old. Older than Grayhollow, older than the wards, older maybe than the kingdom that had drawn its borders along them like a child drawing lines in sand and calling them forever. The stones were fitted without mortar, which I would learn later was its own kind of joke.

By midmorning we reached the first of the ash bells.

They hung from a gallows-frame at the crossroads—a bell the size of a man's head, cast from some dull grey metal that drank the light instead of reflecting it, and beneath it, a placard in official lettering: IN MEMORY OF THE FAITHFUL OF HOLLOWMERE. BURNED FOR THEIR PROTECTION.

"Protection," Marisol said, spitting into the dust. "That's a word that's been used against me my whole life."

"It's a word," Brannoc said, not looking up from the road, "that gets people killed politely."

I had heard of ash bells but never seen one up close. They stood at every village that had lost someone to the Purge fires—a bell cast from the melted remains of whatever the condemned had owned, hung where everyone passing would have to see it. The Church called them memorials. Everyone else called them what they were: warnings nailed to the landscape. *This happened here. It can happen again. Keep your head down. Keep your hands empty. Keep your children from whispering to things they shouldn't.*

Pip rode close beneath it and flinched—I saw it, the small involuntary duck, though there was nothing to hit him. He'd grown up in Grayhollow's underside, where you learned young what bells meant.

"They're not even rung," he said. "What's the point of a bell nobody rings?"

"The point," Marisol said, "is that you hear it anyway."

She wasn't wrong. There was a sound to it—not sound exactly, more a pressure behind the ears, a silence shaped like a note. I felt it in my teeth as we passed under. Seven years of hiding magic will teach you to hear silences the way musicians hear chords.

* * *

Hollowmere itself came an hour later, or rather its bones did.

The village sat in a fold of hills beside a stream that still ran clean, because streams don't know enough to grieve. Thirty houses, maybe forty once. Doors standing open. A cradle on a doorstep, tipped over, weather-grey. Laundry lines with nothing on them, the poles leaning like men waiting for a friend who isn't coming.

Nobody lived here. Nobody had for years, by the look of the moss in the wells and the saplings growing through a collapsed roof. But the doors stood open, and that was the detail that got under my skin and stayed there. People who flee a fire shut their doors. People who are taken don't get the chance.

"Fear emptied this place," Marisol said quietly. "Not soldiers. Not even the Inquisition, not directly. Fear. One family burned two lanes over, and within a season everyone who could carry anything had walked west to the city, where the walls made them feel small instead of safe, but at least the feeling was familiar."

"And the ones who stayed?"

Marisol didn't answer. She didn't have to. The ash bell at Hollowmere's crossroads answered for her.

Brannoc dismounted at the village well and drank from his waterskin without touching the water, which told me his stomach was as bad as mine. He was fifty-odd, built like something quarried rather than born, with forearms mapped in old ward-scars—the pale lattice-work burns that ward-smiths took as a guild mark and everyone else took as a warning. He'd been silent most of the road. Not sullen; Brannoc didn't do sullen. Silent the way a dam is silent, holding back a great weight of something and pretending it's just scenery.

Now, looking at the open doors, the dam developed its first crack.

"I helped build the northern line," he said.

I turned. Pip stopped fidgeting. Even Marisol went still.

"You said that," I said carefully. "In the Broken Compass, when I asked for your help. You said you built the border wards."

"I said I helped build them." His voice was flat as slate. "I didn't say how."

The wind moved through Hollowmere's empty street, stirring dust in the doorways like ghosts doing housework. Somewhere a shutter banged, once, and settled.

"Ward-work needs anchoring," Brannoc said, still facing the well. "Every mage knows that. Power has to be tied to something solid or it bleeds away—everyone learns that in their first year. Stone anchors. Iron anchors. Living wood anchors. The old texts talk about soul-stone, and everyone assumes that means some special mineral. Rare. Deep-mined. Expensive." He laughed, one syllable, no humor in it. "It does mean special. It does mean expensive. It doesn't mean mineral."

Pip's face did the arithmetic first. Pip was always fastest with locks and sums. "Souls," he whispered. "Soul-stone. They used—"

"People," Brannoc said. "The wards are mortared with people."

* * *

He told it walking, because he couldn't tell it sitting still. We followed him down Hollowmere's empty main street while he spoke, past the shrine with its icon scraped blank, past the schoolhouse whose little benches were furred with moss.

"The border wards are three hundred miles long," he said. "You've seen them from the peaks—when they were lit, before they fell. A wall of light running horizon to horizon. Beautiful. The pride of the realm. Every child learns the hymn about the shining rampart that keeps the dark out." His jaw worked. "Do you know what it takes to anchor light like that? What holds it up? Not stone. Stone is just the frame. The frame needs mortar, and the mortar needs to be something that *persists*. Something with a will in it. Something that chooses, over and over, century after century, to hold."

"Souls don't choose to be mortar," Marisol said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very dangerous.

"No," Brannoc agreed. "They don't. That's why the work was done in secret, and why the ward-smiths' guild swore oaths of silence under penalties none of us believed until we saw them enforced." He stopped at the edge of the village green, where a maypole leaned drunk against its own shadow. "There were collection stations along the border. Officially, waystations for pilgrims and travelers. Unofficially—" He rubbed his scarred forearm, hard, like he could scour something off. "The realm needed eleven thousand anchors for the full line. Eleven thousand souls with enough strength in them to burn for centuries. Convicts, mostly, at first. Then debtors. Then the inconvenient. Then anyone the local magistrates wanted gone and couldn't legally hang."

My hand had drifted, without my noticing, toward the sword wrapped in sacking at my hip. I made myself stop. Made myself listen.

"I laid the northern line myself," Brannoc said. "Forty years ago. I was young, I was proud, I was *good*, Wendell. The best apprentice the guild had produced in a generation. And I set those anchor-stones into the earth one by one, and each one hummed when it seated, and I told myself the humming was the craft singing. The beauty of the work." His eyes finally came around to me, and what was in them made me take a half-step back. "It wasn't the craft singing. It was them. Screaming in a key so high that after a while your ears learned to call it music."

Pip sat down abruptly on a fence rail that groaned under him. Marisol had gone grey. I stood very still, and inside me something that had been grief for seven years—grief for a fallen order, for a burned world, for a life amputated—began, slowly, to change temperature.

"The stars," I said. My voice came out strange. "When the wards fell, the stars started going out. We assumed it was the same catastrophe. The same night."

"It was the same mechanism," Brannoc said. "The wards and the sky are older kin than anyone admits. The first ward-masons didn't invent the technique—they *copied* it. Copied it from the court under the mountain. From something that has been anchoring itself with collected souls since before this kingdom had a name. The Ember Court taught us the trick, or sold it to us, or let us steal it—it depends which version of the founding documents you believe, and I've read all of them." He looked north, toward the mountains, toward the black sky above them. "And now the contract that keeps that thing asleep—or fed, I've never been sure which—is coming due, and the interest is being paid in stars, and the last payment is a girl I watched eat burnt porridge and complain about it."

The anger arrived then. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the whole point now, or it had better be, or everything we're riding toward is worthless. For seven years I had kept my grief banked like a coal wrapped in ash—Wendell Brightwater, charm-seller, invisible woman, smiling merchant of harmless trinkets. Grief was a luxury. Anger was a luxury. Survival was the whole budget.

But standing in a murdered village with laundry lines swaying for no one, learning that the shining wall I'd sworn my life to defend was built out of screaming people—eleven thousand of them, and the hymns sung over their graves every festival day—

Grief caught. Anger took the flame. And I understood, in a way I hadn't let myself understand even in Serah Vane's parlor, that there had never been two worlds: the lawful one and the hidden one. There had only ever been one world, and it ran on the same fuel everywhere—on people made into material. On Yara, priced and signed for before she could speak. On me, outlawed for the crime of what I was. On Brannoc's brother, whoever he had been, wherever his stone lay humming in the dark.

"Well," Marisol said at last, into the ringing quiet. Her voice shook, and she let it. "I have spent thirty years hating the Inquisition for burning hedge-witches. Turns out they were the junior partner in the business." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, and then she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and stood breathing until she was steady again.

"We keep riding," I said.

Brannoc looked at me. "That's it? We keep riding?"

"That's it. Because anger that stops in a dead village is just grief wearing armor. Anger that reaches the mountain—" I touched the wrapped sword, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "Anger that reaches the mountain might actually *do* something."

* * *

We camped that night in the shell of a waystation—Brannoc chose it, and chose it deliberately, and I only understood why after we'd made camp and he stood staring at the eastern wall for a long time.

"This is one," he said finally. "A collection station. Or it was. Look at the floor."

I looked. Under the drifts of leaves and bird droppings, the flagstones were laid in a pattern I recognized now—concentric rings around a central socket, channels cut between them like veins. A place where something had been made to flow.

"There's no socket-stone," I said.

"No. They were removed after the guild fell, all up and down the line. The new regime didn't want anyone finding them and asking questions." He crouched and put his palm flat on one of the channel-stones, and I saw the ward-scars on his arm catch the firelight and gleam. "But stone remembers pressure. Give me an hour and I can show you how many."

"You don't have to," I said.

"Yes," he said, "I do. You need to see it, Wendell. All of you. Because tomorrow we cross the old ward-line itself, and you'll feel what's left of it, and if you don't know what you're feeling you'll think it's wind."

So he worked, and we watched. He chalked the channels, sang low notes against the stone and marked where the resonance died, laid his brass frame across the rings and watched the needle swing. Pip held the lantern. Marisol muttered counter-charms to keep the night's attention elsewhere. And at the end of it, Brannoc sat back on his heels and read us the station like a ledger.

"Fourteen," he said. "Fourteen anchors processed here. Over six years. Ages ranging from—" His voice snagged. He cleared his throat. "From nine to sixty-one. The youngest would have come through that door." He pointed at a low side entrance, child-height, deliberately built so the small ones wouldn't see the main hall. Wouldn't see what the room was for until it was too late to matter.

Nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Above us, through the broken roof, the sky was wrong—wronger than it had been in Grayhollow, this far from any city lights. Only a handful of stars left now, scattered and dim, like embers someone had forgotten to rake.

Fourteen. In one station. Along three hundred miles of border. I did the multiplication and then made myself stop doing it, because the number was a hole opening under my feet.

"They sang," Brannoc said suddenly. "The anchors. When they were seated in the line, they sang. I told you—the humming. But it wasn't random. Pip, you hear locks. You'd have heard it true. It was words. Old words, worn smooth, the same phrase over and over, thousands of voices in unison, for forty years." He stared into the fire. "I never worked out what the words meant until last winter, when I finally found a guild cipher-book I'd stolen and never dared open. Do you want to know what eleven thousand souls have been saying, in the dark, inside the walls of the realm, since before you were born?"

Pip's whisper: "What?"

"*Remember us.*" Brannoc's voice broke on it, the dam going all at once, and he didn't hide it, didn't wipe his face, just let it run and kept talking. "Not *avenge* us. Not *free* us. Remember us. Because they knew, even sealed in the mortar, even made into a wall—they knew that being remembered was the one power the masons couldn't take from them. And I forgot. Forty years I forgot, because remembering was dangerous and forgetting was comfortable, and I built a good life on top of the humming and called the humming wind."

I crossed the fire and put my hand on his shoulder. It was like resting your hand on a mountain—vast, and shaking.

"Then we remember them," I said. "Out loud. At the top of our lungs, if it comes to that. That's the other thing I learned this week, Brannoc. Truth spoken aloud is the only magic left that the law hasn't figured out how to outlaw."

He looked up at me through the firelight and the tears, and nodded once, and that was the whole ceremony.

* * *

We crossed the ward-line at noon the next day.

There was nothing to see—that was the first horror of it. No ruined wall, no shattered gate, just rolling moorland and the road running straight as a ruled line toward the mountains, which stood up ahead of us blue-black and enormous, wearing their crown of nothing where the stars used to be.

But we felt it.

Fifty yards before the line, the horses refused. All four of them, together, planting their feet and trembling, and we had to walk them through by hand, murmuring, lying to them the way you lie to frightened animals and frightened children: *it's fine, it's fine, nothing here, keep moving.* The air changed at the boundary—thinned, somehow, or deepened—and every hair on my arms rose, and the sword on my hip woke the way it always woke near large workings, a faint warmth through the sacking, patient and blind.

And underneath everything, so low it was almost imagination, I heard it.

The humming.

Or what was left of it. Most of the line had gone dark when the wards collapsed—Brannoc said the collapse was the anchors failing, or being *called home*, or being *collected*, and he said it in the flat voice of a man reading his own death warrant. But patches remained. Stretches where a few stubborn souls still held, still burned, still sang their two worn words into the dark because that was all they knew how to do.

*Remember us. Remember us. Remember us.*

Yara should have been there to hear it. That thought ambushed me halfway across, bent me double with it, right there on the moor with the others watching. She should have heard the walls of the world saying the only thing they'd ever truly said, because she was going to grow up in the world those walls had bought, and she deserved to know the price, and I was the one who'd decided she wouldn't pay it in knowledge, and look—look what that mercy had purchased. A girl alone on a road to a mountain, following a locket and a lie.

"We'll get her back," Marisol said beside me. Not gently. Marisol didn't do gentle; she did *true*, and today true sounded like a hammer on an anvil. "Say it back so I know you believe it."

"We'll get her back."

"And then?"

I looked north. The mountains. The court beneath them. The last star hanging somewhere above it all, guttering, nineteen days of light left in it by Pip's count, and every hour of the road spending them.

"And then," I said, "we make sure everyone hears the whole story. The sale, the coercion, the mortar, the bells, all of it. Spoken aloud. In front of witnesses." My hand found the sword's wrapped hilt and gripped it, and for the first time in seven years I didn't let go. "Let's see them burn *that*."

We came down off the moor at dusk and found the road ahead blocked.

Not by weather, and not by bandits. By lantern-light, arranged with military neatness across the track: a mounted patrol in the grey-and-silver of the Holy Office of Inquiry, lances leveled, and at their center a rider I knew even before she spurred forward into view—even before the last daylight found the tired, sharp, familiar face above the inquisitor's collar.

Serah Vane drew rein ten paces away and looked at me across seven years and one burning secret, and said:

"Wendell Brightwater. You're a long way from your market stall."
