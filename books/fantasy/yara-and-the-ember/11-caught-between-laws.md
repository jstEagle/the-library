## 11. Caught Between Laws

Serah Vane had been riding for three days on a lie.

The lie was simple, and like all the best lies it wore the uniform of duty: *Inquisitorial business, road clearance, pursuit of unregistered practitioners.* She had written it herself in her own hand, sealed it with her own wax, and shown it to every checkpoint between Grayhollow and the ash country, and not one clerk or garrison captain had thought to ask what, precisely, an inquisitor was doing traveling alone into the dead borderlands with no retinue and no prisoner wagon.

They didn't ask because they didn't want to know. That was the disease of the Order now—or had it always been the disease? She could no longer remember a version of it that asked questions honestly. She only remembered a version of herself that had.

She found them on the morning of the fourth day, at the crossing of the Cinderford, where the old bridge sagged like a tired shoulder and the water beneath ran gray with silt from the collapsed wards. They had made camp badly—a hedge-witch's fire banked too low to hide, a ward-smith's mending hammer ringing off an anvil-stone at an hour when no honest smith worked, a thief's horse hobbled within easy sprint of the trees. And Wendell Brightwater sitting up on watch with a cup of something steaming in her hands, staring at the eastern sky where the stars used to be.

Serah sat her horse at the edge of the tree line for a long moment and watched her.

Seven years since the Purge. Seven years since she had stood in the Square of Cinders with a ledger in her hands and read names aloud while the fires ate, and Wendell Brightwater's name had been third on that list, and by the time Serah reached it the line had already gone empty. Vanished. Erased so cleanly that Serah had told herself for years it was a clerical error, a misspelling, a name crossed out by an embarrassed archivist—and never once let herself believe the truth, which was that someone had warned her, and someone had run, and Serah had been grateful in some rotten chamber of her heart not to have to say the words out loud.

Now here she was. Older. Gray coming in at her temples. Sitting at a dying fire with a sword wrapped in oilcloth across her knees like a sleeping animal she didn't quite trust.

Serah nudged her horse forward onto the open ground and let the hoofbeats announce her.

Wendell moved fast for a woman who was supposed to be a charm seller. The cup went down, the sword came up—not drawn, but her hand rested on the wrapping, and the other three were awake almost before the sound of hooves registered. The big ward-smith had a haft in his fists. The girl—Pip, the waystation gossip had named her, Pip who could hear locks—was already rolling sideways out of her bedroll with a knife in her teeth. The hedge-witch simply opened her eyes, and Serah felt the air change pressure, felt the old prickle along her arms that meant active craft, unregistered, unlawful, three kinds of punishable.

"Morning," Serah said, and dismounted, slowly, leaving her reins loose. "You make terrible camp."

"Inquisitor." Marisol's voice was smoke and gravel. The pressure in the air thickened.

"Marisol Vex. Expelled from the Hedge Compact, year of the Purge plus two, for—" Serah paused. "Actually, I never read why. They didn't tell us. They just gave us the list and the sentence."

"For refusing to burn my own teacher's grimoire." The witch's eyes were flat coals. "You can add the rest to your report."

"I'm not writing a report." Serah pulled off one glove, then the other, and held her bare hands out from her body, palms open—the old gesture, the de-escalation drill they'd all been taught before the Order decided drills were softness. "I'm alone. No retinue. No irons in my saddlebags—you're welcome to check, thief, I know you're already planning to."

Pip, crouched low with her knife still bared, grinned with all the warmth of a drawn blade. "She knows our names. That's charming. That's the last thing everybody says right before the wagons come."

"Pip." Wendell's voice was quiet, but it landed like a stone in a pond. She hadn't taken her eyes off Serah. "You rode three days alone through the ash country to find us. You could have sent riders. You could have sent nothing. So you didn't come to arrest us."

"No."

"Then why are you here?"

It was the question Serah had been carrying for three hundred miles, turning it over like a coin she couldn't read the face of. She looked at the fire, at the gray light rising over the Cinderford, at the woman she had once called friend in the years when friendship between an inquisitor-trainee and a swordspeaker-apprentice had still been technically possible, technically survivable.

"Because I found something in the archives," Serah said. "And it broke something in me, and I need—" Her voice caught on the word, and she hated it, and she said it anyway. "I need to give it to someone who'll do something with it. And there is no one left in the Order I trust to do anything at all."

* * *

They heard her out around the rekindled fire, because Brannoc argued that listening cost nothing, and because Wendell never told him no.

Serah laid it out plainly, the way she'd been trained to lay out evidence: dates, seals, signatures, the chain of custody of a single folder she had copied by hand and burned the original of. Nine weeks before the stars began falling, the Inquisition's Third Table—the bureau nobody admitted existed—had exchanged correspondence with an address inside the mountain. Not interrogation records. Not arrest orders. *Contracts.* Brokered, countersigned, filed under the same heading as grain tariffs and toll-road leases. The disappearance of the border wards: scheduled. The fall of the stars: anticipated, itemized, priced per soul.

"They knew," Serah said. "Not guessed. Knew. The collapse was in the ledgers before it happened. My superiors signed their names to the end of the sky and then stood up in front of the whole city and blamed the unregistered mages. Blamed people like you." She looked at Marisol, then at Wendell, and found she could not look away from either. "I have spent seven years hunting women like you on behalf of men who sold the stars."

The fire popped. Down by the water, the horses shifted and stamped.

Brannoc spoke first, his voice low and ruined. "The wards. You're saying the Order knew how the wards were paid for."

"I'm saying the receipts exist."

The smith turned his face away, toward the river, and his huge hands closed once, hard, around nothing.

"So," Pip said lightly, into the silence, the way she seemed to carry lightness like a tool on her belt, "you've betrayed your entire murderous church, ridden into the wilderness, and handed us proof that would get us all burned twice over. What do you *want*, Vane? Absolution? Because the priest shortage in this country is severe."

"I want the truth," Serah said. "All of it. Not the archive's version. Yours." She turned to Wendell, and made herself say it steadily. "You know why the stars are falling. I saw your face at the market the night the sky went black—I wasn't following you, I swear that, I was walking, and I saw you look up and *recognize* it. Whatever this crew is riding toward, whatever's under that mountain, you knew before any of them did. Before you ask me to stand between you and the Order, I want to know what I'm standing in front of. I won't take you on the road otherwise. And I won't take you at all if you lie to me. Force gets you my sword arm for a day. Truth gets me for however long this takes."

Wendell was quiet for a long time.

Serah watched her weigh it—watched the calculation move behind her eyes, and then watched something else happen, something that looked almost like pain: the calculation stopping. Being set down. Like a pack carried so long its straps had grown into the shoulders, and the moment of unbuckling hurt worse than the weight ever had.

"You'll want to be standing downwind of the thief when I finish," Wendell said. "In case you decide to do your job after all."

"I won't."

"You said that seven years ago too." Wendell's voice didn't sharpen; that was the worst of it. It just stayed level, and true. "You said it the night before the lists were posted. *Whatever happens, Wen, I won't.* And then the bells rang, and you read the names, and I ran."

Serah took it. It went in under her ribs exactly as far as it was meant to. "Yes," she said. "I did. Which is why I'm not asking for your trust, Brightwater. I'm asking for the chance to fail at it differently this time."

Something moved through the little camp at that—not forgiveness, nothing so clean—but Pip stopped fingering her knife, and even Marisol leaned back a degree from whatever working she had half-coiled in her sleeve.

Wendell nodded once. Then she reached into her shirt and drew out a locket on a fine chain—plain silver, dented at one corner—and held it in her palm without opening it, the way a person holds a wound they've spent years learning not to touch.

"Her name is Yara," Wendell said. "She's sixteen. I found her when she was nine days old, or she found me—I've never been sure which of us did the finding. I raised her in Grayhollow. Told everyone she was my late sister's child. Told *her* almost nothing." Her thumb moved over the locket's dent. "And on the night she was born, her mother—dying, fleeing, at the border with the wards closing—made a bargain. Passage through, for herself and her child. Except the mountain doesn't take coin. It takes souls. And the mother couldn't pay both hers and the baby's, and she was going to die anyway, so she signed for the child's."

The fire cracked. Nobody breathed.

"The contract comes due now," Wendell went on, and her voice stayed level in the way Serah recognized as the most expensive kind of control, control purchased at ruinous daily interest for seven years. "The falling stars aren't weather. They're payments being collected. Yara is the last installment. When the final star goes out, the Ember Court takes her soul, and I—" Here, at last, the level voice bent. One degree. "I sold her to them by keeping silent. Every year I didn't tell her. Every night I charmed that locket against her questions instead of answering them. I told myself it was protection. It was cowardice wearing protection's coat. That's the whole of it, Serah. That's everything I am and everything I've done. Now you know why the sky went dark. Arrest me."

Serah did not move.

She sat with it—all of it—and found, strangely, that none of it horrified her as much as it should have. What horrified her was how familiar the shape of it was. A secret kept so long it became load-bearing. A silence mistaken for kindness. She had built her whole career on the architecture of withheld truth; she had simply done it with better stationery.

"The fraud clause," Pip said suddenly.

Everyone looked at her. The thief was sitting cross-legged, eyes bright, knife long since sheathed, turning a lockpick over her knuckles without seeming to notice she was doing it.

"Every court files contracts, and every contract worth its ink has a clause for fraudulent intent," Pip said, rapid-fire. "Waystation vaults, remember? I've read debt-bonds from six principalities. If the original seller's intent can be proven coerced—if she didn't sign *willingly*—the whole instrument dissolves. Void ab initio. The debt dies with the signature." She pointed the pick at Serah like a duelist. "You said your Order brokered it. Brokers coerce. Brokers put knives at tables and call it terms. Tell me your archives keep correspondence."

Serah felt something turn over in her chest—an engine long cold catching, coughing, catching again.

"The Third Table keeps everything," she said slowly. "Including the negotiation record. Including—" and the memory surfaced with the specific chill of a thing glimpsed once and understood only now, "including a marginal note I saw and didn't understand. Next to the mother's signature. An agent's initials, and the notation: *subject encouraged.*"

"*Encouraged,*" Brannoc repeated, in a voice like grinding stone.

"Coerced," Serah said. "If it's documented, it's provable. If it's provable—" She looked at Wendell. "Then Yara may never have owed them anything at all."

For a moment the camp was absolutely still, five people around a small fire under an empty sky, holding between them a possibility the size of a life.

Then Wendell laughed—one short, broken sound, nothing like joy, closer to a wall coming down—and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes. "Nineteen days," she said. "We ride at first light. All of us, if you're riding, Vane. But hear the terms. You ride as one of us, not above us. You don't write reports. You don't carry irons. And when we get to that court, if it comes to choosing between your Order and my daughter, I need to know—truly know—which side of that line you'll die on."

Serah picked up her gloves from the stone beside her and, deliberately, dropped them into the fire. Kid leather. They burned quickly, curling like living things.

"I spent eleven years learning to take truth out of people with pincers," she said, watching them blacken. "Turns out you just have to ask, and offer something real back. Eleven years." She shook her head. "Ride as one of you. Gladly. I'll get you through every checkpoint between here and the mountain, and when the Third Table comes looking for their missing inquisitor—and they will—I'll be standing on your side of the line, holding every document I copied."

"Then welcome to the crew nobody wants," Pip said, and produced from somewhere a battered tin cup and poured Serah a measure of something that smelled strong enough to strip varnish. "Fair warning: the witch snores, the smith argues with rivers, and Brightwater tells bedtime stories with the ending cut out."

Marisol, settling back against her pack, glanced at Serah with those coal-flat eyes and something almost like approval. "An inquisitor," she said. "My teacher would have laughed until she wept. Then she'd have fed you."

"That," Serah said, taking the cup, "is the kindest thing anyone in authority has offered me in years."

Above the Cinderford the gray dawn came up starless and flat as slate. They struck camp with the quick economy of people who had learned each other's rhythms faster than any of them wanted to admit, and Serah Vane saddled her horse alongside outlaws and exiles and found that her hands, for the first time in seven years, were entirely steady.

They were two miles down the ash road, the river behind them and the mountain a bruise on the horizon, when Pip, riding point, abruptly stood in her stirrups.

"Waystation vault," she said. "The one under the Tollmaster's Arch—I passed the seal on the map, I marked it, I *knew* it mattered. If the Ember Court files correspondence anywhere outside the mountain, it's there. Terms, schedules, the original instruments, maybe—" her grin flashed, sharp and sudden as a knife leaving a sleeve, "—maybe the contract itself. Nineteen days, friends. Who wants to help me steal from the people who stole the sky?"
