## 12. Locks That Sing

Pip had stolen her first lock at six years old, and she could tell you this much about the trade: every lock had a voice.

Not a metaphorical voice, either—she'd stopped explaining this to people around age nine, when Brannoc had looked at her like she'd grown a second head and Marisol had simply said, *of course it does, child, now hush while I work.* A lock spoke in tension and tumblers, in the small complaining creak of a pin that hadn't been turned in years or the smug silence of one that had been oiled recently, which meant someone cared about what was behind it, which meant it was worth stealing.

The waystation vault outside Ashbell Ferry spoke in three voices, and all of them were singing.

"Problem?" Wendell asked. She was crouched in the dead bracken beside him, hood up, looking for all the world like a tired merchant's widow and not the most dangerous woman Pip had ever agreed to follow into a mountain. Beside her, Brannoc filled half the ditch with shoulders and bad attitude.

"No problem," Pip said. "Three locks. Three very good locks. First one's cheap iron with delusions of grandeur—she'll sing if I breathe on her right. Second one's ward-bound, Brannoc's department, and don't look at me like that, I know you're *retired*, we're all retired, that's the entire crew's whole personality."

"And the third?" Marisol asked from Pip's other side. The hedge-witch had gone quiet the way she always went quiet before anything that mattered, which Pip had learned to read as either wisdom or indigestion.

Pip pressed her ear close to the cold stone of the vault door, laid two fingers against it, and listened to the third lock the way other people listened to a lover.

"That one," she said, "is Ember Court work, and she's singing a song I've never heard, and I don't like the tune."

* * *

They'd found the waystation by accident, which was how Pip preferred to find everything. Two days out from Grayhollow, on the road of ash bells where the villages stood empty and the border wards hung overhead like the ribs of something long dead, Brannoc had recognized the building—an old Imperial relay post, squat and stone, built back when messages between provinces traveled by rider instead of rumor. The Inquisition used them now for storage nobody wanted to inventory.

But there'd been fresh hoofprints in the mud. And a courier's satchel strap, snapped, left in the weeds. And—this was the part that had made Pip sit up in the saddle—the faint smell of coals on the wind where no fire burned.

"Ember Court correspondence doesn't travel through Grayhollow," Wendell had said. "It travels under the wards. Through channels the Inquisition pretends not to see because pretending is cheaper than war."

"So why's it sitting in an Inquisition waystation?" Pip had asked, and nobody had answered, which meant everybody already knew: somebody up high was reading the mail.

Now, in the last grey light before full dark, Pip worked the first lock.

She was small—that was the joke everyone made, *small hands, quick fingers*, as though being short were a profession—but what she actually had was patience shaped like insolence. She talked to locks because they answered better than people did. This one complained about rust in its second tumbler, apologized for it almost, and gave way with a click like a hiccup.

"She says hello," Pip announced. "She also says the second lock has been telling her secrets, which is flattering for both of us."

Brannoc grunted and moved up beside her. He didn't touch the ward-bound lock so much as hover his scarred hands over it, reading the old script woven into its mechanism—ward-smith's marks, the shorthand of a dead craft. Pip watched his face do the thing it did lately: start hard, then crumple slightly, then set itself again into something worse than hard.

"Standard soul-lattice," he said quietly. "Small one. A fraction. Enough to make the lock refuse anyone who isn't carrying a writ." He paused. "There's a name scratched in the margin of the binding. They always let the smith sign the sacrifice. Merciful, they called it."

"Whose name?" Marisol asked, though her voice said she suspected.

"I don't know him." Brannoc's jaw worked. "That's the point. There are thousands of names in these margins and I don't know any of them, and I helped build the system that collected them." He breathed once, slow, the way Wendell had taught him—or maybe he'd taught her; their griefs had started borrowing each other's habits somewhere on the road. Then he spoke three syllables that weren't words so much as permission, and the lock's lattice unraveled like a knot untied from the inside.

The third lock began, softly, to laugh.

Pip froze. It wasn't laughter exactly—it was the sound a lock made when it knew it was better than you, a bright ringing confidence in every tumbler. Ember Court work. She put her picks away and got out the thin wire she kept for emergencies, the one she'd never needed, and pressed her ear to the mechanism until the singing resolved into something she could almost parse.

"It's keyed to blood," she reported. "Court blood. Or—" she frowned, listening harder, "—or payment-blood. Anyone who's ever been *owed*." Her stomach dropped as the meaning landed. "Yara could open this door by breathing on it."

Silence in the ditch. Somewhere far off, one of the dead borderland's ash bells rang once in a wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else.

"We're not bringing Yara within ten leagues of this door," Wendell said. Flat. Final. The merchant's smile nowhere in sight.

"She's already gone ahead," Marisol reminded her gently, and Pip heard the older woman flinch.

Right. That. Yara had slipped out of camp four days ago with a borrowed coat and the locket humming against her chest, headed for the mountain, convinced—as only sixteen-year-olds and saints could be convinced—that her disappearance would keep Wendell safe. The crew had spent those four days chasing her trail and getting angrier with every league.

Which made the vault door, Pip thought grimly, the least of the evening's problems. But doors were what she understood, so doors were what she'd give them.

"Wendell," she said. "You're oath-bound. Old order, swordspeakers, the works. Does the Court consider you *owed*?"

Wendell went very still. "Every soul in Grayhollow owes someone something," she said. "That's how contracts work. That's how *they* work." But she rolled up her sleeve anyway and pressed her forearm to the lock.

The singing changed key. Went low. Went, unmistakably, *hungry*. Then—click.

Pip sat back on her heels, heart hammering a rhythm she didn't like. "Well," she said. "That's new. That's deeply, deeply new. Note for later: the Court has been waiting for you specifically, and I hate everything about that sentence."

* * *

Inside, the vault smelled of paper and coal smoke and time.

Marisol lit nothing; she carried instead a small jar of grave-glow, that pale green light hedge-witches brewed from things Pip declined to think about too hard, and under its wash the waystation's cellar revealed itself: rows of shelving, dust-sheeted furniture, and crates. Dozens of crates, each stenciled with the Inquisition's flame sigil—and beneath the sigil, stamped over it in red wax that never seemed to fully dry, a seal like a closed eye made of coals.

"They've been collecting it," Brannoc said, pulling a sheet off the nearest crate. "Years of it. Every letter that crossed between the Inquisition and the Court, filed and hidden down here where no clerk would ask questions."

"Why hide your own correspondence?" Pip asked.

"Because correspondence is evidence," Marisol said. Her voice had that quiet quality again, but sharper now. "And evidence of what you're about to see has been worth killing over for a long time."

Wendell broke the wax on the first bundle and read. Pip watched her face by grave-light: the tightening around the eyes, the stillness, the way her thumb whitened against the paper.

"Pip. The dates."

Pip took the letter, because reading was the one skill she owned outright, learned from a fence who'd believed literacy was the best disguise. The hand was cramped, clerical, official:

*...regarding the standing agreement. The installments continue per schedule. His Eminence reminds the Court that the terms specified one soul per annum of appropriate weight, drawn against the original debt, with the balance payable upon maturity of the asset.*

"The asset," Brannoc read over her shoulder, in a voice like gravel sliding.

*The asset will come of age in the nineteenth year. Upon maturity, the remaining stars extinguish in sequence, and the final payment is collected.*

Pip set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down a snake.

"How many stars are left?" she asked, and hated how light she tried to make it sound. Humor was armor; she'd learned that in gutters where crying got you robbed. "Asking for a friend. The friend is the sky."

Marisol was already digging through the crate, pulling letters, sorting by date with the ruthless speed of a woman who had done her own accounting with powers best not discussed. She spread them across a dusty table in a timeline. Her lips moved as she counted.

Then she stopped counting.

"Nineteen days," Marisol said. "Nineteen days, and the last star goes out."

* * *

Nobody moved for a moment. Pip became aware of her own heartbeat, absurdly loud, and of the dust motes drifting through grave-glow like slow snow, and of the fact that she was, at thirteen, going to die someday, a fact she usually kept locked in a box in the back of her skull and which now chose this exact moment to rattle its lid.

"Nineteen days," Wendell repeated. Her voice came out level. Too level. Pip had heard merchants use that voice over broken goods. "Yara turns seventeen in twenty-six days. The contract says—"

"The contract says *maturity*," Marisol said. "Which may be her birthday. May have always been her birthday. Or—" she tapped a letter stamped years old, "—the Court may define maturity however suits it. Look here. 'The asset matures upon completion of the seventeenth year OR upon the extinguishing of the penultimate star, whichever arrives first.'"

"The penultimate star," Pip said slowly, "went out night before last. I counted. Everyone counted. Nobody slept."

"Yes," Marisol said.

"So she's already mature." Pip laughed—a short, ugly sound that surprised even her. "Congratulations to her. Sixteen years old and legally ripe for soul collection. Someone should bake a cake. Someone should bake nineteen cakes, one for each day, and we can eat one per day as the sky gets darker, like some kind of—"

"Pip." Brannoc's hand landed on her shoulder. Gentle, for a man who could bend horseshoes. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing. Breathing's free. That's the one thing the Court hasn't figured out how to bill us for yet." Her voice cracked on *yet*, and she covered it by turning back to the letters with furious energy, because her hands shaking was nobody's business but her own. "Fine. Fine! Nineteen days. We've done worse with less. I once stole a bishop's ring during his own coronation, and I had eight hours' notice and dysentery. This is fine."

Wendell wasn't listening anymore. She'd gone to the far shelf and was pulling bundles at random, faster and rougher, and the papers were whispering under her hands, and when she finally spoke her voice had a crack of its own running through the level.

"There are others. Here—" she thrust a page at Marisol. "Payment records. Souls collected. One per year, going back—" her finger stabbed down the column, "—sixteen entries. Sixteen souls, one for every year of Yara's life. They've been paying off her debt in installments since the day she was born, and every star that died was one of these."

The cellar was very quiet.

"Sixteen people," Brannoc said. "Named?"

"Some." Wendell's voice was sandpaper. "A ferryman. Two ward-smiths. A girl from Grayhollow, age nine, cause of collection listed as 'balance adjustment.'" She looked up, and her eyes in the grave-glow were terrible. "This whole time. Seven years I hid her, starved my own magic, kept my mouth shut and my head down—and the debt was growing the entire time, and the interest was *people*."

"That's not on you," Brannoc started.

"Don't." One word. Brannoc shut up. Even the dust settled.

Pip cleared her throat. It was a talent of hers, clearing throats, breaking silences, being the small loud thing in a room full of large quiet griefs. "So we have nineteen days," she said. "We know where she's going—we found her boot prints on the north road this morning, she's maybe two days ahead and slowing because she's sixteen and traveling alone and probably crying, no offense to her, I cried for a week my first time alone and I was five and it was justified. We know what's collecting her. We know the Inquisition has been filing the receipts in a *cellar*, which means Serah Vane might not know, which means either she's complicit or she's about to have the worst week of her life, and honestly I'd pay coin to watch either."

"You want to send for Serah," Marisol said. Not a question.

"I want *options*. Also I want dinner, but the road of ash bells is fresh out of taverns." Pip flipped through another ledger, and then stopped, because sometimes the universe rewarded insolence with information. "Hello, gorgeous. This one's different."

The others gathered. The letter was written in no clerical hand—this script slanted and curled, inked in a color that wasn't quite brown and wasn't quite red and that Pip chose not to examine under magnification. It bore no seal. It bore something better: a signature.

*"The debt stands,"* it read, *"but debts, like all living things, may be bargained with. The Court honors precision above force. Read the fine terms. —The Herald of Coals."*

"Fine terms," Brannoc repeated. "Contracts this size have riders. Clauses. Conditions written by lawyers three hundred years dead."

"Lawyers," Pip said, feeling the grin crawl onto her face despite everything, despite the nineteen days ticking down in her chest like a second heartbeat, "can be stolen from. Lawyers are just locks with vocabulary. And I," she tapped the letter, "have never met a lock that didn't eventually sing."

* * *

They left the waystation an hour past midnight, packs heavier with stolen correspondence and hearts heavier with everything else. Marisol sealed the vault door behind them with a working that looked, to Pip's untrained eye, unpleasantly like knitting.

Two miles up the darkening road, Brannoc stopped so suddenly that Pip walked into his back.

"What—"

He pointed up.

Through the torn ribs of the dead border wards, in the black wound where the sky used to keep its stars, something was falling. Not fast—slowly, gracefully, the way embers rise from a fire except inverted, wrong, a point of light descending toward the mountains with the unhurried certainty of a thing that knows exactly where it's going.

"Seventeen," Marisol whispered.

The ember touched the peaks and went out.

Eighteen days, Pip thought. Seventeen, after tonight. She pulled her collar up against a wind that smelled of coals, and thought about Yara—stubborn, brave, infuriating Yara—walking alone toward the same mountains, wearing a locket full of other people's memories, believing she was saving them all.

"Well," Pip said aloud, because someone had to, because silence was a luxury none of them could afford, "on the bright side, we'll definitely get there before her birthday. Nobody tell her we missed it. She'll sulk."

Nobody laughed. But Wendell put a hand on her shoulder as they walked on into the dark, and that, Pip supposed, was its own kind of punchline: the joke being that they were going to steal a soul back from the court beneath the mountain with nineteen days, four broken people, and a bag full of other people's mail.

Terrible odds. Wonderful story, someday, if any of them lived to tell it.

Behind them, faint as memory, the waystation's third lock sang on in the dark—keyed to blood, hungry for the owed—and Pip made a mental note, the kind you make when you suspect you'll need it: *find out who else the door is waiting for, and pray to whatever's left to pray to that it isn't the girl.*
