## 13. The Court Beneath the Mountain

Yara had walked for six days, and the mountain had been watching her for five of them.

She felt it the way you feel a stranger's gaze in a crowded market—not on your back but between your shoulder blades, patient and specific. The road of ash bells wound upward through dead villages and fields where nothing grew except a pale gray lichen that glowed faintly at dusk, like the ground itself remembered light. On either side of the road, at intervals too regular to be natural, stood iron posts crowned with tarnished bells. They never rang. Wind came down the slopes and bent the grasses and stirred the loose ash into little ghost-whorls, and still the bells hung silent, heavy, as though they were not bells at all but something waiting to be woken.

The locket knew them anyway. Each time she passed one, it warmed against her sternum, a small coal of heat through her shirt, and hummed a note so low she heard it more in her teeth than her ears. The notes changed—rose slightly, fell slightly—and Yara had begun, without meaning to, to match her steps to them. By the fourth day she realized she was walking in time with a song the bells were singing only to her.

*You are expected*, the song said. *You are expected.*

She should have turned around. She thought about turning around every night when she made her cold camp under the black sky, where no stars remained now, not even the stubborn few that had clung to the horizon over Grayhollow during those first terrible hours. The dark up here was total. It pressed against her eyes like wool. And every night, curled in her bedroll with the locket in her fist, she rehearsed the argument she would make if Wendell found her—if Serah Vane's warning had sent her running up the mountain after all, irons first and apologies never.

*I did this for you,* Yara would say. *They were going to burn you. I saw how you lied to her, how badly you lied, and I couldn't let my face be the reason they took yours.* Sixteen years, and Wendell Brightwater had fed her and taught her letters and charmed her fevers away and never once asked for anything except that Yara stay inside the city walls on festival nights. The debt of that was enormous. The lie underneath it was enormous too. She carried both, and they weighed exactly the same.

But nobody came. Nobody followed. That was the worst part, in the end—that she had run toward the monster's mouth partly hoping someone would drag her back out of it, and the road stayed empty behind her all the way to the gate.

* * *

She smelled the court before she saw it: woodsmoke and hot metal and, beneath both, something sweeter, like chestnuts roasting far away, like the sugar crust on baked apples. Her stomach clenched with sudden, humiliating hunger. She had eaten the last of her travel bread two days ago.

Then the last bend in the road opened, and there it was.

She had imagined a cave. A crack in the rock, a dark mouth, something you had to squeeze into sideways while telling yourself brave things. Instead the mountainside rose before her in terraces of black stone veined with ember-orange, glowing softly from within like a banked fire the size of a country. Stairs climbed the face of it in long sweeping arcs, wide enough for twenty abreast, each step edged in that warm inner light. Above, arched bridges spanned between spires; lanterns of colored glass hung from chains of what looked like woven bronze hair. Doors stood open everywhere she looked. Not a single one was closed.

And people—or figures shaped like people—moved along the terraces and stairs, unhurried, graceful, their faces lit gold from below by the stone's own glow. Some wore robes of deep red. Some wore armor of dull iron that drank the light instead of reflecting it. Music drifted down, thin and sweet, a stringed instrument played by someone who understood patience.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, and Yara hated herself for how strongly she felt it. It was beautiful the way the frost-flowers on Grayhollow's windows were beautiful in the years before she learned they meant the pipes would burst. Beauty that didn't care what happened to you afterward.

The locket burned.

"Ah."

The voice came from just beside her, though a moment ago there had been no one there—a voice like coals settling in a grate, warm and crackling and low. Yara spun, hand going uselessly to the belt-knife Wendell had given her at fourteen and which she had never once needed until she'd decided to need it.

The herald stood at the edge of the road where the ash met the living stone. He was tall and narrow, wrapped in a traveling cloak the color of cooling embers, and his face was kind in the way that made her want to look away—soft around the eyes, lined like someone who smiled often, framed by silver hair that moved in a wind she could not feel. When he bowed, the bow was deep and unhurried, the bow of a servant welcoming home a mistress he has loved since her childhood.

"Yara." Her name, spoken correctly, warmly, the way you say a word you have said many times before in private. "You must be tired. Six days on the road of bells, and the sky so unfriendly. Come in. Come in—the whole court has been waiting."

"You know my name." It wasn't the question she wanted to ask. The question she wanted to ask was *how*, and behind it, quieter, more frightened: *who told you?*

"Everyone here knows your name." He straightened, smiling, and gestured up the stair with an open palm. "Though we've called you other things, over the years. Little ember. The sixteenth candle. The payment." He said the last word gently, tenderly even, the way you might say *the answer to a prayer*, and something in Yara's chest went cold even as her feet, traitorously, took a half-step forward toward the warmth rolling down off the terraces like heat from a hearth.

"The payment for what?"

"For everything," the herald said, as if this explained it, as if it were the most natural exchange in the world. "Come. You can ask questions inside, where it's warm, and there will be food—I'm told you like honey cake? Forgive me. We know a great deal about you. It would be strange if we did not."

He began to walk and she followed, because the alternative was the empty dark behind her, and because her legs were shaking with exhaustion, and because—she admitted it only to herself, in the smallest room of her heart—because he had said *honey cake* the way Wendell said it, on the birthdays, with that particular softness, and some starving animal part of her leaned toward anyone who spoke of her with that much familiarity.

The stairs took them up through the terraces. As they climbed, the figures paused to watch her pass. They didn't stare—they were too well-mannered for staring—but they turned, and inclined their heads, and some of them smiled, and the smiles were all wrong in a way Yara couldn't name. Too symmetrical. Too glad. Like portraits of happiness painted by someone who had heard it described but never seen it.

"They're pleased to meet you," the herald murmured. "You're rather famous here. Sixteen years is a long time to anticipate a guest."

"Famous for what? Being sold?"

The herald stopped. Turned. And for one unguarded instant his kind face did something complicated—something that flickered across it like a shadow crossing a window—and then the kindness resettled, deeper than before.

"Sold," he repeated. "Is that what they told you in Grayhollow? That word is so... transactional. Your mother did not sell you, child. Your mother *trusted* us. There is a difference, though I understand the world outside has grown crude about such things." He resumed walking. "She was dying. The wards were sealed. She came to our gate with you asleep against her breast and she begged—not for money, whatever story they've fed you—for passage. For a future. And the court granted it, because the court honors its bargains to the letter, always, and asks only fair consideration in return."

"What consideration?" But she already knew. The locket had shown her: the signature, the contract, the voice like coals saying *she will be ours when the stars have counted down to one*. "Me. The consideration was me."

"The consideration," the herald corrected gently, "was your *soul*. Which is not the same thing, whatever the hedge-witches say. Your body grew. Your mind grew. Your laughter, your tempers, your terrible handwriting—all of that stayed yours, every day of your life in Grayhollow. Did it feel like slavery, Yara? Did the woman who raised you ever once treat you as property?" He glanced back at her, and his eyes caught the ember-light and held it, orange and patient. "No. She treated you as a daughter. And we let her. Seventeen years of grace—we gave you both that. Very few courts are so generous."

"Sixteen," Yara said. "I'm sixteen today. Yesterday. I don't—" Her voice cracked, and she hated that too. "How do you know these things? How do you know about the honey cakes?"

The herald said nothing for several steps. The music above swelled and faded. Somewhere on a lower terrace, a door closed—closed, finally, one of the hundred open doors—and the sound of it was very final.

"We watched," he said simply. "Not cruelly. The way a gardener watches a tree he planted. You are important to us, Yara. More than you yet understand. And everything important to the Ember Court is tended."

They passed through an archway into warmth.

* * *

Inside, the mountain was a palace, and the palace was a hearth.

Corridors of black glassy stone curved away in directions that made her eyes slide off them, lit by veins of orange fire that pulsed slowly, slowly, like breathing. Fountains ran not with water but with something molten and gold that cooled midair into falling petals of amber. Servants appeared with trays and vanished again—real servants, flesh and blood, though none of them would meet her eyes. The air tasted of cinnamon and hot iron.

They brought her to a chamber high on the eastern face, where the wall was a single sweep of smooth obsidian and the view beyond it was the whole dark world: the ash plains, the dead villages, the distant smudge that was Grayhollow, all of it starless, all of it small. A bath had been drawn already—already, as if they'd known the hour of her arrival to the minute—and clothes waited, folded, in deep reds and golds that matched nothing she owned and everything she was apparently expected to become.

"Eat. Bathe. Rest," the herald said from the doorway. "Tonight there is a welcome feast. The Queen herself may attend, if her duties permit." Something moved through his voice at the words *the Queen*—not fear, exactly. Reverence with a tremor in it. "You'll find the locket works better here, by the way. Fewer lies in the air to interfere with it."

Yara went very still. "What did you say?"

But he was already bowing himself out, backward, gracefully, the door drawing shut behind him with a sound like a held breath released. "Until tonight, little ember. Welcome home."

*Home.* The word sat in her stomach like a swallowed coal.

* * *

She did eat. She was furious at herself, and she ate anyway—honey cake, actual honey cake, dense with spice and dripping, and roasted chestnuts, and a soup of some root vegetable she'd never tasted that warmed her from throat to toes. Whoever cooked in this place cooked for someone they loved. She cried a little into the second cake and told herself it was the smoke from the fire-veins irritating her eyes.

Then she took out the locket.

It had been quiet in her hand since the gate—no, not quiet. Quieter, the way a room goes quiet when the argument you've been having for years is suddenly about to happen. She thumbed it open, and the charm inside woke, and Wendell's voice filled the chamber the way it always did, warm and rough and a little tired:

*"So you don't forget who you are, trouble. Even if everyone else forgets. Even if I do."*

But here, on the mountain, the memory didn't stop where it always stopped. The locket hummed deeper. The vision widened.

Wendell's kitchen. The night of the locket, the night of her sixteenth birthday—but seen now from outside herself, from the corner near the stove where the shadows pooled. Younger-Wendell holding the locket, thumb pressed to the engraving, lips moving. And around her, rising out of the walls of the memory like ink through water, other memories bled through: a mountain gate. Ash bells ringing—ringing, alive, centuries ago. A woman's hands, cracked and feverish, pressing a newborn bundle toward a shape in the dark while a voice like settling coals recited terms. Wendell, younger still, in armor that fit her badly, screaming at someone Yara couldn't see: *"You can't take a child's soul for passage, there has to be another way—"*

And then the contract itself, floating up through the layers like a drowned thing surfacing. Names. Dates. A line of text that seemed written in cooling embers, and beside it, in a different hand entirely—in a hand Yara knew better than her own, because she'd watched it write shopping lists and birthday notes and forged customs manifests for seven years—

Wendell's signature. As witness.

The locket snapped shut in her shaking fingers.

*She signed it. She was there. She knew.*

Every deflected question. Every anniversary she refused to mark. Every time Yara had asked *where did I come from* and Wendell had looked at her with love and guilt braided so tightly together they were indistinguishable, and said *you came to me, that's all that matters*—it hadn't been evasion. It had been a confession delivered too quietly to hear.

Yara sat on the edge of a bed softer than any she'd ever slept in, in a room that had been prepared for her before she was born, and she was not sure anymore whether she had come up the mountain to save Wendell from exposure, or to punish her, or simply because some part of her had always known this place was waiting and the waiting was worse than arriving.

A knock. The door opened onto a serving girl with downcast eyes and a tray of wine, and behind the girl, in the corridor, Yara caught a glimpse—just a glimpse, half a heartbeat—of a doorway further along the hall, standing ajar.

Through it: a nursery. Small beds. Small candles burning on small tables, dozens of them, each flame a different color, each flame guttering at its own pace. And in one bed, a child-shaped figure lying perfectly, terribly still.

The serving girl shifted, blocking the view. The door down the corridor swung shut.

"Wine, miss?" the girl whispered, eyes fixed on the floor.

"Yes," Yara heard herself say, from very far away. "Thank you."

Outside the obsidian wall, the starless dark pressed close. Nineteen candles, the herald had called her. She counted the flames she'd seen through that doorway, and lost count past thirty, and understood, with the calm of deep shock, that the court had been expecting payments for a very long time—and that somewhere below all this warmth and music and honey, something was keeping the books.

She dressed in the red and gold they'd left for her. She braided her hair the way Wendell taught her. And she went down to the feast wearing the smile of a girl who belonged here, because the fastest way to learn what the Ember Court truly wanted from her was to let them believe she was ready to give it.
