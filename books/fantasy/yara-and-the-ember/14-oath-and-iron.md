## 14. Oath and Iron

The mountain had a voice.

Wendell heard it first as they made camp in its shadow—a low vibration beneath the wind, like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. The others felt it too. She could tell by the way Brannoc kept his palm flat against the ground, reading tremors through the rock, and by the way Pip's head tilted, listening for something no one else could name.

"It hums," Pip said. He was crouched on a flat stone, sharpening a knife he had no intention of using on anything but locks. "Whole mountain hums. Like a vault with nobody home."

"Something *is* home," Marisol said. She sat cross-legged near their small fire, feeding it pinches of ash-bell petals that burned green and gave off almost no heat. "Something big enough to need a contract the size of a sky."

Wendell said nothing. She stood at the edge of the camp where the scree began, looking up at the black face of the mountain. Somewhere inside it—beneath leagues of stone and wards built on bones—Yara was being welcomed. Being called *the payment*. Being made comfortable, which was worse than being made afraid. Comfort kept a captive still.

Nineteen days. No—eighteen now. The last star had dimmed again while they walked, a guttering ember visible only when the clouds thinned, hanging in a sky otherwise stripped bare. Wendell had caught herself counting stars the way other people counted coins left in a purse, terrified of finding nothing there.

She flexed her right hand. The old scar across her knuckles—the one shaped like a lightning strike, earned the night the order fell—ached in the cold. It always ached before violence. It was aching now, and there was no violence yet to justify it, which meant the ache was memory, and memory was the one enemy she couldn't parry.

"Wendell." Brannoc's voice behind her, low as gravel settling. "You're going to want to hear this."

She turned. The ward-smith stood by the fire, his massive hands wrapped around an iron rod he'd pulled from his pack—an old surveyor's spike, stamped with sigils so worn they were nearly smooth. His face was gray in the green firelight.

"The wards," he said. "I've been feeling them since we crossed the borderlands. Or feeling where they used to be." He drove the spike into the dirt between his boots and pressed both palms to its top. "There's a resonance under everything here. Under the whole country, maybe. It comes from inside this mountain."

"You said you helped build the outer wards," Wendell said. "You told me they were mortared with souls."

"I told you what I could say out loud." Brannoc's jaw worked. "What I haven't told anyone in thirty years is that the wards weren't just *built* with souls. They're *tuned* like them. Every ward-line in the old network rings at the same pitch. One note. One voice." He lifted his hands from the spike, and Wendell saw that his fingers were trembling—not from cold. "That voice is coming from under this mountain. And tonight it changed."

"Changed how?"

Marisol answered without opening her eyes. "It got hungry," she said quietly. "Listen past the hum. There's a second sound underneath. A pull. Like a tide going out."

Pip had stopped sharpening. He was staring at the dark mouth of the valley ahead. "It's not pulling the tide," he said. "It's pulling *us*. Been doing it all day. Thought it was my own nerves."

Wendell felt it then—now that it was named, she couldn't unname it. A faint lateral drag at the base of her spine, gentle as a hand on the small of her back, guiding. Not toward the mountain's front gate, which glowed faintly coal-orange in the distance, warm and inviting as a hearth.

Toward the back of it. Toward the bones.

"That's the old ward-road," Brannoc said, following her gaze. "The skeleton of the original work. It runs straight down into the deep places. The court sealed it after they finished the outer network—but seals are just locks, and locks—" He nodded at Pip.

"—are just songs waiting to be sung," Pip finished, grinning, though the grin didn't reach his eyes.

"We go in through the bones," Wendell said. "Not the front gate. If Yara's being held as a guest, the gate will be guarded by courtesy, and courtesy is harder to kill than soldiers."

No one argued. That was the thing about the crew nobody wanted: they'd each been desperate before, alone, and they recognized the arithmetic of desperation in each other. You didn't waste breath arguing with a plan when the alternative was standing still while a star went out.

They slept in watches. Wendell took the last one, as she always did—she'd never learned to sleep before dawn, and the seven quiet years in Grayhollow hadn't taught her. She sat up with her back against a boulder, the dead sword across her knees.

It wasn't truly dead. That was the lie she'd told herself for seven years, the comfortable fiction that let her sleep at all: *the sword is only iron now, the oaths are only words, I am only a charm-seller.* But iron remembers. Swordspeaker steel held the shape of every vow spoken over it, the way a bell holds the shape of every note it has ever rung. Her master had told her that, the night she took her oaths, kneeling on cold flagstones with the blade laid flat across her palms.

*An oath is not a promise, little wren. A promise is a debt you owe another person. An oath is a debt you owe yourself—and those collect interest.*

His voice came back to her now with terrible clarity, and with it, the oaths themselves. Seven of them. She had spoken them once, aloud, in front of eleven witnesses, and the steel had drunk them in and gone from gray to a brightness like water catching sun. She had spent ten years keeping them. Then one night in a burning hall, with her order dying around her and the Inquisition's torches coming up the road, she had done the only thing left to do: she had stopped speaking them. Folded them away. Buried them alive beneath a merchant's smile, because a swordspeaker who spoke would burn, and someone had to survive to raise a foundling girl whose soul belonged to a court beneath a mountain.

Seven years of silence. She had told herself silence was a kind of loyalty too.

Now she drew the sword six inches from its sheath and looked at the exposed steel.

In the firelight it looked like any well-kept blade—oiled, honed, the fuller running clean down the center. But when she angled it toward the flame, she saw it: a dullness living *inside* the metal, a fogging, like breath on a mirror that never wipes away. Rust of a different kind. The rust of unspoken things.

Her heart began to pound, slow and heavy, the way it did before battles.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to herself. "No, you don't get to do this. Not tonight."

But her hands were already moving. She rose silently, picked her way past the sleeping forms—Brannoc on his back with his arms folded like a man in a coffin, Pip curled tight as a fist, Marisol breathing in that too-even rhythm of hedge-witches who slept with one ear in the other world—and climbed the short slope above the camp until she stood alone under the starless sky.

The mountain loomed. The hum filled her teeth.

She planted her feet the way she'd been taught, shoulder-width, weight settled, and laid the bare blade across both palms. The position was called *the offering*, and she had held it exactly twice in her life: once at nineteen, taking the oaths, and once at twenty-nine, the night she broke them by surviving.

She opened her mouth.

"*I am the word and the iron—*"

Nothing came out.

Not hesitation. Not fear. She knew fear; fear was a familiar animal and she could have spoken through it if that was all it was. This was different. This was the sensation of reaching for a word in your own language and finding the shelf empty—the syllables *there*, she could feel their shapes, but her throat closed around them like a fist around a moth.

She tried again, slower, forcing each word up from wherever they had sunk.

"*I am the word... and the...*"

Her voice cracked apart on the third word. What came out instead was a sound like dry wood splitting, a rasp that scattered a night bird off the rocks below. She stood there with her mouth open, breathing hard, holding a bright sword across her palms in the dark like a woman offering bread to a god who had already eaten.

*Oaths decay,* her master had warned her, in the same voice, in the same year. *A vow unspoken for a season gathers dust. For a decade, gathers rot. Speak yours every morning, little wren, even when no one hears. Especially then. An oath kept in secret is a fire banked in ash—it can be rekindled. An oath abandoned entirely—*

He had never finished the sentence. She had assumed, in the arrogance of youth, that there was nothing after *abandoned*. Nothing worth naming.

She understood the end of the sentence now. An oath abandoned entirely dies. And dead words cannot be unsaid back to life, no matter how loudly you mean them.

Wendell sank to her knees on the scree. The sword stayed bright in her hands—cruelly, insultingly bright, all that stored light with no voice to spend it. She thought of the seven vows one by one, testing each like a tooth:

*I am the word and the iron.* Locked.

*My blade answers my truth.* Locked.

*I speak, therefore the steel remembers.* Locked, locked, locked. All seven, rusted shut in the dark of her throat like hinges in a house where no one had lived for years.

She had hidden her magic so long, so completely, that hiding had become its own spell—and the spell had worked perfectly. It had silenced her so thoroughly that even she could no longer break it.

A laugh came out of her, and it was not a kind sound. Here she was, at the foot of the mountain, eighteen days from the death of the last star, leading three broken strangers into the deep places of the world to steal back her daughter's soul—and the great weapon she had carried secretly, faithfully, at the cost of everything, turned out to be a corpse she'd mistaken for a sleeping soldier.

The past wouldn't arm her after all. She had spent seven years preserving the past, and the past had rotted quietly in its sheath while she sold charms and smiled and told herself she was being loyal.

*Loyalty kept in silence.* The thought arrived with Marisol's voice attached, from weeks ago, from a fire much like this one: *is just loneliness wearing armor.*

"Then what good am I?" she asked the mountain.

The mountain hummed. Beneath the hum, fainter now—or maybe she was only imagining it—the pull. The tide going out. Somewhere below, a court of coals was teaching her daughter the word *belong*, and every day Yara stayed, the word would set a little deeper, like dye in cloth, until Grayhollow smelled of smoke and cheap tallow and the girl remembered it as the place she had fled rather than the place she had been loved.

Wendell looked down at the sword.

And saw, in the fogged depths of the steel, movement.

She went still. Held the blade closer to the firelight. The dullness inside the metal—the rust-of-oaths—wasn't sitting still. It was *shifting*, slowly, the way sediment shifts in water that has been disturbed from far away. As she watched, the fog gathered along the fuller in a thin line and pulsed. Once. Twice.

Like a heartbeat.

Or like something answering a question she hadn't known she'd asked.

"What good am I?" she whispered again, and the pulse flared—brief, faint, a coal remembering fire—and died.

Not dead, then. Dying. Starved of speech the way a muscle starves of use, but not gone. Oaths decayed, yes—but decay implied something left to decay. Her master had been wrong about one thing, or she had misheard him all those years ago: perhaps the sentence hadn't ended at *abandoned entirely dies*. Perhaps the true ending was crueler and stranger. Perhaps an oath abandoned doesn't die—it goes *underground*, and waits, and will not answer to the old words at all.

To the old words. The ones spoken by a frightened girl kneeling on flagstones, promising herself to an order, to a doctrine, to a law that would one day outlaw her existence.

The old words were rusted shut because they had been built for a world that no longer existed—for an order that burned, for masters who died, for laws written by men who feared what women like Wendell could say. Of course those words had rotted. She had buried them in the ruins of everything they stood for.

Which meant—if there was any truth in the mad, desperate hope suddenly climbing her throat like warmth returning to frozen fingers—that the answer was not to dig up the corpse.

It was to speak new words. Words that belonged to the woman she had become instead of the girl she had been. Words with no order behind them, no witnesses, no law—only a merchant's stubbornness and a mother's fury and eighteen days left on a dying star.

But not tonight. Tonight her throat was raw and her hands shook and the mountain's hunger was rising like floodwater. Tonight she needed to know the ground she'd stand on.

She resheathed the sword, climbed back down to the camp, and found Brannoc awake, watching her with eyes that missed nothing.

"You tried," he said. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"You walked up that hill like a woman going to confession." He shifted, making room beside him at the fire. "My brother tried the same thing, the last night before they took him. Stood on a ridge and shouted his ward-oaths at the sky until his voice gave out." His voice didn't waver, but his hands, wrapped around his knees, went white-knuckled. "Nothing answered him either. The wards don't listen anymore. Maybe nothing does."

"Maybe," Wendell said. She sat. The fire had burned down to coals, and in their light her scarred knuckles looked almost luminous. "Or maybe it isn't the oaths that stopped listening. Maybe it's the words."

Brannoc frowned. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the oaths I swore were sworn to the Order of the Speaking Blade. To masters. To a law." She stared into the coals. "All of it's ash. So maybe the oaths went with it. And maybe—" she hesitated, hating how the hope sounded aloud, fragile as spun sugar, "—maybe whatever's left in the steel is waiting for different words. Mine."

Brannoc was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "My grandmother used to say a tool doesn't care who swings it. Only whether the hand is honest."

"Your grandmother sounds smarter than my entire order."

"She also said honest hands usually wait until the worst possible moment to figure out what they believe." Almost—almost—a smile. "Get some sleep, Brightwater. Tomorrow we go into the bones of the world, and I'd rather face it next to a woman with a plan than a woman with a prayer."

Wendell lay down with the sword against her spine, the way she'd slept for twenty years, and stared up at the blank sky where the last star hung invisible behind cloud.

Eighteen days. Rusted words. A heartbeat in the steel.

As she drifted toward uneasy sleep, the mountain's hum seemed to shift—just slightly, just once—into something almost like a voice. Almost like recognition.

Almost like it knew she was coming, and had already set a place for her.
