## 15. Nineteen Candles

The vault of the Ember Court's waystation did not smell like sulfur, as the stories promised. It smelled like tallow and beeswax and something sweeter underneath—something Marisol recognized from her grandmother's kitchen in Tavarel, before the Inquisition came and made ash of everything sweet.

Candles. The correspondence they'd stolen from the vault was not paper at all, or not only paper. Bundled among the ledgers Pip had cracked open with his crooning fingers were dozens of candles wrapped in oilcloth, each one labeled in a spidery copperplate script: a name, a date, a number.

Marisol had been a hedge-witch for forty years. She knew what a death-candle looked like. She'd carried her own against her ribs since the day the Inquisition burned her village and marked her soul as forfeit—a little stub of gray wax that warmed when she walked toward danger and cooled when she was safe, so that she always knew exactly how much life she was walking into.

She'd never seen a room full of them.

"Wendell," she said, and her voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Wendell, come look at this."

* * *

They'd made camp in the waystation's upper chamber because it had a roof and one intact wall, and because Brannoc said the old ward-lines beneath the floor still held a ghost of their original charge—not enough to stop anything, but enough to warn them if something crossed. Marisol didn't trust it. She didn't trust anything built by hands like Brannoc's anymore. But she spread her cloak on the stone floor anyway, laid out the candles she'd pulled from the bundle, and lit the single lamp she allowed herself.

Eighteen candles. She counted twice. Eighteen.

Each one was tall—taller than any candle had a right to be, a forearm's length of wax the color of old milk—and each one was cold. Not unlit-cold. *Finished*-cold. The particular cold of a thing that had done all its burning and had nothing left to give.

She picked up the first one and read the label.

*Vessia Corrane. Taken under contract, 4th of Deepfrost, sixty-one years past. Paid in full.*

Underneath, smaller, in the same copperplate: *One star.*

Marisol sat down hard on the floor.

"What is it?" Wendell stood in the doorway with her sword hand flexing at nothing—the habit of a woman who hadn't drawn steel in seven years and couldn't stop reaching for it anyway. Behind her loomed Brannoc, huge and grim, and Pip's narrow face peering around his elbow.

"Come here," Marisol said. "And someone sit down before I say what I'm about to say."

Pip sat immediately. He was the only sensible one among them.

* * *

She told them what she knew first, because the knowing was bad enough and she wanted witnesses to the moment it got worse.

"A hedge-witch learns candle-work young," she said, turning the first candle in her hands. "It's small magic. A candle for grief, burned at a funeral. A candle for luck, lit on a doorstep. And—" she hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, because she'd hidden worse than this from worse people, but these people were starting to feel like hers and that was its own kind of terror— "and a candle for the dying. When a hedge-witch knows someone is near the end, she makes a death-candle. It burns while they live and goes out when they do. It doesn't *do* anything. It's a mirror, nothing more. You can't kill a man by pinching his flame."

"You're sure about that?" Brannoc asked. His voice was flat as slate.

"I'm sure about hedge-magic. This—" she turned the candle again, let the lamplight slide along its length— "this isn't hedge-magic. Look at the wax. See how it's gone glassy at the base? That's what happens when a candle holds a whole soul instead of just a shadow of one. My grandmother called it *soul-tallow*. She said no honest witch would make it. She said the last people who tried lived under mountains and answered to no god anyone prayed to."

"The Ember Court," Wendell said quietly.

"The Ember Court." Marisol set down Vessia Corrane's candle and touched the next. *Hale Ondris. Taken under contract, 19th of Sowmonth, fifty-eight years past.* Then the next. *Mira Solenne. Forty-one years past.* Each label older than the last, each date marching backward through the decades like footprints going home.

"Eighteen candles," she said. "Eighteen souls. And every one of them has a star written at the bottom of its name."

She looked up at Wendell and made herself say it plainly, because plainness was the only kindness left in the room.

"There are nineteen stars over Grayhollow, Wendell. There have been nineteen since before you were born. One went out the night the wards broke. One goes out every time one of these candles finishes burning." She spread her hands over the row of dead wax. "These aren't records of the collection. They *are* the collection. Every star that's died in the last two months—every star that's been going out, night after night, while the city blames smugglers and unregistered mages and the phases of some unlucky moon—each one of those was a person. Someone's daughter. Someone's brother. Collected under contract, rendered into tallow, and burned down to nothing. The stars weren't falling, Wendell. They were being *spent*."

Nobody spoke. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, deep in the mountain's roots, water moved through channels that had once carried ward-charge and now carried nothing at all.

Pip broke the silence, because Pip always did, his voice pitched high and thin with the effort of joking through it: "So when we get Yara back, do we get her star relit, or is that extra? Because I feel like that should be included."

"Pip," Wendell said.

"No, I'm serious. I want it in writing. Souls returned, star restored, no additional fees—"

"*Pip.*"

He shut up, but Marisol caught his eye as he did, and he caught hers, and something passed between them that needed no words: he joked because the alternative was screaming, and she let him, because she'd spent forty years learning that the ones who joke longest survive to bury the rest.

* * *

Brannoc was the one who found the final ledger page, tucked inside the oilcloth wrapping of the nineteenth candle—the only one in the bundle that wasn't finished. It still had wick. It was shorter than the others, half-burned already, and when Marisol unwrapped it her own death-stub, riding warm against her ribs, went suddenly, terribly cold.

Not cold like danger. Cold like recognition.

The label read:

*Yara. Payment outstanding. Contract of [name struck through], brokered under seal of the Grayhollow Inquisition. Term: upon the extinguishing of the nineteenth star, payment renders in full. Balance remaining: nineteen days.*

"Nineteen days," Wendell said. She'd gone very still, the stillness of a woman holding a door shut against a flood. "Nineteen days until the last star dies. Nineteen days until—"

"Until the contract executes," Marisol finished. "Yes."

But there was more on the page, and she made herself read all of it, because she was the one who could read it, and because she'd learned long ago that horror deferred is horror doubled. Below the terms, in that same patient copperplate, was a notation—not a record of the contract but a note about its purpose, the kind of marginalia a clerk adds when even clerks need to remind themselves why.

Marisol read it aloud. Her voice stayed steady. She was proud of that, later. It was the steadiest thing in her.

*"Note for the archive: the nineteen are not tribute. They are mechanism. The Court does not collect souls for wealth or dominion; it collects them for the Keeping. Each soul rendered feeds the seal beneath the Mountain-that-Burns. The contract's completion—with the nineteenth payment—will either lull the Kept Thing into its next century of sleep, or, should payment fail, wake it hungry. The Court prefers sleep. The Court advises all brokers to prefer sleep."*

She lowered the page.

"It keeps something asleep," she said. "Or fed. The wording won't commit, which tells me the clerk who wrote it wasn't sure either. But either way, Yara isn't the end of the debt. She's the *last installment*. Her soul goes into the ground under that mountain, and whatever's down there rolls over and sleeps for another hundred years, and the stars stay dark, and the Ember Court goes on selling passage through the wards to desperate mothers with dying daughters."

"And if we steal her back?" Brannoc asked. His voice was rougher now. His hands, Marisol noticed, were fists. "If we pull the nineteenth payment out from under them?"

"Then the contract fails. The seal runs dry. And the Kept Thing wakes up hungry, with eighteen centuries of appetite and nothing between it and the world but us."

* * *

Wendell walked out onto the broken balcony after that. Marisol gave her ten minutes alone, which was nine minutes more than she'd have wanted herself, and then followed.

Grayhollow was a smudge of light far behind them, down the dead road, too distant to see the panic that surely still gripped it. Above, the sky was a wound. Nineteen stars had hung over the borderlands for as long as anyone living could remember—old constellations, older names, the kind of stars that appear on heraldry and in children's rhymes. Now there was one. Marisol found it without looking hard. It hung low over the mountain, reddish, guttering, like an ember deciding whether to become ash.

"That's her," Wendell said, without turning. "That's Yara's star, isn't it."

"Yes."

"It's already dying."

"It's been dying for sixteen years, Wendell. Since the day her mother signed." Marisol came to stand beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. "Death-candles don't lie. That girl's been on borrowed time since before she could walk, and nobody told her, and nobody told you, and the people who knew—" she tapped the ledger page folded in her belt— "the people who knew were wearing white hoods and calling it justice."

Wendell's jaw worked. "I raised her. Sixteen years. I taught her to read and to ride and how to win an argument, and the whole time there was a candle in a vault under a mountain with her name on it, burning down."

"Yes."

"And I didn't know."

"No." Marisol watched the red star flicker. "You didn't. And here's the part that's going to keep you up tonight, Brightwater, so I'll say it now and be done: neither did I, and I've been carrying my own death-candle for forty years. I know exactly what it feels like to have your soul priced and filed away by people who never met you. I should have guessed. I should have *looked*. We all should have. The Inquisition counted on us not looking. That's how rot works—it doesn't hide, it just waits for everyone to be too tired or too scared or too busy surviving to dig."

Wendell finally turned. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, and Marisol thought, not for the first time, that the last oath-bound swordspeaker of a murdered order looked less like a warrior than like a woman who'd been holding her breath for seven years and had just remembered she was allowed to exhale.

"Then we dig," Wendell said.

"We dig," Marisol agreed. "But hear me, and hear me clear, because I'm the one who reads the fine print in this crew: we're not stealing a girl back from a kidnapper. We're walking into a court that's been running a machine for eighteen generations, and the machine needs feeding, and if we break the machine without understanding what it holds down—" she nodded at the red star, at the black sky around it, at the whole wounded dark— "then everything we love is standing in the open field when it gets out. Nineteen days, Wendell. We need more than a sword and a thief and a grudge. We need to know what's under that mountain. We need to know who put it there. And we need to find out whether there's any way to close a door that's been propped open with souls—without letting what's behind it come through."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Wendell said, quietly, "Serah Vane would know."

Marisol laughed—one short, bitter bark. "Your inquisitor friend? The one hunting illegal charms through a city where the real crime is being committed by her own superiors?"

"She left irons on my table and took none of them," Wendell said. "There's a woman under that badge somewhere. And she has access to Inquisition archives that would take us months to crack."

"So send for her."

"She's already coming. Serah Vane has never once in her life let a mystery go unanswered. If I know anything about her—and I knew her when we were girls, before either of us knew what the world cost—she's following our trail right now, and she's furious about it."

Behind them, from inside, Pip's voice floated out, reedy and determinedly cheerful: "Found another candle! This one's labeled *miscellaneous*. Do you think the court has a junk drawer? I bet the Ember Queen has a junk drawer. I bet it's full of—"

"*Miscellaneous,*" Marisol repeated, and felt the cold walk up her spine again.

Because she'd read the labels. All of them. Eighteen names, each with a star, each with a date stretching back across nearly two centuries of quiet, orderly collection. And she'd noted, in the professional corner of her mind where hedge-witches keep the things they don't say aloud, that the dates weren't evenly spaced. There were gaps. Years, sometimes decades, between collections—as though the court collected only when it must, only when the seal ran low, only when the thing below began to stir.

And the most recent gap—the gap before Yara's contract—had lasted thirty-one years.

Thirty-one years of sleep, bought with a single soul. And now, with the seal grown weak and the wards collapsed and the stars nearly spent, the court had come due again.

Marisol picked up Yara's half-burned candle and held it cupped in her palms, careful as an egg, and felt the faint warmth of it—the only warmth in the whole cold bundle. Half gone. Sixteen years of borrowed time, and nineteen days left on the clock.

"Hold on, girl," she murmured, to the wax, to the soul inside it, to the fierce stubborn foundling halfway to a mountain gate by now and trusting no one enough to wait. "We're coming. And we're bringing the truth with us, and we'll see whose law chokes on it."

Above the mountain, Yara's star guttered—and steadied—and guttered again, like a heartbeat trying to remember its rhythm.
