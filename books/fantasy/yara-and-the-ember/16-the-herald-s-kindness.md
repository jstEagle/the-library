## 16. The Herald's Kindness

They bathed her in water that smelled like struck flint.

Yara had expected chains. She had rehearsed screaming in her head for the last three miles of the tunnel—rehearsed biting, kicking, spitting out whatever they poured down her throat. She had imagined the mountain as a mouth, and herself as the meal sliding down it.

Instead, a woman with coal-veined hands had knelt at the gate and said, *Welcome home, payment,* and then everything had become soft.

The bath was carved from a single block of black stone, and the water held its heat without steaming, as if the mountain itself were warming it from below. Servants—were they servants? They moved too quietly, and their eyes reflected light like an animal's—brought linens warmed over coals and combs of dark horn and a robe the color of embers banked for the night. When Yara flinched from their touch, they withdrew instantly, gracefully, without offense, as though they had all the patience in the world and no need at all to hurry.

"Where am I?" she asked, for the fourth time.

The herald answered, for the fourth time, with that same warm voice that made the air feel thick. "In the court beneath the mountain. Where you have been owed since before you could walk." He stood at the edge of the steam with his hands folded, tall and narrow, his face smooth as river stone. His name, he'd said, was Vessin. "And where you will be honored."

"Honored," Yara repeated. The word felt wrong in her mouth, like a coin she suspected was counterfeit.

"You are not a prisoner here, Yara." He said her name carefully, savoring each syllable, the way she'd once heard Wendell say *daughter* on the night of her sixteenth birthday, right before the lie about the locket. "You are a guest of consequence. Everything you see tonight is yours to walk through. Nothing is yours to break. That is the whole of our law."

"And if I want to leave?"

Vessin smiled. It was a good smile. That was the worst part. It reached his eyes, or something behind his eyes, and it made her want to smile back, and she hated how much she wanted to.

"The gate opens when you are ready," he said. "It has always opened. No one has ever walked through it."

She wrapped the ember-colored robe tighter around herself and did not answer, because she understood what he'd just told her: the door wasn't locked. It didn't need to be.

* * *

They fed her in a hall that should not have existed underground.

The ceiling was high enough that she couldn't see it; instead there was darkness up there, and in the darkness, points of dim red light, arranged loosely, drifting slowly, like a sky seen through blood. She counted them while she ate. Forty-one. Then forty-two. Then forty-one again, and one of the lights near the far edge simply wasn't there anymore, and no one else in the hall seemed to notice.

The food was extraordinary. She hated that too. In Grayhollow she'd eaten salt cod and gray bread and, on good days, honey that Wendell bought from the charm-seller two stalls down and pretended was ordinary. Here there was meat glazed with something sweet and smoky, bread that tore open in clouds of steam, fruit she had no names for, wine-dark juice in cups of glass so thin she was afraid to hold them. Every dish arrived already knowing what she liked. There was honey. Of course there was honey.

"You're staring at the ceiling," Vessin observed. He sat across from her, eating nothing, watching everything.

"What are those lights?"

"Guests," he said.

"Guests don't drift."

"Long guests, then." His tone didn't change at all. "Eat, Yara. Growing takes strength, and you have years of it ahead."

That sentence snagged in her chest like a fishhook and stayed there through the rest of the meal. *Years of it.* As if she would be here long enough for growing to matter. As if the court had plans measured in decades.

Afterward they gave her rooms. Rooms, plural. A sleeping chamber with a bed wide enough for four people, hung with curtains the color of cooling coals. A sitting room with a fire that burned without wood—a fire that turned toward her when she entered, the way a dog turns toward its master, and settled lower, warmer, gentler. A window that looked out onto nothing: solid rock, polished black, in which her reflection stood holding a candle that wasn't there.

On the table beside the bed lay a gift, wrapped in cloth stitched with tiny stars.

Her stars. Or the shapes of them—the ones that had fallen out of Grayhollow's sky eleven days ago. She knew them the way you know your own handwriting. Her stomach dropped through the floor of the mountain.

She unwrapped it anyway. Inside was a locket.

Not hers. Hers was hidden in the lining of her boot, humming faintly against her ankle bone, warm as a held hand. This one was larger, gold rather than silver, and cold. Cold the way a well is cold. She almost put it down. Then she thought: *Wendell lied to me for sixteen years. I'm done being protected by other people's silences.* And she opened it.

Inside, where a portrait should be, there was a small round mirror, and in the mirror her own face looked back at her—and kept looking after she closed her eyes.

She snapped it shut and threw it across the room. It landed without a sound.

When she finally slept, curled in the vast bed with her real locket gripped in her fist until its edges printed themselves into her palm, she dreamed of a voice like coals settling, saying her name in the dark, saying it kindly, saying it the way you say a thing you have already paid for.

* * *

In the morning—or what the court called morning, when the corridor lamps brightened from red to amber—Vessin came for her himself.

"Today," he said, "I thought you might like to see where you come from."

"I know where I come from," Yara said. "Grayhollow. Charm Row. The crooked house with the green door."

"That is where you were *kept*," Vessin said gently. "I mean where you come from. Your lineage. Your weight." He spread his hands. "You have spent sixteen years being told you were nobody's. Foundling. Borrowed child. I imagine it chafes, even if you love the woman who told you so."

Yara's jaw tightened. "You've never met Wendell Brightwater."

"I have read her file." Something flickered across his smooth face—amusement, maybe, or appetite. "Come. You deserve to know what you are worth. Everyone deserves to know their own price."

He led her through corridors that breathed. That was the only way she could describe it. The air moved in slow rhythms, in and out, warm against her skin, and sometimes the walls sweated beads of moisture that glowed faintly like dying coals. Servants bowed as they passed—not to Vessin, she noticed. To her. She tried to catch one of their eyes and failed; their gazes slid off her face the way rain slides off oiled canvas.

"You could tell me the truth," she said, as they climbed a stair cut in a spiral so wide it took twenty steps to turn once. "Whatever this is. The contract, the stars, the—" she touched her boot, where the locket hummed. "I found the signature. I saw the gate. My mother sold me. I'm not going to fall apart because someone says it out loud."

"No," Vessin agreed. "You won't. That is one of the reasons you were chosen." He glanced back at her, and his coal-warm kindness was still there, but underneath it, for half a heartbeat, she saw the thing that looked at the world through him—and it was old, and patient, and very hungry. Then it was gone, and he was only a courteous man showing a guest her inheritance.

"Your mother," he said, "was named Sefa Ilvesen. A ward-tinker's daughter from the ash country. Clever with knots and charms. She carried you through three hundred miles of dead borderland while pregnant, because the wards would not let a soul-contracted child be born inside them—they'd have taken you at birth otherwise, and she knew it. Do you understand what that means?" He paused at a landing where a lamp burned white and steady. "It means every day of your life before your first breath was purchased by her courage. You come from stubbornness, Yara. From refusal. The sale was the one surrender of her life, and even that she made with her teeth in someone's arm."

Yara hadn't known any of it. Not the name. Not the ash country. Not the three hundred miles. Something in her chest cracked along an old seam—grief for a mother who had been a person, a whole person, running through the dark with her unborn daughter's soul already signed away, and why, *why*, what could have been bad enough—

"The wards were failing," Vessin said, reading her face with horrible ease. "The winter she crossed, they were failing everywhere. Souls went into the mortar, in those days, and the mortar was thinning. She was offered passage for herself and safe birth for you, in exchange for a debt to be collected on your sixteenth year. She wept for six days. I have the record of it. Would you like to hear it sometime? Her actual words?"

"Yes," Yara said immediately.

"So would we all," Vessin murmured, and resumed climbing. "But records burn, in the courts above. Only we keep what matters."

At the top of the stair, a door of black iron banded with copper stood slightly ajar, breathing warm air into the corridor. Beyond it, Yara could hear music. Faint, sweet, plucked strings.

"Before I show you the rest," Vessin said, and now his voice was softer than it had ever been, gentle as a hand smoothing a blanket over a sleeper, "I want to say something plainly, because you strike me as someone who has been lied to often enough to smell it coming. What is owed to us is owed. We do not apologize for collecting it, and we do not pretend it is anything other than what it is. But between now and the collection, you are ours to care for, and we care well. Ask anyone here. Ask them what the court has given them." He smiled his terrible kind smile. "Ask them if they ever want to leave."

* * *

The nursery was on the level above the great hall, and it was full of children.

That was her first thought, seeing them through the open doorway: *children*, dozens of them, in a long warm room lit by lamps of rose-colored glass. Small ones playing a game with knucklebones on the floor. Older ones reading. One boy, perhaps ten, standing at a window of polished rock, conducting an invisible orchestra with both hands while the plucked-string music drifted from somewhere unseen. They were all ages. They were all quiet. Not sad-quiet. Settled-quiet, the way a house is quiet at evening when everyone in it has stopped expecting anything else to happen.

A nurse looked up—a broad woman with kind eyes and coal-veined hands—and inclined her head to Yara with unmistakable deference.

"This is the Foundling Wing," Vessin said. "Or, as we call it more honestly, the Nursery of Payments." He said it without cruelty. That was somehow worse. "Every soul the court holds under contract spends its childhood here. You would not have been alone, Yara. You would have grown up among others of your own kind. Children whose parents loved them enough to make hard bargains."

One of the little ones—a girl of four or five with braids coming loose—looked up from her knucklebones, met Yara's eyes, and smiled a wide, unguarded, gap-toothed smile.

"Are you the new one?" the girl asked. "Mama said the new one comes when the sky goes dark."

The room went very still. Not the people. The room. The lamps dimmed by a hair, all together, like lungs drawing breath.

Yara crouched down to the girl's level, because that was what you did with small children, because Wendell had taught her that, because the crooked house with the green door had taught her that. "What's your name?"

"Nell."

"How long have you lived here, Nell?"

Nell considered this with the gravity of the very young. "Always," she said. Then, correcting herself with the scrupulous honesty of the very young: "Since my mama went home. Everybody's mama goes home." She picked up a knucklebone, weighed it in her palm. "Except yours. Yours sold you all the way." She said it without malice, the way a child reports the weather. "That's why you get the big rooms."

The floor tilted. Or Yara did. She put a hand against the wall and the wall was warm, breathing, alive, and somewhere beneath her feet—far beneath, deeper than the nursery, deeper than anything—she felt something turn over in its sleep, slow as a season changing, and the warmth in the walls was not the mountain's warmth. It was fed warmth. It was the warmth of a belly that is full.

*Nell,* she thought. *How many stars is Nell.*

Forty-one lights in the ceiling of the dining hall. Forty-two. Forty-one. She thought of the boy conducting his invisible orchestra, and the readers with their books, and the settled silence, and she understood—at last, completely, with a clarity that felt like falling—that none of these children had ever been collected. That the contract did not take them young. That the court raised them, and fed them, and taught them, and let them grow strong and clever and full of years—

Because the older the soul, the heavier it weighs.

"Yara," Vessin said softly behind her. "You've gone pale. Come away from the door. The little ones unsettle everyone at first. You'll grow used to—"

"Don't," she said. Her voice came out low and even and did not sound like hers. "Don't say *used to*. Don't say another kind word. I know what this is now." She straightened, and her hand found the locket in her boot, and it was burning hot against her fingers, humming like a struck bell, like it had been trying to warn her since the gate. "You don't keep children because you're kind. You keep them because you're *patient*. Sixteen years in Grayhollow, and I thought the scariest thing in the world was a bonfire in the market square." She looked at Nell, at her gap-toothed smile, at the knucklebone turning in her small hand. "You're worse than the Inquisition. They burn you fast. You raise us like livestock and call it a nursery."

For the first time, Vessin's smile failed.

It didn't twist into anger. It simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and for a moment the face beneath it was nothing at all—a smooth stone mask with an ancient hunger looking out—and the temperature in the corridor dropped ten degrees, and every lamp on the level guttered at once, and deep beneath the floor the sleeping thing shifted again, closer to waking.

Then the warmth returned. The smile reassembled itself, piece by courteous piece.

"There she is," Vessin said quietly. Almost admiringly. "Sefa Ilvesen's daughter. Teeth in someone's arm."

He stepped aside, gesturing down the corridor with perfect grace, and Yara walked past him with her spine straight and her heart hammering, back toward the rooms that were not hers, in the mountain that had been waiting for her all her life.

Behind her, in the nursery, Nell had gone back to her knucklebones. But as the door swung shut, Yara heard her say—clearly, cheerfully, to no one in particular:

"She'll fight. The fighting ones taste loudest."

That night, Yara packed nothing, because there was nothing of hers to pack. She sat in the window of black glass with her mother's name in her mouth—*Sefa Ilvesen, Sefa Ilvesen*—and the locket hot against her palm, and far above the mountain, in a sky she could not see, she knew the next star was guttering.

Nineteen days. Pip had said nineteen, back in the waystation vault, and she'd run anyway, and now the number lived under her ribs like a swallowed coal.

Somewhere beyond the wards, Wendell was coming for her. Yara was sure of it the way she was sure of her own heartbeat—sure, and terrified, because now she knew exactly what her rescue would cost, and she knew, too, that the Ember Court knew it was coming.

They weren't preparing a defense.

They were setting another place at the table.
