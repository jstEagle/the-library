## 17. What Loyalty Costs

Brannoc Hale had not slept in a proper bed since the night the wards fell, and he had stopped pretending it was an accident.

He sat now on a stool outside Marisol's cottage, where the crew had gathered like survivors of a shipwreck who hadn't yet agreed to row in the same direction. The hedge-witch's garden was a riot of things that should not grow together—feverfew strangling foxglove, a rosebush trained up a gallows-post she'd bought at auction out of pure spite—and Brannoc found he liked her for it. A woman who planted flowers in dead men's wood understood something about him.

Inside, Wendell Brightwater was arguing with Pip about rations. The thief's voice carried, bright and needling: *"—all I'm saying is if we're walking into a mountain that eats souls, I'd rather not do it hungry—"*

And through the window, Brannoc could see Yara sitting at the table, turning her mother's locket over and over in her fingers, listening to all of them with the particular patience of someone who had already decided to leave without them.

He knew that look. He'd worn it himself, thirty years ago, standing before the first ward-stone with a hammer in his hand and his brother's name still wet on his tongue.

*You're going to tell them,* said the voice in his head that had been getting louder every night since the stars went out. *Or you're going to carry it into the mountain and let it crush you there.*

The stars were gone. All of them. And Brannoc Hale knew exactly why, because he had helped build the machine that was eating them.

---

He had been twenty-two when the Inquisition came to the ward-smiths' guild with a commission sealed in white wax, and he had been young enough to think white wax meant clean hands.

*Build us a border no spell can cross,* the commissioners said. *A wall of law against lawlessness. You will be paid in coin, in title, in the gratitude of a grateful city.*

What they did not say—what was written in a codicil Brannoc only read later, by candlelight, with his hands shaking—was what the wards would be mortared with.

Ward-work was old craft, older than Grayhollow, older than the Purge. Every smith knew the theory: a ward is a promise made permanent, and promises cost. A village ward might be fed on a smith's sworn intention, renewed each season with honest labor. A city ward took more—a tithe of memory, perhaps, or the strength of a shared oath spoken by ten thousand throats at once. The old ways were expensive but they were *voluntary*. That was the whole architecture of it. Magic, like law, only held if people consented to be bound.

The Inquisition's wards were built different.

They needed power that would never fail, never need renewing, never depend on the goodwill of the governed. And there was only one fuel in the world that burned forever and asked nothing back.

Souls.

Brannoc remembered the first collection. He had been told it would be a criminal's soul—some murderer from the deep cells, justice repurposed. He had stood at the anchor-stone with his gauges and his tongs, ready to bind whatever they gave him into the lattice, telling himself the lie so smoothly he almost believed it.

Then they brought out a boy.

Not a criminal. A debtor. Nineteen years old, sold by the debt-collectors under a clause Brannoc would later find replicated in a hundred contracts across forty years: *payment rendered in kind.* The Inquisition had learned that souls could be levied like taxes, purchased like grain, and the courts beneath the mountain—the Ember Court, though no one said that name above a whisper—had learned it too, and the two institutions had shaken hands over the heads of everyone they served.

The boy didn't struggle. That was the worst of it. He looked at the anchor-stone, and then at Brannoc, and he said, "Will it hurt?"

"No," Brannoc had lied, because the alternative was admitting he didn't know.

It didn't hurt, as far as anyone could measure. The boy simply stopped being *there*, the way a candle stops being lit—not destroyed, not gone, just... transferred. Into the stone. Into the wall. Into the great humming lattice that stretched from the Ashen Pass to the Saltmere cliffs, three hundred miles of border held upright by four hundred and eleven human beings who had been told their debts were being forgiven, or their passage purchased, or their crimes pardoned.

Four hundred and eleven. Brannoc knew the number because he had kept the ledger himself, in the early years, before he understood what he was keeping. He had written each name in his own careful smith's hand. Entry the first:

**Hale, Tammas. Age nineteen. Debt: fourteen crowns, six shillings.**

His brother.

Tammas had owed money to the wrong people, the way younger brothers sometimes do, and when the Inquisition's agents came offering to clear the debt—for a signature, a formality, a formality, Brannoc—they had come to *him* first, the rising star of the guild, and asked if he thought his brother would consent.

And Brannoc, twenty-two years old and drunk on his own importance, had said: *Tammas will do whatever I ask him to.*

That was the word they hid behind, all of them, for forty years. *Consent.* Tammas signed. Tammas walked to the stone. Tammas asked if it would hurt. But consent given to a brother's request, under pressure of debt, dressed up in official wax—that wasn't consent. That was a lever, and Brannoc had handed them the lever and called it loyalty.

Loyalty. He turned the word over now, sitting in Marisol's garden with his scarred hands flat on his knees. He had spent thirty years telling himself he'd been loyal—to the guild, to the city, to the commission. He had spent thirty years learning, slowly, in the dark hours, that what he'd actually been was *useful*, and that usefulness was the cheapest thing in the world to buy and the most expensive thing in the world to stop being.

When he finally understood—truly understood, the night he found the codicil and traced his brother's name in the ledger—he had gone to the guildmasters. He had gone to the Inquisition. He had stood in offices with white wax seals on the doors and told the truth, all of it, in a voice that shook.

They stripped his certification. They blackened his name in the guild rolls. They let him keep his tools, which was either mercy or mockery, and they let him live, which was neither. A disgraced ward-smith who talked about souls in the mortar was simply a madman, and madmen were not worth burning. Burning made martyrs. Madness made silence.

So he had been silent for thirty years. He had drunk. He had taken work repairing chimney-flues and blessing door-hinges, craft so small it was beneath the law's notice. And once a year, on no particular date, he had walked out past the border stones to the third anchor-point and put his palm against the warm granite, and felt his brother humming in there like a struck bell that never finished ringing, and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the stone didn't already know.

Until the night the stars went out.

---

He had known immediately. That was the thing nobody else understood—Pip thought it was sorcery, Marisol thought it was omen, even Wendell, sharp as she was, had grasped only the shape of the catastrophe and not its engine. But Brannoc had stood in the street with his face tipped up to a sky going dark star by star, and he had felt the pull through the soles of his boots, through the bedrock, through thirty years of guilt hardened into instinct, and he had known: *the lattice is being drawn down. Something is calling in its debts.*

The wards weren't failing. The wards were being *collected*.

Every soul in the mortar was a candle, and candles burn down, and someone beneath the mountain had decided the burning was done. The stars overhead weren't stars at all anymore—they were the visible register of the contract, each one a soul's account, guttering as the Ember Court drew payment home. Four hundred and eleven lights. He had counted them going out. He had counted them the way you count anything when the number includes your brother.

Which brought him to now. To this garden. To this crew of liars and exiles and one girl with a locket, preparing to walk into the mountain and steal back what the law said had been legally, properly, consensually sold.

Marisol came out with two cups of something that smelled like pine tar and courage, and sat on the step beside him without asking permission. She was good at that, the exile's courtesy of not asking questions.

"You haven't touched your gear," she said.

"It's not the gear."

"No," she agreed. "It never is."

Inside, the argument had shifted. He could hear Wendell's low voice now, steady, laying out the route through the dead borderlands, the waystation vaults, the descent. And underneath it, quieter, Yara's voice asking a question Brannoc couldn't catch—but he caught Wendell's answer, or rather the shape of it: a pause too long, a deflection soft as cloth.

The girl deserved better than cloth. So did the woman lying to her. So, God help him, did the brother in the wall.

"I built the wards," Brannoc said.

Marisol didn't startle. She drank her terrible tea and watched him sideways. "I know."

"You *know*?"

"Hedge-witch, love. I've felt the lattice my whole life. It doesn't hum like consent. It hums like a cellar full of locked doors." She shrugged. "I figured somebody built it. Somebody always does."

"Somebody with a name." His hands had closed into fists without his permission. "Somebody with a brother."

She set down her cup. "Then say it inside," she said. "Say it where it counts. Guilt's a fine fire, Brannoc, but it burns the house down if you keep it in the hearth of your own skull."

---

He waited until the maps were spread and the argument had worn itself down to logistics. Then he stood, and the room went quiet the way rooms do when a big man decides to speak.

"There's a thing you should know before you follow me anywhere," he said. "And after you know it, you're free to leave me on the road."

Wendell's eyes narrowed. Pip stopped fidgeting. Yara went very still, the locket pressed flat under her palm.

"The border wards aren't law," Brannoc said. "They're a ledger. Every mile of that wall is mortared with souls—four hundred and eleven of them, bound into the anchor-stones over forty years. The Inquisition called it debt settlement. Passage fees. Criminal restitution. Mostly it was poor people, and the paperwork was beautiful." He heard his own voice go flat, smith-flat, the tone he used for stating weights and measures. "I built the lattice. I bound the first soul myself. And the first soul was my brother."

The silence had texture. It pressed on the ears.

"Tammas," he said, because thirty years of silence deserved at least the sound of the name aloud. "Nineteen years old. Owed fourteen crowns. I told the agents he'd sign if I asked him to. And he did, because he trusted me, and I called that trust *consent* and signed my own name under his in the ledger." He looked at Wendell. "You've been hiding magic for seven years and calling it protection. I've been hiding this for thirty and calling it grief. We're the same kind of coward, Brightwater. I'm just older at it."

He expected anger. He got something worse: understanding. Wendell looked at him across the maps of a country built on his sin, and her face held no judgment at all—only recognition, which is the cruelest gift one guilty person can give another.

"Why tell us now?" Yara asked. Her voice was quiet. "Why not stay silent?"

"Because the stars are the ledger," Brannoc said. "Each one's an account. When the last one dies, the contract closes, and everything in the walls goes home to the court beneath the mountain—or gets eaten, I don't know which, and I built the damn thing. I'm the only one alive who knows how the lattice is joined, where the seams are, which stones can be opened and which will take your hand off." He reached for his pack, and the old tools inside it clinked like a promise. "I'm coming with you. Not for redemption. There's no redemption in a mountain. I'm coming because my brother is in that wall, and when this is done—whatever 'done' means—I want a body to bury."

"There won't be a body," Marisol said gently. "There'll be a light. Maybe."

"Then a light to bury," Brannoc said. "Under a rowan tree. My mother planted rowans for the dead. It's the only rite I remember." He shouldered the pack. "I spent thirty years being loyal to the wrong thing and calling it duty. I'm done with duty. What's left is debt, and I pay my debts with my hands."

Wendell studied him a long moment. Then she rolled up the maps and tucked them into her coat, and something in her jaw set the way it set when she'd stopped deliberating.

"Then we walk tonight," she said. "Before the Inquisition closes the roads."

Pip groaned. Yara stood, locket in fist, eyes already on the northern horizon where the mountain waited under its shroud of missing stars.

And Brannoc followed them out into the dark, feeling lighter than he had in thirty years—not forgiven, nowhere near forgiven, but *armed* at last, guilt converted at long last into its only useful form:

Fuel.

Behind them, Grayhollow's watch-bells began to ring the hour. Ahead, the road north ran straight as a sentence toward the mountain—and somewhere on that road, Brannoc would later learn, an inquisitor with tired eyes was already reading a ledger that would break her faith in half.

But none of them knew that yet. They only knew the road, and the cold, and the four hundred and ten names between them and the first.
