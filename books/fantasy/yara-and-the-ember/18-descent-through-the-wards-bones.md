## 18. Descent Through the Wards' Bones

The entrance Brannoc led them to was not a gate. Gates were for guests.

It was a seam in the mountain's flank, half-swallowed by scree and forty years of frost-heaved stone, marked only by a pillar that had once been white marble and was now the color of old teeth. Wendell recognized the carving on it before she let herself recognize anything else—the interlaced knotwork of the Wardwrights' Guild, the same sigil that had been branded into her master's forge-tools, into the lintel of the order-house where she'd sworn her oath at nineteen, into the doors they'd nailed shut behind her the night the Purge began.

She had not expected it to hurt. She should have known better. Everything up here hurt; that was what the borderlands did now.

"That's it?" Pip said, craning back to look up at the pillar, then down at himself, as if checking he hadn't shrunk. "That's the great secret way into the Ember Court? Looks like a privy wall."

"It's a load-bearing lie," Brannoc said. He had his pack off already and his sleeves rolled, forearms bare despite the cold, and there was something almost gentle in the way he laid his palm flat against the carved stone—gentle the way a man is gentle with a grave. "The whole mountain face here is ward-work. The original lattice. What looks like rock is rock the way bone is meat—there's structure underneath, and the structure is *alive*, or it was." His jaw worked. "We built it into the living stone. You don't cut through it. You don't climb over it. But—" he tapped the pillar twice, a knock, courteous as a caller at a door, "—the Guild always kept a way in for its own. Maintenance. Repairs. The work never stops, even when the workers do."

"And the Guild is thirty years dead," Marisol said.

"The work isn't." Brannoc looked at the seam, and Wendell watched him swallow whatever came up his throat. "The wards are still holding. Barely. Starving. They'll let us in because we smell like the people who made them."

"They'll let us in," Wendell said slowly, "because you're one of them."

Brannoc didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

They went in single file, because the passage allowed nothing wider: Brannoc first with a hooded lantern turned low, then Wendell, then Marisol, then Pip, who had insisted on going last so that nothing could sneak up behind him, and who had also insisted, with equal seriousness, that if anything did sneak up behind him they should all agree in advance that his screaming would be tactical.

The dark closed over them like water.

Wendell had fought in cellars and siege-tunnels and the undercrofts of three burning cities, and none of it had prepared her for this—not the darkness itself but its *texture*. It pressed. It had weight against the skin, like walking through deep water that wanted very much to keep her. The walls of the passage sweated cold, and the air tasted of iron and old smoke and, beneath both, faintly and horribly, of *warmth*—the way air tastes near a banked fire, except they were descending into a mountain that had swallowed stars, and no fire she knew smelled like that.

"Talk," Marisol said from behind her, voice flat. "Anyone. I don't care about what. If this silence goes on much longer I'm going to start hearing my own thoughts, and mine are bad company."

"Once," Pip said immediately, from the rear, "in Vell's Landing, I picked a lock that sang."

"Sang," Wendell repeated.

"Hummed. Harmonized. Whatever." A scrape of boot-sole; he was feeling along the wall with one hand, she realized, keeping contact with the stone the way a child keeps contact with a parent's coat in a crowd. "Locks have voices. Most of them are dull—clunk, grind, *I am a lock, I am very proud of being a lock.* This one was different. It had been made by someone who loved making it. Every tumbler had a note, and when I lifted them in the right order they played—" he hummed four notes, wavering, sweet and small in all that blackness, "—that. Took me an hour to get it right. The house's owner caught me sitting on the floor afterward just listening to the last one ring out. Didn't even call the watch. Said it was the nicest thing anyone had done with his door in twenty years."

"What happened to him?" Marisol asked.

"Burned, later. Unregistered enchanter, apparently. Making locks that sing counts, if you're poor enough." Pip's humor held, but only barely; Wendell heard the hairline crack in it, the sound of a young man carrying more grief than his frame was built for. "Anyway. Point is, everything made with love has a voice. And everything made without it has one too, but you have to listen harder."

He stopped humming after that. None of them asked him to start again. The dark had gotten thick enough that even small kindnesses felt like spending coin they might need.

* * *

An hour in—or what Wendell judged was an hour; time went strange down there, stretching like taffy—they reached the first lock.

It wasn't a lock the way Grayhollow understood locks. There was no door, exactly; the passage simply ended in a wall of stone that was not stone, a surface that gave back the lantern-light wrong, oily, like the pupil of an enormous eye. Set into it, chest-high, was a mechanism the size of a dinner plate: concentric rings of metal around a central boss stamped with the Guild's knotwork, each ring engraved fine as hair.

Brannoc set down the lantern. His hands were shaking. Wendell noticed, and pretended not to, because that was what you did for family.

"Counterweight seal," he said. "There are nine of these between us and the antechamber. Each one keyed to a sequence only a Guild hand knows. I know them." He flexed his fingers. "I built six of the nine."

"You built them," Wendell said. "Thirty years ago. Do they still remember you?"

"They remember the hands that shaped them better than I remember my own name," Brannoc said grimly. "That was rather the point. We weren't supposed to be able to forget."

He set his palms against the rings and began to turn them, muttering the sequence under his breath—a counting-rhyme, Wendell realized, some apprentice's mnemonic dressed up as nonsense verse—and the rings resisted, ground, caught, and then, with a sensation Wendell felt in her teeth more than her ears, *yielded*.

And the seal spoke.

Not words. Not quite. A tone rose out of the mechanism, low and vast, the note a cathedral bell would make if a bell could grieve, and it rolled down the passage behind them and up through the stone above them and everywhere at once, and every hair on Wendell's arms stood up, and Marisol swore softly in hedge-Spanish, and Pip whispered, reverent, "*Oh, that's a beautiful lock,*" which was possibly the most Pip thing anyone had ever said.

The wall of not-stone split along a seam no wider than a knife's edge and drew apart.

Beyond it, the dark was somehow darker.

"After you," Brannoc said hoarsely, and Wendell went, because someone had to, and because she had spent seven years learning that fear was just another weight you carried, and hers was already packed full.

* * *

Between the second and third seals, the wards woke up.

Or part of them did. Wendell felt it first as a pressure change, the deep-water push against her skin suddenly directional, attentive—then as light, or the ghost of light, seeping up out of the walls themselves. Thin veins of it, amber-gold, threading through the black stone like marrow through bone. They pulsed, slow and arrhythmic, a heartbeat with a stutter in it.

"The lattice," Brannoc breathed. He had stopped walking. He stood in the failing glow with his head tipped back and his eyes wet and shining, and for a moment he looked younger than Pip and older than the mountain at once. "It's still trying. Look at it. Thirty years since anyone fed it properly and it's still *trying*."

"What was it supposed to do?" Marisol asked quietly. She had her candle-mother's stillness on, the one she wore like armor. "Before. What was the design?"

"To keep things apart." Brannoc's voice had gone somewhere else, somewhere thirty years distant. "What slept below from what dreamed above. The Ember Court called it a border. The Inquisition calls it the Wards. We called it—" he laughed, one dry cracked syllable, "—we called it the Wall of Small Mercies. Because every soul mortared into it bought a little mercy for everyone else. That's how we sold it to ourselves. Four hundred and ten mercies."

The light in the walls guttered. Somewhere far above them, Wendell knew, another star had gone out—had maybe gone out while they walked—and the lattice felt it the way a body feels a wound.

"How many are left?" she asked. "Souls in the walls. Here."

"Here? This section took eleven." Brannoc touched the glowing vein with two fingers, featherlight. "I can't tell you their names anymore. I used to be able to. I made myself stop." He pulled his hand back. "I'm sorry. That's the truest useless thing I've ever said."

Nobody answered him. There wasn't an answer. They walked on, through the bones of a mercy built out of people, and Wendell found herself doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing: Yara's mother had bought passage through wards like these. Had paid with her daughter instead of her own soul, or been made to pay, or believed she was saving her—and either way the child had crossed the Wall of Small Mercies as cargo, and the wall had taken its tithe, and now the stars were falling out of the sky one by one to settle the account, and Wendell had spent seven years teaching that child to bake bread and mend hems and never once telling her she was already owned.

The dark pressed closer. Wendell let it. She deserved its weight.

"Wendell." Marisol's hand on her shoulder, brief and firm. "Wherever you just went—come back. We need your feet on this floor and your blade loose in its sheath and nothing else."

"I'm here," Wendell said, and mostly meant it.

* * *

At the fourth seal, the mechanism refused.

Brannoc ran the sequence twice, three times, sweat standing out on his forehead despite the cold, and the rings turned and turned and the central boss stayed shut, and then on the fifth try the whole assembly shuddered and went dead under his hands, inert as any ordinary stone.

"No," he said. "No, no—it can't fail, it doesn't *fail*, it's not a machine, it's a—"

"It's grieving," Pip said.

They all turned. The thief had come forward past Marisol without anyone noticing—he moved that quietly when he wanted to—and he was crouched by the dead seal with his ear nearly against it, eyes half-closed, listening with the whole of himself.

"It's not broken. Listen." He beckoned impatiently until Wendell knelt beside him, and she pressed her ear to the cold metal, and at first heard nothing, and then—

Beneath the silence, so deep she felt it in the bone behind her jaw: a tremor. A discord. Nine notes that should have harmonized, jangling against each other like a dropped harp.

"One of the tumblers is out of tune," Pip murmured. "One of the notes changed. Locks remember their maker's voice, right? Well—this one's remembering a voice that's gone, and it's playing the chord wrong because the voice is wrong, because—" he looked up at Brannock with terrible gentleness, "—because part of what you built it with isn't singing anymore. Something in here died recently. Or left."

The last star. Wendell's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. *Yara.*

"It's stuck on the wrong note," Pip said. "So we give it a new one." He spat on his palms, rubbed them together, and cracked his knuckles. "Somebody hold the lantern steady. This is going to require singing."

"Pip," Marisol said, "if you tell me you're about to serenade a four-hundred-year-old deathlock, I want you to know I will remember this forever and bring it up at your funeral."

"Noted." He settled cross-legged before the seal, laid both hands flat on the rings, closed his eyes, and began.

Wendell would spend the rest of her life trying to describe what happened next and never quite succeed. He didn't sing a song, exactly. He sang *notes*—single long tones, hunted and adjusted, offered up to the mechanism one at a time like a man laying cards before a hostile king. The first three he discarded. On the fourth, the dead metal stirred. On the fifth, the discordant chord beneath the silence shifted, grudgingly, toward something almost consonant. And Pip followed it—bending his own voice after it, chasing the fault-line through the dark, sweating, shaking, his voice cracking and recovering, cracking and recovering—until, with a sound like a sigh the size of a landslide, the seal's chord resolved.

The rings spun. The wall opened.

Pip sat back, gray-faced, wiping his mouth. "Never," he panted, "do that again. My throat feels haunted."

"You just talked a lock out of mourning," Wendell said.

"I just *listened* to a lock mourning. Different job entirely." He got up, wobbled, steadied himself on Marisol's arm. "It wants to be fixed, that's the thing nobody understands about locks. Even the ones built out of dead people. Especially those." He looked at Brannoc, and something passed between them that Wendell couldn't read and didn't try to—some settlement, some small forgiveness, thief to smith.

Brannoc nodded once, jerkily, and led on.

* * *

Three seals more. Two opened to Brannoc's sequences alone. One, near the bottom, required Pip again, and afterward the thief walked in silence for a long while with his hand pressed flat against the wall's faint warm glow, and when Wendell glanced back she saw that he was crying without any noise at all, the way you learn to cry in cities that punish weakness, and she reached back and gripped his shoulder and let go, and neither of them mentioned it, and it was somehow enough.

The passages narrowed. The air grew warmer—that banked-fire warmth, stronger now, unmistakably alive—and the walls' amber veins brightened until they needed the lantern less and less, until Brannoc shuttered it altogether and they walked in the pulse of the dying lattice, four small figures moving through the glowing skeleton of a mercy that had failed.

And then the passage simply ended.

They stepped out of the ward-bone and onto a ledge of natural stone, and the space opened around them so suddenly that Wendell staggered, her body still braced for walls that weren't there. An antechamber—vast, vaulted, lit from somewhere unseen by a sourceless ember-glow that turned everything the color of a hearth at midnight. Pillars rose into shadow. Far across the chamber, a corridor ran deeper into the mountain, and from somewhere along it drifted a sound that stopped Wendell's heart mid-beat:

Voices. Distant, echoing, formal. Court voices. And beneath them, thinner, higher—

A girl arguing with someone about protocol.

Marisol heard it too. Her hand found Wendell's arm and gripped hard, a question.

Wendell didn't answer. She was already moving, crossing the antechamber's ember-lit expanse with her sword-hand flexing and her breath coming short and her whole hidden, buried, rusted life narrowing to a single point of light down that corridor—because she knew that voice, she had raised that voice, she had lied to that voice for seven years, and the lie had walked into hell ahead of her and was now, at this moment, within a few hundred yards and several thousand tons of stone.

"She came here alone," Wendell whispered. "Sixteen years old, with a locket and a grudge, and she came *here*."

"Then we'd best hurry," Marisol said quietly, "before she learns what this place does to girls who belong to it."

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, the last seal they'd passed sealed itself shut with a soft, final click—like a lock deciding, gently, that no one was leaving.
