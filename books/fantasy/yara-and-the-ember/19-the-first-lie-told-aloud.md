## 19. The First Lie Told Aloud

The antechamber had been a chapel once, or something like one. Wendell could tell by the shape of the alcoves—empty niches carved into the black stone where statues of forgotten gods had stood before the mountain swallowed them. Now the niches held candles. Hundreds of them, red as wounds, burning without flickering in air that did not move.

She came through the ward-bone passage with her sword drawn and her heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door, and the first thing she saw was Yara.

Yara stood at the center of the chamber, small and stubborn and alive, wrapped in a courtier's cloak some herald must have draped over her shoulders. The ember-light turned her hair to copper and her eyes to coals, and for one terrible heartbeat Wendell thought: *they've already begun to change her.* Then Yara turned, saw her, and her face did something complicated—relief and fury fighting for the same ground—and Wendell knew that whatever the court had done to her daughter, it hadn't touched the part of her that mattered. Not yet.

"You followed me." Yara's voice cracked on the second word. "I told you not to. I left so you *wouldn't* have to."

"I know what you left for." Wendell sheathed the sword slowly, deliberately, the way you'd lower your hands to show an animal you meant no harm. "It doesn't work that way. It was never going to work that way."

"Then explain it." Yara took a step toward her, and the cloak slipped from her shoulders, and Wendell saw the locket at her throat—the locket Wendell had charmed against forgetting, now glowing faintly, humming like a struck bell. "Explain why the locket shows me a mountain gate. Explain why a voice made of coals knows my name. Explain why every star in the sky went out the night I turned sixteen, and why—" her voice broke and rebuilt itself, "—why you looked at me this morning like I was already dead."

The candles burned. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, something vast shifted in its sleep, or its feeding, and the floor hummed beneath their feet.

Wendell had rehearsed this confession for seven years. In the bathhouse steam, in the dark before dawn, in the long gray afternoons between customers at the charm stall, she had composed and recomposed the speech that would make the truth bearable. Every version had been a lie of a different shape—a softer lie, a kinder lie, a lie with better lighting. Standing here now, in the bones of a chapel built by people who fed souls to the dark, she understood that there was no version that would make it bearable. There was only the truth, raw and ugly, or nothing at all.

So she knelt.

Not gracefully. Her knees hit the stone hard enough to bruise, and the sound rang through the antechamber like a dropped hammer. She knelt the way a swordspeaker kneels—palm flat on the floor, head bowed, the old posture of surrender that predated the Purge, that predated the law, that went back to the first oath ever sworn over a blade.

Yara went still. "What are you doing?"

"What I should have done seven years ago." Wendell kept her eyes down. The stone was cold under her palm. "Telling you the truth without armor on."

* * *

"Your mother's name was Sefa," Wendell said. "Sefa Ilvane. She was a hedge-healer from the border villages, the kind of woman who could set a broken bone with one hand and brew fever-tea with the other. She came to Grayhollow eight months pregnant, running from the Inquisition's third sweep of the marches. She'd been flagged as unregistered. A midwife reported her—or maybe she didn't; maybe the report was invented to fill a quota. It didn't matter. By then the quotas mattered more than the truth."

Yara had not moved. Wendell forced herself to keep speaking, each word a stone pulled from a wound that had been packed shut too long.

"She reached the city gates during a storm. The wards were failing that night—I know because I was one of the people who maintained them, back when maintaining them wasn't a crime. She was bleeding. She was terrified. And there were three men waiting for her inside the gate wearing Inquisition gray, and they offered her a bargain instead of irons."

"They sold me." Yara's voice was flat. "Before I was born."

"They sold the contract on you. Your soul, payable on your sixteenth year, to the court beneath the mountain. In exchange they let her pass, gave her papers, gave her a room above a dyer's shop in Lowbridge. They called it *resettlement assistance* in the ledger." Wendell's hands curled into fists against the stone. "I found her two days later, hemorrhaging, alone. She lived long enough to give birth and long enough to hate herself for what she'd signed. She put you in my arms and she said, *keep her far from mountains,* and then she asked me to tell her it would be all right, and I lied to her, and then she died believing me."

The silence that followed had weight. The candles leaned into it.

"And you knew." Yara's voice was very quiet now, which was worse than shouting. "All these years. You knew what I was, what was coming for me, and you—you gave me a *locket*. You taught me to bake bread. You let me believe I was just—just a foundling, just a girl you were kind to—"

"You were never *just* anything to me." Wendell lifted her head at last. "You were the reason I stayed hidden when every part of me wanted to burn the whole rotten system down. You were the reason I sold fake charms to fools and smiled at inquisitors and bit my tongue until it scarred. I told myself I was protecting you. That if you didn't know, the contract couldn't find you. That ignorance was a kind of warding." She laughed, and it came out broken. "But that was the first lie, Yara. Not the one I told you. The one I told myself. Because the truth is I was protecting *me*. I couldn't bear for you to look at me the way you're looking at me now. So I chose seven years of your trust over one day of your anger, and I called it love, and it was cowardice wearing love's coat."

Yara's breath shuddered out of her. She pressed her hand flat against the locket, and it pulsed warm under her fingers, and Wendell saw her wrestling with it—the charm against forgetting, holding every fragment Sefa's signature had shaken loose, every memory the locket had guarded until its bearer was strong enough to carry them.

"The night market," Yara said. "My tenth birthday. You bought me honey-bread and told me my mother died in childbirth, poor and brave, and that she loved me before she ever met me."

"That part was true."

"The rest?"

"A lie. The first of hundreds. Small ones, mostly. Lies of omission, lies of redirection. But a wall is made of bricks, however small they are, and I built one around you and called it a home." Wendell bowed her head again. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to hear all of it, and then decide who I am to you. I'll take whatever you decide. I'll take nothing. I only need you to know that everything I did—every silence, every deflection—came from the same place, and the same place was wrong."

* * *

For a long moment Yara didn't speak. The candles burned their steady, bloodless red. Somewhere below, the mountain breathed.

"You knelt," Yara said finally. "You actually knelt. On stone. With your bad knee."

"My knee has survived worse."

"Don't joke." But her voice wobbled, and Wendell heard the crack in it, the sixteen-year-old underneath the fury, the girl who had run across the ash country alone because she thought she was saving someone. "Do you know what I thought, walking here? I thought—if I go to them willingly, they won't come looking. They won't search Grayhollow. They won't find the charms in the stall, the sword under the floorboards, the *oath*. I thought I could trade myself for your safety the way you traded your truth for mine." She laughed, wet and sharp. "We're the same, you know that? We both decided we knew best. We both decided the other one couldn't be trusted with the weight."

"Yes," Wendell said simply. "We are the same. That's what happens when you raise someone—they learn your shapes, even the crooked ones."

Yara crouched down in front of her. Close enough that Wendell could see the ember-light reflected in her eyes, and behind it, faint but stubborn, the gray-green of the marsh lights outside Grayhollow, the light of home.

"I'm so angry at you," Yara whispered. "I'm angry in my *teeth*. Every birthday. Every bedtime story. Every time you changed the subject when I asked about the mountain, and I thought I was being silly, and I wasn't, I was never being silly—" Her hand shot out and grabbed the front of Wendell's shirt, fisting the fabric, holding on. "And I also know you carried me out of a burning house when I was four. Brannoc told me. On the road. He said you went back in twice."

"He exaggerates."

"He said three times."

"...He exaggerates slightly."

Yara's grip tightened. Her forehead dropped until it nearly touched Wendell's, and when she spoke again her voice was barely more than breath. "I don't forgive you. I want that clear. Not tonight. Maybe not for a long time. What you did—the lying—it broke something, and I don't know yet if it heals or just scars."

"I know."

"But." She pulled back, and her jaw set in the way it always set, the way it had set since she was six years old and decided she would not cry in front of the other market children no matter what they said about her having no mother. "But I didn't walk across the ash country and walk into a mountain full of soul-eaters to do this standing apart from you. So." She stood, and held out her hand—not gently, not warmly, but steadily, the way you hold out a hand across a gap you intend to cross. "Walk beside me. Not ahead of me. Not behind me, guarding. Beside me. That's the only terms I'm offering."

Wendell looked at the hand. Seven years of hiding, and here was the thing she'd hidden from most of all: her daughter's judgment, delivered without mercy and without abandonment. It was more than she deserved. It might be enough to build on.

She took the hand, and Yara hauled her up off the stone with a strength that surprised them both, and for a moment they stood gripping forearms in the candlelight, two people who had lied to each other out of love and were only now learning how heavy that particular coin weighed.

"Beside you," Wendell said. "Terms accepted."

"Good." Yara wiped her face with her free sleeve, fast and furious, allowing herself exactly three seconds of tears before shutting them off like a tap. "Because there's something else. The herald—the one who called me 'the payment'—she said the last star dies in nineteen days. But that was two days ago. Seventeen now. And Wendell—" her voice dropped, and the fear she'd been carrying alone since the gate finally showed itself, "—they gave me a room here. A beautiful room. With a bed, and books, and clothes that fit. And a nursery door at the end of the hall that they told me never to open. And tonight, when I walked past it, I heard singing."

The candles guttered—all of them at once, the first time the flames had moved since Wendell entered the chamber—and somewhere far below, the mountain exhaled.

"It sounded," Yara said, "like children."

* * *

From the shadowed archway at the far end of the antechamber, a figure detached itself from the dark—gray coat, weary eyes, the silver badge of the Inquisition catching the candlelight. Serah Vane stepped into the red glow with a ledger bound in iron clasps held against her chest like a shield, and behind her came the scrape of Brannoc's boots and the jingle of Pip's stolen keys.

"We heard the shouting from the passage," Serah said quietly. "And I have something you both need to see. The names in this ledger—" her voice caught, steadied, "—the agents who brokered your mother's contract, Yara. I served under two of them. And the fraud clause in the original deed means the whole contract can be dissolved—but only if it's ratified before the court's own law, in front of the Queen herself, before the last star dies."

Yara's hand found Wendell's. This time, neither of them let go.

"Seventeen days," Wendell said. "Then we go down together."

Below their feet, patient as hunger, the Ember Court began to sing.
