## 20. Court of Coals and Courtesy

They went down as merchants.

It had been Pip's idea, and like most of Pip's ideas it was equal parts brilliance and blasphemy: the Ember Court still honored its old covenants, and one of the oldest was the right of tribute. Twice a year—once at the dying of the year, once at the turning—surface settlements that remembered the old bargains sent bearers down the mountain road with gifts of salt, iron, honey, and memory. The court could not refuse them entry. It was written into the stones themselves.

"Written into the stones," Brannoc had repeated, when Pip laid out the plan in the waystation's lamplight. "You're telling me there's a door in this mountain that opens for anyone carrying a jar of honey."

"I'm telling you there's a *law* older than the Queen," Pip said, grinning around a mouthful of stolen cheese. "Laws are just locks with better manners."

So they became tribute-bearers.

Brannoc forged the seals from memory—he had built half the wards on that mountain, and he knew the sigils the way a man knows his own handwriting. Marisol wove the glamour that made five tired, armed fugitives look like six weary traders from a valley village that no longer existed; she had erased it off the maps herself, years ago, and the irony sat in her mouth like a stone. Serah wore her Inquisitor's coat turned inside out, its gray lining showing, because—as she put it with terrible dryness—"no one looks twice at a woman dressed as penance."

And Wendell carried the honey.

It was Yara who had insisted on coming, and none of them had found the words—or the will—to stop her. She walked at the front of their little procession with a yoke across her shoulders, two clay jars swaying at either end, and she held her chin high in the manner of someone performing a role she intended to make true. The locket hung beneath her collar. Wendell could feel it humming against her own chest through the charm-link she'd woven, a thin silver thread of awareness that said, over and over: *she is here, she is here, she is here.*

The mountain road was not a road so much as a wound. It descended in switchbacks through rock that grew warmer with every turn, and by the third mile the walls began to sweat beads of orange light, as if the stone itself were feverish. Fossils glittered in the rockface—not the fossils of anything Wendell recognized. Spiral shells the size of wagon wheels. Ribs like fallen rafters.

"Nobody sing," Pip said cheerfully, somewhere near the back. "I mean it. The last time I was in a singing cave, the cave sang back, and we didn't get along."

"Is that true?" Yara asked.

"Everything I say is true. That's what makes me such an excellent liar."

Below them, patient as hunger, the Ember Court sang.

Wendell heard it first as heat—a pressure behind the ears, a fullness in the throat. Then it resolved into melody, or something adjacent to melody: voices layered in canons that never quite resolved, harmonies that leaned always toward a note they refused to reach. It was beautiful the way a snare is beautiful, all tension and intent.

Marisol stopped walking. Her face had gone the color of tallow.

"That's a collection hymn," she said. "They sing them over souls. To soften them." She spat, deliberately, onto the hot stone. "Keep walking. Don't hum along. Whatever you do, don't find yourself liking it."

*Too late,* Wendell thought, and did not say.

* *

The gate was exactly as the locket had shown Yara: a seam in the mountainside framed by pillars carved with kneeling figures, worn smooth by centuries of hands pressed to them in greeting or surrender. The figures' faces had been polished blank. As they approached, the singing above swelled—and then, abruptly, stopped, the silence arriving like a held breath.

Heat rolled out of the opening, thick and sweet as breath from a sleeping animal. And standing in it, framed against a glow that came from everywhere and nowhere, was a herald.

Yara had described him to them twice, each time with less certainty, as though remembering him were like trying to hold water. He looked, Wendell saw now, almost human—tall, courteous, dressed in robes the color of banked fire, his face pleasant and slightly unfinished, like a portrait painted by someone who had been told about faces but never seen one up close.

"Six bearers," the herald said. His voice was warm gravel, hearthside, kind. "From Vellin Hollow. Welcome. Your village remembers us, and we remember your village." He smiled. The smile did not quite finish either. "Come. The Queen holds audience tonight. You've arrived at a fortunate hour."

He stepped aside, and the dark beyond him opened like a throat.

As Wendell passed, close enough to smell the coal-smoke on his robes, the herald inclined his head toward her—only her—and murmured, so softly she might have imagined it:

"You carry more than honey, swordspeaker. But then. So do we all."

She kept her face a merchant's smile and walked into the mountain.

* *

The Ember Court was not what Wendell had prepared for.

She had expected a cavern, a throne, guards—something she could map, something with corners to fight in. Instead the passage opened onto a city inside the mountain: terraces descending into a vast hollow lit by rivers of slow-moving emberlight, buildings of black glass and warm stone rising tier upon tier, bridges arcing between them like frozen lightning. And everywhere, people. Not monsters. People—some unmistakably of the deep, tall and ember-eyed and faintly luminous, but others surface-born, plainly and terribly human, going about their evenings with baskets and lanterns and children.

"They look happy," Yara whispered. Her voice had gone strange.

"Look again," Marisol said. "Count how many are smiling. Then count how many are smiling the same smile."

Yara counted. Wendell watched her count. She watched the girl's shoulders change.

"It's one smile," Yara said quietly. "They're all wearing one smile."

"Audience is being held," the herald announced, to no one and everyone. "Tribute-bearers will proceed to the Hall of Coals. Gifts will be received in order of arrival. The Queen sees everything, misses nothing, and forgets no one." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Even those she has met before under other names."

They joined a line of other tribute-bearers winding down the central stair—a family of goat-herders with wheels of hard cheese; two sisters bearing bolts of dyed wool; an old man alone, carrying nothing but a small lidded basket he would not let anyone look into. Nobody spoke. The singing had resumed somewhere below, softer now, almost tender.

Pip drifted along beside Wendell, hands in pockets, eyes moving constantly. "Vault's under the dais," he breathed, barely louder than the hymn. "I can hear it from here. Big lock. Old lock. Locks that old have gotten bored, and bored locks talk." His grin flickered. "Get me within thirty feet of that throne and I'll have the whole correspondence archive singing a different tune."

"And if the Queen hears you hearing it?"

"Then we run, and I die famous."

Ahead, the hall opened.

* *

The Hall of Coals had been a cathedral once, perhaps, or something older than cathedrals. Its ceiling vanished upward into darkness shot with drifting points of light—candles, thousands of them, floating unattended at every height, each burning with a small, steady flame of a slightly different color. They reminded Wendell of something she refused to name until Marisol said it aloud, flatly, for all of them:

"Stars. They've hung stars in their ceiling." A beat. "Those aren't decorations."

The audience was underway. At the far end of the hall, upon a dais of layered obsidian, sat the Ember Queen.

Wendell had braced herself for horror—something vast, scaled, many-limbed, the thing from the cautionary tales. What sat upon the throne instead was a woman. Tall, robed in ember-red, crowned with a circlet of cooling coals that pulsed gently, like breathing. Beautiful in the way the herald was beautiful: complete except for some final essential detail, as if she existed only in summary. Her face was serene. Her attention, when it moved across the hall, moved like a searchlight.

One by one, the tribute-bearers came forward. The Queen received each gift with a few murmured words, and each bearer walked away wearing that single identical smile, serene and slightly unfinished, and took their place among the smiling court.

The goat-herders. The sisters with the wool. The old man with the basket—the Queen looked into it for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted, almost like hunger, almost like grief, and she said, "Accepted with thanks," and the old man smiled his new smile and walked away lighter, somehow, in a way that made Wendell's stomach turn.

Then it was their turn.

*Vellin Hollow,* Wendell reminded herself. *We are six tired traders from a village that burned. We carry salt, iron, wool, honey, memory. We bow. We speak when spoken to. We leave.*

They laid their tribute on the receiving table. The Queen's gaze traveled the offerings—Brannoc's forged seal she examined for one heartbeat too long before setting it down; Marisol's glamoured jars she touched without comment—and then rose to their faces.

It rested on Yara.

The hall seemed to lean in. The floating candles dimmed by a fraction, all together, like a drawn breath.

"Vellin Hollow," the Queen said. Her voice filled the space without effort, warm as a hearth in winter. "A faithful village. Long loyal. Long—" the pause was silk over stone, "—indebted. We thank you for your gifts. Is there anything your village asks of the court in return?"

Every plan they had made ended at this moment, because no plan survives being spoken to directly by the thing you came to rob. Silence stretched. Serah's hand drifted, casually, toward a knife. Brannoc's weight shifted forward onto the balls of his feet.

And then the Queen spoke again—and the voice was wrong.

Not wrong the way a lie is wrong. Wrong the way a ghost is wrong. The warmth fell away from it mid-word, and what remained was a clipped, precise, battle-trained cadence, a voice that had shouted drill commands across frost-covered courtyards, a voice that had said *again, Brightwater, your guard drops when you're tired* ten thousand times—

"—asked you a question, child."

Master Aldous Vey. Dead seven years. Burned on the second day of the Purge with forty other swordspeakers while Wendell hid in a root cellar and lived. That was his voice. Every rasp, every dry impatience, every buried kindness.

The floor tilted. Somewhere far away, Pip was saying something urgent. The candles overhead wheeled slowly. Wendell found she had taken a step forward without deciding to, and another, and the Queen watched her come with an expression of gentle, terrible welcome, and said, in Master Aldous's voice:

"There she is. Seven years, Brightwater, and you stand like a merchant. Shoulders back. A swordspeaker does not slouch through her own grief."

"Stop it." Her own voice sounded very small.

"Stop what? I am receiving tribute graciously. Come closer. Let me look at my student's daughter." The Queen's coal-crowned head tilted toward Yara, and the voice softened into Aldous's rarest register, the one he'd used exactly once, the night before the Purge, when he'd pressed his signet ring into Wendell's palm and told her to run: "You have her mother's chin, little payment. And your father's stubbornness, whoever he was. Come. Stand in the light where I can see what seventeen days of patience has bought."

"Don't call her that."

"No?" Mild. Curious. The serenity never cracked. "What shall I call her, then? She was named in our ledger before she drew breath. *One soul, freely given, in consideration of safe passage.* Her mother's mark is at the bottom, plain as morning. Would you like me to read it aloud? I keep all my promises, Brightwater. It's what distinguishes us from your order." The Queen rose from the throne, unhurried, robes pooling like poured metal. Around the hall, hundreds of smiling faces turned in perfect unison. "Your master sold his own students to save his skin, in the end. Did you know that? Names, locations, the vault-keys of the swordspeakers—all of it, traded for a swift flame instead of a slow one. He begged so politely." A pause, exquisite. "In this exact voice."

Wendell's hand had found her sword hilt without her permission. Iron under her palm, cold in all that heat, the only honest thing in the room.

"Ah," the Queen said softly, watching the gesture with something like delight. "There she is after all. Go on, then. Draw. Break covenant in my own hall, before my own court, on the night your girl's debt comes due. Give me a reason that even the fraud clause cannot touch." She spread her hands—an invitation, a trap, a mirror. "Or set down the honey, take your tribute of thanks, and walk out the way you came. Those have always been the choices, Brightwater. You've simply spent seven years pretending otherwise."

The candles overhead guttered, all together.

And beneath the floor, beneath the dais, beneath miles of singing stone, something enormous turned over in its sleep—and the whole mountain sighed.

Yara stepped forward, past Wendell, past the reaching hands of everyone who loved her, and looked up at the Queen with the locket glowing faintly through her collar.

"You wanted to see what you bought," she said. "Here I am. But I came to read the fine print."

The Queen's smile—for the first time—finished.
