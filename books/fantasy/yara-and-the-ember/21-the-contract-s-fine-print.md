## 21. The Contract's Fine Print

Pip had stolen from kings. Well, one king, and he'd been drunk, and it had mostly been a matter of lifting a signet ring off a finger that was busy holding a wine cup, but the principle stood: Pip Thistledown did not get caught.

So when Marisol said the original contract would be kept in the Ember Court's archive, deep in the mountain's third vein where the walls breathed heat and the locks were grown rather than made, Pip had said, "Right, then," in the same tone she used for *pass the bread*, because fear was a luxury and she'd never been able to afford it.

What she hadn't said—what she'd swallowed down with the hardtack and the bitter tea Brannoc brewed over a coal taken from the mountain's own skin—was that she could already hear the locks.

She always heard them. That was her curse and her trade. A lock spoke before you touched it, if you knew how to listen: the little iron song of tumblers waiting, the hum of wards like a held breath, the fat satisfied silence of something that believed itself unopenable. Grayhollow's locks sang in major keys, smug and simple. Inquisition strongboxes growled in minor ones. But the Ember Court—

The Ember Court's locks *sang*. Actual melody, low and slow, like something humming to itself in the dark because no one had ever told it that dark things shouldn't be beautiful. Pip had lain awake half the night in the waystation listening to the memory of them, and she'd thought: *I could learn that tune.*

And then she'd thought: *and then what does it learn about me?*

"Oi." Brannoc's voice, gravel over gravel. He crouched beside her at the mouth of the side-gallery they'd found, a crack in the mountain's ribs wide enough for three people who didn't mind breathing each other's air. "You sure about this?"

"No," Pip said cheerfully. "That's why I'm going alone. If I'm caught, you're just lost travelers who've never seen me before in your life."

"We're carrying swords."

"You're *pilgrims*. Very armed pilgrims. The borderlands are dangerous." She grinned at him, and he didn't grin back, because Brannoc hadn't grinned since he'd told them about his brother, and she suspected he might not grin again until there was a grave to stand over. "Wendell knows the plan."

"Wendell hates the plan."

"Wendell hates every plan. She's a swordspeaker; plans are beneath her dignity." Pip checked her kit by feel: picks of bone and blackened silver, a wedge of wax, three feet of oiled silk cord, and the small clay whistle Marisol had fired for her—the one that screamed a note only wards could hear, the note that meant *stop listening, pretend I'm part of the wall*. "If I'm not back before the second bell after midnight—"

"We come after you."

"—you wait for Wendell, and then you come after me, and not one moment sooner, because a rescue that gets everyone caught is just a funeral with extra steps."

Brannoc looked at her a long moment. In the coal-light his face was all planes and old grief, and for once he didn't look at her like she was a child playing at thief. He looked at her like she was a colleague.

"Nineteen days became seventeen," he said quietly. "Whatever you find down there—find it fast."

Pip touched two fingers to her brow, the old thieves' salute, and slid into the crack.

* * *

The mountain's inside was nothing like its outside.

Outside, the peak above Grayhollow's dead borderlands was all scree and ash and stubborn pine, gray on gray under a sky missing most of its stars. Inside, the rock was veined with emberlight, slow rivers of orange running through black stone like blood through skin, and the air tasted of hot metal and something sweeter underneath—something like sugar burned almost past saving. The walls were warm through her clothes. Sometimes she thought she felt the whole mountain breathe, a swell and settle so vast it might have been imagination, except that Pip had an excellent ear and it wasn't.

And everywhere, the singing locks.

They hung from the stone like fruit. Grown, Marisol had said, and she'd been right: each lock was a knot of dark metal that had clearly been *encouraged* out of the rock rather than forged, its mechanism a whorl like a fingerprint, its keyhole an open mouth. As Pip moved along the service galleries—narrow ways worn smooth by centuries of tribute-bearers who preferred not to be seen—the locks turned their attention toward her. She felt it as a pressure behind her eyes. Listening. Weighing.

Pip did what she always did when something large and dangerous paid attention to her: she made herself boring. She shuffled. She let her shoulders slump into the posture of a servant girl hauling coals, which she'd practiced in front of a cracked mirror for three years in Vell's Landing and could hold longer than most people could hold a grudge. Servants were furniture. Furniture didn't sing back.

The first real lock stood across a gallery arch at the entrance to the third vein. It was the size of a dinner plate, and its song stopped her cold.

It was singing Yara's name.

Not the word—Yara's *name*, the shape of it, rendered in tones so pure and patient that Pip's throat closed up. The lock had been tuned, deliberately, lovingly, to the soul it guarded. Everything past this point belonged to her contract. The mountain had been humming it for sixteen years, waiting.

"Well," Pip whispered. "*That's* unsettling."

She pressed her palm flat against the stone beside it, closed her eyes, and listened the way she'd taught herself to listen in the counting-houses of Vell's Landing: not for the notes but for the spaces between them. Every lock had a hesitation. Every lock, somewhere in its endless patience, had a moment where it forgot to be certain. Human locksmiths built doubt into their work without knowing it—a tumbler set a hair too loose, a spring wound a quarter-turn shy. This lock had been grown, not made, but growth had hesitations too. Rings in wood. Knots in grain.

There. Between the fourth note and the fifth, a silence thin as a knife's edge, where the melody reached for something it couldn't quite remember.

Marisol's whistle, when Pip blew it, made no sound she could hear—but the lock flinched. Its song stumbled. And in that stumble, Pip moved.

Bone pick for the hesitation. Silver wire for the breath after it. Wax to muffle the one note that wanted to cry alarm, pressed soft against the whorl like a finger to lips. Her hands knew the work the way a spider knows silk, and the emberlight ran warm over her knuckles, and somewhere far below, the thing the contract fed dreamed on, unaware.

The lock sighed open like a sleeper turning over.

Inside the arch, the third vein opened into the archive, and Pip forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

* * *

It was not gold. She'd expected gold; courts kept gold. It was *paper*.

Contracts, thousands of them, each sealed in lead and stacked in niches cut into the living rock, and each niche lit by a single coal that never went out—and Pip understood, with a lurch, that the coals weren't candles. They were the contracts' subjects. Each little flame flickered with a rhythm too much like breathing, and the older stacks, the ones whose papers had yellowed to lace, held coals that barely glowed at all. Embers of souls, banked and filed.

Somewhere in here was a niche with a flame that burned steady and bright and sixteen years old, and its name was Yara.

"Don't think about it," she muttered. "Think about paper. Paper is just paper."

But paper was never just paper—that was the whole point of her trade. Paper was power that had agreed to hold still long enough to be read. And the contract she needed would be recent, by this place's standards, and important, by any standard: the payment that was going to close the account. Important documents got their own keeping.

She found it in a niche apart from the others, near the gallery's heart, behind a lock that sang in a voice like cooling embers and took her four minutes and a bleeding fingertip to open. The case inside was iron, and inside the iron lay a single scroll wrapped in black silk, and the seal on the scroll was still warm.

Pip unrolled it on her knees in the coal-light, and read.

Most of it was court-language, dense and ceremonial, all *whereas* and *hereinafter* and phrases that had probably been old when the mountain was young. But contracts had spines, and a spine could be read even in a foreign tongue. Payment terms. Duration. Conditions of collection. Pip traced them with her fingertip, sounding the words aloud in a whisper, because some things had to be spoken to be understood—

"—the debt shall be discharged by the surrender of the named soul upon its sixteenth year—" yes, they knew that part, they'd known that since the waystation, nineteen days become seventeen become sixteen, the clock grinding down— "or, should collection prove impossible, by the willing gift of any soul of equal weight—"

Pip stopped.

Read it again.

*Any soul of equal weight.* Not *the* soul. *Any* soul. And *willingly given*, which was doing more work in that sentence than Pip had ever seen five words do. The court wanted a soul freely surrendered—it had always wanted that, she realized, the way a trap wants the animal to step in rather than be pushed, because there was a difference in the meat, or the magic, or whatever it was souls were made of. A willing soul weighed more. A willing soul was worth a coerced one.

Which meant Wendell's plan—her terrible, noble, obvious plan, the one she thought nobody had noticed, the trade of herself for Yara—would technically satisfy the letter of the thing.

Pip sat back on her heels and felt the hope arrive and immediately distrust itself. There was always a second clause. There was *always* a second clause.

She read on.

"—save that the entirety of this agreement is void ab initio, and all consideration returned, should it be proven before the Court's own law that the seller entered this covenant under fraud, duress, or false pretense; the burden of such proof resting upon the claimant; the Court's law being binding upon the Court."

She read it a third time. A fourth. Her lips moved through the court-language, translating as she'd learned to translate in the counting-houses—not words, but *intent*. What did the sentence want? It wanted proof. Proof of fraud, of coercion, proven before the court's own law, which bound the court. The mountain had written itself into a corner sixteen years ago, or its lawyers had been honest in the old style, where a loophole was a form of worship.

*Fraudulent intent dissolves everything.* Not reduces. Not renegotiates. *Dissolves.* All consideration returned—which meant Yara's soul back, whole, and whatever else had been paid out along the way undone or refunded, and the last star—

The last star might not have to die at all.

Pip's hands were shaking. She laughed, once, high and disbelieving, and clapped both palms over her own mouth, because the archive's locks had begun, very faintly, to listen again.

Think. *Think.* Proof of the seller's fraudulent intent. The seller was Yara's mother—a dying woman buying passage through the border wards, sixteen years ago, in the last desperate weeks before the Purge reached her village. Passage sold by whom? Wards built by whom? And Wendell had gone pale as milk when Brannoc said the words *souls as mortar*, and Serah Vane—inquisitor, turncoat, walking ledger of everything the Inquisition had ever done—had gone paler still.

Someone had brokered that sale. Someone wearing gray had stood at a dying woman's bedside and told her it was the only way, and maybe it was a lie, and maybe the lie had been ordered from above, and if it could be *proven*—

Serah. They needed Serah. Serah, with her records and her rank-that-was and her complicity sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. Serah, who had chosen truth once already at the roadside, demanding it in exchange for passage. The clause didn't need Wendell's soul. It needed testimony. It needed paper. It needed the Inquisition's own rot dragged into the light before a court that was legally obligated to honor it.

Hope, Pip thought, was just strategy that hadn't done its arithmetic yet. So do the arithmetic.

Obstacles: getting the contract out. Getting Serah to testify. Getting the court to hear the claim instead of simply eating the claimant, because *binding upon the Court* was one thing and courts were famous for surviving their own laws. And under all of it, ticking like the world's cruelest clock: sixteen days. Fifteen, after tonight.

Pip copied the crucial clauses onto the inside of her sleeve in charcoal, folded the original contract back into its silk, and paused with her hands on the iron case.

Take it or leave it?

A thief's instinct said take it. Evidence in hand beats evidence in a mountain. But the case was warded, the niche was logged—she'd seen the tally-scratches, some clerk-thing kept count—and a missing contract would tell the Ember Queen exactly what the intruders knew, and close the loophole's door forever. No. The copy was the weapon. The original stayed, sleeping, unsuspected.

She resealed everything, relocked the niche, and stood on legs that prickled from kneeling. The archive breathed around her, thousands of little flames breathing with it, and somewhere among them Yara's coal burned on, bright and steady and fifteen days from guttering.

"Not today," Pip whispered to it. "I've got paperwork."

* * *

The way back was worse than the way in, because hope makes noise. It rattled in her chest like coins in a cut purse, and twice she had to stop in the dark galleries and press her forehead against warm stone until her breathing remembered its manners. The locks sang their patient songs. The mountain hummed its long digestion. And Pip Thistledown, who had never in her life believed in anything bigger than her own quick fingers, carried a loophole up through the veins of the world like a coal cupped in bare hands.

Brannoc was waiting at the crack, and behind him, by lantern-light, Wendell Brightwater stood with her arms crossed and her face carved out of worry.

"You're late," Wendell said.

"I'm *successful*," Pip said. "Late and successful. Learn the difference, it'll save your heart some wear." She held up her sleeve, charcoal letters ghost-pale in the lantern glow. "Sit down, all of you. Marisol, you especially—you're going to want to hear the fine print."

They gathered. Wendell's eyes never left the sleeve. And Pip, grinning like the thief she was, gave them the only two sentences that mattered:

"The debt can be paid by any soul given willingly—or dissolved entirely if we prove the seller was cheated. Your mother wasn't selling Yara, love. She was being robbed. And robbery," Pip said, "has never once survived good testimony."

Silence. Then Wendell said, hoarse: "Proof. Of fraud. From sixteen years ago."

"Yeah," Pip said, and her grin sharpened into something fierce. "Good news. We happen to know an inquisitor with a conscience and a filing system."
