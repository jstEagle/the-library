## 23. The Queen's Offer

The Ember Queen did not send for Wendell. She sent for the sword.

Two of the coal-veined guards—things that might once have been men, their skin shot through with seams of dull orange like cooling slag—came to the cell the Inquisition's old waystation had become, and they did not speak. They simply stood in the doorway, patient as furniture, until Wendell understood that refusal was permitted but pointless. They took her sword from Brannoc's hands with a gentleness that frightened her more than violence would have. Then they walked her down through the mountain's warm dark, and the mountain breathed.

She felt it in the soles of her boots. A slow, vast rhythm, like a sleeper's chest, like a bellows, like something that had been inhaling for a thousand years and had not yet decided to exhale. The walls sweated a faint amber light. Pipes of glass ran along the corridors, and in them flowed something that was not quite liquid and not quite fire, and Wendell did not let herself look at it long, because looking too long made her think of souls, and she had seen enough of those in the last three days to last several lifetimes.

The throne room—court, the herald had called it, as if that word could civilize what it contained—opened around her like the inside of a struck bell.

It was not a hall so much as a hollowed heart. The ceiling arched up into darkness where veins of emberlight crawled like slow lightning. The floor was smooth black stone worn to a mirror by centuries of feet, and in it Wendell saw herself reflected a dozen times over, each reflection holding a sword she could no longer speak to. The court itself was gathered in tiers along the walls: the coal-veined guards, the glass-piped servitors, the herald in her robes of woven ash, and others—courtiers, if that was the word, whose faces were smooth as river stones and whose eyes held the patient hunger of things that had waited so long that waiting had become their whole nature.

And at the center, on no throne at all—she sat, simply sat, on a block of raw basalt as though thrones were a human vanity she had outgrown—the Ember Queen.

She was beautiful the way a forest fire is beautiful from a safe distance. Her hair was the color of the last coal before dawn, her gown a fall of slow smoke, and her face—

Her face was the worst of it. Because it was kind. It was the face of a woman who had comforted the dying and meant it, who had held children and grieved for them, and Wendell knew that face was real, that the kindness in it was not a mask worn over cruelty but something the cruelty had grown around, the way a tree grows around a nail.

And her voice—

"Welcome, swordspeaker."

The Queen spoke in the voice of Master Aldren Vey.

Wendell's whole body flinched. Seven years, and she had heard that voice in dreams, in the half-second before waking, in the space between one heartbeat and the next: dry and warm and wry, with the faint rasp of a man who had laughed too much and slept too little. The voice of the man who had taught her the swordspeak. The voice of the man the Purge had burned on the steps of the Grayhollow cathedral while she hid in a cellar two streets away and did nothing.

"I know," the Queen said gently, in his voice, with his cadence, with the little pause before a joke he used to make. "It is cruel of me. I could speak in your mother's voice instead, if that would be kinder. I have so many voices. They come to me, in the end, all of them." She folded her smoke-colored hands. "But I thought you should hear this part in the voice of someone you trusted. Because what I have to say is a bargain, and bargains deserve trust, don't you think?"

Around the walls, the court did not stir. The glass pipes sang faintly, a note so low Wendell felt it in her teeth.

"I'll stand," Wendell said. Her voice came out rough. "And you can use your own voice. I'll hear the bargain either way."

Something moved behind the Queen's kind face—surprise, perhaps, or pleasure. "As you like." And when she spoke again the voice was her own: low and layered, like coals settling, like a chorus heard through a wall. "You have come a long way, Wendell Brightwater. Through the wards' bones. Through the ash country. Through the law of your own city, which would burn you if it knew what you are. You came for the girl."

"I came for my daughter."

"Your daughter." The Queen tasted the words. "She was sold to me before she could speak. Her mother signed in her own blood, of her own hand, with witnesses. The contract is iron. It is the oldest kind of iron there is—older than your Inquisition, older than your wards, older than the word *law*, which your kind only invented because you could not bear the older kind." She rose, and the smoke of her gown drifted upward, which smoke should not do. "And yet you came to steal her back. That is either very brave or very stupid, and in my long experience the two are difficult to tell apart."

"Most people say the same thing about love," Wendell said.

The Queen smiled. It was a real smile. That was the horror of her; every terrible thing she did, she did with something true behind her eyes.

"Then let us speak of love," she said, and descended the three low steps from her block of basalt, and crossed the mirror floor until she stood an arm's length from Wendell, close enough that the heat of her came off her like a banked hearth. "You have been in my house for three days. You have seen the nursery." Wendell's stomach turned; she did not answer, and the Queen nodded as if she had. "You have seen what becomes of the payments who are collected before their time. You know that in"—she paused, and somewhere far above them, Wendell felt it, a faint lessening in the mountain's breath, a star guttering—"in fifteen days now, the last star dies, and the contract executes, and I collect the girl's soul whether you are standing in my hall or a thousand miles away. You know all this. And still you came. Why?"

"Because she's mine," Wendell said. "Not yours. Mine. I found her in a basket with a burned blanket and a name pinned to it, and I fed her and taught her to read and lied to her for seven years to keep her safe, and every one of those lies was a brick in a wall between us, and I would build the wall again, and I would tear it down again, and either way she is *mine*—not because I bought her, because nobody did, because love isn't a contract." Her hands had closed into fists without her permission. "That's why."

The Queen regarded her for a long moment. The emberlight crawled across the ceiling. Somewhere in the tiers, one of the smooth-faced courtiers tilted its head, the way a bird does.

"Yes," the Queen said softly. "I believe you. That is what makes this next part bearable—for both of us, I hope." She turned, and gestured, and the air above the basalt block shimmered, and there hung a contract.

Wendell knew contracts. She had seen the one that bound Yara, in Pip's stolen pages: the mountain gate, the signature in blood-brown ink, the clauses like teeth. This one was older. The vellum was dark at the edges, as though it had been near a fire its whole existence and had never quite caught. The seal at its foot was not wax but a drop of something still wet after centuries, still gleaming, still *alive*.

"This is the founding contract," the Queen said. "The first payment. The one that built your border wards—yes, your Brannoc has told you the truth of it, I imagine, or enough of it. Souls for mortar. It was not my idea, whatever your Inquisition's histories say. Your ancestors came to *me*, Wendell. They had an enemy at the border that they could not defeat and could not name, and they came to the thing beneath my mountain and bargained with it, and I—" something flickered across her face, old and tired and briefly, terribly human, "—I was the one who wrote the terms. I have kept the seal ever since. I have kept the thing asleep ever since. Every star I take, every soul the contract collects, goes into the seal. That is what your stars have been doing all these centuries. Not falling. *Holding*."

"Then let them go," Wendell said. "Dissolve the contract. Give me Yara and keep your seal."

"The seal is not mine to dissolve." The Queen's voice was quiet now, and the whole court leaned in without moving, the way a room leans when the truth is being spoken in it. "It is fed or it fails. There is no third door. Fifteen days, and the last star gutters, and if the contract is not complete by then, the seal cracks, and the thing beneath this mountain wakes, and it comes up through the wards' bones and out into your ash country and your Grayhollow, and it is hungry, Wendell Brightwater, in a way that your language does not have a word for. I have seen it feed once. Once, a thousand years ago, before the first contract. There are no villages in the old maps of that valley anymore. There are no *ruins* of villages. There is a valley."

The glass pipes sang. The mountain breathed. Wendell stood very still.

"So I am going to offer you a trade," the Queen said. "Not a trick. Not a clause. A trade, plain as bread, and you may take it or refuse it, and either way I will not force you, because a freely given thing is the only coin this seal accepts." She lifted her hand, and the old contract unrolled itself in the air between them, and new words began to write themselves across its face in ink that gleamed like the drop of the seal. "The girl goes free. The contract dissolves as to her—fully, irrevocably, her soul returned to her body before she takes another breath. The debt is not forgiven. It is *transferred*."

"Transferred to what?"

"To a soul of equal weight." The Queen's kind eyes held hers. "A soul that has kept an oath for seven years in silence. A soul that has loved a child not its own with its whole self. A soul that has already spent seven years dying by inches in a city that would burn it, and never once broke, and never once ran." She let the silence stretch. "A swordspeaker's soul, Wendell. Freely given. Willingly surrendered. Spoken aloud, before my court, in words of your own choosing—because that is the only way this seal takes anything. It must be *given*. It cannot be taken."

The words hung in the emberlight.

Wendell heard them, and heard under them the mountain breathing, and heard under *that*—far down, beneath the seal, beneath the tiers of smooth-faced watchers—a sound she had been refusing to hear since she entered the mountain. A vast, patient, empty sound. The sound of a hunger the size of a valley.

"You'd say yes," the Queen said, and it was not a question, and it was not cruelty either. It was recognition, one soldier reading another. "I can see it. You were going to say yes before I finished the sentence. You have been saying yes since the night the stars went out." She tilted her head, and for a moment—in Aldren's voice, horribly, gently—she said: "You always were the one who ran toward the fire, child."

"Don't use his voice for that."

"Forgive me." She did not sound as though she meant it, or rather she meant it the way the fire means the warmth. "Consider what you would be buying. The girl lives. She grows. She takes a surname, perhaps; she learns a trade; she loves someone badly and well; she grows old in a city that may, by then, have learned to be less afraid. And you—" the Queen's voice softened further, "—you would not suffer, as your kind understands suffering. The seal does not torture what it takes. It *keeps*. You would become part of the holding. Part of the light your people will see in the sky and call stars and wish on. Is that so terrible a thing, for a woman who has spent seven years wishing to be invisible? You would be the most visible thing in the sky."

Wendell laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked, half sob. "You've been practicing that speech."

"For three days," the Queen admitted, and there was real humor in it, real and sad. "Since your thief read my correspondence. I knew you would come to this room eventually. You are the kind of creature who comes to the room."

Wendell looked at the contract turning slowly in the air. She looked at the seal-drop gleaming at its foot, wet and alive and patient. She thought about Yara's face in the antechamber, two nights ago—Yara who had demanded the truth and gotten it, all of it, every rotten brick of the wall, and who had not forgiven her, who had said *I'm not ready*, and who had walked beside her anyway. Yara who was sixteen years old and had already been sold once and was not going to be sold again, not by the Ember Court, and not by her.

*She would say no,* Wendell thought. *She'd fight it. She'd stand in this hall and refuse to let me—*

And that was the thing. That was the exact shape of the thing, and Wendell saw it whole, and it almost broke her right there on the mirror floor: the trade was not a trap. The trade was *real*. Yara's freedom, truly, for her soul, truly. The Queen was not lying. The Queen never needed to lie; she only needed to be the one standing between the world and the hunger, holding the arithmetic no one wanted to do.

And the arithmetic said: one soul, freely given, against a girl's whole unwritten life. It was not even arithmetic. It was not even a choice. It was the easiest thing Wendell had ever been asked to do, and that was precisely why her hands were shaking.

Because she wanted to say yes. Because saying yes would be *clean*. Seven years of hiding, of the merchant's smile, of the oaths rusting shut in her chest, of lying to the one person she loved most in the world—and here, at the end of it, a single sentence spoken aloud in a hall of coals, and everything paid for, everything finished, everything *worth it*. No more slow dying. A quick bright trade. A star.

"Say the words," the Queen said quietly. "In your own voice. That is all. The seal will know if they are free."

Wendell stepped forward.

She felt the court go still around her, the whole held breath of the mountain. She felt the heat of the Queen's nearness like a hearth in winter, and gods forgive her, it felt like *safety*, like the first warm thing in seven years, like an ending she could finally stop running from. She opened her mouth. She reached, deep in her chest, for the place where her oaths had rusted shut, and she found—she found that the rust had cracked. That the place was warm. That the words were there, waiting, in her own voice, in her own words, and they were not the words of the old order, they were not *I swear by iron and by the fallen light*, they were simpler than that, they had always been simpler than that—

*My name is Wendell Brightwater. I made a promise to a girl in a burned blanket, and I—*

"No."

The voice came from the doors at the far end of the hall, and it was young, and it was furious, and it was the most beloved sound Wendell had ever heard in her life.

Yara stood in the doorway with the locket open in her fist and its light spilling between her fingers like something escaping, and behind her—gods, behind her, impossible, impossible—Serah Vane, in a torn inquisitor's coat, with a satchel of documents clutched to her chest like a shield, and Pip, grinning like a knife, and Brannoc, whose face was wet.

"Yara," Wendell said, and her voice broke on the second syllable, and the words she had been about to speak—the free words, the given words—died in her chest, unfinished, ungiven, and the seal-drop on the old contract pulsed once, hungry, and went still.

"You don't get to do this," Yara said, and started down the length of the mirror floor, and every smooth face in the court turned to watch her come. "You don't get to buy my life with yours and call it the same thing they did. I've *read* the fine print, Wendell. We all have. And I have something to say to this court before anyone gives anybody's soul to anybody."

The Ember Queen looked at the girl walking toward her throne, and then at Wendell, and something in her ancient kind face shifted—something that might, in a lesser creature, have been called fear.

"Then speak," the Queen said.
