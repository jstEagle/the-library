## 24. Refusal Is Also a Choice

The floor of the Ember Court was polished obsidian, and Yara's reflection walked beneath her feet like a second girl—one who moved a half-step behind her, as if even her shadow hesitated. She did not look at it. She looked at the throne.

The Ember Queen sat coiled in coal-light, wearing the face of something grandmotherly and kind, and around her the court assembled itself out of heat-shimmer: courtiers of cinder and courtesy, heralds whose smiles were too symmetrical, things that had once been souls and now wore their forgetting like silk. Every one of them had turned to watch Yara cross the mirror floor, and every one of them was smiling.

She had spent sixteen years learning to be invisible in Grayhollow—chin down, hands quiet, never ask twice. She had spent nineteen days unlearning it. Nineteen days since the stars went out, since the locket hummed against her collarbone and showed her a mountain gate and a voice like coals and a line of ink that had signed away everything she was before she could hold up her own head.

Nineteen days to understand one thing completely: she had been sold because no one had asked her. And she would not be bought back the same way.

"You want to know what I have to say," Yara said. Her voice carried strangely in that hall; the walls drank sound and gave it back warmer, sweeter, as though the room itself were trying to make her words comfortable. She refused to be comfortable. "I'll tell you. But first I want everyone here to hear what's already been said."

* * *

It had happened three nights ago, in the vault under the waystation, when Pip spread the original contract across an anvil and read it aloud by candlelight while Marisol annotated it in the margins with a charcoal stub. Yara remembered every word now. She had made herself remember. In the Ember Court, memory would be the only weapon they let her carry through the door.

*Item the first,* the contract began, *the bearer sells into the keeping of the Ember Court one soul, entire and undivided, being the soul of the child unborn or newly born, in exchange for safe passage through the border wards for the seller and such company as the seller shall name.*

Yara's mother had named no company. That detail had kept Yara awake for two full nights. A woman desperate enough to sell her daughter's soul would have at least brought a sister, a friend, someone. She had come alone. She had been alone. Someone had made sure of it.

*Item the fourth: this exchange is entered freely, without coercion, deception, or duress, and should any party prove otherwise, the contract is void entire and all payments made under it shall be returned.*

Freely. Without coercion. The words were right there, in the court's own ink, in the court's own law—and Serah Vane had spent those same three nights finding proof that both were lies.

Now Yara stopped ten paces from the throne, close enough to feel the Queen's warmth on her face like an open oven door, and she reached into the lining of her coat.

* * *

They had argued about this. Of course they'd argued. Wendell had wanted to stand beside her; Yara had told her, in the antechamber where they'd finally said all the true things, that this part she had to do alone—not because Wendell didn't matter, but because the whole architecture of the contract rested on the idea that Yara had never been consulted. If Wendell spoke for her now, even to save her, even to refuse the Queen's terrible bargain, then the pattern held. Adults deciding. Yara carried along like luggage.

"You're not luggage," Wendell had said, hoarse.

"No," Yara agreed. "I'm the payment. And payments don't get opinions—which is exactly why I'm going to give one."

So Wendell stood at the hall's edge now, among the tribute-bearers and the cinder-courtiers, her rusted sword loose in its scabbard, and Yara felt her there the way you feel a hearth through a wall. But she did not look at her. Not yet.

From the coat's lining she drew a folded packet of papers, wrapped in oilcloth against the mountain's damp, and she held them up where the whole court could see.

"Evidence," she said. "Sworn testimony. Inquisition records. Sealed correspondence bearing the counterseal of the Office of Wards." She let the pause stretch. Somewhere in the hall, a coal crackled. "I know what you are. I've seen your nursery—the room behind the amber door, with the little beds and the little candles, one for each child who came before me. You keep meticulous records. So do your brokers above ground. That's how I know you're going to hate what these papers say."

The Queen's smile did not waver. "Child," she said, in a voice like banked fire, patient and warm, "this court has heard ten thousand pleas. We honor grief. We honor defiance—it burns so brightly, so briefly." She gestured, and the nearest courtiers rippled with soft laughter, like paper catching. "But contracts are not undone by sentiment. Your soul was tendered. Tendered souls are collected."

"Read item four," Yara said.

Something flickered across the assembled faces—a ripple, quick as a draft through a candle-flame, gone before it could be named.

"The contract entered freely," Yara recited, and her voice did not shake, because she had practiced this in front of a cracked mirror in a waystation room while Pip timed her and Brannoc pretended not to listen, "without coercion, deception, or duress. Should any party prove otherwise—" she looked up, straight into the Queen's ancient kind eyes— "the contract is void entire, and all payments returned. Your law. Your ink. Your seal."

"And you claim otherwise." The Queen leaned forward on her throne of slag and glass. "On what evidence? The word of oath-breakers and thieves?"

"No." Yara unfolded the first page. "On the word of your own brokers."

* * *

Serah Vane had found the records in a subvault beneath the Grayhollow chapter house, in a drawer labeled only *Ward Maintenance, Historical*. Inside: the ledger of passage-fees. For decades, the Inquisition—the holy office sworn to burn magic out of the world—had been quietly selling passage through the border wards to people who needed to flee it. Mages' families. Dissenters. Desperate mothers. Each passage priced not in coin but in collateral, and each collateral recorded in a neat clerical hand: *one soul, minor, female, newborn. Seller isolated prior to negotiation per standard practice. No kin notified.*

*Seller isolated.* Yara read that phrase now, aloud, in a voice that rang off the obsidian.

"'Seller isolated prior to negotiation.'" She turned the page toward the court, though most of them had no eyes to read with; it didn't matter. What mattered was the ritual of it, the publicness, the words spoken in the open air instead of buried in a drawer. "My mother wasn't alone because she chose to be. Your agents made sure of it. They followed her for six days. They turned away the midwife who might have helped her. They waited until she was starving at the wards with nothing left to trade, and then they offered her the only price they'd accept." Her throat tightened, and she let it. Let them see it. "You call that a free exchange. Your own contract calls it fraud."

"The acts of surface-dwellers," the Queen said smoothly, "do not bind this court."

"Don't they?" Yara pulled the next page. "Then explain the commission. Dated eleven days before my birth, countersigned with your own herald's mark. 'Payment accepted contingent upon verification of isolation.' Contingent. You didn't just take the deal. You *specified* it. You required that she be cornered first, because a soul given freely—" Yara smiled, and it was not a nice smile; Pip would have been proud— "a freely given soul is worth nothing to you, isn't it? It has to be taken from someone who had no other choice. That's the whole recipe. That's what feeds whatever sleeps under your pretty floor."

The hall went very still.

Not quiet—still. There is a difference. Quiet is absence of sound; stillness is everything holding its breath at once. The coal-light dimmed by half a shade, and in the darkened shimmer Yara saw the courtiers plainly for the first time: not smiling now, not quite. Their symmetry had slipped. Faces that had been smooth as river stones showed seams, hairline fractures running through cheek and brow like cracks in cooling glass.

And the Queen—

The Queen's mask shifted. Only a little. Only for a heartbeat. The grandmotherly kindness thinned like smoke over a flame, and beneath it Yara glimpsed something vast and old and *hungry*, something with a mouth that went back further than mercy, and then the kind face settled back into place, seamless again.

Almost seamless.

"I have worn many faces," the Queen said, and her voice had lost its warmth. It was coals now, and ash, and the deep slow grinding of the mountain itself. "I have worn kings and saints and mothers at gravesides. I wore your dead master's voice for the swordspeaker, girl, and she wept. Do you imagine your papers frighten me?"

"No," Yara said honestly. "But your law does. Yours. Not ours." She held the pages higher. "You built this court on contract. You built the wards on contract. You taught the surface world that ink is sacred—that a bargain sealed is a bargain kept, no matter who bleeds for it. You made *law* your religion so no one would ever look underneath it." She took a breath. "Fine. Then live under your own religion. Item four. Fraud proven. Void entire. Return the payments."

"Return," the Queen repeated softly.

"All of them." Yara's voice cracked wide open now, and she didn't try to mend it. "Every soul in your nursery. Every candle on your shelves. My mother's payment—me—you give back. Because if you don't, then every word you've ever spoken about law and order and the sanctity of bargains is a lie, and everyone in this hall knows it, and *you* know it, and the thing you're feeding knows it too."

* * *

Later, Wendell would tell her what it looked like from the edge of the hall: how the obsidian floor shivered like the skin of struck water; how the coal-light guttered blue, then white, then a color with no surface-world name; how the courtiers fractured openly now, seams splitting, and behind the seams there was not flesh or fire but *absence*, hollows shaped like people. How the great braziers flared and bent toward the throne like grass in wind.

Yara experienced it from inside: the floor bucking beneath her feet, the air pressure dropping until her ears ached, the locket at her throat burning hot as a coal—her mother's charm, charmed against forgetting, humming the way it had hummed on the night the stars went out. She planted her feet. She thought of the nursery, the small beds, the candles each no bigger than her thumb. She thought of her mother, starving and alone at the wards, holding a newborn and signing her life away to men who had arranged the hunger.

She thought: *I am not the transaction. I am what was transacted over. Those are different things, and I will die making this court learn the difference.*

"Void entire," she said again, into the convulsing light. "Or I walk out of here, and I take your law with me, and I read it aloud in every market square from Grayhollow to the sea. Let the surface world hear what item four says. Let them check their own ledgers. How many passages did your brokers sell, do you think? How many souls in this mountain were *cornered* first?" She raised the papers higher. "Your whole treasury might be fraud, Your Majesty. Every coin of it."

The Queen rose.

She had been seated since Yara entered; she had watched Wendell's duel-offer and the swordspeaker's refusal from her throne with the serenity of a mountain watching weather. Now she rose, and rising, she stopped pretending, and the mask came off all the way—not torn away, but set aside, deliberately, the way a woman removes a shawl before work.

What remained was beautiful the way a forest fire is beautiful. Tall beyond reason. Clothed in slow ember. Its face was every face it had ever worn, layered and shifting—kings, saints, mothers, and beneath them all a darkness with patience in it, geological patience, the patience of a thing that had been fed on schedule for nine hundred years and had never once been interrupted.

"You are either the bravest creature ever to stand on my floor," it said, in a voice that was many voices, "or the most foolish. It hardly matters. Contracts are not voided by accusation, child. They are voided by *proof*, weighed and witnessed under this court's own rites—and even then, even then, the debt does not vanish. Debts are like heat. They only move."

"Then weigh it," Yara said.

The thing tilted its head. Around it, the fractured courtiers drew back, and back, leaving a clear ring of scorched stone between the throne and the girl with the papers.

"You bring accusations," the Queen said. "Do you bring witnesses? Under our rites, fraud must be attested by three parties: one who suffered it, one who performed it, and one who profited. Have you such witnesses? Will your dead mother rise to speak? Will the brokers of the Inquisition descend into the dark to testify against their own office?" Something like amusement moved through the layered voice. "Come. You have papers. Papers are kindling."

"One who suffered it," Yara said. "That's me. I'm standing right here, and I'm not going anywhere."

She folded the pages closed and held them against her chest, over the burning locket, over her own heart, which was hammering but which was hers, entirely hers, the one thing in nineteen years that had never been anyone else's property.

"One who performed it," she went on, "and one who profited. You're wrong that they can't be found. One of them is already in this mountain. She came down with us carrying her own confession written in her own hand, and she's standing at the edge of your hall right now, and when I call her name she will step onto this floor and say it to your face."

At the hall's edge, Inquisitor Serah Vane—who had burned mages, who had hunted children like Yara across two borders, who had spent nineteen days becoming someone new—drew a breath that shook the length of her body.

"And the third," Yara said, turning last to the throne, to the hungry layered dark wearing its crown of embers, "is you. You profited. You commissioned the fraud. Under your own rites, Your Majesty, you are not the judge of this proceeding."

She smiled, and it was her mother's smile, though she'd never known that until the locket showed her.

"You're the defendant."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats.

Then the mountain, all the way down to its oldest roots, began to wake up.
