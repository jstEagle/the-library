## 26. The Oath Remembered

The hall of the Ember Court was burning from the inside out.

Not with flame—nothing so merciful. The coals that served this place for light had turned hostile all at once, flaring white-hot when the last star died overhead, and now they hung in their iron chandeliers like a thousand watching eyes, and every one of them had turned toward Yara.

Wendell could not reach her.

She had tried. She had crossed half the hall in the first heartbeat after the contract executed early, when the Queen's mask slipped and the courteous faces of the court split open into something older and hungrier. She had gotten perhaps thirty paces before the collection engine answered its mistress—a sound like a bell being swallowed—and the floor itself rose against her, flagstones peeling up into grasping hands of slag and stone. Brannoc roared somewhere to her left, his ward-smith's hammer ringing against constructs that should not have existed outside of nightmare. Pip's voice threaded through it all, thin and bright, singing locks open that were never meant to be sung, freeing souls in trickles of light while the great engine drank and drank and did not slow.

And Yara—

Yara stood alone on the dais steps where the contract had held her, surrounded by the Queen's honor guard, six of them, tall as doorways, armored in cooled lava and courtesy. They moved without haste. That was the worst part. They moved like men who had done this ten thousand times, who had collected a nursery full of payments across centuries, and who saw no reason to hurry for one more girl.

"No," Wendell said, and the word tore out of her raw and useless.

A fist of slag closed around her ankle and wrenched her off her feet. The stone floor hit her back hard enough to empty her lungs. Through the haze of pain and coal-light she watched her daughter—the girl she had raised on charm-seller's lies and market-stall suppers, who had never once been allowed to know what she was—draw a sword she had taken from a fallen guard not two minutes past, a sword too long for her, too heavy, held wrong.

Yara had never been taught to fight. Wendell had made sure of it. Seven years of making sure of it.

The girl set her feet anyway.

*Where did you learn that stance?* Wendell thought wildly, watching Yara's knees bend, her weight settle low, the blade angled not to slash but to *guard*. And then she knew. Every night for sixteen years, Wendell had practiced in the dark of their little room above the charm stall, moving through forms in silence because silence was survival, because swordspeak spoken aloud was death, and she had believed Yara asleep every single time.

The child had learned to fight by watching her mother pretend she wasn't a weapon.

"Little payment," said the nearest guard, and its voice was like a banked fire settling. "Be still. It only hurts if you resist."

Yara resisted.

It was not skilled. It was not graceful. She swung the sword like a girl swinging a scythe at wheat taller than herself, and the guard caught her blade on its forearm-plate and backhanded her across the steps, and Wendell screamed and pulled against the slag-hand until her ankle bled, and Yara got up. She got up and spat blood on the coal-lit stones and raised the sword again.

"It only hurts if you resist," the guard repeated.

"Then it's going to hurt forever," Yara said, and there was something in her voice that Wendell recognized with a lurch—inflexion for inflexion, it was her own voice, the one she used at the night market when a buyer lowballed her thrice and she smiled and named a higher price. Stubbornness worn like armor. Brightwater stubbornness, though the girl carried no such name. Not yet. Not yet.

The second guard came at her from behind. Yara heard it—turned—caught the blow on her crossguard and went down to one knee, arms shaking, holding.

Holding.

Wendell stopped struggling.

It was not calm that came over her. Nothing so gentle. It was more like the moment in a fever when the shivering stops and the burning begins, a stillness with a furnace underneath it. She lay on her back among the grasping stones of the Ember Court and looked up at the chandeliers full of watching coals, at the Queen enthroned above it all wearing the face of a dead man, at her child kneeling alone under six tons of armored hunger—and she understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives at the very end of every other option, why her oath would not come.

For seven years she had tried to speak it. In the dark of the road, in the years right after the Purge, she had stood at midnight and opened her mouth and reached for the words her master had given her: *By iron I am bound, by the order I am sworn, by the Circle of the Bright Blade I speak and am spoken.* Seven years of reaching and finding nothing there but rust. She had believed the order's fall had broken something in her. She had believed the law had burned it out of her, or cowardice had, or time.

She had been wrong about all of it.

The oath had never belonged to the Circle of the Bright Blade. The Circle was men. The Circle was banners and charters and a grandmaster who signed the warrants that became the Purge, whose signature she had seen in Serah's ledger with her own eyes. An oath given to *men* could rot when the men rotted. Of course it rusted shut. It had been rusting since long before Grayhollow passed its edicts, since long before the fires, since the day she'd sworn it to an institution instead of to the thing underneath the institution—the only thing that had ever mattered.

To protect.

That was the whole of it. That had always been the whole of it. Every form she'd drilled, every vigil she'd kept, every year she'd spent selling lucky charms to farmers and swallowing her own name—it had never been loyalty to the fallen order. Loyalty kept in silence was never loyalty at all; Marisol had told her that once, gently, over cards, and Wendell had thrown it back in her face. But protection—protection needed no charter. Protection was not a debt owed to the dead. Protection was a choice made fresh each morning, or it was nothing.

She looked at her daughter holding the line alone, and she chose.

The slag-hand around her ankle was tightening. Above, the guards were closing in a ring, patient as tide. There was no time left, and there had never been any time, and Wendell Brightwater lay on the floor of hell with her lungs full of ash and did the only thing left in the world to do.

She spoke.

Not the old words. Those were dust. She spoke her own.

"I have no order." Her voice cracked on the first word, rust flaking off seven silent years. "I renounce none of them—they renounced themselves when they sold the weak to buy the strong. I have no master. He is dead, and he died human, and I forgive him that. I have no banner, no circle, no charter written by any hand but mine."

The coals in the chandeliers flickered. The guards paused, heads tilting, some old instinct in their forged bodies waking like animals scenting weather.

"But I have these two hands." Louder now. The rust was breaking apart; beneath it, the steel of her voice had been waiting all along, bright as the day it was forged. "And I have one charge, given to me not by any order but by a dying woman in the snow, and I have failed her, and I will fail her no longer."

Brannoc had stopped fighting. Pip's song had gone quiet. Even the engine's swallowing bell seemed to lean in.

"So hear my oath, spoken aloud, in the open air, in defiance of every law of every city that ever feared the truth—" Wendell rolled, wrenched her bleeding ankle free of the slackening stone, and rose to her feet, and drew her sword, the one she had not touched in seven years, the one she had wrapped in oilcloth and buried under the charm stall's floorboards and carried here anyway because some part of her had always known she would need it. "*While I draw breath, no one takes her. While I stand, she stands behind me. While iron remembers my hand, I will remember why hands are given: to hold, to shield, to strike only what strikes the helpless.*"

She lifted the blade flat across both palms, the way her master had taught her, the way she had taught no one, until tonight.

"This is my whole oath. I wrote it just now. It is small, and it is true, and I will keep it until I cannot."

The hall went silent.

Then the iron answered.

It began in her own sword—a hum, low and sweet, climbing through the tang and into her palms, up her wrists, her arms, her sternum, a vibration like the first note of a song she'd forgotten she knew. The oilcloth-wrapped years fell away from the steel; she felt them go, felt the metal wake along its whole length, edge and spine and fuller, every hour of practice she had ever sunk into it rising up out of storage like blood returning to a numbed limb. And then it spread. The guards' lava-armor trembled. Brannoc's hammer rang once, unstruck, a pure clear note. The shackles Pip had been singing open fell away all down the hall, a hundred small metallic kisses against stone, and the freed souls streamed upward past the chandeliers, and the coals bent away from them like grass before wind.

Every piece of iron in the Ember Court had heard her. Every piece of iron remembered its speaker.

"Oh," whispered Pip, somewhere behind her, in a voice stripped of all its gallows humor. "Oh, *that's* what it sounds like."

Wendell moved.

There was no deciding to move; there was only motion, the way there is only falling once the ledge is passed. The first guard turned toward her and she was already inside its reach, and her sword—which had spent seven years as a merchant's hidden shame—went through cooled lava-plate the way a hot knife goes through tallow. The construct folded with a sound like a fire dying. She took its falling axe without breaking stride and put it through the second guard's knee, spun, cut the third from shoulder to hip. It was not elegant. Elegance had burned with the Circle. It was *true*, and that was better—every stroke landing exactly where her eye said, her body remembering everything her conscience had tried to bury, and underneath the fury there was a kind of terrible joy, because this was what she had been, and pretending otherwise for seven years had nearly cost her everything.

"WENDELL!" Brannoc's roar. He was at the engine's base, hammer blurring, and beyond him Pip clung to the machine's flank like a burr, both hands buried in its workings, singing three songs at once. But the guards near the dais were regrouping, four remaining, and they had stopped being patient.

They had stopped being gentle.

The fourth guard seized Yara by the hair. The fifth raised its blade.

Wendell was forty feet away. She knew the arithmetic; she had always known the arithmetic; her whole life had been arithmetic like this. Forty feet, two strides a second, and the blade would fall in one.

She threw the axe.

It crossed the hall spinning, trailing sparks, and buried itself in the fifth guard's helm to the socket. The fourth flinched—one instant of flinch—and Yara twisted out of its grip leaving a fistful of her own hair, dropped, rolled, came up driving her stolen sword upward into the gap beneath its jaw. The guard toppled like a felled tree.

Two left. They closed ranks around the girl, and Yara stood her ground inside their shadow, chest heaving, blood on her teeth, and she looked across the chaos of the hall and found Wendell's eyes.

"You're late," Yara called. Her voice shook. Her stance didn't.

"You slept in," Wendell called back, and something that was almost a laugh broke loose between them, wild and impossible, there in the burning heart of the Ember Court. "Hold the steps! Don't let them touch the dais!"

"Why?"

"Because whatever the Queen is, it needs that throne to be it!"

Yara nodded once—her mother's nod, sharp and total—and turned to meet the guards, and this time she was not alone even standing alone, because every soul Pip had freed was streaming past her now in rivers of pale light, and the light bent around her like a tide bending around a lighthouse, and the guards faltered in it.

Wendell ran for the dais.

The Queen rose from the throne of coals. Up close, wearing her dead master's face, the thing was worse than it had been at distance: the gray eyes were right, the scar on the jaw was right, the patient half-smile he'd worn when correcting her grip was *right*, and it was all of it a mask stretched over an absence, a costume sewn from a stolen memory. Behind the eyes there was no one looking out. Behind the eyes there was a depth, and the depth was hungry, and the depth went down further than the mountain went up.

"There she is," the Queen said in Corvane Ashgrave's voice. "My last swordspeaker. Do you know, I offered him the same bargain I will offer you? All those years ago. He refused. Sentimental man. Look where sentiment got him—wearing my face for eternity, and grateful for it."

"You don't get to wear him," Wendell said. Iron hummed along her blade. "You don't get to wear anyone anymore. Your contract is fraudulent, your claim is void, and your court is coming down around your ears."

"Mm." The Queen descended one step, and the coals dimmed wherever its shadow fell. "The fraud clause. Yes. The little thief found it, and the little inquisitor proved it. Clever vermin. You misunderstand the architecture, though. Contracts are not laws, child. Contracts are *load-bearing*. Tear mine out and see what falls."

Below them, the engine shrieked—a new note, higher, wounded. Brannoc's hammer had found something vital. The floor of the hall shuddered, and far beneath the mountain, deeper than the deep, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the shift was felt in the teeth and the marrow of everyone present, and every coal in every chandelier guttered at once.

The Queen smiled with her dead master's mouth.

"Now," it said, drawing a blade of pure ember-light from the air itself, "let us see if the last swordspeaker is worth the star I spent collecting her."

Wendell set her feet the way Yara had set hers on the dais steps—low, grounded, guarding—and lifted iron that remembered her name.

"Together, then," she said, and attacked.
