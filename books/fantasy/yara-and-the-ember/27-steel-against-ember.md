## 27. Steel Against Ember

The hall of the Ember Court was built for echoes.

Wendell felt them before she heard them—the way the stone drank sound and gave it back changed, slower, deeper, as if the mountain itself were considering every word before repeating it. Coal-light ran in veins through the walls like blood through a body, pulsing faintly, and above the dais where the Ember Queen sat, the last star hung in a cage of black iron, guttering like a candle in a draft.

Nineteen days had become one hour. The contract had not waited to be polite about it.

Yara was fighting.

That was the thought that burned everything else out of Wendell's mind—the image of her daughter, her girl, sixteen years old and three days into knowing what she was, standing alone on the obsidian floor with Serah's stolen evidence scattered at her feet and the court's heralds closing in like a hand folding shut. Yara had no sword. She had a locket, a voice that did not shake, and a fury Wendell recognized because she had spent seven years teaching herself to smother the same fire in her own chest.

She would not watch it be put out.

"Brannoc." Wendell's voice came out low and flat, the merchant's smile gone, the swordspeaker rising up through seven years of ash. "The engine."

Brannoc didn't look at her. He was already looking at it—the collection engine, the vast brass-and-bone apparatus sunk into the floor behind the throne, all wheels and bellows and glass vessels where souls turned slowly like moths in jars. His hands, scarred from a lifetime of ward-work, flexed once at his sides.

"I built its grandmother," he said. "I know where it hurts."

"Pip."

"On it." Pip was already moving, small and quick along the shadowed wall, fingers trailing the stone the way other people trailed banisters. The vault-doors of the engine's soul-chambers sang to him—Wendell could see it in the tilt of his head, the half-smile that wasn't a smile at all but concentration worn as armor. "Forty-one locks," he murmured. "They're all singing the same sad song. Give me a minute and I'll change the tune."

"And me?" Marisol asked, hedge-witch's eyes bright with something too fierce to be fear.

"You hold the door," Wendell said. "Whatever comes through it, we don't need it behind us."

Marisol grinned, and there was grief in the grin, and Wendell understood later—too late—that the woman had been saying goodbye even then.

* * *

Wendell walked onto the floor of the Ember Court the way water walks downhill: without hurry, without apology.

The heralds saw her coming. Of course they did. The coal-veins in the walls flared as she passed, reading her, tasting her, and somewhere beneath the mountain something enormous shifted in its sleep—or its feeding—and the whole hall shuddered like a struck bell.

Yara saw her first.

The girl's face did something complicated—relief and rage and terror all fighting for the same ground—and then a herald's coal-tipped spear came down toward her and she dropped, rolled, came up with a shard of broken obsidian in her fist, and Wendell stopped thinking in words entirely.

Seven years.

Seven years of harmless charms and merchant's smiles, of measuring every word twice and swallowing most of them, of lying awake listening to Yara breathe and telling herself that silence was shelter, that invisibility was love, that an oath kept hidden was still an oath kept. Seven years of rust forming over the language of iron and intent, until in the antechamber, kneeling before her daughter, she had tried to speak the old words and found them locked tighter than any vault Pip had ever sung open.

But the antechamber had taught her something the order's masters never had.

The oath was never the words.

The oath was this: the moment a herald's spear arced down toward Yara and Wendell crossed forty feet of obsidian faster than the coal-light could flare, and her hand closed around a fallen guard's blade—a thing of ember-forged steel, ugly and heavy—and it answered her grip like a dog answering its name.

Not the old words. Her own. Spoken aloud, because truth spoken aloud was the only magic the law could not outlaw, and she was done being lawful anyway.

"I keep what I love," Wendell said, and the blade woke white-hot in her hands. "I speak what is true. I do not hide. Not anymore. Not ever again."

The first herald came apart at the shoulder seam of his armor and folded like wet paper.

* * *

The duel, when it properly began, was not a duel at all for the first thirty seconds; it was a harvest.

Heralds fell. The Ember Court's guards were beautiful things, tall and burnished and wrong, their faces smooth as cooled wax, and they fought with a coordination that should have been fatal to face alone—but they had never fought a swordspeaker who meant every word she said. Wendell's blade left lines of pale fire in the air where it passed, each stroke a sentence, each parry a refusal. She had spent seven years learning how much strength it cost to stay silent. She spent all of it now, in one long exhalation, and discovered there was more.

Yara fought beside her—not beside, exactly; behind and around, quick and desperate, using the obsidian shard less like a weapon than like a needle, jabbing at joints and seams, shouting warnings in a voice that cracked and did not stop. Once, when a guard's pike took Wendell across the ribs—a line of cold fire, a cost she'd pay for later—Yara screamed and drove the shard into the back of the thing's knee, and it went down, and mother and daughter stood back to back in the wreckage of the court's honor guard, breathing hard, and neither of them said anything, because everything had already been said in the antechamber and some things are only said once.

Then the Queen stood up, and the room changed temperature.

* * *

She wore a crown of cooling slag and a gown the color of banked fires, and she looked at Wendell with eyes like the moment before a forge lights, and she spoke in the voice of Master Aldous Vey—Wendell's dead master, dead eleven years now, dead in the Purge whose anniversary Wendell had dreaded every single year in Grayhollow.

"Swordspeaker." The stolen voice was gentle. That was the obscenity of it. "You've come so far to make such a small mistake."

"Give her back her name," Wendell said. "Give her back her soul. Then we'll discuss mistakes."

"The contract is law." The Queen descended the dais step by step, unhurried, and where her bare feet touched the obsidian the stone glowed and remembered. "Law older than your city. Older than your Inquisition, who helped write it, incidentally—they were so reasonable, your inquisitors, so very helpful when the price was someone else's child." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You hid her for seven years, Brightwater. You let her believe she was nobody's. And now you arrive at the end of the world to teach her the truth of it: that she was always a payment. Everything she is, she learned under a false name."

Every word was aimed. Wendell could feel them sliding between her ribs, finding the same wounds the antechamber had opened, the ones Yara's non-forgiveness had left carefully unbandaged.

"That's the difference between us," Wendell said quietly. "I lied to protect her. You're lying to feed."

And she attacked.

* * *

The Ember Queen fought the way a landslide fights—without technique, without mercy, with the patience of geology. Fire answered her hands the way iron answered Wendell's, and the hall filled with heat and light and the shriek of obsidian splitting. Wendell's new oath held her together: *I keep what I love* became a parry that turned a lance of flame aside an inch from Yara's cheek; *I speak what is true* became a cut that opened the Queen's sleeve and showed the void beneath, the dark hollow where a body should have been, the thing wearing majesty like a coat.

"You're empty," Wendell breathed, understanding arriving like cold water. "The contract—it isn't keeping you alive. It's keeping you *fed*."

"Everything eats," the Queen said in Aldous Vey's warm dead voice, and struck.

Wendell went down. Obsidian met her spine. The blade skidded from her grip, spinning, and the Queen raised one hand and the coal-veins in the walls surged toward her like rivers running backward, and Wendell thought—with strange clarity, pinned under the weight of a starving god—that she had hidden for seven years to buy exactly this amount of time, and it had been enough, it had been just barely enough, because—

Because behind the throne, Brannoc was screaming at the collection engine in the old ward-smith's cadence, the language of unmaking, and the great brass heart of the thing was beginning to crack.

* * *

Pip heard the locks break before anyone else did.

All forty-one of them, in sequence, a chord resolving after nineteen days of dissonance, and then the soul-chambers of the collection engine stood open and the stored souls rose out of their glass vessels like sparks off a struck anvil—forty-one points of light, then hundreds, the accumulated payments of centuries, every village's tithe, every frightened mother's bargain, every soul the Ember Court had ever weighed and kept.

They rose through the hall like weather.

The heralds faltered. The coal-veins stuttered. And the Ember Queen—for the first time since the audience began—turned away from Wendell to look at the ruin of her larder, and the mask of borrowed majesty slipped, and what looked out through her eyes was hunger, pure and ancient and suddenly afraid.

"No," the Queen said, in her own voice at last, and her own voice was the sound a mountain makes when it decides to fall.

Brannoc drove his smith's hammer into the engine's cracked heart one final time, and the collection engine died with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and the last of the souls poured upward through the mountain's throat toward the sky they had been stolen from.

* * *

Wendell got her blade back.

She didn't remember crossing the distance. Later, Yara would tell her she moved like something out of the old stories—the stories the Purge had tried to burn, the ones about swordspeakers who spoke and the world listened—and Wendell would say she didn't feel like a story, she felt like a woman with cracked ribs and seven years of arrears to pay. But in the moment there was no feeling at all, only motion, only the oath made flesh: *I keep what I love.*

Steel met ember in the center of the dying court.

They fought through the falling light of freed souls, through the collapse of Brannoc's great machine, through the wailing of heralds unraveling without their engine to draw upon. The Queen was stronger. The Queen was older. The Queen had eaten kingdoms' worth of borrowed time. But the Queen was also starving—Wendell could see it now, see the hollowness flickering through her like wind through a ruined house—and Wendell was full, unbearably full, full of every word she had swallowed for seven years finally spoken aloud, full of a daughter's fury and a friend's forgiveness and a crew's stubborn, impossible loyalty, and it turned out that grace weighs more than hunger.

She beat the Queen down onto the steps of her own throne.

The blade rested against the hollow of the Queen's throat, and the coal-light of the entire court dimmed toward that single point of contact, and Wendell stood over the thing that had eaten stars and children and her master's voice, and she found—this surprised her—that she was not furious anymore.

Fury had been fuel. Grace was what remained.

"End it," the Queen rasped, in Aldous Vey's voice, in her own voice, in a dozen voices at once, all of them tired. "Or bargain. Those are the only two verbs left to you, swordspeaker. Choose."

And Wendell opened her mouth to choose—

—when the mountain shuddered, deep and vast and waking, and the floor split in a slow seam from the dead engine to the far wall, and from below, from the place Marisol's candle-magic had shown them in the reading of nineteen candles, from the sealed dark where the contract's true beneficiary had slept and been fed for a thousand years, came a sound.

Not a roar. Worse. A *pulling*. A hunger so large it bent the air, drew the breath from the hall, dragged the last freed souls sideways in their flight like leaves toward a drain.

The Ember Queen laughed. It was the most honest sound she had made all night.

"You've killed my kitchen, Brightwater," she said. "Do you understand yet what you've done? The contract was never for me. I was the *warden*. And now the seal is failing, the last star is dying, and the thing beneath my mountain is awake—" her burning eyes fixed on Wendell's, and there, buried in all that hunger, was fear, "—and it is *hungry*, and one of you is going to have to decide, right now, what an empty warden's life is worth against everything you came here to save."

Above them, through the shattered ceiling, the last star guttered.

Below them, the dark pulled.

Between them, Yara took her mother's hand.
