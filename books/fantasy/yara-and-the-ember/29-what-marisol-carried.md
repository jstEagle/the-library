## 29. What Marisol Carried

The mountain had stopped pretending to be stone.

Somewhere below them—below all of them—the collection engine groaned like a whale in deep water, and the walls of the antechamber sweated heat that had no source. The air tasted of copper and candle smoke and something older underneath, something that Marisol recognized from her grandmother's stories: the smell of a thing dreaming just beneath the floor of the world.

She stood apart from the others, the way she always did, one shoulder against a pillar of black rock veined with emberlight. Wendell was arguing strategy with Brannoc in low, clipped voices—*the hall, the throne, the contract table*—and Pip was crouched by a door that sang to him in frequencies only he could hear, his fingers moving over the lock like a man comforting a frightened animal. Serah stood watch with her inquisitor's blade drawn, though it was useless here and she knew it.

And Yara—

Yara was glowing.

Not metaphorically. Not the way young people glowed with fury or fear. The girl literally glowed, faint gold light leaking through the seams of her skin, through her eyes when she blinked, as if her soul were pressing against the inside of her body trying to get back where it belonged. Nineteen candles' worth of stolen light burning down to the last star above them, and Yara's body holding on by threads.

Marisol looked at her for a long time. Then she reached into her coat.

* * *

The candle was small. Smaller than anyone would expect, for what it held. Tallow the color of old bone, poured into a tin cup she'd taken from a waystation kitchen eleven years ago and never replaced. The wick was dark. It had never been lit. It didn't need to be.

A death-candle. Her own.

She'd made it herself, in the first winter of her exile, following hedge-craft so old it predated the Inquisition's founding and probably predated Grayhollow itself. You couldn't buy your soul back once it was forfeit. But you could carry it. You could keep it close, warm, unspent—a coin you refused to spend even when every merchant in the world demanded payment.

Her grandmother had carried one too. So had her grandmother's mother, back through a line of women who practiced the small magics in kitchens and birthing rooms and graveyards while men in fine robes wrote laws about which kinds of power were permitted and which were abomination. The Inquisition called it hedge-witchery and burned it. The Ember Court called it raw material and collected it. And the women themselves? They called it nothing at all. They just did the work, and kept their souls in tins, and died anyway, because everyone dies, and at least they died whole.

Marisol turned the candle in her fingers. In the emberlight of the antechamber, the tallow seemed almost translucent, and if you tilted it right—if you were carrying it, if it was yours—you could see the shape inside. A little knot of light, curled up like a sleeper. Like her, but younger. Like her before the Inquisition came to Vellamarch with their ledgers and their irons and their reasonable voices asking her to name her neighbors.

She hadn't named her neighbors. That was why she was an exile instead of a collaborator, why her name was on lists in three provinces, why she'd spent eleven years moving from hollow to hollow teaching girls the old crafts in whispers. She'd paid her price proudly.

But pride wasn't the same as innocence. And here was the arithmetic she'd done a thousand times in a thousand cheap rooms: the wards were built on souls. The contract beneath this mountain ran on souls. Every system of power in the world above and below ran on souls, freely taken from people who never understood they were spending them—and she had escaped, she had survived, she had kept hers, while the mountain drank everyone else.

Eleven years of survival. Eleven years of hoarding the very currency the world ran on blood for.

"You're doing your thinking face."

Marisol started. Pip had materialized beside her without sound, which was his particular gift, along with hearing locks and stealing anything not nailed down and several things that were. He was fourteen, freckled, missing two teeth, and he was looking at the candle in her hand with an expression she couldn't read.

"Go away, chico."

"It's a soul," Pip said. Not a question. He'd seen enough of them tonight—Pip had been the one to crack open the vault of stored lights, and none of them had slept well since. "Yours?"

"My grandmother taught me the pouring." Marisol closed her hand around the tin. "It's mine. It has always been mine. I've just been... keeping it."

"Keeping it from who?"

"From everyone." She smiled, thin and tired. "That's what we do, we hedge-witches. We keep things."

Pip studied her with those quick thief's eyes that missed less than anyone gave him credit for. Then he nodded slowly, the way he did when he'd mapped a room and found the exit.

"The seal's going to need something," he said quietly. "Brannoc won't say it out loud but I did the math. The engine runs on given souls. Willingly given. That's the whole mechanism—that's what makes it stronger than any ward up top. Nobody volunteers, so it takes." He glanced toward the pillar where the groaning came from, deepest now, rhythmic, patient. "But someone could volunteer."

"Pip—"

"I'm not volunteering," he said quickly, hands up. "I'm fourteen, I have a whole career ahead of me. I'm saying I know how locks work, and that thing down there is a lock, and every lock wants a key that fits. And you've been standing there holding yours like you already decided."

Marisol looked at him for a long moment. Then, despite everything, she laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first laugh in days.

"You're going to be dangerous when you grow up."

"I'm already dangerous. Ask any door."

* * *

She waited until the plan was set. Until Wendell had laid out the assault on the court in her swordspeaker's cadence—*steel at the front, fire at the wings, truth in the center*, though the oath was still rusted in her throat and everyone pretended not to hear the cracks in it. Until Brannoc had marked the engine's supports on the floor with chalk lines only he understood, his jaw tight as a trap around his brother's name. Until Serah had finished copying the fraud evidence onto three separate copies of three separate papers, because Serah trusted institutions exactly zero times now and backed everything up like a woman expecting a fire.

Until Yara came and sat beside her on the cold stone, glowing faintly, hugging her knees.

"Do you think my mother knew?" Yara asked. It was the question she'd been circling for days. "What she was selling. Do you think she understood?"

Marisol considered lying. She was good at comfort; she'd delivered babies and sat with the dying for twenty years, and both trades ran on knowing when to soften truth. But Yara's mother had sold her daughter's soul under coercion, under threat, with Inquisition agents standing in the doorway—and the fraud clause that might save them all hung on proving that coercion. Softening things now would be a betrayal worse than silence.

"She knew she was buying you passage," Marisol said. "Through the wards, past the purge-wagons, into a life. She knew she was out of choices. Did she understand what the Court would do with the debt?" She shook her head. "No one understands that until the bill arrives. That's the design of it, mija. Every contract down here is written so the seller can't imagine the price. If they could imagine it, they wouldn't sign. And then the Court would have to take honestly, and it doesn't know how."

Yara absorbed that, chin on her knees. The glow in her skin pulsed gently, like breath.

"Wendell lied to me for seven years," Yara said. "To protect me. I'm still angry about it. I think I'll be angry about it for a long time." A pause. "But I understand it better now. Lying is just love with nowhere safe to go."

Marisol's chest tightened. She put an arm around the girl's shoulders, careful of the light leaking off her like warmth off a stove.

"Your mother loved you like that," she said. "So does Wendell. So do I." And then, because it was time, because there would never be a gentler moment and gentle moments were for spending: "Which is why I have to tell you all something. And then I have to ask you to let me go."

* * *

They gathered in the antechamber's mouth, where the corridor sloped down toward the engine hall and the air grew thick enough to chew. Marisol stood before them and took out the candle, and held it up, and let them see it.

"A death-candle," she said. "Hedge-craft. Older than your Inquisition, Serah. Older than your order, Wendell. When a hedge-witch's soul is forfeit—outlawed, exiled, condemned—we pour it into tallow and we carry it. Unlit. Unspent. Because a soul nobody can take is a soul nobody can use." Her voice stayed level. Twenty years of practice. "I've carried mine for eleven years. Since the night I ran from Vellamarch with the law at my back and my grandmother's recipe in my head."

Wendell went very still. "Forfeit," she repeated. "Marisol, forfeit means—if the Court ever claimed it—"

"They can't claim it. That's the craft. As long as I hold it, it's mine. As long as I never give it, it stays mine." Marisol turned the tin slowly in the emberlight, and the little curled light inside turned with it, sleeping. "But giving is different. Giving is the one thing the Court cannot refuse. The engine downstairs runs on willing souls—it always has, that's its genius and its horror. Nobody ever willingly gives, so it learns to trick and coerce and defraud. Brannoc's brother. Yara's mother. Everyone in that vault Pip opened." She met each of their eyes in turn. "It has never once been paid fairly."

The silence was enormous. Somewhere far below, the thing under the mountain rolled over in its sleep, and dust sifted from the ceiling like grey snow.

"So don't," Wendell said. Her voice came out rough. "Don't pay it fairly. Marisol—there's another way, there's the fraud clause, there's—"

"The fraud clause saves Yara." Marisol said it gently, the way she said hard things in birthing rooms. "It dissolves her contract. It does not put out the fire the contract feeds. Something under this mountain is held asleep—or fed—by a flame that needs tending, and the last star is guttering, and whether Yara burns or not, someone must stand at the seal and hold it. You told us yourself. Your arithmetic. Someone stays."

"That was before—" Wendell stopped. Her hands were fists. Seven years of hidden oaths, and here was a woman offering to walk into a furnace, and Wendell Brightwater, who had broken every rule she had to save one girl, could not find a single word that would change a mind that had clearly been made years ago.

"There was never a before," Marisol said. "There was always going to be a seal, and someone was always going to have to hold it, and I was always going to be the one who could. Think it through, swordspeaker. Wendell—you have Yara. Yara has a whole life she hasn't lived yet, a name she hasn't taken, a craft she hasn't learned. Serah has testimony to give, and a reckoning to deliver, and I want her alive to deliver it—I want to haunt her with it. Pip is fourteen. Brannoc has a brother to bury under a rowan tree." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the rowan tree. Just slightly. "And I have a candle I've been saving for eleven years for exactly one purpose, and I am fifty-three years old, and I am so tired, hijos, I am so tired of running."

"Marisol." Yara stood up. The glow around her flared with her heartbeat. "You can't—"

"I can," Marisol said. "That's the whole point. That's the only point. They built a world where power is taken—taken from girls in kitchens, taken from children in nurseries, taken from brothers and daughters and mothers. Taken, taken, taken. And everyone told us that was just how the world works." She lifted the candle higher, and the light inside it stirred, waking, recognizing the moment. "But it isn't. Watch. This is how it works. Freely. On my feet, facing forward, nobody's hand on my back."

She looked at Wendell last. Longest.

"You told me once, on the road, that loyalty kept silent was never loyalty at all," Marisol said. "You were right, and it broke my heart, and I've been turning it over ever since. So here's my truth, spoken aloud, at ruinous cost, the only magic the law can't outlaw:" She drew a breath. "I love you people. All of you. Thieves and traitors and oath-breakers and inquisitors and one stubborn girl made of borrowed light. Eleven years I thought exile meant I'd lost everyone worth loving. And then a swordspeaker walked into my hut with a stolen child's problem and a crew of beautiful disasters, and I found out I'd just been waiting."

"Don't," Wendell whispered. "Please."

"'Please,'" Marisol echoed softly. "Seven years hiding, and the great Wendell Brightwater begs with a single word. There's my monument. Carve it somewhere." She smiled, and it was a real smile, crooked and warm, the smile she'd worn over a hundred cradles and a hundred deathbeds. "Settling accounts, mi gente. That's all this is. The world took from everyone I ever delivered or buried or taught. I'm paying the world back the only coin it never expected. Honest coin. Freely given."

Then she kissed the tin cup—her grandmother's gesture, the old blessing, *carry it warm, carry it whole*—and turned, and walked down the slope toward the engine hall, and none of them moved to stop her because her back told them everything: this was not a woman being taken anywhere.

Pip made a small broken sound. Serah bowed her head, inquisitor to the end, giving the dead their formalities early. Brannoc pressed his fist to his heart, ward-smith's salute to a working structure, even one about to fail.

Yara started forward—and Marisol stopped, and looked back, and the look held her.

"Live loudly," Marisol told her. "Take your mother's name. Learn the swordspeak. Burn bright enough that every girl in every kitchen in every gray city hears about the foundling who beat the mountain, and decides she's allowed to burn too." Her eyes shone. "That's my payment. That's plenty. Go on now."

* * *

The engine hall was a cathedral built by something that hated cathedrals—vaulted, vast, wrong in its proportions, lit entirely by the slow pulse of the machine at its center. The collection engine squatted over the seal like a spider over an egg sac: brass and bone and running channels of liquid light, nineteen sockets along its crown where stars used to burn, eighteen of them dark, one flickering above through a shaft of broken stone like a dying eye.

And beneath it, set into the living rock, the seal: a circle of interlocked runes older than the Court itself, glowing a sullen, exhausted red, the lock on the cage of the sleeping thing. The lock that wanted a key.

Marisol walked to it across a floor that trembled. Above her, distantly, steel began to ring against coal-fire—Wendell and the Queen, the battle already starting, the diversion bought with blood and fury. She didn't look up. She'd attended enough births and deaths to know you didn't look away from the work.

She knelt at the seal's edge. Set the tin cup on the stone. Took a breath.

"Grandmother," she said quietly, in the old tongue, the tongue the Inquisition never managed to outlaw because it lived in lullabies, "I'm settling up. Tell Mamá the accounts are clean. Tell them all—the ones who carried candles and never got to spend them right—tell them one of us finally got to choose."

She tipped the candle.

The tallow caught on the seal's heat and ran, and the light inside it rose up out of the wax like a swimmer surfacing, and Marisol felt it leave her—the strangest sensation, grief and relief braided together, eleven pounds set down all at once, her edges going soft and bright. The little knot of her soul hovered above the runes, pulsing, waiting, and the seal drank in its warmth like a starving thing offered bread.

"Not taken," Marisol said aloud, to the engine, to the Court, to whatever ancient listening thing lay beneath. "Given. Write it in your books. Write it in whatever passes for law down here. A hedge-witch from Vellamarch paid you honestly, and you are *paid in full*."

The light descended into the seal.

The runes flared white. The engine screamed—not mechanically, but in some register that bypassed ears entirely—and every channel of liquid light reversed direction, flowing backward, downward, into the circle, feeding the lock, strengthening the cage. The trembling floor went still. The groaning thing beneath the mountain went quiet—not dead, not gone, but *held*, sealed behind a will that had chosen this, freely, completely, and would keep choosing it for as long as the candle burned.

And the candle would burn a long time. Hedge-craft was thrifty. Grandmother's recipe.

Marisol knelt there as her own light spread through the stone, and she felt the seal settle around her like a second body, like a coat in winter, like arms. She was aware of the battle raging above, dimly, the way you're aware of weather. She was aware that somewhere up there, Wendell was dueling a queen wearing a dead man's voice, and a girl made of borrowed light was refusing to be anyone's payment, and a thief was picking the lock on a contract, and an inquisitor was learning what testimony cost.

Good. Good. Let them be loud. Loudness was the whole point.

She settled in. She folded her hands. And in the last corner of her mind that was still entirely her own, Marisol the hedge-witch, exile, carrier of candles, keeper of the small magics, thought:

*Warm. Whole. Chosen.*

Above her, through the broken shaft of stone, the nineteenth star guttered once—twice—

—and held.
