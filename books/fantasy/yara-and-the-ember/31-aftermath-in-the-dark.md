## 31. Aftermath in the Dark

The mountain did not want us to leave.

Every step of the descent had been a fight against stone and dark and the singing pressure of the wards' bones, but the climb back up was worse in a way I hadn't prepared for—worse because nothing opposed us at all. The corridors stood open. The locks Pip had sung open stayed open, their tumblers hanging slack like throats. The Ember Court lay behind us, cracked and cooling, its coal-light guttering down to a dull red memory, and no hand rose to stop us.

I kept waiting for it to. I think we all did.

Brannoc walked at the front with Marisol's candle cupped in both hands, cradled against his chest the way you'd carry an injured bird or a newborn, something that could be killed by carelessness. He had refused to let anyone else hold it. When I'd offered, back in the hall where the contract burned itself into ash and light, he'd looked at me with an expression I will carry to my own grave—grief so old it had worn smooth, like riverstone—and said only, "I carried her soul out of a court once before. Different soul. Same court." And then, quieter: "Let me finish the errand."

So he carried her, and we carried him, in whatever way you carry a man who won't accept help.

The candle was small. That was the thing none of us said aloud. A whole life—all her sharp edges, her hedge-curses, her grudge against the Inquisition worn like armor until she'd taken it off and shown us what was underneath—the whole of it reduced to a bead of white flame no bigger than a thumbnail, trembling in its wax shell every time Brannoc's boots struck stone. It didn't flicker the way fire flickers. It pulsed. Slowly. Like breathing.

Like a heartbeat slowed almost to stopping.

Yara walked beside me. She hadn't let go of my sleeve since the hall—not clutching, just holding, two fingers pinching the wool as if to confirm, at intervals, that I was still there. Her other hand pressed flat against her own sternum, over the place where the light had gone back in. She kept doing that. Every few minutes her palm would rise and press and settle again, checking for something, and every few minutes she'd find it, and her shoulders would come down from around her ears a little more.

"How does it feel?" I asked her, somewhere past the third gate. The words came out rough. I hadn't spoken much since the mountain began to shake, and my voice sounded like it belonged to someone else.

She considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, head tilted, eyes on the dark ahead.

"Big," she said finally. "Like I swallowed the sky. Or like the sky swallowed me and gave me back wrong." She frowned. "No. Not wrong. Just—*more*. There's a room inside me now that wasn't there before. It has a window in it."

"What do you see through the window?"

"I don't know yet." She said it without fear, which frightened me more than any answer could have. "It's still being built."

Pip snorted from behind us—a wet, exhausted sound, half laugh, half hiccup. The thief had cried most of the way out of the collection engine's chamber, quietly, without changing expression, tears just running down a face that stayed busy picking the next lock. "A room with a window," Pip muttered. "Listen to her. I've got a room inside me too. It's full of stolen spoons and unresolved feelings about my mother."

"Sounds about right," Yara said.

"You're supposed to be solemn. We're carrying a dead woman up a mountain."

"She's not dead," Brannoc said, without turning around. His voice came out of the dark like something quarried. "Look at the flame."

We looked. In the black of the passage the candle burned steady and white, and beneath it, faint as breath on cold glass, there was a second light—a shimmer in the wax itself, moving, coiling, *restless*. Not flame. Presence.

Marisol had never once in all our weeks together done anything quietly when she could do it loudly. Of course her soul would pace.

* * *

We came out of the mountain's throat near dawn—or what would have been dawn, if the sky had remembered how.

Grayhollow's stars were still gone. All of them, except the one.

That was the sight waiting for us at the tree line below the summit ridge: the eastern sky holding its first bruise of gray, and above the mountain's broken crown, high and alone in an ocean of black, a single point of light burning where no star had burned for nineteen days.

Small. Faint. White-gold, with a pulse to it, slow and patient.

Nobody spoke. We stood among the frost-killed pines with the wind pulling at us and looked up, and the looking was its own kind of prayer.

"It's her," Yara whispered. "Isn't it. That's her star."

"It's hers," Brannoc said. He knelt then, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and set the candle on a flat stone facing the sky. "The freed souls have to find their way home on their own. Through the dark. Through everything the wards tore apart." His voice caught, ground forward. "But the ones who give themselves freely—" He stopped. Started again. "The seal needs a keeper. But a keeper isn't a prisoner. What she did down there—it opened a door *up*, not just closed one down. Look."

And we looked, and I saw what he meant.

From the candle's flame, thin as spider silk, a thread of white light rose—straight up, unwavering, through kilometers of night air, to meet the star. And along that thread, barely visible, something traveled. Not light exactly. More like warmth. More like *her*—the shape of her laugh, the bite of her curses, the terrible gentleness she'd hidden under twenty years of exile's bark—pouring upward out of the wax, out of the mountain, out of the world, into that single burning point.

The star brightened as we watched. Just slightly. Just enough.

"She's settling in," Pip said hoarsely. "Making herself comfortable. Rearranging the furniture. Stars probably hate that, new tenant comes in and immediately moves the—"

"Pip," Brannoc said.

"Yeah."

"Shut up."

"Yeah," Pip agreed, and sat down heavily in the frost, and put their face in their hands.

I crouched beside the candle. Up close I could feel the warmth of it, and under the warmth, unmistakable now that I knew to listen, a sound. A hum, very low, very steady. The same note, held forever.

Swordspeak, I realized. Or something like it. The oldest kind—the kind that predates oaths and orders and iron. The kind that's just a soul saying *I am here, I am here, I am here* into the dark, and meaning it.

"Marisol," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable and I let it. Seven years I'd practiced saying things smoothly, safely, meaninglessly. I was done practicing. "You stubborn, magnificent woman. You told me once that exile was just a word for being free in the wrong place." The thread of light shivered. Brightened. "You weren't wrong. You never were."

The star overhead pulsed once—quick, sharp, impatient, *exactly* like her—and I laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway out, and I knelt there in the frost with my daughter's fingers in my sleeve and grieved.

* * *

We buried the empty wax at the summit ridge, under a rowan sapling that Brannoc found growing impossibly out of a crack in the ward-stone—the same species, he told us, that the first ward-builders had planted along the border, because rowan roots were said to hold souls gently, the way cupped hands hold water.

He dug the hole himself. Refused the shovel Pip tried to hand him; used his knife, and his hands. When it was done, and the little mound of turned earth sat dark against the frost, he stood over it for a long time without speaking.

Then: "She made me promise something. Down there. Right at the end, while the rest of you were fighting." His jaw worked. "She said, *Tell Grayhollow I was here. Tell them a hedge-witch they burned in effigy twice saved their worthless city.*" He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely dry, which is how men like Brannoc cry. "I'm going to tell them. Loudly. Whether they want to hear it or not."

"They'll hear it," Yara said. "They'll have to."

"Why?"

Yara touched her chest again—that gesture, the checking gesture, the window-in-the-room gesture. "Because I remember now," she said simply. "Everything the locket kept. Everything the court took. Including her voice. She talked to me, Brannoc. While the light was coming home. She talked to me the whole way." She smiled, and it broke my heart, that smile—joy and mourning in the same curve, neither one winning. "She complained about the weather in here. Said eternity better have decent tea."

Brannoc made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could bleed.

"And that's why they'll listen," Yara went on, fierce now, stepping toward him. "Because she's not gone. She's *up*. Anyone can look up." She flung her arm at the sky—at the one star burning in all that black. "You can outlaw magic. You can outlaw books and charms and names. But you cannot outlaw the sky, Brannoc. They'll look up tonight. All of them. And she'll be looking back."

The wind moved through the rowan sapling. Its leaves, dead these nineteen days, stirred anyway.

None of us said anything. None of us needed to.

* * *

We camped that night on the mountainside rather than descend—we were too spent, and the road down through the ash-bell country was a day's walk even for fresh legs, and none of us trusted ourselves not to stumble off a cliff weeping. Serah built the fire. She'd been quiet the whole climb, quieter than grief, working through something with the methodical persistence she brought to ledgers and interrogations, and when the flames caught she fed them another branch and said, to the fire rather than to us:

"The Inquisition will know by tomorrow. What happened here. They'll spin it before the week's out—say the court fell to bandits, say the stars are a punishment withheld, say whatever keeps their authority intact." She turned a stick in the flames. "I need to get ahead of it. There are records. Testimonies. Names—including mine. If I go back to Grayhollow now, alone, and start putting documents in front of people before the Council hears the Inquisition's version..."

"You'll be arrested," I said.

"I'll be heard." She met my eyes across the fire. "Those aren't the same thing anymore. Maybe they never were." A pause. "Wendell. What I did to you. What my office did—to Yara's mother, to everyone we ever called criminal for the crime of being afraid in the wrong direction. I can't burn it down. But I can testify. It's the only magic I have left."

I thought of the charm I'd sold her across a market stall seven years ago, both of us lying beautifully, both of us knowing. I thought of irons she hadn't clapped on me at my own door.

"Come with us instead," Yara said suddenly. "Rest first. Eat something. Then go." She hesitated, then added, with the blunt mercy only children manage: "You look like a candle that forgot to stop burning."

Serah stared at her. And then—inquisitor, hunter, the sharpest blade the law ever aimed at people like me—she laughed. Short, cracked, real.

"Aye," she said. "One night. Then testimony."

Above us, the single star burned on. And as the fire settled and the others slept, I watched it, and I noticed—for the first time—far to the south, so faint it might have been a trick of tired eyes, a second point of light.

Barely there. Trembling on the edge of visibility. As if something freed from the collection engine had found the dark, and chosen a direction, and begun—slowly, across all that distance—to come home.

I didn't wake the others. Some news should arrive with morning attached.

But I sat up the rest of the night, my daughter asleep against my shoulder, watching the sky begin, one soul at a time, to remember itself.
