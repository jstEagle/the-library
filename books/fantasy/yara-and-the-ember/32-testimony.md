## 32. Testimony

The council chamber of Grayhollow had not been full in Serah Vane's living memory. She knew this because she had stood in it, empty, on the morning she took her oath—twelve years ago, a young inquisitor with a new badge and a heart like a struck bell. The benches had been bare then, the gallery silent, the high windows letting in a gray light that made everything look washed and provisional.

Today every bench was occupied. People stood along the walls. They crowded the doorway and spilled down the marble steps outside, where criers repeated each word to those who couldn't fit. Farmers with ash still under their fingernails from the Road of Bells. Charm-sellers who had spent years selling nothing but knotted string and whispered reassurance. Mothers holding children who had never once seen a star.

They had come to watch her burn.

Serah understood that. She had come to let them.

She stood at the witness table in her formal grays—the uniform of an Inquisitor First Class, though she had already surrendered her badge that morning into the hands of the chamber clerk, who had taken it without meeting her eyes. Without the badge the uniform was just cloth, gray as old ash, and she felt it that way: a skin she was shedding in public, slowly, seam by seam.

The nine councilors sat beneath the city's seal—a mountain split by a sword, which had always struck Serah as an odd thing for a city to be proud of, a weapon driven through its own foundation. Presiding was Chancellor Aldous Wren, a narrow man with a voice like dry paper and a reputation for fairness that Serah had once believed in the way children believe in the floor beneath them.

"Serah Vane," Wren said. "You stand accused by your own confession. Before we proceed—do you understand what you have asked of this chamber?"

"I do, Chancellor."

"You have asked us to put you under oath of truth. Not testimony freely given—oath-bound. You understand that if you lie under it, the penalty is forfeiture of tongue."

A murmur ran through the gallery. Someone laughed, short and ugly.

"I understand," Serah said. "I'm asking for it anyway."

Wren studied her for a long moment. "Why?"

Because no one would believe me otherwise. Because I wouldn't believe me otherwise. Because I have spent twelve years extracting truth from people who did not want to give it, and I know exactly how much a confession weighs when it costs nothing.

She said only: "Because the truth I carry is about the institution I served. No one should have to take my word for it. Let the oath take it instead."

The chamber clerk brought forward the small iron brazier, and Serah pressed her palm to the truth-plate the way she had pressed a thousand suspects' palms before hers. The metal was cold. It stayed cold. Whatever magic lived in it—and there was some, thin and old and half-starved after decades of confiscations—did not warm for her. She almost laughed at that. Even the instruments of the law had learned distrust.

"State your name and office," Wren said.

"Serah Vane. Formerly Inquisitor First Class, Third Circle, Grayhollow Chapter." *Formerly.* The word tasted like a door closing.

"And you swear, under plate and penalty, that all you speak this day is true?"

"I swear it."

The plate warmed. Barely. A thread of heat against her palm, like a hand deciding whether to hold hers or slap it away. But it warmed, and the clerk marked it, and somewhere in the gallery a woman said, loudly enough to be heard, "Well. At least she'll hang honest."

Serah kept her eyes forward. She had rehearsed this moment in the cell they'd given her—not a cell, really, a room with a lock on the outside that she'd been permitted to lock herself—and in every rehearsal she had imagined shaking. Her hands were steady. That surprised her more than anything else that day. Perhaps because the worst part was already over. The worst part had been the night Wendell Brightwater told her the whole of it on the road below the mountain, starlight dying overhead, and Serah had sat with it in her chest like swallowed glass and understood that she could never unknow it, never un-carry it, never again be the woman who didn't know.

That woman had died somewhere on the ash road. This one had come home to bury her.

"Begin," Wren said, "at the beginning."

* * *

"The beginning," Serah said, "is seven years before the Purge."

Her voice carried strangely in the chamber—too well, as if the room itself wanted to hear. She found the faces in the crowd she needed: the charm-seller Maren Ott, whose stall Wendell Brightwater had worked beside for years; the ward-clerk Tomas Bell, gray-haired now, who had signed the transit papers; and, near the back, a girl with dark hair and a locket at her throat, standing beside a broad-shouldered woman with a swordspeaker's stillness. Yara. Wendell. They had come. Of course they had come. Serah wasn't sure which of them she'd dreaded seeing more.

"Before the Purge, the Inquisition was not what it became. We were auditors. Record-keepers. Our mandate was registration, not eradication. And our greatest responsibility was the border wards—the great lattice that keeps the deep things asleep beneath the mountains and the hungry things out of our fields.

"In the year before the Purge, the wards began to fail. Not everywhere. In seams. Quiet places. The Chapter commissioned a study—I was junior clerk on it—and the study concluded what the old texts had always said but no one wanted written down: the wards were built on souls. Willing ones, in the first age. Given freely, one spark per contributor, bound into the lattice. That was the founding covenant. Consent was not sentiment. Consent was the mortar.

"But the founders' sparks were burning out. Seven hundred years is a long time for anything, even a soul given gladly. And the covenant's own law was clear: a spark must be *given*. It cannot be taken. Taken souls rot the lattice—they had tried it once, in the second age, and the result was the Hollowing, which the histories record as a plague and which was nothing of the kind.

"So the Chapter faced a problem. The wards needed renewal. The covenant required consent. And consent, freely sought from a free people, was not forthcoming. People liked their souls where they kept them.

"There was a faction within the Chapter—senior, well-placed—that proposed a solution. Not theft. Theft was forbidden and detectable. Something cleverer. *Purchase.* Find people desperate enough to sell. Desperation, they argued, was a form of consent. It was in the minutes. I have read the minutes. I will submit them."

She nodded to the clerk, who passed forward a leather folio. Wren turned its pages, and his face went carefully blank, the way a man's face goes when he recognizes handwriting.

"These are Chapter minutes," he said slowly. "Sealed minutes. How—"

"They were sealed to the rank of Second Circle," Serah said. "I was promoted to Second Circle four years ago. I read them then. I did nothing. That is also part of this testimony, Chancellor. I want that entered plainly. Everything I say tonight indicts me alongside everyone else. I knew. For years I knew, and I filed it, and I slept."

The gallery had gone very quiet. Not the quiet of respect. The quiet of a held breath.

"Continue," Wren said.

* * *

"The sale of Yara—" Serah stopped. Corrected herself. She had thought of the child as *the asset* for so long, in the language of the files, and she refused the language now, here, in front of the girl herself. "The sale of Yara Solenne was brokered by agents of the Grayhollow Chapter six days after her birth."

At the name *Solenne*, the girl in the back went still. Beside her, Wendell Brightwater closed her eyes.

"Her mother was Ilse Solenne, a hedge-healer of the Fenmarch villages, unregistered—which in those days meant merely undocumented, not criminal. When the Purge came, it came to the Fenmarch last, because the Fenmarch was far and poor and the Chapter cleared the cities first. Ilse had time. Time to run. What she did not have was passage. The border wards, in the chaos of the Purge, were being keyed to reject anyone with even a trace of gift—and a hedge-healer's child registers faintly from the womb. Ilse could not cross. Her daughter could not cross. And behind them came the purge-fires.

"She came to the Chapter's transit office in Vell's Crossing. There is a record of it—petition number 4471, which I have submitted. She asked for asylum passage. She was refused. Refused twice. On the third visit, she was seen not by a transit clerk but by an agent named Corvan Hale, who held a commission you will find in the sealed minutes. Hale's function—his *entire* function—was to identify the desperate and offer them the option the transit clerks were forbidden to name.

"He offered Ilse Solenne a choice. He called it a choice. Passage through the wards for herself, immediate, that night, ahead of the fires—in exchange for the child's soul, sold to a buyer he declined to name, through a contract he declined to show her. He told her the contract was a formality. He told her the buyer was 'a charitable trust of the mountain folk.' He told her the child would live a long and happy life and never know the difference.

"He did not tell her that the buyer was the Ember Court. He did not tell her that the court's contracts collect on majority. He did not tell her that her daughter's sixteenth birthday would be the date the stars began to fall—one star per year of the debt, until the last star guttered and the last soul crossed over. He did not tell her any of this because the contract's validity, under the court's own ancient law, required *informed* consent—and informed consent would have destroyed the deal. So he lied. Deliberately. Under commission. With the Chapter's seal on his papers.

"A coerced sale is a fraudulent sale. A fraudulent sale is void. That is not my opinion. That is the Ember Court's own contract-law, older than Grayhollow, older than the Inquisition, and it is the clause under which the contract was dissolved three nights ago beneath the mountain. I did not find that clause. Pip of the Lowmarket found it, stealing it fair and square, and I will note for the record that a thief has done more for the rule of law this season than the entire Third Circle.

"But the dissolution of the contract is not why I am here. The contract is dead. The girl is free. The stars are coming back. I am here because the fraud did not end with Ilse Solenne. Corvan Hale brokered eleven such sales in the Fenmarch alone. Eleven children. I have the names. Four of those children died in the collection—soul-taken, their bodies left breathing, which is a mercy I will let this chamber sit with. Seven survive, scattered, most of them unaware of what was taken from them. Two of the eleven contracts remain technically live, held in abeyance pending the court's restructuring since the fall of the Queen. Those two can still be dissolved. They require only what Hale's fraud denied their mothers: the truth, entered into record, by someone with standing.

"I have standing. I was Second Circle. I read the minutes and did nothing. So I am here to do the something now, twelve years late, at whatever price this chamber sets."

She stopped. Her hands were no longer quite steady. She looked down at them and let them shake, and did not hide it.

"Ask your questions," she said. "I'll answer all of them. Under plate."

* * *

They asked for three hours.

Chancellor Wren was scrupulous—she would grant him that, and she did grant it, bitterly, because scrupulosity in a man who had presided over burnings was its own kind of obscenity. He asked dates. He asked for the chain of custody on the minutes. He asked her to repeat Hale's exact words as recorded in petition 4471, and she did, from memory, because she had read that petition so many times in the past weeks that Ilse Solenne's voice had taken up residence in her skull—a tired young voice, asking the same question three different ways: *is there no other way?*

Councilor Dree, who had voted for more pyres than any sitting member, asked the question everyone had been waiting for.

"If you knew," Dree said, "if you read these minutes four years ago—why come forward now? Why not when it might have saved the four dead children? Why only after the mountain, after the thief, after the swordspeaker made it public anyway? What did you hope to gain, Inquisitor, that you couldn't gain by staying quiet?"

There it was. The real question. Serah had known it would come, and she had prepared an answer, a good one, about institutional reform and the necessity of public record.

She abandoned it.

"Nothing," she said. "I gain nothing. I want to be precise about that, Councilor, because precision is the last professional habit I intend to keep. I gain nothing. The four dead children stay dead. The seven living ones get their contracts dissolved, yes—but I could have delivered that evidence anonymously and spared myself this room. I came here to be stripped in front of you. All of you." She turned, slowly, and took in the gallery—the farmers, the charm-sellers, the mothers. "Because for twelve years I knocked on doors like yours and took things from your houses and called it law. Because I stood at three burnings and held the lantern so the reading of charges could proceed after dark. Because I told myself the institution was broken in parts and sound in its bones, and that a good officer inside a rotten system was a dam against the flood. And then I read the minutes and learned that the flood *was* the system—that the thing I served had sold children to the dark and called it conservation—and I stayed anyway, because leaving was hard and staying was easy and I had a rank I liked.

"Wendell Brightwater hid for seven years. One woman, one gift, hiding in a market stall, and I hunted her for it. She broke her own sacred oath to save a child, and it cost her everything she was, and she paid it. Marisol Vega carried her own death-candle into the dark and gave it willingly to hold a seal shut over something that would eat this city. Brannoc Kell buried his brother. These people—people I arrested, people I *burned*—paid prices I never once asked of myself. So no, Councilor, I don't come here for gain. I come here because justice that only ever flows downhill isn't justice. It's plumbing. And I am done being a pipe."

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Serah heard the wind in the high windows, and beneath it, faintly, the sound of the crowd outside repeating her words to the street.

Dree sat back down without another question.

* * *

The deliberation took less than an hour, which Serah spent in the antechamber with her palms flat on a table, watching the light move. Wendell came to her there. Neither of them spoke at first. Seven years of hunting a woman buys a strange kind of intimacy; they had said the large things on the road and the small things seemed too fragile for the room.

"You don't have to accept exile," Wendell said finally. "The council might just—"

"They won't. And I'd refuse it if they offered pardon." Serah smiled, and found it was the first real smile in days. "You of all people should understand. Some sentences you serve because they're true, not because they're imposed."

"Where will you go?"

"Fenmarch, first. The seven families. Someone owes them the truth face-to-face, with a name attached, and I'm the only name I've got." She paused. "Then wherever the work is. Serah heard there's ward-work going up on the borders now—consent-built, spark by spark, volunteers lining up for miles in some towns. They'll need registrars who actually believe in consent." She huffed something between a laugh and a sigh. "An auditor. Twelve years auditing souls for taking. Maybe I can spend the next twelve auditing them for giving. Making sure nobody's pressured. Making sure the willing are truly willing."

"That sounds like penance."

"It sounds like work," Serah said. "Penance is for people who think forgiveness is available. I'm not sure it is, and I've stopped waiting for it. Work is better. Work shows up every morning regardless."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. "For what it's worth—and I know what it's worth, coming from me, after what I did to you with my silence—thank you. For the road. For the ledger. For today."

"It's not worth anything yet," Serah said. "Ask me again in ten years, if I'm still showing up."

The doors opened. The clerk called her name.

* * *

The verdict was read standing, as judgments of the full chamber were, and Chancellor Wren's dry-paper voice filled the room to its corners.

For crimes against the covenant of office—knowledge withheld, duty abandoned, complicity sustained across four years of service—Serah Vane was stripped of rank, title, pension, and standing. Her name was struck from the rolls of the Inquisition. She was barred from holding civic office in Grayhollow for life. And because the chamber judged that her crimes had been sins of silence rather than acts of malice—because, Wren said, in a tone that suggested the distinction cost him something—the penalty of fire was waived.

In its place: exile. Banishment from the city walls, effective immediately, revocable only by a future council in a future age, if such an age proved wiser.

The gallery did not cheer. Serah had braced for cheering. Instead there was a low, shifting murmur, like wind through wheat, and somewhere a child asked its mother a question Serah couldn't hear, and the mother's answer was a long time coming.

She surrendered the gray coat at the bar. Beneath it she wore plain travel clothes she'd bought with the last of her own coin, deliberately, weeks ago, so that walking out she would not be wearing the institution even one step past the door. The chamber clerk handed back her badge—not restored, returned, the way one returns a confiscated knife to a disarmed man—and she set it on the bar without looking at it.

"Serah Vane," Wren said, and for a moment the dry paper cracked and something almost human came through. "This chamber notes, for the record, that the evidence you presented has already begun proceedings against eleven serving officers of the Third Circle, including two members of this council's oversight committee. Those proceedings will be public. That is your doing."

"No, Chancellor," Serah said. "That's the record's doing. I just stopped locking it away."

She walked the length of the chamber aisle. Faces turned to follow her—not with hatred, which she had prepared for, nor forgiveness, which she had not dared prepare for, but with something more unsettling: attention. Consideration. The look of people re-sorting a category they had kept simple for a long time.

At the doors, Yara Solenne was waiting.

The girl stepped into her path, and for one absurd heartbeat Serah thought: *arrest me, then, finish it* —but Yara only held out her hand. In it lay a small charm on a cord. Knotted string, market-grade, the kind Wendell used to sell for a copper. Serah recognized the knotting. It was charmed against forgetting.

"Maren Ott says it's badly made," Yara said. "She says Wendell could do better. But Wendell says the badly-made ones hold best, because you have to mean the knots yourself." She pressed it into Serah's palm and folded the fingers shut over it. "So you don't forget. Any of it. The good or the bad. We're not doing that anymore—any of us. Right?"

Serah's throat worked. "Right," she managed.

Yara nodded once, satisfied, and stepped aside.

Serah walked out through the crowd on the steps, and the crowd parted, and no one spat, and no one cheered, and an old farmer near the bottom touched his cap to her, awkwardly, the way men touch caps to weather they don't fully understand but have decided to walk in anyway.

Outside the walls, on the south road, the evening was coming on gold and clean. Above the mountains, where for seven years the sky had been a wound, the stars were returning—one, then another, then a scatter of them, tentative as embers breathed upon. Travelers on the road had stopped to watch. A family beside a cart pointed upward, and the children were counting.

Serah stood on the bare earth beyond Grayhollow's jurisdiction, carrying a badly made charm and nothing else, and watched the sky fill in.

Behind her, hooves on the road. She turned. A rider came down from the city at an easy canter, leading a second horse, and raised a hand in greeting—Brannoc Kell, broad and gray-bearded, with the particular set of a man who had buried what he set out to bury and discovered he was still alive afterward and hadn't yet decided what to do about it.

"Heard you're headed for the Fenmarch," he called. "Ward-work's going up along the whole southern stretch. They're paying in coin and gratitude, mostly gratitude." He drew rein beside her and glanced back at the kindling sky. "Could use a registrar who reads the old covenant law. Could use, frankly, someone who knows exactly how the rotten versions were built—so we build the new ones right."

Serah looked at the second horse. Then at the stars. Then at the small knotted charm in her hand, which had gone, very slightly, warm.

"Well," she said, swinging up into the saddle. "Someone has to make sure the willing are truly willing."

They rode east together, toward the Fenmarch, toward the work, while above them the second star brightened—and then, far off at the edge of the dark, a third.
