## 34. Yara's Own Name

The paper on the council clerk's desk was the color of old milk, and it trembled when I breathed on it.

I hadn't expected that. Six months ago I had walked out of Grayhollow in the dark with a locket humming against my sternum and a lie sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal, and my hands had been steady then—steady all the way to the mountain, steady through the nursery of candles and the herald's honeyed voice and the moment the last star began to die above us. My hands had only shaken once, in all of that, and that was when Wendell knelt on the antechamber floor and told me everything.

But this was just a piece of paper. A line for a name. And my hands would not stop trembling.

"You don't have to do it today," Wendell said beside me. She'd kept her voice low, the way she did in the market when she didn't want the charms to hear her being soft.

"I know."

"No one in this room will think less of you if you wait."

"I know, Wendell." I looked up at her. The scar through her eyebrow caught the clerk's lamplight. "That's rather the point. No one gets to think anything about it but me."

She shut her mouth. She'd gotten better at that, since the mountain. Better at letting me carry things without trying to lift them out of my hands.

The clerk—a narrow man named Ostrell who had ink stains up both forearms and the patient expression of someone who processed grief and joy in equal bureaucratic measure—cleared his throat. "Whenever you're ready, miss. Full legal name as you wish it entered. Given name first."

*Yara.* That part was easy. It had never belonged to anyone else. My mother had chosen it, the records said—Serah had found the midwife's ledger, the real one, buried under three layers of Inquisition forgery—and she had spoken it aloud over me before she ever signed anything, before the agents in their gray coats leaned over her bed and made the sale sound like mercy. *Yara,* she'd said. A name given freely, before anything in her life was taken by force.

It was the second half of the line that mattered.

For sixteen years I had been Yara Brightwater, and I had loved the name the way you love a coat that fits—warm, familiar, and never quite yours. Brightwater meant Wendell. It meant the shop above the charm stall, the smell of beeswax and iron filings, the sound of her humming off-key while she polished silver for spells she no longer performed. Taking it off felt like betrayal.

Keeping it felt like a lie.

And the whole point—the whole point of everything since the mountain, since the contract burned and Marisol's candle kindled alone in a sky still mostly empty—was that lies were how we got here. Lies were mortar. Lies were what the wards had been built from, what the Inquisition's paperwork ran on, what the Ember Court fed on. Every soul in those collection engines had been taken by a document that called theft *agreement* and coercion *consent*. I had spent nineteen days as the payment on such a document. I knew exactly what a comfortable untruth weighed.

"Miss?" Ostrell said gently.

"My mother's name," I said. "Can you find it? In your records?"

He blinked. "This is a name registration, not a genealogy inquiry—"

"Then I'll say it and you'll write it down." I put my palms flat on the desk so he could see they'd stopped shaking. "Maren Solenne. That was her name. The woman the Inquisition coerced into selling her daughter's soul to buy passage through the border wards. Her name is in your archives somewhere—I've read the testimony Serah Vane gave the council, and it's in there, in the parts your superiors tried to redact. Maren Solenne, of the village of Hallowfen, died on the road north two days after she signed. She thought she was buying me a life. They were buying me for fuel."

The lamp flame stood very still, as if it were listening.

Ostrell looked at Wendell. Wendell looked at me. And then, slowly, deliberately, she turned her gaze to the window—as if to say: *this is yours, I'm not speaking for you anymore.*

"Miss," Ostrell said, "the court has already ratified the fraud clause. Your contract is dissolved, your soul restored, and the council has publicly acknowledged—" he cleared his throat again, more carefully this time, "—acknowledged wrongdoing. You owe this city nothing. Least of all an accusation in its records."

"I'm not accusing anyone." I heard my own voice come out level and wondered whose voice it was. It sounded older than mine. "Accusing is what the Inquisition does—find someone small and make them carry the whole weight. I'm not carrying anything anymore. I'm just setting something down where people can see it. Her name belongs in the light. That's all. Write it down or don't, but either way I'm going to walk out of here as Yara Solenne."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, Grayhollow went about its morning—a cart rattling past, a fishmonger crying her prices, the ordinary music of a city learning, star by returning star, that it might have been wrong about magic all along.

Ostrell dipped his pen.

"*Yara Solenne,*" he repeated, writing. "Daughter of record: none. Guardian of record: Wendell Brightwater." He glanced up. "Shall I amend that as well?"

I opened my mouth to say *yes, of course*—

"No," I said instead, and surprised myself. "Leave it. But add a line after it."

His pen hovered.

"Add: *by choice, renewed.*"

Wendell made a small sound behind me—not quite a laugh, not quite anything else—and when I turned, she was pressing the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes, the way she did when she refused, on principle, to cry in front of officials.

"There," Ostrell said, blotting the page. "It's done. You'll want to check the spelling."

I checked the spelling. *Solenne.* Five letters I had never written before, and my hand knew them anyway, the way a river knows a channel it hasn't run in a hundred years. I stared at the ink until it dried.

Outside, the morning was cold and clean, and above the eastern rooftops, faint as a needle-prick, one new star held its place in the pale sky. Not Marisol's—that one burned brighter now, almost low enough to touch, warm orange where the others were white. This was another one. Pip swore there were four now. Brannoc counted five. Nobody agreed, and everybody watched.

"Yara Solenne," Wendell said, trying the weight of it.

"It's heavy," I said.

"Good things usually are."

* * *

We didn't go straight home. We walked, because Wendell believed most problems could be walked around until they looked smaller, and because the city itself wanted looking at these days.

Grayhollow had changed the way a body changes after fever—not all at once, not everywhere, but in places you could point to. The ash-bell posts along the Wardens' Way, which had rung to summon burn-crowds for two generations, stood silent, and someone had wound ribbons around the bell ropes, dozens of them, colors fading in the weather. The notice board outside the guildhall still carried edicts, but new papers were layered over the old ones: petitions, arguments, a hand-lettered demand that the Purge trials be reopened. Someone had chalked a slogan on the fountain rim in letters a meter tall: *THE STARS CAME BACK. ASK YOURSELF WHY.*

"They're scared," Wendell said, following my gaze. "People who built their whole lives on a law are scared when the law starts wobbling. Scared people do ugly things before they do brave ones."

"Is that what happened to you? When the wards fell?"

She was quiet long enough that I almost took it back. Then: "When the order fell. The wards came later. And yes. I did ugly things. I hid. I let you grow up inside my hiding place and called it love." She said it plainly now, without flinching—she'd practiced saying it, I realized, the way I'd practiced writing my mother's name. "You're allowed to bring that up whenever you want, you know. Forever. It doesn't expire."

"I know." I hooked my arm through hers. "I'm saving it for special occasions."

That earned me a real laugh, short and startled, and I hoarded it the way I hoarded all of them.

We cut through the night market square, empty in daylight, stalls shuttered, and stopped—for no reason either of us said aloud—at the spot where Wendell had lit a charm openly, weeks ago, and waited for the mob that hadn't come. The cobbles there were scorched in a little crescent where the emberlight had kissed them. People avoided stepping on it. Or maybe they stepped on it on purpose; I'd seen an old woman pause there yesterday, press her boot sole flat against the mark, and stand a moment with her eyes closed, like touching a saint's footstone.

"Wendell." I'd been carrying the question since the registry. It sat in my chest now, taking up all the room the coal used to. "Teach me swordspeak."

Her arm went rigid under mine.

"Not like that—not—" I pulled back so I could see her face. "I'm not asking you to hand me your legacy. I don't want the order. I don't want the oaths, the history, whatever the swordspeakers owed kings. That's dead, and it should stay dead, honestly, because look what it did to you. Look what keeping silent cost."

"Yara—"

"Let me finish. Please." The market square was empty except for us and the scorched crescent and the pigeons, and I made myself stand in it the way she'd stood there with her charm lit, waiting to see what the world would throw. "On the mountain, when you spoke your oath in your own words and the iron answered—you weren't doing their magic. Their magic rusted shut years ago, you told me so yourself. You made something new out of the truth. That's the only inheritance I want. Not what they gave you. What *you* found, down there in the dark, when everything they taught you failed and you reached past it anyway." I swallowed. "My mother gave me a name and got swindled out of me for it. Everyone else in my life has assigned me something—foundling, payment, Brightwater. I want one thing that's mine because I chose it. Teach me the way *you* speak to steel. Not the order's way. Yours."

The wind moved through the shuttered market, rattling awning rings, stirring the ribboned bell ropes somewhere out of sight.

"You understand what you're asking," Wendell said at last, very quietly. "It isn't a trick you learn. It's a way of standing in the world. Every time I speak to a blade now, I have to mean it—all the way down, no reserve, no merchant's smile. Swordspeak built on anything less than total truth goes hollow. You saw what seven years of silence did to my voice." She touched her throat. "If I teach you, I can't teach you to hide. There's no hidden version of this. The law is changing, maybe, but laws wobble, remember. The day may come when speaking steel aloud costs you something."

"Good." I said it before I could be sensible about it. "Everything worth having has cost somebody. My mother paid for my name with her life. Marisol paid for all of ours with—" My voice snagged. I pushed through. "I'm done getting things free. Free things come with fine print. I'd rather pay in the open, where I can see the price."

Wendell looked at me for a long moment. The morning sun crested the guildhall roof and laid a bright bar across the cobbles between us, across the scorched crescent, and she stepped into the light like a woman crossing a threshold she'd been circling for years.

"When I was seventeen," she said, "my master asked me why I wanted the sword. I gave him the answer from the recruitment pamphlet. Honor. Protection of the realm. He hit me with the flat of a practice blade and asked again." Her mouth curved, rueful. "It took me eleven tries to give him an answer that rang true. The blade knows. That's the first thing you learn—it always knows."

"What was your twelfth answer?"

"'Because I couldn't bear to watch and do nothing.'" She shrugged. "Seventeen-year-old me thought that was a small reason. Turns out it's the only one that matters." She studied me, head tilted, and I felt the look go all the way through me, cataloguing, weighing—no merchant's smile anywhere in it. "So. Why do you want it, Yara Solenne? And mind—if the answer is revenge, or proving something, or finishing what I started, the steel will hear the hollowness and refuse you, and I'll send you home, and we won't speak of it again for a year."

I thought about lying. It rose in me automatically, smooth and ready—*to protect people, to honor my mother, to keep Grayhollow safe*—all true things, arranged like a shop display, none of them the truth underneath.

The steel would hear the hollowness.

"The night the stars went out," I said slowly, feeling for each word like footing on scree, "I ran toward the mountain because I thought I was saving you. That's what I told myself. But halfway up the pass I stopped believing it, and I kept walking anyway, because some part of me already suspected the truth—that I was the payment, and some rotten corner of me wanted to see the court that had bought me. Wanted to belong to something that had chosen me, even if it chose me as currency." My hands were shaking again. I let them. "And then I saw the nursery, Wendell. All the ones who came before me. And I understood that belonging isn't something you're given. Not by courts, not by contracts, not even by mothers who love you and sign anyway. It's something you forge. With your own hands. Out of what's actually true." I lifted my chin. "I want swordspeak because it's the truest thing I've ever seen anyone do, and I am finished—*finished*—with living in other people's versions of me. If that answer rings hollow, ring your blade and tell me."

Wendell didn't move for a long moment.

Then she drew.

Not fast—there was nothing of the duelist in it. Slow, ceremonial, the way dawn comes up: the steel sliding free of the leather with a whisper like a held breath released, and the morning light running down the blade and pooling at the tip. She held it flat across both palms, the way I'd seen her hold the locket, the way she handled every object in her life that remembered more than it should.

"Swordspeak isn't words," she said. "That's the first lesson, and everyone gets it wrong. It's attention. The blade has been folded and quenched and ground—it remembers fire and hammer and the smith's intention, layer over layer, like a tree remembers years. When you speak to it truly, you're not commanding it. You're introducing yourself. You're saying: *here is what I am, all of it, nothing held back—and will you stand with that?*" She turned the blade so the light slid toward me. "Most people can't do it. Not because they lack talent. Because they lack the stomach for the introduction."

"And if I have the stomach?"

"Then we start tomorrow. Dawn. The yard behind Brannoc's forge—he's offered, and his anvil has better manners than most students." She extended the hilt toward me, not handing it over, just offering the grip for a single touch. "One rule above all others, Yara Solenne. In my yard, you never lie. Not to the steel, not to me, and hardest of all—not to yourself. The day you catch yourself performing instead of speaking, we stop, and we sit, and we find out what you were hiding. That's the whole curriculum. Everything else is technique."

I wrapped my fingers around the grip. The metal was cold, then warm, then—briefly, unmistakably—*attentive*, the way a sleeping animal goes still when it decides whether you're friend or threat.

"Here is what I am," I said softly, to the steel, to Wendell, to the empty square and the mother whose name I'd inked an hour ago and the father I'd never learn and all the payments in the nursery who never got a choice. "All of it. Nothing held back."

The blade hummed—one clear note, low and brief, gone before I could decide I'd imagined it.

Wendell's breath caught audibly.

"That," she said, "took me three months."

* * *

We were nearly home—past the fountain, past the chalked slogan, someone had added a smaller line beneath it in different handwriting, *and ask who paid*—when Brannoc came pounding up the Warden's Way with his apron still on and soot to his elbows, moving with the urgent, rolling gait of a man carrying news too heavy to jog with.

He skidded to a halt in front of us, breathing hard, and for a moment he just looked at us both, and I saw him decide something—saw the decision move across his broad face like weather.

"Message from the valley wards," he managed. "Pip's at the relay station now, listening to the locks—says the correspondence vault at Hallowfen waystation just unlocked itself. All of it. Every drawer." His voice dropped, roughened. "Yara. Girl. Hallowfen was your mother's village."

The word landed in me like a struck bell.

"There's more," Brannoc said, and his eyes went wet and furious all at once. "The drawers that opened—they're the ones with the original sale ledgers. Your mother's contract, the agents' orders, all the paperwork the Inquisition burned copies of. And pinned to the top of the stack, addressed in ink nobody's used in twenty years—" he swallowed, "—it's signed *M. Solenne.* Something in that vault has been waiting for you."

The star overhead seemed, absurdly, to sharpen.

Wendell's hand found my shoulder. "When do we leave?" she said.

"Now," I said, and my hands, for the first time all day, were perfectly steady.
