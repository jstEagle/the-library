## 35. Letters to the Dead

The rowan tree stood alone on the hill above Grayhollow's eastern wall, and it had been there longer than the wall, longer than the city, maybe longer than the law that had broken him. Brannoc had chosen it three days ago and then spent those three days finding reasons not to come: the candle had to be wrapped properly, the grave had to be dug at the right depth, the soil turned the right way, the hour chosen according to ward-smith's custom that no one alive practiced anymore but him.

He knew what he was doing. He'd been doing it his whole life—building small ceremonies into fortifications so that feeling couldn't get through.

The candle sat in a box on his knees now, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with copper wire he had drawn himself from ore he had mined himself, because if he was going to do this he was going to do all of it, every last stubborn piece of craft in his hands, or he was going to sit down in the dirt and never stand up again.

Tam's candle.

It had been one of hundreds in the Ember Court's collection engine, racked in iron cradles like bottles in a cellar, each one labeled in a script that glowed faintly when you read it aloud. *Tamsin Galloway-Bell* — no, that wasn't right. *Tamsin Bell*. Just Bell. His brother had been just Bell to the court that ate him, a name on a shelf, forty-one years of laughter and bad carpentry and a whistle he did off-key reduced to a stub of wax two inches tall.

Brannoc had carried him out of the mountain in his coat pocket, against his heart, and had not let anyone touch the box since. Not Wendell, though she'd offered gently. Not Marisol, who was beyond offering anything now. Not even Pip, which had surprised him most of all, because Pip touched everything—that was how the thief understood the world, hands first, eyes second, questions third. Pip had looked at the box once, for a long moment, and then had simply nodded and never reached for it.

Now the hill was cold and the wind came up from the valley smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and Brannoc knelt in the rowan's roots with a spade he'd sharpened that morning for no good reason, and dug.

The ground gave easily. Rowan roots hold soft earth open, letting rain and worms do their work; his father had told him that, or Tam had, one of them, back when the world was a workshop and a kitchen and nothing else. He dug until the hole was the depth of his forearm, and then he stopped, because the custom said the depth of the forearm—the measure of what a person can reach, the distance a hand can travel toward something it loves—and he set the spade down and took the box into his lap again.

He should say something. Ward-smiths said words over candles. He'd said them over a hundred in his working years, the old formula: *What was given returns; what was taken is answered; sleep in the wood, wake in the light.* He opened his mouth and none of it came out. The words were rusted shut like Wendell's oath had been, and he understood her now, in a way he hadn't in the mountain's dark when she'd knelt before Yara and finally spoken. Some languages die in the mouth before they die in the heart.

So he said it plain, in the only language left to him.

"Tam." His voice cracked on the single syllable like green wood in a fire. "Tam, I brought you home."

* * *

He had rehearsed the rest for three days too. That was the shameful arithmetic of grief: you could plan a confession down to the breath, and still, when the moment came, find yourself saying things you hadn't agreed to say.

"You were twenty-six," he told the box. "You were twenty-six and I was thirty-one and I signed the work order because the pay was good and the Inquisition's engineers flattered me, called me the finest smith south of the mountains, and I believed them. I believed them because I wanted to. That's the part I've never said out loud. It wasn't ignorance. I read the specifications. I read the section on *fuel sources* and I told myself it meant charcoal."

His hands shook around the box. Below, Grayhollow lay in its bowl of rooftops, chimney smoke standing straight in the still air. Somewhere in that sprawl Yara was probably arguing with Wendell about sword-forms, loudly, joyfully, the way the living argue about things that don't matter, and Brannoc envied it the way you envy warmth through a window.

"The wards held for sixty years," he went on. "Sixty years of border villages sleeping safe, and every night they slept on my brother. And I built the frame that held him. And then I drank for eleven years and told everyone—including myself—that it was because of my hands, the tremor, the shame of a craftsman who can't hold a chisel steady. It wasn't my hands. It was that I knew what was under the mortar."

A crow landed in the rowan's crown and shifted, and a few red berries came loose and fell, bright as sparks, one landing on the turned earth beside the hole.

"I'm not asking forgiveness. I asked the Queen for it and she laughed at me, and she was right to. Forgiveness isn't hers to give and it isn't yours either—you're past all that—but I need to say it anyway where you can hear it, or where the tree can hear it, or where whatever's left of Brannoc Bell after this can hear it. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I built your coffin and called it a border."

The candle, when he unwrapped it, was smaller than he remembered. Grief does that—shrinks the dead down to what fits in your hands. But as the oilcloth fell away the flame inside stirred, that ember-light the court had bottled, and it leaned toward him the way a flame leans toward a draft, and he chose—it was a choice, he made it deliberately—to believe it leaned the way a face turns toward a familiar voice.

"Forty-one years," he whispered. "They kept you burning forty-one years. You always said you wanted to be useful."

And then, because the custom demanded a letter and he had none, because letters to the dead are written in the body and not the hand, Brannoc Bell bent over the shallow grave and began to weep.

* * *

He heard footsteps on the hill path and knew without turning who it was. Pip walked like a rumor—you heard the absence of sound rather than sound itself—but the lockpick's breathing had a rhythm Brannoc had learned in the mountain's dark, when they'd crawled through the wards' bones shoulder to shoulder in the black and Pip had sung locks open in a whisper while Brannoc tried not to think about what souls sounded like when they burned.

Pip didn't announce themselves. Pip never announced anything; announcement was for people who needed permission to occupy space, and Pip had decided long ago that they occupied space by default. They simply arrived at the edge of the grave and stood there, hands in pockets, hood back despite the wind, and watched.

Brannoc should have stopped crying. That was the rule he'd lived by for decades: grief is a door, close it fast, bar it, build a wall in front of it and a lie in front of the wall. He'd done it at the funeral that never happened—there had been no body, only a notice of conscription, only an engineer's ledger entry in his own handwriting—and he'd done it every year after on the anniversary, drinking quietly in a locked room, mourning in the dark like something illegal.

But the thing about walls is someone has to maintain them, and Brannoc was tired, tired in the marrow, tired the way the wards must have been tired, holding and holding and holding until the night the stars went out.

So he cried in front of Pip, ugly and open-mouthed and hiccuping, a grown man kneeling in the dirt with a dead man's soul-candle in his shaking hands, and Pip stood there and let him.

That was the gift of it. Pip didn't crouch and put an arm around him. Pip didn't murmur *there, there*, didn't offer the cheap architecture of comfort. Pip stood witness, still as a fencepost, and after a while sat down cross-legged at the graveside with the patience of someone who has waited outside a hundred locked doors and knows that every lock opens eventually—not forced, not picked, but *ready*.

"Sorry," Brannoc managed eventually, wiping his face with the heel of his hand. "I know you came up here to—"

"To sit on a hill," Pip said. Their voice was low and roughened, the voice of someone who spoke rarely enough that speech stayed strange to them. "Hills are good. Wanted to see the city from somewhere it doesn't look like itself."

Brannoc huffed something that was almost a laugh. From up here Grayhollow did look different—smaller, grayer, a knot of fear that had mistaken itself for a civilization. "It never looks like itself from anywhere. That's the problem with the place."

"Mm." Pip studied the candle. The ember-flame had steadied, burning upright now, calm. "Can I?"

Brannoc hesitated—one heartbeat, two—and then lifted the box, offering it. Pip took it with both hands, the way you take something holy or breakable, and held it at eye level, and for a long moment thief and candle regarded one another.

"Heard it," Pip said quietly. "In the vault. When I cracked the engine's housing. All of them, humming. Hundreds of voices under the noise." They tilted their head. "Yours was singing."

"Singing?" Brannoc's throat closed. "Tam couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. Our mother used to—"

"He was carrying it anyway." Pip passed the box back with exaggerated care. "Off-key. Loud. Like somebody who doesn't care whether anybody likes it."

And that undid Brannoc worse than the weeping had, because it was true, it was exactly Tam, Tam whistling through the workshop with sawdust in his hair, Tam singing at the top of his lungs on the walk home from market, Tam who had once told a visiting Inquisitor to his face that the man's hat was ridiculous and gotten away with it because even Inquisitors didn't know what to do with joy that fearless. Forty-one years in an iron rack under a mountain, and the song hadn't died. Souls weren't fuel. Fuel didn't sing.

"Help me," Brannoc said. "I want to do it right. The old rite needs two—a speaker and a keeper. Speaker says the words, keeper holds the light till it's laid down." He swallowed. "I can't be both. I tried to be both my whole life and look what it made."

Pip nodded once and moved to the other side of the grave, kneeling, and cupped their hands around the little flame when Brannoc tipped it free of its wax bed onto his palm. It sat there warm as a coal, patient as dawn.

"What was given returns," Brannoc said, and the old words came—not rusted, not dead, just waiting, the way songs wait. "What was taken is answered. Sleep in the wood, wake in the light."

He lowered his hand into the grave. The flame transferred from skin to soil without hesitation, sinking into the dark earth like a coin into water, leaving a glow beneath the surface, a heartbeat of gold.

"Tamsin Bell," he said, and said it the way the court never had—loud, off-key, unashamed. "My brother. Builder of nothing but good chairs. Singer of terrible songs. Kept against his will, returned against the odds. Let the rowan hold what I couldn't."

Pip, keeper's hands still cupped over the spot, added softly: "Let the light go where it likes."

The glow pulsed once beneath the soil—twice—and then settled into a faint steady warmth that Brannoc felt more than saw, radiating up through his knees, through the ground, into the roots. Above them the rowan shivered though there was almost no wind, and a handful of berries came loose in a slow red rain, and Brannoc would swear until his dying day that the tree leaned, very slightly, toward the grave.

He filled it in with his bare hands. No spade. Some work wants tools; some work wants skin.

* * *

They stayed until the sun went down behind Grayhollow and the city lit its lamps, one by one, like a slow constellation being assembled by nervous hands. Pip had produced bread and hard cheese from somewhere inside their coat—of course Pip had; Pip prepared for contingencies nobody else imagined—and they ate sitting against the rowan's trunk, shoulder to shoulder, watching the lights.

"Wendell's drawing plans," Pip said eventually, around a mouthful of cheese. "For new wards. Her and the big drawings and the numbers. She asked if you'd look at them."

Brannoc stared at the valley. "She knows what I did."

"She knows what you survived." Pip shrugged, a compact motion. "Also she's Wendell. She'll keep asking until you answer, that's the whole woman in one sentence."

"The wards were built on souls, Pip. Every mile of them. Whatever she's designing, if I help build it, everyone will know what I was. Ward-smiths talk. Guild records survive. There'll be a day when some apprentice reads the old specifications next to the new ones and sees my name on both."

"Yeah," Pip said. "Probably."

"That doesn't trouble you?"

Pip considered the question with the seriousness they gave to everything, chewing, watching a lamp far below flicker and steady. "Trouble's a lock," they said at last. "Most people stand outside it their whole lives, rattling the handle. You already went through. Door's behind you now. Only thing left is what you build on this side." They glanced sideways at him, quick and dry. "Also I heard your brother singing in a cage under a mountain and he didn't sound ashamed of anything. Might be worth asking why."

Brannoc looked down at the fresh-turned earth at the tree's roots, at the faint warmth he could still feel rising from it like the memory of a hearth. Tam had never been ashamed of anything. Not his singing, not his failures, not the time he'd ruined an entire season's commission through pure cheerful incompetence. Tam had loved out loud, worked out loud, grieved out loud—Brannoc remembered now, with a pain that was also a kind of relief, Tam weeping openly at their mother's graveside while Brannoc stood rigid beside him building walls—and Tam had gone into the dark with his name sung wrong and still come out singing.

Loyalty kept in silence was never loyalty at all. Wendell had learned it kneeling before her daughter. Maybe it was a lesson with more students than one.

"All right," Brannoc said. "Tell her I'll look at the plans. Tell her—" He stopped, started again. "Tell her I want the new wards built different. Not hidden, not secret. Every contributor named. Carved into the stone, every spark, every volunteer. So nobody ever gets to call it *fuel* again."

Pip smiled—rare as a comet, brief as one—and knocked their shoulder against his. "Was going to tell you anyway. She already carved your name at the top of the first page."

Brannoc closed his eyes. The wind moved through the rowan, and beneath the roots, faint and unmistakable and gloriously off-key, something hummed.

Below them, in the darkening city, a bell began to ring from the council tower—three strokes, pause, three more—the signal that had spread through Grayhollow these past weeks for one purpose only. A star was kindling. The second this month, the fourth since the mountain. People would be pouring into the streets right now, tipping their heads back, pointing, afraid and glad at once.

"Coming?" Pip asked, already on their feet.

Brannoc rested his palm flat on the warm earth one last time. "Go ahead. I'll catch up." He waited until the thief's footsteps faded down the hill path, and then he said, quietly, to the tree, to the light, to the letter he would keep writing here for the rest of his life:

"Watch this, Tam. We're going to build something worth singing about."

And high over the eastern hills, where the sky had been empty for seven years, a new star rose trembling into view—and Brannoc could have sworn, as he stood and shouldered his spade, that it flickered once, twice, like a tune finding its key.
