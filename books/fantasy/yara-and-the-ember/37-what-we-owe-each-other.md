## 37. What We Owe Each Other

The kitchen of the Brightwater house had never been large enough for what it held. A stove that Brannoc had rebuilt twice. A table Pip claimed to have stolen fair and square from a nobleman's garden, though everyone knew he'd bought it and was embarrassed about the sentiment. Dried herbs from Marisol's last planting hanging in bundles from the rafters, still fragrant a year after her death, because none of them could bring themselves to take them down.

And now, on the morning of Yara's seventeenth birthday, it held flour.

Wendell stood at the counter with her sleeves rolled and her forearms dusted white, attempting something she had not attempted since before the Purge: her mother's honey cake. The recipe lived only in her hands, in muscle memory older than any oath, and her hands were shaking.

"You're strangling it," Yara said from the doorway. She was seventeen today and looked it—taller than Wendell remembered expecting, her hair cut short in the summer heat, a smudge of charcoal on her jaw from sketching at dawn. She'd taken to drawing the mountain lately. The mountain, and the slope beneath it where a seal glowed steady as a hearthstone. She never said why. She didn't have to.

"I'm *kneading* it," Wendell said. "There's a difference."

"You're threatening it." Yara crossed the room, washed her hands, and took half the dough without asking, the way she'd done since she was six years old and tall enough to see over the counter. Their elbows knocked. It was the most natural thing in the world, and Wendell had spent seven years believing she would never have natural things again.

They worked in silence for a while. Outside, Grayhollow was waking into its second spring of open sky. Somewhere down the lane, a neighbor was singing—not quietly, either. A year ago, singing charm-words in the street would have earned a pyre. Now old Mistress Corvel sang her grandmother's weather-chants loud enough to wake the roosters, and the roosters complained, and that was all.

"Seventeen," Wendell said.

"Don't."

"I'm allowed to say it once."

"You said it yesterday. You said it when I woke up. You'll say it again when I blow out the candle." Yara flipped the dough with a deftness Wendell hadn't taught her, because there had been no time to teach her anything but survival. "You get one more, and you should save it."

Wendell laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that hurt a little on its way out because it used muscles she'd let go slack. "Fine. I'm saving it."

* * *

The cake came out crooked. They iced it anyway, badly, arguing about whether honey went in the icing or on it, and by the time the argument resolved both options had happened and the result was too sweet and perfect. They ate the first slices standing up over the sink like thieves, because that was how they had always eaten things that mattered—quickly, secretly, before the world could take them back.

Then Yara set down her fork, and Wendell saw it coming the way you see weather coming: a change in the light, a pressure behind the ears.

"Wendell." She almost never called her *mother*. Not out of cruelty. Out of precision—the word meant something specific, and they were still deciding together what their thing was. "I want to talk about the years. All of them. The ones you didn't tell me about."

Wendell wiped her hands on her apron. Once, this sentence would have dropped a stone through her chest. Now it just felt heavy, the way truth always did—carryable, if you braced properly.

"All right," she said.

"Not tonight. Not with everyone here. Just—" Yara gestured at the kitchen, the crooked cake, the morning light coming slantwise through Marisol's herb bundles. "Just us. While it's still my birthday somewhere, maybe."

"It's your birthday here."

"Then while it's my birthday, tell me about the night you took me in. Not the version for Serah's testimony. Not the version for the council records. The true one. The one with you in it, being afraid."

So Wendell sat down at the stolen table, across from the girl who had been sold before she could speak, and began.

* * *

"You know the shape of it," Wendell said. "A woman came to the safehouse in the dead borderlands with a baby and a wound that wouldn't close. Your mother. Her name was Sefa Ilvane—I learned that later, from Serah's papers. That night she was just *the woman*, and I was just the swordspeaker whose order had fallen apart around her, hiding in a house full of people pretending to be less than we were."

Yara nodded. She had heard fragments. Everyone had, during the trials, the testimonies, the long public unraveling of what the Inquisition had done. But fragments weren't the same as the telling, and they both knew it.

"She was dying," Wendell went on. "The ward-crossing had cost her more than passage. And she told me what she'd signed. Who she'd signed it with. She made me swear—" Wendell's voice caught, and she let it, because this was the point, letting it catch where Yara could see. "She made me swear to keep you hidden from the court, and to keep the contract hidden from you, because she believed—if you grew up knowing you were *owned*, even by something far away, you'd grow up small. She'd seen it happen. Children who knew their price walked through the world like debtors. She wanted you to walk through the world like a freeborn girl who liked honey cake and asked too many questions."

"And instead I grew up asking one question over and over, and you lied to me over and over, until the locket answered it better than you ever did."

"Yes." Wendell didn't flinch from it. She had learned, kneeling in an antechamber under a mountain, that flinching was just another form of lying. "Every time. And every time I lied, I told myself it was protection. Her wish. My duty. But that wasn't the whole of it, Yara. The whole of it is that I was afraid. Afraid that if you knew what you were—what had been taken from you—you'd stop being mine. That you'd go looking for the court, or hate me, or both. I chose my fear over your right to know yourself. I dressed it up as loyalty. It wasn't. Loyalty kept in silence was never loyalty at all. I learned that too late, and it cost—" She stopped. Listed it, silently: seven years of stars. Nineteen candles. Marisol, who had carried her own death-candle to the deep places so that Yara could sit here eating too-sweet cake. "It cost more than I had any right to spend."

Yara was quiet for a long moment. She turned her cup in her hands—a habit she'd gotten from Wendell, though neither of them mentioned it.

"Do you know what I remember," Yara said finally, "from before the locket showed me anything? Before the mountain? I remember being eight, and finding a charm in your coat pocket. A real one. Warm. And I remember bringing it to you, and watching your face go through about five different kinds of terror before you said it was 'a keepsake.' And I remember deciding—eight years old, deciding—that I would never make you look like that again. So I stopped looking. In pockets, in drawers, in your silences. I built myself a little blind spot the exact size and shape of your secrets, and I lived in it for eight years, Wendell. I *chose* not to know. So when I say I understand why you lied, understand that I'm not saying it kindly. I'm saying it because I did the same thing. We were both keeping you safe from me, or me safe from you, and neither of us was brave enough to say which."

Wendell felt her eyes sting. Seventeen years old. Seventeen, and already wiser than Wendell had been at forty.

"When did you know," Wendell asked, "that I knew?"

"The night of my sixteenth birthday." Yara smiled, small and crooked. "You gave me the locket and couldn't look at me while you did it. You've given me gifts my whole life and you always watch my face. That year you watched the table." She reached across and turned Wendell's hand over, palm up—the sword callus gone soft now, mostly, fading the way old scars fade but never quite leaving. "I knew then that whatever was coming, you'd known it was coming for years. I was angry for a long time after the mountain. You know that."

"I know."

"I'm not angry anymore. I want to be clear that it's not forgiveness, exactly—or it is, but not the kind where the debt gets erased. More like..." She searched for it. Yara was good with words; she'd inherited that from a mother she'd never met, and these days she let herself believe it. "More like we agreed on terms. You owe me the truth from now on. Even when it's ugly. Even when it costs you. And I owe you the same—which means I owe you the truth that sometimes I still wake up angry. Sometimes I dream about the nursery, and the other payments, and I wake up furious at everyone, including you, including her, including the whole rotten arrangement of the world. And I stay anyway. I choose this kitchen. Every morning I choose it. That's the deal. Not absolution. Selection."

Wendell turned her hand and gripped Yara's. "Terms accepted."

"Good." Yara blinked fast, twice, refusing on principle to cry on her own birthday morning. "Because there's one more term."

"Name it."

"The cake. Next year, we make two. One for us, and one we take up the slope and leave at the seal. Marisol would have complained about your baking for an hour and eaten three slices. She should get to keep doing it."

Wendell's throat closed. She nodded, because words weren't going to come, and Yara squeezed her hand once—hard, the way you grip someone in rough water—and let go.

* * *

The party was everything a birthday in a healing city ought to be: too loud, too crowded, Brannoc arriving with a wooden bird he'd carved and pretending it was nothing, Pip producing a bottle of wine of genuinely suspicious provenance ("It fell off a cart. The cart was moving very slowly and I was holding it"), Serah coming late and standing awkwardly at the edge until Yara dragged her in by the sleeve, because Yara had decided long ago that Serah Vane's penance would include learning to be loved by the people she'd once hunted, and Serah had, grimly, agreed to the terms.

They ate. They sang, badly, except Pip who sang beautifully and denied it. Brannoc got misty-eyed over the wooden bird and blamed woodsmoke. When Yara blew out the candle, she didn't wish silently—new tradition, new city—she said it out loud:

"For everyone still finding their way home."

Everyone drank to that. Everyone understood who *everyone* included.

Later, when the guests had gone and the dishes sat abandoned, Wendell found Yara on the back step, knees drawn up, looking east toward the mountain where the seal glowed faint and warm against the dusk.

"Happy seventeenth," Wendell said, sitting beside her. "That's my one."

Yara leaned her head briefly against Wendell's shoulder—an allowance granted perhaps four times a year, treasured beyond reason. "Best one yet," she said. "Despite everything."

"*Especially* despite everything."

They sat in the blue hour, and the first stars came out.

Except—

Wendell saw it at the same moment Yara went rigid beside her. Low in the eastern dark, above the shoulder of the mountain, where for a year there had been only the seal's steady ember-glow and the empty black: a new point of light. Faint. Trembling. Then steadying, like a candle flame finding its footing in still air.

Not Marisol's star. That one had kindled a year ago and burned low and constant near the horizon, and they visited it the way you visit a grave.

This one was higher. Newer. And as they watched, breath caught, a second point bloomed beside it—and a third, further out, like sparks lifting from a fire that had never truly gone out.

"They're coming home," Yara whispered. "The freed souls. They're actually—they're *finding their way*."

Wendell put her arm around her daughter—not by blood, not by law, not by contract, but by choice renewed every single day—and together they watched the sky begin, star by patient star, to fill.
