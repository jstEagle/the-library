## 38. The Second Star

The night Marisol's star kindled, Grayhollow did not sleep.

Wendell stood in the market square with Yara's hand in hers and watched the whole city do a thing it had never done in seven years: look up without flinching. People had come out of their houses in their nightclothes, carrying lanterns they didn't need, because for the first time since the sky went black there was something up there worth seeing by. A single point of light hung above the mountain's shoulder—small, steady, warm as a coal cupped in two hands.

"Is that her?" asked a voice near Wendell's elbow. A baker's boy, flour still on his wrists, staring upward with his mouth open.

"It's someone who chose to stay," Wendell said. "And someone who chose to shine."

She felt Yara shift beside her, and when she glanced down she saw her daughter—no, not daughter, Wendell corrected herself; *chosen*, which was harder and better—was crying without any noise at all, tears sliding down her face while her chin stayed level, the way she'd learned to cry from watching Wendell do exactly that for sixteen years.

"She'd have hated this," Yara said finally. "All these people being sentimental about her."

"She'd have loved it," Wendell said. "She just would have made us all pretend otherwise."

The crowd in the square was larger than Wendell could count. She'd lived in Grayhollow long enough to know its silences—the way people moved through the night market with their hoods up and their voices down, the way neighbors watched neighbors the way one watches a lit fuse. That silence was gone tonight. Strangers were talking to strangers. An old woman was telling anyone who would listen that she'd seen the stars go out one by one from her rooftop, and now she'd seen one come back, and she intended to see them all return if it took the rest of her life. Nobody told her to be quiet. Nobody looked over their shoulder for gray cloaks.

There were no gray cloaks anymore. Serah Vane had seen to that, at the cost of everything she'd been.

* * *

Serah watched the star from the steps of the council hall, where three weeks ago she had stood in full regalia and read out her own testimony until her voice cracked and the chamber went so quiet she could hear the candles burning.

They had stripped her rank that same day. They had also, to her lasting surprise, believed her. The ledgers she'd carried out of the Inquisition's archive—the brokerage records, the names of the agents who had coerced a dying woman on the mountain road into selling her child's soul for safe passage—had done what Serah's faith never could: they had made the rot undeniable. Three arrests. Two suicides before arrest. One trial still grinding forward like a millstone through wet grain. And an edict under review, the old one, the burning one, with words like *repeal* attached to it now in official ink, as though magic were a law's to permit or forbid rather than a fact of the world like rain.

Serah wore no uniform tonight. Just a wool coat and her mother's scarf, and the strange lightness of a woman who had set down something enormous and did not yet know how to walk without its weight.

"You're staring at the sky like it owes you money."

Brannoc lowered himself onto the step beside her with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. He held two cups of mulled wine, offered one, and drank the other like it was penance.

"I'm staring at the sky," Serah said, "like I've never been allowed to before. Do you know I spent nineteen years in that order and I can count the nights I looked up on purpose? We were trained to watch the ground. The ground is where the crimes are." She turned the cup in her hands. "Marisol should be here for this."

"She is," Brannoc said. "That's the entire point of the exercise." He nodded upward. "Pip says the freed souls are finding their way home. Says he can hear them. Says it sounds like—" he paused, hunting for the thief's phrasing— "like locks opening. Thousands of them. Very far away, very patient."

"Do you believe him?"

"Pip once heard a lock through four feet of granite and a lie," Brannoc said. "I believe him about anything now."

Below them, in the square, the crowd had begun to sing. Badly, and in three different keys, an old harvest song that everyone knew and no one had dared sing aloud in years, because singing drew attention and attention drew the Inquisition. Serah found she could not join in. Her throat had closed around something older than the song. But Brannoc sang, low and rough and slightly flat, and after a verse she leaned against his shoulder and let his voice carry hers the way a wall carries a climbing rose.

* * *

Pip heard the second star before anyone saw it.

She was up on the roof of the Waystation—which was no longer a smugglers' vault but something the neighborhood had started calling *the counting house*, because Pip kept the ledger of returned souls there, scratched in chalk on slate, nameless but numbered—and she was halfway through her nightly listening when the sound changed.

It had been like this for weeks: a vast slow music underneath the world, the sound of souls unspooling from the Ember Court's broken engine and drifting toward whatever they had been before they were taken. Some of them she could almost resolve into voices. One hummed like a spinning wheel. One wept in a language she didn't know. One, every night, sang three bars of a lullaby and then laughed, as if it had forgotten the rest and found this funny.

Tonight, beneath all of it, came a new note. Brighter. Rising.

Pip sat bolt upright. "Oh," she said. "Oh, you're going to want to see this."

By the time she'd scrambled down the ladder and pounded on doors, half the street was already in the lane, because the sky itself had announced it—a flicker above the eastern hills, like a struck match. Then steadiness. Then light.

A second star.

The lane erupted. Not cheering, not at first—it was more like the sound a congregation makes when the miracle actually happens, a great collective intake of breath, and then the noise broke loose all at once and Pip was lifted off her feet by a fishmonger twice her size who was shouting her name, *her* name, though she'd never told him it and he surely knew better than to ask how she knew what she knew.

"Third one!" she yelled over the din, pointing east. "Give it a minute, there's a third coming—I can hear it warming up!"

She was right. It took eleven minutes. Eleven minutes during which nobody in that lane went back inside, during which doors stood open all down the street, during which a woman brought out her dead husband's fiddle and played the harvest song and got it right, unlike the square across town. When the third star kindled—farther off, over the southern wards' bones, hanging above country that had emptied itself of the living—Pip felt something crack open in her chest that she suspected would never close again.

Because she could hear them arriving. Each new light was a soul finding its door. And the chalk numbers on her slate inside, the careful tally she kept, suddenly seemed absurd—counting them like inventory, like stolen goods recovered. They weren't goods. They were *people*, going home, and the sky was filling up with porch-lights.

Pip sat down hard on the cold cobblestones, right there in the middle of the celebrating lane, and cried until a stranger put a blanket around her shoulders and a mug of something hot in her hands, and nobody asked her to explain, because tonight nobody in Grayhollow needed explanations. Tonight the whole city spoke fluent wonder.

* * *

Yara climbed the mountain alone at dawn.

Not far. Just to the first shelf of rock where the seal glowed in the mountainside—a seam of ember-light running through stone like a vein, steady and warm, humming the note Marisol had taught them was the sound of a kept promise. Yara pressed her palm against it. Warm as bread. Warm as the locket had ever been.

"I brought you the tally," she said, and unfolded Pip's copied page. "Forty-one stars back as of last night. Forty-two counting yours. Pip says the rate is increasing—that the farther ones are moving faster now, like they know the way is open. Like word travels among them." She smiled. "Like they're telling each other it's safe to come home."

The seal pulsed, very gently. It always did that, when Yara talked to it. Wendell said she imagined things. Yara had stopped needing Wendell to agree with her about what was real.

"I'm taking my mother's name today," Yara told the stone. "Serevan. Yara Serevan. Wendell found it in the contract—her true name, the one the court used to bind me. I figure if they wanted it erased, that's exactly why I should keep it." She traced the seam of light with one finger. "You'd have liked her, I think. Stubborn. Bad at lying. The kind of person who sells everything she has to buy her kid a future and then haunts the paperwork about it forever."

She sat with the seal until the sun cleared the ridge and the ember-light drowned in daylight, and then she went back down the mountain, because there was a ceremony at noon, and a city waiting, and a sword in her hand that she was only beginning to learn the language of.

* * *

Noon found the market square fuller than it had been for any festival in living memory.

Wendell stood at the fountain's edge with Yara and watched Grayhollow gather. The charm-sellers were out—not hiding now, not hooded, stalls open in broad daylight with little wards and blessings and luck-knots displayed like ordinary wares, because the council had suspended the edict pending repeal and the city had decided, in the particular way of cities, that suspension meant permission and permission meant celebration. A hedge-witch Wendell didn't know was selling weather-charms next to a man selling pies, and neither thought it strange.

"They're afraid anyway," Yara murmured. "Look. Some of them. The charm-sellers keep checking the crowd."

"Of course they are. Seven years teaches a body habits the body doesn't unlearn in seven weeks." Wendell squeezed her hand. "Courage isn't the absence of the flinch. It's setting up your stall anyway."

"Did you make that up just now?"

"I've been workshopping it since breakfast."

Then the crowd hushed, because Brannoc had mounted the fountain's rim, and beside him—bareheaded, in plain clothes, her gray cloak left folded somewhere she'd never retrieve it—stood Serah Vane, holding a scroll.

Serah read the council's proclamation in a clear voice: the edict repealed, the Inquisition dissolved pending reconstitution under civil oversight, a general amnesty for unregistered practitioners, and—this line she read slowly, and Wendell saw her hand shake on it—a public record entered naming the victims of the Purge, all of them, the list taking the better part of an hour, name after name after name, while the city stood in the cold and listened and did not look away.

When it was done, Serah folded the scroll, stepped down from the fountain, and walked to the edge of the crowd, where she stopped as if unsure whether she was included in the city she had just helped unchain.

Wendell crossed the square to her.

"For the record," Wendell said, "the last time you came to my door, I lied to you badly."

"The last time I came to your door," Serah said, "I threatened you with irons and then didn't use them. Between the two of us, I think my sins ran heavier."

"We could argue about it for the rest of our lives."

"We could," Serah agreed. Something loosened in her face—not forgiveness exactly, nothing so tidy, but the settling of two old debts into terms both parties could live with. "Marisol would say we're being maudlin."

"Marisol would say worse, and then she'd pour the drinks."

They stood together as the sun went down.

* * *

The sky did it again at dusk.

Wendell saw it first—or rather, she saw the city see it, six thousand faces turning eastward in the same instant like iron filings answering a magnet, and then she looked, and there it was: the fourth star, then a fifth before the fourth had finished brightening, two points of light kindling within a minute of each other, and somewhere beneath her feet Pip was surely hearing the locks turn.

The crowd did not cheer this time. What it did was quieter and stranger and it caught in Wendell's throat like a fishbone: the whole city, together, went silent. A silence with thousands of people in it, held deliberately, the way you hold your breath passing a sleeping child. A silence of witness. Then someone began the harvest song again, and this time the whole square took it up, all keys at once, gloriously wrong, and Wendell found Yara's shoulder against hers and Serah on her other side and Brannoc bellowing the chorus like a man exorcising something, and above them all the stars were coming home one by one like travelers cresting a hill at the end of a very long road.

Yara tilted her head back. The new starlight caught her face, and Wendell saw her mother in it—the dying woman on the mountain road, whom Wendell had known only in fragments and guilt—and saw too the girl Yara was becoming, which was neither her mother nor Wendell nor the payment the court had named her, but someone entirely new, forged in the gap between all those things.

"How many are left?" Yara asked.

"Hundreds," Wendell said. "Maybe thousands. Pip says some will take years. Some may never find their way—they were taken too long ago, too deep." She paused. "But the sky doesn't have to be full by tomorrow. It just has to be filling."

Yara was quiet a moment. Then she said, in the tone she used when she'd been turning something over all day and had finally decided to set it down:

"When the last one comes home—whenever that is—I want to be on this mountain. At the seal. I want Marisol to see the whole sky."

"She'll see it from wherever she is."

"I know. But I want to be standing next to her when she does." Yara looked at her, and the starlight was in her eyes now, and she was smiling. "You'll come with me?"

"Every time," Wendell said. "For as many years as it takes. That's the whole job, as far as I can tell. You show up. You stay. You keep choosing it."

Below the mountain, Grayhollow burned its lamps in answer to the returning stars, window by window, street by street, a city lighting itself from the inside out—and high on the slope, the seal glowed steady and warm, keeping its promise, keeping its keeper, while overhead a sixth star kindled, and then, before the song had ended, a seventh.

But it was the eighth light that made Wendell stop singing.

Because it didn't rise in the east, or the south, or anywhere over the darkened borderlands where the lost souls wandered. It bloomed directly overhead—low, close, impossibly bright—and it hung there, pulsing, in a rhythm Wendell knew.

She had seen that rhythm once before. In a candle flame, cupped in two hands, carried up a mountainside by a woman who had already given everything and called it settling accounts.

Except Marisol's star had been shining for weeks. This was a different light. Newer. And it was falling—not drifting home like the others, but descending, deliberate, trailing embers, straight toward the market square, toward the fountain, toward the space directly in front of Wendell and Yara where the crowd was already stumbling backward, already screaming, already parting like water around a stone—

Yara's hand found her sword hilt. Wendell's found hers.

The light touched down in the center of the square, and the singing stopped, and the whole city held its breath to see what had chosen to come home tonight—and to whom.
