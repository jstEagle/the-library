## 39. An Oath Renewed

The morning of the swearing, Wendell Brightwater woke before dawn and could not go back to sleep.

She lay in her narrow bed above the charm shop—her shop now, though she still thought of it as the old woman's, the one whose lease she'd inherited through a chain of quiet favors seven years ago—and listened to Grayhollow wake around her. Cart wheels on wet cobbles. The bakery boy's whistle, off-key and cheerful. Somewhere a gull arguing with the chimney pots about ownership of a fish head.

Ordinary sounds. A year ago they would have been the sounds of a city that would burn her for what she was. Now they were just sounds, and she lay beneath them like a swimmer floating in safe water, still not quite believing it held her.

Yara was asleep down the hall. She'd moved back in after the mountain—not all at once, not as if nothing had happened, but in increments. First a night. Then a week. Then her things had migrated back shelf by shelf, cloak by cloak, until one evening Wendell had come upstairs to find both beds occupied by their owners and neither of them remarking on it, because some repairs were better left unannounced while the mortar cured.

Wendell rose, dressed in the dark, and went downstairs to do the thing she had been avoiding for three weeks.

The charter paper sat on her workbench where she'd left it. Serah had brought it—a genuine document, council-sealed, offering to restore the Order of the Swordspeakers under a new name, with Wendell as Grandmaster, with funding, with a hall in the old garrison district and authority to train and license mages throughout the city's jurisdiction. It was everything the old order had been. It was everything she had lost.

She had read it eleven times. Each time, her eye caught on the same word: *authority*.

She picked up the charter now, held it over the candle she kept burning on the bench—the first flame she'd ever lit openly in this room—and almost did it. Almost fed seven years of grief to the fire and let the paper be the funeral the old order never got.

Instead she set it down, unburned, and took out a fresh sheet of plain paper instead.

*No order,* she wrote at the top, then crossed it out, because even that sounded too grand. *No anything,* she tried. That was worse. She sat there until the candle guttered and dawn came gray through the shutters, and what she finally wrote filled less than half a page:

*There will be no hall. There will be no rank. There will be no one but me, and whoever chooses to learn from me, and whatever we owe each other. If you are looking for a banner to follow, look elsewhere. If you are looking for someone who will tell you the truth even when it costs—that is the whole of what I can offer, and I have learned it late.*

She signed it. Then, because the old habit of secrecy died hard, she almost folded it away into a drawer.

Almost.

* * *

The market square had changed since the night she'd lit a charm in front of everyone and waited for the mob.

Not dramatically. Cities rarely changed dramatically; they changed the way wards were now built—one small consent at a time. But there were differences, if you knew where to look. The Inquisition's old checkpoint booth stood empty, its boards pried up for kindling by enterprising thieves who had somehow never been caught. A chalkboard outside the baker's listed the day's bread prices next to a child's drawing of the new star—Marisol's star, steady and faintly amber, which the whole city had taken to calling the Baker's Star because it rose highest over the bakery district, an accident of geography that Marisol, wherever she burned now, would have found hilarious.

And in the corner near the well, where the charm sellers had always clustered, there were three of them now instead of one. An old man selling knotwork against nightmares. A young woman whose charms were honestly terrible but whose prices were honest too. And between them, space—the kind of deliberate, unspoken space that said *this row is open*, the way a table leaves a chair out for someone expected.

Word had gotten around. Of course it had. Pip heard everything, and Pip told Brannoc, and Brannoc told anyone who bought him a drink, which was a great many people.

So when Wendell walked into the square at midmorning with a sheet of paper in her hand and Yara at her shoulder, she was not surprised to find a crowd waiting. Two hundred people, maybe more. Shopkeepers with flour still on their aprons. Dockworkers off shift. Serah Vane standing at the edge in plain clothes, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman attending a funeral and a christening simultaneously and refusing to say which. Brannoc looming behind her like a hill that had learned to stand upright. Pip perched on the well's rim, swinging her legs, grinning like the cat who had personally arranged the cream.

"I hate crowds," Wendell muttered.

"You love crowds," Yara said. "You just love them quietly."

"That's hating them."

"Mother." Yara used the word deliberately—she'd been using it more since the mountain, testing its weight, finding it held—and something in Wendell's chest turned over the way it always did. "You've faced down an Ember Queen. This is Grayhollow. Half these people buy sleeping charms from you."

"The other half?"

"Are here to see if you'll actually do it. Pip took bets."

"Pip—" Wendell began.

"Took bets," Pip confirmed cheerfully from the well. "Odds were against until Tuesday. You're welcome for the campaign."

Wendell looked at the crowd. At the faces. Some hopeful, some skeptical, a few openly hostile—an old woman near the fountain whose family, Wendell happened to know, had lost kin in the Purge, and who had every right to be there and every right to her anger. Wendell met her eyes and did not look away, and after a long moment the old woman gave a single nod: *go on, then. Show us.*

Wendell stepped up onto the low stone lip of the well. Her boots scraped. The crowd hushed the way crowds do—not silent, but leaning.

"I'm going to disappoint most of you," she said, and a ripple of laughter ran through the square, loosening it. "I know why you're here. Word's gone round that the last swordspeaker means to re-found the Order. That there'll be a hall, and ranks, and oaths sworn on iron, and a place for your children to learn the old ways properly."

She held up the council's charter.

"There's a document that says exactly that. Signed, sealed, funded. I could read it aloud and half of you would cheer and the other half would remember what the last order became when it had halls and ranks and iron to swear on." She let that sit. "I was the last of them. I watched what we turned into when we decided our oath was a possession instead of a promise. We hoarded it. We policed it. And when the law came for us, we discovered we had spent so many years guarding the magic that we'd forgotten how to guard each other."

In the crowd, Serah's jaw tightened. Wendell caught it, and gentled her voice—not much, but enough.

"So no. No order. No hall. No charter." She folded the paper once, twice, and held it out toward the crowd without ceremony. "Serah. You brought this. You can decide what happens to it."

Serah came forward through the parting crowd, took the charter, and looked at it for a long moment. Then she walked to the bakery's wood-fired oven door, opened it, and fed the entire future of the Order of the Swordspeakers to the morning's bread.

The smell of burning vellum mixed with baking loaves. Someone laughed. The old woman by the fountain laughed hardest.

"What remains," Wendell said, when the noise settled, "is smaller than any of you wanted, and harder than all of it put together. One teacher. One apprentice. One sentence, spoken aloud, in front of witnesses—which is more than I managed for seven years, so forgive me if my voice shakes."

She turned to Yara.

Her daughter stood in the square where she'd been sold before she could speak, in the city that had outlawed the magic in her blood, wearing her mother's surname like a coat she'd cut to fit herself. Sixteen years old and already twice as brave as Wendell had ever been at any age, because Yara had never learned to hide.

"Wendell Brightwater," Yara said formally, because they had agreed on the form the night before, sitting at the kitchen table over cold tea, "I ask to learn."

"And what will you do with what you learn?" Wendell asked, because that question mattered more than any other, and Yara knew it, and answered it looking directly at the crowd rather than at her.

"Whatever needs doing. Whether or not it's lawful. Whether or not it's safe. And I'll tell the truth about it after—even the parts that make me look bad." A pause, and the ghost of a grin. "*Especially* those parts."

"Then hear my oath," Wendell said, "and witness it, all of you, because I mean it to be witnessed."

She had written forty drafts. She had discarded charters' worth of language, the old order's liturgy, the formal invocations her master had taught her, syllable by iron syllable. All of it had been armor, and armor was what had failed her. What remained was one sentence, plain as bread.

She drew breath. The square was absolutely silent now. Above the rooftops, pale in the daylight, Marisol's star hung visible even at noon—a small amber point that the scholars said should not exist and the bakers said rose right on time.

"My oath is this," Wendell said. "I will tell the truth, protect the unprotected, and keep choosing the people I love—even when it costs me, even when it shames me, even when silence would be easier—for as long as I draw breath, and I swear it in my own name, needing no other."

Iron did not sing. No sword leapt from its scabbard. No wind rose, no ward flared, no ancient power descended to stamp the moment with grandeur.

But every charm-seller in that square felt it pass through them like warmth through cold hands—the old man with his knotwork, the girl with her terrible charms, and Wendell herself, who felt her own words settle into her bones not like iron at all but like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was locked. Small magic. Human magic. The kind the law had never managed to outlaw because it had never recognized it as magic in the first place.

It was only loyalty, spoken aloud. It had only ever been loyalty, spoken aloud.

The crowd exhaled all at once, and the sound was like the tide.

* * *

Afterward there was bread—Serah distributed loaves from the bakery with the gravity of a woman distributing medals—and there was cider, and Pip collected her winnings from roughly a dozen disgruntled bettors with the efficiency of a tax collector. Brannoc said perhaps nine words total, all of them to Yara, all of them advice about stance, and Yara listened the way she never listened to anyone, because Brannoc's advice was always paid for in advance by things he didn't talk about.

By late afternoon the square had emptied, and Wendell found herself standing by the well with Serah, watching Yara and Pip attempt to teach each other lock-hearing and swordspeak simultaneously, with results that were loud and largely educational.

"You could have had the hall," Serah said. "The funding. You'd have been good at it."

"I'd have been good at building the thing that gets people burned," Wendell said. "I've seen how that goes. From inside."

Serah was quiet a moment. "I testified again last week. Third time. The council's dragging the investigations out—they'll bury half of it, you know. Institutions protect themselves. It's what they're for."

"I know."

"They offered me a position. Reformed Inquisition. Oversight." She smiled without humor. "I told them I'd found better work."

"Which is?"

"Watching you," Serah said. "Someone has to make sure the woman who wouldn't take power doesn't need taking down someday. Consider it my own one-sentence oath. I haven't finished drafting it."

They stood together in the settling dusk, two women who had spent years on opposite sides of a law neither believed in anymore, and found that the silence between them had finally stopped being a wall and become simply a silence—comfortable, unhurried, the silence of people who had said the worst things to each other already and survived it.

"Yara's good," Serah said at last. "Better than you were at sixteen. Worse liar, obviously."

"Obviously."

"She'll outgrow you within five years."

"I'm counting on it."

* * *

That night, Wendell climbed the stairs to the shop's little attic room, where Yara sat cross-legged on the floor with the charmed locket open in her palm. It no longer showed fragments—no mountain gates, no voices like coals. Since the contract burned, it showed only what lockets show: light, and the memory of light.

"Nervous?" Yara asked, without looking up.

"Terrified."

"Good. Brannoc says teachers who aren't terrified are dangerous." She closed the locket and finally looked up, and her face in the lamplight was sixteen years old and older than mountains, both at once. "First lesson tomorrow?"

"Dawn. The square. Bring the practice blades."

"Real ones?" Yara's eyes lit. "You said—"

"Practice blades," Wendell said firmly. "For a year at least. Iron answers to intent, not enthusiasm, and your intent currently involves showing off for Pip."

Yara grinned and didn't deny it, and for a while they sat together in the lamplight, saying nothing, needing nothing, the silence between them no longer a wound but a room they both lived in.

Then Yara frowned slightly, tilting her head toward the window the way she did when something snagged at the edge of her hearing—she'd been developing her mother's senses faster than either of them liked.

"Do you feel that?" she asked quietly. "From the mountain. Like... a warmth. Steady. Almost like it's—"

"Calling?" Wendell said.

Yara nodded slowly.

Wendell rose and pushed open the shutter. Far to the north, above the black shoulder of the mountain, Marisol's star burned steady and amber—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a second glow had begun to kindle along the mountainside itself, low on the slope, warm as banked coals, pulsing gently. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

Like a seal keeping something asleep—or something else entirely, waking kindly, knocking at the door of the world.

"Tomorrow," Wendell said softly, watching it pulse. "Dawn. Lessons first."

But she did not close the shutter, and neither of them slept well, and in the morning they would pack for the mountain.
