## 40. Emberlight

The mountain path was kinder than it had any right to be.

Wendell had expected scree and switchbacks, the old road's broken ward-stones jutting like teeth. Instead she found the trail swept clean—by pilgrims, Brannoc said, who had been coming up from Grayhollow since the second star kindled, carrying candles and small offerings and, in one memorable case, an entire roast goose. The city that had burned mages for a century now climbed its mountain to sit near a warm light and say nothing at all.

Yara walked ahead of her on the slope, and Wendell let herself do nothing but watch. Sixteen years of watching sideways, through shutters and shop-windows and the corners of her eyes, always careful not to be caught looking too long at the girl who was never truly hers. Now she could look openly. It still felt like theft. She was learning that some habits outlive their reasons by years.

"You're doing the thing," Yara called back without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The staring thing. Like I'm going to evaporate."

"Old habit," Wendell said. "Hard to break."

"Break it." Yara stopped on the path and waited until Wendell drew level with her. Her hair was tied back with the blue ribbon Pip had stolen for her sixteenth birthday—the only gift he'd ever stolen honestly meant—and in the morning light coming up over the valley she looked, for the thousandth time, like nobody's payment and nobody's foundling. Just herself. "You're allowed to look at me now. That's what all of this bought."

*All of this.* A dead queen. A collapsed engine. Marisol, folded into amber light forever on the slope above them. Wendell nodded and said none of that, because they had agreed—one night over Yara's birthday cake, in the kitchen that still smelled of cinnamon—that grief would be spoken when it needed speaking and carried quietly when it didn't, and today, so far, it didn't.

They climbed.

The seal came into view just past the last stand of rowan trees, where Brannoc had buried his brother's candle and refused, ever after, to explain why rowan specifically. The ground there rose in a low swell of stone, smooth as if something vast lay sleeping beneath a blanket of earth, and across its whole surface glowed the emberlight: deep amber, steady, pulsing in slow rhythm. Not firelight. Nothing about it flickered or fed or consumed. It simply *was*, the way a heartbeat is—noticed only when it stops.

Wendell felt it before she saw it, the way she always had. Seven years of hiding her magic had sharpened her senses into strange instruments, and the seal sang to them now—a low, patient note, like a held breath that never ran out.

"She's warm," Yara said softly. She knelt at the edge of the glow and pressed her palm flat against the stone. "She's always warm. Every time."

"That's how you know it's her," Wendell said, kneeling beside her. "Brannoc checked the ward-lattices twice. Says the seal shouldn't radiate anything. Says it's inefficient, undisciplined, structurally sentimental." She smiled at the stone. "Says it's Marisol all over."

They sat in the quiet together. Below them, Grayhollow spilled down the valley in its thousand lights—lamplight and hearthlight and, since spring, the strange new glow of charm-sellers' stalls lit openly in the market square, no shutters drawn, no oaths swallowed. The city looked smaller from here than it had ever felt. All those years Wendell had thought of Grayhollow as a cage built of watching eyes; from the mountain it looked like what it had also always been: a place where people got up in the dark and made bread.

Above the city, the sky.

Four stars now. Marisol's, amber and constant. Then the second—a thin white point that had kindled over the harvest moon, which Pip swore up and down was the soul of a locksmith he'd once robbed, and whom he apologized to nightly with theatrical sincerity. Then two more, fainter, newer, kindled within days of each other while the whole city stood in the streets and watched.

Not all the stars were coming back. They all knew that. Some souls had gone into the wards' bones, some into the deep, some into places even Serah's recovered ledgers couldn't map. But four stars hung over Grayhollow where for seven months there had been none, and every night more people stood outside to look up instead of locking their doors against the sky.

"Wendell." Yara's voice had changed. Wendell turned. Yara was still touching the stone, but her face had gone very still, the way it did when the locket hummed—though the locket hung silent at her throat, its work done, a keepsake now rather than a key. "She's saying something."

"The seal?"

"No. *Marisol.* Underneath it." Yara frowned, pressing harder, listening with whatever new sense the court had left in her like a splinter of coal. "It's not words exactly. More like—" She struggled. "Like the way she used to hum when she was brewing. You knew the song but you also knew it was *her* singing it."

Wendell laid her own hand on the stone. She felt only warmth, only patience—but then, Marisol had always spoken most clearly to the ones she'd loved without announcing it. The hedge-witch had carried her death-candle for eleven years and told no one, had taken the seal as *settling accounts*, and had spent her last hour in the dark teaching Pip a bawdy song so he wouldn't hear her afraid.

"She's not trapped," Wendell said slowly, understanding arriving like dawn. "That's what you're hearing. She's not suffering. She's *settled*."

"Yeah." Yara exhaled. "Yeah. That's it." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, and laughed a little. "Gods. She'd be insufferable about it. 'I told you I was good at candle-magic.'"

"She'd have charged us for the reading."

"Double rates. Family discount revoked."

They laughed together, and the laugh broke something open in Wendell's chest that had been sealed longer than any ward—the specific grief of missing someone funny, which no solemnity can touch. Beneath their hands the emberlight pulsed once, slightly off-rhythm, and Wendell would have sworn on her rebuilt oath that it was the seal equivalent of snorting.

* *

They stayed through the afternoon. Yara talked to the stone—actual conversation, one-sided but earnest, updates on the market stall, on Serah's exile cottage down in the borderlands, on Brannoc's ongoing war with the ward-guild's committee structure. Wendell listened half of it and watched the valley half of it, and somewhere between the two she drifted into the particular peace she had spent fifty-three years believing was a story sold to children.

Loyalty and truth, hand in hand. She kept turning the phrase over because it had cost so much to learn. For seven years she had believed the two were enemies—that to keep Yara safe she must lie, must hide, must fold her love up small enough to fit inside silence. And the silence had nearly eaten them both. What saved them hadn't been the sword, in the end. It hadn't even been the oath, though iron had answered it and she would feel that answering in her arm for the rest of her life. What saved them was a girl standing in a burning hall saying *I am no one's payment*, and an inquisitor laying her evidence on the coals and saying *this is true, and I will sign my name to it*, and a thief reading fine print aloud, and a smith naming his brother, and a witch walking into the dark on purpose because someone honest had finally told her the truth of what she carried.

Every one of them had spoken. Every one had paid. And the paying had not been fair, and the speaking had not been safe, and yet here was the light, steady and warm, proving that the ledger balanced differently than fear had promised.

"Dawn lessons," Yara said, as the sun began to slide. "You promised."

"I promised lessons at dawn. It's nearly dusk."

"You promised *dawn*. Yesterday's dawn. Which we missed packing. So you owe me interest." Yara stood, brushing off her knees, and there was the fierce grin Wendell had loved since the girl was six years old and had stolen Wendell's practice blade to fight the neighbor boys who mocked her accent. "Unless swordspeakers don't honor debts. I could check with the Ember Court's lawyers. They seemed thorough."

"Cruel," Wendell said, rising, her knees protesting. "Cruelty, from my own apprentice."

"Not your apprentice yet." Yara's grin softened into something else. "Tomorrow, right? In the square? You're really—you're sure? No order, no charter. Just us."

"Just us," Wendell said. "One sentence. Out loud. Where everyone can hear."

"And if they burn us for it?"

"Then we'll at least go up warm," Wendell said, and touched the seal in farewell, and started down the mountain with Yara laughing beside her.

* *

Yara could not sleep, so she went up instead.

It was full dark now, the fourth star newly risen, and the mountain path by night was nothing to fear anymore—not with emberlight spilling down the slope like a lantern left in every window of the world. She moved easily between the stones, cloak pulled tight, and settled cross-legged at the seal's edge where the warmth reached her face like an open oven door.

Below, Grayhollow glowed. Above, four points of amber and white. Between them, her whole strange life: sold before speech, bought back by a liar who loved her, claimed by a court that wanted her as currency, reclaimed by her own voice in front of everyone.

She thought about names. Tomorrow, after the oath, she would file the papers—she and Serah had drafted them together, in the careful legal language the older woman wielded like a scalpel. *Yara Vessin.* Her mother's surname, dug out of a coerced contract and scrubbed of its shame. Not Brightwater. Not *the payment*. Hers. Chosen the way an inheritance should be chosen: examined, understood, accepted with open eyes.

Her mother had sold her. And her mother had been forced to sell her, coerced by agents of a law that ate its own people, and the difference between those two sentences had turned out to be the difference between drowning and breathing. Yara had spent months furious at a dead woman before Serah put the records in her hands. She wasn't finished grieving—maybe never would be—but the fury had cooled into something she could carry: a promise that no one else would be sold where she could see it, hear it, speak against it.

"You'd have liked her," she told the seal. "My mother. Wendell says I get the stubbornness from her." The emberlight pulsed, warm against her cheek. "Don't encourage me. That's how I ended up with a sword."

Somewhere down the slope, a door creaked—Wendell, following her, the way she always did now, openly, no more pretending not to watch. Yara smiled and didn't turn around.

"She knows you're out here," Wendell said, arriving, settling beside her with the groan of a woman whose joints had dueled a queen. "She sent me up because apparently I'm the only one who doesn't pretend I'm not worried."

"Liar. You were already dressed."

"I sleep in boots. Occupational habit." Wendell was quiet a moment, looking at the seal, then past it, down at the city, up at the stars. "Penny for them."

"They're not worth a penny. I was just telling Marisol about tomorrow." Yara leaned her head briefly against Wendell's shoulder—an allowance granted rarely and never mentioned, worth more than the contract had priced her at. "One sentence, you said. What sentence?"

Wendell was quiet long enough that Yara lifted her head to look at her. In the emberlight, her guardian's face—all hard planes and old scars—looked almost young.

"Haven't written it yet," Wendell admitted. "Forty years in the old order and the oath was three pages long. Latin and steel-terms and lineage-names. Rusted shut in seven years because it was never mine." She flexed her right hand, the one iron had answered. "This one has to be mine. So I'm still listening for it."

"It'll come," Yara said. "Everything true does. Usually at inconvenient moments."

"That," Wendell said gravely, "is wisdom. Clearly you get it from me."

"Clearly."

They sat until the cold came up and the warmth of the seal drove it back, and below them Grayhollow's lights answered the returning stars the way they did every night now—window by window going dark as people stepped outside instead, faces tilted upward, unafraid. Somewhere in that glow, Pip was surely teaching city children to pick sweets out of locked jars ("educational demonstration," he'd insist). Somewhere, Brannoc sat under his rowan tree with a cup of tea poured for a brother who no longer needed it but deserved the courtesy. Somewhere in a borderland cottage, Serah wrote testimony by lamplight, having chosen exile the way other people choose honor.

And here, on the slope, a seal breathed amber and steady, keeping the hungry dark asleep, glowing not like a wound but like a hearth—which, Yara thought, was exactly what Marisol would have wanted: to be remembered as warm.

"Come on," Wendell said at last, rising, pulling Yara up by the wrist. "Dawn comes early. And I've finally got my sentence."

Yara stopped. "You do? When—what is it?"

Wendell smiled, and for once didn't deflect, didn't wrap the truth in merchant's packaging. She simply said, "You'll hear it with everyone else. That's rather the point."

They walked down the mountain together, side by side, two small figures descending through emberlight toward a city of answering lamps—and far above them, faint and new, at the very edge of the sky where no star had burned in a year, a fifth point of light trembled into being, amber-white and stubborn, like a candle refusing the wind.

Yara saw it first. She stopped dead on the path, gripping Wendell's arm.

"Wendell. Look."

High above Grayhollow, the fifth star brightened—and pulsed, once, gently, in rhythm with the seal behind them.

Like breathing. Like laughter. Like someone, somewhere on the long road home, had heard her name spoken aloud—and was following it back.
