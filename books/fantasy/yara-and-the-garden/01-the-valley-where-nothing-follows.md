## 1. The Valley Where Nothing Follows

The Godless Valley earned its name honestly. Paloma Crane had known that since she was six years old, the first time she'd stood at its mouth and felt something in her chest go quiet — not peace, exactly, but a kind of absence with weight to it, like a room where a clock had been removed and no one could say what was missing. Magic did not follow past the valley's rim. Neither did gods. Whatever else lived in the world beyond — the bright, terrible, singing world of her company's oldest stories — it stopped at the ridgeline as if at the edge of a held breath.

Inside, there was only weather. Only stone and scrub pine, the gray-green river, and the long road that ran through the valley floor like a scar that had never quite healed.

Paloma rode point on the escort column and counted wagons for the fourth time since dawn. Nine. Salt, wool, iron ingots, and one merchant's strongbox whose contents she'd been paid not to wonder about. Twelve hired blades from the Crane Company — her company, though *hers* was a generous word for it — spread along the line at intervals drilled into them so often they no longer needed to think about it. That was the trouble with the valley, she thought. Nothing here required thinking. A person could do this work asleep, and some days she suspected half of them did.

"Crane." Dagny drew up beside her, squinting east where the sun was doing its best through a lid of cloud. "Merchant's asking how long to the far pass."

"Tell him two days if the ford's low, three if it isn't."

"He'll ask why we can't know which."

"Then tell him the river doesn't file reports." She kept her eyes on the treeline. It wasn't a joke, exactly, but Dagny laughed anyway — a short bark, the way she laughed at everything Paloma said that wasn't strictly an order. Twenty years old, quick with a knife, born inside the valley like every soldier under forty in the company. She had never seen anything that magic couldn't touch. She had never wanted to.

Paloma had been born on the other side of the ridge. Barely. Her mother had gone into labor during the last crossing out of the wide world, and Paloma had drawn her first breath eleven miles shy of safety, close enough that her grandmother used to swear — laughing, always laughing when she said it — that a little of the world had gotten into her with the air. *That's why you pray, little bird,* her grandmother would say. *The rest of them pray out of habit. You pray because something in you remembers.*

She didn't pray anymore. Not aloud, not where anyone could see. But she still did it, alone, in the hour before dawn, kneeling on cold ground with her palms open the way the old rite demanded, speaking to a god who had been silent for a hundred years. The company called itself godsworn still. They wore the sigil — a closed eye stitched in gray thread — and spoke the words at funerals and oaths and the turning of the year, but nobody living had heard so much as a whisper back. The silence predated everyone's grandparents. It was simply a fact of the valley, like the altitude or the short summers: their god did not reach this place, and so their god did not answer, and so the prayers had curdled over generations into something closer to etiquette than faith.

Only Paloma still bothered. She'd never told anyone why. Partly because she couldn't have explained it without sounding mad, and partly because the explanation would have led, eventually, to her grandmother, and her grandmother had left instructions the way other people left curses: *Don't ask, little bird. Don't dig. Some doors are locked from the loving side.*

The column wound down toward the river through a stand of bare birches, and Paloma let the thought drop the way she always did, like a stone into deep water, watching it fall until it vanished.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a granite shelf, and the routine unfolded with the smoothness of something performed ten thousand times. Fires in a shallow pit, screened with cut brush. Watch rotations posted before anyone sat down to eat. The merchants' drivers grumbling about hard ground; the soldiers ignoring them with professional serenity. Paloma walked the perimeter once, then again, finding nothing but rabbit sign and the usual evening wind coming down off the peaks carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet.

At the second fire, she found Old Merrit holding court, which meant someone had asked him about the Leaving.

It was the company's founding story, worn smooth as a river stone from a century of retelling. How their god had spoken plainly to the founders, once — had walked with them, some versions said, had fought beside them in others, had wept over them in the oldest and strangest tellings. And then, a hundred years ago, the whole company had crossed into this valley and shut the door behind them, and the voice had stopped at the ridgeline like a man forbidden to follow.

"Why'd they come in, if it meant losing him?" asked Joss, the youngest of the recruits, all elbows and questions.

"To be safe," Merrit said, in the tone of a man reciting scripture he didn't believe but had memorized anyway. "Magic can't follow in here. Curses can't. Witches can't. Gods—" he shrugged, "—a god is just the biggest kind of magic. The valley doesn't pick favorites."

"But the god was *theirs*. Wouldn't he have protected them outside?"

"Protection costs," said a dry voice, and Paloma realized too late that she'd spoken without meaning to. Half the fire turned to look at her. She considered letting it lie, and then — because the restlessness had been sitting under her ribs all day like a swallowed coal — she didn't. "Nothing follows in here. Not blessings either. You want to live in a place where nothing can reach you, you live in a place where nothing can reach you. It cuts both ways."

"So you'd leave," Joss said, leaning forward, delighted by the heresy. "You'd take us back out."

"I'd take myself out," Paloma said. "You're free to stay."

Dagny snorted. Merrit shook his head with the weary patience of a man who had watched Paloma's mother ask the same questions and her grandmother refuse to answer them. Someone threw a pinecone at Joss, the conversation dissolved into the age-old argument about whether the wide world was worth the dying it cost, and Paloma slipped away from the fire before anyone could turn the question back on her, which they always, eventually, tried to do.

She climbed the granite shelf above camp and sat with her knees drawn up, looking north along the valley's axis, toward the far pass and everything beyond it. The stars came out hard and clean, undimmed by any sorcery, because there was none to dim them. That was the bargain her grandmother's generation had struck, or accepted, or inflicted — depending on who told it. Safety. Perfect, airless, endless safety.

She was twenty-nine years old, captain by competence if not by commission, feared in three market towns, bored in all of them. She knew every mile of the valley the way a prisoner knows the dimensions of a cell: precisely, and with hatred.

"Gods," she said quietly, to no one, to the absent one, to the century of silence. "If you're there, I'd take a word. One word. Even a dull one."

The wind moved through the birches below. Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered.

She almost missed it — the light coming up the valley road. A lantern, or what she took for a lantern, swaying with the uneven gait of a traveler on foot. She watched it for a while, marking its pace, and decided it was a trader who'd misjudged the dark rather than anything worth waking the camp for. Still, she went down herself. It was her name on the contract, after all, even if the word *her* kept meaning less every year.

* * *

He was older than she'd guessed from the light — sixty at least, maybe older, gaunt in the way of men who had been riding urgency instead of horses. He had a scholar's soft hands and a courier's ruined boots, and he was walking with the deliberate, rationed steps of someone who had promised himself he would not stop until he dropped, and was very near collecting on the promise. When he saw her, he did not reach for a weapon or a purse. He reached into his coat with the care of a man protecting something more fragile than himself, and his eyes went over her face — the black hair, the sharp jaw, the gray thread of the closed eye at her collar — and he made a sound that was half laugh and half sob.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, thank every abandoned heaven. It's your face. It's exactly your face."

"That depends on what you wanted it for," Paloma said. Her hand rested easy on her sword hilt, which was how she rested her hand anywhere. "You're a long way from anywhere, grandfather, and later than safe. Camp's that way, if you're buying protection. We don't sell prophecy."

The word landed strangely. He flinched as if she'd struck a bruise he'd forgotten he had, and then he smiled, and the smile was worse than the flinch.

"No," he said. "No one sells it. It sells you." He swayed, caught himself against a birch trunk, and Paloma saw — with the flat professional appraisal she brought to all approaching disasters — that he was dying. Not soon. Now. There was blood on his lips when he wiped them, dark in the lantern-light, and his color was the gray of old ash. "I need to sit down, Captain Crane. I've come a very long way to sit down, and I would prefer to do it before I fall down, if the valley will extend me the courtesy."

"How do you know my name?"

"You're going to wish I didn't." He slid down the birch to sitting, breathing in careful sips. From inside his coat he drew a book — small, bound in leather gone dark and soft with handling, wrapped in oilcloth that he peeled back with trembling fingers. "But you should know it anyway, before the end. Names matter in this business. Yours especially. Yours most of all."

"We don't hold with magic in the valley," Paloma said, and heard how foolish the sentence sounded even as she said it, because the man was plainly a scholar and plainly dying and plainly carrying nothing but paper.

"No," he agreed. "That's rather the entire point of you."

Something in the way he said it — *of you*, as if she were a thing with a purpose built into it, a tool with an edge ground by someone else's hand — sent a cold finger down her spine. She crouched across from him, keeping her weight balanced, and held out her hand. "Give it here, then. If it's mine, I'll read it myself."

"You misunderstand." He laid his palm flat on the cover, protective even now, even bleeding. "It opens for one reader. It's written for one reader. I was only ever the postman." He coughed, wet and long, and steadied himself against the tree. "Page nine, Captain. Look at page nine, and then look at me, and then — I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I was given thirty years to find you and I used twenty-nine of them. I would have liked to give you more warning than this."

With visible effort, he turned the book around and opened it across his knees.

The pages were heavy, cream-colored, dense with handwriting — a woman's hand, slanted and sure, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood. He turned past a page, another, and stopped.

And Paloma's name began to burn.

Not the letters themselves — not only the letters. The burn came up through her skin, through her blood, along paths she had never known her body contained, as if somewhere under her breastbone a door had been locked her whole life and the writing on the page was the key turning in it. She looked down and read, in that slanted, certain hand:

*On this night — and she gave the date, and it was tonight, this exact night, this impossible date circled twice in ink a century old —*

*the Crane of the dateless night comes down from the silent valley.*

*Black-haired. Sharp-faced. Kneels to a god who cannot hear her.*

*She is the unmaker of crowns. The valley's daughter. The last faithful child of a faithless age.*

*To her I leave this book, and this charge: go to the crowned king, who is two men wearing one face, and unbind him.*

*Little bird. Forgive me. Forgive me for the silence, and forgive me for the ending of it.*

The world tilted. Paloma heard the words in her grandmother's voice — the same voice that had sung to her, lied to her, locked every door in her life from the loving side — and her hands were shaking, and the burning under her breastbone was spreading outward now, warm and vast and awake, like something enormous rolling over in a sleep of a hundred years.

"That's not possible," she said. Her own voice sounded far away. "There's no magic in the valley. Nothing follows. Nothing—"

"No," the archivist whispered. He was slumping now, sliding down the birch, his eyes fixed on her face with something like awe. "Nothing follows in. But it seems, Captain Crane, that something can be carried out."

The air shuddered.

There was no other word for it. The dead, magic-eating air of the Godless Valley — the air she had breathed for twenty-nine years, air as inert and obedient as stone — shuddered like a sheet in wind, and the book flared with pale light, and the pressure under Paloma's breastbone became a current, and the current became a pull. The ground tilted. Or she tilted. The birches bent inward around her, impossibly, all their white trunks leaning toward the open book like a congregation, and the archivist was saying something — his mouth moving, *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, run, or don't* — but his voice was stretching, thinning, dissolving into the roar of the valley itself being asked, for the first time in a century, a question it could not refuse.

Paloma reached for her sword. Reached for the camp, the fires, the voices of her company — and found them already distant, already receding like a shoreline seen from a departing ship.

The last thing she saw of the Godless Valley was its perfect, empty sky.

Then the page took her, and the world turned to ink.
