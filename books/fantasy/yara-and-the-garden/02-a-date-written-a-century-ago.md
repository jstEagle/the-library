## 2. A Date Written a Century Ago

The stranger arrived at dusk, when the Godless Valley did its best work of pretending to be any other place in the world.

Paloma saw him first from the watchfire, a smudge against the amber light of the western cliffs — a figure walking with the careful, deliberate gait of someone who had counted his remaining steps and was determined not to waste them. He came down the old drover's road that no one used anymore because nothing needed to be driven anywhere in the valley; the valley gave nothing and took nothing and had long ago been forgiven by every road that mattered.

"Rider coming," said Doss from across the fire, not looking up from his whetstone. Doss announced everything in the same tone he used for weather and bad stew. "One. Old. Sick."

"You can tell he's sick from here?"

"He walks like a man carrying his own funeral."

Paloma stood, brushing ash from her knees out of habit rather than need. She had learned young that in the Crane Company you rose for strangers, even useless ones — especially useless ones, her grandmother used to say, because useful people arrive already knowing what they want, but the useless come carrying what you didn't know you'd lost.

She had hated that kind of saying as a child. She collected them now, the way other soldiers collected scars: without quite meaning to, and unable to stop.

The man reached the fire as the last light failed. Up close he was worse than the distance had promised — a narrow body wrapped in a traveling coat gone gray with road dust, cheeks hollowed to architecture, hands that trembled around a leather satchel clasped to his chest like a second heart. His eyes were the worst part. They were bright. Fever-bright, yes, but under the fever there was something else: the particular ferocity of a scholar who has run very far with something very important and knows he is going to die before he can put it down.

"Crane," he said. It wasn't a question. It wasn't even a greeting. He said it the way a drowning man says *shore*.

"There are forty Cranes at this fire alone," Paloma said. "Fifty if you count the ones pretending they aren't listening."

"Not you." He swayed. Doss half-rose; Paloma waved him down and stepped forward herself, catching the archivist by the elbow. His arm weighed nothing. That frightened her more than his eyes did. "The prophecy names a Crane. Dark hair cut like a soldier's. A scar—" he raised his own hand, trembling, and drew a line along the outside of her left forearm, not touching her, "—here, from a blade you caught at fourteen because you were too proud to wear bracers."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind her, someone stopped sharpening something.

Paloma looked down at her arm. The scar was there, pale and old, exactly where he'd drawn it. She had gotten it at fourteen, showing off, refusing bracers, catching a training blade she should have let pass. No one living knew that story except the company — and none of them would have told a stranger on the drover's road.

"Who sent you?" she asked quietly.

"Your grandmother."

"My grandmother has been dead eleven years."

"Yes." The archivist smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a man agreeing with a fact he'd spent a century arguing with. "Yes, she has. May I sit? I have walked a very long way to die in the right place, and I would prefer not to do it standing up."

They gave him a stool by the fire. They gave him broth he didn't touch. The company gathered in a loose ring at the edge of the firelight, the way soldiers do when they smell a story — close enough to hear, far enough to claim later they hadn't cared. Old Maren brought the man a blanket without being asked. Even the valley seemed to lean in; the wind died, and the silence that always lived here, the flat dead quiet where prayers go to starve, felt suddenly attentive.

He introduced himself as Ferren, late of the Archive at Callowmere, which meant nothing to anyone, which he acknowledged with a small, dry nod as though he'd expected nothing else. And then, with hands that shook so badly Paloma finally steadied them with her own — an intimacy she would think about later, how easily she touched this dying stranger, as if some part of her had been waiting for him all her life — he unclasped the satchel and lifted out the book.

It was small. That was her first absurd thought. All this, for a book smaller than a bread loaf. Bound in green leather gone dark with age, corners worn round, a strap of the same leather holding it shut. It looked like a psalter. It looked like a merchant's ledger. It looked like nothing.

"The binding is original," Ferren said. "The hand inside is your grandmother's. I have spent thirty years verifying it. Ink, script, marginalia, watermarks in the paper — all consistent with the year it claims to have been written. One hundred years ago, Crane. Your grandmother wrote this before your father was born."

"You've mistaken me for someone with a family that does things."

"Open it."

"I don't read prophecies. I escort grain wagons."

"Open it," he said again, softer, and there was something in his voice that made her obey — not command, but pity, as though he could see how short the distance was between her laughing and her not laughing, and wanted her to reach the far side quickly, while there was still time to do something about it.

The strap was stiff. The cover opened with a sound like a sigh held for a century.

The pages were heavy rag paper, dense with a small, precise hand — her grandmother's hand, which Paloma recognized instantly and completely, the way you recognize your own face in a mirror. She had seen that handwriting on supply manifests and letters home and one bitter, unfinished will. Her whole body went cold and hot at once.

*Read the page,* the hand commanded, or the ink commanded, or the night commanded. *The one marked with today.*

Ferren turned the pages for her. He moved with a scholar's economy past years of entries — dated observations, diagrams, a sketch of the valley itself rendered with loving, resentful accuracy, notes in margins that curled into what might have been poetry or prayer. Then he stopped, and laid his finger on a date.

Tonight's date. Not approximately. Exactly. Day, month, and the year written out in full, a hundred years in advance, as casually as a woman noting when the plums would ripen.

Below the date, in the same unhurried hand:

*The Crane comes to the fire at dusk. Dark-haired, soldier-cut. Scar on the left forearm from a prideful blade at fourteen. She will laugh. Let her laugh; laughter is the sound a heart makes when it refuses a weight it must carry anyway. Tell her this: the king wears the crown and the crown wears another's voice, and only the Crane of the dateless night can unbind what was bound to save her. Tell her she will hate me for writing it. Tell her she is permitted to. Tell her the god is not silent. Tell her the god is bound, and the binding was mercy, and mercy has a cost, and the bill has come due.*

Paloma laughed.

It came out of her the way Ferren had apparently predicted, sharp and ugly, startling the men at the ring's edge. She laughed until her ribs hurt, and then she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and kept laughing, quieter now, because somewhere in the middle of it the laughter had stopped being refusal and started being grief, and she couldn't have said why.

"A dateless night," Doss offered from the darkness, helpfully. "That's tonight. No moon. I noticed earlier."

"Thank you, Doss."

"It's a compliment, probably. In prophecy terms."

"Doss."

Ferren watched her with those bright dying eyes and did not laugh with her, which was the courtesy of a man who had done all his laughing decades ago.

"You wrote the date yourself," Paloma said finally. "You found a blank ledger of hers and you filled in tonight, and you walked here to make a fool of a mercenary captain because—" Because why? What did anyone gain? The question collapsed under its own weight. Nobody crossed the Godless Valley to play tricks. Nothing followed anyone into this place. That was the entire point of it, the founding miracle of her whole dull life: magic could not enter here. Gods could not speak here. Whatever power had written kings and burned cities and set her company wandering a hundred years ago simply *stopped* at the ridgeline, like weather at a window.

"Touch the page," Ferren said.

"No."

"Captain." He leaned toward her, urgent now, spending strength he clearly could not afford. "I am going to die within the hour. I would like, before that, to be wrong about something. Anything. Give me that. Touch the page."

And because she was, at bottom, a soldier, and soldiers know that sometimes you give a dying man his last request precisely because it costs you nothing — she touched the page.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. This was the Godless Valley. Nothing ever happened here—

The air shuddered.

There was no better word for it. The air itself — the flat, dead, blessedly empty air she had breathed for twenty-six years, the air that had never once whispered or hummed or *noticed* her — shuddered like a struck drumhead. The fire flattened sideways without wind. Every man in the company flinched at once, a ripple of animal wrongness passing through them, horses screaming in the picket lines beyond the camp.

"What," said Maren, in a small voice, "was that."

The book grew warm under Paloma's fingers.

Not metaphor-warm. Warm like a living thing, warm like a hand, and the warmth climbed up through her fingertips and into her wrist and she tried to pull away and could not. The pages turned themselves — slowly, deliberately, the way Ferren had turned them — back to the dated entry, and the ink began to move, her grandmother's precise letters rearranging themselves, adding a line beneath the ones Paloma had just read:

*If she is reading this in the valley, the binding is failing. Come to me, little crane. Come out of the safety I built you, because it is killing you slower than the world would, and I would rather you die free than safe and hollow.*

"No," Paloma said. It was a reasonable word. It was the correct word. She said it again, louder, to the book, to the valley, to her dead grandmother, to the forty silent gods of forty silent soldiers. "No. Nothing follows us here. Nothing works here. This is the one place—"

The valley shuddered again, deeper this time, and Paloma felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her teeth, in whatever root ran deeper than teeth. The mountains on either side of them were black shapes against the stars, and for one vertiginous moment she was certain they were leaning closer. The dead air was filling up with something. Pressure, presence, attention — a century of locked-out magic pressing against the valley's skin from every direction at once, finding the one thin place, the one crack, and the crack was a green-leathered book open in a dying archivist's lap.

The book pulled.

There was no gentler way to describe it, and Paloma Crane did not deal in gentle descriptions. It pulled her bodily — off the stool, over the fire's edge, boots scrabbling in cold ash — and the company shouted and grabbed at her, Doss locking both arms around her waist, Maren seizing her belt, four other hands finding wrists and sleeves, and for three full seconds the entire Crane Company played tug-of-war with a book.

Then the pull became a tearing.

The last thing Paloma saw clearly was Ferren, still seated, still calm, watching her with tears running freely down his ruined face and an expression of unbearable relief — the look of a courier whose package has finally left his hands. The last thing she heard was Doss roaring her name, and under it, faintly, impossibly, rising out of the ground itself, out of the dead soil of the Godless Valley:

A word.

One word. Her grandmother's voice, or the book's voice, or the valley's voice, speaking for the first time in a hundred years, and the word was —

*Come.*

The world folded.

* * *

She fell out of the world onto wet grass.

For a long time there was only breathing — hers, ragged and enormous, loud in ears still ringing with the tearing sound of everything she knew being folded up and put away. Cold air. Real air, moving air, air with things alive in it that brushed against her skin and *noticed* her. Grass soaked her knees. Somewhere nearby, water moved over stones with a music the valley had never permitted.

She was lying in an orchard. Pear trees, old ones, silver-barked, their branches hung with fruit that glowed faintly in the moonless dark like lanterns no one had lit. The scent was overwhelming — blossom and ripe flesh and underneath it something older, something that smelled the way her grandmother's workshop had smelled, paper and ink and patience.

Paloma pushed herself upright and immediately understood two things.

The first was that she was no longer anywhere near the Godless Valley. The stars were wrong. The horizon was wrong. Magic was in the air here the way salt is in sea-air — everywhere, unnoticed by the born to it, suffocating to her.

The second thing was that her blood was burning.

It started low, in the center of her, a heat like swallowed coals, and spread outward with every heartbeat. She looked down at her hands expecting to see them glowing and they were ordinary hands, shaking, grass-stained — but the burning rose anyway, steady and patient, as though something inside her had waited a century behind a door and had just heard it unlock.

In her pocket — impossibly, undeniably — the book. Green leather, warm as a sleeping bird.

Somewhere among the trees, footsteps. Soft ones. Deliberate. Someone walking toward her who had known she was coming, perhaps, or someone who had heard a stranger fall out of the night into their orchard and was approaching with the caution such arrivals deserve.

Paloma got her feet under her. She reached for a sword that was no longer at her hip. The burning in her blood climbed another degree, and above her, on the branches of the pear trees, the glowing fruit pulsed gently — in rhythm, she realized with distant horror, with her own heartbeat.

The footsteps stopped. A figure stood between the trees, silhouetted against the lantern-glow of the fruit, and Paloma Crane, unmaker of crowns, expelled from the only safe place in the world, did the only thing her whole life had trained her to do.

She raised her fists.

The figure sighed — a tired, fond, deeply unimpressed sound — and said:

"Oh, put those down. You look half-dead, and I have soup."
