## 4. What the Trees Remember

Paloma woke to birdsong and did not trust it.

Birdsong meant trees. Trees meant cover, ambush lines, a dozen places for a knife to come from — and her body knew this before her mind did, because her body had spent nineteen years learning which silences in the Godless Valley were safe and which ones were merely waiting. Her hand went to her hip for a sword that wasn't there. Her other hand found soft linen instead of bedroll canvas, and a pillow stuffed with something that smelled faintly of lavender and old rain.

She lay still and took inventory. No pain worth naming — only the deep, bruised ache of someone who had been pulled through the skin of the world like a thread through an eyelet. Her mouth tasted of iron and pear.

Pear. She remembered pears. A tree leaning over her like a curious animal, fruit glowing with that impossible inner light, and then nothing.

The room was small and round-walled, more grown than built — the plaster had the grain of wood in it, and the window was not a rectangle but a gap where two beams simply declined to meet. Through it came the orchard: rows of trees in silver morning light, each one heavy with fruit that glowed like lanterns left burning by accident. She watched one tree for a long time. The light inside its apples shifted color slowly, the way a face shifts when it remembers something sad.

*Magic*, she thought, and flinched from the word the way she'd been trained to flinch from it. The Valley had no word for magic except *gone*. Everything else was story, was rumor, was what happened to people foolish enough to leave.

She got up on legs that held better than expected. Someone had removed her boots but left her clothes; someone had also set a clay cup of water on the sill, sweating in the cool air, placed precisely where a waking person's hand would fall. That small courtesy told her more about her host than any interrogation would have.

The door — a door made of woven willow whips, alive at the edges, tiny new leaves budding along the frame — opened onto a path between the tree rows.

The woman from the pear tree stood at the end of it with a basket on her hip, and she did not look surprised to see Paloma upright. She looked like she'd been waiting for exactly this moment, and mildly annoyed at how long it had taken.

"You're the witch," Paloma said. Not a question. Her voice came out rough as gravel.

"I'm Yara." The woman had soil to her elbows and grey threading her dark braid, and eyes the brown-green of things growing in shade. "You're the Crane."

Paloma's spine straightened on reflex. "I never told you my name."

"You didn't have to." Yara set the basket down on a flat stone and lifted out a pear, holding it up so the light moved through its flesh like a lamp through honey. "The trees told me. They've been telling me about you since the hour you fell out of nowhere into my north row. You arrived screaming a date, do you know that? In your sleep. Over and over. The date written in your book."

"The book isn't mine."

"No," Yara said. "But it's about you. And until I know how much, you'll eat breakfast and rest and ask your questions sitting down, because you nearly died in my orchard and I don't drag corpses back from my gate twice."

It was not, Paloma reflected later, the worst recruitment speech she'd ever heard. She sat down on the flat stone because her legs agreed with the witch, and ate bread and hard cheese and drank water that tasted of minerals and something greener, and said nothing while Yara pruned a row of quinces with quick economical hands. It was a mercenary's patience. Wait. Watch. Let the silence do work.

The orchard worked on her, though. She tried to keep her attention narrow — exits, sightlines, the treeline, the low stone wall — but the trees kept pulling her gaze. Every fruit glowed faintly, and no two glowed alike. One plum burned a sullen red-orange, like a fire remembered rather than seen. An apple near the path pulsed a soft gold, steady as candlelight. And high in the pear tree above her, half-hidden in leaves, hung one fruit that shivered whenever she looked directly at it, its light flickering like a guttering wick.

"That one," Yara said without turning around, "is yours. It grew overnight after you arrived. The orchard doesn't usually make fruit that fast."

"What does it grow?"

Yara turned then, wiping her hands on her apron, and studied Paloma with an expression that was equal parts assessment and pity. "Memories," she said. "This is a garden of memories. Each tree grows out of a life. The plums there belonged to a shepherd who died eighty years ago and wanted his flock remembered. The gold apple was a midwife's. I tend them. Sometimes I eat one, when I miss someone, or when I need to understand something the living won't tell me plainly." She nodded at the shivering pear. "That one grew from whatever spilled out of you when you crossed over. I haven't picked it. I'd suggest you don't either. Fruit from a stranger's spill is potent, and you're already carrying more than one life's weight."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because you're the sort of person who eats forbidden things the moment they're labeled," Yara said dryly. "I could see it in your face. Better you know the price going in."

Paloma finished her breakfast and did not touch the pear, and both of them pretended this settled the matter.

* * *

By afternoon the fever found her.

It started as heat behind the eyes, then spread down her neck and arms until her skin felt too tight, and Yara — whose touch, when it came, was cool and sure — eased her back onto the cot in the round room and muttered words that were not words, more like the sound of water deciding where to flow. "Your body crossed a boundary it wasn't built to cross," she said. "The Valley kept you like a cellar keeps preserves. Out here, everything wakes up. Including things in your blood." She pressed a damp cloth to Paloma's forehead. "Sleep. If you dream, don't fight the dreams. Fighting them makes them worse."

Paloma slept, and dreamed of the orchard.

Or she believed she dreamed, until she found herself standing again on the path between the rows, barefoot in full daylight that was somehow also midnight, the stars overhead arranged in patterns she almost recognized. The fever had walked her out of her body and into the garden's grammar, or perhaps the garden had reached into the fever and taken her hand. Either way, the shivering pear now hung at her eye level, close enough to smell — and it smelled like her childhood. Like tallow candles and oiled leather. Like the company chapel where the god's empty alcove gathered dust, and like her grandmother's hands, cracked and strong, teaching her to hold a blade.

Her grandmother had been dead nine years. Nobody in the company spoke of her much anymore, and when they did it was in the flattened tones reserved for founders and weather.

Paloma reached up and picked the pear.

The taste was her grandmother's kitchen: salt, rosemary, woodsmoke. Then the taste became a door, and the door opened, and she fell through it into another woman's life.

* * *

She was young — the grandmother, young — and Paloma knew it the way you know your own reflection in a warped mirror, by the set of the jaw and the stubbornness of the mouth. The world around her was not the Valley. It was green and loud and saturated, hills running with streams, a city below with towers that leaned like old men gossiping. Magic sat on everything like pollen. Paloma could feel it even secondhand, even through the fruit: a pressure, a music, a vast indifferent attention. This was what the world outside the Valley was. This was what had been kept from her.

The young woman — Maren, the name arrived with the memory like a label tied to a stem — walked those hills with a sword on her back and a god at her shoulder.

Not a statue. Not an empty alcove. A presence, warm and enormous and *attentive*, moving beside her the way a mountain range moves beside a traveler — always there, mostly silent, occasionally shifting in ways you only notice at dusk. Paloma felt her own throat tighten, because she had prayed into that silence for nineteen years and here, in a dead woman's memory, the silence answered. Not in words. In weight. In the way Maren's fear receded when she gripped her sword-hilt, the way luck bent around her like current around a stone.

*So it was real*, Paloma thought, grief arriving ahead of understanding. *It answered us once.*

The memories came faster now, tumbling like fruit off an overturned cart. Maren as a captain. Maren swearing oaths with a hundred blades raised under a sky split by auroras. Maren kneeling in a temple while something vast touched her forehead and left a mark like frost. Maren falling in love with a scholar with ink-stained fingers who laughed at her superstitions. Maren pregnant, terrified, radiant.

And then the war.

Paloma had fought skirmishes, escort actions, one ugly siege. She had thought she understood war. Maren's war was different — it was *theological*. Armies marching under banners she half-recognized from the company's oldest relics, and above the battlefields shapes that were not armies at all: presences, gods colliding like storm fronts, and mortals dying beneath them by the thousand, collateral in arguments too large to hear. She saw a city burn in a single night. She saw a river run black. She saw the thing the histories called the Sundering — the moment the gods' war broke the world's spine — and she saw Maren standing in the wreckage of it, holding her infant daughter, watching the divine attention of the world turn toward her child like a searchlight.

Because of the mark. Because of the frost-shaped blessing on her brow. Because her blood, now, carried godstuff the way some blood carries poison.

*Maren ran*, the memories said. Ran with a hundred sworn swords who chose exile over abandoning her, ran east into a wound in the world where the gods' attention slid off like rain off glass — the Valley, the Godless Valley, named backward, named by outsiders, because from inside it wasn't godless at all. It was *safe*. It was the one place her daughter's blood could grow quiet. She bound it herself — Paloma felt the binding happen, felt Maren kneel in the dead center of the valley with her hands in the dirt and pour a century of silence into the ground like planting a bulb, felt her bargain with something old and patient that lived beneath the valley floor, felt the cost of it go through her like a spear.

And then Maren grew old in exile, and the silence she had planted grew into walls, and her granddaughter was born under those walls and never knew the sky had once been loud.

Paloma wept inside the memory, and the memory did not stop for her tears.

* * *

The last years came slow and close, the way an old person's memories do — fewer, heavier, each one held longer.

Maren old, her sword hung on a wall she never polished. Maren writing. That was the strangest part, seeing her grandmother hunched over a desk by lamplight, because the company's founder was supposed to be a figure of iron and action, not ink. But she wrote every night, in a hand that deteriorated year by year, in a book whose pages Paloma recognized with a lurch that nearly broke the memory open — vellum gone amber, leather cracked like drought earth, the same book now lying in her pack a few feet from her sleeping body.

The prophecy book. Being written, not discovered. Composed, not prophesied.

Paloma watched her grandmother's hands move across a page and heard her murmur the words as she wrote them, and the words assembled themselves in Paloma's mind with terrible clarity:

*A Crane shall walk out of the dateless night, when the crown has grown roots and the king has grown quiet, and she shall unmake what was made to hold him — or the silence she inherits shall be the last silence, and no voice, mortal or divine, shall wake beneath it.*

"It's not a prophecy," Paloma whispered into the memory, into the lamplit room. "It's instructions."

Maren — old Maren, dying Maren, her hands spotted and shaking — looked up from the page as if she'd heard. Her eyes were clouded but they fixed on Paloma with an accuracy that made her skin crawl, and her mouth shaped words that came across a century like a knock at a door:

*Forgive me. There wasn't time to explain. There is never time to explain — that is the first thing they take from us, the time. Find the archivist. Trust the gardener. And when you learn what I bound, remember that I bound it out of love, and that love and cruelty are often the same act viewed from different distances.*

The memory folded shut.

* * *

Paloma came back to her body gasping, sitting bolt upright on the cot in the dark, sweat-soaked, the taste of rosemary still in her mouth. The fever had broken. The room was dim; someone had left a lantern burning low and a fresh cup of water on the sill, placed precisely where her hand would fall.

She sat for a long time, letting the pieces settle, and they did not settle gently.

The archivist. The dying man who had crawled into the Valley carrying a century-old book that burned with her name — he hadn't stumbled onto her. He'd been *sent*. Her grandmother had written a book disguised as prophecy, seeded it with tonight's exact date and her own granddaughter's description, and dispatched it through channels Paloma couldn't begin to map, timed to arrive when the crown had "grown roots" — which meant someone in her family, or trusted by her family, had been watching Aldric's coronation, watching the usurper, watching for a century. Watching while Paloma prayed to a silence her grandmother had installed like a lock, and called it faith.

*The founding oath*, she thought suddenly. *Sworn to keep the Cranes watched.* Brannoc's stories about the old commanders, the ritual phrases, the way the company always seemed to know where she was. She had thought it was protection. It might have been custody.

And underneath all of it, colder than the rest: *the binding is in me.* Maren had poured the silence into the valley, but the mark — the frost-blessing, the godstuff in the blood — had passed down mother to daughter to granddaughter, and the silence had to be anchored somewhere. Paloma thought of Yara's careful hands on her fevered forehead, of the words *things in your blood are waking up,* and felt the shape of a truth assembling itself just past the edge of sight.

Her whole life had been a cell, and her grandmother had built it lovingly, brick by brick, and died before she could leave the key — or before she would.

Paloma got up. Barefoot, silent, mercenary-quiet, she retrieved her pack from the corner and drew out the book. In the lantern light its pages seemed less like vellum now and more like bark, like something grown rather than made, and she understood that this too had been planned, that even the book was a seed her grandmother had planted in the future and waited, blind, to see flower.

At the back of the book, past the final dated entry, she found a page she was certain had been blank three days ago. Ink was surfacing on it slowly, rising through the vellum like a bruise coming up through skin — new writing, in her grandmother's hand, that had waited a hundred years to be legible:

*If you are reading this, you have eaten, or soon will. Good. Now go to the gardener and ask her what she buried under the north row. Ask her why she really keeps an orchard of memories so close to the capital. Ask her whose memories she tends that aren't her own.*

Paloma looked up through the gap-window, out at the moonlit orchard, at the north row where Yara's cottage stood dark and still.

Somewhere beneath it, she was suddenly certain, something was buried that her grandmother had trusted to a stranger — and every instinct she owned was telling her that the trusting was the part she needed to fear.
