## 5. The Crown That Whispers

The crown spoke to Aldric most often at dawn.

It was not a voice he heard with his ears — his ears had long ago learned to flinch away from it, the way a hand learns to avoid a stove after one burning — but something deeper, a resonance conducted through the bone of his skull itself, as if the metal had grown roots into the grey matter beneath and now drank from him as readily as it poured into him. It had been seven years since the coronation. Seven years since the smith's tongs had lowered that circlet of black iron and pale gold onto his head, and instead of the expected weight of ceremony he had felt a small, intimate pain, like a needle finding a vein.

*Good morning, little king,* it said, in a voice like honey poured over broken glass. *You slept poorly again. You called out for her.*

He did not answer. He had stopped answering aloud years ago; answering aloud encouraged it, and besides, the servants — the ones who watched him, who were not servants at all but the usurper's eyes dressed in palace livery — had begun to notice when their king muttered to empty air. Now he answered only in the privacy of his own skull, where no one could see, and where, increasingly, no one could reach.

*I did not call out for anyone,* he thought back, carefully, the way one walks across ice.

*You said "Mother." You said "the roses." You wept.* The voice was gentle, so gently mocking, warm as a hearth and cold as the grave beneath one. *I only mention it because I worry, Aldric. We are bound together, you and I. Your grief is my grief. Your dreams are my dreams.* A pause, delicate as a held breath. *Though lately your dreams have been growing quieter. Smaller. Like a garden left untended.*

Aldric rose from his bed and crossed to the window of the royal apartments, which were royal in name only now. The capital lay below him in the grey light: the river silvered with mist, the market square already filling, the great banner of House Vane snapping from every tower where his family's stag had once flown. Occupied. The word still caught in his throat like a fishbone, even after six years. Not conquered — Vane's soldiers had been careful about that distinction, careful in the way of men who wanted the city breathing but docile — but occupied, governed, *held*, the way a hand holds a bird: firmly enough to prevent flight, loosely enough to feel the heartbeat hammering against the palm.

His heartbeat. He sometimes wondered if the whole kingdom could feel it.

*Look at them,* the crown murmured, and he realized with a lurch that he was looking — that his eyes had moved without his permission to sweep across the waking city, taking in the crowds, the carts, the children running along the embankment. *So many small lives. So fragile. So easily frightened. You keep them safe, Aldric. Whatever else you believe about me — whatever bitter little stories you tell yourself in the dark — you know that much is true. There has been no war in the streets. No purge. Because you sit on this throne and behave.*

"I sit on this throne," he said aloud, before he could stop himself, "because you will not let me stand."

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere below, a cartwheel squeaked against stone.

*Oh, little king.* The voice softened into something almost tender, which was always, always worse. *You could stand any time you liked. Walk out those doors. Go down to the square and tell them all the truth — that their king is a prisoner in his own skull, that the man they call Lord Protector rules through a circlet of iron that whispers. Do you think they would follow you? Do you think they would even recognize you?*

And there it was, the question that lived at the bottom of everything, the one he kept trying to stop himself from asking and asked anyway, every night, in the dark behind his eyes: how much of him was left? Seven years of the crown's slow conversation, seven years of thoughts arriving that he could not swear were his own, seven years of memories that seemed slightly too smooth, too polished, like stones worn by water. He remembered his mother's face. He was nearly certain he remembered his mother's face. But when he reached for it, the memory came to him pre-arranged, framed, lit like a scene on a stage — and he could no longer say whether the warmth he felt was love or merely the crown's excellent imitation of it, offered up to keep him cooperative, to keep him *comfortable*, because comfortable prisoners do not claw at their chains.

He pressed his forehead against the cool glass. Below, a woman was selling early apples from a basket, and a boy was paying her with a smile and a copper piece, and none of them — not the woman, not the boy, not one soul in that whole sprawling city — knew that their king stood thirty feet above them, fighting a war that had no battlefield and no allies and no end in sight.

*There he is,* the crown said fondly. *There is my brave, sad king. Come. The Lord Protector requests your presence at midday. There is a matter of grain tariffs, and he wishes you to nod at the appropriate moments.*

***

Midday found Aldric in the long council chamber beneath the great hall, sitting on the left side of a table built for two thrones and occupying neither.

Lord Protector Cassian Vane sat at the table's head, though technically it was not his seat — the throne room above remained closed, its doors sealed by Vane's own order, "out of respect," he said, "until His Majesty is well enough to resume his duties." Everyone understood the courtesy for what it was: a locked room, a king in storage. In practice, Vane ruled from the council chamber, from the barracks, from the gallows outside the west gate, and Aldric attended these sessions the way a portrait attends a sitting — present, silent, ornamental.

Vane himself was not what Aldric had expected, back when expectation was still a thing he was permitted. He had imagined a brute, a warlord, some scarred butcher of the borderlands. Instead he got a man of fifty with soft hands and reading glasses and a scholar's stoop, who quoted agricultural yields the way other men quote poetry, and whose cruelty was never cruelty at all but merely *necessity*, delivered in the mild tone of a physician recommending amputation. He frightened Aldric more thoroughly than any monster would have, precisely because there was nothing to hate cleanly. Only a man doing monstrous arithmetic and showing his work.

"The southern provinces are withholding again," Vane was saying, tapping a ledger. "Three weeks' delinquent on the grain levy. I've sent terms — generous terms, I want the record to show that — amnesty for the ringleaders if the stores are opened within the fortnight." He glanced up, and his gaze rested on Aldric with the mild patience of a man checking on a convalescent. "Your Majesty concurs with the approach?"

Every eye at the table turned to him. This was the ritual, performed daily: the question posed, the nod given, the fiction of governance maintained. And every day Aldric faced the same small war — whether to nod, or to use the moment, this one moment, to open his mouth and say something true.

Seven years ago he would have spoken. Six years ago he might have. Five years ago he had tried — he remembered it with terrible clarity, that one afternoon when he had risen from his chair and said, *This council serves a thief,* and gotten four words out before the pain began. Not the crown's pain. His own. A white-hot screw turning behind his eyes, his jaw locking, his tongue going thick and foreign in his mouth, until he had knelt on the council floor drooling while Vane watched with something that was almost compassion and ordered the session adjourned. Afterward, alone, the crown had stroked the inside of his skull and explained, patiently, the way one explains fire to a child.

*You see what happens. I don't do that to you, Aldric. You do it to yourself. Every time you fight me, you bleed. I am part of you now. Hurting me is hurting you. When will you learn to be kind to us both?*

That was the genius of it, he thought now, giving the required nod while Vane made a note. Not the pain — pain only taught hatred, and hatred was fuel, and he had burned plenty of it over the years. The genius was the *we*. The crown never spoke of itself as separate. It wove itself into his sentences, his reflexes, his griefs, until fighting it felt like self-mutilation and surrender felt like rest. It had taken him years to understand that this too was a technique, a slow carpentry of the self, each day driving one nail more, and that somewhere in the walls of his mind there might still be rooms the crown had never entered — but he could no longer find the doorways. He had stopped being sure, some winters ago, which thoughts were load-bearing.

"Your Majesty is thoughtful today," Vane observed, folding his glasses away.

"I am concerned for the southern provinces," Aldric heard himself say, in a voice that was steady and reasonable and entirely, horrifyingly calm. "Amnesty is generous. Generosity is well received in the south. My father—" He stopped. The sentence had been building toward *my father won their loyalty with mercy after the Harrow Rebellion*, a real memory, a good one, his father's broad hand on his shoulder in the harvest fields — and then the memory shuddered, blurred, and reassembled itself wrong, the fields grey, the hand absent, the whole scene drained of color like a tapestry left in the sun. Gone, or altered, or perhaps never quite what he thought. He stood in the rubble of it, smiling, while the crown purred somewhere deep in the bone.

*Careful, little king. You'll hurt yourself rummaging in there.*

"My father," Aldric finished, "valued mercy."

"As do we all," Vane agreed pleasantly, and moved on to road levies.

***

That evening, in the small garden.

It was the one place he still went to fight. Not the royal gardens — those had been closed, flagstoned over, declared structurally unsound in the first year of the occupation, though Aldric had walked them since he could walk and knew there was nothing wrong with their soil. No; this was a forgotten corner behind the old chapel, a yard no larger than a stable, where someone's grandmother had once planted roses and then the gardeners' wars and budget cuts and occupations of history had let it go wild. Brambles, bindweed, three ragged rosebushes that bloomed stubbornly every June as if they hadn't heard who ruled the city now. He had found it in his second year, desperate for green, and had kept its existence secret even from the crown — or tried to. He no longer trusted the boundary between what he hid and what it permitted him to hide.

But here, among the roses, he had a discipline. He sat on an overturned trough with his hands flat on his knees and he rebuilt his mother, brick by brick, the way a mason rebuilds a wall that something keeps kicking down.

Her name had been Elira. She had smelled of crushed thyme because she pulled weeds barehanded rather than glove them. Her garden had been on the south terrace, and she had grown — he gripped this — she had grown *roses*, red ones, and a plum tree against the warm wall, and she had told him, kneeling in the dirt with a seedling cupped in her earth-brown hands, that a living thing only needed three things and patience was the largest of them. He could see the seedling. He could see her hands. He could—

*She's gone, Aldric,* the crown said softly, from inside his own skull, wearing the gentleness like a stolen coat. *She died nine years before I ever touched you. Everything you're holding onto is dust and wishfulness. Why do you torture yourself? Let me keep her for you. I remember her perfectly. I'll show you her whenever you like, exactly as she was, better than she was — I can give you back the smell of the thyme, the sound of her laugh, the plum harvest of your ninth summer, every minute, flawless, forever. All you have to do is stop fighting. Stop clinging to these rotting fragments. Give me the ruins and I will build you a cathedral.*

"No," Aldric whispered. His knuckles were white on his knees. "No. Yours wouldn't be her."

*How would you know?* The honey turned, just slightly, just at the edge, showing the glass beneath. *You couldn't tell mine from hers now. That's the truth you can't look at, little king. That's why you come to this ugly little yard — not for the roses. For the fear. Fear keeps you sharp, doesn't it? Fear is the last thing I haven't figured out how to sweeten.*

He breathed. In through the nose, out through the mouth, the way his fencing master had taught him, a hundred years ago, in another life. The trick — his one trick, discovered by accident, guarded like the last coal of a fire — was that the crown grew bored. It was patient beyond human measure, but it was not infinite, and when he gave it nothing — no rage, no despair, no bargaining, just a man sitting among weeds thinking about thyme — it eventually withdrew to whatever place it went, leaving him alone with the evening and the roses and the small, hard kernel of himself that he chose, every single time, to believe still existed.

Tonight, though, it lingered.

*A letter arrived today,* it said, idly, the way one mentions weather. *From the archives. Old business. Prophecies, would you believe — people still write them, apparently, like letters to the dead. One surfaced in a valley far from here, a valley with no magic in it at all. Isn't that curious? A book that should have been silent, speaking from the one place silence reigns.*

Something in Aldric went very still. Not because of the words — because of the *tone*. The crown was amused, but underneath the amusement ran something else, something thin and quick, and in seven years he had learned that the crown's rare slips of unease were worth more than all its honey.

*It names a Crane,* the crown continued, conversational now, probing. *A soldier. A woman, carrying blood that hasn't been seen in a century. Blood that knows things. Blood that remembers how I was—* It stopped. Actually stopped, mid-word, and the silence that followed was the loudest thing Aldric had ever heard.

Then, smoothly, as if the pause had never happened: *The Lord Protector wants her found, naturally. Books make martyrs. Martyrs make armies. You'll nod tomorrow when he says so.*

But Aldric barely heard the instruction. He was crouched inside his own skull, in the dark, turning over what he'd just caught — the slip, the almost-word. *How I was.* How it was what? Made? Bound? *Planted?*

For the first time in seven years, the crown had told him something it hadn't meant to. Small as a hairline crack in a dam — but he had learned from his mother's garden that cracks were how the water got in, and how the roots got out.

That night he dreamed, for the first time in years, a dream the crown did not narrate: his mother's south terrace, the plum tree heavy with fruit, and beneath it a woman he had never seen, dark-haired, road-worn, standing at the gate with a soldier's stride and a book under her arm — looking at the garden as if she already knew the way in.

When he woke, dawn had not yet come, and the crown was quiet, and for one full, unmeasured moment Aldric lay in the dark smiling — because whatever was coming, whatever Crane the prophecies had named, the crown was afraid of her.

And anything the crown feared was, at last, a friend.
