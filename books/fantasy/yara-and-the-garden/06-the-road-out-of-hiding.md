## 6. The Road Out of Hiding

Yara knew something was wrong before she smelled the smoke.

The orchard told her first. Memory-trees were sensitive things — they grew from soil steeped in recollection, and they reacted to strong feeling the way ordinary trees reacted to wind. All morning the pear rows had been shivering without a breath of air moving through them, their leaves rattling out small fragments of other people's summers: a child's laughter caught in bark, the taste of someone's wedding cake bleeding sweet into the shade. Fear, Yara realized, crouching to press her palm against the warm earth. Not hers. The land's. The land was afraid.

She stood in the third row with her hands still dirty from the morning's grafting and listened — not with her ears but with the roots, which ran deeper than any well and heard more than any spy. Somewhere east, past the honey farm and the millstream, hooves. Many hooves, moving too fast for farmers, too spread out for pilgrims.

And beneath them, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards: fire beginning.

"Paloma," Yara said aloud, though the mercenary was still asleep in the cottage, worn hollow by fever and memory-fruit. "Wake up."

* * *

The stranger had been with her nine days now. Nine days of fever, of muttering names Yara didn't recognize — Brannoc, the company, the Godless Valley — and of waking at odd hours with her hand on a knife that Yara kept having to talk her out of drawing. Nine days of watching the prophecy's arrival settle over the woman like a coat she couldn't take off. Paloma Crane did not weep and did not pray and did not thank anyone for anything, but she ate what was given her, and she asked sharp questions about roads, and Yara recognized in her the particular hunger of someone who had never once been allowed to want a thing openly.

Yara had hidden for eleven years. She knew every species of wanting there was.

Now Paloma stood in the cottage doorway, half-dressed, knife already in hand, hair loose and dark with sweat. "How many?"

"I don't know yet. Enough." Yara was already moving, pulling jars down from the rafters, tucking them into a satchel with the efficiency of long practice. "Scouts, if the roots are reading true. Riding the eastern lane toward the Aldern farm."

"The Alderns," Paloma said. It wasn't a question. She'd met them — old Maren Aldern had brought buttermilk up the hill twice since Yara's guest had stopped being delirious, and had pretended not to notice that the garden-witch suddenly had company. "They'll talk?"

"They're good people."

"That's not what I asked."

Yara closed her eyes briefly. Good people talked. Good people always talked; it was how good people survived, trading small truths to buy mercy from large cruelties. "Yes," she said. "They'll talk."

Then the wind shifted, and the smoke arrived ahead of the horses, and there was no more time for predictions.

* * *

From the crest of the orchard's southern slope they watched the Aldern farm burn.

It was not a battle. There was nothing to fight. Six riders in ash-grey livery — the usurper's colors, the color of a crown gone wrong — moved through the yard with the unhurried competence of men doing chores. One held a torch to the thatch while another kicked open the door. Two dragged something from the barn. Yara could not see clearly at this distance whether it was a person or a sack of grain, and some part of her would be grateful for that ambiguity forever.

Maren Aldern stood in her dooryard with her hands bound behind her, and she was not screaming. That was the worst of it, Yara thought. The old woman had lived through the last purge, thirty years ago, when hedge-witches from three valleys had been pulled up like weeds. She must have known exactly what was happening, and exactly how it would end, and she stood very straight and let them do it, because screaming would only have made her grandsons run, and running meant arrows.

The house went up with a soft whump, the way houses do, almost gently, as if it were only lying back to sleep.

Paloma's knuckles had gone white around the knife hilt. "Six riders," she said quietly. "I've killed six riders before breakfast."

"No."

"You don't know what I—"

"I know exactly what you are, Paloma Crane. You told me enough times while you were feverish." Yara didn't look away from the burning farm, because looking away felt like a betrayal of Maren Aldern, who deserved witnesses. "But you're still weak, and there may be more than six, and if you die on my hillside today then the prophecy dies with you, and everything those people are suffering down there will have been for nothing. Do you understand? Their deaths only mean anything if you live. That is a horrible arithmetic. I hate it. But I refuse to make it worse."

Paloma stared at her. For a moment Yara thought the mercenary might strike her, or laugh, or both. Instead she said, in a voice scraped raw: "You've done this before. Hid. Run."

"Eleven years," Yara said. "I planted this orchard the week after they burned the last garden-witch out of Vessa. Every tree in it is a memory somebody begged me to keep alive. And in eleven years no one has come up this road, because I have been so careful, so quiet, so *small*—" Her voice cracked on the word and she hated that too. She took a breath. "So. We are going to stop being small. Both of us. Starting now."

Below them, one of the grey riders turned his horse toward the orchard path.

* * *

There was one tree in the orchard older than all the rest, and it was not Yara's planting.

Her grandmother — and Yara used the word loosely, the way garden-witches did, for the woman who had taught her rather than the woman who bore her — had grown it from the memory of a drowning. That was what she'd told Yara once, sitting under its heavy shade with cider going sour in her cup. *Not her own drowning,* the grandmother had said. *A stranger's. A whole village watched a boy go under the millpond and none of them could swim, and the grief of forty helpless people soaked into the ground so deep that when I planted an apple pip there, it came up hungry.* The tree grew apples that tasted of cold water and panic and the strange blue peace at the bottom of things. Nobody ate them twice. But the tree itself was enormous, ancient beyond its years, and its roots went down further than anything else in the valley — further, Yara suspected, than the valley's dead magic went, down into whatever living dark fed all gardens everywhere.

A tree that remembered drowning could also remember hiding. Could remember, if you asked it properly, the sensation of holding something safe inside yourself while the world burned above.

"You want me to get *inside a tree*," Paloma said flatly.

"I want you to get inside the tree's memory. It's different." Yara was already kneeling at its base, her fingers working into the soil, finding the root-flare where the wood gave way to earth. "The roots make a hollow — a pocket of held space, like the eye of a storm. If I ask it to remember being a refuge instead of a grave, it can hold you. Nothing above ground will find you. Not dogs, not witch-hunters, not the crown's own scrying. You'll be a memory inside a memory."

"And if it remembers wrong? If it decides I'm the thing drowning?"

"Then it holds you very gently forever, and I'll be honest, that is not the strangest death available to either of us today." Yara looked up at her. Sunlight came through the burning haze and turned the whole sky the color of weak tea. "Paloma. I need you to trust me for about four minutes. I know you don't have practice at it."

Something moved behind the mercenary's eyes — the same thing Yara had seen in the fever nights, the flinch of a woman whose whole life had taught her that trust was just a slower form of ambush. But the hoofbeats were on the lane now, and the smoke was in Yara's throat, and Paloma Crane put her knife back in its sheath, which Yara understood was the largest gift she had ever been offered by another human being.

"Four minutes," Paloma said. "If I suffocate in an apple tree, I'm haunting you."

"Noted. Now breathe out, and give me your hands."

* * *

The tree took her the way the sea takes a diver — reluctantly at first, then all at once.

Yara felt it through her buried fingers: the great roots flexing, the wood remembering. She poured everything she had into the asking. *Remember the seed-coat. Remember being small and safe and wrapped in brown silence before the world ever found you. Remember that you once held a whole future folded inside yourself, and no frost, no axe, no fire touched it.* The roots groaned. Bark split along the trunk in seams that were not wounds but doors, and Paloma sank into them — actually sank, her boots vanishing into wood-grain, her face going calm and far away, until only her eyes showed, wide and dark and trusting, and then not even those.

The seams closed. The tree stood ordinary again, vast and green and slightly trembling, an old apple tree among pear trees, nothing worth a second glance.

Yara sat back on her heels, shaking. She had never done that. Eleven years of quiet tending, of coaxing, of growing memories the way other people grew vegetables, and she had never once asked a tree to become a fortress, never bent a living thing so hard against its nature. It had cost her something. She could feel the absence already, a hollowness behind her ribs like a tooth drawn. Magic always took payment; her grandmother had said that too, and had added, with the smile Yara still dreamed about: *Pay gladly, and it costs less.*

Hoofbeats crunched into the orchard gate.

* * *

There were two of them, not six — the others still busy at the farm, or spreading along the valley. Young, both of them, in ash-grey coats that fit badly, the way borrowed cruelty always fits badly. The nearer one had a brand on his cheekbone, the crown's mark, still pink and new. He looked barely old enough to shave. He looked at Yara kneeling in the dirt among her trees, and he looked frightened, and that frightened her more than a hard man would have.

"Witch," he said. It wasn't an accusation so much as a question he'd been ordered to ask.

"Gardener," Yara corrected, and rose slowly, showing him empty, dirty hands. "Can I help you, soldier?"

"There was a report. A woman. Fevered, wounded, come up from the south." His eyes moved across the orchard, and Yara watched him look at the pear rows and see nothing but fruit. "Prophet-bearer. The general wants—"

"The general wants a lot of things," Yara said mildly. "I'm alone here. Have been for years. You can search the cottage if you like; mind the fermenting jars on the left shelf, they bite." Let him search. Let him find the fever rags, the second cup, the evidence of a guest — a guest who had recovered and walked on, that was the story, a mercenary passing through. People believed stories shaped like the ones they already carried.

But the young soldier wasn't listening. He had stopped at the old apple tree, and his head had tilted, and Yara felt the blood leave her face.

"This one's strange," he said. "It smells like—" He frowned, reaching up toward a low branch, toward an apple that hung there fat and cold and full of someone else's drowning. "Like water. Like a pond in winter. Why does it smell like—"

"Don't eat that," Yara said, too fast, too sharp, and saw the wariness bloom in his face like ink in water.

He drew the apple down. Turned it in his fingers. Looked at her.

Behind his shoulder, his companion laughed nervously and said something about witches' fruit, about stories his gran told, and the young soldier smiled — but he didn't put the apple down. And Yara stood in her own orchard with her empty hands and calculated, with terrible clarity, how fast she could reach the sickle in the grass, and what it would make her if she did, and how many seconds Paloma had inside the tree if Yara died here among the pears.

The apple gleamed. Cold water. Panic. The blue peace of the bottom.

The soldier raised it toward his mouth —

— and the orchard screamed.

Every tree at once. Every memory in every branch, released in a single wave of sound and scent and overwhelming presence: wedding-cake sweetness and funeral lilies and a child's laughter and an old man's last winter and forty kinds of grief and joy, all of it pouring off the trees like a burst dam, and the two soldiers staggered, clutching their heads, and the young one dropped the apple as if it had burned him, and the horses at the gate screamed too, rearing, tearing their pickets free, and through it all Yara heard — or imagined she heard, or perhaps did not imagine at all — a voice coming up through the ground, through the roots, through the oldest tree:

*Run. Now. While they're drowning. RUN.*

* * *

They fled north along the drover's track with the sun still high and the smoke of the Aldern farm rising behind them like a black banner.

Yara had taken nothing but the satchel of jars, the seed-case strapped to her back — eleven generations of seeds, each one carrying a memory that could be regrown anywhere, her whole inheritance in a leather roll — and one last act before leaving, done quickly and without letting herself think about it. She had laid her hand on the old apple tree's trunk and said, aloud, in front of two groaning soldiers who would carry the tale whether they wanted to or not: *"Thank you. Grow well. Tell them what you held."* An open declaration. A witch claiming her work in front of witnesses, the way no hedge-witch in thirty years had dared.

Her first act of open defiance. It should have felt larger, she thought. It mostly felt like walking away from home.

Paloma walked beside her in silence for the first mile. The mercenary had emerged from the tree pale and silent as a ghost, wood-shavings in her hair, and had not spoken since except to say *which way*, and Yara was beginning to wonder if the silence was anger when Paloma finally said:

"It showed me something. In there." Her voice was rough, as if the tree had kept her somewhere damp. "While it was holding me. It shared — I don't know, one of its apples' worth of memory. A boy drowning. A village watching." She swallowed. "And then it showed me you. Planting it. Your grandmother's hands over your hands. You were crying."

"The day I planted it, yes."

"Why?"

Yara walked several paces before answering. The track climbed through gorse and bracken; behind them the smoke thinned; ahead the hills folded northward toward Vessa and the lowlands and everything that would try to kill them.

"Because it was the first thing I ever grew entirely alone," she said at last. "And because I understood, kneeling there with my hands in the ground, that grief isn't the opposite of growth. It's the *soil* of it. Everything I've grown since came out of that afternoon." She glanced sideways at Paloma. "You'll think that's foolish."

"I think," Paloma said slowly, "that I spent nine days in your house waiting for you to want something from me. Everyone wants something. My whole company, my whole god, my whole family — everyone I've ever known had a use for me and called it love." She was quiet a moment. "You hid me in a tree and gave up your home and never once asked me for anything. So either you're the best liar I've ever met, or—"

"Or?"

"Or I don't know what. I don't have a word for it yet."

They walked on. Gorse scratched at their legs. Somewhere far behind, the soldiers would be finding their horses and deciding what to report — *a witch, an orchard that screamed, a tree that smelled of drowning* — and the report would travel faster than they could walk. By tomorrow, every hunter the usurper owned would know which way they'd gone.

"So we're partners now," Yara said. "Wary ones. Is that how you'd name it?"

"I'd name it a debt," Paloma said. "And I pay my debts. Ask anyone I've ever fought for." Then, grudgingly, as if the words cost coin: "But — partners. Until the debt's paid."

"Until it's paid," Yara agreed, and did not add that she intended to be owed for a very long time.

They crested the ridge as the light failed. Below, the northern road unspooled toward the free cities, toward councils and mercenaries and armies — toward Vessa, where Paloma said the merchant princes counted coins while the usurper counted legions. Yara adjusted the seed-case on her back and felt its weight, eleven generations of remembered lives riding between her shoulders.

"We'll need allies," Paloma said. "Real ones. Armies, walls, gold. The kind of things that don't grow in gardens."

Yara looked at the road, and at the woman beside her, and felt the hollow place behind her ribs where the tree had taken its payment — and something else settling into that hollow, something with roots.

"No," she said. "But they grow from seeds. And I happen to be carrying quite a few."

Far to the south, unseen, the old apple tree stood alone in the emptied orchard, holding in its heartwood one final memory it had not been asked to keep: the exact shape of the moment a garden-witch chose to burn her own hiding-place down — and the taste, new to it, of hope.
