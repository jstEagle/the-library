## 7. The Free Cities Disagree

Vessa smelled of salt and money.

Paloma had been in a hundred cities in her twenty-six years, and she had learned to read them the way she read terrain — by what they chose to defend. Vessa defended its harbors with chained booms and ballistae; it defended its warehouses with private guards paid better than any soldier of the line. What it did not defend was its gates. The great eastern gate stood open all day and most of the night, because closing it cost trade, and trade was the only god Vessa had ever answered to.

"Godless city," Yara said quietly, walking beside her through the press of carts and fishwives and sailors shouting prices in three languages. "I mean that kindly."

"I know what you mean." Paloma adjusted the strap of her pack. Her shoulder still ached where the memory-tree's roots had gripped her during their flight from the orchard, a deep bruise that bloomed violet-black down her arm. "It's not godlessness. It's worse. It's *confidence*. They think coin can buy them out of anything."

"And can't it?"

"Not this." She thought of the archivist dying on the valley floor with the prophecy book open across his knees. She thought of the burned farmstead they'd passed two days south, its chimney standing alone in a field of ash like a finger pointing at the sky. "Coin doesn't stop an army that's already decided you're heretics. Coin just decides which day they arrive."

The Council Hall of Vessa rose at the city's heart like a fat merchant's purse turned to stone — squat, ornate, and utterly without grace. Its steps were marble imported from quarries four hundred miles inland, each block carved with the name of the family that had donated it. Paloma climbed those steps counting names and found she'd run out of patience before she ran out of stairs.

At the top, flanked by bronze doors tall enough to admit a siege engine, stood two guards in lacquered scale. They looked at her — at the sword, at the mercenary company's broken-clasp badge stitched at her collar, at the road dust she hadn't bothered to shake off — and looked through her.

"We have a petition," Paloma said. "For the Council of Nine."

"The Council hears registered factors, guildmasters, and sworn envoys," said the left guard, in the bored tone of a man reciting something he'd said forty times before breakfast. "Not sellswords."

"Sellswords saved your sister-city's granaries from the river raiders six years ago," Paloma said. "The Silver Chain Company. Under contract to your own harbor office. You're wearing boots made with leather we escorted through the Kellin Pass."

The guard glanced down at his boots, then back up, unbothered. "Then you'll know how contracts work. This one requires a seal."

Yara stepped forward, and something in the quality of her stillness made both guards shift. She was a small woman, brown as good bread, with dirt permanently worked into the creases of her knuckles, but there was a way she held herself — rooted, was the only word for it — that made men instinctively check the ground beneath their feet.

"We carry no seal," Yara said. "We carry word of a king who is prisoner in his own skull, and an army marching under colors that eat the people they conquer. We carry a book a century old that names what's coming. And we are asking to be heard *before* the smoke reaches your harbor, when hearing us will be cheaper by every measure you people count in."

A silence followed, the kind that meant the words had landed somewhere soft.

From within the hall came a voice, dry as ledger paper: "Let them in. Contempt is best administered in person."

* * *

The Council Chamber was a half-moon of tiered benches overlooking a floor of polished stone, so that petitioners stood literally below the men and women who judged them. Paloma counted nine seats, seven occupied. The two empty ones belonged, according to a whispering clerk, to families currently summering in the hills, safe from the war that was, as yet, merely a rumor with a body count.

The speaker was Master Ondrel Quay, a man whose face seemed assembled entirely for the purpose of expressing skepticism. He wore gray silk and a chain of office heavy enough to hang someone.

"A hedge-witch," he said, after Yara had spoken for perhaps four minutes — about Aldric, about the crown grown into bone, about the orchard, about Sarel Vane's terms and the garrisons that accepted them and were never seen again. "And a mercenary of a company whose god abandoned it before either of you was born. Petitioning the Free Cities to raise arms against General Vane, whose forces outnumber everything we could field by — I believe your own account suggests — five to one."

"Six to one now," Paloma said. "His army grows every week. That was rather my point."

"Your point," said a woman on the third tier, Mistress Corvane of the Salt Guild, "is that we should spend our reserves, our levies, and our trading relationships on the word of two strangers, one of whom claims a fruit tree told her secrets."

"The tree didn't tell me secrets," Yara said evenly. "I grew the tree. It remembers what I put in it. If you'd like, I can grow one that remembers your grandmother, and you can ask her yourself whether I'm lying about what magic does."

A ripple went around the benches — part laughter, part unease. Paloma watched the councilors sort themselves by their reactions and marked the ones who laughed too quickly. Fear dressed as mockery. She'd seen it in green recruits watching their first battle lines form.

"General Vane has offered Vessa generous terms," Master Quay continued. "Continued autonomy. Continued trade. A token garrison. Why should we bleed for a king none of us crowned, in a capital that has never once favored free cities over its own lords?"

"Because his terms are how he eats you," Paloma said.

She stepped forward onto the polished stone, letting her voice carry the way she carried it across battlefields, pitched low enough to make people lean in.

"I've served contracts in nine cities and I've seen what 'generous terms' look like afterward. Vane takes the granary first — always the granary — and calls it provisioning. Then the armory, called disarmament. Then he asks for lists of able-bodied men, called census. And somewhere in the third month, the crown's agents arrive, and the garrison that surrendered goes into the harbor with stones around its neck, and the terms turn out to have been a menu." She paused. "You've had letters from Marrow's Bend. Three weeks ago. Ask yourselves why no answer ever came back."

Mistress Corvane frowned. "Marrow's Bend is a fishing town. Their scribes are illiterate drunks—"

"Their harbormaster corresponded with your guild twice a season for eleven years," Paloma said. "I escorted those letters myself in another life. Silence is an answer, mistress. It's just not the one you want."

Something shifted in the chamber — a discomfort, a few glances exchanged. But Master Quay folded his hands atop his ledger-strewn desk and smiled the smile of a man who has heard many true things and found none of them profitable.

"Even granting all of it," he said, "even granting that the usurper intends exactly what you say — you offer us nothing but warning. No host. No coin. No claim on any throne. Two women and a dead man's book. The Free Cities survive, madam, because we do not gamble. We arbitrage. And there is no return on the investment you propose."

"The return is that you exist in ten years."

"There is no *return*, madam. There is only risk." He gestured, almost gently, toward the doors. "Vessa thanks you for your counsel. The Council will deliberate."

"That's a no," Paloma translated.

"That is a *deliberation*."

"It's a no wearing a nicer coat." She felt heat climbing her throat, the old battlefield anger, useless here, dangerous here. Beside her, Yara's hand brushed her elbow — a light touch, grounding, the way one steadies a horse before it bolts. *Not yet,* the touch said. *Not here.*

But the chamber wasn't finished with them. From the highest tier, a seat Paloma had taken for empty resolved into a figure — an old woman, thin as a whip handle, who had sat so still among the shadows of the colonnade that Paloma had genuinely missed her.

"Before they go," the old woman said, and her voice, though papery, cut the room like a blade through wet canvas. "I've a question for the mercenary."

Every head turned. Even Quay straightened.

"Mistress Halloway," he began, "the Council agreed your seat was honorary—"

"The Council agrees to a great many things it doesn't understand." The old woman leaned forward into the light. She wore no silk. Her clothes were plain wool gone silver-gray with age, and her hands, folded on the rail before her, were a map of old scars. "My question, girl, is simple. You say you've fought. You say you know what's coming. Prove you're worth listening to. Not with words — everyone in this room can buy words." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of someone remembering something. "Fight for it."

* * *

They fought in the Court of Chains behind the Council Hall — an old arena where, in less enlightened decades, disputes between guilds had been settled by hired champions, a practice officially abolished and privately maintained, because Vessa never truly abolished anything that entertained it.

Word spread through the city with the speed of anything scandalous. By the time Paloma stripped off her pack and coat, the galleries were full: merchants in silk, dockworkers in canvas, children perched on the shoulders of men who pretended not to be enjoying themselves.

Her opponent was chosen before she reached the sand. Torv Anselm, they called him — the Council's champion, a former pit-fighter from the southern rings, a head taller than Paloma and built like the marble pillars upstairs come to life. He carried a blunted longsword and a shield, and he moved through his warm-up forms with the unhurried economy of a man who had done this perhaps sixty times and lost perhaps twice.

"He'll try to tire you," Yara said, lacing Paloma's bracer for her. Her fingers were quick and sure, a gardener's fingers. "He's younger, heavier. He thinks time is on his side."

"Time isn't on anyone's side lately." Paloma rolled her shoulder, wincing at the bruise. "Yara. If I go down out there—"

"You won't."

"If I go down, don't let them throw us out before nightfall. Get the book in front of Mistress Halloway. She fights like someone who remembers real wars. She'll read it right if anyone will."

Yara tied off the strap and held Paloma's wrist a moment longer than the work required. "Paloma Crane. You survived the valley spitting you out like a swallowed stone. You survived my trees, which is more than most manage. Go lose beautifully."

"That's your tactical advice? *Lose beautifully?*"

"My advice is that you cannot beat him, so stop planning to." Yara's dark eyes were calm as turned soil. "Plan to make him wish he'd stayed home. There's more than one kind of winning, and merchants only understand one of them. Show them the other."

Torv Anselm met her at the center of the sand. Up close he was broader than he'd seemed, with a nose flattened by decades and eyes gone patient and flat as a butcher's.

"No hard feelings, little wolf," he rumbled. "Council pays me either way."

"Good," Paloma said. "Then you won't mind if I don't hold back out of politeness."

The bell rang — an actual ship's bell, salvaged, its rope worn glossy by generations of hands.

He came on exactly as Yara had predicted: steady, pressing, content to walk her backward step by step, letting his weight do the work while her arms absorbed blows that rang up her bones like hammer strikes on a bell. Blunted steel still bruised. Still broke ribs, if it landed clean. Paloma gave ground, gave ground, circled, and listened with her whole body the way she'd taught recruits to listen — to the rhythm of his breathing, the hitch before his committed strikes, the tiny telegraph of his lead shoulder.

He was good. Genuinely good. Sixty matches and two losses, and she could see why: he never got excited, never chased, never overextended. He was a wall that walked.

Walls, however, were built for sieges that ended. And Paloma Crane had spent her entire life in a valley where nothing ended — where patience wasn't a virtue but the water she swam in. She had waited out storms that lasted nine days. She had waited out a silence from God that lasted a century. She knew things about waiting that a man with sixty victories in an arena had never needed to learn.

So she stopped trying to win.

She stopped attacking altogether. She flowed. Every strike he threw, she was already leaving; every push, she turned aside and turned him slightly with it, spending almost nothing, a leaf in his current. The gallery began to murmur. He frowned. He pressed harder, spent more, breathed deeper — and she let him. Ten minutes. Fifteen. The sun crawled. Sweat cut pale lines through the dust on his shoulders.

In the twentieth minute, impatient at last — a champion's oldest wound, vanity nicked by time — Torv Anselm overcommitted. Just a fraction. Just a half-step too far into a finishing blow aimed at her ribs, certain she was spent.

She wasn't.

Paloma dropped under it, inside his guard — the one place his reach was worthless — and drove her blunted tip up into the joint of his sword arm, then pivoted with the momentum and put him down onto the sand with a hip-throw that used his own exhausted mass against him. She landed knee on his chest, blade resting against the hollow of his throat, and held there, chest heaving, while the Court of Chains went silent as a held breath.

She could feel his heartbeat under her knee. Fast. Slowing. Steadying.

"Well fought, little wolf," he wheezed.

"Don't get up," she said. "I don't want to do it twice."

She rose and offered her hand. After a moment, he took it, and let her pull him up, and something passed between them in that grip that the whole gallery saw and understood: respect, the only currency that couldn't be minted.

Then she turned to the council box, blood singing, ribs screaming, and raised her voice so the whole court heard it.

"A draw!" someone shouted from the docksman's gallery, because technically the bell had rung before the throw, and technically—

"She won," said Mistress Halloway, rising from her seat with the creak and grind of old joints, "and I'll thank the Council to note that she won by refusing to fight the battle her opponent wanted. Which is precisely the war she's proposing we fight." The old woman's gaze swept the tiers, and councilors who would not meet Paloma's eyes met hers. "I was at the Broken Bridge in my youth. I buried a husband and two sons under the flagstones of this hall. I have spent fifty years telling myself the Free Cities' caution was wisdom." She picked up a cane that Paloma realized, with a jolt, had been a spear shaft once. "It was fear with good bookkeeping."

Master Quay's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"The Council," he said at last, in the voice of a man reading his own eulogy, "will hear the petition in full."

* * *

They heard it in full. Three hours of it — the book passed hand to hand along the tiers (Yara hovering like a hawk over a nest), the maps unrolled, the dates laid against grain shipments and troop movements until even the arithmetic-deniers went quiet. In the end, Vessa being Vessa, they did not pledge an army. They pledged something better and worse: a contract. Arms, armor, harbor access, a subsidy for raising mercenaries, and formal recognition of King Aldric's lawful claim — *contingent upon demonstrated results*. First results due within the month.

"Which means," Paloma said afterward, standing in the emptying court with the contract's wax seal still warm in her hand, "they've handed me a leash and called it a gift."

"They've handed you a start." Yara nodded toward the sand, where Torv Anselm was waiting, arms crossed. Behind him, filing into the court through the dockworkers' gate, came perhaps thirty men and women with the callused hands and direct stares of soldiers between contracts. "And it appears the start comes with volunteers."

Anselm stopped before them and inclined his head — a real bow, shallow but honest.

"Twenty-two years in these pits," he said. "Fighting other men's arguments. I find I'd like to fight one of my own." He jerked his chin at the newcomers. "That's the harbor watch's second shift, plus whoever else heard you talk sense today. Word's moving. It moves fast in a port town." His flat eyes flicked to the contract in her hand, then to her face. "Question is, who's giving the orders?"

Paloma looked at the thirty faces. At Yara, solid at her shoulder. At the contract, the first thing anyone had ever given her that weighed more than coin.

She had spent her life taking orders — from commanders, from contracts, from a silent god she'd kept faith with anyway. The weight settling onto her now was different. It fit differently. It fit like something she might have been carrying toward all along, down all those dull, safe years in the valley.

"I am," she said. "Get me their names. All of them. And send riders — I need to write some letters tonight." She was already turning toward the gate, toward the dusk and the harbor lights and the enormous distance between here and everything that mattered. "There's a man named Brannoc who owes me a very long conversation."

Behind them, unnoticed, Mistress Halloway stood at the edge of the sand, reading the prophecy book by torchlight — and her thumb had stopped, pressed flat against a page near the end, on a line written a hundred years ago in a hand Paloma would soon learn to recognize:

*When the Crane stands before councils of coin and is refused, she will learn that refusal is the first door.*

*Beyond it waits the commander who hates her name.*
