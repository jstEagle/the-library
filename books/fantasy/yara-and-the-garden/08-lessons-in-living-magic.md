## 8. Lessons in Living Magic

Yara woke before the sun, as she always did, and lay a moment listening to the orchard breathe.

It was not a figure of speech. The trees breathed. In the grey hour before dawn, when the dew was still deciding whether to be rain or light, she could hear the slow swell of it — leaves opening their thousand small mouths, roots drawing water up through the dark in long patient sips. Most people thought her orchard grew memories because of some trick of soil or spell, something she had done. The truth was humbler and stranger: the trees simply remembered everything they drank, and everything they drank passed through her hands first.

She dressed, braided back her hair with the silver thorn-pin her mother had worn, and went down into the rows.

The pear tree over the south wall was still holding what Paloma Crane had left in it — the mercenary had slept beneath its branches her first fevered night, and the tree had drunk her dreams from the ground like spilled wine. Yara pressed her palm to the bark and let one fruit's worth rise to meet her: smoke and iron and a valley where nothing grew, nothing sang, nothing answered. A silence so complete it had a texture, like felt laid over the ears. And beneath the silence, faint as a heartbeat through a stone floor—

Yara pulled her hand back.

There it was again. That thing beneath the silence. She had been refusing to look at it for six days now, the way one refuses to look at a wound that has begun to change color.

*Later,* she told herself, as she had told herself every morning. *Teach her first. Understand it after.*

The cottage smelled of barley porridge and last night's lamp oil. Paloma was already awake — of course she was; mercenaries woke like knives coming out of a drawer, all at once and pointed at the world — sitting at the table sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

"You're up," Yara said.

"Your rooster started an argument with the dawn. I took the dawn's side."

"He always loses." Yara ladled two bowls. "Eat. Today you learn why my pears remember things."

Paloma ate the way she did everything, efficiently and without complaint, but Yara had learned the woman's silences in the week since the farm burned and the two of them fled east together, sleeping in ditches and hedgerows until the orchard swallowed them home. Paloma's silences were load-bearing. She built things inside them — doubts mostly, and plans, and a grief for Brannoc and the company that she carried like a stone sewn into her coat lining.

"The usurper knows about this place," Paloma said finally. "Scouts burned the Hollis farm looking for prophecy-bearers. How long do we have?"

"Seasons, if we're lucky. Weeks if we're not." Yara sat across from her. "Which is why we're not waiting to be lucky. If you're going to carry what you're carrying — the blood, the name, whatever your grandmother wrote your name into that book for — then you need to stop being afraid of it. Fear makes magic sloppy."

"I'm not afraid of magic. I'm afraid of *wanting* it."

Yara looked at her a long moment. That was the most honest thing the mercenary had said since arriving, and she had said it while looking at her porridge.

"Good," Yara said. "Then you'll be careful. Come on."

* * *

The teaching garden lay behind the cottage, walled in old brick gone soft with moss — a square of tended earth no bigger than a threshing floor, but dense with growing things arranged like a grammar lesson. Row by row, Yara walked her through them.

"This is patience." A low bush of blue-green needles. "Rosemary grown from a cutting of a cutting going back nine generations. It remembers every hand that ever pruned it, which is why I never prune it carelessly. This—" a sprawl of white flowers nodding on thread stems "—is forgiveness. Chamomile, mostly, but it grows sweeter here than anywhere else because I apologized to it once, years ago, for planting it in bad soil. It never forgot. Or rather — it never stopped remembering me apologizing."

"That's not how plants work," Paloma said.

"No," Yara agreed pleasantly. "That's how *this* garden works. Everything alive keeps a record. Flesh keeps it in scars, wood keeps it in rings, water keeps it in — well, that's a longer lesson. Living magic isn't making things do what you want. It's learning to read what they already know, and asking nicely when you want them to know something new. Watch."

She knelt beside a bare patch of earth and pressed both hands flat to the soil. She did not chant. She did not gesture. She only listened — listened until she could feel the seed lying dormant three fingers down, a hard little knot of patience waiting for permission — and then she gave it. Warmth. Water rising. The green thought uncoiling in the dark.

A shoot broke the surface between her palms, unfurled, climbed, budded, opened. A marigold, whole and nodding, in the space of ten breaths.

Paloma stared. "Do it again."

"Your turn."

"I don't—"

"Kneel."

Paloma knelt. She planted her hands in the dirt the way she'd have gripped a sword hilt, which was the first problem, and Yara said so. "You're not taking it prisoner. You're introducing yourself."

"I don't know how to introduce myself to dirt."

"You've been doing it your whole life. You just called it marching." Yara moved behind her, adjusted the angle of her wrists. "Close your eyes. Find the seed. Don't reach — *listen*. It's small and it's asleep and it has been waiting its whole existence for exactly this moment. All you have to do is tell it the wait is over."

For a long time there was nothing but wind in the rosemary and the distant idiocy of the rooster. Then Yara felt it — a shudder go through the ground, through the walls, through the soles of her own knees, like the first tremor of something vast turning over in its sleep.

The seed sprouted. Then it kept sprouting.

The shoot came up thick as her thumb and kept thickening. It split, branched, split again, leaves unfolding enormous and dark green and glossy as lacquer, climbing past knee height, past waist height, stems groaning, the woody base swelling into something halfway to a trunk. Buds burst open along every branch — marigold blossoms the size of dinner plates, orange shading into red shading into a bruised purple that marigolds had no business knowing. The flowers opened all at once with a sound like wet silk tearing, and the smell hit the back of Yara's throat, sweet and wrong, sweet the way rot is sweet.

And still it grew. Vines were reaching for the wall now, curling around the brick, and Yara saw with cold clarity that the tendrils hadn't found the wall — they were *searching*, sweeping the air in slow hungry arcs, questing for anything living to climb, to wrap, to take.

"*Stop,*" Yara said, and put her hands over Paloma's, and poured herself into the ground like a dam breaking — *enough, enough, sleep, rest, that's all, you're done* — until the great plant shuddered, sighed, and stood still, towering over them both, absurd and magnificent and quietly terrifying.

Paloma opened her eyes. Her hands were shaking under Yara's.

"What did I do?"

"You asked it to grow." Yara sat back on her heels. Her heart was going like a war drum and she made very sure none of it reached her face. "And it grew. Very enthusiastically."

"It's a flower. It looks like it wants to eat the chickens."

"Yes." Yara stood, brushed off her knees, studied the thing. Up close the petals were veined with red, and the red moved — slowly, slowly, circulating, like blood remembering itself. "Let's call that a first lesson in restraint."

* * *

They spent the afternoon failing in instructive ways.

Yara had her try the simplest workings — coaxing a wilted lettuce upright, waking a bed of sleepy onions, encouraging the beans to climb their poles. Each time the same thing happened. The lettuce stood up and then doubled in size overnight-tough in a single hour. The onions woke and kept waking, tops whipping in a wind that wasn't there. The bean vines shot up the poles and then kept going, up past the pole tops into empty air, twining around each other in a green helix that creaked audibly in the evening quiet.

By dusk the teaching garden looked like the aftermath of a very polite riot.

"It's me," Paloma said. She was standing in the middle of it, arms crossed, watching a cabbage swell gently against its own leaves. "Whatever I'm doing wrong, it's not technique. You touched my hands. You know it's not technique."

"It's not technique," Yara admitted.

"So what is it? What am I?"

Yara looked at her. Sunburned, exhausted, streaked with good brown soil to the elbows, more alive than any godless woman had a right to be. And underneath — there it was again, the thing she'd felt in the pear tree, the thing she felt now radiating off Paloma's skin like heat off a forge. Not absence. Not emptiness. The godless valley had taught everyone, Paloma included, to read her blood as a blank page.

But Yara could read. And the page wasn't blank.

It was *bound*. Tied shut with something old and deliberate and loving, the way you bind a book you cannot bear anyone to read — the way you muzzle something not because it's wicked but because its voice would shatter windows. Paloma didn't lack a god. Paloma carried one, folded down to the size of a whisper, sealed under a century of silence, and every working she attempted cracked the seal another hairline, and the power leaked through the crack like light through a shuttered door, and everything it touched grew too fast and too hungry because a god's attention is not a gentle thing even in droplets.

Her grandmother hadn't cut the Cranes off from the divine. She had wrapped the divine in swaddling clothes and buried it alive in her descendants, generation after generation, to keep it — or keep something safe *from* it — and Paloma had spent her whole life resenting the silence without once suspecting she was the silence's keeper.

Yara turned this over inside herself, carefully, the way she turned a seedling out of its pot.

If she told Paloma tonight, the mercenary would want to tear the binding off immediately. Yara knew her that well already. She'd dig into her own blood with those competent hands, and either the binding would hold and scar her, or it wouldn't hold and a century-old god would come up out of her like floodwater through a broken levee — here, in the garden, in the cottage, in the world, with the usurper's scouts already burning farms two valleys west.

Or Yara could say nothing. Could teach her slowly, could watch the seal, could learn what it was made of before anyone touched it. Could keep the one secret that mattered from the one person who deserved it, for her own good, the way Yara's mother had kept secrets from her, the way secrets became inheritances, the way love curdled into custody if you weren't careful.

*She'll forgive you,* said the part of Yara that wanted to keep her safe.

*No, she won't,* said the part of Yara that had been betrayed before and remembered it in her hands.

"She won't *what*?" Paloma asked, and Yara realized with a lurch that she had spoken part of it aloud.

"Nothing. Thinking about supper." She gestured at the monstrous marigold, the bean helix, the cabbage straining at its seams. "We should harvest some of this before it achieves sentience."

Paloma laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter — and went to cut the greens. And Yara stayed where she was a moment longer, one hand resting on the great marigold's rough stem, feeling the god's leak-light pulse faintly in its veins, and made her choice the way all cowardly choices are made: slowly, silently, and telling herself it was mercy.

*Soon,* she promised the seal, and the girl, and maybe the god. *When I understand it. When it won't break you.*

Behind her, Paloma paused with her knife half-drawn through a cabbage stem. She was looking east, toward the road, toward the low hills where the evening smoke rose — too much smoke, and too black, and from the wrong direction for any farmstead's cooking fire.

"Hazel," Paloma said quietly, using the false name they'd traveled under, though they were alone. "How far is the nearest village?"

"Four miles. Why?"

"Because I can smell that fire," Paloma said, "and four miles is too close."

* * *

They didn't sleep. They worked by lamplight, Yara digging at the roots of the memory-pear while Paloma stood watch on the wall with a bow she'd found in the loft, and somewhere past midnight Yara's fingers closed around what she'd been searching for — a taproot thicker than her arm, grown straight down through a hundred years of soil toward something far below, something deep and silent and bound.

The root was warm.

And against her palm, faint as a word spoken through a closed door, it whispered: *soon.*
