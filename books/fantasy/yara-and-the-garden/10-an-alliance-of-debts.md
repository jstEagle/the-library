## 10. An Alliance of Debts

The ledger was older than Paloma was, and it smelled like it — tallow smoke and old blood and the particular sourness of ink that had been wept over. She had carried it out of the Godless Valley in her saddlebag alongside the prophecy book, because her grandmother had taught her one lesson before anything else: *a debt unwritten is a debt unpaid.* The Crane family ledger held eleven years of favors, loans, saved lives, and quietly buried bodies. Every name in it owed something to a Crane.

She spread it across the table in the back room of the Drowned Bell, Vessa's least reputable tavern, and looked at what eleven years of competent violence added up to. Forty-one names. Some were dead. Some had skipped cities. Some would laugh in her face.

But forty-one names was more than anyone else in this war had bothered to count.

Yara sat across from her with a cup of wine she wasn't drinking, turning it slowly by the stem. "You're going to spend your whole inheritance," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm going to spend everyone else's debts." Paloma dipped her pen. "There's a difference. An inheritance you keep. Debts you collect."

"You sound like your grandmother."

Paloma's pen stopped above the page. The fruit from Yara's orchard still sat uneasily in her memory — the taste of pear and iron, and then her grandmother's hands, younger than any hands Paloma had ever known, writing the date of tonight into a book a century before it existed. She hadn't told Yara everything she'd seen in that fruit. Some of it she hadn't sorted out for herself yet.

"Good," she said finally. "Someone in my family should be worth sounding like."

* * *

The first three days taught her what a ragged host actually looked like when it was being born.

Denna Kell came first, because Denna Kell always came first when there was money or trouble, and Paloma was offering both. The Kell company had been reduced by the usurper's levies to nineteen spears and a bad attitude, and they arrived at the Drowned Bell smelling of river mud and marched straight through the taproom as if they owned it, which, given the state of their arrears with the tavern's owner, they nearly did.

"Crane." Denna dropped into the chair opposite, put her boots on the table, and studied the ledger upside down. "You're calling in the Vessa markers. All of them?"

"All that are alive to answer."

"That's maybe two hundred swords across five companies. Maybe less, once they hear who else you've invited." Denna's eyes flicked up, sharp as a drawn blade. "You're sending for Brannoc."

"I am."

"He'll come. He hates you too much not to." Denna turned her boots off the table and leaned forward. "But hear me, Crane. Whatever oath holds the Grey Company together, it isn't loyalty to you. Ask him about it before you put him behind you in a battle line. Or better — don't put him behind you at all."

"Paranoia doesn't win wars, Denna."

"No," Denna agreed, standing. "But it keeps captains alive long enough to try."

After Denna: the Ferro brothers, twin siege engineers whose debt dated to a bridge in the Salt Marches that Paloma had held for six hours against a tax revolt so they could finish demolishing it properly. After them, old Maren Ost, who commanded nothing anymore except the loyalty of forty veterans scattered through the free cities like seeds in fallow ground. After Maren, a boy of perhaps seventeen named Quill who owed Paloma his life twice and his tongue once, and who arrived breathless to say that the usurper's riders had been seen on the north road, burning farms and asking after a witch.

Yara heard that last part with her hands very still around her untouched cup.

"They'll find the orchard eventually," she said that night, on the tavern's sagging balcony, under a sky that Paloma still hadn't learned to read — the Godless Valley had no stars worth naming; its sky was a blank gray ceiling, and the stars here were so loud she sometimes couldn't sleep beneath them. "It doesn't matter if I'm there. They'll burn it anyway. That's what they do with growing things."

"Then we make sure there's something left worth returning to." Paloma said it more firmly than she felt. "You said the trees can be moved. Uprooted, carried, replanted."

"Some of them. If I do it right. If I have time." Yara looked out at the city, at its crooked towers and its harbor lights. "I spent thirty years making that garden, Paloma. Thirty years learning how to grow a memory instead of letting it rot. You don't just — carry thirty years in a cart."

"No," Paloma said. "You carry the roots, and you grow it again somewhere else. Isn't that what you told me? That's how gardens survive winters. Not by keeping every branch."

Yara was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lighter, wry, but underneath it something that might have been gratitude. "You've been eating my pears again."

"One or two."

"They're not sweets."

"They're better than sweets. They're the only honest thing anyone's shown me since I left the valley." Paloma leaned on the railing. Below them, the Drowned Bell's yard was filling with bedrolls and cook-fires, with the particular chaos of soldiers arriving without orders. Two hundred swords, Denna had guessed. Looking down at it, Paloma thought maybe two hundred and forty, if the weather held and the wine didn't run out. Against the usurper's armies, it was nothing. It was less than nothing.

But it was hers, and it was here, and it had come because she had asked.

"There's one name left," she said. "The biggest one."

"Brannoc."

"Brannoc." She said the name carefully, the way you'd handle a blade by the flat. "Eleven years ago he stood in front of the whole Grey Company and told me my blood was a curse on them. That my grandmother's bargain had made them godless beggars in a hole in the world. Then he shook my hand and wished me luck, which is the most confusing thing anyone's ever done to me."

"And you still sent for him."

"He's the best commander in the free cities. Better than me. And he owes me a life-debt from Rennick's Ford, and Brannoc pays his debts even when it chokes him." She paused. "Especially when it chokes him."

* * *

Brannoc came on the seventh day, alone, on foot, which surprised her. She had expected twenty retainers and a war-horse. Instead there was only a big grey-bearded man in a plain cloak, with a commander's slouch and a pilgrim's staff, standing in the Drowned Bell's doorway looking around the room as though measuring it for a coffin.

"The last time I saw you," he said, without greeting, "you were sixteen years old, and I told you to get out of the valley and never come back."

"You also told me the valley was the only safe place left in the world. One of those things turned out to be true."

He grunted — almost a laugh — and took the chair she kicked out for him. Up close, the years had written themselves deep into his face. The Brannoc of her memory had been a wall of a man, immovable. This one had been weathered, cracked, patched. But his eyes were the same: pale, patient, and missing nothing.

"You have a host forming," he said. "Two hundred-odd. Green spears, old veterans, and the Kells, who'd fight their own mothers if the price was right. And you mean to march them against Sarel Vane and whatever the crown has grown in the capital." He folded his hands on the table. "Say why I should care."

"A life-debt. Rennick's Ford."

"Paid," he said immediately. "Paid the day I pulled you out of the river after. We're square, girl. You know we're square."

"We're not." She opened the ledger to his page and turned it toward him. "Read it."

He read it. She watched his face while he did, and watched the color drain slowly out of it, and understood that whatever he had expected, it wasn't this.

*The Grey Company, sworn at Rennick's Ford, owes to Paloma Crane the debt of its founding. Let no commander refuse her call.*

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "That's not a standard entry. Who wrote that?"

"My grandmother did. Fifty-two years ago. Before you were born, Brannoc. Before your grandfather ever swore the Grey Company's first contract." She kept her voice level. "You want to talk about oaths? Talk about that one. Because I've been reading my family's papers, and I've learned that the Cranes never did anything without writing it down twice."

Brannoc stared at the page for a long time. Around them the tavern roared and sang, and the two of them sat inside the noise like stones inside a river.

"Your grandmother," he said at last, "ruined us."

"I know the story."

"You know the story the valley tells itself." His voice stayed low, but something underneath it had begun to shake, the way ice shakes before it goes. "A hundred years ago, the Grey Company had a god. A small one, a quiet one — a hearth-god, a road-god, whatever you want to call it. It answered prayers. It healed our wounded. When we went into battle, we went knowing that something vast and patient was watching over us, and I promise you, girl, you have never fought a battle until you've fought one with a god at your shoulder." His knuckles whitened on the table. "And then the Cranes made their bargain, and the silence fell, and the god stopped answering, and the company stopped being a company and became — this. Exiles. Beggars with good discipline. Hiding in a dead valley where magic can't follow, because the thing your family did to protect *its own blood* broke something in the world, and we've been living in the broken place ever since."

"My grandmother did it to protect—"

"To protect *Cranes*." The word cracked like a whip. Half the tavern glanced over; Brannoc lowered his voice, but only barely. "Every story you've been fed, every pretty reason, it all comes back to the same shape. The Cranes needed hiding. The Cranes needed silencing. The Cranes needed a century of other people's faith sacrificed to keep one bloodline safe. And now the prophecy comes due, and suddenly the whole world has to bleed so that the last Crane can walk out of her hole and be *important*." He pushed back from the table, breathing hard. "No. Find another way. I'd rather hang."

"Sit down."

To her own surprise, and visibly to his, he sat. Perhaps it was the ledger between them. Perhaps it was something older than either of them.

"You think I don't know what the silence cost?" Paloma said. "I grew up in it. I prayed to your silent god every night of my life while the rest of the company laughed at me for bothering. I know exactly what was taken, because I'm the only one left who ever missed it enough to grieve." She leaned forward. "But here's what I found in an orchard full of memories, Brannoc, and you're going to listen, because it's the truth whether you hate it or not: the binding wasn't mercy and it wasn't greed. My grandmother bound the god to hide a weapon. Something that can unmake the crown on Aldric's head. The silence was armor around that weapon, and the armor has been killing my family slowly for four generations, and I will probably die of it before this war ends. So spare me the story where the Cranes sat fat and safe while the world suffered. Nobody in my line has been safe since before your grandfather drew breath."

The tavern noise washed over them. Somewhere near the fire, Denna Kell was singing badly, on purpose.

Brannoc's anger hadn't gone — she could see it banked in him like coals — but something else had entered the room alongside it. Doubt, maybe. Or the beginning of grief, which looks the same from a distance.

"If that's true," he said slowly, "then your grandmother made slaves of us. Watchdogs. A century of service nobody asked us for."

"Yes."

"And you're asking me to serve again."

"I'm asking you to choose," Paloma said. "Which is what none of us got to do the first time. My grandmother decided for everyone — for you, for me, for the god himself. I'm deciding differently. I'm asking."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man confessing rather than commanding, he said:

"There's something you should know about the founding. Your grandmother wrote that entry in the ledger, but she didn't write the oath itself. The oath came first. Before Rennick's Ford. Before the ledger. Before the exile." He rubbed a hand across his jaw. "When the Grey Company was young, we weren't hired swords. We were sworn wardens. Our first contract wasn't with any city or any king. It was with the Crane family. To watch them. To guard them. And — gods forgive us — to make sure that whatever the Cranes bound, stayed bound."

The words landed in Paloma's chest like cold water.

"*Watched,*" she repeated. "Not protected."

"The words are close together in the old tongue. Deliberately so." Brannoc met her eyes, and there was no defiance left in his face, only a tired and terrible honesty. "Every commander of the Grey Company for a hundred years has known it and kept it from the ranks. Your grandmother knew it and paid us to keep it. We weren't your family's shield, Paloma. We were your cage. Me, your father, your grandmother's own guards — we were all jailers wearing the livery of friends. And when I told you to leave the valley at sixteen, it wasn't kindness. It was the oath. The binding was fraying, the signs were showing, and my duty was to move the prisoner's daughter beyond the walls before anyone noticed what she was."

Paloma discovered she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to hurt her hands. She made herself let go.

"So when you refused my call," she said, "it was the oath speaking. And when you came anyway—"

"I came because I'm tired, girl." For the first time since the doorway, something in his face softened — not into warmth, but into something rawer. "Tired of keeping a prison for people I've loved. Tired of serving a bargain made by frightened women a century dead. If your grandmother's binding breaks, then the oldest lie in my company dies with it, and maybe we can swear something new — something true — before I'm in the ground." He reached out and put one blunt finger on the ledger, on the entry in his grandmother-of-the-company's hand and hers. "So yes. I'll march with you. Not for the debt. For the chance to end the oath."

"Then welcome to the worst army ever assembled," Paloma said, and her voice almost held steady.

Almost.

* * *

They worked through the night, the two of them, converting a tavern back room into a war council. Brannoc knew supply lines the way Yara knew soil; by the third bell past midnight he had redrawn her muster rolls, cut her baggage train in half, and identified the four companies whose debts were worthless and the six whose debts were gold.

At some point near dawn, he paused over the map, quill hovering, and said without looking up:

"The valley. When this is done — if we live — someone has to go back and tell them. The company. What they were. What they were for."

"I'll go myself," Paloma said.

"They'll want to know if the god answers now."

Paloma thought of the gray ceiling of the valley sky, of the little shrine behind the armory where she had prayed alone for eleven years, of the wordless pressure she had felt since leaving — like a held breath the size of the world.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "But I'll ask."

Brannoc nodded once, satisfied, and bent back to the maps.

It was Quill who burst in at first light, white-faced, shaking rain off a rider's cloak he had no right to be wearing. "Captain — Captain Crane — riders on the north road. Usurper's colors. A full column, flying the black-and-gold, and they're not slowing for the gates." He gulped air. "They're not marching on Vessa, captain. Their herald says they're marching on *us*. They know about the host. They know about the witch. And the herald — he gave a name for whoever leads us." Quill's voice dropped, as if the word itself might summon something.

"He said to tell the Crane that the crown remembers her family. And that it's coming to collect a century of back prayers."

Outside, the dawn bells of Vessa began to ring — fast, urgent, the alarm pattern Paloma had hoped never to hear. Brannoc rose from the map table like a mountain deciding to move, and for one strange moment his eyes and hers met across the candlelight, jailer and prisoner both, allies at last.

"Get me the muster," he said. "All of it. Now."
