## 11. The Garden Beneath the Palace

The capital of Aldric's kingdom had a smell, and the smell was ash and lye.

Yara walked through its streets with a basket on her hip and a widow's shawl drawn over her hair, and she let the city's grief settle over her like soot. She had spent three days learning it — which gates opened at dawn, which guards took bribes in copper rather than silver, which alleys stank enough to make even hungry dogs avoid them. She had learned that the usurper's soldiers called this quarter the Cinder Ward, because when the old king's household had been purged seven years ago, they had burned the granaries and let the wind do the rest. She had learned that people here did not look at one another. That was the truest mark of an occupied city: not the banners, not the checkpoints, but the way a thousand souls could share a street and none of them would meet another's eyes.

Yara met no one's eyes either. She counted flagstones instead. She always counted flagstones. A garden-witch learned early that stone remembered pressure, and pressure remembered weight, and weight remembered everything that had ever crossed it. The paving under her boots was telling her a long, dull story of carts and coffins and marching feet, and Yara listened with half her mind while the other half navigated by the sun.

The palace rose ahead of her, grey granite gone black along its southern face where the fires had reached it. The usurper kept his court there now, though rumor said he rarely left the high tower — rumor said many things about General Vane's master, most of them whispered so quietly they were barely rumors at all. What Yara knew, from Paloma's mercenary contacts and from the fruit of her own trees, was simpler and worse: something lived in that tower that was not entirely a man, and it was fed by roots that ran deeper than anyone suspected.

She meant to find those roots.

* * *

Getting into the royal garden required less cunning than she'd expected and more patience than she liked. The garden lay behind the palace's eastern wing, walled, gated, and — according to every account she'd gathered — dead these seven years. The usurper's stewards had sealed it after the purge. No one tended it; no one entered. The gate was chained, but chains were for people who feared what was inside, and Yara had already begun to suspect the usurper feared nothing inside those walls precisely because he believed there was nothing left to fear.

She went over the wall at dusk, when the guard changed and the watchfires threw their light the wrong way. She was fifty-one years old and her knees informed her, in detail, that wall-climbing was a young woman's trade, but she dropped into the garden's dark without sound, crouched among dead stalks, and waited until her heart agreed to continue.

Then she breathed in, and nearly wept.

It was the smell. Seven years untended, and still — faint as a coin at the bottom of a well — the garden smelled of living soil. Not the mineral nothing of dead earth, not the sour reek of rot. Soil. Dark, complicated, breathing slowly the way soil breathes when something beneath it is alive.

She knelt and pressed both palms flat against the ground, the way she did every morning in her own orchard, greeting the roots before greeting the trees. And she felt it. Faint. Starved. But there — a pulse beneath the flagstones, slow as a sleeping heart, patient as winter wheat.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, you clever, stubborn thing."

The royal garden had been paved over. That was the purge she hadn't known about: not burned, not salted — paved. Flagstones laid edge to edge over every bed, every path, every root-run, as thorough a murder of a garden as stone could commit. Someone had wanted it not merely dead but entombed. And yet beneath the stone, in the dark, in the starving years, something had held on.

Yara moved through the garden on her knees, reading the stones. Here, where the paving buckled in a long ridge, a taproot had swelled against its ceiling and pushed. There, a seam between flagstones wore a crust of green — moss, real moss, fed by water that must seep down through some fault in the mortar. And near the garden's far corner, where an old sundial stood listing on its plinth, she found the rosebush.

Or what had been a rosebush. It grew up through a crack no wider than her thumb, a single cane, leafless, thorned, bent almost horizontal by years of growing in the only direction the crack allowed. It should have been dead. Everything about it said dead — grey bark, brittle tips, the whole gaunt architecture of a plant that had given up on leaves and settled for survival.

But when Yara laid her hand on the cane, the pulse jumped.

Not much. A stutter, like a frightened bird. But it turned toward her touch the way a seedling turns toward a window, and Yara felt tears start in her eyes and did not bother wiping them away. Seven years under stone. Seven years of darkness and drought and the slow poison of whatever the crown poured into this ground. And this one rose had kept a thread of itself alive, the way a prisoner scratches marks into a cell wall — not to escape, but to prove it still existed.

"I know," she murmured to it. "I know. I'm late."

* * *

Working magic in the capital was like shouting underwater. The crown's presence saturated everything — Yara had felt it since the city gates, a low wrongness in the ground, a silence with teeth. Her orchard's magic ran on memory and patience, slow spells woven over seasons, and here she had neither season nor safety. If she pulled too hard, whatever watched this ground would feel her the way a spider feels a fly testing the web.

So she did what gardeners do. She worked small.

From her basket she took a clay pot of orchard soil — soil that remembered rain, that remembered her hands, that remembered ten thousand mornings of being spoken to kindly. She crumbled it around the base of the rose cane, working it into the crack with her fingers, whispering the whole time. Not spells, exactly. Greetings. Introductions. *This is my dirt; it comes from trees that grow remembering; you are not alone anymore.*

Then she unwrapped the second thing she'd carried three hundred miles pressed against her ribs like a stolen child: a cutting from the memory-orchard, taken from the tree under whose roots Paloma Crane had once hidden from the usurper's scouts. A whip-thin shoot, barely alive itself after the journey, its leaves curled with thirst.

She planted it beside the rose, in the pocket of orchard soil, and gave it the last of her water flask.

And then she sat back on her heels in the ruined garden of a murdered king, in sight of the tower where something wearing a crown whispered to a man who used to be free, and she waited for dawn the way gardeners wait — which is to say, without any certainty at all, armed only with the knowledge that seeds come up or they don't, and that despair is just impatience wearing a wise face.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the smoke-haze. Yara had not slept. Somewhere past the middle of the night she had felt it happen — felt the little cutting find the seam of life beneath the flagstones and drink, felt the buried pulse strengthen by one degree, felt the rose cane's thread of vitality lean toward the new arrival like a drowning man toward a rope. Small magics. Root-magic. The kind that happens below the notice of anything that watches with eyes.

By full light the rose had done something impossible.

Three buds stood along the cane where yesterday there had been bare wood. They were small, misshapen, the color of iron — roses grown in hunger grow strange — but they were buds, swelling with a slowness that Yara recognized as effort, as will, as a plant deciding to spend its last hoarded strength on one act of defiance.

And through her hands, resting lightly on the soil, Yara heard — not words, not yet, but pressure, intention, the root-language that predates speech —

*hungry*

— and beneath the hunger, astonishingly,

*who*

"You may call me Yara." She said it aloud, barely breathing the words, her lips against the cane's rough bark. "I'm a friend of the garden. I keep an orchard far from here, where the trees grow remembering. I've come about the king."

The pulse beneath the flagstones went very still. Even the buried heartbeat seemed to hold its breath.

Then, slowly, reluctantly, like someone opening a door they'd been beaten for opening before:

*the king is gone*

"No," Yara said gently. "The king is *buried*. I know the difference. My whole art is knowing the difference." She thought of Paloma asleep under the pear trees a hundred miles west, eating a memory-fruit by accident and waking up haunted by a grandmother she'd never met. "Things that are buried can be dug up. That's rather the point of me."

The rose trembled. One bud split, just slightly, showing a thread of color within — not red, not quite. A deep, bruised pink, the pink of things remembered rather than seen.

And then the pressure against Yara's palms resolved, for the first time, into something almost like words — worn smooth, ancient, careful:

*he holds a memory. he hides it from the crown. a garden. a woman kneeling in a garden. his mother. he visits it every night so the crown cannot take it*

Yara closed her eyes. "A garden," she repeated. "He keeps a garden in his head."

*it is dying. he is dying. the crown eats slowly. it wants him hollow by midsummer*

"Midsummer." Yara did the arithmetic against her breastbone, cold and quick. Weeks. Only weeks. "Can he hear me? Through the rose — through the root-run — if I speak into the ground, does it reach him?"

Silence. Long silence. The bud strained wider, and Yara understood that the rose was spending itself, spending decades of hoarded life, to hold this channel open.

*i think*, said the garden at last, *he has been listening all along. i think he is crying.*

* * *

Yara knelt there until her legs went numb, and she spoke to the soil in a low, level voice, the voice she used for transplanting and for funerals, and she told the buried roots everything.

She told them about the prophecy book, and the Crane of the dateless night. She told them about a mercenary woman who prayed to a silent god and resented every hour of it, who fought a champion in Vessa to a draw with a broken rib and then apologized to him for the rib. She told them about an alliance of debts, a ragged host gathering in the west, a general named Sarel Vane whose defection might or might not come in time. She did not know how much traveled down through the stone, how much reached whatever fragment of a mind clung to life in the throne room above. She only knew that the rosebud drank her words, and the buried pulse beat a little stronger with each name she offered, and that hope, like water, goes wherever the crack is.

When she finished, the third bud had opened fully. It was ugly and beautiful, a rose the color of an old wound healing, and its scent — thin, but unmistakable — was the scent of the queen mother's garden as it had been thirty years ago. Yara knew this because the rose told her, pouring the memory up through her palms like cold clean water: white walls, bees, a boy prince hiding among the bean rows while his nurse searched, laughing.

"He remembers it too," Yara whispered.

*every night*, the garden answered. *it is why he is not yet hollow. tell the crane woman: hurry. and tell her —*

The pulse stuttered. Faded. Rose-bud petals, spent, began to curl at their edges; the cane sagged against the sundial plinth. Whatever strength the plant had borrowed, it had spent.

*tell her the crown is afraid of her*, the garden finished, faint as frost. *it has been afraid of the crane line for a hundred years. that is why it paved us. that is why it killed us first.*

Then, weaker still, almost lost beneath the flagstones:

*and tell the king — tell him his mother's beans came up again. tell him spring found a way in. he will understand.*

Yara sat in the dead garden with her hand on the cracked stone and wept, quietly, the way she wept for transplanted trees — grief and planting in the same gesture. Then she wiped her face, took up her empty basket, and turned once to look at the high tower, where behind smoked glass something that wore a king like a coat sat whispering to itself.

"Hold on," she told the tower softly. It was not a kindness. It was a warning, and a promise.

She went over the wall at dusk, an old woman with a basket, unremarkable, unseen. By midnight she was on the west road, walking fast, composing in her head the message she would send ahead to Paloma by the fastest rider money could buy:

*Found the garden alive. Found the king alive, barely. The crown fears your name — use that. Come soon. Midsummer is the deadline, and spring has already found a way in.*
