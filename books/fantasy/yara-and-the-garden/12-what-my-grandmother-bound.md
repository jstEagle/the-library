## 12. What My Grandmother Bound

The letters had led her east for nine days, and on the ninth day they led her down.

Paloma stood at the mouth of a cave she had walked past twice already — once going up the mountain, once coming back, cursing the archivist's cramped hand. *Where the shepherd's path forgets itself*, the last letter said, and only now, standing at the place where the trail split into three and none of them went anywhere sensible, did she understand. The path didn't fork here. It *forgot*. The middle way simply stopped existing to the eye, folding back into rock like a word swallowed mid-sentence. Only when she closed her eyes and let her boots remember what her grandmother's blood remembered did the gap open, cool air breathing out of it, smelling of dust and lamp oil and something older — old paper, old ink, old patience.

She lit no torch until she was thirty paces in. Habit from the valley: never show your light before you know your dark. The passage descended in long switchbacks, the walls close enough to brush both shoulders, and with every step downward Paloma felt the strangest sensation, one she could not at first name. Not fear. Not the god-touched burning that had been living under her skin since Vessa, since Yara's garden, since every green thing reached for her too hungrily.

It was *quiet*.

Not the valley's quiet — that dead, flat silence where prayers fell like stones into deep water. This was a quiet with texture, with intention. A held breath. She realized, halfway down, that her hands had stopped aching for the first time in weeks, and that frightened her more than anything the usurper's agents could have done. Whatever was down here had been built to hold magic out. Built the way the valley held it out. Built by someone who knew exactly how such things were made.

Because someone in her family had built them before.

The passage opened without warning into a chamber that stole the words out of her mouth.

It was not large — perhaps the size of the company mess tent, though she'd have sworn walking in that it should be smaller, that the space was folded somehow, tucked into the mountain the way the path had been tucked into the hillside. Every wall was shelved floor to ceiling with books, jars, bundles of dried herbs tied with red thread. A workbench ran along the far wall beneath a window that could not possibly exist — there was solid rock above them, she was certain of it — yet gray daylight fell through it onto the bench as if the sky had been persuaded to make an exception. A cot in the corner, neatly made, decades unmade. And at the center of the room, a chair facing the empty hearth, positioned the way a person sits when they are waiting for someone.

Waiting for *her*.

"Grandmother," Paloma said aloud, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, because the woman in her memory was always laughing, always sharpening a knife, always telling her *a Crane does not flinch, little bird, a Crane watches* — and this room was not a laughing room. This room was a fortress built against the end of the world by a person who expected to lose.

She made herself walk. That was the mercenary discipline her grandmother had drilled into her: when the ground turns strange, keep moving, because stillness is how ambushes find you. She moved along the shelves, reading spines. Some were grimoires in scripts she couldn't parse. Some were company ledgers — the Crane company's own records, going back further than Brannoc had ever claimed, back past the exile, back past the founding. One shelf held nothing but jars of soil, each labeled in her grandmother's hand with dates and places. *Vessa, before. The capital, before. The royal garden, before.*

Before.

Paloma set the jar down very carefully, as if it might go off.

Then she saw the desk. Small, in the corner behind the cot, and on it a stack of papers weighted with a river stone, and beside the papers a letter addressed not in the archivist's hand but in a script she knew better than her own name — the hand that had written *little bird* on the flyleaf of every book she'd owned as a child.

*For Paloma. For when the book finds you.*

She sat down. Her legs decided it before she did.

---

The letter was long. Her grandmother had never been a woman who wasted words, but this was a woman writing to be read after death, and death apparently loosened tongues.

*Little bird. If you are reading this, then the archivist found you, which means my timing has run out, which means the crown has begun to wake. I am sorry for everything I am about to tell you, and sorrier for everything I did not.*

*You were raised to believe the Godless Valley is our punishment. That the company sinned, or I sinned, and the god turned his face away, and we have lived a century in his silence as penance. That is a story I let your commanders tell because it was simpler than the truth and kept people alive. Here is the truth.*

*The silence is not his absence. The silence is ours. We made it.*

*Your great-great-grandmother was a garden-witch — yes, little bird, there were others before Yara, the line is older than any crown — and she learned what I have spent my life confirming: the crown on Aldric's head is not an object. It is a fragment of our own god, torn loose a century ago when he tried to intervene in the succession and was bound by men who feared him. The binding shattered him. The largest piece fused itself to the rightful king and began to whisper. The rest went silent — exiled from himself — and the Cranes, who had served him longest, chose to bind his voice entirely rather than let the hungry fragment call to its missing pieces through us.*

*We did not lose our god. We locked him away from himself to save what remained of his mind. And the price is the valley — a wound in the world where nothing living-magic can follow, including the piece of him that lives in our blood.*

*I bound the silence into the Crane line itself. Into you. Every prayer you ever whispered in the valley fell on ears that were gagged by your own grandmother's working, and every step you take into the world of living magic strains that binding, because the fragment feels you, little bird. It knows its own blood when it tastes the air around you.*

*Which brings me to the part I cannot soften.*

*The binding is killing you. Slowly — I designed it slowly, so you would have time — but the longer you remain within reach of living magic, the harder your blood pulls against the silence, and the harder the silence holds, the more it burns what it holds. You are feeling it already. I know you are. The ache in your hands. The heat under the skin. You thought it was the god reaching for you. It is worse than that. It is yourself, fighting your own protection.*

*And yet I must ask you to stay in that reach. Because there is one weapon that can unmake the crown, and it is not steel and it is not sorcery. It is a Crane's bare hands, holding the fragment while it is torn free — our blood is the only thing it will answer, the only thing strong enough to hold a god-piece without being destroyed, provided the binding is first undone. Provided you choose to release the silence I wrapped around your life.*

*I built you a cage, little bird, and called it safety, and the truth is I built it slow enough to kill you gently if you stayed, and fast enough to kill you quickly if the crown ever took you. There was no version of your life in which you were safe. There was only a version in which you got to choose how.*

*A Crane does not flinch. But you may weep. I give you permission, this once.*

Paloma read it twice. Then she became aware that she was weeping, sitting in her dead grandmother's chair, in her dead grandmother's mountain, holding the letter so hard the paper had gone soft and damp at the corners.

She gave herself the permission. She used all of it.

* * *

Afterward — she could not have said how long afterward — she washed her face from the water jar and did the thing soldiers do, which is to turn grief into inventory. The room became a list. Books: knowledge, portable. Jars of pre-fall soil: Yara would want these, Yara would *need* these, soil from the royal garden before the crown poisoned it. Ledgers: proof, leverage for the free cities. She packed with steady hands and let the steadiness be a lie she told her body while the truth caught up at its own pace.

But one thing refused to become inventory.

On the workbench, beneath the impossible window, lay a wooden case the length of her forearm, and inside it — when she lifted the lid with hands that had finally stopped shaking — a knife. Not a war knife. A grafting knife, the kind Yara used, curved and gentle, its handle worn smooth by generations of palms. Wrapped around the blade was a strip of parchment bearing one line in her grandmother's hand:

*The weapon is not a weapon. Remember what grows.*

Paloma stared at it until the words rearranged themselves into sense. *A Crane's bare hands.* Not fists. Hands that could hold and cut and graft. Her family had not been warriors who happened to serve a god. They had been gardeners. The company's mercenary work, the escort jobs, the dull safe exile — all of it a shell around a seed, a century of watching over a single task waiting for a single heir.

Her whole life, she had resented the valley for making her small. It had never occurred to her that she was being kept like a blade in oil — preserved, honed, hidden, *meant*.

The ache flared in her hands then, sudden and hot, as if her blood had heard her thoughts and answered. She gasped. Looked down. Beneath the skin of her palms, faint as veins in marble, thin lines of light were moving — silver-white, branching like roots, like the cracks in the crown she had seen in Yara's scrying bowl. The binding, straining. The silence, fraying. Every hour she spent in the world's living air, spending her.

She closed her fist. The light dimmed but did not leave.

"So that's the price," she said to the empty room, to the woman who wasn't in it. "Slow, or quick. You never gave me a third option."

The room, of course, said nothing. Gods were silent; grandmothers worse.

She took the grafting knife. She took the letters, the ledgers, the jars of soil. At the door she paused, looking back at the cot, the waiting chair, the impossible window letting in its stubborn ration of borrowed sky — a woman's whole life compressed into a room that fit her like a shroud — and Paloma saluted it, the company way, fist to heart, because whatever else her grandmother had been, she had held the line for a hundred years alone.

Then she climbed, out of the quiet and the cold, into the gray afternoon light of the mountain's shoulder — and stopped.

Below her, on the road toward the lowlands and Vessa, dust rose in a long column. Too disciplined for raiders. Too large for scouts. The usurper's army, marching on the free cities, weeks ahead of anything Sarel's stolen plans had promised.

And in her hands, the binding burned like a fuse lit at both ends.

Paloma spat on her palms, gripped the pack straps, and started down the slope at a run — toward the army, toward Vessa, toward whatever was left of the time her grandmother had bought her.
