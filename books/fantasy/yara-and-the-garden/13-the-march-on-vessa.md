## 13. The March on Vessa

The army moved at dawn the way a river moves through a gorge — slow, inevitable, and loud enough to drown thought.

Sarel Vane rode at the head of the vanguard, her gray mare picking carefully over the frozen ruts of the old trade road, and watched ten thousand men and women unspool behind her like a wound bandage pulled from a body that had never been allowed to heal. Banners snapped in the cold: black fields bearing the silver boar of the usurper's house. Supply wagons groaned under grain, salt pork, arrows, siege timber. Somewhere back in the third column, the crown's agents rode in their lacquered coaches, and every soldier in the line gave them a wide berth without ever quite admitting why.

She had marched before — in border wars, in suppression actions, once across the Saltmarsh in a season so wet the men had wept from the rot between their toes. She knew the rhythms. The first day was always full of song. By the fifth, the songs died and the feet began to complain. By the tenth, an army stopped being people and became weather, something you endured rather than joined.

This time she counted the days differently. This time she was counting toward something she could not yet name aloud, even inside her own skull.

*Vessa*, the orders said. *Take it. Hold nothing back.*

*Hold nothing back.* Four words written in a hand she did not recognize, sealed with the boar's sigil, delivered to her tent by a courier whose eyes had been wrong — flat as coins left too long in water. The crown's agents had begun writing the orders themselves. General Aldren's staff, the usurper's own secretaries, all of them bypassed now, as if the war had grown a new throat and was speaking through it directly.

Sarel turned her face north into the wind and let it scour her cheeks raw. Behind her, ten thousand people believed they were marching to glory or at least to wages. She alone knew they were marching to be spent.

* *

The sabotage began small, because small was survivable.

On the fourth night out from the mustering field, she walked the supply park under a fat moon, wrapped in a common officer's cloak, her rank stripped away along with her name. The quartermasters knew her by sight and saluted anyway; she waved them off with the weary gesture of a general who could not sleep, which was true enough. She paused at wagon after wagon, laying her palm against the grain sacks, listening the way her mother had taught her to listen to bread dough — not hearing anything, exactly, but feeling whether the thing beneath her hand was alive.

At the third wagon she found what she wanted: a rear axle with a hairline crack running through the seasoned oak, invisible to any inspection made in daylight. She pressed her thumb into the crack and worked it wider, a fraction, no more than a knife-blade's width. In two hundred miles of bad road, that axle would give way. The wagon would spill its load of arrowheads into a ravine, and by the time anyone thought to wonder why, the evidence would be scattered stone and rust among a thousand other accidents.

It was nothing. It was almost nothing. It was the first honest thing she had done since she burned Corvess.

That word again. She carried it now the way soldiers carried shrapnel, deep where the flesh had closed around it. *Corvess.* The free city that had opened its gates to her on her sworn promise of generous terms — food for the hungry, amnesty for the garrison, no reprisals — and then stood aside while the crown's agents walked through the surrendered barracks with their long knives and their patient, coin-flat eyes. Three hundred men who had laid down arms on her word. She had screamed until her voice broke. It had changed nothing. The agents had not even looked at her. That was the detail that lived under her ribs now, sharper than the memory of the blood itself: they had not considered her worth looking at. She was furniture in someone else's room, and the room was being redecorated in red.

So: the axle. A little crack, widened. A wagonload of arrowheads that would not fly at Vessa's defenders.

She moved on to the next wagon, and the next, and by the time the moon set she had weakened two axles, salted one barrel of hardtack with river sand, and mislabeled a crate of fuses as tent pegs. Small things. A hundred small things would starve a march; a thousand would kill it. She had a long road ahead of her and a long habit of patience, learned in twenty years of turning boys into soldiers and soldiers into survivors.

What she did not have was time. Because the crown's agents were learning to count.

* *

They came to her tent on the ninth night, and she knew before the flap opened who waited inside, because her sentries had stopped meeting her eyes that morning.

There were two of them. They stood on either side of her campaign table as if they owned the ground, which perhaps they did. Their coats were the color of wet slate, cut close and buttoned high, and their faces were the faces of people who had been told once, long ago, what expressions were for, and had practiced only the ones required. The taller one — she thought of him as Coin-Eyes, though he surely had a name somewhere — gestured politely at her map.

"General Vane. You will forgive the hour."

"I forgive most things before midnight," Sarel said, and hung up her cloak with hands that did not shake, because she had trained them not to, and because shaking would have been a confession. "You're in my tent at midnight. Say what you came to say."

Coin-Eyes smiled. It sat on his face like frost on a window — beautiful, thin, and promising nothing but cold. "The march falls behind schedule."

"The roads fall apart in winter. Armies fall behind schedule. Kings fall behind schedule." She let the last word land and watched to see if it drew blood. It didn't; his frost merely thickened. "I've taken eleven cities. I know how long a march takes."

"Eleven cities," he agreed softly. "And yet the losses accumulate strangely. Wagons lost to broken axles — four this month, when our records show the fleet averaged less than one per season. Grain spoiled. Fuses missing. Powder damp." He tilted his head, a scholar consulting notes he did not need. "One accident is fortune. Ten is a pattern. We are very good with patterns, General. It is most of what we are."

"Then find me better carpenters and drier warehouses," Sarel said. "Or find me another general, and explain to your masters why Vessa still stands when the spring thaws come."

The second agent spoke for the first time. He was younger, and his voice had a curious doubled quality, as if he were speaking in unison with someone standing just behind his teeth. "The master is not concerned with explanations, General. The master is concerned with results. Vessa must fall. The prophecy-bearers gather there — the witch, the Crane woman. What grows in that city must be pulled up while the roots are shallow." He paused, and the doubling in his voice deepened, became almost harmonious, a choir of one. "Roots grow quickly this year. The ground is… generous."

Sarel held his gaze and felt something cold walk down her spine wearing her own footprints. She had faced madmen and zealots and one sorcerer, years ago, in the passes above Kell. None of them had sounded like that — like a mouth borrowed by something patient and enormous and hungry underneath.

"Then I'll get them Vessa," she said. "Faster, if you get me more carts that don't break."

"We will watch the carts," Coin-Eyes said pleasantly. "We will watch everything. It is what we are for." He bowed — a precise, correct bow, exactly deep enough. "Goodnight, General. Sleep well. The master values you."

The flap fell shut behind them, and Sarel stood alone in her lamplight, counting her breaths the way she'd taught young officers to count them after their first battle. In. Out. The lamp flame leaned and steadied.

*The master values you.* She had heard that tone before, from handlers talking about hounds. Valued things were kept. Valued things were fed. And valued things, when they stopped performing, were put down and replaced, and no one at the kennel wept.

She looked down at her map, at the little lead figure she kept marking Vessa's position, and understood with perfect clarity that she had run out of middle ground. There was no longer a version of this where she served faithfully and quietly steered the war toward mercy. The agents would watch the wagons now. Every axle she cracked would be inspected, traced, attributed. Every grain barrel would be weighed twice. They would catch her — not today, maybe not next week, but soon, and when they caught her they would not bother with trials, because she had seen what happened to officers the crown decided were roots instead of branches.

Her defection had stopped being a question of conscience weeks ago. Now it was a question of timing, and timing was everything, and she had perhaps one move left to make before the board closed around her.

She needed proof. Not suspicions, not patterns — proof. The true battle-plans, the ones written in that flat, drowned handwriting, the ones that showed what the crown actually intended for Vessa and its people. If she ran to Paloma Crane with nothing but her word, the mercenary would be right to put a knife to her throat and leave it there. Sarel had burned Corvess. Whatever she brought across the lines had to outweigh that, or she deserved whatever she got.

And proof meant going deeper. Deeper than the wagons, deeper than the supply ledgers, into the sealed coach at the center of the column where the agents kept their papers and their flat-eyed silence. Into the dark of it, alone, with every eye in the army beginning to turn her way.

* *

Three nights later, the opportunity came dressed as a disaster, and she took it with both hands.

A ford on the River Ollen — swollen with early thaw, the ice going rotten from the banks inward — claimed six wagons in a single afternoon. One of the broken axles was hers; the rest were genuine winter luck. The crossing dissolved into chaos, teams screaming in the floodwater, drovers wading chest-deep to cut loose drowning mules, and in the confusion the column compressed, the baggage train tangling with the rearguard, and the crown's coaches — which never mixed with common mud — were forced to halt within thirty yards of the wrecked wagons while the road cleared.

Sarel was there in minutes, cloak off, sleeves rolled, hauling men out of the water alongside everyone else, because that was what her soldiers expected of her and because visibility was armor. Let the whole army see General Vane in the flood, bellowing orders, saving lives. No one watching that scene would later believe she'd slipped away in the dark to pick the crown's lock.

But she did slip away. An hour after midnight, when the salvaged wagons were stacked and the dead mules dragged clear and the camp sank into exhausted sleep, she crossed the trampled snow toward the coaches with a lantern shuttered to a slit and a set of picks sewn into her glove, tools her father had given her at fourteen with the warning that locks were the only honest conversation some doors would ever have.

The sentries outside the agents' coach had been relieved at dusk by men she did not know — big, silent, wrong in the same way the young agent's voice had been wrong. But the flood had shaken the camp's order, and one of them had wandered toward the horse-lines to steal warmth from the fires, and the other stood facing away from the wind, collar up, and the wind blew from the direction Sarel came.

The lock was good work. Her father would have admired it. It took her four minutes and cost her a strip of skin off her knuckle, and then she was inside, in air that smelled of ink and something older — cold stone, deep water, the smell of a cellar where something had been stored too long.

The papers were in a locked case beneath the sleeping berth, and they were worse than she had imagined, because she had imagined cruelty and found arithmetic instead. The true plan for Vessa was not a siege. Sieges ended. The true plan was quarantine and starvation through the spring, the city sealed and left to eat itself, and then — page after page in that flat, drowned hand — schedules. Rations per capita. Rates of attrition. Projected dates by district. Someone had calculated, in neat columns, how long each quarter of Vessa would take to die, and had noted with mild scholarly interest that the harbor district, being poor, would fail first, and that this was acceptable.

And beneath the schedules, a separate folio, tied in black cord. She untied it with fingers gone numb past feeling.

It was a list of names. Officers, mostly. Hers was at the top.

Not *arrest*. Not *question*. Beside each name was a single word in that same drowned script: *hollowed.* And beside hers, a date. Eleven days hence.

Sarel stood in the swaying coach with the list trembling in her hands and felt the last door close quietly behind her. There was no middle path. There had never been one; she had simply been walking down a corridor that narrowed while she told herself it was wide. Eleven days. They had already chosen the day they would open her skull and pour something else into it, and they had let her keep commanding armies right up until the appointed hour, because valued things were used fully before they were spent.

She copied the plans. All of them — the starvation schedule, the assault timings, the dispositions, the names. Her hands steadied as she wrote; that was the strange thing, that terror finally burned off into something clean. She had spent months cracking axles in the dark, stealing spoonfuls from a mountain, and now here at last was the whole mountain, small enough to carry, heavy enough to mean something.

She retied the cord, relatched the case, and let herself out into the freezing dark. The sentry still faced away from the wind. The camp slept the sleep of the flooded and exhausted. Behind her, in the coach, her death warrant lay in its locked case with its tidy date, patient as arithmetic.

Eleven days. The army would reach the hills above Vessa in nine.

Which meant she had a window the width of a knife blade: late enough that her flight would not trigger the agents to change the plans she carried — early enough that Paloma Crane could act on what those plans revealed. Two days, perhaps three. She would have to desert in the shadow of her own victory parade, betray her oath in front of the army she'd built, and throw herself on the mercy of the woman whose city's sister she had burned.

Sarel walked back to her tent through the snow, past the banked fires and the sleeping thousands who trusted her, and found that her fear had quietly become something harder and more useful.

Resolve keeps a schedule. So did she.
