## 14. The Battle of the Broken Bridge

The fog came off the Vessan river at dawn, and Paloma Crane stood on the broken bridge and watched it swallow the world.

Below her, the water ran brown and fast with spring melt, churning against the pilings the usurper's engineers had cracked a decade ago and never mended. The bridge itself was a skeleton — two spans still standing, the middle one dropped into the current like a fallen man, its stones furred with moss. It was the only crossing for twenty miles, which was why Sarel's army had to come this way, and why eleven hundred starving soldiers, two free-city militias, one hedge-witch, and a company of mercenaries who hated her name were going to stand on it until they died or won.

Paloma counted her forces again. She'd been counting all night. The numbers didn't improve with repetition.

"Eleven hundred," said Brannoc behind her, "and half of them can't hold a spear point down."

She didn't turn around. She hadn't learned yet how to look at him without the old anger rising, and today she couldn't afford the anger. He came to stand at the rail beside her anyway, a mountain of a man gone grey at the temples, his knuckles scarred into permanent ridges. The Crane Company's black-and-white sash hung loose at his throat.

"They'll hold," she said.

"You don't know that."

"No. But they'll believe I do, and belief holds a line better than spears."

Brannoc grunted — the sound he'd made for thirty years when a junior officer said something too clever by half. Then, unexpectedly: "Your grandmother used to say something like that."

"I know."

"She stole our god, girl. Stole the silence right out of our mouths. And now you're asking me to die on a bridge for a king we've never seen, because a book told you to." His voice wasn't cruel. That was the worst of it. It was tired, the way stone is tired after weather. "I'm here because eleven hundred people are going to stand where I tell them to stand, and if someone has to tell them wrong, I'd rather it be me than a merchant's son."

Paloma finally looked at him. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Just don't waste them."

He walked away down the span, bellowing the companies into position, and Paloma gripped the cold rail and let herself feel, for exactly ten breaths, the terror she could not show anyone. Then she turned to Yara.

The garden-witch stood at the bridge's landward end among the wagons, her hands buried to the wrist in a barrel of river mud, her eyes half-closed as though listening to something very far away. Around her, in careful rows, stood forty saplings in pots — stunted, twisted things that looked more dead than alive, their bark silvered like old mirrors.

Yara had carried them three hundred miles. She'd slept holding them through the cold nights, whispering to them, feeding them — Paloma didn't ask what — from her own cupped palms.

"They're ready," Yara said, without opening her eyes. Her voice had the calm of deep water. "They're hungry, but they're ready."

"Hungry for what?"

"For whatever walks past them." She opened her eyes, and they were green as new leaves, and older than that. "A memory-tree doesn't grow fruit unless there's a mind nearby to remember. Today there will be four thousand minds on this field. The trees will not be hungry long."

Paloma looked at the little dead-looking things and felt her skin crawl. "And the men? Our men?"

"They'll see fog and trees and nothing worse than their own regrets. The trees take what's offered freely." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Mostly."

* * *

The usurper's vanguard came up the east road an hour after sunrise, and the first thing Paloma felt was the ground.

Four thousand men make a drum of the earth. She felt them in her boot soles, in her back teeth, in the pit of her stomach where fear lived alongside something else — something hot and bright that she recognized, with shame, as joy. This was what she'd been born for, maybe. This was what the Godless Valley had starved out of her: the terrible animal exultance of standing in front of a storm and refusing to move.

"Archers," she said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and loved herself a little for it.

The first arrows went out over the fog and came down like iron rain. Screaming. The vanguard wavered, reformed, came on. They were professionals, most of them — Sarel Vane's regulars, marching in good order under the crown's black banners, and Paloma understood with cold clarity that order would kill them all if she let it keep forming.

"Now," she said.

Yara had spent two days preparing for this word. At the landward end of the bridge, forty memory-trees went into the churned mud of the approach road, roots down, quick as planting onions, while the militia screened them with shields and bodies and dying. Yara moved among them barefoot, mud to her knees, singing something low and wordless, and where her shadow crossed the saplings they *woke*.

Paloma saw it happen and knew she would dream about it forever. The silvered bark flushed green. Branches that had been twisted sticks uncurled like fingers opening. In the space of fifty heartbeats, forty scraggly saplings became forty full-grown trees, broad and dark and heavy with fruit, and the fog rolled through their branches, and the usurper's vanguard marched straight into them because the road went through them and roads go where roads go.

Then the trees began to feed.

It was not dramatic, at first. Men simply stopped. They stopped marching and stood beneath the branches with their helmets tipped back, reaching up, plucking pears that gleamed like lamplight. Paloma watched a sergeant of the regulars bite into one and collapse to his knees — not wounded, just *opened*, sobbing, both hands full of some morning thirty years dead. She watched another man laugh, helplessly, horribly, laughing and weeping at once as he ate and ate and ate. The trees grew taller as they fed. The road disappeared. The field became a grove, and the grove became a labyrinth, and every path through it was lined with the usurper's own soldiers remembering everything they had ever lost.

"What do they see?" Paloma asked quietly.

"Whatever they regret," Yara said. She was pale now, swaying slightly, the planting costing her more than she admitted. "Their mothers. Their brothers. The villages they burned. The orders they followed. The crown doesn't pay enough for a man to stop remembering." Her hand found Paloma's arm. "It won't hold the veterans long. The ones who've made peace with it. But the young ones —"

The young ones were done. Whole companies of the vanguard simply dissolved into the trees, sitting in the roots, eating, weeping, surrendering without ever thinking of it as surrender.

But Paloma had counted the banners, and the vanguard was only the first third. Behind it, coming up through the burning fog now, marched the regulars of the second column, and with them the thing she had dreaded since the scouting report: the crown's agents. Twelve of them, in grey, walking untouched through their own army's panic, faces smooth as eggs, carrying no weapons she could see.

She could see them anyway. Every mercenary in the valley learned to feel magic the way you feel weather — not in the Valley itself, never there, but in the stories, and in her blood, which had been burning stranger and hotter every day since she'd left. The agents came on like twelve holes in the shape of men, and where they passed, the memory-trees *wilted*, fruit dropping rotten, and the weeping soldiers were hauled up by their collars and driven forward with flat grey voices.

"Second column's going to reach the bridge intact," Brannoc reported, arriving at her shoulder spattered with someone else's blood. "Agents are cutting your witch's garden out from under her. We hold the spans or we lose everything."

"Then we hold the spans." She drew her sword. It felt absurdly small. "Crane Company with me. Militias on the flanks. Nobody crosses the middle gap — we fight them on our two spans and let the river have the rest."

Brannoc grinned then, and for a moment he was thirty years younger and so was she. "There's my captain," he said, and it broke something loose in her chest that she would spend the rest of her life paying interest on.

* * *

The bridge fight lasted from midmorning until the light went long and gold, and Paloma remembered almost none of it afterward in sequence — only flashes, the way trauma keeps its accounts.

She remembered the shield wall forming on the near span, Crane veterans locking shoulders with Vessan dockworkers who had never held a shield before, and the wall *holding*, the way walls do when everyone in them decides at the same moment to be a wall.

She remembered the agents reaching the bridgehead and what happened to the men nearest them — not killed, *emptied*, soldiers dropping their weapons and standing slack-faced while grey voices poured into them. She remembered killing her first agent, driving her sword through a body that barely bled, feeling the wrongness of it crawl up the blade into her arm — and feeling, underneath the wrongness, her blood answer. Burn. Reach. The agent's smooth face cracked open with something like fear as Paloma's god-touched veins lit up along her forearm, and the grey emptiness recoiled from her like a hand from a stove.

*They know what I am,* she thought, wild. *The crown knows what I am.*

She remembered Yara on the far bank, arms raised, sweat-soaked, dragging the labyrinth wider, buying minutes with years of stored growing. She remembered the moment the second column broke — really broke, not the orderly retreat of professionals but the blind animal stampede of men fleeing their own memories — and the sound eleven hundred hoarse throats made when they realized it.

And she remembered Brannoc.

She remembered it last, always, and first, always. He was on the far span, anchoring the flank, when the three remaining agents did what agents do when a battle turns: they spent themselves. Grey light came off them like heat off a forge, and the span's stones cracked, and Brannoc — who could have stepped back, who had earned the right to step back a hundred times over — Brannoc stepped *forward*, into the light, because behind him were forty boys from the docks and he had told them to stand.

Paloma saw him take the blow meant for the boy next to him. She saw it the way you see lightning: complete, instantaneous, already past.

She crossed the distance between them and did not remember crossing it. She killed the second agent herself — burned him, actually, her blood flaring white through her gloves, the binding inside her tearing wider with every heartbeat and not caring, not caring at all — and the third fled, grey light guttering, back into the labyrinth where Yara's trees were waiting with four thousand regrets.

Then she was kneeling in the rubble of the span, and Brannoc was in her arms, and the battle was still roaring somewhere far away, on the other side of a wall of grief that had come down between her and the whole world.

"Captain," he said. His mouth was bloody. He was smiling. Thirty years of anger, and at the end he called her captain.

"Shut up. Save your breath. We'll get you —"

"The oath." His hand found hers, crushed it. "You should know. Your grandmother — she didn't exile us to protect us. She exiled us because we *swore to watch you*. Founding oath. Keep the Cranes where the god can't follow. Keep them small. Keep them safe." Blood at his teeth; he laughed at it. "We weren't your protectors, girl. We were your cage. And you —" His grip tightened, then gentled, impossibly. "You got out anyway. Fly, then."

His eyes went to the sky over the broken bridge, where the fog was lifting and the memory-trees stood silent and enormous, heavy with uneaten fruit, and he watched something there that Paloma could not see, and then he was not watching anything at all.

* * *

They counted the cost by torchlight, because that is what armies do after victories, and the counting took until midnight.

Three hundred and six dead. Two hundred wounded, forty of those touched by the agents, sitting apart with blank eyes that Yara fed broth and small green leaves and quiet words, trying to call them back from wherever the grey had put them. Against that: the usurper's second column destroyed, the vanguard scattered into the orchard of their own regrets, and the road to Vessa — the road *to* them, the siege road — closed for a season at least.

Victory. The men were singing on the west bank, ragged and off-key, and the free cities would hear of it by week's end, and somewhere across the map General Sarel Vane would read the reports and understand that the war had changed shape.

Paloma sat on the broken span with her boots hanging over the brown water, and she did not sing.

Yara came and sat beside her. For a long time neither of them spoke. Downstream, the river carried petals from Yara's trees — silvered, seedless, sterile blossoms that had given everything they had — out toward the sea.

"He called me captain," Paloma said finally.

"He meant it."

"He hated me yesterday."

"He hated what your family did. Those aren't the same thing, and he knew the difference before you did." Yara's hand found hers, mud-caked and warm. "That's what command is, Paloma. Everyone thinks it's giving orders. It isn't. It's spending people. You spend them like coin, carefully, and you pray the thing you buy is worth the price, and you learn that some prices —" She stopped. Started again, softer. "Some prices you carry for the rest of your life. That's the whole job. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never done it."

Paloma looked at her hands. Under the dirt and the dried blood, her forearms still glowed faintly, veins like embers under skin. The binding, fraying. The archivist's letters had warned her; her grandmother's workshop had explained it. Every day inside magic's reach, the silence her grandmother had bought burned through her a little faster, and someday soon there would be nothing between her blood and the god but ash.

"Yara," she said. "When this is over — when we've taken the capital, when the crown is dealt with — will there be enough of me left to sit in your orchard and watch things grow?"

Yara was quiet for a moment. Then she reached into her coat and brought out something wrapped in linen: a single pear, silvered and perfect, cut that afternoon from the tree that had grown over the spot where Brannoc fell.

"It's not his memory," she said. "The trees don't keep the dead. But it grew from the ground that held him, and the ground remembers the shape of everyone it holds." She pressed it into Paloma's hands. "Eat it when you're ready. Not tonight."

Paloma closed her fingers around it and found she was crying, silently, uselessly, the way she hadn't cried since she was a child in the Valley where nothing follows and nothing answers.

Far to the east, past the burnt farms and the drowned road, the usurper's army regrouped around its puppet king, and in the throne room of the capital a crown fused to a skull screamed silently at the taste of garden-magic on the wind — screamed, and began, for the first time in a hundred years, to be afraid.

Paloma felt it. She didn't know how she knew, but she felt it, faint and far and furious, like a toothache in the world.

"It felt us," she whispered.

Yara nodded slowly, watching the dark horizon. "Yes. And frightened things with crowns do one of two things, Paloma. They run—" she shook her head— "or they send everything they have left. We've won ourselves a season. We'd best spend it learning how to end this, before it spends itself on us."

Downriver, the petals kept moving toward the sea, and Paloma sat in the torchlight holding a pear that weighed as much as a man, and began — alone, in the way commanders are always alone — to count what was left.
