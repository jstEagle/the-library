## 17. The Siege of the Heart

The capital came out of the dark like a jaw.

Paloma had seen it once before, years ago, from a caravan road she'd been paid to forget: white walls, gold roofs, the palace rising above everything like a fist closed around the city's throat. Now she saw it by torchlight and moonlight both, from a ridge Sarel Vane had chosen with a general's cold precision, and the walls were not what she looked at. She looked at the dry riverbed beneath them.

"The Serpent's Run," Sarel had said, unrolling her stolen plans by candlelight three nights ago. "They bricked it over when they built the western quarter. My engineers did the work — I signed the orders myself." Her mouth had twisted at that, as if the words tasted of ash. "It runs under the walls, under the outer bailey, and comes up in the old cistern yard below the palace gardens. The usurper's maps don't show it because he never cared how water reached his fountains."

"And your people didn't mention it to him?"

Sarel had met her eyes across the table without flinching. "My people are dead, or mine. That's the whole of my army now."

So they went into the dark instead of against the stone — four hundred mercenaries, two hundred free-city volunteers who had stopped laughing at Paloma after the Broken Bridge, Yara with her seed-satchel and her steady hands, and Aldric's name spoken only in whispers, because hope was still a thing you could jinx by saying too loudly.

The riverbed was low and dry and smelled of old rain and older stone. Paloma walked at the head of the column with her sword drawn and her blood on fire.

She had been burning for days now. She knew what it was; Yara had told her gently, the way you tell someone a wound is mortal when there is nothing left to do but walk. The binding her grandmother had wound around the Crane line was fraying, thread by thread, and each thread snapped somewhere deep in her veins. It felt like swallowing embers. It felt like the god's silence finally thinning enough that something behind it pressed through — not words yet, not voice, just *pressure*, a warmth behind her sternum that grew every hour she spent inside magic's reach. The closer they came to the crown, the hotter she burned. The prophecy was coming due, and it was coming due in her body.

"Paloma." Yara's hand found hers in the dark, soil-dark fingers lacing through calloused ones. "Breathe. The fire follows the breath. If you panic, it spreads."

"If I slow down, we die in this tunnel."

"If you burn up before we reach the throne room, we die anyway." Yara's thumb moved across her knuckles, once, deliberate as planting. "I've watched things grow too fast all my life, love. They topple. Grow at your own speed."

There was no time to answer. Ahead, the tunnel mouth opened into the cistern yard, and the night was full of torches that were not theirs.

* * *

Sarel's plans held until they didn't.

The first hour went almost insultingly well. They came up through the cisterns in silence, took the gate-watch from behind, and were into the palace's lower courts before the alarm bells began. Paloma led the left column through the servants' galleries — narrow corridors where tapestries hung like sleeping animals — while Brannoc's veterans, or what remained of them, took the right. The free cities held the courtyard. On paper, in the plans, in the clean geometry Sarel drew with charcoal, it all worked.

But the crown had been drinking from this place for a century, and its servants did not fight like men.

Paloma understood it fully in the long gallery outside the council chambers, when the chamberlain came toward her walking wrong. He was an old man, silver-haired, in court dress, and he walked with his joints moving in sequences joints should not make, smooth as poured wax, his face serene and his eyes empty as two coins dropped down a well. Around him, dozens more emerged from doorways — clerks, guardsmen, ladies-in-waiting, kitchen children — all wearing that same terrible calm.

"They're hollowed," Yara said beside her, voice tight. "Like the usurper. The crown drank them and left the shapes."

"Hollowed things can still hold knives," Paloma said, and then they were among them.

Fighting the hollowed was unlike anything in her twenty-nine years of soldiery. They did not flinch. They did not tire. A guardsman took her blade through the shoulder and kept reaching for her throat with a smile of perfect peace, and she had to open his throat entirely before he stopped — and even then he fell like a marionette with cut strings, no death in him anywhere, just absence collapsing. She fought through the gallery step by step, breath rasping, and with every kill the fire in her blood climbed another degree, as if the binding were tearing faster wherever the crown's hunger had already been.

*He knows you're coming,* she thought, cutting down a clerk whose hands were soft as dough. *He's feeding you to me on purpose.*

Maybe. Or maybe the crown was simply afraid, and fear made it greedy, and greed made it stupid. Either way, the gallery ended at a stair, and the stair went up, and up was where she needed to be.

Yara kept pace at her shoulder, and where Yara walked, seeds woke. Paloma saw it between exchanges — the witch scattering handfuls from her satchel like a farmer sowing a battlefield, and green things answering in seconds: ivy bursting from mortar cracks to snare hollowed ankles, thornbrambles erupting across doorways, a fig tree splitting the flagstones whole and shaking its branches so that leaves rained down thick as snow. The palace had been dead ground for a century, drained by the crown's rooting, but Yara carried living soil in her satchel and memory in her trees, and life is patient, and life is stubborn, and given one inch of crack it will take the mile.

"Left," Yara gasped, hauling Paloma back as a hollowed knight's halberd swept through the space where her neck had been. "The throne room is left and up. Sarel's map—"

"Sarel's map ends at the doors." Paloma wrenched her sword free and put her shoulder to a serving girl gone wax-smooth and wrong, sending her sprawling down the steps. "After that it's just us and whatever's wearing the king."

They climbed.

* * *

The burning got bad on the second staircase.

Paloma had learned to read it now, the way soldiers learn weather. Low fire meant distance; high fire meant the crown was near, its rooted power pressing up through the palace stones like heat through a forge floor. By the time they reached the gallery of kings — a long hall lined with painted monarchs going back nine generations — the pressure behind her sternum had become a second heartbeat, arrhythmic and enormous, and she could taste metal at the back of her throat and light at the edges of her vision.

She stopped, braced a hand against the wall, and was sick, quietly and thoroughly, into a decorative urn.

"Paloma—" Yara started toward her.

"Don't touch me." She wiped her mouth. "Not right now. I think — if you touch me while it's like this, I'll pull the magic through you, or it'll pull me through you, or something. I felt it with the last one I killed. The god pushed. Through *me*."

Yara went very still. In the torchlight her face was half shadow, and Paloma saw the thing in it that Yara had been carrying since the orchard lessons, the thing she'd hidden and then confessed on the eve of the march: *Your god isn't absent, Paloma. It's bound. Your grandmother tied it shut, and you are the knot.* Even knowing it, seeing it confirmed in the witch's eyes hurt differently than hearing it had.

"It's trying to come through," Paloma said. "Isn't it. The binding's going, and it's using the hole."

"Yes."

"And if it comes all the way through—"

"I don't know." Yara's honesty was the cruelest kindness anyone had ever shown her. "No Crane has ever been this close to their own god with the binding this thin. Your grandmother made sure of that. She built her whole life around making sure of that."

"Then she wasted her life," Paloma said, "because here I am." She straightened. The fire roared. Somewhere above them, faintly, through feet of stone, she heard a voice that was not a voice — honeyed, ancient, hungry — saying something she could not parse and understood anyway, the way you understand heat. *Come, little crane. Come home. I have waited so long.*

"It knows my name," she said.

"It's always known your name. Your family wrote it down a century ago." Yara pulled a fresh handful of seeds from her satchel and pressed them into Paloma's bloody palm. "Hold these. Not to plant. Just — hold them. Living things steady other living things. It's why I gave you the pear-wood charm months ago. You've lost the charm somewhere in this filth, so hold the seeds."

Paloma closed her fist around them. Small, hard, alive. Sunflower, she thought. Something ordinary. Something that wanted nothing except sun and soil and to become itself.

"Better?" Yara asked.

"Liar," Paloma said, and smiled, and went up.

* * *

The last corridor was the worst, and it was worst precisely because nothing attacked them in it.

It was the coronation gallery, she realized — the same paintings continuing, the dynasty marching forward through centuries toward the present, and the portraits had changed. The later kings stood in dimmer rooms, wore heavier crowns, and their painted eyes had all been turned slightly, subtly, toward the viewer. Toward her. As she passed, the fire in her veins rose to meet each frame, and she had the obscene certainty that the paint was watching her pass, generation after generation, ancestors and predecessors alike turning their heads in oil and pigment to mark the crane coming home to roost.

At the end of the gallery stood the doors to the throne room.

They were oak, twice a man's height, banded in gold, and carved with the royal arms — the stag crowned, the wheat gathered, the river flowing. Except the river in the carving was wrong now. Sometime in the last century someone had re-cut it, deepened it, widened it, until it looked less like water and more like a crack, like a seam in the world, like the exact shape of the wound the binding had torn in the sky over the Godless Valley the night the book dragged her out.

From beyond the doors came light. Gold light, sourceless, seeping under the doors like spilled honey.

And from within came Aldric's voice.

She had never heard it before — only Yara's reports, whispered through a rosebush in a dead garden. But she knew instantly which voice it was, because it was fighting. It was ragged and human and cracking, a man's voice arguing with something vast and patient inside his own skull, and the words came through the oak in broken pieces:

"—will not — you hear me — I am *not* your door—"

Then the other voice, the honeyed one, the one Paloma had heard in the stairwell, filling the corridor now until the torch flames bent toward the doors:

*BUT YOU HAVE BEEN SUCH A GOOD ONE.*

Paloma set her hand on the door. The wood was warm. It pulsed, very faintly, like something asleep and breathing.

"Whatever happens in there," she said to Yara, without turning, "don't let me say yes to anything."

"To anything?"

"It's going to offer me something. I've known since the workshop. Everything my grandmother hid, everything she burned her life walling up — it's all leverage, and it's all mine, and it knows exactly what I want." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "A century of prayers, Yara. A century of talking into silence. And tonight the silence is going to talk back, and it's going to offer me everything I ever asked for, and some piece of me is going to want to take it."

Yara stepped close behind her, close enough that Paloma could feel the living warmth of her, soil and sap and stubbornness. "Then I'll be standing here," she said, "being the other thing you want."

Paloma breathed once — in, out, the way Yara taught her, the fire following the breath — and pushed the doors open.

* * *

The throne room blazed.

Every window was bright with moonlight though the moon was nowhere in the sky's proper place; every banner on the walls glowed with its own soft gold; and at the center of it all, beneath the great dome painted with the gods' old concord, sat the throne, and on the throne sat the king.

Aldric was younger than she had imagined and worse off than she had feared. He was chained — not by iron, though there was iron, ceremonial and absurd, at wrists and ankles — but by the crown itself. She could see the roots now, even across the width of the room. Pale, woody tendrils, fine as hair, growing out from beneath the circlet of gold and down into his scalp, into his temples, threading behind his ears, vanishing into skin like frost spreading across a windowpane. His hands gripped the arms of the throne hard enough that the wood had splintered. His eyes found hers across the distance, and in them was a man drowning and waving.

"There she is," he said — and then his face spasmed, and the honeyed voice rolled out of the same mouth, the same lips shaping it, the crown flexing its grip:

*THE CRANE OF THE DATELESS NIGHT. COME TO UNMAKE ME. COME, THEN.*

Paloma walked forward. Every step made the fire climb. By the tenth step she could see her own veins glowing faintly through the skin of her forearms, gold-white, like rivers of dawn under her flesh, and she understood with cold clarity that she was dying by inches and had been since the valley expelled her, and that her grandmother's century of protection had simply been a very long, very loving way of scheduling her death for today.

Good, she thought, with a mercenary's pragmatism. At least the timing's convenient.

"Let him go," she said.

*LET HIM GO?* The crown laughed, and the laughter shook dust from the rafters. *LITTLE CRANE. LITTLE KNOT IN THE GREAT WOUND. WHO DO YOU THINK HOLDS HIM? LOOK AT WHAT HOLDS YOU.*

The pressure behind her sternum surged, and for one vertiginous moment she felt it clearly from the other side: the bound god straining against her grandmother's weaving, vast and blind and furious, and the crown's fragment straining against its own prison of gold and skull, and the two of them — she saw it then, she would know it forever — the two of them were *the same kind of thing*. Two pieces of one silenced divinity, imprisoned in separate cages, screaming across a century at each other in frequencies no one else could hear.

Her grandmother hadn't bound a monster. She had locked a god in a box and nailed the lid with her granddaughter's life — and the god had broken off a piece of itself to keep knocking.

"Aldric," Paloma said aloud, ignoring the thunder, addressing the man. "My name is Paloma Crane. My grandmother wrote a book about you a hundred years before you were born. It says I'm supposed to get you out of that chair. It doesn't say how. So I'm going to need you to hold on a little longer, and I'm going to need Yara, and it is going to hurt."

His eyes cleared. Just barely, just for a heartbeat, a man surfacing from deep water — and he grinned at her, bloody-mouthed and defiant, and said through teeth clenched against the roots in his skull:

"Took you — long enough —"

Behind her, Yara opened her satchel, and the smell of living earth filled the burning room like a promise.

The crown began to scream.
