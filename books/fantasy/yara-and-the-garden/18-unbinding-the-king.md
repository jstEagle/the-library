## 18. Unbinding the King

The throne room doors were bronze, and they were screaming.

Not the hinges — Yara had known screaming metal before, in smithies, in war, in the one bad year she did not let her orchard remember. This was different. The doors themselves were crying out as Paloma's sword bit into them, and the sound that came off the blade was high and thin and human, a chorus of voices with nowhere left to live. The crown's servants had been people once. Yara could taste it in the air, the way she tasted everything: old grief, worked into the grain of things until it shone like lacquer.

"Through here," she said, and hated how steady her voice came out. Steadiness was easy. Steadiness was what you learned when your whole life was a held breath. "Paloma — *through.*"

Paloma went through.

Yara followed, and the doors swung shut behind them with a groan of relief, sealing out the corridor of corrupted soldiers, the smoke, the shouting, the particular terrible music of a palace dying from the top down. Sarel's plans had gotten them past the walls; Brannoc's dead had bought them the stairs; and now it was only this — the last hundred feet of marble between them and the throne, lit by windows of colored glass that showed no saints Yara recognized, only seasons. Planting. Harvesting. The long dark sleep of winter.

Someone had loved this room once. Someone had hung glass about growing things in a hall where men argued over taxes.

The throne sat at the far end on a dais of black stone, and King Aldric sat upon the throne, and he was not moving.

Yara had imagined him so many ways since the rosebush first whispered his name to her through six feet of flagstone — imagined him monstrous, hollowed, a puppet with a king's face stretched over something else entirely. What she saw was worse in a smaller, quieter way. He looked *tired*. Young under the tiredness, maybe thirty, with his mother's cheekbones — Yara knew those cheekbones; the garden had shown her, once, in a fruit grown from soil the royal gardeners had turned forty years ago — and a fine beard gone ragged, and hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a verdict he has already heard read.

And the crown —

The crown was not gold. Or it had been gold once, but gold is patient and the thing wearing him was more patient still, and over a hundred years the two had come to an understanding. Roots of pale metal ran from the circlet's band down into his temples, down behind his ears, down along the line of his jaw, branching and branching again, fine as mycorrhiza, fine as the hair-roots of an apple tree seeking water. It had grown into him the way Yara's own trees grew into the hillside: not violently, not all at once, but completely, so that neither could say anymore where the ground ended and the tree began.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was beautiful the way her orchard was beautiful, and Yara felt her stomach turn against her own craft.

"Yara of the orchard." The voice came from Aldric's mouth but not from Aldric; it moved his lips the way wind moves a curtain. It was honey poured slow over broken glass. "And the Crane of the dateless night. You are late. We have been waiting a hundred years for the date."

"We're not late," Paloma said. Her voice was rough. She had been fighting for hours, and the binding in her blood had been burning her longer than that — Yara could see it now, up close, the way she hadn't let herself see it on the road. A faint glow under Paloma's skin, along her forearms and throat, like embers seen through cloth. Like roots seeking water in reverse. The binding her grandmother had woven was unraveling in the presence of its own work, and it was eating Paloma as it went. "We're exactly on time. The book said tonight."

"The book said many things." The crown-Aldric smile was gentle. "Books lie. Gods lie better. Shall I tell you what your god would say, mercenary, if it could speak? Shall I tell you what a century of silence sounds like from the inside?"

"Don't," Yara said quietly.

But the crown was already turning, root by root, drawing Aldric's head around to face Paloma, and Yara saw the king's eyes — his own eyes, brown, frightened, present — flicker toward hers for half a heartbeat. *Hurry,* said nothing at all, and everything. Then the other voice took the mouth again, warm as a hearth, and began the oldest work in the world.

* * *

"Here is what I offer," said the fragment of the bound god, speaking through a stolen king, in a room full of stained-glass gardens. "Not a bargain. A gift. Gifts cost nothing; that is what makes them gifts."

Paloma stood ten feet from the dais, sword point dropped to the floor, breathing hard. The glow beneath her skin had brightened. Yara could smell it now — ozone and cut stems, the smell of a wound in the world's green fabric — and every step closer to the crown made it worse, because the binding and the crown were two ends of one knot, and knots pull when you near them.

"You have prayed," the crown said. "Do not be ashamed of it. Prayer is hunger honestly named. You have knelt in a valley where nothing answers, year after year, while the others stopped kneeling, and you kept kneeling, because you believed — oh, you *believed*, little Crane — that silence was a test. That faith meant speaking into the dark and trusting the dark to hold your words somewhere safe."

"I don't listen to puppets," Paloma said.

"No. But you will listen to this." And the voice changed.

It changed the way a season changes. One moment honeyed and ancient and wrong; the next — Yara actually stepped back, her heel scraping marble — the next moment it was *vast*. Not loud. Vast. A voice like weather, like the pressure before rain, like the deep aquifer under the Godless Valley that no map had ever found. It spoke perhaps six words and it did not raise itself above a murmur, and every stained-glass window in the hall sang in sympathy, and Yara's trees — the memory-cutting hidden in her pack, the living wood against her heart — leaned toward it like seedlings toward sun.

*"Daughter,"* said the voice of the god, through the crown, through the corruption of a century's imprisonment, *"you kept the faith. I heard every word. I could not answer. I am sorry."*

Yara watched Paloma Crane come apart.

She had seen the woman fight a champion to a draw in front of a jeering merchant council. She had seen her call in debts from men who hated her, march starving soldiers through a night of sleet, hold a bridge with her body when the host broke around her. She had never seen her weep, and now she saw it happen all at once, ugly and total, tears cutting tracks through the soot on her face while her sword arm stayed exactly where it was, because Paloma Crane's discipline lived deeper than her heart.

"That's not him," Yara said. "That's not even it. It's wearing him—"

"It's wearing my god," Paloma whispered.

"It's wearing a *fragment*. A splinter of the binding, cut loose and starved. Your grandmother didn't imprison your god, Paloma — she built a cage around something already wounded, to keep the wound from spreading. And this—" Yara pointed at the crowned king, at the roots of pale metal, at the tired eyes behind them. "This is what a century of hunger does to a piece of the divine. It doesn't stop being holy. It just stops being *kind*."

"Let me finish," the crown said gently, in its honeyed voice again, and there was almost tenderness in it, which was the worst thing yet. "I will not deceive you, Crane. Deception is for the small gods. Here is truth: your god is real. Your prayers were heard. Every one. I carry them — a hundred years of them, stored in me like seeds in a granary, waiting for a spring that the binding forbade. Unbind me, and they fly free. Refuse, and walk away, and I will give them back to you myself. Your god's voice. In your ear. Every morning, every night, for the rest of your mortal life. No more valley. No more silence. No more kneeling into a dark that holds nothing."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere below, muffled by floors of stone, the battle went on, but here there was only the sound of Paloma breathing, and the sound of Yara's own heart, and the faint creak of roots in a king's skull.

"All you must do," the crown said, "is walk away. Leave him to me. He is nearly mine anyway; another year, another two, and there will be no Aldric left to save, only a shape I wear well. Go home to your valley. Pray aloud. Be answered. Is a stranger's mind worth a century of longing? Is one man's freedom worth your *faith*, little Crane?"

And Yara understood, then, with the sick clarity that comes to gardeners watching blight move down a row — this was not a bribe. This was a *harvest*. The fragment had been starved of devotion for a hundred years, and Paloma's love, her terrible stubborn century-deep love, was the richest food it had ever been offered. It wanted her to choose it. It wanted her choice the way roots want water.

It wanted her to kneel one last time.

* * *

"Say no," Yara said. "Paloma. Look at me. Say no."

But Paloma was looking at the king. At the man inside the king — at the flicker of brown eyes, the fear, the small furious humanity that had survived a century of occupation the way a rose survives a wall's shadow: thin, persistent, alive. Yara had spoken with that man through a rosebush. She had felt his grip on himself like a hand gripping a cliff-edge, and she knew — they both knew — how close his fingers were to opening.

"Yara." Paloma's voice was wrecked. She wasn't looking away from Aldric. "When I was nine years old, I prayed for my mother to stop coughing. I prayed every night for a winter. Nothing. When I was nineteen, I prayed to know if I should leave the company, and I got nothing, and I stayed, and I told myself the silence meant *stay*. My whole life is built on top of that silence, Yara. Everything I am is a house built on it." She wiped her face with the back of her sword hand, smearing soot and blood. "And it's standing right there offering to talk to me."

"I know."

"And it's lying. Isn't it. Part of it's lying."

"Part of it," Yara said. "The part that's been alone too long. But the offer — the voice, the answering — that part might be true. That's what makes it a knife instead of a hook." She moved forward, slowly, until she stood beside Paloma, close enough that their shoulders touched, gardener and mercenary, hedge-witch and god-touched soldier, the two least likely rescuers any besieged king had ever been sent. "So I'll ask you the only question that matters. If your god were free — the whole god, unwounded, unbent — what do you think it would tell you to do right now?"

Paloma closed her eyes.

Yara watched her stand in the ruins of a hundred years of prayer. Watched the ember-glow pulse under her skin, faster now, the binding fraying toward its final thread, each heartbeat costing her something she would not get back. Watched her lips move, silently, in the oldest habit of her life — the habit of kneeling into the dark and trusting the dark to keep her words.

And Yara, who grew memories instead of fruit, who had tasted a thousand lives in her orchard and knew precisely what devotion weighed, understood that whatever Paloma said next would be the truest sentence of her existence.

"He'd tell me to put it down," Paloma said.

Her eyes opened. They were red-rimmed and blazing and wet, and they were not looking at the crown at all.

"He'd tell me that faith isn't the voice. Faith is the walking toward. He'd tell me—" and here her voice broke clean in half, and she let it, and went on anyway, "—he'd tell me that a god who wanted me to buy my prayers with a man's mind wouldn't be worth hearing. Even if the price were cheaper. Even if the price were *nothing*. Because that's not what he *is*." She drew a shaking breath. "I spent a hundred years — *they* spent a hundred years — keeping the silence so this couldn't happen. So some starving piece of heaven couldn't wear a person like a coat. And you want me to hand you the last Crane standing because I'm lonely?" She laughed, and it was the worst sound Yara had ever heard, grief and love in the same breath, one note, indivisible. "No. No. I won't. I *choose* him. I choose the stranger. Let it cost what it costs."

The crown screamed.

There was no other word. All the honey went out of it at once and what remained was vastness without warmth, a century of imprisonment finding its voice, and the stained-glass windows shattered outward in a rain of painted seasons, and the roots of pale metal writhed against Aldric's skull like something drowning.

"*Then take him,*" it thundered, "*and see what he is without me. Take him, and be empty together. I kept him ALIVE, little Crane. I kept him WARM—*"

"You kept him in a cage," Paloma said, walking forward, sword discarded, hands open and bare, "and now we're going to open it."

She climbed the dais. She took the king's face in her burning, glowing, fraying, mortal hands.

"Aldric," she said. "Your mother had a garden. Hold onto it. We're here to bring you home to it."

And Yara stepped up beside her, opened her pack, and set the grafting knife and the memory-cutting on the black stone of the dais, and said the words every gardener says before the hardest pruning of the year:

"Hold still, your Majesty. This will hurt more than anything has ever hurt you — and then it will grow."

Above them, in the ruined windows, the evening sky was turning the color of new leaves, and somewhere in Paloma's veins the last threads of a grandmother's binding were singing as they parted — and the crown, rooted deep in a living king, began, at last, to be afraid.
