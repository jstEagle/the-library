## 19. What Wears a Crown

The throne room smelled of scorched stone and old incense, and beneath both—faint, stubborn, impossible—the green smell of living sap.

Paloma noticed it as she crossed the threshold, because she had spent the last hour fighting through servants whose eyes had gone white and whose blood had gone black, and every one of them had screamed in the same honeyed voice, and she had expected to arrive at a place made entirely of that voice. Instead there was this: the ghost of a garden, rising through cracks in the marble like water through cloth.

Aldric knelt on the dais steps. Not at the throne—he had dragged himself away from it, she saw, three steps down, as far as the crown would let him go. His hands were pressed flat against the stone. The crown sat on his skull like something grown there, which it was: pale root-work threading through his hair, down behind his ears, its tendrils vanishing into skin that had healed over them years ago. It did not gleam. It was not gold. It looked, in the guttering light of Yara's lanterns, like bone that had learned to bloom.

He was weeping without sound.

"Paloma." Yara's hand found her elbow, steadied her. Paloma hadn't realized she was swaying. Her own blood was burning now, truly burning, the binding her grandmother had woven fraying thread by thread with every step she'd taken into magic's reach, and it felt like carrying coals under her skin, like a fever with a will. "The graft has to happen fast. Once I cut the crown's root free of him, it will have nothing to hold. It will try to take *him*. Or you."

"Or me," Paloma said. Her mouth was dry. "Yes. You mentioned."

Yara looked at her then, really looked, and Paloma saw the fear in her face and understood it wasn't for the king. It was for her. Somewhere between Vessa and the bridge and this long red climb through the palace, the hedge-witch's caution had turned into something else, and neither of them had said it aloud, because there had been no time and because saying it might make it a debt due too soon.

"You don't have to be the one who holds it," Yara said quietly.

"Yes, I do." Paloma rolled her shoulders. Drew the knife from her belt—not to fight with, but because her hands wanted a task. "It's my god. My grandmother bound it. My family built this cage, whatever else they meant by it. If someone's going to hold the screaming thing while it comes apart, it should be a Crane."

She walked up the dais steps alone.

Up close, Aldric was younger than the coins suggested and older than his years, his face mapped with sleeplessness, his jaw set in the particular way of a man who has been holding still against pain for so long that stillness has become his whole posture. When he lifted his eyes to hers, they were his. She could see that immediately. Whatever the crown had taken, whatever it whispered, what remained looking out of him was a person—a tired, frightened, stubborn person who had refused, even now, even offered his mind back for the price of her life, to become the thing wearing his face.

"You came," he said. His voice rasped like a door unused for years. "I heard the fighting. I thought—I hoped—"

"We're not very quiet," Paloma said. "Sorry about your guards."

"They weren't mine anymore."

"No," she agreed. "They weren't."

Behind her, Yara climbed the steps with her arms full: a sapling torn whole from the dead royal garden below, roots trailing soil, its young wood still wet and alive. She had carried it through fire and corrupted flesh the way other people carried infants. Now she knelt beside Aldric and began, without ceremony, without prayer, to work.

Paloma had watched surgeons in mercenary camps. She knew the rhythm of it—the assessment, the apology, the cut. But she had never seen anything like this. Yara's hands moved over the crown's root-work the way a musician's move over strings, reading it, finding where each tendril had wound itself into the king's skull, where living tissue ended and god-tissue began. Where she touched, the pale roots flinched. The air filled with a low vibration Paloma felt in her teeth rather than heard, a note held too long, a scream compressed into patience.

*You come into my house,* the voice said, and it was everywhere at once, honey poured into every corner of the room. *You come armed with dirt.*

"With a tree," Yara corrected mildly, and made the first cut.

---

The sound Aldric made was not a sound a king should make. It was not a sound anyone should have to make. Paloma had heard men die badly on a dozen fields; she had thought herself past flinching. She flinched.

"Talk to him," Yara snapped, not looking up. Her fingers were buried to the second knuckle in the root-mass, teasing a single tendril loose from the bone behind Aldric's ear. Blood welled dark around her wrists. "Keep him here. Keep him *himself*. If he goes under while I'm working, the crown takes the space he leaves."

Paloma dropped to her knees in front of the king. Took his face in her hands—it shocked him, she saw, being touched gently, and that was its own kind of grief—and put her forehead nearly against his.

"Aldric. Look at me."

His eyes were rolling back, the whites flooding, the honeyed voice pouring in through the wound Yara was making. She could feel it pressing at the edges of the room like weather.

"Your mother's garden," she said. "Tell me about it."

"What?" The word came out broken.

"The rosebush. Yara told me—you kept a memory of it. Tell me about the garden. Now. Quickly. Everything you remember."

And because there was nothing else left to hold, he held that.

"There was—" His breath shuddered. "There was a wall of hawthorn on the north side, and my mother planted peas along it because she said hawthorn made peas sweeter, which isn't true, but the peas were sweet anyway, maybe because she believed it. There was a bench with a broken leg propped on stones. She used to sit me on it and tell me the names of everything growing, even the weeds. *Especially* the weeds, she said. A weed is only a plant nobody's asked what it wants to be."

The tendril came free with a wet tearing sound. Aldric screamed. The room shook. Dust sifted from the rafters, and somewhere far below them the palace groaned on its foundations.

"Keep talking," Yara gasped.

"She let me eat things off the ground. Dirt and all. She said—" He was crying freely now, blood running from his nose, his ears, his hairline where the crown gripped. "She said a king should know what the world tastes like, or he'll think it's made of gold."

*It IS made of gold,* the crown said, sweet and vast and suddenly close, suddenly *right there*, filling the space between their faces like smoke. *I can make it so. I can make everything so. Little king. Little soldier. I have been hungry for a hundred years inside a silence, and you are all standing in the pantry—*

"It lies," Paloma said, loud, to the room, to the king, to herself. "That's all it knows how to do. It's been locked in a box since before either of us was born and everything it says is hunger wearing a pretty voice. Aldric. What did the peas taste like?"

He stared at her. Something surfaced in his eyes through the white flood—surprise, absurdly, at the question.

"Sweet," he whispered.

"Then that part's real, and it's yours, and no voice in any crown gave it to you. Hold onto that."

Two more tendrils. Three. Yara worked in terrible silence now, sweat cutting tracks through the dust on her face, the sapling waiting beside her with its roots open like arms. Each time she freed a strand of the crown from the king's skull, the fragment's voice lost a little of its reach, and each time it compensated by screaming louder, and Paloma understood with a cold clarity that they were not rescuing a hostage. They were performing surgery on a god. And gods, it turned out, bled the same way everything else did: by taking it out of whoever was nearest.

Her own veins answered the call. The burning under her skin rose to a roar as the binding her grandmother had woven finally, mercifully, catastrophically began to fail, and she felt the god's attention swing toward her like a lighthouse beam—the silence that had raised her, the absence she had prayed into for twenty years, suddenly *present*, suddenly aware, suddenly furious and grieving and reaching—

*Crane,* it said. Her name in its mouth was not honey. It was something rawer. Older. *Little Crane. You kept praying. All those years in the dead valley. Did you know I heard none of it?*

"I know," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "I know you didn't. That's why I'm here."

*Why?*

"Because nobody should be kept in the dark. Not kings. Not soldiers. Not gods." She laughed, wet and awful. "Ask my grandmother. Oh—wait. You can't. She made sure of that too."

The last great root came free.

---

What happened next happened fast and slow at once, the way the worst moments always do.

Yara seized the freed crown—the whole writhing mass of it, roots whipping like an eel's body, shrieking now in frequencies that cracked the marble—and jammed its severed base against the sapling's opened heartwood. Green light flared. Bark crept over pale root. The graft, begun.

But a graft needs time to take, and the fragment had perhaps thirty seconds of freedom left before the tree closed around it, and in those thirty seconds it went, with the desperate cunning of a dying animal, for the nearest living thing that could contain it.

For Paloma.

She felt it hit her hands before she saw it—a piece of the crown, torn loose in the struggle, small as a fistful of white root, leaping for her bare palms like iron to a lodestone. And she had a heartbeat to pull away, a heartbeat in which every survival instinct she owned, every lesson of a lifetime of escorts and ambushes and knowing when to run, screamed at her to *let go*—

She held on.

Because it was her god. Because it was screaming. Because she had spent her whole life loving a silence and here, at last, was the sound the silence had been hiding, and even broken, even corrupt, even starving—it was *hers*, in the way that wounds are yours, in the way that family is yours.

The pain was beyond describing. It was not heat or cold or cutting; it was *presence*, a century of imprisonment pouring itself into her through her palms, all the hunger and loneliness and rage of a divine thing locked in a box by a woman who loved it, and Paloma threw her head back and screamed with it, matching it note for note, giving it somewhere to go that wasn't deeper into madness.

"Hold the graft!" she heard Yara shout, from impossibly far away. "Hold the tree! Aldric—the last of it—in YOU—"

And then hands on her shoulders, steadying her like a post in a storm, and Aldric's ruined voice in her ear saying, "Peas are sweet. Peas are sweet. Peas are—" and through her own agony she felt him lean past her, felt him grip what remained of the crown on his own skull with both bleeding hands, felt him *tear*—

The world went white.

---

When Paloma came back to herself, she was lying on cold marble with Yara's coat folded under her head, and the throne room was silent except for dripping water somewhere, and the smell of sap was everywhere, thick and clean as a spring morning.

She sat up slowly. Every nerve in her body hummed like a struck bell fading.

The sapling stood where Yara had planted it—right there on the dais, roots driven down through the marble into whatever lived beneath, and it had already grown, impossibly, to the height of a man, its branches spreading, its bark smooth and new. And inside its trunk, visible where the wood had grown translucent around its captive, was the crown. Still. Quiet. Root-work threaded through living grain, pale against brown, like frost melting into earth.

At its base, on his knees among the shattered remains of a hundred years of tyranny, King Aldric was touching his own head with trembling fingers. Bare head. Scarred, bloody, bald in patches where the roots had been—but bare.

"It's quiet," he kept saying. "It's quiet. It's quiet."

"Let him be a moment," Yara murmured. She was kneeling too, her hands filthy with sap and soil, her face gray with exhaustion, and when Paloma met her eyes there was such relief in them that Paloma had to look away before she started crying.

But then she saw it.

On the marble beside the tree's roots lay a single fragment of pale root, no bigger than a finger joint, overlooked—or released, she would wonder later, unable to decide which frightened her more. It twitched faintly. Fading. Dying, maybe, without a skull to root in or a tree to grieve in.

Her hands moved before her mind did. She picked it up.

It should have burned her again. It didn't. It lay in her palm like a splinter of moonlight, warm, fluttering, small—so much smaller than the voice had ever seemed, smaller than the prophecy, smaller than the war, smaller than a hundred years of silence. A prisoner the size of a prayer.

And it spoke.

Not with honey. Not with command. Not with the vast resonant pressure that had filled throne rooms and battlefields and her own skull. It spoke the way a child speaks, or a bird, or wind through a doorway—small and plain and true, the first honest words it had managed in a century of captivity:

*Thank you.*

Paloma's breath left her all at once.

*It hurts less,* the fragment said, wondering, in that small clear voice. *The light. I had forgotten there was light.*

"It's a tree now," Paloma managed. Her tears fell onto her cupped hands, onto the little root, and where they landed the pale wood flushed faintly green. "You're in a tree. In a garden. Nobody's going to shut you in the dark again. I swear it. On my name, on my grandmother's name, on every prayer I wasted and every one I didn't."

The fragment pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat settling.

*Blessings on the crane,* it whispered. *Blessings on the gardener. Blessings on the king who knows what peas taste like.* A pause, and then, fainter, almost shy, almost amused: *Tell the company their god is sorry about the valley.*

Paloma laughed—a broken, astonished, healing sound—and closed her fingers gently around the smallest god in the world, and carried it to the tree, and pressed it into the soil at its roots, where it sank down willingly, gratefully, home.

Above them, through the shattered windows of the throne room, dawn was coming up over the capital. And somewhere below, in the occupied streets, boots were running and bells were ringing and the usurper's hollow empire was beginning, all at once, to learn that its heart had stopped.

Yara took Paloma's hand.

"Come on," she said. "There's still one tyrant left to bury."
