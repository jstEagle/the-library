## 21. The Orchard of New Fruit

Yara had never trusted a tree she could not taste.

She stood in the restored royal garden on the first true morning of spring, barefoot in the wet grass, and considered the small tree at its center with the wariness of long habit. It was not a beautiful thing, yet. Its trunk was crooked where the graft had taken — iron-grey bark over living wood, like a scar that had decided to become useful — and its branches spread thin and uncertain, as if it were still deciding what shape it wanted to be. The leaves were ordinary green. That was the miracle, and Yara checked for it every morning: ordinary green, no silver shimmer, no whisper when the wind moved through them.

No power. Only shade.

She reached up and touched one leaf anyway, because trust was a practice, not a feeling, and practices required repetition. The leaf was cool and slightly waxy against her fingertips. It did not offer her a memory. It did not murmur with a dead man's voice or a god's hunger or a usurper's honeyed lies. It simply existed, doing the plain work of being alive — drinking light, breathing slow, asking nothing of anyone.

"Good morning," Yara told it.

The tree said nothing back, which was exactly right.

Behind her, the garden was waking the way gardens wake — messily, imperfectly, in defiance of every plan ever drawn for them. She had spent three months on her knees in this soil, coaxing life back into ground that had been sealed under flagstones for a century, and the garden repaid her by growing whatever it pleased wherever it pleased. Roses climbed through the cracks of a fountain that would never hold water again. A pear tree — grown from a cutting she'd smuggled out of her own orchard wrapped in damp cloth, pressed to her chest the whole desperate road to the capital — leaned at a drunken angle near the eastern wall, already setting blossom far too early because it remembered spring differently than this valley did. Weeds came up in profusion, and Yara let most of them stay. A garden that only grew what it was told was called a grave.

She heard footsteps on the gravel path — two sets, unhurried — and did not turn. In the months since the siege, she had learned the garden's footstep-language the way she'd once learned birdsong: Aldric walked like a man still surprised to own his own head, light and listening. Sarel walked like a soldier who had put down her sword but could not convince her spine of it.

"You're talking to it again," Sarel said.

"I talk to everything. You should be flattered when I do."

Aldric laughed — a real laugh now, unguarded, the laugh of a man whose mouth answered only to himself — and came to stand beside her at the tree. He looked better than he had any right to. There was still the pale seam of scarring around his hairline where the crown had been torn free, a ring of old iron's kiss that would fade but never wholly vanish, and he wore his hair short now so everyone could see it. He had made that choice deliberately, Yara knew. He wanted no one to forget what a crown had cost him. He wanted no one to think the next one might come cheaper.

"It's taller," he said.

"An inch, maybe." Yara tilted her head, judging. "It grows slowly. I think that's the point of it. Whatever it was before — all that hunger, all those centuries of wanting — it's learning to be patient. Soil does that. Sunlight does that. Things that grow fast are usually trying to escape something."

"And you're certain there's nothing left in it? Nothing... listening?" Aldric asked it quietly, the way he asked everything now — carefully, as if words were load-bearing beams.

"The only thing listening is me," Yara said. "And the worms. Worms hear everything and repeat nothing. There's a lesson in that for governments."

Sarel snorted. "You should advise the council more often. They'd govern better and hate themselves for laughing."

* * *

They walked the garden together, the three of them, because this too had become ritual. Once a week, when the season allowed, the king and the general and the witch walked the rows of the restored royal garden, and anyone in the palace who wished to speak to them plainly could simply come and find them there. No throne room audiences. No kneeling. Aldric had abolished the kneel-and-wait within his first month, and half the court had been scandalized, and the other half had been relieved, and both halves had gotten used to it faster than anyone predicted.

That was how mending worked, Yara had come to understand. Not like healing, which closed things cleanly if you were lucky. Mending was grafting — binding two living things that were never meant to be joined, wounding them both a little to make them one, then holding the joint still and patient until they forgot they had ever been separate. It looked ugly for a long time. Then one morning you noticed the branch was bearing fruit, and you couldn't say anymore which tree the fruit belonged to.

The free cities' envoys arrived today for the spring council, and the whole palace was stiff with ceremony-anxiety, so the walk in the garden was also, quietly, a rehearsal.

"Vessa's envoy wants the bridge rebuilt with toll rights shared," Sarel said, ticking matters off on her fingers. "Corvei wants grain tariffs suspended through harvest. And the hill clans want — " she paused, and her mouth twitched, " — the hill clans want Aldric to come drink with them and settle it properly, which their envoy insists cannot be done sober or by proxy."

"He's not wrong," Aldric said.

"Aldric."

"Sarel. They lost forty fighters at the Broken Bridge holding my cause. If they want me drunk on fermented goat's milk swearing brotherhood, I'll drink the goat dry." He smiled, then let it fade into honesty. "I ruled for six years wearing someone else's voice in my skull. I signed orders I don't remember signing. I burned villages I loved from the inside of my own eyes. Every decision I make now, I want witnesses. I want argument. I want it slow and loud and sometimes drunk on goat's milk, because the alternative — the quiet, certain, unquestioned word of a king — is how the last crown got its roots into me in the first place."

Yara watched Sarel take that in. The general had refused every title offered her — refused *general*, finally settling for *marshal of the roads* because it sounded like a job rather than an honor — and she stood now with her hands clasped behind her back, weight even on both feet, a woman permanently braced for an order she'd have to disobey. But at Aldric's words something in her shoulders eased, just a degree.

"Then we argue," Sarel said. "I'm good at arguing."

"You're good at winning arguments," Aldric said. "Try losing one occasionally. It builds character in the winner."

* * *

Midmorning brought the first of the envoys up the garden path, and Yara withdrew to her work bench by the potting shed, because councils were not her country and she had seedlings that needed her more than politics did. But she kept half an ear on the proceedings, the way a gardener keeps half an ear on weather.

The Vessa envoy was a merchant named Odile Ferran, sharp-eyed and sharper-tongued, who had once mocked Paloma Crane to her face in front of her own council and had lived to regret it with unusual grace. She walked the garden now with visible respect, touching nothing without asking.

"The council's position," Ferran said, "is that the kingdom and the cities should be bound by trade first, treaty second, and sentiment third."

"My position," Aldric said, "is that I agree entirely, and that sentiment will sneak in regardless, the way mint sneaks into a garden. You may as well plan for it."

Ferran glanced at Yara. "Does everything here grow whether you want it or not?"

"Yes," Yara said, not looking up from the seedling tray. "That's what makes it alive. Dead things hold still."

The Corvei delegation came at noon, and the hill clan speaker came mid-afternoon smelling gloriously of woodsmoke, and between them Yara repotted eleven seedlings, pruned the roses back from the dead fountain, and turned the compost, which she found more honest than most diplomacy. Once, she caught herself listening to Aldric explain — patiently, without a trace of command-voice — why the grain tariff suspension should run two seasons instead of one, and she realized she was smiling at a pile of rotting vegetable matter like a fool.

Once, she would have called this scene impossible. A king arguing terms as an equal. A soldier-turned-marshal keeping order without raising her hand. Cities sending envoys instead of mercenaries. And underneath it all, a planted crown growing shade.

But Yara had spent her whole life learning that impossible was mostly a word people used for things that hadn't been tended yet.

* * *

In the golden hour before dusk, when the envoys had gone off to feast and argue elsewhere, Paloma came to the garden.

Yara heard her before she saw her — the particular stride of her, boots and certainty — and felt the familiar warmth arrive ahead of her, the way it always did now. Six months ago, Yara had hidden this woman inside the uprooted roots of a memory-tree while scouts burned a farmstead fifty yards away. Six months. It felt like a different life, and it was; neither of them lived in it anymore.

Paloma stopped at the gate and looked around at the garden with her hands on her hips, taking inventory the way she took inventory of everything — walls, exits, sight lines, and then, always last, beauty.

"Blossom's early on your pear," she said.

"It remembers a warmer spring. From the orchard. I grafted it with a branch that grew the memory of the best year we ever had." Yara wiped her hands on her apron. "It blooms early and drops its fruit late and sulks if you move it. It's the most sentimental tree I own."

Paloma came and stood beside her at the small crooked tree at the garden's heart. For a while they just looked at it together. This had become a habit too, though neither of them had agreed to it aloud. On Paloma's rare visits to the capital, she always ended up here, always at this tree, always saying little.

"It's shading the bench now," Paloma observed. "Last time I sat there it was full sun."

"Told you. An inch at a time."

"Do you ever..." Paloma paused. Yara had learned to wait through her pauses; the mercenary spent words like coin and gave away change only carefully. "Do you ever hear anything from it? Even now?"

"No." Yara said it gently, because she knew what lay under the question. Paloma had held the god-fragment screaming in her bare hands. Paloma had felt it go still, and soft, and pass into the soil like rain soaking into drought-ground. Some part of her needed to know it hadn't died screaming. "It dreams, maybe. Roots dream. But if there's anything left of it in there, it isn't hungry, and it isn't lonely, and it isn't speaking. It's just... being wood. Which I think, for a piece of a god, might be the kindest retirement anyone's ever arranged."

Paloma nodded slowly. "My grandmother bound it to protect us. Your garden freed it to rest. Between the two of you—" she huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh, "—it got a better ending than either of us probably will."

"Don't jinx my compost."

* * *

They stayed through sunset. Aldric joined them eventually, without escort, carrying three cups of wine and no ceremony, and the four of them — king, marshal, witch, mercenary — sat under the young tree's new shade like the world's least dignified council, and talked about nothing important. The price of wool in Vessa. Whether goats or geese were stupider (the hill clans' contribution to the day's debates, relayed secondhand). Whether Yara's pear tree could be taught to grow memories on purpose again, now that it was happy, and what a person might want to remember well enough to plant it.

"A wedding," Yara said, and then felt her ears go hot, because she hadn't meant to say that one aloud.

Paloma went very still beside her.

"I mean — hypothetically," Yara added, staring hard at her wine. "If someone wanted to. Remember a wedding well. Grow it. Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically," Paloma repeated, in the tone of a woman cataloguing the word for later use.

Aldric, with the exquisite timing of a man who had spent years listening to a voice in his skull and had developed a genius for knowing when *not* to speak, stood up, announced that the wine was better indoors, and departed with Sarel in tow, leaving them alone in the blue dusk under the young tree.

Silence. Blossom-scent. Somewhere beyond the wall, the city going about its unremarkable evening — cart wheels, a dog, children shouting at a game — the sound of a kingdom that was merely alive, which after everything was the most remarkable sound Yara knew.

"The orchard needs tending," Paloma said at last, to the middle distance. "Your orchard. The real one. The cuttings you brought, the mother trees — Brannoc's people have been watching them, but they're gardeners, not..." She gestured vaguely. "Not you."

"They've managed fine."

"They've managed adequately. There's a difference. Ask any soldier." Paloma turned then, and looked at her straight, the way she did everything — directly, with her whole stubborn weight behind it. "I'm at loose ends. The company's settled its debts, the prophecy book has a blank last page, and I find I don't know how to want anything anymore except—" she stopped, jaw working, and tried again, "—except mornings that aren't going anywhere in particular. With someone who talks to trees."

Yara's heart was doing something undignified. She made it wait.

"That," she said, "was almost romantic. For a mercenary."

"I'm working with limited material."

"Come at harvest," Yara said. "Help me bring in the fruit. Stay through the pruning. Learn which branches to keep and which to let go." She reached over and took Paloma's hand — callused sword-hand, warm as banked coals — and laced their fingers together with the deliberate care of someone setting a graft. "Stay past that, if the wanting holds. Gardens are patient. So am I. Mostly."

Paloma looked down at their joined hands for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough.

"Hypothetically," she said, "that sounds like the best campaign I've ever been offered."

* * *

Later — much later, when the stars were out and the palace slept and Yara walked the garden alone as she always did before bed, checking each tree like a shepherd counting sheep — she paused at the young tree at the center and laid her palm flat against its crooked trunk.

The bark had softened over the winter. Where it had once been iron-hard, it was now merely wood, faintly warm from the day's sun, and beneath her hand she felt the slow lift and fall of sap rising, the tree breathing its long green breath. No whisper. No hunger. Just growth, steady and unambitious, an inch at a time.

Above her, the new leaves stirred in the night wind, and among them — high on the highest branch, where she hadn't thought to look — Yara saw, for the first time, the pale beginnings of buds. Flower-buds, tiny and tight and utterly ordinary.

The tree that had worn a crown was going to bloom in the morning.

Yara stood in the dark a long moment, hand on the warm wood, and found she was smiling, and that the smile had tears in it somewhere, and that both were perfectly in order.

"Well then," she whispered to it. "Let's see what kind of fruit you make."

The wind moved through the garden, through the roses and the pears and the weeds she'd let stay, and carried the scent of blossom out over the wall, into the sleeping city, toward the morning coming — and somewhere far off, at the valley's edge where the Godless hills met the living green, Yara would have sworn the air itself leaned toward it, listening, waiting for spring to keep its promise everywhere at once.
