## 22. Yara and the Garden

The word came in spring, on a morning so ordinary it should have been ashamed of itself.

Paloma was kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, pressing a sapling's roots into the dark soil of the valley's edge, when the god spoke.

*Welcome.*

That was all. No thunder. No command wrapped in gold and fire. No voice like the crown's had been, honeyed and hungry, coiling into the ear. Just one word, plain as bread, arriving not through her ears but through some older door she hadn't known she carried — and then gone again, the way a bird leaves a branch without disturbing it.

Paloma sat back on her heels. Her hands were filthy. There was mud under her fingernails and a smear of it along her jaw where she'd pushed back her hair, and the sun was warm on the back of her neck, and somewhere behind her Yara was arguing gently with a plum tree about the angle of its new graft.

*Welcome.*

She laughed. It came out of her sideways, half a sob, startling a finch off the fence post. A hundred years of silence. A company of mercenaries who had stopped praying because prayer felt like shouting down a well. A grandmother who had bound that silence around her own bloodline like a bandage around a wound, trading her descendants' faith for their safety, and never told them the price. And after all of it — the prophecy book, the road, the battle at the broken bridge, Brannoc's body going still beneath the memory-trees, the throne room where Paloma had held a screaming fragment of divinity in her bare hands while it offered her everything she'd ever wanted if only she would walk away — after all of that, the god's first word to her was *welcome*, spoken the way a neighbor says good morning over a fence.

No commands. That was the part that undid her. She had spent her whole life imagining what an answered prayer would feel like, and in every version of it there had been orders. March here. Kill this man. Bear this name. She had assumed that was what gods were: commanders too large to see, issuing instructions through the static of the world. But the binding had been the command, all along. The silence had been the order. And now that it was dissolved — now that Yara's grafting-knife and Paloma's own burned hands had unmade it root and branch — what remained underneath was something almost embarrassingly simple. A presence, vast and patient and unhurried, that had been waiting a century for its people to come home, and whose entire message upon their arrival was: you are welcome here.

Paloma pressed both muddy palms flat against the earth and stayed like that for a long time, breathing.

Behind her, Yara had stopped arguing with the plum tree. Paloma didn't have to look to know the garden-witch was watching her; she could feel it the way she'd learned to feel weather coming, a change in pressure, a hush. When she finally turned, Yara was standing among the young trees with her hands folded in her apron, dirt to the wrists, silver-streaked hair escaping its braid, wearing the particular expression she reserved for things she refused to comment on until asked.

"Well?" Paloma said. Her voice cracked on it.

"You heard something."

"One word." Paloma wiped her face with the back of her wrist and only succeeded in redistributing the mud. "One ordinary word. I held a piece of a god in my hands and it screamed at me for hours, and my own god says *welcome* like I've come in from the cold and left my boots at the door."

Yara smiled. It transformed her whole face, as it always did — the careful, guarded hedge-witch vanishing for a moment and someone younger looking out. "What did you expect?"

"I don't know. Judgment. Instructions. A list." Paloma looked down at her hands. The scars from the throne room had healed into pale ridges across both palms, the mark of a woman who had gripped divinity without gloves. "I expected it to want something from me. Everything wants something. The crown wanted Aldric. The usurper wanted the crown. The free cities wanted an army they didn't have to pay for. My whole life, everything that ever claimed to love me also claimed to own me." She flexed her scarred fingers. "And this just — opens the door."

"Maybe," Yara said, crouching beside her and pressing a palm to the newly planted sapling, "that's what makes it a god worth hearing."

They worked in silence after that, and the silence was different now. Paloma noticed it all day — the way quiet had changed texture since the unbinding. Before, silence had been absence, a held breath, a wall. Now it was fullness. The valley's edge hummed faintly with living magic, the first green shoots of it creeping up from the lowlands where the Godless Valley's dead air met the world's returning warmth, and every growing thing in it seemed to be murmuring to itself, and beneath all of it, steady as groundwater, the god's presence pooled without asking anything at all.

By evening the sapling was settled, watered, mulched. It was the fourth of the new orchard — cuttings and seedlings brought from the royal garden, from the tree that had grown where the crown had been planted, the tree that bore no power and no fruit, only shade. This one was different. This one Yara had grafted with a cutting of memory-wood, the old orchard's line, and when Paloma asked what it would grow, Yara had only shrugged and said, honestly: "I don't know. That's rather the point."

* * *

The house at the valley's edge had belonged to a shepherd once, before the wars emptied the borderlands. It was small and crooked and its roof leaked in exactly two places, which Yara insisted was a manageable number. They had repaired it together over the winter, badly at first and then less badly, and by spring it had become the kind of place Paloma had never lived in: a place that wasn't a camp, wasn't a barracks, wasn't a fortress or a hideout or a cell. A place that was simply hers. Theirs. She still caught herself cataloguing exits every time she entered a room, counting sightlines, noting where a blade lay within reach. She suspected she always would. But the exits were beginning to matter less than the hearth, and the two chairs Yara had made from the wood of the uprooted memory-tree — the same tree whose roots had hidden Paloma from the usurper's scouts on the night they fled together — and the shelf where the prophecy book lived.

The book sat there now, closed, its leather cover soft as old skin. Paloma fetched it down after supper, while Yara trimmed the lamp, and turned to the last written page.

Her grandmother's hand. She knew it well now, better than she had ever known the woman herself — the small, fierce, forward-leaning script of someone writing against time. *The Crane of the dateless night shall unmake the crowns.* All those years Paloma had thought the prophecy was a sentence passed on her, a fate written by strangers. Then she had learned it was written by family, and thought it a cage built by blood. Only at the end, in the workshop beneath the ruined chapel, reading the letters her grandmother had left like breadcrumbs for a granddaughter she'd never meet, had she understood the truth: it was neither sentence nor cage. It was a map out of a trap. Her grandmother had seen what the binding would cost — had watched it drain the life out of her own daughter, Paloma's mother, slowly, invisibly — and had known she couldn't undo what she'd done without dooming everyone the silence protected. So she had done the only thing left to her. She had written the way out, sealed it in a book, and sent it wandering through a century of dying archivists until it found the one person who could follow it.

*Unbind the king,* the prophecy commanded, *and the Crane goes free.*

It hadn't mentioned that freedom would cost Brannoc. It hadn't mentioned Sarel Vane kneeling in the ashes of two cities, offering stolen war-plans at knifepoint, and Paloma choosing to trust her. It hadn't mentioned Aldric, crowned and hollowed, clinging to his mother's garden the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, and how he had wept when the crown came free and the whispering stopped, and how the first thing he'd said with his own voice, ragged and disbelieving, was his mother's name. Prophecies are maps, Paloma thought, turning the page. Maps show the road. They don't show you who you'll be carrying by the end of it.

The next page was blank. So were all the pages after it, down to the worn leather of the back cover. Her grandmother had filled the book with one journey and left the rest empty, and Paloma had understood, standing in the throne room with the god-fragment cooling in her hands, why the final page had always been meant for her.

She dipped the pen. Yara watched from across the table, saying nothing, trimming nothing, giving her the room.

Paloma wrote the date. Then she wrote:

*The crowns are unmade. The king is free. The god has spoken one word, and it was enough.*

She stopped. The pen hovered. Below those lines stretched the blankness her grandmother had left her, and Paloma found, to her surprise, that she did not want to fill it. Every instinct of the mercenary, the commander, the woman who had called in a hundred debts to raise a ragged host and held a bridge against an army — every instinct said: finish it. Write the ending. Name what comes next so it can't surprise you. Control the ground.

But that was the old grammar. Command was a language for war, and the war was over. What remained was a garden, and gardens did not obey sentences.

So instead of an ending, Paloma wrote a single line more:

*What grows here, no one has written yet.*

Then she closed the book, returned it to its shelf, and went to stand in the doorway where the evening was coming down purple and gold over the young orchard, and let the future stay unwritten.

* * *

Aldric's letter arrived with the month's trade cart, along with a crate of Vessa glassware that Sarel had sent with a note reading, characteristically, *For the leaky roof. Fix it or I'll hear about it forever.* The letter was in Aldric's own hand now — no scribe, no crown dictating — and it wandered amiably, the way letters do when their writer is still relearning the pleasure of his own thoughts.

He wrote that the free cities' council had ratified the new accord, each city seated as an equal beside the throne, and that the first session had devolved into a three-hour argument about grain tariffs, which he described as the happiest argument of his reign. He wrote that Sarel had refused the marshalcy twice and accepted it on the condition that she be allowed to spend the first year cataloguing the dead of both armies, city by city, name by name, so that no one could ever again call a war generous. He wrote that the crown-tree in the restored royal garden had put out blossom that spring — white flowers, scentless, harmless — and that children were allowed to play beneath it, and that he sometimes stood at his window watching them and thinking about gardens.

*You were right,* he wrote, in the last paragraph, in ink slightly blurred as though the page had waited under a falling drop. *Power surrendered is the only power that doesn't rot. I am trying to learn to set things down. It is harder than holding them. Come visit when the roads are safe. Bring your witch. The roses miss her.*

"The roses miss her," Paloma read aloud, and Yara, mending a net by the fire, snorted. "He's flirting with you."

"He is *not*. He's being gracious."

"He named a rosebush after me, Paloma. In the royal garden. Sarel told me. It's called *Yara's Defiance* and it blooms out of season out of pure spite."

"That's — " Paloma considered. "All right, that does sound like flirting."

Yara threw a sock at her.

Later, lamp trimmed, roof ticking as it cooled, Paloma lay awake listening to the valley's edge breathe. The god was there — not speaking, not needing to, present the way the mountain is present, the way the water table is present. Some nights she still reached for it the way an old soldier reaches for a weapon that isn't at his hip, expecting silence, bracing for the ache of it. And some nights — more of them now — she remembered in time, and the remembering itself was a kind of prayer, and the answer was always the same patient, unhurried, undemanding nearness. Welcome. Still welcome. Always welcome, apparently, whether she knelt or cursed or forgot it entirely for weeks at a time.

She had prayed for a century for a voice. She had imagined epiphany, ordination, purpose handed down like a sword. Instead she had gotten a door left open, and the discovery that what she'd actually wanted, all along, was simply not to be alone in the dark.

Beside her, Yara murmured in her sleep and flung an arm across Paloma's middle, pinning her to the bed with the casual tyranny of the deeply asleep. Outside, wind moved through the young orchard, and Paloma fancied — only fancied, she was a mercenary, she trusted steel and ledgers and little else — that the memory-grafted saplings rustled with voices not quite their own. The trees remembered. That was their nature. Somewhere in their rings were stored the usurper's regrets, the garrison's last prayers, Brannoc laughing the winter before the bridge, her grandmother's pen scratching through a century-old night. And someday, perhaps, these new trees would hold other memories: this room, this wind, this exact weight of an arm and this exact quality of peace, kept green long after the two women in the bed were soil themselves.

Paloma fell asleep mid-thought, unafraid, for the first time in her life, of what tomorrow might remember about tonight.

* * *

Planting season came, and with it the whole strange congregation of their new life.

Brannoc's sword hung above the door, cleaned and oiled, retired. Once a year, on the anniversary of the bridge, Paloma took it down and carried it up the hill to the cairn they'd raised him beneath, and told him about the year — the tariffs, the roses, the roof that still leaked in one stubborn corner — and once a year she swore she could hear him complaining that she'd wasted a perfectly good mercenary career on agriculture. The company itself had splintered into honest shapes: half had taken Aldric's offer of land and stipend, half had signed on with Sarel's new border watch, and a dozen stubborn ones had drifted back to the valley's edge with their tents and their debts and their refusal to stop calling her Captain, and now they grew apples badly and guarded the orchard fiercely and had, to a woman, started praying again. Not because anyone ordered it. Because the god answered, now, in its one plain word, and it turned out that a single *welcome*, repeated faithfully over years, was enough to rebuild what a century of silence had torn down.

On the first warm morning of the season, Paloma and Yara walked the rows with baskets of cuttings: scions from the crown-tree, budwood from the old memory-orchard, and — Yara's newest experiment, handled with tongs and visible suspicion — a few seeds from the plum that had grown, inexplicably, where the god-fragment had been buried after the unbinding. Those seeds were warm to the touch. Yara wouldn't plant them near anything else. "Let's see what it wants to be first," she'd said. "Everything deserves a chance to introduce itself before it moves in next door."

They worked until noon, and at noon they sat in the shade of the first tree — the original, the one grown from the planted crown, broad now and heavy-limbed, casting a circle of cool dark that bore no power and asked for nothing — and ate bread and hard cheese and the season's first strawberries, which tasted, Paloma thought, exactly the way peace tasted: ordinary, specific, impossible to describe to anyone who hadn't earned it.

"Do you ever miss it?" Yara asked suddenly. She was looking out at the rows, but Paloma knew her well enough now to know she wasn't seeing them. "The road. The work. Being the Crane of the dateless night. You were made for it, in a way. You were *good*."

Paloma chewed the strawberry slowly, considering. The honest answer was yes, sometimes — in dreams, mostly, where she still walked walls at night and counted exits and woke with her hand curled around a sword-hilt that wasn't there. But the fuller truth was stranger. She missed the road the way you miss a fever: aware of its heat, grateful for its breaking.

"I was good at it," she said at last, "because I was angry. Anger aims well. But it doesn't grow anything." She brushed crumbs from her hands and nodded at the orchard, the house, the smoke rising thin and straight from their crooked chimney. "This is the first thing I've ever been part of that gets better when I'm patient. I'm learning patience. Badly. Ask the plum tree."

"I heard about the plum tree."

"The plum tree and I have an understanding."

Yara laughed, leaned her head against Paloma's shoulder, and for a while they just sat, two women in the shade of an unarmed crown, while bees worked the white scentless blossoms and the god said nothing at all, which was, Paloma had come to understand, its way of saying everything was well.

That evening, she took down the prophecy book one last time that season. Not to write — she had written her line, and meant to leave the rest of the pages to whoever needed them next, some granddaughter not yet born, some stranger carrying a burning name through a valley of silence a hundred years from now. She took it down only to look, running her thumb over her grandmother's fierce handwriting, over her own plainer script beneath it, over the clean white emptiness waiting beyond.

Then she set the book on the windowsill, open to the first blank page, where the evening light could reach it — an invitation rather than a record — and went out to close the gate against the goats, and the last light of the dateless night's long ending fell across the empty page and found nothing written there at all.

Which was, Paloma decided, exactly right.

The future, like the orchard, was planted. What it would grow was no one's to say — not a prophet's, not a god's, not even hers.

Only the garden's. And the garden was in no hurry.
