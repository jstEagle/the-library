## 1. Charms for the Godless

The bell above the stall had not rung in seven years, and Bram Oyelaran had stopped listening for it.

She said that to herself every morning as she knotted the first cord of the day—*it has not rung, it will not ring, and you will go on anyway*—because a lie told at dawn had a way of holding until dusk, and dusk was all she needed. The Shepherd's voice had gone out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers: no drama, no thunder, just an absence where warmth used to be, and then seven years of learning to live in the shape of that absence.

She worked the cord now with hands that knew their business. Hemp, three strands, nine knots, the last one left deliberately loose—a luck-knot, sold to sailors and gamblers and new mothers, worth two coppers because it was pretty and four because of what she whispered into the final loop when nobody was watching. What she whispered was nothing. That was the trick of it. She moved the power without naming it, shaped the blessing without speaking it aloud, kept everything so small and quiet that even she could almost pretend it wasn't there.

A gift too faint to catch. A god too silent to answer. Between those two mercies, she had built a life.

"Oyelaran." The voice came from down the row of stalls, gravelled and familiar, and Bram did not look up from her knotting. "You're up early."

"Sergeant Doss is up early," she corrected, tying off. "I never went to bed."

Doss leaned against the post of her awning the way he always did, as if he owned it and was merely permitting her to stand beneath it. He was greyer than he'd been when they'd marched together under the Shepherd's banner, back when hedge-witches like her were worth more to the crown alive than dead—scouts, camp-healers, charm-menders for men whose boots leaked and whose fevers burned. That contract had lapsed quietly, the way good things do. Now he wore the city watch instead of the king's colours, and he brought her stolen plums in season and news out of season.

"You heard about Corren Marsh?" he said, low.

Bram's hands kept moving. They were very good hands for keeping still-looking while doing something else entirely. "Corren Marsh is a day's ride east and none of my concern."

"The Hedgewife of Marsh Hollow was taken last week. Dragged out of her own garden." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; he'd learned it from watching her. "They're saying cleansing now. Official word. Not bandits, not fever. Cleansing."

"Cleansing," Bram repeated, tasting the word like bad water. Her needle-fingers finished the ninth knot and left it loose. "That's a church word. There hasn't been a church with teeth since the old king died."

"There's a new kind of tooth coming, then." Doss straightened, glancing down the market row where the morning crowd was thickening—fishwives and coal-haulers, a knot of students from the chandler's college, two children chasing each other between the barrows. Ordinary noise. Ordinary morning. The kind of morning that made you forget you were hiding inside it. "I'm telling you because I liked fighting beside you, Bram, and because I don't want to be the man who has to arrest you. Watch yourself. Whatever you sell in these knots—" his eyes flicked down, brief and deliberate, to her hands— "sell less of it."

He dropped a copper on the board, took a luck-knot he didn't need, and walked away whistling something tuneless, a big slow man pretending very hard to be nothing but a big slow man.

Bram watched him go, then looked at her stock: forty luck-knots, a dozen fever-charms stitched into felt, six cradle-bells, three love-tokens she charged extra for out of contempt. Two coppers each. Four if they worked.

She thought about Corren Marsh, and the Hedgewife pulled out of her garden by the roots like a weed.

Then she thought about rent, and the cold coming, and the girl-shaped hole where courage usually lived, and she pinned the sign to her awning that said *CHARMS & KNOTWORK — FAIR PRICES*, and she began to sell.

* * *

The market of Vell woke around her the way it always did, in layers. First the fishmongers, shouting prices before the sun cleared the eastern wall. Then the bakers, whose smell did half their advertising for them. Then the whole tide of it—the city pouring itself into the square, ten thousand lives braiding together, and Bram sitting in the middle of the braid with her little table of string and felt, invisible on purpose, useful on purpose, safe on purpose.

She sold a fever-charm to a laundress whose son was coughing through the night. She sold a luck-knot to a young man shipping out on the *Pale Meridian* who laughed and said he didn't believe in it, and paid anyway, and asked her to make sure it was a strong one, and she lied beautifully and said all hers were the same. She sold cradle-bells to a grandmother with forearms like ship rope, who haggled her down from four coppers to three and then, in triumph, gave her five.

This was the life she'd built. This was the price of it: small magic, small sales, small hours. Never enough power in one place to draw an eye. Never enough hope in one heart to break her own.

It was nearly noon when the woman came.

Bram noticed customers in categories, a habit from her soldiering days—young or old, hurried or browsing, trouble or no trouble—and this one arrived as *no trouble*: middle years, drab wool dress, the soft hands of someone who did fine work indoors. A seamstress, maybe, or a clerk's wife. She stood at the stall turning a luck-knot over in her fingers with the particular intensity of a person who has come for one thing and is working up to it.

"For my husband," the woman said finally. "He's a carter. The north road." Her mouth did something complicated. "There are wolves on the north road this year. Or people say there are."

"People say," Bram agreed, and reached for the strongest cord she had.

And that should have been the end of it. Two coppers, a blessing folded small, a lie about how strong it was, a woman walking away safer and none the wiser. Seven years, hundreds of sales, and the pattern had held so long it felt like law.

But the woman lingered. And Bram, who survived by noticing things, noticed that her eyes were red-rimmed, and that she kept touching the same spot below her collarbone, and that when she spoke again her voice had changed—had *dropped*, somehow, into a lower register, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere far away.

"They said you were good," the woman said. "At the real ones."

The words landed in Bram's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard the splash long before she heard anything else.

"I don't know what you mean," Bram said pleasantly. "Mine are all the same."

"My mother was gifted." The woman's hand came up from her collarbone, and hanging from it, on a cheap tin chain, was something Bram recognized with a sick lurch: a fever-charm. Not one of hers. Older. Hand-stitched, the felt gone grey with age, the knotwork in a style thirty years out of fashion. A dead woman's charm, worn against the skin like a relic. "Before the last purge. She taught me to feel them. To feel—" the woman's voice caught, and something underneath her skin seemed to catch with it— "to feel whether they're *alive*."

"No," Bram said, and didn't know which question she was answering.

Because the woman was right. That was the horror of it. The old charm on the tin chain was dead, cold as riverstone, its blessing long since bled out of the world—but the luck-knot in her other hand, the one Bram had tied at dawn with her dawn-lie whispering through it, was not dead. It was waking. Bram could feel it happening, feel her own small careful power rising off the cord like heat off a road, responding to proximity, to grief, to whatever raw open thing the woman carried in her blood like an unhealed wound.

*I made it too well today,* Bram thought, with terrible clarity. *Seven years of making them badly enough, and today I got tired, and I tied one true.*

"Stop," she said, sharper now, reaching across the table. "Give it here. Give it—"

The charm flared.

Not much. That was the obscene part, later—that after everything, it took so little. A thread of light along the hemp, gold-white, bright as a struck match, coiling once around the woman's fingers like something glad to be seen. Half a heartbeat. Less. In a dark room, nobody would have noticed. But the market of Vell at noon was not a dark room, and the sun chose that moment to slip behind a cloud, and three separate people within ten feet saw a seamstress's wife standing at a charm-stall with light running over her hands like water over stones.

The woman gasped. The luck-knot fell. Bram snatched it out of the air before it hit the boards and closed her fist around it, smothering the last of the glow against her palm, feeling it gutter and die like a firefly cupped in a child's hands.

Silence, for exactly as long as it takes a market to decide what it has seen.

Then the noise started—not screaming, not yet, but the particular murmur, the *did-you-see*, the turning of heads, a fishwife's laugh dying halfway out of her throat. A boy pointed. Somewhere behind Bram a shutter banged. And at the edge of the crowd, unhurried, a man in plain grey clothes who had been examining cheese stopped examining cheese and began, very casually, to move closer.

Inquisitor. Bram didn't have a word for it yet—wouldn't have the word for another few hours—but her body knew, the way a deer knows, the way every hunted thing knows. Plain clothes, patient feet, eyes that weren't looking at the cheese anymore and never really had been.

"Take your charm," Bram said through her teeth, pressing the dead knot into the woman's hand and folding the woman's fingers shut over it. "Go home. Don't wear the old one. Burn it tonight, hear me? Burn both of them and scatter the ash in running water."

"But my husband—"

"*Go.*"

The woman went, clutching her fistful of evidence, and Bram turned back to her stall and began packing her goods with the smooth efficiency of long practice, and all the while her mind ran cold and fast ahead of her body, laying out the arithmetic of ruin.

One flare. One witness. Maybe three. In the old days—one year ago, even—it would have been nothing. A drunk's tale, a trick of light, a charm-maker's sleight of hand. People wanted explanations that kept the world ordinary, and they would have found one, given time.

But Corren Marsh had been cleansed. The Hedgewife of Marsh Hollow had been pulled out of her garden. And Sergeant Doss had stood at her awning this very morning and told her, kindly, in front of God and everybody, that the crown was sharpening a new kind of tooth.

The man in grey had stopped moving. He stood perhaps fifteen feet away, at the corner of the chandler's stall, doing a masterful imitation of a man waiting for someone. He was good. He was better than good—he had the slouch, the boredom, the whole costume of it. But he hadn't bought anything, and he wasn't meeting anyone, and his eyes kept returning to her stall with the flat regularity of a man counting.

Bram finished packing. She tied the canvas shut. She called to the pot-boy to watch her pitch until evening—paid him double, which he'd remember, which would give her a story—and lifted her basket, and smiled at the man in grey as she passed him, and saw, in the half-second their eyes met, that he smiled back with perfect, practiced warmth.

Her heart was a drum. Her face was a wall. Seven years of practice, and this, at least, still held: the smile that said *I am nobody, I am nothing, I am a woman selling string.*

She made it three stalls before she heard him fall into step behind her, matching her pace, close enough to be a stranger, far enough to be polite.

"Madam," he said. "A moment of your time?"

* * *

She talked to him because running was worse. That was the first rule of her old trade, learned in border wars where hedge-witches were questioned by captains instead of burned by churches: *never run from a question you can survive.* So she stopped, and turned, and gave the man in grey her full attention and her best empty face, while the market flowed around them both like water around two stones.

He introduced himself as Master Ellery, an assessor of the Royal Census Bureau, and showed papers that were probably genuine and certainly meant nothing. He wanted to know about her charms. Their construction. Their materials. Whether any of them contained, and here he smiled again, that warm practised smile, "*any substances of an unusual nature.*"

"Hemp, felt, tin, beeswax," Bram said. "Prayer, if you count prayer as unusual. My mother was a chapel-woman. Old habits."

"And the light?"

"What light?"

For a moment—a single, suspended moment—Master Ellery's face did nothing at all, and Bram understood that she was looking at the true face underneath, the one that never smiled, and it was patient the way deep water is patient. Then the warmth came back, seamless, and he laughed as if she'd said something charming.

"A trick of the sun, I'm sure. These autumn clouds." He produced a small notebook, wrote something in it—she could not see what—and tucked it away. "Thank you for your time, Mistress Oyelaran. Oyelaran. An old family name?"

"It's mine."

"Of course." He inclined his head, stepped aside, and let her pass.

She walked home the long way. She checked her reflections twice in shop glass, doubled back through the tannery district where the stink covered all trails, and let herself in through the rear door of her rented room above the cooper's shop with hands that only shook once she'd barred the door.

Then she sat on her narrow bed in the failing light, in the small room that held everything she owned, and took out her god's broken prayer-bead.

It was black wood, elder-wood, snapped clean through the middle, and she had carried it through three wars and seven silences and it was the only object in the world she had never once considered selling. The Shepherd had given it to her the day she swore to him—she'd been nineteen, all elbows and certainty, kneeling in a shrine that smelled of pine smoke while something vast and gentle had pressed the bead into her palm like a coin into a beggar's hand. *Keep this,* he had said, in the voice she still heard sometimes at the edge of sleep, the voice that had been the floor under her whole life. *So you'll know I keep you.*

Seven years ago the floor had opened.

No warning. No falling star, no rival god, no sin she could name or repent. Just silence—one morning the warmth behind her sternum was there, and by evening it was gone, and the bead in her hand had gone cold as the grave, and it had stayed cold ever since. Every night she held it. Every night she prayed into that cold the way you call into a well you know is dry, and every morning she got up and tied her knots and lived.

"We're getting sloppy, old friend," she told the bead now, turning it in the lamplight. "Seven years careful, and I tie one true knot on the wrong morning."

The bead said nothing. It was very good at that.

Down in the street, a crier's bell began to ring—not the market bell, not the hour, but the sharp triple-stroke the city used for proclamations. Voices gathered. Through her shuttered window Bram heard the crowd thicken below, heard the first murmurs ripple outward from the square like a stone dropped in still water, and something in the sound of it—in its size, its urgency, its strange hush underneath the noise—made her stop with the prayer-bead halfway to its hiding place in the floorboards.

She listened. The crier's voice rose, formal and carrying, reading words that had been written somewhere high and cold above the city, and though she could not yet make out the sentences, she could read the crowd beneath them perfectly. Fear moves through a marketplace faster than fire, and Bram Oyelaran had spent a lifetime learning its grammar.

Outside, the light was going. Somewhere across the roofs of Vell, Master Ellery was writing in his little book. In her fist, the broken bead sat cold and silent as it had for seven years, faithful to nothing, keeping nothing.

"Watch yourself," Doss had said. She had watched. It hadn't been enough.

Bram blew out the lamp, knelt, and pried up the floorboard—and paused, the bead in her hand, as the crier's voice finally resolved through the window glass into words:

*"By order of His Majesty, all persons bearing the unsanctioned gift shall surrender themselves for cleansing..."*

The floorboard waited. The bead lay cold in her palm. And Bram, kneeling alone in the dark above a city that had known her name for forty years, began—for the first time since the silence—to pray like she meant it.
