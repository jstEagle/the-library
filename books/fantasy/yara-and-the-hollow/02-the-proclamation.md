## 2. The Proclamation

The bells woke the city before the criers did.

Bram heard them from her cot above the shop—six strokes instead of the usual five—and lay still in the grey light, counting. Vell rang its bells for fires, for coronations, for the deaths of great men. It did not ring a sixth stroke without reason, and in seven years of selling luck-knots and fever-charms to the Godless Quarter, she had learned that unscheduled bells were how trouble announced itself: politely, from a distance, giving everyone time to decide whether to look up.

She looked up.

By the time she had dressed and come down into the shop with its shutters still closed, she could hear the criers working their way up Tanner's Row, their voices carrying over the rooftops in the flat, official cadence that meant the words had been written by someone in a clean office far from the people who would have to live inside them.

*By order of His Majesty, the King-in-Vell.*

She unlatched the shutters. Morning came in thin and cold.

*Whereas the realm suffers under the burden of unlicensed sorcery; whereas the gift of magic is a trust held by the Crown alone; whereas hidden practitioners are a rot in the body politic—*

The first crier was a boy of maybe fourteen, standing on the mounting block outside the chandler's with a scroll he clearly couldn't read well, sounding out the hard words while a clerk beside him mouthed corrections. Behind them, at the corner of Cooper's Lane, two men in grey-and-silver livery stood with halberds and the particular stillness of soldiers who have been told to be seen rather than to act.

Bram leaned in her doorway with a cup of chicory she had no intention of drinking, and listened.

*—all persons possessing the gift, however slight, shall present themselves to the Office of Cleansing within thirty days, where they will be examined, registered, and purified according to their degree—*

*Purified.* The word went through the crowd like a fishbone through a gull. She watched it happen: the flinch, the quick sideways glances, the way old Marta the chandler's widow stopped mid-haggling with a customer and turned her head as if she'd heard something behind her.

*—and be it further known that any person who harbors, conceals, or fails to report a practitioner of the gift shall share in their judgment—*

There it was. Not just the gifted. Everyone. The whole quarter made into a net, each knot a neighbor.

*—and informers presenting true testimony shall receive ten crowns per name confirmed.*

Ten crowns. A winter's coal. Two months of honest rent. Enough to make a man do arithmetic about his friends.

The boy finished reading. The clerk rolled the scroll. For a moment nobody moved, and then the market resumed all around Bram like water closing over a stone—louder than before, too loud, everyone talking at once so that no one had to talk *to* anyone.

Marta caught Bram's eye across the lane. Marta, whose grandson's fever Bram had broken last winter with a charm stitched into his nightcap, a charm small enough that the boy's mother believed the physician did it. Marta smiled at her. Then, halfway through the smile, something flickered behind her eyes—a recalculation, fast and involuntary, the kind you couldn't stop any more than you could stop blinking—and Bram watched the smile become an effort.

She raised her cup anyway. Drank the bitter dregs. Went back inside and sat down on her stool among her stock and felt the shape of the day settle over her like wet wool.

* * *

The shop did good business, which was its own kind of horror.

They came in ones and twos, her neighbors, buying the way people buy when they're frightened—small things, protective things, things they could pretend were only superstition. Old Ferro wanted a knot against bad luck for his mule. The cooper's daughter bought a fever-thread "for my aunt." A woman Bram didn't know, new face, asked twice whether the charms "really worked," and laughed at her own question, and bought three.

Bram sold them all with steady hands and the patter she'd worn smooth over years: *woven on the right days, nothing in it but thread and patience, a little luck never hurt anyone.* Every sale was a performance of harmlessness. Nothing I make can register, she wanted to say. Nothing I make would light a candle at forty paces. I am the least magical woman in this city, please believe me.

But the Inquisition wouldn't measure the charms. They'd measure *her*, and what they'd find was not nothing. Seven years since the Shepherd's voice had gone silent in her chest, seven years since she'd felt the last warmth of him gutter and go out—but the vessel remained. Whatever had taken his voice had left the rest of her intact: the sight, the knack, the low banked ember of the gift itself. Small. Containable. Exactly the size of thing the new Edict had been written to scoop up.

*However slight.* Whoever drafted it had thought of people like her specifically. That was the worst part—not the cruelty of it but the competence.

At midday, Doss came in.

He filled the doorway the way he always had, big and deliberate, sergeant's braid gone shiny at the cuff from twenty years of resting his hands on his belt. He was city watch now, had been since the war ended, and he still came by the shop sometimes under the pretense of needing a luck-knot for his boots or a charm for his rheumatic knee, and Bram still charged him full price because anything else would be a debt between them, and debts between a watchman and a hedge-witch were dangerous currency.

"Busy morning," he said.

"Everyone wants luck suddenly."

"Lots of it going around." He took a knot off the pegboard, turned it over in his enormous fingers. He didn't look at her when he spoke next, and that was how she knew the words mattered. "You hear the criers?"

"I hear the criers."

"They're posting lists tomorrow. Names of the registered examiners. There'll be a station in the Godless Quarter itself—old tithe-house on Candle Street." He set the knot down. Picked it up again. "Bram."

"Don't." She kept her voice pleasant, a shopkeeper's voice, because there might be ears. There were always ears now. "I'm a thread-seller, Doss. You've seen my stock. Thread and knots and dried feverfew."

"I've seen your stock." He finally met her eyes, and his were doing the thing she hated most in the world: caring at her, helplessly, from behind a wall. "Thirty days," he said. "People will get desperate near the end of it. Desperate people sell each other. Sell cheap, even." He put two coppers on the counter for a copper knot and left without waiting for change.

Through the window she watched him go, shoulders squared against the wind, and understood that he had just spent his whole allowance of courage telling her to run, and that he would deny it if ever asked, and that she loved him for it and resented him for it in equal measure, because where exactly was she supposed to run? She had run once already—from a dead god, from a war, from everything she'd been—and Vell was where the running stopped. Vell was the hiding place she'd built out of thread and patience.

Now the city itself had been conscripted against her.

* * *

The evening was worse than the day.

It wasn't anything anyone said. It was the quality of the silences. She took her supper at the cookshop on Tanner's Row because she always did, because routine was armor, and the room was full the way it always was—but the tables had rearranged themselves somehow, subtly, the way iron filings rearrange around a magnet no one can see. Men who'd shared benches for years sat angled away from each other. Talk happened in a lower register, in clusters of two, heads close. When Bram came in, a conversation at the nearest table died with the specific abruptness of guilt, and she had to stand there while someone made room for her, too much room, a whole bench's worth of space opening up like a wound.

She ate her stew. She complimented the cook. She paid and left and felt every eye on her back the whole length of the street, and told herself it was imagination, and knew it wasn't.

Because here was the arithmetic the criers had done with their ten crowns: the Godless Quarter held perhaps four thousand souls, and every one of them had, at some point, bought something from someone, and every one of them now owned a private ledger of suspicions accumulated over a lifetime. The woman whose kettle sang when she was angry. The man whose garden grew too well. The child born during the lightning storm who never seemed to bruise. None of it proof. All of it *testimony*. The Edict hadn't outlawed magic so much as it had legalized memory, weaponized every oddity anyone had ever noticed and half-forgotten.

And Bram had spent eleven years being generously, conspicuously ordinary to these people—which meant she was also conspicuous in the other direction now. The one who never got sick. The one whose luck held. Little mercies she'd handed out for years, disguised as coincidence, were suddenly receipts.

On her walk home she passed the Widow Sarel's step, where three neighborhood women sat shelling peas, and their talk stopped when she drew level, and started again four steps after she passed, slightly too loud. One of them laughed at something. The laugh had a wrong note in it, bright and brittle, the sound of someone performing ease.

Seven years ago, Bram would have prayed for them. Would have asked the Shepherd to soften whatever fear was curdling in the lane, to keep the quarter's heart warm. She caught herself reaching for the habit—the old reflex, the inward turn, the asking—and hit silence, as always, the great blank wall where her god used to live. And for the first time in those seven years she was almost grateful for it. Because if he'd been listening, he'd have heard what she was thinking, which was that she didn't know anymore whether she pitied those women or feared them, and that both feelings were probably correct.

That was what the Edict had done in a single day. It hadn't found a single witch. It had made everyone a potential one, and everyone else a potential witness, and it had done it with six bell-strokes and a boy who could barely read.

* * *

She burned the stock at midnight.

Not in the hearth—hearth smoke could carry smells, and charmed thread burned strange, sweetish, wrong. She took the whole inventory down into the cellar: four hundred luck-knots, the fever-threads, the birthing-charms wrapped in oilcloth, the travel-blessings, the little knotted cords against nightmares that children wore until they wore them out. Eleven years of careful, patient, invisible work. Her living. Her cover story and her livelihood in the same basket.

She fed them into the forge she kept for soldering—kept for looking like a craftswoman—and stood watching them go.

The knots burned green at the edges. She'd always liked that; the Shepherd had liked it too, or she'd imagined he had, back when imagining his opinions had been possible. Green flame, brief and pretty, and then ash that smelled like nothing incriminating at all.

She worked methodically, because methodical was how you kept your hands from shaking. Knots first. Threads next. The birthing-charms last, because they were hardest—she remembered every woman she'd made them for, remembered sitting up nights during the hard deliveries, remembered the small wet cries that followed. Burning those felt like burning names.

When the last of it was ash she raked the coals apart, drowned them, ground the residue into the floor sweep where it would pass for ordinary soot. Then she sat on her stool in the cold cellar with the forge ticking as it cooled, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing all day.

*Run.*

Doss had said it without saying it. Every instinct she'd sharpened across a decade of soldiering said it. Thirty days was not thirty days—it was however long it took the first frightened neighbor to remember the first suspicious thing, minus the time it took the Inquisition to act. The smart move was tonight. Pack light, take the sewers she knew from the war, be two towns gone by dawn.

She didn't move.

Because running was the thing she was best at, and she was tired of being best at it. Because the Hollow beneath the city held people who'd run from earlier purges and she'd never once gone down to find them, because finding them would mean admitting she belonged in the category. Because somewhere in this city, right now, there were others like her—small magics, quiet magics, people who'd spent years perfecting their invisibility—and some of them were going to be slow, or proud, or hopeful, or simply too poor to flee, and the tithe-house on Candle Street would open its doors and begin its examinations, and *purified according to their degree* would reveal itself to mean something none of them wanted to learn.

Her hand found the prayer-bead at her throat. Broken strand, one bead left, the others lost across a war she didn't talk about. The glass was warm from her skin, or from something older than her skin—she'd never known which, and in seven years of silence had stopped testing it.

"Shepherd," she said, out loud, into the empty cellar. Habit. Just habit. "If you're anywhere—if anything of you is anywhere—I could use a word. Any word. Even a bad one."

Nothing. The forge ticked. The house settled. Somewhere above, the city slept around its new fear like a fist around a nail.

Then—far off, faint, more feeling than sound—a bell began to ring. Not the cathedral. Not the watch. Something smaller, thinner, from the direction of the river districts, ringing fast and ragged, alarm-pattern, and beneath it, rising, a smell she knew from the war: smoke, but not woodsmoke. Something sweeter underneath. Something burning that shouldn't burn.

Bram stood up in the ash-grey dark, one broken prayer-bead clenched in her fist, and listened to the fire-wardens' bells multiply across the sleeping city—one district, then another, then another—like a fever spreading through a body.

Somewhere out there, someone's small magic had just failed to stay invisible.

And the Inquisition had thirty days, but it wasn't going to wait.
