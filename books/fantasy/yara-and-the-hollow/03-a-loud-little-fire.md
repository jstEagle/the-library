## 3. A Loud Little Fire

The kettle sang at midnight, and Yara wanted to strangle it with her bare hands.

"Shut up," she whispered, wrapping both arms around its dented belly the way you'd hug a stubborn goat. "Shut *up* shut up shut up."

The water had gone cold an hour ago. She hadn't lit anything under it. She never lit anything—that was the whole point of living in a chimney flue, that she could keep warm without fire, without smoke, without any of the little betrayals that got people like her dragged into the square and tied to posts. The warmth came out of her instead, leaking through her palms and into the old black iron until the water inside decided, all on its own, to boil.

The kettle's song rose to a shriek.

Yara clamped a hand over the spout and immediately regretted it. Steam punched up between her fingers, hot enough to sting, and in the dark of the flue the light came—her light, the traitor light that lived somewhere behind her breastbone and spilled out of her whenever she felt too much of anything. It glowed gold through the seams of her fists, through the gaps between her ribs, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well but *there*, unmistakably there, if anyone happened to be looking up a chimney at midnight in the Cindercroft ward where nobody looked up at anything except rain.

She held her breath. She counted to sixty, which was what Mama used to make her do when she was angry, back when there had been a Mama and a kitchen and a floor that didn't slope. By forty the kettle had stopped singing. By fifty-five the light had guttered down to nothing. By sixty Yara let the air out of her lungs in one long thread and slumped back against the soot-black brick.

"Stupid," she told herself. "Stupid, stupid, loud little fire."

That was what Mama had called her magic, once, before Mama learned what the word for it really was. Not *gift*. Not *blessing*. In the last year, when they'd still been together, Mama had called it *a loud little fire* too—but by then she'd said it the way you'd say *plague*, hushed and frightened, with the shutters closed.

Yara pulled her knees up under her chin. The flue was barely wide enough for her—she'd grown since she'd claimed it, two winters ago now, and her shoulders brushed both walls when she stretched. Above her, three stories of chimney narrowed to a slot of sky where the moon hung thin as a paring knife. Below her, wedged on a ledge of old mortar, sat everything she owned: a tin cup, a wool blanket gone gray with ash, half a loaf she'd bought with two days of chimney-sweeping wages, and a wooden bird she'd whittled herself from a broken shutter slat.

She picked up the bird and turned it in her fingers. It was supposed to be a wren. It looked more like a lumpy pigeon with ambitions. But she'd carved it on the good days, the quiet days, and holding it was like holding a string of them all together—the day the baker's wife had paid her in crusts for clearing the oven flue, the day she'd outrun the tanner's dog, the day she'd found the flue itself, abandoned and cold, with a view over three rooftops and a gap in the mortar big enough for a skinny girl with no last name anyone would admit to.

"You're my family," she informed the bird. "You and the kettle. Congratulations."

The kettle said nothing, which was polite of it.

Sleep came slow and shallow, the way it always did. She dreamed, as she often did, of her mother's hands—flour to the wrists, kneading bread, humming—and woke before dawn with wet cheeks and a warm spot in the blanket shaped exactly like her own curled body, because even asleep she leaked heat like a stove with a cracked door.

She lay still and listened.

Cindercroft woke in layers. First the gulls, screaming off the harbor masts. Then the fishwives' carts rattling over cobbles toward the morning market, wheels complaining. Then voices—shutters banging open, somebody's baby howling, the rag-and-bone man crying his wares in a voice like a rusted hinge. Normal sounds. Safe sounds.

Except.

Yara frowned. Beneath the ordinary noise ran another sound, one that didn't belong: boots. Not one pair. Many pairs, moving down Midden Row in step, unhurried, deliberate, the way soldiers walked when they wanted you to hear them coming.

She wriggled up the flue until her chin cleared the pot rim and peered out.

Fog lay in the street like spilled milk. Through it came six men in gray-and-white livery she knew from every wall in Vell these past weeks—the sunburst-and-chains of the King's Inquisition, fresh-painted over older paint, over even older paint. Two carried pikes. One carried a cage, and in the cage something low and heavy-paced shifted its weight from foot to foot, and the fog seemed to bend around the cage as though afraid of it.

A hound.

Not a dog. Everyone in Cindercroft had dogs, yappy terriers and lazy mastiffs and one three-legged thing named Bishop that stole laundry. This was different. This was big as a calf, hairless, gray-skinned, its head too flat and its nostrils working the air in long, patient pulls. The Inquisition's scent-hounds. The criers' proclamations hadn't mentioned hounds, but the whispers had, in market queues and behind tavern doors: *they've got beasts that smell the gift on you, smell it through stone, through skin, through prayer.*

Yara's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water.

They stopped four doors down. At the Cobbler Rennick's. Sweet old Rennick, who'd once given her a heel of cheese and never asked why a child lived in a chimney.

No. No, not Rennick. Please not—

The hound's keeper unlocked the cage. The beast poured out onto the cobbles like smoke given bones, put its nose down, and began to work. Along the gutters. Up the doorposts. Across the wall of Rennick's shop, its claws ticking against the plaster, reading the stone the way Yara read the carvings children scratched into bridge rails.

Then it stopped. Turned its flat head.

Looked directly at her chimney.

Yara dropped below the pot rim so fast she cracked her elbow on the flue wall and didn't even feel it. Her heart slammed against her ribs hard enough that she was sure they could hear it in the street—*thud, thud, thud*, a drumbeat announcing *here, here, here is one, come and get her.*

It couldn't have seen her. It couldn't. The fog, the dark, the distance—

But the hound wasn't looking with eyes. It had caught the warmth baked into her bricks. Three winters of her heat soaked into that chimney like grease into a cook's apron. Every night she'd slept safe and warm, she'd been writing her name in fire across the whole flue, and now the thing downstairs was reading it aloud.

Boots on the doorstep below. A fist on wood. "Open in the name of the Crown's Cleansing!"

Rennick's voice, thick with sleep and fear. "There's nothing—I live alone, I'm just a cobbler—"

"The beast does not lie, old man."

Yara was already moving.

She'd planned this. Of course she'd planned this—in the first week after the Edict went up, when she'd lain awake mapping escape routes the way other children mapped treasure. Out the flue top, over the pot, left along the ridgeline to the washline pole, drop to the Harrow's roof, then the tannery, then the gap-jump to the granary, and from the granary eaves you could go anywhere in Cindercroft a girl could fit.

What she hadn't planned was doing it while shaking so hard her teeth chattered.

She stuffed the blanket—no. No time. Blanket stays. Tin cup stays. She took the bread and the bird, jammed both inside her shirt, and climbed. The flue was slick with creosote and her palms were slick with sweat and she climbed anyway, fingers finding the old handholds she'd worn smooth, breath sawing in and out of her chest.

Below, splintering wood. Rennick shouting. The hound making a sound she'd never heard a dog make—a low, resonant hum, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and every hair on Yara's arms stood up beneath her shirt.

She burst out of the pot into air so cold it burned.

Fog everywhere, moonlight silvering its top surface like skin on milk. The ridge of the roof ran ahead of her, tiles furred with damp moss. She went left, low, running crouched the way she'd learned from watching cats, and the tiles gave that soft drumroll underfoot that sounded, in her ears, loud as church bells.

Behind her, below, a window shattered. Light bloomed in the fog—not her kind of light, hard white lantern-light—and a voice bellowed, "The roof! It goes to the roofs! *Up!*"

They'd known. They'd watched the chimney while knocking on the door. Clever. She'd remember that, if she lived to remember things.

The washline pole. She grabbed it, swung, dropped—three stories is nothing when you're terrified, it turns out, terror makes wings of the ordinary—and hit the Harrow's shingles in a roll that knocked the wind clean out of her. She lay gasping for one heartbeat, two, listening to boots thunder up a stairwell somewhere beneath her, and then she was up again, across the Harrow's spine, down its far slope, over the tannery with its reek of lime and hides so strong it made her eyes stream.

The gap to the granary was next. Six feet of empty air over a ten-foot drop into the alley between buildings. She'd jumped it a dozen times in daylight, practicing, always landing short and scrambling the last bit over the edge.

In the fog, in the dark, chased—

Behind her, the granary's near wall shuddered. Not from her. From below. Boots on stairs, lanterns rising, and oh, they weren't just chasing her trail, they were cutting off the route, they were learning fast—

Yara jumped anyway.

For one endless moment she hung in the fog with nothing under her and the whole city holding its breath, and the light came pouring out of her, gold and blazing, streaming off her shoulders like a banner, because fear did that, fear always did that, her body shouting *here I am, here I am* at the exact moment she most needed to be nowhere at all—

Her feet hit the granary's edge. She pitched forward, caught the eave, hauled herself up, and lay flat on the roof, glowing like a lamp in the fog, utterly visible, utterly done.

Below and behind: shouting. Lanterns converging. And threading through all of it, closer than the rest, that glass-rim hum, climbing.

*Move.* She slapped her own face. *Move, you loud little idiot, MOVE.*

She scrambled up the granary's slope on hands and heels. The light wouldn't stop. She pressed it down the way Mama had taught her—think small, think cold, think of winter mornings, think of the space between stars—and it dimmed to embers and flared again with every sobbing breath, a lighthouse built of panic.

At the ridge she stopped, heaving, and looked back.

The hound stood atop the tannery roof.

It shouldn't have been able to do that—it should have been too heavy, too boneless—but there it was, planted on the shingles with its head low, fog coiling around its legs, and its eyes were open now and they were pale, milk-pale, and they were fixed on her with an attention that had nothing animal in it at all. Behind it, lantern-bobbing, came three gray-clad figures scaling the tannery wall with ropes and hooks, quick and practiced.

The hound opened its mouth. The hum deepened until Yara felt it in her teeth, in the hollows of her bones, and the granary's old lead flashing began to buzz against the roof timbers.

Something in her answered it.

She felt it happen—felt the fire behind her breastbone lean toward that awful song like a moth to a lamp, felt her own magic rise up eager and enormous and *loud*, wanting to meet the hound halfway, wanting to pour out of her and fill the whole foggy sky. Her fingertips lit blue-gold. Her breath came out visible with warmth in the freezing air, a plume, a flag, a confession.

"No," she gritted. "*No.*"

She thought of Rennick, whose door they'd broken because of her. She thought of Mama's hands, flour to the wrists, and the last thing Mama ever said to her—*be small, Yara, whatever happens, be small*—and she did the hardest thing she'd ever done in her short loud life.

She made herself small.

Not outside. Inside. She took the rising fire and folded it, the way you fold a sheet too big for the basket, corner over corner, smaller and smaller and hotter and denser, cramming all that light down into a space behind her heart no bigger than the whittled wren. It *hurt*. It hurt like swallowing a coal, like holding your hand over a candle and counting past sixty and past ninety and past any number Mama had ever asked for, and tears streamed down her face and froze in the wind, and she held it, she *held* it—

The plume of her breath thinned. The glow in her fingertips died. The fog closed over her like water closing over a sunk coin.

On the tannery roof, the hound's head swung left. Right. Left again. Its song faltered, searching, finding nothing, because there was nothing to find—only a granary roof, and fog, and a city full of chimneys and ovens and forge-fires, a thousand small warmths, none of them hers.

The gray-clad figures reached the tannery's peak and conferred, lanterns swinging. One pointed toward the harbor district. The hound flowed down the far side of the roof after him, and the hum faded, swallowed street by street, until Cindercroft's ordinary noises closed back over the silence like a scar.

Yara stayed flat on the granary roof until her arms shook and her legs were dead weights and the eastern sky had gone from black to iron to the color of dirty dishwater. Only then did she let the fire unfold, corner by careful corner, letting it warm her from the inside the way the blanket used to. It spread through her like forgiveness.

She sat up. Her whole body ached. Her throat was raw from breathing through her teeth. From inside her shirt she drew out the wooden bird and checked it for cracks, because checking the bird was easier than checking herself.

"That," she told it, voice wobbling only a little, "was close. That was very—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, firmer. "But we're fine. We're fine, and we're smart, and we're never sleeping in that chimney again."

Even saying it cracked something in her. The flue had been hers. The first thing in seven years that was hers.

And they'd read her name off its bricks like a menu.

She looked out over Cindercroft waking below—smoke rising honest from honest chimneys, fishwives crying their wares, life going on willfully deaf and blind—and she understood, with a clarity that felt older than her eleven years, that she couldn't stay above ground anymore. The streets had eyes now. The roofs had hounds. The whole bright world of Vell had become a hand slowly closing.

Which meant down. The word rose in her unbidden, from some story she'd half-heard in a tavern or a market queue, one of the ones adults told each other when they thought children weren't listening. *Down*, where the old city drowned. Where the purged and the vanished went, if they went anywhere at all. Where even hounds, maybe, couldn't follow.

She didn't know the way. Nobody knew the way. That was rather the point of it.

But she knew who might.

Old woman Sef, the beggar with clouded eyes who sat by the fountain in Tanner's Square and spoke to the drains. The children mocked her. The adults crossed themselves. But twice now Yara had heard her muttering—not prayers, not ravings—*directions*, murmured soft as secrets: *third grate past the cooper's, mind the drop, tell them Sef sent you.*

Yara tucked the bird back inside her shirt, beside the fire she'd learned tonight to fold, and stood up on the granary ridge with the dawn breaking gray behind her.

"Be small," she whispered. Then she shook her head, and grinned, fierce and frightened and alive. "No. Be *gone*."

And she went—down the roof, down the drainpipe, down into the waking streets, toward the fountain and the blind woman and the dark mouth of the world beneath the world.

Behind her, unnoticed, in the ruins of Rennick's broken doorway, a single gray feather lay on the cobbles, already soaking in the fog.

It was not a feather any bird had ever molted.
