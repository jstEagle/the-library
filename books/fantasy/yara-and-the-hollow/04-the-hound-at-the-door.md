## 4. The Hound at the Door

Three days after the flare in the market, Bram woke before dawn to the sound of her own name being spoken too quietly.

Not aloud. Not by anyone in the room. It was the old mercenary instinct, the one that had kept her alive through the border wars and the wet gray years after—the sense of being *counted*, of eyes moving along a street and pausing where they shouldn't. She lay still under her blanket in the loft above her shop, listening to Vell wake below: cart wheels on cobbles, the fishwives' argument starting at the well, someone's rooster with delusions of importance. All of it ordinary. All of it wrong.

The rhythm of the street had changed. She'd sold charms on Copperlane for six years, and she knew its mornings the way a sailor knows a tide. Today there were too many people standing still. Too many conversations that stopped when a door opened. And at the corner by Tallowman's chandlery, two men in undyed wool who had no reason to be there at that hour, doing nothing but existing, which was what Inquisition watchers did when they wanted you to know they'd learned patience.

Bram sat up slowly and looked at the little shop below her—her workbench, her spools of dyed cord, the jars of river-stones and knucklebones, the drying racks hung with luck-knots that were, mostly, just knots. She had spent three days telling herself the flare had been small. A flicker, a spark off a fever-charm, gone in half a breath. Old Marta had seen it, and the boy with the bad leg, and maybe two others, but old Marta was half-blind and the boy owed Bram money, and surely—

Surely nothing. She was a hedge-witch who had survived seven silent years by never once believing in *surely*.

She dressed in the dark, moving without sound out of habit, and took stock like a quartermaster. Coin purse: eleven marks and some copper. Knife: the good one, at her belt, not the display piece. The broken prayer-bead on its cord around her neck, half a bead of white stone worn smooth as a tooth. That went last, always last, pressed against her sternum where it had lived since the day the Shepherd's voice went out of the world.

Everything else could burn.

She was down the ladder and standing in the middle of her own shop, deciding what a person was allowed to grieve for, when the knock came.

Not the Inquisition's knock—that would come later, and it wouldn't be a knock. This was three raps, spaced evenly, deliberate. A soldier's knock. She knew exactly one soldier who knocked like that anymore.

Bram crossed the floor and put her eye to the shutter crack.

Sergeant Doss stood on her step looking like a man carrying something heavy in his stomach. He'd aged badly since the war—not old, just *used*, the way a road gets used. Gray coming into his beard early. His sergeant's sash was new, the Inquisition's dove-and-flame stitched on it in thread that probably cost more than her rent. He didn't look at the door. He looked at the street, both ways, twice, the way he used to check a ridgeline before signaling the column forward.

She opened the door four inches. "You're wearing their colors."

"I'm wearing a wage." His voice was low and flat. "There's a difference I need you to believe in for the next thirty seconds."

"Doss—"

"Six of them." He said it fast, quiet, a man unloading cargo. "An Inquisitor named Corve and five sworn hands, plus a hound-handler with a scent-hound that's been given your charm-work to smell. They're coming up Copperlane from the well side. They'll be here inside two minutes, maybe less. The warrant has your name on it, Bram. Not 'the charm-seller.' Your name. Oyelaran."

The word landed in her chest like a dropped stone. Her name. She hadn't heard anyone say it in years; in Vell she was *the knotwoman*, *mistress*, *hey, you*. Names were how the fire started. Everyone knew that now. The criers had made it a lesson in arithmetic: name, then warrant, then pyre.

"How?" she said. It came out almost calm, which surprised her.

"The flare. Someone talked. Doesn't matter who." His jaw worked. "It matters that I'm standing here instead of walking with them, and that when they ask me later if I saw anything on my rounds, I'm going to say no, and that lie is going to cost me everything I've got left. So don't waste it arguing."

She should have argued. She should have asked him why, after eight years of silence between them—since the last night of the war, since the thing he'd said to her at the river crossing that she had never forgiven and never forgotten—why he would burn his career for her now. But two minutes is a terrible time for questions, and his face answered the only one that mattered: he wasn't doing it for forgiveness. He was doing it because somewhere under the sash and the wage, the man who'd pulled her out of the burning mill at Harrow Ford was still in there, suffocating.

"Back door," he said. "Through the tannery yard, down the steps past the glue-house. The sewer grate at the bottom—it's rusted but it lifts. I've walked this route in my head all morning." He almost smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen all week. "Old habits."

"Doss." She caught his sleeve. "Why?"

He looked at her hand on his arm, then at her face, and for one second the sergeant's mask slipped and she saw the boy he'd been, the one who'd shared her watch-fires and her god, back when the Shepherd still spoke to both of them through the same prayers.

"Because they burned a girl in Ashmarket on Tuesday," he said, "and she was sixteen, and all she'd done was make her mother's garden grow in winter. And because I stood there and watched, and my feet didn't move." He stepped back off her step, becoming a sergeant again, voice dropping to gravel. "My feet move now. Go."

Then he turned and walked away up Copperlane, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be, placing himself squarely between her door and the corner where the watchers stood—and Bram understood that was the real gift. Not the warning. The standing there. Every second he delayed them at the corner was a second bought with his own neck.

She moved.

* * *

The back of her shop opened onto the tannery yard, which smelled the way it always had, like money rotting into leather. Bram went over the fence rather than through the gate—gates had hinges, and hinges had sounds—and dropped into the mud on the far side with the practiced ugliness of a woman who'd done it before in worse places. The glue-house squatted ahead, its windows fogged with steam, and beyond it the steps went down toward the old drainage channel in crooked flights of mossy stone.

Behind her, muffled by walls, she heard the sound she'd been waiting three days to hear and would spend the rest of her life hearing in dreams: the crack of her front door breaking. Not opening. *Breaking*, wood splitting around the lock, followed by boots and the flat official voice of an Inquisitor reading a warrant to an empty room.

They'd find the shop cold. She'd banked the hearth before dawn. They'd find knots and stones and jars, evidence enough of craft but nothing that proved power—a hedge-witch's inventory was deliberately innocent, every charm explainable as superstition and string. But they had her *name*, and names didn't need proof. Names needed only witnesses, and the flare had made witnesses, and somewhere on Copperlane one of them had chosen eleven pieces of silver over six years of fever-charms and fair prices.

That was the part that sat in her throat like a fishbone. Not fear. Grief. She had charmed half that street without their knowing—slipped a warming knot into the widow Sennet's shawl every winter, tied a stilling-charm into the cradle-rope of the cooper's colicky baby, spent her small hoarded sparks like a miser until the street stayed just a little luckier than it had any right to be. And one flicker in the market, and the whole ledger of that quiet kindness had been weighed against silver, and silver had won.

She took the steps two at a time, down past the glue-house, down into the green-smelling dark where the drainage channel ran. The sewer grate was where Doss said, rusted at one hinge, and it lifted with a scream of iron that she felt in her teeth. She paused—one breath, listening upward—for pursuit, and heard instead, faint and terrible, the baying of a hound.

Not behind her. Above. At her shop.

They'd brought the scent-hound anyway, even though she was gone, even though the warrant was already served. Which meant the hound wasn't there for the arrest. It was there for the trail. Corve wasn't a man who closed cases; he was a man who opened them, and Bram Oyelaran's name was now an open case, and the case had a nose.

She dropped through the grate and pulled it shut above her, and the city's noise cut off like a door closing on a lit room.

* * *

The sewers of Vell were older than the sewers of anywhere else, because Vell was older than itself—built on top of an older city built on top of something older still, each generation paving over the last. Bram knew the upper tunnels from her mercenary years, when smugglers paid better than soldiers and the paymaster's map of the undercity had been worth more than her sword. She'd sworn then she'd never go back down. The dark down here had a texture to it, a patient weight, and the sound of water moving in places water shouldn't be.

She went anyway, because the alternative was a pyre, and because the broken prayer-bead against her chest seemed to hum faintly when she touched the tunnel walls, like a dog stirring in its sleep.

She told herself it was imagination. She was very good at telling herself that. Seven years of practice.

The main channel ran wide and waist-deep at the center, walkways of slick brick along either wall, and she moved along the left-hand path with her knife drawn and her breathing controlled, counting junctions the way the old map had taught her. First junction, take the high arch. Second, keep the water on your right. Third—

At the third junction, the hound cried again, and this time it echoed *up the tunnel toward her*, and Bram understood with cold clarity that the hunt had entered the drains.

She ran.

Not blindly—she was too old a hand for blind running—but fast, boots slapping wet brick, splashing through overflow channels, taking the high arch at the fourth junction even though the map said low, because the low route doubled back toward the well-side entrance and the well-side entrance would have a handler waiting at it with a torch and a leash. The dark swallowed her and gave back only echoes: her own footsteps, the drip of the deep water, and behind it all, growing, the *chuff-chuff-chuff* of a big dog moving at speed through a tunnel, and beneath that, worse, the measured tread of men who did not need to run because the dog would do the running.

Her lungs burned. Her knee—the one that had never been right since Harrow Ford—sent up its usual white complaint, and she told it, as she always did, *not today.*

The tunnel forked where the map ended and memory began, and memory failed her, and she skidded to a stop in a chamber where three passages met and the water pooled black and still, and for one horrible moment she stood in the silence listening to the hound get closer and knowing she had gambled six years of careful invisibility on a dead man's map and lost.

That was when the water moved.

Not the drip-water. Not the channel current. The pool itself shifted, a slow displacement, as if something very large beneath the surface had turned over in its sleep. Bram backed against the wall, knife up, every hair on her arms standing. Down here, in the oldest dark, the stories said there were things that had been old when Vell was young. Mercenaries didn't tell those stories for entertainment. They told them as instructions.

The surface bulged. Broke. Something rose—just barely, just enough: a long curve of pale wet shape, and then an eye, huge and lidless and ancient, catching what little light there was and giving back nothing. It regarded her the way a mountain regards weather.

Bram did the only sensible thing. She went absolutely still, lowered the knife, and thought, with the desperate clarity of the nearly dead: *I am not worth the trouble.*

The eye held her for a long moment. Then, distantly, from the passage she'd come by, the hound's cry rang off the walls—and the thing in the water sank without a ripple, and was gone, and the pool lay black and innocent again.

Leaving Bram alone in the dark with a choice of three tunnels, a closing pack behind her, and the sudden, irrational certainty that the eye had not been looking *at* her so much as *past* her—at the bead against her sternum, which was warm now, unmistakably warm, warm the way a coal is warm an instant before it catches.

"Seven years," she whispered to the dark, to the god who wasn't there. "*Now* you have opinions?"

The hound bayed, close, echoing. Boots splashed. Torchlight wavered orange at the mouth of the passage behind her.

Bram chose the middle tunnel, the narrowest, the one that sloped down where maps said nothing should slope, and ran into the deep of Vell with her god's broken bead burning against her heart like a promise or a warning—and behind her, faint but gaining, came the hound, and ahead of her, in the dripping dark, something else was already moving toward the sound.
