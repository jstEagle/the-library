## 5. What the Sewers Remember

**Bram**

The tunnel narrowed until her shoulders brushed both walls, and the smell changed—old water and older stone, the mineral breath of a city's buried bones. Bram counted turns the way she'd learned to count paces in a retreat: not to find her way out but to know how far in she was.

Three lefts. A stair of slick brick. The hound's voice had fallen behind, or gone quiet, which was worse. Hounds that went quiet were being given time to think.

Her boots found standing water ankle-deep, then knee-deep, cold as a grave-wash. She kept moving because stopping meant listening, and listening meant hearing the thing behind her, and hearing it meant her legs would remember they were fifty-one years old and had been running on charm-seller's bread for seven years.

The prayer-bead against her sternum had stopped burning. Now it hummed, faint and steady, like a struck tuning fork buried in wool.

"I know," she muttered. "I feel it too."

The tunnel opened without warning into a junction chamber—a vaulted knot where five passages met, their mouths black as held breath. Someone had been here before her, long ago or recently; there were marks on the wall, chalk sigils half-dissolved by seepage, and a rusted iron ring bolted at head height with no rope attached. Mercenary work. Wayfinding for people who expected to be chased.

She'd run these tunnels twenty years ago, in another life, escorting smugglers and fleeing magistrates through the undercity of Vell when Vell still pretended its sewers didn't have names. The smugglers had named them anyway. This chamber they'd called the Fist, because five tunnels closing on one room looked like knuckles around a palm.

She stood in the center of the palm and let herself breathe, three counts in, four out, the way she taught soldiers before a breach.

That was when she saw the light.

It came from the second passage on the left—no torch-orange, no lantern-yellow, but a soft blue-white shimmer, like moonlight trapped underwater, pulsing gently with something like a rhythm. A heartbeat. A child's fast heartbeat.

Bram's hand went to the knife at her hip out of pure habit, though steel would do nothing against whatever made light like that. Light like that was gift. Loud, leaking, uncontrolled gift—the kind the Inquisition could smell through three feet of masonry.

The light stuttered. Guttered. And a voice came out of the dark passage, small and furious and cracking down the middle:

"Go away."

**Yara**

The girl had been running since the rooftops, and her body had finally sent word that this was unacceptable.

She sat in the flooded passage with her knees drawn up, water soaking through the man's coat she wore, and tried to make herself small the way she did when the leak started. Small thoughts. Small feelings. *I am a pebble. I am a bit of soot. Nobody looks at soot.*

But the leak didn't care about soot. The leak cared about fear, and fear was the biggest feeling there was, bigger than hunger, bigger than the flue, bigger even than the night the fever took Mam and the candles in the death-house all lit themselves one by one without hands.

Light pooled off her skin now, blue-white, washing up the wet brick. Somewhere behind her the dog-thing had lost her scent in the water, but water carried other things, and she could feel them coming—the way you felt weather in your teeth. Something else was in these tunnels besides her. Something old and slow and patient.

And now footsteps. Splashing. Coming toward her light.

"Go away," she said, and hated how young her voice sounded.

The footsteps stopped. A woman stood at the edge of the glow—not an Inquisitor, no white sash, no hound-chain, just a lean gray figure with a knife out and a face like a closed door. Old enough to be somebody's grandmother, hard enough to be nobody's anything.

"You're leaking," the woman said.

"No I'm not."

"You're lighting up a sewer like a festival lantern." The woman crouched slowly, keeping her hands visible the way you did with spooked animals, which Yara resented deeply because she was not an animal, she was a person, mostly. "How long can you hold it in?"

"I don't *hold* it. It just—" The light flared with her anger and she clamped her arms around her knees. "It does what it wants."

"That's what I thought." The woman's eyes moved past her, into the dark of the passage behind, and something in her face tightened. "Listen to me, girl. My name is Bram. I'm not Inquisition. I'm the opposite of Inquisition, if there is such a thing tonight. Can you run?"

"I've been running all night."

"Can you run more?"

Yara opened her mouth to say no, and that was when the water moved.

Not rippled. Not stirred. *Moved*—all of it at once, the whole flooded length of the passage behind the woman lifting slightly, like the back of a great animal shifting under a blanket. The surface tension broke with a sound like a gasp.

Then the sound came. Deep below, deep under the water, deep under even the sewer: a groan of stone giving way, long and grinding, the sound of a door opening that had been shut for a hundred years.

"What's that?" Yara whispered.

"That," said the woman, "is the drainage flood. Every third bell-night, the sluices upstream open and flush the whole system." Her face had gone very calm, the way faces did right before everything went wrong. "We're below the sluice line."

"How far below?"

"All the way below."

The water rose. Fast. Ankle-deep became knee-deep became thigh-deep between one breath and the next, and it wasn't rising like filling—it was rising like *arriving*, a wall of black water shouldering down the main channel toward the Fist, carrying things with it: broken barrels, a door, something pale and bloated turning over and over.

"High ground!" the woman shouted, already moving, hauling at the iron ring bolted to the wall. "There's supposed to be a ledge—"

There wasn't. Or there had been once, and the water had eaten it. Yara saw the woman understand that in a single terrible instant, saw her start calculating distances to the tunnel mouths, saw her realize none of them were high enough, and Yara felt the fear come up through her like fire through a chimney—

—and the leak answered.

**Bram**

The girl screamed, and the water *stopped*.

Not slowed. Stopped. A forty-foot wall of churning floodwater hung at the mouth of the largest channel like a held breath, foam frozen mid-curl, a dead cat suspended inside it turning slowly, impossibly, like a fly in amber.

And Yara stood in the middle of the chamber with both arms thrown out and every drop of water in the room trembling, and she blazed. There was no other word. The blue-white light poured off her now in sheets, and beneath it Bram could see the shape of the working—not a spell, nothing so disciplined, just raw will hammered flat into the world like a fist through a window. The girl was holding back a flood the way a child holds back a door: with her whole body, and only for as long as her whole body lasted.

Bram had seen gifted soldiers in the war. She'd seen a stormcaller level a fortification, seen a truth-speaker crack a general's mind open like an egg. None of it had looked like this. This was power with no craft at all wrapped around it—power the way a forest fire is warmth.

"Girl." She waded toward her, fighting the current that still swirled at her calves. "You can't hold that long. You'll burn out your channels before you're twelve."

"I don't—know what—that *means*!" Each word cost the girl visibly, her face graying, blood threading from her nose in a thin bright line.

"It means stop pushing and start *shaping*. Don't fight the water. Give it somewhere to go." Bram reached her, gripped her shoulders, and the light flared hot against her palms—not painful, exactly, but insistent, like grabbing a live wire wearing wet gloves. "There's a relief shaft in the floor of the north passage. Grate over it, but it opens. You push the flood there instead of holding it. Do you hear me? Redirect, don't resist."

"I can't—I don't know *how*—"

"Yes you do. It's what you've been doing your whole life without knowing it. Kettles sing because you give the heat somewhere to go. Your candles light because you give the flame a shape. Same thing. Bigger kettle." She put her face close to the girl's, close enough to see her own reflection in eyes gone white-blue. "*Give it somewhere to go.*"

For a moment nothing. The wall of water groaned, straining against a child's will, and Bram felt the moment tilt—the instant where either the girl broke and the flood took them both, or—

Yara turned her hands. Turned them outward, palms up, the way you'd offer something. And screamed a different scream—not *no* but *there*, and the wall of water bent, curved, swung around the chamber in a roaring arc like a river remembering its course, and slammed down the north passage. They heard it hit the grate, heard the grate shear off its bolts, heard the flood plunge away into whatever deep place the old engineers had cut for exactly this, and the water around their legs dropped, dropped, drained to shin-depth, to ankle-depth, to a few inches of exhausted swirl.

Yara folded.

Bram caught her before she went under. The girl weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks in an oversized coat, and she was shivering violently and laughing at the same time, a cracked hysterical little laugh, blood still on her lip.

"Did I do it?" she whispered.

"You did it." Bram lowered her onto a dry lip of stone at the chamber's edge and knelt beside her. Her heart was doing something complicated she refused to examine. "What's your name?"

"Yara." A cough. "Who are you?"

"Bram Oyelaran. Charm-seller. Formerly other things." She pulled off her own coat and put it around the girl's shoulders, over the coat already there. "Formerly, apparently, a fool who walked into a drainage chamber on flood night."

"You knew about the flood."

"I knew about the *shaft*. The timing I'd have bet on too. Twenty years ago the sluices ran on the fourth bell." She shook her head slowly. "Everything down here runs late now. Like the whole city forgot to wind itself."

Yara's eyes had drifted to the chain at Bram's throat—to the single bead resting there, cracked clean through, still humming faintly. The girl frowned at it the way you frown at a sound you almost recognize.

"That's loud," she said.

Bram's hand closed over the bead. "It's broken."

"Not broken. Just—" Yara squinted, groping for words. "Like a bell with the clapper taken out. I can hear it anyway. It's saying the same thing over and over. It's saying..." Her brow furrowed. "*Down.*"

The word settled into the chamber like a stone into still water.

"The bead says *down*?"

"It doesn't say words. It's more like—" The girl waved a dripping hand. "Like when someone points. It's pointing down. Hard." She looked up, suddenly wary again, the animal coming back into her face. "Why? What's down?"

Bram sat back on her heels. Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the fourth bell was ringing across Vell, and somewhere up there the Inquisition was kicking in doors and her street was learning her name was worth thirty silver crowns. Behind them, a hound had lost a scent in moving water. Ahead of them—

Ahead of them was the deep tunnel, the one the smugglers wouldn't take even paid double. The one that sloped past the maps' last page, down toward the drowned quarter, where the old pre-city slept under its hundred feet of black water and the refugees of earlier purges had supposedly vanished, decade after decade, like rain into a river.

Sef. If Sef still lived. If the old stories were true and the keeper of the dragon-fire still kept her ledger of the burned.

Bram looked at the bead in her palm. Seven years silent, and now, in a sewer, in a drowned chamber, pointed at by an eleven-year-old who held back floods: *down.*

"Something's down," she said aloud. "Or someone. And either they want us, or we've got nowhere else." She stood, joints protesting, and offered the girl her hand. "Can you walk?"

Yara looked at the hand for a long moment. Bram watched her weigh it—the stranger, the knife, the coat, the night—and understood perfectly when the girl said, "If I walk with you, are you going to turn me in?"

"No."

"Why not? There's a reward. Everyone always turns people in. That's what everyone *does*."

Bram held the girl's gaze. "Because I spent twenty years as a soldier learning what happens to people who sell each other, and seven years as a shopkeeper watching this city practice it. I'm done contributing." She flexed her fingers. "Also, you just moved a river. Turning you in would be the stupidest trade I ever made."

A ghost of a grin crossed Yara's bloody face. Then it faded, and she asked the question children ask, the ones with knives in them:

"Do you have magic too? I can't tell. You're all... quiet. Quiet like a house where somebody died."

The bead hummed against Bram's sternum. Seven years of silence, and the girl had put her hand flat on it in ten seconds.

"My god went quiet," Bram said. "The rest of me followed. Come on."

They went down.

* * *

**Yara**

The tunnel sloped forever.

Yara kept one hand on the wall and one on the hem of the strange woman's coat, because the dark down here was a different dark than the flue-dark she knew. Flue-dark was small and warm and yours. This dark was enormous and cold and full of echoes that arrived late, as if the tunnel were thinking about whether to repeat them.

Bram walked like the dark belonged to her. Left hand tracing the wall, counting under her breath, pausing at junctions to read marks Yara couldn't see until Bram touched them—scratches, chalk ghosts, a notch shaped like a bird.

"Mercenary roads," Bram said, when Yara asked. "Smuggler roads. People who needed to move without being seen built all this, long before the Inquisition gave them a reason." She glanced back, and in the leak-light Yara carried with her now—she couldn't fully put it out, it just dimmed to a candle-glow—she saw the woman's face do something complicated. "You're leaking again. Slower. Good. Keep it at a candle."

"I'm trying. It's like holding a sneeze."

"It's like breathing. You've just been doing it wrong your whole life." Bram faced forward again. "First lesson, then, since we're apparently doing lessons. Power isn't a thing you have. It's a thing that moves *through* you. Water, heat, light—all of it wants to keep moving. Trouble starts when you dam it up. So don't dam. Channel. Little doors, opened on purpose."

"Is that how you got quiet?" Yara asked, before she could stop herself. "Did you dam yourself up?"

Bram's boot-steps hitched, half a beat. Then resumed.

"Different thing," she said. "Somebody else built my dam."

They walked on. Yara turned that over. Somebody else's dam. Somebody else's silence. She thought about the bead that pointed down, and about how Bram touched it sometimes through her shirt without seeming to know she was doing it, the way Yara touched the button from Mam's coat.

The air changed first. Warmer, and tasting of metal, and smelling—faintly, impossibly—of smoke. Real smoke, wood-smoke, cooking-smoke.

Then the light changed. Far below, past a bend where the tunnel stepped down into a vast black space, there was a glow that wasn't hers. Red-gold. Flickering. Alive.

Yara felt it before she understood it: a warmth answering the warmth in her own chest, like recognizing like. The leak surged happily toward it and she reeled it back, candle-flame, candle-flame.

Bram had gone still at the bend. Her hand was pressed flat against the cracked bead at her throat, and her lips were moving—no sound coming out, just shapes, like a prayer rehearsed for seven years finally finding its audience.

"What is it?" Yara whispered. "What's down there?"

Below them, across the drowned dark, a guttering vein of red-gold fire burned in the roots of the world—and around it, tiny figures moved, tending it, dozens of them, the discarded and the disappeared, alive.

Bram exhaled a answer that was half laugh, half sob.

"Everyone," she said. "Everyone they ever burned. Come on, girl. We're expected."

The tunnel steps led down, and the fire waited, and Yara took the first step toward the only light in the world that hadn't tried to catch her.
