## 6. Down to the Hollow

The tunnel Yara had come down through was not the way back up. That was the first thing we learned, and we learned it by trying.

We climbed for an hour through brickwork that grew older as it rose—sewer stone giving way to something older still, blocks fitted without mortar, each one carved with a script I didn't recognize and Yara swore she could almost read. Then the passage simply stopped. Not blocked. Stopped, the way a sentence stops, the last stone set with an air of finality that no rubble ever manages.

"Turn around," I said.

"I *knew* this way," Yara said, but her voice had gone thin. In the dark her magic kept leaking—a faint gold shimmer along her knuckles, like sparks caught in wool. She'd gotten better at caging it in the hours since the flood, better than I had any right to expect from a frightened child, but fear wore grooves in her control, and the ceiling of stopped stone was wearing at her badly.

"We'll find another way up," I said. "There's always another way up."

She looked at me with the particular flat skepticism only orphans and soldiers can manage. "You don't know that."

"I've been a mercenary eleven years, girl. The whole trade is finding another way up." I turned us around. My hand found the broken prayer-bead in my pocket—the half of it, the cracked amber bead I'd carried since the Shepherd went silent, since the morning seven years ago when every prayer I owned fell out of me like water through torn cloth. I rubbed my thumb over the crack without meaning to. Habit older than my sword calluses.

Behind us the tunnel breathed. That was the second thing we learned: the deep places of Vell breathe. Slow drafts moved against our faces, warm and mineral, smelling of iron and old smoke—and under the smell of smoke, something else. Something living. Sulfur and hot stone, the way a forge smells from three streets away.

Yara felt it before she smelled it. Her head came up like a hound's. "There's fire down here," she whispered. "Real fire. It's—" She frowned, hunting for the word. "It's *old* fire."

"Then it's been keeping warm longer than either of us," I said, and put her behind me, and walked toward it.

* * *

The way down was a staircase that had once been a street.

I understood that gradually, the way you understand things in bad light. Steps worn to shallow bowls by centuries of feet, descending between walls that had been building fronts once—doorways bricked shut, lintels carved with shop-signs in the dead script, a window's arch still standing where everything around it had slumped into itself. Vell, I realized, had not been built on rock. Vell had been built on Vell. On some older city that had drowned or sunk or been buried on purpose, and everyone above walked around on top of it never knowing, the way people walk around on top of everything they'd rather not think about.

"How far down are we going?" Yara asked. Her voice echoed wrong down there—too many returns, as if the dark were answering itself.

"However far it takes to stop hearing hounds."

The hounds had not followed us past the flood. Whatever the drainage surge had been—her magic called up out of panic, or the tunnels' own temper—we'd heard them turn back, whining, at the wall of water. But sound carries strangely underground. For all I knew they were a hundred yards behind. For all I knew they'd gone home. I chose not to bet either way.

The steps ended at a landing where the air changed temperature. Not colder. *Warmer*—a dry, breathing warmth, like standing near a sleeping animal. And light: not Yara's leaky gold, but a deep red glow rising from below, staining the underside of the arches, moving slightly, the way firelight moves even when the fire is very far away and very patient.

"Oh," Yara said softly. "Oh, it's *beautiful*."

"Whatever it is, we don't touch it," I said, on reflex, the way I said such things. Then I actually looked, and the words died somewhere embarrassing.

We stood at the mouth of a street. A whole street, whole houses, a drowned quarter of the old city laid out below us and lit from within—not flooded, not anymore, though the waterline showed on every wall as a band of pale mineral scum two men high, marking where the sea or the river had once lived and left. Down the center of the street, running along what had been its gutter, lay a vein of fire.

Not flame. Vein. A seam of molten color in the stone itself, red-gold, pulsing slow as a heartbeat, sometimes brightening to white at knots along its length, sometimes dimming nearly to ember-dark. It ran the length of the street and bent out of sight around a corner, and the light of it filled the hollow windows of the dead houses and made them look inhabited. Made them look *warm*. Steam rose off it in slow ribbons and vanished into shafts above where, impossibly, gray daylight filtered down through breaks in the world we'd left.

People lived there. That was the part that took longest to land. Figures moved between the houses—thin figures, wrapped figures, hanging wash that steamed gently on lines strung across the street. A child chased something small along the vein's edge, close enough that the heat should have taken her eyebrows off, and didn't seem afraid. Someone had planted something green in a cracked cistern; I could see the leaves shining in the red light.

"It's a city," Yara breathed. "It's a whole city under the city."

"It's the discarded part of one," I said, before I could stop myself. Eleven years of soldiering teaches you to name a thing fast: friend, enemy, terrain, trap. This was terrain, and the people in it were the kind I'd spent eleven years being paid not to think about.

But nobody paid me now. Nobody would pay me again, probably, ever. So I walked down the worn street into the light of the oldest fire I'd ever seen, with a loud little orphan girl leaking gold at my heels, and I tried to remember how to do the thing I'd forgotten how to do, which was arrive somewhere empty-handed and ask for shelter anyway.

* * *

They saw us long before we reached the first house. Of course they did. Down there, strangers were weather—weather that killed. Doors closed. The child was swept inside. Wash-lines went still as figures withdrew into doorways with the unhurried care of people who had practiced disappearing.

An old man stepped into the street to meet us. He carried no weapon that I could see, which meant he was carrying several I couldn't. He was built like a root—low, dense, immovable—and his eyes went to Yara first, then to me, then to my empty hands, in exactly the order I'd have used.

"You're from up top," he said. Not a question. Up-top accents carry, even underground.

"We are." I kept my hands loose and visible. "We're not from the Inquisition. We're running from it."

"They all say that." He said it without heat. It wasn't an accusation; it was arithmetic. "Running from what?"

"Hounds. Two days ago they raided my street. Yesterday they took a charm-seller on Cordwainer Row alive. Today—" I glanced at Yara. "Today it's her they're hunting. She's eleven."

Something moved behind his face, quickly buried. Down there, eleven-year-olds had been hunted before. You could read the whole history of the place in how fast he buried it.

"The little one's leaking," he said instead.

"I know. I'm working on it."

"She leaked on your boots just now."

I looked down. Faint gold, yes, pooled around Yara's heels like spilled lamplight, trembling with her pulse. The old man watched it the way a sailor watches weather.

"She's strong," he said, and there was grief in it, plain as a scar. "Strong ones don't last long down here unless somebody teaches them. And the teachers are mostly gone."

"Then I'm looking for whoever's left," I said. "The woman who keeps the fire. There's always someone who keeps the fire. Mercenaries' gossip, ten years back—there's a keeper under Vell, half-blind, keeps a ledger, knows every soul that ever came down. If anyone can teach a loud child to be quiet—"

He stared at me for a long moment. Then, quietly, to the nearest doorway: "Marta. Get Sef."

* * *

Sef came slowly, and the whole street seemed to come with her by degrees—doors easing open, faces gathering in the red light like moths deciding whether it was safe.

She was ancient the way the fire was ancient: not large, not fierce, but clearly the thing everything else had arranged itself around. Small and bent, wrapped in layered shawls gone the color of old blood in the vein-light, eyes clouded with a milk-white film that she aimed slightly to the left of everything she looked at. Half-blind, the gossip had said. What the gossip hadn't said was that she didn't need eyes. She crossed the uneven street without a stumble, reading it through her feet, and stopped an arm's length from me and did not look at my face at all.

She looked—or listened, or whatever it was—at my pocket.

"You carry a bead," she said. Her voice was dry paper, but steady. "Broken. Amber. Old make. Take it out, girl. Let me hear it."

My hand was around the broken prayer-bead before I'd decided to move it. I drew it out into the red light: half a bead, cracked clean through, the amber gone cloudy at the fracture line. The last piece of the Shepherd I owned. Every sworn mercenary of his order had carried a strand of nineteen; mine had snapped the same hour he went silent, seven years ago, all nineteen beads splitting at once in my fist while I screamed at a sky that had stopped listening.

Sef held out both hands. Her fingers were ruined with age, knuckles swollen, but they were steady too, and when I set the bead in them she cupped it to her ear like a shell.

The street held its breath. So did I. I hadn't let anyone touch that bead since the day it broke.

"Ah," Sef said softly. "Ah. Oh, you poor thing."

"If it's worthless, I'll take it back," I said, rougher than I meant to.

"It isn't worthless." Her clouded eyes found my face at last, missing by inches, finding me anyway. "It's grieving. Do you know what a consecrated bead is, hedge-witch? Everyone thinks it's a token. A souvenir of your god. No. It's a thread. A god spins a thread down into every soul that swears to him, and the bead is the anchor-end, the knot on the outside so you can hold on from this side." She turned the cracked halves gently in her palm. "When your Shepherd went silent, everyone thought he died, and the threads died with him. But threads don't die quiet. Threads get *cut*. And this cut—" she pressed the fracture to her ear again, listening to something I would have given my sword arm to hear— "this cut is clean. Too clean. A god dying tears. This was *severed*, by something that wanted the thread intact enough to keep."

"Keep for what?" My voice came out strange. Distant. Like hearing myself from the top of the stairs.

"That," said Sef, "is a longer story than tonight has room for, and I have told it before, and I will tell it again because the ledger demands it." She closed her fingers over the bead and, to my astonishment and against every instinct I owned, I let her keep it. "But you should know one thing tonight, before you sleep, because you came down here carrying it and you deserve to know what weight you've been hauling for seven years."

The steam rose. The vein pulsed. Somewhere a child was being fed, and the small sounds of it carried down the drowned street like the sounds of any street anywhere, and I stood in the oldest light I'd ever seen holding nothing in my empty hands.

"Your god is not dead," Sef said. "And he did not leave you."

The world tilted, very slightly, the way it does when the ground confesses it was never solid.

"He was *taken*," she said. "Like every other god this realm has swallowed in the last fifty years. And I know where the swallowing is done." Her clouded eyes drifted upward, through stone and street and palace floors, all the way to the throne. "It's done upstairs, girl. It's done under the crown."

* * *

They fed us. That undid me more than anything else that night—that a handful of refugees in a drowned city, hoarding warmth scraped from a dying vein of dragon-fire, put a bowl in front of a stranger and a bigger bowl in front of her loud little burden, and asked nothing first except names.

Yara gave hers easily, around a mouthful of something like barley, grown somehow down there in beds of ash and steam. I gave mine and watched it mean nothing to anyone, which was its own strange mercy. Bram Oyelaran, charm-seller, deserter-of-nothing, seller-of-luck-knots: nobody's legend. Just another body come down the stair.

Afterward Sef took us to her house—third from the corner, closest to the brightest knot of the vein, its walls sweating gentle heat. Inside it smelled of ink and dried herbs and decades of smoke. Shelves lined every surface, and most of the shelves held books, but between the books, row after row, stood small clay tablets, fired hard, incised with columns of tiny marks. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

"What is this place?" Yara whispered, awed into politeness for the first time in my acquaintance.

"My ledger," Sef said. She hung the shawls on their pegs by feel, precisely, in order. "Every soul that ever came down. Name, gift, date, what became of them. Those who went back up and survived." Her fingers walked the shelves. "Those who stayed." More shelves. "Those who were taken." The longest rows of all. She touched the end tablet of the longest row and rested her hand there a moment, the way you'd rest a hand on a grave-marker, or a shoulder.

"You've been counting the burned," I said.

"Fifty years," Sef said. "Before me, another keeper counted. Before her, another. Purges come in cycles, girl, like fevers. And they're getting worse, and they're getting *closer together*, and if you plot the dates on the wall the way I have—" she nodded at a great slate covered in chalk marks, columns descending in a pattern my eye refused at first and then, sickeningly, completed— "you'll see they aren't random. They're harvests. Regular as tithes. Somebody upstairs is being *fed*."

"And the gods," I said. My thumb had found the groove of my empty pocket. "You said taken. Not dead. How do you take a god?"

Sef lowered herself into a chair by the vein-warmed wall, and for the first time since we'd met her, she looked tired—tired in the marrow, tired the way only someone who has been right about something terrible for decades gets to be tired.

"That is the story I tell the ones who stay," she said. "It begins with a king who was crowned young, and something already waiting in the throne. It ends—" she turned her clouded eyes toward me, and this time they didn't miss by an inch— "it ends with a severed thread, and the question of why the cutter left yours unbroken. Sit down, Bram Oyelaran. You've been walking on ground that lies to you for seven years. Tonight you learn what the ground down here remembers."

Yara curled up beside me on the warm bench, her head heavy against my shoulder, her magic finally—blessedly—quiet, banked low in the presence of a fire so much older and vaster than hers. Outside, the drowned street glowed and breathed. Above us, miles of stone and lie, the king slept in whatever passed for sleep beneath his crown.

Sef drew breath to begin, and reached for the first tablet of the oldest row.
