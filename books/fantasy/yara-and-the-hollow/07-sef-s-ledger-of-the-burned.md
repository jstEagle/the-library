## 7. Sef's Ledger of the Burned

The fire kept its own hours. Sef had learned them the way you learn a stubborn animal—by sitting with it, by watching which way its breath leaned, by knowing when to feed it and when to let it sulk. Tonight it leaned toward the girl.

That was the first thing worth noting, in her ledger of small observations: the dragon-fire liked Yara. It stretched a little when she came near, like an old dog hearing footsteps it remembered. Sef had seen it do that only once before, forty years ago, for a woman whose name had been scraped off every wall in Vell before Sef ever learned it.

The second thing worth noting was Bram Oyelaran's hands.

They were mercenary's hands, scarred across the knuckles, but they hadn't stopped moving since the prayer-bead came out. Turning it. Pressing it flat against her palm as if she could feel a pulse in it. Seven years that bead had been dead weight, and now the woman held it like something newly wounded.

Sef poured the third cup of nettle-broth without asking. In the Hollow you didn't ask; you poured, and people drank, because refusing kindness down here was a luxury nobody could afford twice.

"Drink," she said. "Then I'll tell you why your god went quiet. And after that, girl—" this to Yara, who was crouched at the fire's rim with her chin on her knees, eyes wide as coins "—you'll want to hear it too, though it won't be kind."

* * *

"I was not born under the city," Sef began, settling onto her stool with the groan of joints that had carried too many stairs. The firelight caught the milk-white ruin of her left eye—the one that saw nothing in this world and, if the Hollow's gossip was to be believed, a great deal in others. "I was born above it. In Vell proper. A dyer's daughter off Tanner's Row, back when Tanner's Row still smelled of dye and not of ash."

Bram said nothing. Good. The ones who interrupted wanted a story shaped their way; the ones who listened let it come out true.

"My mother had the gift. Small things—a mordant that took color deeper than any bought chemical, a blessing over the vats so the cloth never rotted. Nothing loud. Nothing that would make a candle jump." Sef's mouth twisted. "You understand that kind of quiet craft, I think. You've been selling it yourself, wrapped in knot-work and market patter."

Bram's jaw tightened, but she drank her broth.

"In those days there was no Edict. There was no Inquisition with hounds and white cloaks. What there was, was a king who began to change." She let the fire pop once before going on. "This was under old King Aldric's reign—I've outlived three kings, don't look so surprised, the Hollow keeps you like brine keeps fish. Aldric had been a middling man, fond of hunting, fond of his mistress, forgettable in the way most kings are forgettable. And then one winter he woke up *perfect*. That's the only word for it. Perfect posture, perfect memory, perfect edicts falling from his mouth like coins from a torn purse. His own wife said he smelled of cold stone."

Yara shifted. "Was he sick?"

"He was *occupied*, child. But we didn't know it yet. What we knew was what happened next. The purges started small. A hedge-witch in the river districts. A choir-singer whose voice could settle fevers. Each one burned publicly, each one burned with ceremony, and here is the part no one above remembers, because no one above was counting—" Sef raised one gnarled finger. "The dragon-fire grew."

She gestured at the vein of it behind her, that guttering orange thread running through the black drowned stone like a heartbeat trapped in rock. It had been brighter then. Everything had been brighter then; that was the whole tragedy in a sentence.

"This fire is old. Older than Vell, older than the pre-city we're sitting in the bones of. It's a wound, if you want the truth—a dragon died here before men built walls, and what's left of its burning never finished dying. For centuries it just smoldered, minding itself, warming the drowned quarter where the desperate and the discarded hid. Refugees from every purge since the founding have sheltered around it. It doesn't heal, exactly, but it *holds*. You feel it?"

Bram nodded slowly. Sef had watched her feel it already, on the descent—that moment when the cold water-gloom broke against a wall of living warmth and even the hardest mercenary flinched toward it like a child toward a lit window.

"Every burning fed it," Sef said. "Not metaphorically. I've stood where you're standing while they burned a gifted man above ground, miles away, and watched this flame stand up straight as a soldier. Grief has a smell, and fire knows it. We thought—we *hoped*—that meant the burnings were not entirely waste. That something of the burned came home here. That the fire was a ledger keeping their souls safe until better days."

Her good eye found Bram's face.

"It isn't a ledger, Bram Oyelaran. It's a *counterweight*. And I am old enough, and fool enough, to have gone looking for what sits on the other end of the scale."

* * *

"There's a chapel down here," Sef said. "Drowned, like everything else. The Chapel of the Nine Shepherds—it gave this place its name, did you know that? Hollow. Not hollowed by water. Hollowed by *absence*. When the pre-city flooded and the nine gods' congregations moved uphill to build Vell, they left the chapels standing, and the gods—well. Gods are anchored to their houses. They lingered. Faintly. Like heat in a wall after the stove's gone cold."

"The Shepherd," Bram said. Her voice came out rough, scraped thin. "One of the nine."

"One of the nine," Sef agreed. "The last one to go. The others faded or fled over the long centuries—gods are shy of cities that stop needing them. But the Shepherd stayed sworn to a company of mercenaries, and a god with sworn soldiers doesn't fade easily. Sworn blood is an anchor chain, girl. As long as one of the line lives and keeps the faith, the god holds on." She watched Bram's hand close around the broken prayer-bead until the knuckles whitened. "Your grandmother's grandmother's line. I'd wager my other eye on it."

"You said you went looking," Bram managed. "For the counterweight."

"I did." Sef folded her hands. This was the part of the telling she hated, and she had told it perhaps six times in forty years, each time to someone about to need it. "Forty years ago, the purges were bad again—bad the way they're getting bad now. And the fire was growing fat and strange, and I was younger and stupider, and I followed the scale down. If every burning fed this warmth, I reasoned, something must be drinking the rest. Fire doesn't come from nowhere. It's a transfer. Always has been. So I went into the deep foundations—the ones that run up under the palace rock, the ones the pre-city builders cut before the flood—and I walked until the water ended and the *cold* began."

Yara had gone very still by the fire. Even the flame seemed to lean away from her, listening.

"There's a root system under Vell," Sef said quietly. "Not tree roots. God-roots. The old chapels weren't built *on* the land, child—they were built *into* it, into channels where divinity ran like groundwater. And threaded through those channels, wound tight around them like wire strangling a vine, is a thing that has been eating for a very long time."

"Cold," Bram said. Not a question.

"Cold," Sef confirmed. "Colder than the flood-water. Colder than the dark between stars, if the stories are honest. I got within sight of it—one glimpse, through a seam in the rock, before my hair froze to my skull and I crawled back half-dead. One glimpse was enough. It's vast, and it's patient, and it is *bound*—chained into the foundations by the same old covenant-channels that anchor the nine. It can't move. It can't reach. So it does what any starving thing does." Her voice dropped. "It grows a mouth where the food comes to it."

"And the food," Bram said, "is the crown."

"The crown sits directly above the deepest channel. Of course it does—you think kings choose their thrones? The palace wasn't built on bedrock, it was built on the *seal*. And somewhere in the last century, whether by ambition or accident or something whispering sweetly through the stone, the crown became a door. Every gifted soul burned in the city square above—their gift doesn't rise to heaven and it doesn't sink to this fire. It runs *down*. Along the grief-lines. Into the channels. Into the thing beneath the throne. The dragon-fire is just what spills over, the residue, the crumbs off a table set for something far hungrier."

Yara spoke without lifting her chin from her knees. "It eats gods."

"It eats *bound* divinity, yes. Loose magic slips it—too quick, too scattered, like trying to swallow smoke. But a bound god is a feast. Anchored, channeled, held steady by sworn bloodlines and consecrated ground—it can't run. It can only be drained." Sef turned her good eye full on Bram. "Seven years ago, girl, what happened? Tell me exactly. Not what you assumed. What you *felt*."

Bram's silence lasted long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Nothing," she said finally. "That was the horror of it. No death-dream, no falling star, no omen. The prayers just stopped being answered. Like speaking into a well and hearing the rope come back up empty. I thought—" Her breath caught. "I thought I'd failed some test. I thought the Shepherd had judged me wanting and withdrawn. I spent seven years ashamed of a silence."

"You spent seven years mourning a swallowed god and blaming yourself for the swallowing." Sef's voice was gentle, which cost her something; gentleness always did. "Listen to me, Bram Oyelaran, and mark it well, because I will only say it once. Your Shepherd did not leave. Your Shepherd did not die of neglect. Your Shepherd was *eaten*—slowly, year by year, along with the last of the nine, dragged down through the channels your own sworn bloodline maintained. The purges aren't cleansing anything. They never were. They are *feeding*. Every witch burned above ground is another mouthful for the thing wearing your king, and the Edict, the Inquisition, the hounds and the pyres—all of it, all of it, is a farm. A farm with one crop, and the crop is divinity, and the farmer has been hollowing this realm for longer than you've been alive."

* * *

The fire had gone quiet, the way it did when it listened. Yara reached out and laid two fingers against the warm stone, and the flame leaned into the touch like a cat.

"That's why it likes me," the girl said. Not a question either. Children asked statements in that tone when they'd understood something too large for questions.

Sef studied her. Loud magic, the mercenary had called it—magic that leaked, that couldn't hide. Warmth in winter rooms, singing kettles, light off her fingertips. Sef had lived long enough to know what loud magic usually meant: a gift too big for its vessel, pressure escaping every seam.

Or a vessel the fire recognized.

"Loud magic slips the thing below," Sef said aloud, deciding to give them both the truth whole. "Loose, wild, unchanneled—it can't be drunk. That's why the Inquisition burns the loud ones first, girl, whatever their priests say about order. Not because you're dangerous to *them*." She tapped the stone floor with her staff. "Because you're dangerous to *it*. A leaky pot can't be emptied cleanly. They want you bound first—bound gifts pour. Bound gods pour. Everything chained and sworn and solemn flows downhill into that cold mouth. Everything free stays free."

Bram had gone the color of the drowned stone. "The binding rites. The swearing-oaths. The consecrated ground. All of it—"

"A plumbing system," Sef agreed. "Built by pious hands, over centuries, for gods who needed anchoring to survive. And repurposed." She spat, an old woman's contempt, precise as a knife. "Whoever—or whatever—first taught the crown to drink knew the channels intimately. Knew them from the inside. I've had forty years to wonder about that, and I'll tell you where wondering led me: nowhere good. The pre-city builders left records in the chapel murals, if you can read them. Most are water-eaten. Some aren't. Some show a ninth shepherd walking down into the deep places with a rope around his own waist, and the rope coming back up *empty*."

"Stop," Bram said.

Sef stopped. She had given enough hard truths tonight; the woman's hands were shaking around the prayer-bead, and mercy meant knowing when the cup was full.

But Bram lifted her head, and there was something in her face that Sef recognized from forty years of watching soldiers and fugitives pass through her firelight—the expression of a person reassembling their entire world around a new load-bearing fact.

"It answered me anyway," Bram whispered. "Once. Three years after the silence. One night, one prayer, and I felt—something. Cold. I thought it was the Shepherd, changed. I thought I'd been forgiven." She looked down at the broken bead. "It wasn't him. Something heard me praying down the drain-line and *tasted* me."

"Perhaps," Sef said. "Or perhaps a fragment of him still fights in there, and reached for his own. Either way, mercenary, understand what you're carrying. That bead is a piece of a devoured god, and you are the last of his sworn blood, and the thing beneath the palace knows both facts even if you didn't." She rose, joints crackling, and shuffled to the recess in the far wall where she kept the only object in the Hollow more precious than fire: a book, water-swollen, hand-copied across four generations. The ledger. Not of debts. Of names. Every gifted soul burned above ground since Sef's grandmother began the count, recorded here because nowhere else would remember them.

She set it in Bram's lap.

"Read it tomorrow, when your legs work and your mind is steadier. Read how many. Read how *long*. And then ask yourself the question I've been asking for forty years, the question that brought me down here and kept me here." Her good eye glittered. "If the feeding stops—if the flow is cut—what happens to everything the thing has swallowed? Because a god isn't digested all at once, girl. And I have heard, on quiet nights, through forty feet of drowned rock, this fire humming with voices. Prayers. *Answered* prayers, from the wrong direction."

Above them, faint as a memory, the bells of Vell rang the hour—and beneath the ringing, so deep that only the Hollow's oldest residents felt it rather than heard it, something far below shifted in its sleep, and the dragon-fire bent, briefly, toward the palace.

Sef watched Bram's face as the woman felt it too.

"Now you know why your god went silent," Sef said. "Tomorrow you decide what you're going to do about it. But I warn you, Bram Oyelaran—the answer to that question is why I keep this ledger. And the next page in it may yet have room for a name you love."
