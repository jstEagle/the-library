## 8. The King's Cold Court

The throne room of Vell smelled of lilies and cold stone, and Maren Voss had learned to hate both.

She stood in the shadow of a pillar with her hands folded behind her back, watching the morning audience the way she watched everything: for cracks. The hall was built to overwhelm—a hundred feet of vaulted ceiling painted with clouds that never darkened, columns carved with the likenesses of kings going back nine generations, banners of white and gold hanging motionless in air that no window dared disturb. Light came from everywhere and nowhere, filtered through alabaster panels until it fell soft as milk. It was a room designed to make every visitor feel small and slightly blind, and it worked on everyone but her.

Voss had spent thirty years learning not to be small. She had been a border girl from the Salt Marches, the daughter of a customs officer who kept ledgers so precise they were almost prayers, and she had come up through the Inquisition by being the one person in any room who counted things twice. She knew how many steps it was from the great doors to the dais—forty-seven. She knew how many candles burned at the king's evening devotions—three hundred, one for each parish, though half those parishes now had no faithful left to light them. She knew the exact weight of the Edict scroll currently unrolling before the throne, because her office had commissioned the vellum: four pounds, three ounces, sealed in wax mixed with ash from the last pyre in Tarrow's Field.

And she knew—had known for some years now, in the place where she kept the knowledge she did not examine—that something was wrong with the way King Aldous sat his throne.

He was beautiful. That was the first thing everyone said, and it was true. Fifty-eight years old and he had the face of a man of forty, smooth and pale as the alabaster overhead, with eyes of such a clear grey they seemed backlit. His posture was flawless. His hands, folded on the armrests of the throne, did not tremble. He never coughed, never shifted, never cleared his throat during the long hours of audience. Court physicians called it remarkable vigor. The priests called it divine favor.

Voss called it what it was, in the privacy of her own skull: stillness. Not the stillness of health. The stillness of a pond so deep and cold that nothing living moved in it.

"Read it again," the king said.

The herald, a young man whose voice had been breaking all season, swallowed and smoothed the scroll. "By decree of His Majesty Aldous, Third of That Name, Sovereign of Vell and Protector of the Faith: all persons found to carry the unsanctioned gift shall present themselves at the houses of cleansing within thirty days. Those who harbor them shall share their—"

"I know the words," the king said gently. "Again."

There was a ripple among the courtiers—confusion, quickly smothered. Voss did not move. She watched the king's face as the herald read the proclamation a third time, and there it was again, the thing only she seemed to notice: the faint, absent pleasure that crossed the king's features as the words went by, like a man listening to a favorite piece of music rather than a sentence he had, presumably, dictated himself. He mouthed along with the final clause. *Shall be cleansed root and branch.* His lips curved. It was almost a smile. Almost.

The court applauded when it ended. The sound was thin, obedient. The king raised one hand and the applause died mid-beat, cut off like a snuffed wick, and no one remarked on how precisely it stopped.

"General Voss." His voice carried across the marble without effort; it always carried exactly as far as he wanted it to. "Attend me."

She crossed the floor. Forty-seven steps; she made it thirty-one, cutting the diagonal, because protocol bent for Inquisitors-General even if nothing else in this reign did. At the foot of the dais she bowed—not deeply, not shallowly—and waited.

"You have numbers for me," the king said.

"I have numbers for anyone who asks, Majesty." She produced the ledger from beneath her cloak. It was a habit from her father, carrying it against her ribs like a second heart. "As of yesterday's audit: two hundred and eleven gifted persons taken into custody since the Edict's proclamation. Sixty-three cleansed after confession. One hundred and forty-eight awaiting process. Informer reports received: nine hundred and six. Verified: one hundred and twelve."

"And the rest?"

"Inaccurate." She let the word sit. "Neighbors settling debts, Majesty. A miller in Copperlane denounced a rival's flour. A widow denounced her late husband's brother over an inheritance. We are generating more smoke than fire."

The king considered this. Or—he held the ledger's information the way a drain holds water; it went into him and was gone, leaving nothing behind. "Smoke is useful," he said. "Smoke keeps people looking up. Continue the process. And General—" those clear grey eyes settled on her, and for a moment, just a moment, Voss felt something pass over her skin like a draft from a cellar door, "—your hounds lost a scent in the Lamplighters' Quarter three nights ago. A child."

"The report is in my office, Majesty. An unregistered gifted, age approximately eleven, escaped custody through the rooftops. Pursuit continues."

"A child," the king repeated, and now he did smile, fully, warmly, the smile of a grandfather at a hearth, and it was the worst thing Voss had seen all week, and she had watched a woman burn on Tuesday. "Children burn brightest. Bring me that one, General. Do not disappoint the crown."

"As Your Majesty commands."

He turned away. Dismissed. She descended the dais and took up her post by the pillar once more, and the audience rolled on around her: petitioners with drainage grievances, a delegation of wool merchants, the Lord Chamberlain reading out appointments. And through all of it the king sat in his perfect stillness, granting, denying, blessing, his voice falling from the throne in tones of warm honey, and Voss stood in her pillar's shadow and did the arithmetic she could not stop doing.

Seven years ago, she reminded herself, the realm had been coming apart. She had seen it herself—the famine years, the god-plagues, churches emptying as the Shepherd and the Mother-of-Streams and the Ninefold went silent one after another, whole provinces sliding into banditry and despair. She had been a captain then, holding a bridge in the Salt Marches while refugees poured over it, and she had believed, with the simple ferocity of a woman watching children starve, that anything was better than the abyss. Then Aldous had taken the crown and the silence had ended—or something had ended—and order had returned like a tide. The roads were safe. The granaries filled. The pyres burned, yes, but the city slept at night without fear.

Order required a center. Every institution she had ever served had taught her that, and she had watched what happened to places without one. If the center had a flaw—if the man on the throne was more vessel than man—then the flaw was the price of the walls. She had paid worse prices. Everyone in this hall had. They simply didn't count what they paid.

That was the creed she had built, stone by stone, over fifteen years of service. Most days it held her weight.

Today it creaked.

* * *

The Inquisition kept its offices in the old Customhouse on the Ropewalk, a building of black basalt that had once weighed imported silk and now weighed souls. Voss worked past sunset, which was usual, and sent her clerks home, which was also usual, and then unlocked the drawer where she kept the reports she had not filed.

There were eleven of them now. She laid them out on the scarred oak desk and weighted the corners with inkwells, and looked down at eleven documents that should not have been able to coexist.

The first was a census abstract, five years old: the gift appeared in roughly one Vell citizen in four hundred, per the assessments conducted under the old Church, which had tracked its faithful the way she tracked contraband. Applied to the capital's population, that gave a steady-state expectation of perhaps ninety new cases a year in the city and its environs, most of them minor—warm-handed healers, charm-sellers, children who made kettles sing.

The other ten documents were the purge tallies, quarter by quarter, since the Edict. Two hundred and eleven taken in the city alone. Four hundred and six across the realm in the same span. And that was only the verified number; the informer system ran hot, the border chapters reported in their own formats, and the pyres at Tarrow's Field had burned for six days without full registry because the recording clerk had been conscripted as a stoker.

The numbers did not balance. They did not come close to balancing. Either the gift had begun appearing at ten times its historical rate—which would itself be a crisis demanding study—or the crown was burning through the population's small magics like a man burning furniture in a house where something else was cold.

Voss stood over the papers for a long time. Down in the yard, the duty hounds began to bark, then stopped all at once, in unison, the way they sometimes did near the palace district, and she noted that too, and hated that she noted it.

Her father had taught her arithmetic as a moral discipline. *A ledger that balances is honest,* he used to say, running his thumb down the columns of salt duties and bribes-that-were-fees. *A ledger that doesn't balance means someone is stealing, and the thief is always upstream.* She had built her career on that principle. She had ruined men with it. She had sent three corrupt magistrates to their own pyres with columns of figures, and the crowds had cheered, and she had slept well.

So: someone was stealing. Upstream. From whom? From what?

She thought of the king mouthing the edict along with the herald. She thought of the hounds falling silent in unison. She thought of the physician who had examined His Majesty last spring—a careful, frightened man named Ellery—who had told her afterward, over brandy he drank too fast, that the king's heartbeat was slow. Very slow. Eleven beats to the minute, sustained, and yet the body functioned perfectly, better than perfectly, and when Ellery suggested further examination, the king had smiled and thanked him and Ellery had requested reassignment to the plague posts within the month.

Eleven beats a minute. Voss had looked it up. Hibernating bears ran faster.

She put her hand flat on the eleven documents, as if she could hold them still. There was a version of her—the border girl, the customs officer's daughter—who took these papers to the Privy Council, or to the Archprelate, or to whoever in this rotting edifice still had a spine. There was another version who burned them and never spoke of it and rose higher on the strength of her perfect discretion.

Both versions were lies. She knew that. The papers would go nowhere; half the Privy Council flinched from the king's shadow, and the Archprelate hadn't preached a sermon containing an original sentence in six years. And burning them would only mean the next auditor started from zero, ignorant, the way Voss herself had been ignorant for years, trusting the machinery because she had helped build it.

There was a third option, and it had been waiting for her at the bottom of the drawer for months, patient as debt: keep counting. Quietly. Build the case no one asked for. Falsify nothing yet—but round the tallies down in the copies that went upstairs, misfile the sharpest reports, slow the machine by fractions, the way a customs officer might slow a shipment with stamps and inspections until the smuggler's cargo rotted in the hold. She had done it before, to slavers, early in her career. She remembered how it had felt: like pushing against a river with your palms.

But the river, this time, wore a crown. And she had seen what happened to people who pushed against it. The purge lists grew weekly, and lately she noticed that names were appearing on them that she had not submitted—names from her own secondary files, her watch-lists of the marginally gifted, the ones she'd flagged for observation rather than arrest. Someone above her was reading her files and feeding from them. Or something was.

Voss squared the papers, returned them to the drawer, and locked it with the key she wore on a cord around her neck, under her shirt, where no search would casually find it. Then she sat down and wrote the evening dispatch to the palace, in her precise, upright hand, reporting the day's progress in terms calculated to satisfy without illuminating—and for the first time in fifteen years of writing dispatches, she caught herself, pen hovering, choosing a false emphasis. Reporting two hundred and eleven taken as though the hunt were expanding, when the truth was the trail in the Lamplighters' Quarter had gone cold as a dead hearth. Reporting confidence where she felt none.

*The child remains at large. All resources committed.*

It was even, technically, true. But she wrote it knowing what she was doing, and she set the seal in green wax instead of red—the color of routine—and understood, watching the wax cool, that she had just crossed a line so fine she could not see it behind her, only feel it, the way you feel a threshold underfoot in the dark.

Somewhere above the Ropewalk, in the palace on its hill, the king was awake. He was always awake. She had checked the guard rotations once, idly, professionally, and discovered that no one had seen His Majesty sleep in recorded memory. He sat in his apartments with the lights burning, or he walked the colonnade at three in the morning, alone, and the guards on duty swore afterward—they swore it laughing, embarrassed, wanting to be disbelieved—that his shadow did not always move when he did. Sometimes it arrived a half-second late. Sometimes it pointed the wrong way.

Voss did not believe in ghosts. She believed in ledgers, and the ledger said: *something upstream is taking more than it reports.* And the ledger said: *the theft has been going on far longer than the Edict. The Edict is not the crime. The Edict is the harvest.*

She blew out her lamp and stood in the dark of her office, listening to the building settle, and permitted herself one thought she had forbidden herself for years, letting it rise like a bubble through deep water:

*What am I serving?*

She had no answer. She suspected, with a certainty that felt like frost forming, that the answer was already sitting on the throne, wearing a dead man's face, and had been for longer than anyone dared remember.

Tomorrow she would pull the founding records of the Inquisition. Tomorrow she would find out who had written its charter, and when, and why its mandate spoke so oddly of *bound* divinity, as though binding were the natural state of gods.

Tonight she stood in the dark, and downstairs the hounds began to howl—all of them at once, all of them at nothing—and Maren Voss, true believer, servant of order, listened, and did not go down to quiet them.

She was afraid, and for the first time she let herself know it.

Far above, on the palace hill, a single light burned in the royal apartments, steady and white and unwavering, like an eye that never blinked, and it watched the city all night long.
