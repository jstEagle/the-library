## 9. Training the Storm

The Hollow had a rhythm to it that Vell above never managed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark in a slow, patient count—drip, drip, drip—like the city's old heart still beating under all its stone. Bram woke to it every morning now, or what passed for morning down here, where Sef's people marked time by the pulse of the dragon-fire and not by any sun.

Three days since they'd come down off the Deep Stair with Yara half-frozen and burning hot at the same time, her magic spitting sparks like wet wood thrown on a blaze. Three days of listening to refugees whisper about the girl who glowed.

That wouldn't do. Glow long enough down here and someone would sell you up the stair for bread money. Fear made people honest about their own survival if nothing else.

So Bram did what she'd done her whole mercenary life: she turned a liability into drill.

"Again," she said.

Yara sat cross-legged on the flat stone before the fire-vein, the great guttering ribbon of it running along the far wall of the chamber like a river deciding whether to live or die. The heat came off it in waves, orange light shivering across the drowned brickwork overhead. A dozen Hollow-folk watched from the edges, pretending not to watch, which was its own kind of pressure—and pressure was the point.

"I'm *doing* it," Yara said through her teeth.

"You're doing the thing where you squeeze your eyes shut and hope." Bram crouched across from her, elbows on knees. "Hope isn't a cage. Open your eyes."

Yara opened them. They were too bright—not fever-bright but gift-bright, that faint amber bleed at the iris Bram had seen once in a comrade back in the Border Wars, right before the man burned out his own heart trying to level a fortress wall.

"Now. The kettle trick. Show me."

Yara exhaled and held out her hand. Above her palm, a bead of warmth gathered—a small golden shimmer, no bigger than a marble, wobbling like water in an upturned cup. It was beautiful. It was also leaking; little threads of heat curled off it and set the dust motes spinning, and somewhere behind the watching crowd a copper pot began, very softly, to hum.

"The pot," Bram said.

Yara's jaw tightened. "I know."

"It knows your shape. Every loose thing you carry goes looking for metal and fire because those are easy doors. You're not containing it, girl. You're just holding the lid down with one finger while everything else boils over."

"Then tell me how to hold it with *both hands* instead of talking about pots!"

Bram let the shout land. Let it echo. Down here the echo always came back twice, off the brick and off the water, so that Yara heard her own anger from outside herself—which was, incidentally, lesson one.

"Feel that?" Bram asked quietly. "That's what everyone else hears when you lose your temper. Twice as loud as you think. Now breathe. In through the nose, four counts. Hold four. Out four."

"That's stupid."

"That's how I stayed alive through nine campaigns while men around me lit themselves up like festival lanterns." She held Yara's glare until the girl broke first, dragged in a breath, counted it out raggedly. "Better. Again."

* * *

The truth—the part Bram didn't say, couldn't say—was that she was teaching Yara her own discipline secondhand, filtered through things she'd learned from other people because she'd never needed to learn it herself. Bram's gift had always been *quiet*. Small workings, small costs: a charm that turned a blade a hair's width, a knot that kept fever down, a word that made a sentry blink at the wrong moment. The Shepherd had been a god of thresholds and margins, of the space between the wolf and the flock, and his magic fit in the palm the way his prayer-bead fit there now, worn smooth as a river stone against her thumb.

Yara was not a god of margins. Yara was a god of the storm front.

Which meant the mercenary tricks had to be bent, reshaped, hammered into new shapes late at night while Yara slept, because Bram refused—*refused*—to teach her anything that might crack her open the way she'd seen the loud ones cracked open. There had been a woman in the Third Company. Ilsette. Who could call lightning down out of a clear sky and laughed while she did it, and died at thirty-one because she'd never learned that a gift that big needs walls, not wings.

Bram thought about Ilsette more these days than she liked.

"Again," she said, for the eleventh time that session.

* * *

It went badly for two days. Of course it did. Yara's power didn't leak so much as *insist*, pushing at whatever container she built for it like a child testing a locked door. The breathing helped. The counting helped. What helped most, unexpectedly, was rope.

Bram had found a coil of it among the salvage near the fire-vein, tarred hemp, mercenary-grade, and she'd taken to making knots while Yara practiced—luck-knots, the old patterns, her fingers moving without her. On the second evening Yara stopped mid-exercise and stared.

"What are you *doing?*"

"Habit." Bram tied off the last loop. "You want to learn containment? Watch my hands. Don't watch the light. Watch the hands."

She undid the knot slowly. "Every knot is a promise. You put tension in exactly the places it belongs and nowhere else. Too tight here—" she pulled a loop taut "—and the whole thing strangles itself. Too loose here, and it slides apart when you need it most. Magic's the same. It's not about crushing your gift down small. It's about knowing where the tension lives."

Yara frowned at the rope like it had insulted her. Then, grudgingly: "Show me again."

They worked until the fire-vein dimmed toward its rest-cycle, Sef's people drifting off to their niches and hammocks, and by the third day something shifted. Yara held the warm bead above her palm for a full hundred count and nothing else in the chamber hummed, nothing sparked, the dust hung still. When she finally let it go, she let it go gently, folding it back into herself like a bird returning to a roost, and looked up at Bram with an expression of such naked, startled pride that Bram's chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

"Well?" Yara demanded.

"Adequate."

"*Adequate?* I held it for a hundred! Old One-Eye over there bet Torvin I'd blow us all up!" She jerked a thumb at a gap-toothed dredger who had the decency to look sheepish.

"A hundred is a start. Inquisitor hounds don't count to a hundred and stop."

But Yara was already laughing, bright and sudden, and throwing her arms around Bram's neck before Bram could decide whether to allow it. She allowed it. She stood very stiff and patted the girl's shoulder twice, like a man defusing something, and told herself the tightness in her throat was the smoke from the fire-vein.

* * *

Afterward they ate—barley and eel stew, the Hollow's staple, better than it sounded—and Yara talked with her mouth full about everything and nothing, about Torvin's stories of the flooded opera house, about whether fish could see in the dark, about whether the king really slept in armor. Bram mostly listened. This, too, had become part of the drill, though neither of them named it: the girl needed noise after containment, needed to pour herself out somewhere harmless, and Bram had discovered she made an acceptable drain.

Then Yara went quiet in that particular way she had, the way that meant a real question was loading.

"Bram?"

"Mm."

"Why don't you ever use yours?"

The spoon paused halfway to Bram's mouth. Around them the fire-vein pulsed, slow and low, and somewhere deep in the drowned dark the water counted its endless drip.

"My gift is small," she said. "Charms. Knots. Things too little to matter."

"That's a lie."

Bram looked at her. Eleven years old, stew on her chin, eyes steady in that unnervingly adult way she sometimes had—as though she'd been waiting her whole short loud life for someone worth asking.

"It's not a lie," Bram said carefully. "It's a smaller truth than the whole one."

"So give me the whole one."

*And what would I give you, girl? That my god went silent seven years ago and I've been shouting into that silence every night since, alone, ashamed, like a widow talking to an empty chair? That I keep his broken prayer-bead in my pocket and can't decide if it's a relic or a reproach? That every charm I sell is a coin tossed down a well, and I don't know anymore whether I'm paying a debt or burying something?*

"There was a war," Bram said instead. "There's always a war. I spent mine learning to be quiet, and quiet became a habit, and habits become bones. You don't undo your own skeleton."

Yara chewed on that, literally and otherwise. "Your god's still there, though. Right? Gods don't die. Sef says—"

"Sef says a lot of things."

"She says yours is one of the old ones. The shepherd-kind. That they used to walk the borderlands before the cities got big." Yara's voice dropped, going sly-gentle the way children do when they think they're being comforting. "Maybe he's not silent. Maybe he's just far away. Maybe he'd come back if you called louder."

Something in Bram's chest turned over, slow and cold.

*He's not far away,* said the part of her that had done the arithmetic seven years ago and never stopped checking the sum. *Gods don't go silent all at once, everywhere, for every sworn servant at the same hour unless something took them. And if something took him, then either it was stronger than him—in which case calling is pointless—or he left on purpose, in which case calling is worse than pointless.*

She didn't say any of it. She reached across the stone and tapped Yara's bowl.

"Eat. Tomorrow we work on holding two beads at once, and you'll want the strength."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Bram agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

But the question followed her, the way questions did, down into the niche she'd claimed near the fire-vein's lower reach where the heat kept the damp out of her joints. She lay awake with the prayer-bead in her fist, thumb moving over its broken face, the fracture line where it had snapped the night her god went quiet—she'd been mid-prayer, kneeling in a ditch outside Marrow Ford, and the bead had cracked like ice on a pond and the presence she'd felt at her back her whole life, patient and faintly warm, had simply... gone. Not faded. Gone. As if a door she hadn't known was open had closed.

Seven years of selling charms into a silence that never answered.

Seven years, and now an eleven-year-old asked *why don't you use it?* like it was simple, like the gift were a tool on a shelf, and the terrible thing—the thing Bram rolled over in the dark to avoid—was that the gift still answered. Her small workings still worked. Whatever had swallowed her god's voice had apparently left the plumbing intact. She could knot luck into rope and fever out of a child's brow and she had not done it, not once, not properly, since the bead broke, because each small working felt like speaking into a room where she couldn't see who—or what—might be listening on the other end.

Tonight, though, sleep wouldn't come and the question wouldn't leave, and at last she sat up, checked that Yara was truly asleep—sprawled, snoring, one arm flung out like she owned the world—and struck a spark to a candle stub.

She took a length of cord from her kit. Ordinary hemp. And she tied a luck-knot, the first pattern her mother's mother had taught her, fingers remembering what pride had tried to forget.

The knot took. Warmth bloomed along the cord, faint and familiar, the Shepherd's old signature—threshold-magic, margin-magic, the blessing of safe passage. It worked.

It worked, and for one heartbeat Bram felt something at the edge of it, at the far end of the working where the god's attention used to rest like a hand on her shoulder—

Not silence. Not quite. Something like silence, but shaped. Like a room where the furniture had been removed but the outlines still pressed into the carpet. Like a voice with nothing left but the dent it had left in the air.

Bram sat very still until the candle guttered.

Then she untied the knot, thread by thread, put the cord away, and lay back down staring at the underside of the drowned city, where old brick arched overhead like the ribs of some enormous dead thing.

In the morning, she decided, they'd work on holding two beads. And after that, three. Because if Yara's storm was going to be aimed anywhere, it would be aimed by Bram, and Bram needed time—time before anyone upstairs found them, time before whatever lived in that shaped silence noticed the knot she'd tied tonight, time before the girl started asking the questions Bram wasn't ready to answer.

Time, she was beginning to suspect, was the one thing the Hollow didn't sell.

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone and water, a bell began to ring—one of Vell's bells, distant and distorted, tolling out some announcement to streets they could no longer see.

Yara stirred, frowned in her sleep, and murmured, "Is that for us?"

"No," Bram said softly, to a girl who couldn't hear her. "Not yet."

But she lay listening to the bell a long time, counting the strokes, and it did not stop soon enough.
