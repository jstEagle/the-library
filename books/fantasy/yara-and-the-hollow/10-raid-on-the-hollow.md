## 10. Raid on the Hollow

The leak was a boy of fourteen named Pellin, and Maren Voss knew it before her hounds did.

She stood in the interrogation room above the Saltgate barracks and watched him through the observation glass — a thin, river-pale child with soot under his nails and a story he'd rehearsed too carefully. He had told them about fish markets. He had told them about his aunt's lodgings. He had not yet been asked about the Hollow, and already his eyes kept flicking toward it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

"He went down for candles," Sergeant Ives said beside her. "Three times this month. Nobody buys candles three times in the drowned quarter. There's nothing down there but rats and—"

"Refugees." Voss said the word flatly, the way she said all words. "Yes."

The reports called them remnants. Stragglers from the purges of thirty years past, forty, sixty — generations of the gifted who had slipped through nets that were supposed to be perfect. For two years the Inquisition had known they existed the way one knows there is rot under a floorboard: certain, and unwilling to pry up the boards because what lived beneath might be worse than the smell.

But the numbers troubled her now. They had troubled her for weeks, though she could not have said why out loud without sounding like a madwoman. The burned exceeded the census. The arithmetic of the cleansings refused to balance, as if the city itself were lying to its own ledgers.

And a king who repeated edicts in a voice like water over stones did not invite questions.

"Prepare a descent," Voss said. "Full company. Hounds first. We go at low tide, when the river gives us the old stairs." She paused, watching Pellin weep into his hands, and felt nothing she could name. "And Ives. No fires below. If there are children down there, I want them brought up breathing."

Ives blinked. It was not an order Inquisitors gave. "Breathing, General?"

"Dead witnesses tell no tales," Voss said. "Live ones tell me where the rest are hiding."

It was even true. She chose to believe it was only that.

* * *

Bram smelled the hounds before anyone heard them.

She was awake because Yara was asleep badly — the girl radiated heat in her sleep like a banked forge, and the little alcove off the fire-vein had grown too warm again, sweat-dark and close. Bram had gone out to the main gallery for air, to sit with the ember-light on her knees and turn Sef's prayer-bead over in her fingers, matching bead to bead against her own broken one, when the smell arrived: wet dog and oiled leather and something underneath, chemical and wrong, the scent they rubbed on hounds to sharpen them against magic.

Seven years of mercenary instincts woke in her chest like a struck bell.

She was moving before she finished thinking. Through the sleeping galleries — hollowed cargo vaults from the pre-city, each one curtained with sailcloth and river-stink — shaking shoulders as she passed. "Up. Up *now*. Quietly." Old reflexes from the border wars, from retreats that became routs. Her voice came out in the low flat register that soldiers obeyed without questioning, and the Hollow's people obeyed it too: a tanner-woman with a stunted gift of mending; two brothers who glowed faintly in the dark if they forgot themselves; old Wenna, ninety if she was a day, who could still coax green things from dead wood and who took one look at Bram's face and began moving her grandchildren toward the deep tunnels without asking why.

Sef met her at the mouth of the fire-gallery, standing in the guttering red light like a stump of driftwood, half-blind eyes turned toward the stairs beyond.

"How many?" the old woman asked.

"Company strength. Maybe more. Hounds." Bram's mouth was dry. "Minutes. Less."

Sef nodded slowly, unsurprised, and Bram understood with a lurch that the keeper had been waiting for this night for decades. Had probably rehearsed it every day since she was young enough to run.

"The ledger," Sef said. "Take the ledger."

"I'm not leaving you to—"

"*The ledger.*" Not loud. But the fire dimmed at the word, as if the vein itself flinched. Sef pressed a wrapped bundle into Bram's arms — oilcloth, heavy, dense with pages — and then her cracked palm against Bram's cheekbone, brief and fierce. "You carry names now, hedge-witch. Names are heavier than swords. Go down the Deep Road stair and don't look back at anything, least of all me."

"Sef—"

"I held this fire when your mother was a rumor," the old woman said. "I will hold it tonight. That is not courage, child. It is just my work." She smiled, and her milky eyes caught the ember-light and seemed almost to see. "Besides. Someone must teach the fire which strangers to burn."

Then the hounds came down the stairs, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

They came in a flood of lantern-light and barking, and the Hollow answered.

Yara felt it happen the way you feel a storm arrive — pressure first, then sound. She had been herded with the others toward the deep tunnels, Bram's hand iron on her collar, when the first hound cleared the last step and the world split open into noise. Dogs screaming. Men shouting orders that dissolved into screams of their own. And through it all, rising from the fire-gallery, a sound Yara would hear for the rest of her life: Sef's voice, cracked and ancient and utterly unafraid, speaking words in no language Yara knew while the dragon-fire vein roared up from a whisper to a wall.

The heat reached them even three galleries away. Yara looked back despite everything Bram had said, and saw the tunnel mouth behind them filled with light — red-gold, alive, *angry* — and shadows crossing through it. Shadows of dogs that would not enter. Shadows of men who entered anyway, and did not come out.

"She's burning them," one of the brothers whispered, horrified. "The old woman's burning them alive—"

"She's holding the door," Bram said. Her face was stone. Her hand on Yara's shoulder was trembling. Both things were true at once. "Move."

They moved. Down and down, through passages that narrowed to crawl-spaces, a ragged line of the discarded: Wenna with a child on each hip, the tanner-woman hauling a man too old to walk fast, the glowing brothers lighting nothing because Bram had made them wrap their hands in wool. Behind them the roar of the fire swelled and shook the stone. Then it stuttered.

Then it dimmed.

Everyone felt it. You couldn't not feel it — the Hollow's warmth had been the only constant of their lives, the heartbeat under the city, and when it faltered the whole refugee column stumbled as if their own hearts had missed a beat. The fire was tied to its keepers. Sef was the last keeper. And somewhere above them, the vein was learning what it meant to lose her.

"No," Yara said. Small. Stupid. She dug her heels in. "No, we have to go back, we have to help her, we can't just—"

Bram caught her by both arms and crouched to her level, and Yara saw something in the woman's face she had never seen there: guilt, raw and fresh as a wound, worn openly because there was no time to hide it.

"Listen to me." Bram's voice cracked down the middle. "If we go back, she dies for nothing. If we live, she dies for us. Those are the only two choices left in the world tonight, and I will not let her death buy *nothing*." Her grip tightened. "Do you hear me? We are what she bought."

Yara heard. She hated hearing. She let herself be pulled onward, sobbing without sound, and behind them the fire dimmed another degree, and another, like a lamp running out of years.

* * *

Voss descended the last stair into hell and found it almost over.

The fire-gallery was a cathedral of dying light. Half her company was dead — she counted eleven bodies before she stopped counting, men she had trained, men whose names she knew — and the hounds had fled back up the stairs and would not come again, cowering and whining at the top like whipped animals. At the center of the gallery, at the heart of the dragon-fire vein, stood a small ancient woman with her arms raised, and the fire rose with her like a thing obedient and grieving.

"Inquisitor-General." The old woman did not turn around. Her voice carried impossibly through the roar. "Come to count us? Bring your ledger. I'll save you the trouble."

Voss raised a hand and her remaining soldiers halted. Something in her wanted — absurdly, unforgivably — to ask the woman's name.

"You are harboring the gifted," Voss said. "You are harboring children."

"I am harboring everyone your king threw away," the old woman said, "for longer than your king has been king. Ask yourself sometime why the crown fears a hole in the ground full of grandmothers." Now she turned, and her blind white eyes found Voss across the fire with terrible accuracy. "Ask yourself what the crown does with what it burns. Ask why the ash never weighs what the people weighed."

"Stand away from the fire," Voss said, and hated how small it sounded.

"No," Sef said pleasantly.

The fire went up.

Not at Voss — around her, a ring, a cage of light that melted stone and made the air scream. Voss stood in the eye of it and understood, with a clarity that would follow her into every sleep thereafter, that the old woman could have killed her in the first breath and had chosen the shape of a warning instead. A door held open long enough. No more.

By the time the fire fell back to embers, the gallery's far tunnel stood empty, and the old woman stood alone in the center of it, swaying, spent, smiling at nothing.

"Take her," Voss said. Her voice was steady. Inside her chest something had begun, quietly, to crack along a line she hadn't known was there.

Sef laughed — a dry rustle, almost kind — and spoke her last words to the whole gallery, to the dark tunnel, to no one:

"The ledger goes deeper than you think, General. Read it before you burn another name."

The soldiers moved. The old woman did not fight them. Whatever she had spent, she had spent it all, and the dragon-fire vein sank as they bound her wrists — sank from gold to red, from red to a sullen orange coal-color, dimming by degrees like a heart slowing. Voss watched it happen and thought, unbidden: *it is mourning her.*

She ordered the vein watched, the galleries searched, the survivors' trail mapped. She wrote none of it in her report that night. She could not have said why.

* * *

They ran until the stone changed.

Bram knew the moment by feel — the walls sweating, the air going cold and mineral, the sound of water somewhere below shifting from drip to rush. The Deep Road stair. Sef had told her of it in fragments over weeks of careful questions, and the fragments assembled themselves now under her feet: steps older than Vell, cut by hands that predated the crown, descending toward the barrow-water where the river-thing kept its tolls.

Behind them, twelve survivors strung out in a wounded line. Ahead, darkness with weight to it. Above, a fire going out.

Bram stopped at a landing where the stair widened, and made herself do the arithmetic she'd been refusing. Twelve. She had known the Hollow's people for six weeks. She had eaten their food, slept under their sailcloth, learned the brothers' names and Wenna's grandchildren's names. And she had brought the flare-charm customer's problem down here in the end — her visibility, her trail, her careless surviving — the way a dropped lamp brings fire to a hayloft. Sef had died holding a door that Bram's presence had marked.

*You carry names now.* She hugged Sef's ledger to her chest and felt every page like a stone.

Yara sat down abruptly on the step beside her, gray-faced, and put her head between her knees. Bram sat too. Neither of them spoke for a long time. Below, the water rushed. Above, the warmth was gone, truly gone, and the survivors clustered together against the cold with the instinctive closeness of sheep after a wolf has passed.

"She knew," Yara said finally. Her voice was scraped raw. "That night you came. She looked at your bead and she *knew* this was coming, didn't she. She'd always known."

"Yes."

"And she stayed anyway."

"Yes."

Yara wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, and stared down into the dark. "Then someone has to read that stupid ledger," she said. "All of it. Every name. Out loud, someday, somewhere they can hear it. That's the rent we owe her."

Bram looked at the girl — eleven years old, orphaned twice over now, motherless and Hollow-less in one night, sitting in the dark at the top of a road that led beneath the palace foundations — and felt something shift in her chest that had been calcifying for seven years. Not hope. Something harder and less comfortable. Obligation, maybe. Or the beginning of a vow.

"Someone will," Bram said. "Starting with us."

Wenna came hobbling up the line, a child asleep against her shoulder, and pressed a strip of dried fish into each of their hands without a word. The glowing brothers wrapped their hands in wool and lit the stair ahead, two faint blue stars in the black. One by one the survivors rose and faced downward, because there was nowhere else, because down was the only direction the world had left them.

Bram stood last. She looked back once, up the stair, toward the distant gallery where an old woman's fire was guttering to nothing, and she let herself feel all of it — the loss, the guilt, the debt — because Sef had deserved better than a coward's forgetting.

Then she turned, and led the twelve down into the dark, toward the water that remembered everything and forgave nothing, toward the barrow-road where a river-thing waited to collect its tolls — and where, though none of them knew it yet, the price of passage would be paid by the smallest among them, in the coin that hurts most to spend.
