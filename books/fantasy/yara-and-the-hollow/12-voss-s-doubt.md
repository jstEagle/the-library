## 12. Voss's Doubt

The ledgers of the Inquisition were kept in a room with no windows, on the third floor of the Hall of Cleansing, because Maren Voss believed that numbers, like faith, should not be exposed to weather.

She had built the system herself. Ten years ago, when she was still a captain with ink-stained cuffs and a reputation for catching gifted children that hounds missed, she had designed the audit: every arrest logged, every confession transcribed, every execution weighed and recorded to the ounce. The crown demanded accountability. The crown demanded proof that the fire was working.

She had never once doubted the arithmetic.

Until tonight.

* * *

The counting room smelled of tallow and cold stone. Voss sat alone at the long table, sleeves rolled, her graying hair pinned back with the plain iron clasp she'd worn since her husband's funeral. Around her, stacked in towers of leather and vellum, lay nine years of purges. Nine years of names.

She worked the way she did everything—methodically, without sentiment. Census rolls first: the tithe-books of Vell's districts, the parish counts, the guild registers of hedge-crafters and cunning-folk licensed in the old lax years before the Edict. She totaled them by hand, twice, then a third time when the second total refused to match the first.

It matched the third time. That was the problem.

"Say it plainly," she murmured to the empty room, because her father had taught her that a fear spoken aloud shrinks to its true size. "Say what you've found."

What she had found was this: the census of Vell and its surrounding provinces counted, at the outside, four thousand one hundred souls carrying even a trace of the gift—the flicker-witted, the charm-menders, the midwives who could ease a fever with a touch. Four thousand one hundred, generously reckoned.

The Inquisition had burned eleven thousand six hundred and forty.

Voss sat back. The candle guttered in air that suddenly felt too thin.

There were explanations. Of course there were. People lied on censuses; families hid their talented children from the tithe-collectors as they'd hidden them from tax-men for centuries. Travelers passed through and were caught uncounted. The provinces beyond the census rolls—

She pulled the provincial ledgers toward her and worked until her hand cramped. Even folding in every plausible margin of error, even assuming the census undercounted by half—a scandalous assumption, an impossible one—the numbers would not balance. There was a gap of nearly four thousand souls, a city's worth of extra dead, and the gap did not shrink. It grew, year over year, at a rate so steady it looked less like error and more like appetite.

She turned to the arrest records themselves, reading them now not as a commander but as something colder and older—an auditor, a woman who had once caught a grain-merchant falsifying weights across three parishes because his totals were too round.

Too many of the burned had been listed with identical confessions. Not similar—identical. Word for word, down to the phrase *"I did feel the gift stir within me, and I repent it."* Forty-two women in three different towns, all aged between fifty and sixty, all widowed or unwed, all confessed by the same formula. She checked the interrogating officers' names. Different men, different years, different handwriting—and yet the same sentence, repeated like a prayer from a book none of them owned.

Her skin prickled. She had signed off on these records. She had read hundreds of them, approved thousands, and never seen the pattern because she had never been looking for a pattern. She had been looking for confirmation.

That was the crack, she thought later. Not the numbers themselves. The realization that she had spent ten years mistaking agreement for truth.

* * *

Near dawn she did something she had never done. She went down into the archive vaults beneath the Hall, past the sealed shelves of confiscated grimoires, to the oldest cabinet—the one holding the pre-Edict surveys, the ones commissioned by the old king, the father, back when the crown merely wanted to know how many gifted lived in his realm rather than how many could be made ash.

The old survey was thinner than she remembered. She knew why: half its pages had been removed by order of the Crown itself, stamped with the privy seal, the year the young king took the throne and the Edict was drafted. She had always assumed the removed pages held embarrassing indulgences—lists of court magicians the new king wished to spare.

But the surviving pages told their own story. The old king's surveyors had been thorough men, and they had numbered their findings in the margins. Page after page bore the same notation, in different hands:

*Total count withheld by Crown command. See master roll.*

And on the final surviving leaf, in the surveyor-general's own careful script, a note that read like a man leaving a message in a bottle:

*By His Majesty's express instruction, the true number is not to be compiled, copied, nor spoken. I am ordered to report only that it is sufficient.*

*Sufficient.* For what?

Voss stood in the cold vault for a long time, the lantern shaking very slightly in her hand, and admitted to herself, in the privacy where she kept all her honest thoughts, that she did not want to go up those stairs and ask the question aloud.

She went up anyway.

* * *

She requested a private audience through the proper channels, citing an audit discrepancy of grave magnitude. The chamberlain granted it within the hour—which itself was strange. Audiences with the king were rationed like wartime bread. Hers came without a single question asked.

At midnight she climbed the long stair to the royal apartments, escorted by guardsmen who would not meet her eyes. The palace above the third floor had always unsettled her; the corridors there were too quiet, the tapestries too clean, as if the dust itself had been given orders. Tonight the silence had texture. It pressed against her ears like deep water.

The king received her in the map room, standing before the great table where the realm lay rendered in painted wood. He was dressed for sleep, or for an audience—one could never tell which, with him. He was forty-one years old and had ruled for twenty-three, and he did not look a day older than he had at her presentation ceremony nine years ago. She had told herself it was good breeding, northern blood, a portrait-king's luck.

"My lord," she said, and bowed. "Forgive the hour."

"You are always welcome, Inquisitor-General." His voice was mild, pleasant, and precisely pitched—the voice of a man reading words he agreed with completely. "You have found a discrepancy."

"Yes, sire." She laid the ledger on the map table, open to the reconciliation page. "In the purge totals. Against every census we hold. We have burned near three times the number the realm contains. I have checked it nine ways. It does not balance."

The king looked down at the page. He did not touch it. He smiled—the warm, unhurried smile that had charmed ambassadors and frightened her, though she could never have said why.

"Numbers are a kind of pride, Maren," he said. He almost never used her name. It landed strangely, familiar as a hand on her shoulder. "The census counts what people admit to being. The fire counts what they are. Why should they agree?"

"The difference is four thousand lives, sire. At minimum. Someone is feeding us false confessions, or someone is—" She stopped. She heard herself, and hated the word before she spoke it. "Or someone is burning people who were never gifted at all."

The smile remained. Nothing else about him did.

It happened in the space of a heartbeat, and afterward she would turn it over a thousand times trying to make it ordinary. A draft, she told herself first. Then: a trick of the candles. But there were no candles lit near the wall, and no window open, and no draft in a palace that breathed this little.

The king's shadow lay long across the map table, cast by the hearth behind him. And while the king himself stood motionless, hands folded, smiling his mild smile—the shadow moved.

It leaned. Slowly, deliberately, the way a man leans over a ledger, its head tilting to read the page. Its outline blurred and reformed, longer than the man casting it, jointed wrong, and where its face should have fallen across the painted provinces of the north, the little wooden towns seemed to darken, as if seen through deep water.

Then it settled back into stillness, an ordinary shadow again, obedient to the firelight, and the king said:

"The numbers serve the crown. They do not command it." He lifted his gaze from the ledger to her, and for one instant—she would swear this on any relic, before any tribunal—the eyes were not vacant at all. They were *interested*. Hungry the way a well is hungry, deep and patient and bottomless. "Is there anything else?"

Her mouth was dry. Every instinct honed by ten years of interrogation screamed at her: do not let him see what you saw. Do not become a discrepancy yourself.

"No, sire," she heard herself say. "I will reconcile the figures. There may be errors in my transcription."

"I am certain there are," the king said warmly. "You have always been precise, Maren. Precision is a virtue. Do keep it." And as she bowed and gathered her ledger, he added, gently, almost kindly: "The fire must be fed. You understand that, don't you? Whatever else changes. The fire must be fed."

She did not run. She walked, each step placed like a soldier crossing ice, down the long stair, out into the night air of the courtyard, where she stood beneath the cold stars breathing until her hands stopped trembling enough to hold a pen.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in her office with the shutters closed and wrote two reports.

The first was the true one—the audit as she had found it, the impossible totals, the identical confessions, the old surveyor's note about the number that was not to be spoken. She wrote it in full, plainly, damningly. When it was done she read it twice, imagining the king's mild voice saying *the fire must be fed*, imagining the shadow leaning over the page to read her own name in it.

Then she fed the true report into the stove, piece by piece, and watched the edges curl and blacken, and thought: *this is what we do to truth. This has always been what we do. I simply never stood close enough to feel the heat.*

The second report she began at dawn, and it was the hardest thing she had ever written, harder than her husband's eulogy, harder than the order that had sent her first hound-squad into a weaver's tenement nine years ago. It was a masterpiece of the craft she had perfected without knowing it. She reconciled the totals against a revised census methodology—sampling error, undocumented migration, double-counting across parish lines. She introduced discrepancies of her own making, small ones, the kind that would draw an auditor's eye away from the wound and toward the bandage. She reduced the year's purge count by two hundred and fourteen souls—not enough to be noticed, enough to matter. Enough that somewhere, maybe, two hundred people would live through a season that should have killed them.

She signed it with her own name. Let the record show, someday, if there was ever a someday, that Maren Voss had begun lying in the spring of the ninth year, and that every lie was entered neatly, dated, and initialed.

A true believer does not crack all at once, she thought, sealing the wax. A true believer cracks the way a dam does—from some small internal flaw that was there from the beginning, that the water finds and worries and widens, until one morning the whole structure comes down and everyone downstream asks how anyone could have trusted such a fragile thing.

She had believed in the Edict. She still believed in it, in a way—in the danger of the untrained gift, in the child whose magic had burned a barn, in the necessity of order. That belief was not the flaw. The flaw was the other belief, the one she had carried since girlhood: that the crown served the realm, that the fire served the crown, that someone wise and human sat at the top of all this burning and grieved every necessary death.

No one sat at the top. Something wore the top like a coat.

She opened her dispatch case and drew out the next week's raid orders—three targets marked in the Hollow approaches, based on a leak her hounds had traced through the refugee network. Her pen hovered. Two hundred and fourteen saved in the ledger; here was a chance to save a few more in flesh.

She struck the largest target—the deep quarter, the old drowned streets—and wrote in the margin: *Insufficient evidence. Reinvestigate.* It was thin. It might buy days only. But the Hollow refugees would scatter at the reprieve, melt deeper, and her hounds would come back with nothing but wet boots.

Days. Perhaps only days.

She blotted the orders, rang for her clerk, and gave instructions in a voice steady as poured stone. When the young man had gone, she sat alone in the gray light and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to feel the full weight of what she had become: an instrument that had turned in its maker's hand.

One minute. Then she unlocked the bottom drawer of her desk, took out a fresh sheet, and began—at the top, in her precise, unfalsifiable hand—a private list. Not of the guilty. Of the numbers.

Every discrepancy. Every duplicated confession. Every year the gap had grown. Dates, totals, margins of growth, and beside the final column, the rate itself, which she now calculated for the first time and which sat on the page like a heartbeat:

Steady. Increasing. Patient.

*Like a thing being fed on schedule,* she wrote, and then crossed it out, because some sentences, once written, could not be unwritten.

Far above her office, in the map room, she imagined the shadow leaning over the realm, reading. She did not know—could not yet know—that below the city, in the dark, a witch and a loud little girl were already climbing toward the foundations of everything, following the roots of the crown.

She only knew that the numbers had finally told her the truth, and that the truth had a shape, and the shape was hunger.

She blew out her candle and began, quietly, to build her case against the throne.
