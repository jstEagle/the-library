## 13. The Price of the Deep Road

The survivors came down out of the burning tunnels in ones and twos, coughing ash, carrying what they could. Yara counted them the way Bram had taught her—by feet, not faces, because faces you could lose and still be whole. Twenty-two pairs of feet. Then twenty-one. Then nineteen.

She sat on a ledge of wet stone above the last dry chamber of the Hollow, knees pulled to her chest, and watched the dragon-fire gutter in its iron basin across the water. It was smaller than it had been three days ago. Everything was smaller than it had been three days ago.

Sef was dead. The old keeper had gone into the smoke with a fire-hook in her two good hands and bought them eleven minutes with her life, and the fire she'd kept for sixty years had dimmed like a lamp running out of oil, as if some part of it had loved her back.

Yara's hands were shaking again. They did that now. Not from cold—the Hollow never got truly cold, not with the ember breathing somewhere below—but from the leak, the awful bright leak that lived under her skin. Sparks crawled up her forearms when she wasn't watching. The stone under her bare toes was warm. She kept warming things without meaning to. She kept *meaning* things without meaning to.

"You're doing the thing," said a voice behind her.

Bram lowered herself onto the ledge with a grunt, favoring the ribs she'd cracked in the raid. Her gray hair was singed at one temple. She looked, Yara thought, like something the world had tried to burn twice and given up on.

"I'm not doing anything," Yara said.

"You're boiling the puddle."

Yara looked down. The puddle at her feet was steaming gently, little bubbles rising like it had opinions. She closed her fists and made herself small inside, the way Bram taught her—count the stones, name their colors, gray and gray and gray-black and gray—until the heat sank back down her arms and went quiet, sulking, waiting.

"Better," Bram said.

"It won't stay better."

"No," Bram agreed. "It won't. That's why we're moving." She nodded across the black water, toward the far wall where an archway yawned, older than any archway had a right to be, its stones carved with shapes that might have been waves or might have been teeth. "That's the Deep Road. Kessil's territory. Beyond it, the old service ways run all the way to the palace foundations. It's the only way left that Voss's hounds haven't mapped."

Yara stared at the dark arch. The air coming out of it smelled wrong—not rotten, exactly. Old. Like the inside of a sealed room, like a book no one had opened in a hundred years.

"What's Kessil?"

"A river-thing. Older than Vell, probably older than the kings. It doesn't eat flesh and it doesn't want gold." Bram's jaw worked. "It wants memories. One per traveler, paid before passage. It takes them out clean, or so I'm told. You don't feel it happen. You just find a hole where something used to be."

"That's horrible," Yara said.

"It's honest," Bram said. "Which in my experience is worse, but rarer."

* * *

They gathered the others by the fire-basin. Nineteen left: a tanner named Corvel who hadn't spoken since his daughter didn't make it out; the twins, Pell and Pemmy, ten years old and mute since the raid, holding each other's sleeves; old Haskin the lamplighter with his burned hands wrapped in rags; six more whose names Yara knew now because Bram had made her learn them—"names are how we keep people," she'd said—and a fisherwoman called Maren-not-Voss, whom everyone just called Fish now, for reasons that were grimly funny if you let yourself laugh about anything at all.

Bram laid it out plainly, the way she laid everything out, as if plainness could armor you against it.

"The Deep Road gets us under the palace. It costs each of us a memory. Kessil chooses which one—I've heard it goes for whatever sits heaviest. You can refuse, and stay here, and wait for the Inquisition to finish mapping these tunnels. Nobody will think less of you." A pause. "Most people will think less of you. I would. That's the truth."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of stone, the city bells rang the hour, and Yara thought of the market, of luck-knots, of a life that seemed to belong to somebody else.

Corvel stood first. "I'll pay," he said hoarsely. "If it takes her face too, then maybe it's doing me a kindness." No one answered him. There was nothing to answer with.

One by one, they agreed. Haskin. Fish. The twins, who only nodded when Pemmy squeezed Pell's hand. Yara watched them decide, each in their own silence, and felt something heavy settle in her chest, because she already knew she was going to say yes too, and she already knew what Kessil would take from her, because she'd been thinking about it for two days straight, turning it over like a stone with something living underneath.

Kessil took what sat heaviest.

And there was nothing in Yara's life heavier than her mother's face.

She couldn't even remember how long ago she'd lost her mother. Five years? Six? The flue, the rooftops, the kettles that sang for her—all of it blurred. But the face she had. She guarded it like a coal cupped in bare hands. Brown skin, darker than hers. A scar over one eyebrow shaped like a comma. Eyes the color of strong tea, and the particular way they crinkled when she laughed, which was sideways, like she was laughing at a joke she'd told herself. When the nights in the flue got too cold and the loneliness got too big, Yara would close her eyes and hold that face in front of her mind's eye until she fell asleep. It was the last thing of hers. Everything else—the house, the smell of bread, the lullaby with the wrong words—had worn away years ago. Just the face remained. Just the face.

And Kessil took what sat heaviest.

* * *

The crossing point was a shelf of black stone at the water's edge, and the river-thing rose to meet them at moonrise—or what passed for moonrise down here, the strange pale glow that seeped through cracks in the drowned quarter and touched the water silver.

It did not rise so much as *arrive*. One moment the water was water. The next it had a shape, tall and narrow and flowing, a figure made of current and shadow with a face like the surface of a stream—always shifting, always almost resolving into something you recognized and never quite getting there. Its voice, when it spoke, came from everywhere the water was.

*Nineteen,* Kessil said. *Nineteen tolls.*

Its eyes—if those two slow eddies in its face were eyes—moved across the group and stopped on Yara, and stayed there, and Yara felt seen in a way that made her skin crawl, felt weighed, felt *priced*.

Bram stepped forward. "We know the toll. We agree. But the children cross together or not at all, and nobody pays twice."

*No one pays twice,* Kessil agreed, in its voice like water over stones. *Each pays once. Each pays true.* The eddies turned back to Yara. *The loud one first.*

"Why her first?" Bram's hand found Yara's shoulder, and Yara realized she'd already taken a step forward.

*Because she has been deciding since she saw me,* Kessil said, and there was something in its voice that might have been amusement, or pity, or neither. *Deciding is payment begun.*

Bram looked down at her. "Yara." Just her name, low, meant for her alone. "You don't have to—"

"Yes I do," Yara said. "We all have to. And besides—" she lifted her chin, because her chin wanted to tremble and she wouldn't let it— "you said names are how we keep people. Well, memories are too. And mine's the heaviest I've got. So it should buy the most. Right?"

Bram's face did something complicated. "That's not how barter works."

"That's how *my* barter works."

She walked to the water's edge before Bram could stop her, before she could stop herself. Up close, Kessil was taller than she'd thought, and colder, and the shifting face resolved for half a heartbeat into something almost kind.

*What do you carry, little storm?*

"You tell me," Yara whispered. "You already know."

*Tell me anyway. That is the rite. Name the toll and it is mine; keep silent and it stays yours, and the road stays shut.*

Her heart hammered. This was the door, and once she named it there was no un-naming it, no taking back, no finding the face again in some corner of her mind where she'd hidden it. She thought of Bram, who'd lost her god and kept going. She thought of Sef, who'd died so that other people could carry their burdens a little farther down the road. She thought of the twins, who'd pay tonight too, and of Corvel, who wanted to forget.

Survival cost. Sef had known it. Everyone down here had known it. You didn't get to walk out of a fire with your whole self. You chose which pieces to leave behind.

"My mother's face," Yara said. Her voice cracked in the middle but held. "The way she looked when she was happy. All of it. Every part of remembering her face."

The water around the shelf went very still.

*A fair weight,* Kessil said softly. *Heavier than most grown souls bring me.* The current-figure bent, bringing its stream-face level with hers, and the two slow eddies of its eyes regarded her with what she would swear, later, was respect. *Are you certain? Memory cannot be bought back. I do not sell. I only keep.*

Yara thought: *if I say yes, tomorrow morning I will wake up and not know what my own mother looked like.* She thought: *I will look at other mothers in the market someday and feel nothing.* She thought of the tea-colored eyes, the comma scar, the sideways laugh, and she memorized them one last time, deliberately, fiercely, saying goodbye to each piece the way you kiss a sleeping child before leaving in the night.

Certain. She was certain, because the alternative was staying here while the hounds came down, and then the memory would burn with everything else anyway, and it would have bought nothing.

"I'm certain," she said. "Take it."

*Hold still.*

Kessil reached out—not a hand, but a curling ribbon of water, cool as evening—and touched her brow.

There was no pain. That was the terrible thing. There was barely anything at all. A sensation like a page turning. Like a breath let out. The tea-colored eyes. The comma-shaped scar. The sideways laugh—

—and then Yara was standing at the edge of the water, and the river-thing was straightening, and she was trying to remember why her cheeks were wet, why her chest ached like a bruise, why the words *mother's face* echoed in her skull like a bell rung in an empty room.

Something was gone. Something important. She could feel the hole where it had been, smooth-edged, clean, exactly as promised. She groped for it the way you grope for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found nothing, and understood, distantly, that this was the price and she had paid it and she could not even grieve properly because grief needs a shape and hers had been taken.

"Yara." Bram was beside her, gripping her arm, searching her face. "What did it take? What did you give it?"

"I don't—" Yara frowned. "I don't remember."

Bram went white. "Yara. What did you offer?"

"I offered..." The hole again, the smooth place. "Something. Something heavy." Her voice came out small. "I think it was important."

For a moment Bram just looked at her, and Yara saw something move through the older woman's expression—grief, fury, and underneath both, a terrible tenderness. Then Bram pulled her into a hug, hard enough to hurt her own cracked ribs, and Yara let her, though she didn't entirely understand it.

"You brave, stupid, wonderful girl," Bram said into her hair. "When this is over, we're getting it back. Whatever it takes. Do you hear me? Kessil keeps things. Keeping means it can be stolen from. We're stealing it back."

*Stealing from me is not advised,* Kessil observed, without malice.

"Neither was burning the Hollow," Bram said, "and someone did it anyway."

The river-thing made a sound like rain on a roof. It might have been laughter.

*Passage is paid,* it said. *Nineteen tolls, nineteen travelers. Walk the Road and do not touch the water. Do not answer any voice that calls your name—you will hear voices, for the Road remembers everyone who has ever crossed it. And, loud one—*

The eddies fixed on Yara one final time.

*What I keep, I keep well. If ever you stand before me again and ask for it by its true name, I must return it. That is the oldest law, older than your kings, older than your crown. So grow strong. Grow clever. Come back someday and ask me the right question.*

Then it sank, and the black water smoothed over like a page closing, and the archway of the Deep Road stood open before them, breathing its old-book breath into the dark.

* * *

They crossed in single file, Bram first, Yara last except for the twins, whom she kept between herself and the wall. The Road was a causeway of ancient stone running through a flooded cavern vast enough that the light of their one lantern died before it found any ceiling. On either side, the water lay flat as glass and just as reflective, and in it Yara saw reflections that didn't match—people walking beside them who weren't there, faces tilted up toward the surface of the water from below.

Once, a voice called her name out of the dark. Soft. Familiar. A woman's voice, warm, the kind of voice that had sung lullabies.

Yara's heart lurched toward it before her mind caught up—and then hit the hole, the smooth place, and found nothing to hold on to. She didn't know the voice. She couldn't know it. Whoever it belonged to, that door had been sold.

"Keep walking," Bram said quietly, ahead of her, not turning around. She'd heard it too. Everyone had. Each of them hearing, maybe, their own particular ghost.

"Was that—" Yara started.

"Don't answer it," Bram said. "Don't even wonder at it yet. Wondering is how they pull you. Wonder later, when we're under the palace and it doesn't matter anymore."

So Yara wondered silently, walking, one foot and then the other, counting stones like Bram had taught her, keeping the leak caged, keeping the ache caged, holding the smooth-edged hole in her chest up to the light of her own attention again and again like a coin she couldn't read.

She would get it back. The river-thing had said so, and rivers kept their laws—it was the one thing about Kessil that felt solid. *Ask me by its true name.* Which meant she had to learn the name. Learn what she'd lost before she could demand its return. Somewhere between here and the throne of a hollowed king, she would have to find out what her mother's face had looked like—from records, from strangers, from anyone who might remember—and rebuild it piece by piece, knowing she could never trust it, knowing the real one was sitting at the bottom of a river in the dark, kept well, kept waiting.

Ahead, past Bram's lantern, the Road began to slope upward, and the air changed—colder, drier, carrying a faint mineral smell and, beneath it, something else. Something vast and quiet and suffering, like a hymn hummed by a throat full of dust.

Bram stopped. Raised a fist. The column stilled.

"Do you feel that?" the old mercenary whispered. "Everyone. Quiet. Feel it."

Yara felt it. Through the soles of her bare feet, up through the warm cage of her own wild gift, a whisper rising out of the stone itself—a prayer in a language she shouldn't have known but somehow did, thread after thread after thread of prayer, hundreds of them, thousands, woven together into one endless voice.

And threaded through all of it, faint and frayed but unmistakable, a voice she'd never heard and somehow recognized from the way Bram sometimes went silent at the mention of gods:

The Shepherd was down here. Or what was left of him.

"We're close," Bram breathed. "Gods help us. We're under the throne."
