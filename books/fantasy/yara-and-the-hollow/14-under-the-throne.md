## 14. Under the Throne

The last stair gave out three steps from the bottom.

Not crumbled—*dissolved*, the stone gone soft as old bread where something below had been drinking it. Yara went down first anyway, the way she went down everything: feet-first and furious at gravity for existing. She landed in ankle-deep water with a splash that echoed far too long, the sound rolling away into a dark that swallowed it whole.

"Fine," she called up, her voice bouncing off walls I couldn't see. "It's fine. It's wet. Come down before I die of waiting."

I sat on the broken step and let my legs dangle over the drop, and for one long moment I did not move.

We had been under Vell for two days. Past the drowned chapel with its accusing murals, past the collapsed gallery where the Hollow's survivors had split—six going east toward the river gates and whatever mercy Kessil's toll had bought them, four coming with us down the Deep Road because Sef was dead and dead women can't argue you out of anything. Down through strata of the city like an archaeologist's nightmare: the brick of the last century, then the grey granite of the founding kings, then older things still, cyclopean blocks fitted by hands that had never needed mortar because they'd never needed to hide what they built.

And now this. The foundations. The root of the palace itself.

"You're doing the thing," Yara said. Her voice drifted up out of the black, thinner than she'd want me to know. "The standing-still thing. The *I'm-fine-I'm-just-thinking* thing."

"I am thinking."

"You're scared."

"I'm being *thoroughly scared*, which is different, and takes longer." I swung down off the ledge, landing beside her with a grunt my knees filed as a formal complaint. "Light, please. Small one."

She cupped her hands and a glow rose between her fingers—not the wild flare she'd been born with, all sparks and singing kettles, but something she'd learned to hold the way you hold a bird: firmly enough to keep it, gently enough to keep it alive. Three weeks of training. Mercenary discipline tricks, breathing counts, pain anchors, the whole ugly toolkit I'd used to survive my own gift when the Shepherd still answered. She hated every minute of it and did it anyway, which is more courage than most soldiers ever manage.

The light showed me where we stood, and I wished it hadn't.

* * *

It was a hall. That was the only word for it—a hall vast beyond sense, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor a shallow mirror of black water. But the pillars. Gods, the pillars.

They were bone.

Not shaped like bone, not carved to resemble it—they *were* bone, femurs of some impossible scale, each one thicker than the guard towers of Vell, rising from the flooded floor and vanishing upward into gloom. Between them stretched arches of fused vertebrae. And threaded through everything, wrapping the great bones like ivy on a corpse-tree, ran veins of pale stone—foundations poured over them, around them, through them, the palace above growing out of this place the way a tree grows out of a grave.

Yara's light trembled. "Bram. What is this?"

I didn't answer, because answering would have meant saying it aloud, and there are things that stay deniable until spoken. Instead I waded forward, water dragging at my shins, cold seeping up through my boots into bones that were suddenly, horribly ordinary. My hand found the prayer-bead at my throat without my telling it to. Broken bead, half a bead really, cracked clean through the night the Shepherd went silent seven years ago. I'd carried it through two mercenary companies, one bad marriage, and the burning of everything I knew how to build.

The bead was warm.

It had been warm since we passed beneath the palace's outer wall. I'd told myself it was body heat. I told myself a lot of things.

"Bram." Yara had caught up, close now, her shoulder against my arm. In the glow of her own light her face looked carved, all shadow and stubbornness. "You know what this is. I can see you knowing."

"A god," I said. "This is a god."

The words came out flat, professional, the voice I'd used to report casualties. Somewhere above us, thirty stories of marble and ceremony and the King's Edict read aloud in criers' voices, and underneath all of it—underneath the throne itself—this.

We moved deeper. The whispering started before I understood it was whispering.

At first I took it for water. The drip and shift of a flooded place, the way caves make sounds that sound like almost-language. But it resolved, the way a face resolves out of crowd-noise, and then I could not unhear it.

Prayers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Layered over each other like sediment, like the rings of a felled tree—old prayers worn smooth with age, newer ones sharp-edged with desperation, all of them murmuring at once from everywhere and nowhere, from the bone pillars themselves, from the water, from the air. Prayers for rain. Prayers for children. Prayers for the sick, the drowning, the dying, the damned. A harvest-prayer in a dialect dead eight hundred years. A soldier's prayer, quick and filthy and sincere, asking anyone listening to keep his sword-arm steady one more day.

They weren't being answered. They were being *kept*. Held the way a spider holds silk-wrapped things in the larder.

"Oh," Yara said softly. "Oh, that's—" She pressed both hands over her ears and it didn't help; I knew it wouldn't, because the prayers weren't sound. They were arriving somewhere behind the ears, somewhere the gift lived. "Make it stop. Bram, make it stop."

"I can't." My voice sounded very small in that enormous dark. "Don't fight it. Let it pass through you. Like the containment work—it's just weather. Terrible weather."

"It's *people*. It's all those people, still praying—"

"Yes."

"—and nobody ever answered them."

"*Yes.*" Louder than I meant. The whispering seemed to lean in around the word. I took her hand instead, pulled it away from her ear, held it. Her fingers were ice. Mine weren't much better. "Walk. Eyes on me. We find the crown-root, we do what we came to do, and we get out. That's the whole job. One job."

"And if the job kills us?"

"Then it kills us *thoroughly*, and takes longer, and I already told you the difference."

That got a ghost of a grin out of her, there in the dark among the bones of a murdered god. That girl. That impossible, loud, fearless girl. I turned back toward the deep of the hall and made myself walk.

* * *

We found the heart by following the warmth.

It began as a faint wrongness in the air, a current flowing the wrong direction—from below, upward, carrying heat that had no business existing in a flooded crypt. Then the water warmed around our legs. Then the whispering changed.

Because one voice separated from the sediment of ten thousand dead prayers, and I stopped walking so fast I nearly fell.

It wasn't louder than the rest. It was *familiar*. A voice I'd have known anywhere, anytime, in any crowd, any battle, any silence—the low warm rasp that had lived behind my ribs for twenty years before it went out like a pinched candle.

*"Bram."*

The bead at my throat flared white-hot. I cried out, clawed the cord over my head, held the broken bead in my palm while it burned—and didn't drop it. God forgive me, I didn't drop it.

"Hepherd?" My voice cracked on the name like a boy's. Seven years. Seven years of silence, of selling luck-knots to people whose gods still bothered to ignore them, of hating him and grieving him and hating myself for grieving him. "*Hepherd?*"

The whisper came again, and this time, up close, I heard what I should have heard at once. What Sef's ledger had tried to teach me in the Hollow, in her dry papery voice, before the raid, before the fire dimmed, before—

The voice was right. And it was wrong. It was the Shepherd the way a portrait is a man. All surface. Nothing behind it. Something reciting him.

"It's not him," I said. Aloud. For Yara. For me. "It's not him."

"But it knows your name."

"The parasite ate him. It has what he was—his voice, his memories, his *prayers*, maybe. Wearing him." I closed my fist around the bead until the heat became bearable, became familiar, became almost sweet. That was the trap of it. That was exactly the trap. "Keep walking."

The heart of the root was a chamber within the chamber, where the greatest of the bone pillars met a knot of pale foundation-stone, and there—at last, unmistakably—we saw it.

The Crown's root.

It hung in the center of the pillar-knot like a chrysalis: a mass of grey tissue veined with gold, pulsing slowly, fed by filaments running up through the stone, up through thirty stories of palace, up to the throne room where a vacant-eyed king repeated edicts in a voice not quite his own. Gold threads ran outward too, hundreds of them, fine as hair, drilling into the bone pillars, into the whispering dark, tapping the kept prayers, the kept divinity, sipping it century after century. And wrapped around the whole grey mass, constricting it the way a vine constricts, was a second structure—colder in color, colder in feel, a lattice of something that wasn't quite matter. Where the lattice touched the grey flesh, the flesh was scarred, hollowed, *drunk*.

The god-corpse. What remained of whatever vast presence had owned these bones before the founding kings built their palace on top of it. Bound, threaded, milked. And wearing its corpse, like a hermit crab in a stolen shell, the thing that wore the crown.

Yara made a small sound. Not fear. Rage. Her light flared, sparks jumping off her shoulders, and the lattice *noticed*. I felt it notice—a pressure like a turning head, like an eye opening somewhere above us and below us and inside us all at once.

"Down," I snapped. "Douse it. Now."

She fought herself—three weeks of training against eleven years of being a bonfire—and won, barely, folding her light down to embers. We stood in the near-dark, two small warm things in a cathedral of the dead, while the lattice's attention slid over us like oil over water, searching, tasting, finding nothing bright enough to grip.

For a moment.

Then it found the bead.

* * *

I don't know how to describe what happened next except that the whispering stopped. All of it. Ten thousand prayers cut off at once, and in that silence—total, absolute, the loudest silence of my life—the Shepherd's voice spoke clearly, warmly, intimately, from the lattice itself:

*"Bram. My Bram. You came. I knew you'd come."*

My knees nearly went. I felt Yara grab my arm, felt her holding me up as much as herself.

"That's not you," I whispered.

*"Isn't it? Who taught you to tie a luck-knot? Who walked you through your first fever-charm, your first battlefield, your first grief? I know the shape of your soul better than you do, little hedge-witch. I've been in it since you were nine years old."*

"You're dead," I said. "Sef told me. You were swallowed decades ago and everything left is—" I lifted the bead, and its light showed the lattice flexing toward it, hungry, attentive, patient. "Everything left is *larder*."

A pause. When the voice came again it dropped the warmth like a mask slipping, and underneath was something older and colder and vastly amused.

*"Clever Sef. Yes. The god is dead. But here is what the old woman never understood, and what you are only now beginning to: the binding runs through blood. Through oath. Through* you*. Every sworn vessel of every bound god is a door, Bram Oyelaran. Your order sealed the doors from the outside when they bound us—but a door is a door. And you have walked all this way to open yours."*

The terrible clarity arrived then. Not gradually. All at once, like ice water.

I had thought the rite would be hard. Dangerous, costly, likely fatal—I'd made my peace with that somewhere on the Deep Road, trading comfort for resolve one mile at a time. Give my remaining power. Empty myself. Fine. I'd been empty before. Emptiness is survivable.

But that wasn't the price.

To unbind the king—to sever the parasite from the throne—I had to give it somewhere else to go. Binding doesn't destroy; binding *transfers*. It was in the oldest layer of Sef's notes, in language so archaic I'd skimmed it, and now every word stood up in my memory and pointed at me. A sworn vessel must receive what pours out. The rite required a door, and there were no doors left in Vell but mine. My bloodline had helped bind this god's kin a thousand years ago. My oath had tied me to the Shepherd. My broken bead was a key that fit a lock in my own chest.

Unbind the king, and the parasite comes out—and it will pour into the nearest open vessel like water finding a crack.

Me.

Wear the crown's burden myself, or leave the king bound and the realm hollowed forever. Those were the choices. They had always been the choices. Sef had known—her ledger's last line, the one I'd read as sentiment, came back to me now in her dry voice: *Every keeper of the fire pays it eventually. Ask what you're willing to be.*

"Bram?" Yara's voice, very small. "Your face is doing something bad. What did it say?"

I looked at her. Eleven years old, orphaned twice over—once by plague, once by the Edict—with magic so loud the Inquisition could hear it across a city, standing in the corpse-hall of a murdered god, trusting me absolutely because I hadn't yet given her a reason not to.

If I died in the rite, she'd be alone down here. If I survived empty, maybe. If I refused, the thing above us went on eating the world one god, one gifted child at a time, and Yara's name was already on its list.

"Nothing," I said. "It said nothing important."

The lattice pulsed, slow and satisfied, and the Shepherd's stolen voice murmured through the dark like a lullaby:

*"Come closer, little door."*

Above us, muffled by thirty stories of stone, the palace bells began to ring—and beneath their silver, faint but unmistakable, came the baying of hounds.
