## 15. Doss's Choice

The order came down at dusk, written in the Inquisition's new hand—clean, angular letters that had replaced the old cursive of the King's Chancery sometime in the last year, though no one could say when the change had been made official.

*By decree of His Majesty and the office of the Inquisitor-General: all accesses to the undercity are to be sealed with fire and stone. No gate, grate, culvert, or stair to remain passable. The drowned quarter is declared a nest of sedition. What lives below is to be left below.*

Doss read it twice by lantern light in the garrison at Coppersmith Gate, and his first thought—not his last, but his first—was that he'd never seen a royal decree use the word *what* for people before. Not *who*. *What*.

He folded the order into his breast pocket and went to find his squad.

* * *

There were eleven gates into the undercity that anyone still remembered, and Doss had spent thirty years learning most of them the hard way. He'd been a younger man then, a spearman in the Vellish irregulars, back when going underground wasn't heresy—it was just soldiering, dirty and cold, chasing smugglers through culverts that predated the city above them by a thousand years. The old city under Vell. The Hollow, some called it now. Once it had simply been *down*, and down was where you went when the fighting got close and you needed a wall at your back.

He knew the smell of those tunnels still. Wet stone and old iron and something sweeter underneath, like rotting honey—the breath of whatever fires burned down there that maps said shouldn't exist.

His sergeant-major, a broad woman named Kettleby with burn scars lacing her forearms like vine work, met him at the muster point with the list of gates in her fist.

"We start at Ropewalk Stair," she said. "Masons are already loading stone on the carts. We seal, we torch what won't seal, we move on. Inquisitor-General wants it done in three days."

"Three days." Doss looked out over the darkening rooftops. Somewhere beyond them, the river ran black toward the sea, and beneath the river ran everything else. "That's fast."

"That's orders." Kettleby spat into the gutter. "You've been strange since the street raids, Doss. Since the Oyelaran house."

He kept his face still. Thirty years of keeping your face still while officers talked—that was a skill too, and he'd earned it same as his rank.

"The Oyelaran house was empty," he said. "Nothing strange about an empty house."

"Except you were seen standing in it for a quarter hour after we cleared it. Just standing. Looking at the hearth."

"Checking for flues. Fire risk. The whole row's timber."

Kettleby looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable, then shrugged and turned to bellow at the masons. Doss let his breath go slowly through his nose. Above them, on the bell tower of Saint Ferren's, the criers were calling the evening edict again, their voices thin as wire in the cold air:

*Blessed is the cleansed city! Blessed is the faithful! Surrender the gifted, and be blessed!*

Thirty years ago, Doss would have crossed himself at that. Now the words just sat in him like stones dropped down a well, falling and falling without ever hitting water.

* * *

Ropewalk Stair went first.

It was a hole in the earth behind a ropemaker's shed, a spiral of worn steps descending past strata of older and older brickwork until it opened into a vaulted gallery where the air moved like something breathing. Doss stood at the top while the masons broke up the paving and the torch-men poured pitch, and he made himself watch. Made himself listen to the echo change as the stones went in—hollow, then duller, then dead.

A sound came up from below while they worked. Faint. He couldn't have said if it was voices or water or just wind finding its way through stone, but it rose once, briefly, and then the masons set another course of rock and it didn't rise again.

One of the younger men—a boy called Petch, sixteen if he was that, all elbows and Inquisition zeal—grinned at Doss across the growing wall. "Hear that, Sergeant? Like rats."

"Aye," Doss said. "Like rats."

He thought about Bram Oyelaran's face the night she'd pulled an arrow out of his shoulder with a field charm and a mouth full of curses, telling him the god she served didn't care about pain so long as you kept walking. He thought about the prayer-bead he'd seen hanging at her throat, cracked down the middle, and how she'd touched it the way other soldiers touched their mothers' rings.

Not rats. Never rats. But he said nothing, and the wall went up, and the pitch went down, and Ropewalk Stair became a scar in the ground that by morning already looked like it had always been there.

Two more gates died that day. Then three more. By the second evening Doss had stopped counting, because counting was the kind of thing that led to thinking, and thinking was the kind of thing that led to the question he could not afford:

*What if she's down there?*

* * *

She was down there. He knew it the way you know weather coming—some animal part of him reading signs his mind refused to name.

It started small. A fisherman on the river stairs mentioned, unprompted, that he'd seen eels moving wrong lately, all heading upstream against the current, as if something below had frightened them or fed them. A washerwoman complained that the drainage grates behind the tanneries had gone quiet—no rats anymore, not one, as if the whole rat population of that district had decided, collectively, to move. And then there was the matter of the bread.

Vell's bakeries had been short of flour for a month, requisitioned for the Inquisition's garrisons, and yet a beggar child had told Petch—bragging, trying to buy silence with gossip—that down where the river-people lived, nobody was hungry. That there was bread down there. Warm bread.

Petch reported it. Doss signed the report. But he read it four times, and each time he read it he saw a woman with grey in her braid and a hedge-witch's patched coat, doing exactly what she'd always done: finding the hungriest people in a city and feeding them something they weren't supposed to have.

On the third morning, with only two gates left unsealed—the Deep Culvert and the old Chapel Drain—Doss walked the line alone before the crews arrived, and he found the sign he'd been dreading.

Chapel Drain opened behind the ruined chapel of Saint Adela, through a broken arch half-swallowed by ivy. It was supposed to be impassable; the flood of '61 had collapsed its inner passage. But the mud on the threshold stones had been disturbed. Boot prints, small ones—a child's, maybe two children's—and larger prints over them, a long stride, a walker's even pace. And pressed into the mud beside the largest print, half-filled with seep water but unmistakable, a mark his hands remembered better than his eyes: the heel-stamp of mercenary issue boots, the kind with the iron crescent, discontinued twenty years ago. The kind Bram had worn through three campaigns.

Doss crouched there a long time, letting the cold come up through his knees.

He could walk away. Report the prints as ambiguous. Let the masons seal the drain and let whatever fate waited below arrive on its own schedule, without his name on it. That was the sane choice. That was the choice of a man with a pension, a room above a chandlery, and a reputation clean enough that even Kettleby's suspicions were only suspicions.

Or.

He looked down the dark throat of the drain, where the water whispered over stones, and he thought about the order folded in his pocket. *What lives below is to be left below.* He thought about the word *what*, and about a king on a throne who never blinked quite right, and about the numbers—because Doss was a simple man but not a stupid one, and even simple men could count. Four hundred souls taken in the purges by the Inquisition's own records. Nine hundred by any honest census of the gifted districts. And every pyre ash accounted for, and every record signed, and none of it balancing, none of it ever balancing, and the king repeating his edicts in that voice like a man reciting words he'd found written somewhere.

Bram had told him once, drunk on cheap plum brandy after the Marsh War, why she kept serving a silent god. *"Because silence isn't absence, Doss. Sometimes silence means someone's holding a hand over your mouth. You learn the difference when you've fought beside enough quiet men."*

He'd laughed then. He wasn't laughing now.

* * *

The crews arrived at noon. Kettleby brought the masons, the pitch-carts, and a new face—an Inquisitor's clerk in dove-grey robes, sent to witness the final sealing personally, which meant someone above was nervous about these last two gates.

"Chapel Drain first," the clerk said. Her voice was dry as ledger paper. "Inquisitor-General Voss believes the fugitives may use it. There has been... movement detected in the lower galleries."

"What movement?" Doss asked.

"Fire," the clerk said, and did not elaborate.

They worked through the afternoon. Doss directed it—his oath, his rank, his duty, all the words lining up like soldiers on parade—and he watched the wall grow across the broken arch stone by stone, and inside himself something grew alongside it, colder and heavier, until by dusk he could barely breathe around it.

The clerk departed at sundown to report. Kettleby took the men to eat. And Doss stood alone before the nearly finished wall—one gap left at the base, knee-high and arm-wide, waiting for its last stones in the morning—and he made his choice the way he'd made every choice that had mattered in his life: quickly, quietly, and without looking back.

He went to his quarters. He took off his boots. He put on the old pair from the bottom of his footlocker, the mercenary pair with the iron crescent heels that he'd never had the heart to throw away, and he wrapped his sergeant's cloak around the plainest clothes he owned, and he filled a satchel with what a person running needs: bread, dried fish, a flint, a coil of rope, the little tin of rendered tallow he'd kept for thirty years because Bram had once shown him how to make a light that smoke couldn't betray.

Then he walked back through the sleeping garrison, past the sentry who saluted him, past the pitch-carts, to the gap in the wall.

The last stones were stacked neatly beside it, ready for morning. He picked up the first one, weighed it in his hand—and set it down again, deliberately, in the mud where no mason would think it had been placed for use. Then the second. Then he dragged a rotted plank across the opening and propped a broken barrel-head against it, a screen that would fool a passing glance and nothing more.

It was a poor hiding. It would not survive daylight. It was not meant to. It was meant to last one night, and one night was all he could give, and he gave it knowing the cost to the copper.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd finally done, down where all his falling stones had landed: an oath was a promise made to something. That was the whole of it. An oath to a crown was an oath to whoever wore the crown—and whatever wore this crown, Doss had watched it long enough to know it was no king of his. His oath had been sworn to Vell. To the city, and the people in it, and the small stupid stubborn decency of people feeding each other bread in the dark. If the thing on the throne had stolen the city's name to wear as a mask, then breaking this order wasn't betrayal.

It was the only way to keep the oath intact.

He knelt at the gap, pulled the plank aside, and looked down into the dark. Cold air breathed up at him, wet stone and old iron and that faint sweet rot, and far below—so far he might have imagined it—something glimmered. Not torchlight. Warmer than torchlight. The color of a forge at rest, of coals banked for the night, of a fire someone had tended carefully for a very long time.

"Hold on, Bram," he said quietly, to no one, to everyone. "Coming down."

He swung his legs into the gap and began to lower himself.

* * *

The voice behind him was young and eager and absolutely certain, and it stopped his heart mid-beat.

"Sergeant Doss!"

Petch stood ten paces away with a lantern raised, come back for a forgotten tool-roll, and the boy's face went through wonder and confusion and then—worst of all—understanding, each expression arriving like a door slamming shut. His hand dropped toward the whistle at his collar. The garrison alarm-whistle. One blast and the camp woke. Two blasts and the hunt began.

"Petch—" Doss started, still half-in the wall, absurd, caught, a man frozen between worlds with his trousers muddy and his satchel swinging.

"You're helping them." The boy's voice cracked on the word *them*. "You're—you're one of the corrupted. The Inquisitor-General warned us. She said the corruption reaches high. She *said* it—"

"She lied," Doss said, and heard his own voice go low and level, the voice he'd used to talk boys through their first battles. "Listen to me. The numbers don't balance, son. Count them yourself. The pyres don't match the censuses. The king doesn't blink right. Whatever's up on that throne isn't—"

The whistle went up.

One blast, shrill and terrified and true, splitting the night like a knife through sailcloth, and Doss moved without deciding to move—thirty years of instinct taking over where choice ended. He dropped. The dark swallowed him whole as boots thundered toward the chapel ruin behind him, as lanterns bloomed, as Kettleby's voice rang out sharp with disbelief:

*"Doss? Doss is down there? Seal it—seal it NOW—"*

Stone scraped. Voices shouted. Somewhere above, the gap he'd opened began to die, rock grinding against rock, and Doss pressed himself flat against the drain wall in the rushing dark with the alarm still ringing overhead and his heart hammering like a drummer boy's.

Let them seal it. Let them think it closed. He knew this tunnel—or he had, thirty years ago, and tunnels remember longer than men do. There was a side-passage fifty yards in, past the collapse of '61, a crawl behind the fallen vault that the flood had opened instead of closing, and after that the old processional way, and after that—

After that, the fire. The warm color in the deep. The people who were not *what* but *who*, and among them, if the gods he no longer trusted had any mercy at all, a hedge-witch with a broken prayer-bead and a loud little girl who needed every sword-arm she could get.

The grinding above faded as the masons worked farther along the wall. The last light died to a coin, then a thread, then nothing.

Doss stood in the absolute dark of the undercity of Vell, a traitor to his order and the only honest man he knew, and he began to walk.

Behind him, faintly, muffled by fresh-laid stone, he heard Kettleby shouting for torches.

Ahead of him, deeper than any shout could reach, something ancient and patient flickered in the dark like a held breath—and far away, in the drowned heart of the Hollow, the dragon-fire stirred in its bed, as if it had felt the weight of one more soul step onto the long road down.
