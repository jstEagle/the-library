## 16. The Unbinding Rite

The drowned chapel had a roof of stone ribs and a floor of black water, and in the seven days since the raid it had become everything they had left.

Bram knelt at the water's edge with Sef's notes spread across her knees, pages soft as old skin, ink gone the brown of dried blood. She had read them eleven times. On the eleventh reading she had stopped hoping she'd misread them, which was its own kind of grief. The script was cramped, practical, the hand of a woman who had spent eighty years writing by gutter-light with half her eyes: *the crown is a root; the root is a mouth; to pull the root you must give the mouth something to bite.*

A sworn vessel. That was the phrase that kept circling back to her like a hound that had caught a scent it liked. Not a vessel. A *sworn* vessel — someone bound by oath and old blood to hold what poured out of the king when the parasite was cut loose. And there was exactly one bloodline in Vell still carrying the old swearing, one family whose grandmothers had signed their names into the god-service generations ago, one woman crouched now in a drowned chapel with a broken prayer-bead on a cord around her neck.

She had known since the murals. She had simply been very good, for seven days, at not thinking about it.

"Bram." Yara's voice came from up among the stone ribs, where the girl perched like a starling in the broken archway, watching the water. "It's doing it again."

Bram looked up. In the deepest part of the flooded nave, where the chapel's altar had sunk below the surface, the ember from the Hollow sat in an iron brazier Sef's survivors had carried down the Deep Road at terrible cost. It should have died days ago. Instead it breathed. Every few minutes the water above it would glow faintly gold, light rising through the dark in slow coils, like breath seen through a window on a cold morning. The last dragon-fire in Vell, keeping itself alive out of sheer stubbornness, and Bram had never loved an object more.

"It's supposed to do that," Bram said. "Sef's notes say the fire remembers what it burned for."

"What did it burn for?"

"Us, I think. Once upon a time."

Yara slid down from the archway, landing in a spray of shallow water, and came to sit beside her — too close, always too close, knees against Bram's knee, shoulder against her shoulder, the way children press warmth against cold things without knowing they're doing it. Eleven years old and already she had learned that proximity was a kind of argument.

"You've read those papers a hundred times," Yara said.

"Eleven."

"You keep making the same face. Like when Doss's stew was bad but you didn't want to hurt him."

Bram almost smiled. Almost. "And what face is that?"

"The one where your mouth says fine but your eyes say run."

*Seven years a hedge-witch*, Bram thought, *and undone by an orphan's eye for faces.* She folded Sef's notes closed, deliberately, the way you close a door on a room you don't want anyone looking into.

"Eat," she said, pushing the day's ration toward her — half a loaf gone dense with tunnel-damp, some smoked fish Kessil's toll hadn't taken. "Then sleep. Tomorrow we go under the palace, and I need you sharp."

"I'm not stupid, Bram."

"I know you're not stupid."

"Then stop talking to me like I am." Yara didn't raise her voice. That was the worst of it. Since the Hollow had burned, since Sef had died holding the tunnel with her bare hands and her whole ancient heart, the girl had stopped raising her voice about anything that mattered. Everything important now came out quiet, level, terribly adult. "You're going to do it yourself. Whatever's in those papers. You're going to wait till I'm asleep and go do it alone, and you're going to tell me after, if there is an after."

The water glowed gold and dimmed. Somewhere far off, through fathoms of stone, the city went on ringing its bells, arresting its neighbors, burning its lists.

Bram said nothing, which was answer enough.

* * *

She waited until Yara's breathing went long and even, curled against Doss's bedroll near the fire-brazier with the other four survivors — a tanner named Osk, two sisters who'd been Hollow-born and had never seen the sun until this week, and a boy of fifteen called Fen who hadn't spoken since the raid except once, at night, saying Sef's name like a question no one could answer.

Then Bram took the notes and the prayer-bead and waded out onto the sunken steps above the altar, where the ember's light reached thin fingers through the water, and she made herself read the rite one final time with clear eyes.

It was beautifully simple, the way lethal things are simple. Cut the root. Open the vessel. Speak the unbinding while holding both the king's name and her own. The parasite, torn loose, would need somewhere to pour — a sworn vessel, opened and willing, could hold it briefly the way a cup holds floodwater, long enough for the dragon-fire to consume it. Briefly. Sef's marginalia were honest about *briefly*: *a strong vessel might last the length of three prayers. Most last less.*

There was an alternative buried on the last page, written smaller, in ink that looked like it had been applied reluctantly: a vessel need not be willing. It needed only to be present, and sworn. A child of the line would serve. Any child.

Bram sat with that page for a long time, listening to the ember breathe, feeling the weight of the girl asleep twenty feet behind her.

That was the design, wasn't it? That was what the murals in the deeper chapel had shown — her own face painted eight hundred years dead among the Shepherd's sworn, the binding running through her blood like a river through country. The parasite had eaten her god because her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother had helped chain divinity into vessels, and the chain had learned, over centuries, to eat its keepers. She was not standing outside the design. She was the last living hinge of it.

So let the hinge take the strain. Let the flood pour into her, three prayers' worth of time or less, and let Yara wake to a world with a human king and no Bram in it, and grow up fierce and loud and free and — eventually — all right. Children were resilient. Everyone said so. Everyone who'd never been one.

Bram took out the broken prayer-bead. Half a bead, drilled through, worn smooth by seven years of her thumb. The Shepherd had never spoken through it again after the silence came, and she had carried it anyway the way you carry a scar: not because it does anything, but because removing it would mean admitting what happened.

"You'd have told me not to," she said to it. Her voice came out rough. "You'd have said there's no sacrifice I can make that buys anything, that love isn't a ledger. You were always insufferable that way."

The bead did not answer. Gods dead things stay dead.

But the ember, across the black water, pulsed once — gold climbing through the dark like a held breath — and Bram chose to believe it was only fire being fire, and not judgment, because she was a coward about exactly one thing, and it was this.

She began to prepare.

* * *

She got as far as drawing the first circle before Yara's voice said, "I can see your hands glowing."

Bram froze, chalk hovering. The light around her fingertips was faint — her last stored scrap of gift, hoarded these seven years like coins in a mattress, now spent freely to prime the vessel-work. She had thought herself quiet. She had thought the chapel dark enough.

Yara stood at the edge of the steps, hair wild from sleep, eyes reflecting the ember's gold. She didn't look frightened. That was the thing nobody warned you about children who survived purges: they skipped the fear and went straight to the arithmetic underneath it.

"How long have you been awake?" Bram asked.

"Since you stood up. You walk loud when you're trying to be quiet. Guilty people walk loud." Yara came down the steps into the ankle-deep water, unhurried, and stopped just outside the chalked circle. She looked at it for a long moment — the vessel-marks, the opening sigils, the small neat script of a dead woman's warnings — and then she looked up, and Bram saw the arithmetic finish.

"No," Bram said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"No, Yara. Whatever it is. No."

"It's me." Quiet. Level. "The notes say any child of the line. I heard you reading that page three nights ago when you thought I was asleep. I hear everything, Bram, you know I do, my magic hears first and tells me after." She lifted her chin. "I'm a child. I'm closer to the line than you are — my mother was a Winterbourne cousin, nobody ever talks about it, but Sef told me before she—" Her voice caught, one crack, and she mastered it the way she'd mastered everything since the raid. "I'm the better vessel. Younger. Louder. If someone has to hold it for three prayers, it should be the cup that doesn't break."

"And if it does break?"

"Then it breaks fast, and I won't feel most of it."

The sentence landed in Bram's chest like a knife going in flat. She had heard mercenaries talk about wounds exactly that way, veterans with forty years of killing, and here it was in an eleven-year-old's mouth, learned in nine months of being hunted by her own government.

"Come here," Bram said.

Yara came. Of course she came — that was the whole problem with Yara, she came straight at things, floods and Inquisitors and grief, no flinch, no weave. Bram took her by the shoulders and felt the magic humming in the girl like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing. Loud little fire. The loudest gift in Vell, uncageable, unteachable past a certain point, and Bram had spent weeks teaching her discipline tricks anyway because control was survival — but standing here now, hands on those narrow furious shoulders, Bram understood suddenly that she'd had it backwards.

They hadn't been teaching Yara to cage the storm. They'd been teaching the storm to stand in front of a grown woman and offer itself up like a ration.

"Listen to me," Bram said. "What pours out of that thing is decades of devoured gods. It ate my Shepherd. It has eaten others older than my Shepherd. It is not floodwater, it is hunger wearing floodwater's shape, and it will pour into whoever opens for it and it will *speak*. It knows every voice you've ever loved. It offered me mine tonight." She turned the broken bead in her fingers so Yara could see it. "It said: kneel, and have him back, whole, seven years returned. And do you know why I'm the one who goes into that circle tomorrow? Because I've already lost everything it can offer me. It has nothing left to buy me with. But you — you have a whole life it could lie to you about. Your mother's face. A family. A future. You are exactly what it wants, and I will die before I hand you to it, and yes, I said *die*, and no, you don't get to argue, because I'm the grown-up and this is the one thing I get to be selfish about."

Yara stared at her. Water dripped from the stone ribs. The ember breathed.

Then, horribly, the girl smiled — small, wet, devastating.

"You just proved my point," Yara said. "You said it offers people what they love. You love me. So it'll offer you me, Bram. Alive, safe, forever. And you'll have to say no to *me* while you're drowning." Her chin trembled and she locked it steady. "Whereas I've already lost my mother's face. Kessil took it. It can't buy me either. We're the same, and I'm stronger, and you know it, and the only reason you won't say it is because you love me, which is the worst reason in the world to waste the person who can actually do this."

Bram's throat closed. Seven years of silence, seven years of hiding, seven years of charms too small to register — and this child had walked into her life and dismantled every wall she owned in a single sentence, using nothing but the truth.

"That's not fair," Bram managed.

"No," Yara agreed. "None of it is. Sef dying wasn't fair. My mum wasn't fair. The Edict isn't fair. So we don't get to fight fair either. We fight *together*." She stepped forward, into the circle's edge, water lapping, and held out her hand — palm up, the way you offer trust, the way you offer a spark. "Both of us. Both vessels. Your line chained the gods; my fire can burn what pours out. You open the door, I stand in it. Three prayers, right? Then we split it between us and neither of us holds enough to break."

"It's never been done—"

"Neither was surviving the Hollow. Neither was crossing Kessil. Neither was *you*, seven years ago, deciding to keep going." Yara's hand stayed open, steady, gold light beginning to gather in her palm without her permission — the loud little fire answering the moment, eager, enormous. "You taught me that power kept is power corrupted. Well, you've been keeping this plan like a secret for a week and look how that's going. Share it. Share *you*. Or I follow you under the palace tomorrow and open myself in front of that thing whether you've circled me for it or not, and then we'll both die stupid instead of one of us maybe living."

For a long moment Bram couldn't move. The ember's light moved across the black water, patient, ancient, remembering what it burned for.

Then she laughed — a cracked, wrecked sound with tears in it, the first laugh since the raid — and stepped forward, and took the girl's hand.

"Three prayers," Bram said. "And if it offers either of us anything — anything at all — we squeeze each other's hand twice. That's the signal. Twice means *it's lying.*"

"Twice means it's lying," Yara repeated, solemn as an oath.

And the ember flared — high and bright and sudden, gold roaring up through the water, and beneath their feet, deep beneath the drowned chapel, through fathoms of stone and the roots of the palace above, something vast and cold shifted in its sleep, and turned its attention downward, and *noticed* them.

Bram felt it like frost on the back of her neck.

"It knows we're coming," she whispered.

"Good," said Yara, and did not let go of her hand.
