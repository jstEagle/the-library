## 17. What Wears the Crown

* * *

Bram

The chapel beneath the throne room had no roof that any living architect had built. Its vaulted dark went up and up until it became the palace's foundations, and threaded through those foundations—rooted in them, grown through them like ivy through a skull—was the corpse of a god.

Bram knelt at its base with the dragon-fire ember cupped in an iron dish before her, Sef's stolen notes weighted flat with stones, and she did not pray. Prayer was for gods who answered. This was work, and she had always been better at work.

The rite required three things: fire that remembered being wild, words that remembered being sworn, and a vessel with a claim on the bound. She had the first two. The third was her own bloodline—the Shepherd's old sworn company, seven generations thinned down to one hedge-witch with a broken prayer-bead and nothing left to lose.

"Say it again," Yara whispered beside her. The girl's hands were shaking, and Bram could feel the heat coming off her like a banked oven. "The words. I want to know them too, in case."

"In case of what?"

"In case you're wrong about what it costs."

Bram almost smiled. Almost. Instead she took Yara's hand and pressed the broken bead into it. "Hold this. Whatever happens, whatever I say or do or become—hold this, and don't let go until it goes cold. Can you do that?"

Yara looked at the bead, then at her, and something old moved behind the girl's young eyes. "You're planning to lie to me," she said. "Later. You're going to tell me it didn't cost much, and you'll be lying."

"Yes."

"Okay." Yara closed her fist around the bead. "I'll hold it anyway."

Bram turned back to the corpse.

Up close, in the ember's light, the god-corpse was not a body so much as a wound given architecture. Vast pale roots of something that had once been will ran through the stone, and where the parasite had fed, the roots were hollow—drained to translucence, like horn. But deep in the structure, faint as a pulse under frost, whispers still moved. Prayers. Thousands of years of prayers, swallowed and kept, because even a devourer does not waste.

And among them—one voice. Low. Warm. Familiar as her own heartbeat had once been.

*Shepherd,* she thought, and hated herself for thinking it.

She began the unbinding.

Sef's script was older than the Edict, older than the Inquisition, older than the crown itself, and it did not ask permission. It named the binding for what it was—*theft*—and demanded restitution. Bram spoke the first line and the ember flared green-white; spoke the second and the whispers in the walls stopped, all at once, like a congregation holding its breath; spoke the third and the thing above them woke.

It came down through the foundations not as sound but as pressure—a vast attention turning, slow and cold, toward the small flame burning in its root. Bram felt it the way a mouse feels the owl's shadow: whole-body, ancient, absolute.

Then it spoke. Not through the walls. Through her.

*Little sworn one.* The voice was the king's voice, and it was also every voice the king had ever repeated, layered and patient and terribly kind. *You have come so far to give me a gift. Let me give one back.*

The chapel filled with light that wasn't light. And in it—

The Shepherd.

Whole. Alive. Standing in the ember-glow the way he had stood in her mother's stories: tall, gray-cloaked, crook in hand, his face kind and tired and *present*, looking at her with seven years of warmth held in reserve. She could feel him the way she hadn't since she was twenty-two years old—feel the gift humming in her blood, the luck-knots true, the fever-charms hot, the prayer-bead whole in her memory.

*Seven years,* the parasite said through his borrowed mouth. *You mourned me. You starved yourself of craft and called it faithlessness. Kneel, and have it all again. Kneel, and I will never leave you silent. Every charm you ever sold too cheap—I will make it sing.*

Bram's knees bent.

She felt them bend. Felt the sweet, obscene relief of it, like setting down a weight carried so long the shoulders had forgotten their shape. Seven years of silence, ended. Seven years of selling lies to desperate people, ended. One word. One knee on cold stone. A god restored, a witch made whole, and all it would cost was everything Yara was, everything the Hollow's dead had bought with their lives, everything—

The bead in Yara's fist flared red-hot, and the girl cried out but did not let go, and the sound of her pain came to Bram across the false holy light like a stone through a window.

*No,* Bram said—to the parasite, to the vision, to herself. *That's not him. He'd never ask me to kneel. That's how I know.*

She straightened her legs.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the sewers, harder than Sef's funeral pyre, harder than watching Doss's gate close between them. She stood inside the offered miracle and refused it, and the refusal tore something loose in her chest that she knew would never grow back, and she opened her mouth and spoke the fourth line of Sef's rite:

*"What was bound is loosed. What was taken is returned. What wears the crown—"*

The light shrieked.

*"—wears it alone."*

* * *

Yara

The world cracked.

Not the ceiling—though stones began to fall from that too—but underneath everything, a crack like the sky splitting along a seam nobody was supposed to see. Yara felt it in her teeth, in the bead searing her palm, in the wild heat that lived under her skin and had never once obeyed her.

Above them, through the broken vault, the throne room blazed into view: the king on his throne, rigid, his mouth open in a soundless scream, and pouring out of him—out of his shadow, out of his eyes, out of the crown itself—came the thing. It had no shape Yara's eyes could hold. It was hunger with a history. It was cold moving with purpose. And it was coming down, off the throne, through the floor, toward the woman kneeling-no-longer at the ember's side.

Toward Bram.

Because Bram had refused it, and refused things need somewhere to go, and a sworn vessel with a claim on the bound—that was a door, Sef's notes said, that was exactly what a door was—and Yara understood, all at once and completely, what Bram had been lying about.

"No," Yara said.

The thing poured toward the door.

And Yara—who had spent eleven years leaking, scorching, singing kettles and lighting candles and getting hounds set on her, who had been caged and drilled and taught to hold it in, hold it down, keep it quiet—did the opposite of everything she had learned.

She let go.

Every wall Bram had taught her to build, every trick of breath and counting and clenched fists, every cage—she opened them all at once, and the storm that had lived frightened and cramped inside her small body came out the way storms are meant to come out.

It was not gentle. It was not controlled. It was loud in the way her magic had always been loud, magnified past anything, a furnace-wind of raw unshaped gift flooding the chapel, and when the parasite hit the edge of it, the thing recoiled—because the parasite ate what was *bound*. Gods were bound. Oaths were bound. Crowns and vows and seven generations of sworn bloodlines, all of it neat and graspable, all of it food.

But Yara's magic had never been bound to anything in its life.

It had no shape the thing could grip. No vow in it, no covenant, no handle. It was just a child's enormous, ungovernable, unsworn power, blazing loose in the dark, and the parasite floundered against it like a drowning man against water—there was nothing to drink, nothing to bind, nowhere to put its hooks. It screamed without a mouth. It thrashed between the girl's storm and the woman's open door, finding purchase on neither, and Yara stood in the heart of her own wildfire with her hair lifting and her eyes full of light and shouted the only spell she knew:

"*Get away from her!*"

The thing broke.

It came apart the way ice comes apart—not destroyed, but *unfastened*, decades of swallowed divinity tearing free of the crown in a flood of returning light. Prayers poured back out of it, thousands upon thousands, the stolen voices of burned witches and silent gods streaming upward through the broken vault like reversed snowfall. Somewhere in the flood, brief and unmistakable, a gray-cloaked figure with a crook turned to Bram—and this time it was only a memory saying goodbye—and dissolved into the general brightness, going wherever unbound things go.

The crown fell from the king's head.

It rang against the marble like a dropped bell, and rolled, and stopped.

* * *

Voss

Inquisitor-General Maren Voss had commanded purges for nine years. She had signed four hundred and eleven death warrants and attended sixty of the burnings personally, and she had slept, because order required it, because someone had to carry the arithmetic, because the alternative was chaos and chaos killed more than fire did.

She had told herself that every morning for nine years.

Now she stood in the wreck of the throne hall with ash sifting down through the shattered vault like gray rain, watching the king—the *king*—slump forward against his own restraints, gasping, human, weeping, and she understood with terrible clarity that every warrant had been feeding this. Every gifted person dragged to the pyres had been a meal delivered. She had not been keeping order. She had been *catering*.

Her Inquisitors stood frozen around the hall, thirty of them, swords half-drawn, waiting—for orders, for explanation, for anything.

"General Voss." The king's voice was wrecked, rusted, real. He lifted his head and his eyes were his own for the first time in decades, and they were horrified. "How long," he whispered. "Maren. How long has it been wearing me?"

And Voss looked at him—at the ruined man, at the fallen crown, at the little girl standing in a corona of fading fire with her fist clenched around a broken bead—and she made the choice she should have made years ago, the choice she would spend the rest of her life paying for, and it was the first honest act of her career.

"Inquisitors," she said, and her voice did not shake, because she would not permit it. "The Edict of Cleansing is hereby voided. By my authority—which I am about to surrender—you will stand down."

They stared at her. Captain Hesk, nearest the throne, frowned. "General, the edict comes from the crown—"

"The crown is *on the floor*." She pointed at it, rolling bright and evil in its circle of ash-light, and something in her voice made more than one man step back. "Look at your king. Look at what we served. Nine years, Hesk. We burned children to feed that." Her hand found the seal of office at her throat—the cold iron badge she had polished every day like a sacrament—and she tore it free and threw it at the fallen crown, where it struck with a small, final clang.

"I am done," Maren Voss said. "Anyone who wishes to remain an Inquisitor may arrest me now. Anyone who remembers what we were supposed to be protecting—follow me."

Silence. Ash falling. The king weeping softly on his ruined throne.

Then Sergeant Bren of the Third Vigil unbuckled her sword belt, laid it on the marble, and walked to stand at Voss's shoulder. Then another. Then six. Then most of them, in ones and twos, weapons clattering down like the last of a long fever breaking.

Hesk did not move. Hesk stared at the crown on the floor with an expression Voss recognized, because she had worn it for nine years: the look of a man whose entire architecture of meaning had just been shown to be load-bearing for a monster.

"Hesk," she said quietly. "Leave it. Leave it there. Some things aren't worth picking up."

He left it.

And in the center of the hall, Bram Oyelaran—the hedge-witch, the fugitive, the name on forty warrants with Voss's own signature—staggered upright with the girl catching her arm, and Voss saw that the witch was changed. Emptied. Diminished in some fundamental way, like a lamp after the oil is gone. But standing. Choosing to stand.

Their eyes met across the ash, hunter and hunted, executioner and survivor.

"You'll want to testify," Bram said hoarsely. "When they ask what happened here. Tell the truth. All of it. Especially the parts about you."

Voss nodded slowly. "I intend to."

"Good." Bram leaned on the girl, and the girl bore her up without complaint, fierce and blazing and ordinary now, just a child holding up a tired woman. "Because the city's going to want someone to hate. Better it's honest."

Above them, through the broken vault, dawn was starting—thin and gray and utterly indifferent, the first morning in decades that the palace had no god in it at all.

The crown lay in the ash, and no one picked it up.

But far below, in the drowned dark of the Hollow, the last vein of dragon-fire flickered—and guttered—and held, waiting to learn whether it had been saved, or merely outlasted everything else.
