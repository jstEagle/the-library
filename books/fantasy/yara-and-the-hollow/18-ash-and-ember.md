## 18. Ash and Ember

The light went out of the throne room like a tide going out of a harbor.

One moment the parasite's scream filled every stone of the palace foundations—a sound that was less a sound than a subtraction, a vast cold absence howling as Yara's uncaged magic burned through every hollow place it might have hidden—and then there was silence, and Bram was on her knees on wet flagstone with her hands pressed flat to the floor, and she could not feel anything at all.

Not pain. Not stone. Not her own fingers.

*It worked,* she thought, from a great distance, the way you think things in dreams. *God help me, it actually worked.*

Above her, the king made a small noise.

Bram had heard men make that noise before—mercenaries dying badly on foreign fields, old soldiers waking from nightmares of battles no one else remembered. It was the sound a person made when they came back into themselves and found themselves ruined. She lifted her head.

King Aldric Vell sat slumped in the great chair that had been his father's and his grandfather's before him, and he was weeping without any dignity at all. The perfect vacancy was gone from his eyes. What replaced it was worse: seventy years of lost time arriving all at once, decades of stolen mornings pouring back into a skull that had been kept empty so something else could wear it. His hands shook against the armrests. His mouth moved, shaping names.

"Maren," he said. "Where is—my daughter was nine years old when—" He stopped. Started again. "There were crops. There was a plague year. I remember signing—I don't remember *signing*. Who signed?"

"I did," said a voice behind her.

Voss. The Inquisitor-General stood in the shattered doorway of the underchapel, soot on her white collar, one eye swollen half-shut where debris had caught her during the rite. Her hounds—the ones still living—flanked her, and not one of them looked at their master's face. Behind them, through the broken doors, came the flicker of orange light. Someone had brought the ember up from the Hollow. Of course someone had. Yara would not have let it be left behind.

"Your Majesty," Voss said, and knelt. It was the strangest thing Bram had ever seen—the woman who had built the pyres going down on both knees on filthy stone—but her voice held steady. "You have been ill for a very long time. Longer than anyone knew."

The king looked at her. He looked at the kneeling Inquisitors, at the dragon-fire light crawling up the walls, at Bram on the floor with her hands spread like a drowned woman's.

"What are those lights," he whispered. "What is burning?"

"The truth, sire," Voss said. "Finally."

* * *

They got him down the steps with an arm over each of two guards' shoulders, an old man suddenly, older than his years by thirty at least, blinking at torchlight like a man surfacing from deep water. Bram walked behind them because her legs would carry her and nothing else would, and because Yara had a fist twisted into the back of her coat and would not let go.

The girl had not stopped shaking since the rite ended. Her magic was quiet now—quieter than Bram had ever felt it, pulled in tight around her like a child hiding under blankets after a storm. When Bram glanced down at her, Yara stared straight ahead, jaw set, eyes too bright.

"You're bleeding," Yara said.

"So are you."

"That's different. I'm supposed to bleed. You're not." Her voice cracked on the last word. "You're not supposed to do that anymore. You gave it away. All of it. I felt it go through you, Bram, I felt it *leave*, and you just let it, why did you—"

"Yara."

"—why didn't you *stop*, you could have taken some back, there was a moment, I saw the door open—"

"*Yara.*"

The girl shut her mouth. Her grip on Bram's coat tightened instead of loosening, which told Bram everything about what the next hour was going to cost them both.

They came up out of the foundations into the palace proper—or what remained of it. The rite had not been gentle with architecture. Corridors gaped. A staircase hung in midair, its lower flights simply gone, as though the building had been a page and something enormous had torn through the middle of it. Servants cowered in doorways. Somewhere above, bells were ringing—not the measured peal of ceremony but the frantic, overlapping clatter of every tower at once, each ringer deciding independently that something had happened and the city should know.

Dawn was coming through the eastern windows when they reached the great hall, and Bram stopped in the doorway because the light was doing something impossible.

It was gold. Ordinary, morning, unremarkable gold—and yet every surface it touched seemed to hold it a moment longer than it should have. Sparks drifted in the air like slow embers, settling on shoulders, on sills, on the upturned faces of servants who had come running at the bells. Bram recognized them. She had spent seven years learning to sense the smallest traces of divinity, the motes too fine for any charm, and these were that—but multiplied ten thousandfold, scattered loose across the whole of the palace like pollen from a burst flower.

The Shepherd's remains. Whatever the parasite had not digested. Whatever the unbinding had shaken free of the crown's roots.

"It's everywhere," Yara breathed beside her. "Bram, it's—it's landing on people."

She was right. A chambermaid stood transfixed near the hearth, arms out, as sparks settled along her sleeves and sank into the wool without burning. A guard captain crossed himself with the old country sign, the one the Church had forbidden sixty years ago, and the light pooled in his palm like water. An old cook dropped to her knees and began, absurdly, terribly, to laugh.

And Bram felt none of it.

That was the thing she could not stop noticing, standing in that golden hall while divinity snowed down on the living: she felt *nothing*. Seven years she had mourned her god's silence, and silence had at least been a presence—an absence shaped exactly like a voice, an ear turned toward her even if the mouth never answered. Now there was no shape at all. Where the Shepherd had been, where her own gift had lived since childhood—a low warm hum behind her sternum, the sense of knots wanting to tie themselves, of luck leaning toward her hand—there was only Bram. Plain, mortal, finished Bram, standing in a room full of miracles and feeling about as much as the furniture.

She had known this would happen. She had chosen it. Sef's notes had been explicit, and Bram had read them three times by ember-light while Yara slept, and she had gone into the rite with her eyes open.

Knowing did not help. Knowing never helped.

"Bram?" Yara's voice, very small. "Can you feel it? Any of it?"

"No," Bram said. It came out steady, which surprised her. "No, little storm. I can't."

Yara's face did something complicated—grief and guilt and a fierce, stubborn refusal of both, all fighting for space on eleven years old. "I can give you some," she said immediately. "I can feel it in me, it's like—like bees, like a whole hive settled in my chest, I can give you—"

"You can't, and you shouldn't, and if you try I'll tie your sleeves together for a month." Bram crouched to her level, took the girl's chin in one rough hand. "Listen to me. What fell tonight didn't fall for me. It fell for everyone who never had it. That was the whole point. That was the *only* point, or none of this was worth what it cost."

"Sef," Yara said, and now she was crying, finally, the way children cry—all at once, ugly and total. "Sef died for this. And the fire-keepers, and Doss is in a cell somewhere, and you're *empty*, Bram, you're empty and everyone up here gets the bees—"

"Yes." Bram pulled her in, and Yara came, fists balled against Bram's ribs, forehead pressed hard under her chin. "Yes, love. That's the arithmetic. I'd do it again. I'd do it slower and worse and twice, if it meant this morning."

Above them the sparks kept falling. One drifted down past Bram's cheek, close enough that she watched it the whole way—close enough that she could have sworn it hesitated, a heartbeat, a held breath, as though something in it remembered her.

Then it landed on Yara's hair and sank in, and Bram felt precisely nothing at all.

* * *

They carried the king down into the Hollow at his own insistence.

He would not stay in the palace. He had walked its halls for an hour like a stranger in his mother's house, touching walls, asking after people dead twenty years, flinching from portraits painted while something else wore his face. When Voss told him what lay beneath the foundations—the god-corpse threaded through the stones, the roots of the crown, the decades of feeding—he had listened without moving. Then he had asked to see it. Then, worse, he had asked to see what else lived below.

"They hid my kingdom from me," he said, on the stairs, gripping the rail with a hand like a bundle of sticks. "Every purge. Every pyre. I signed the first Edict myself, I remember the pen, I remember thinking *this is necessary*, and I cannot remember one face, one name, not one single person I sent to the flames. They kept me clean. They kept me *empty* so the burning wouldn't stain." His voice failed and recovered. "If there are survivors under my city, I will look at them. I owe them that much. I owe them considerably more than that, but I'll start with looking."

So it was that Bram found herself walking back down into the Hollow at midmorning, behind a ruined king and a disgraced Inquisitor-General, carrying the dragon-fire ember in an iron lantern because her hands were the steadiest available and Yara refused to surrender it.

The ember had changed.

In the tunnels beneath the palace it had guttered low, dimming as the Hollow's keepers died, and Bram had feared through the whole long night that it would simply go out—that the last vein of dragon-fire in the world would end in an iron box in a sewer. But as they descended past the flood-line, past Kessil's dark water sliding silent and satisfied along its bartered channel, the ember brightened. By the time they reached the drowned quarter it was burning high and clean, throwing light further than such a scrap had any right to throw.

And ahead of it, in the great flooded chapel where the refugees had begun creeping back at dawn, the ember's opposite waited.

It was small. That was the horror of it. After everything—the scream, the centuries, the gods eaten and the gifted burned and the realm hollowed decade by decade—the thing that had worn the crown was now scarcely larger than a cat: a knot of absolute cold crouched on the chapel's altar step, drinking what little warmth the water carried, thinning even as they watched. Unanchored. Starving. The vessel it had ridden for generations was an old man weeping on the far side of the room, and there was nothing left below for it to pour itself into.

Except, for one terrible moment, there almost was.

Bram felt the pull before she understood it—a tug behind her sternum, at the exact hollow place where her gift had been. The creature turned what served it for attention toward her, and she felt it *consider* her: a door left open, a house swept clean, a sworn vessel still marked with the old rites, blood-bound to a devoured god. Come, it did not say, because it had no mouth anymore. It simply leaned, the way cold leans toward a warm body, and Bram's emptied self leaned back before she could stop it—

—and then Yara stepped between them, and the ember flared.

Not gently. The iron lantern went white at the seams, and heat rolled out across the flooded nave, and the cold thing on the altar step *recoiled*. Dragon-fire, Sef had told them once, was the one flame in the world that burned memory as well as matter, and the parasite was made entirely of stolen remembering. It had nowhere to stand against that. It shrank from the light the way shadow shrinks, folding inward, thinning, unraveling into threads of frost that the warm water took and killed one by one.

Bram forced herself to watch. She owed it that much—she had fed it seven years of her grief, believing her god had simply abandoned her, and the least she could do was witness the ending.

It did not scream this time. Perhaps it had spent its voice in the rite. It simply grew smaller, and colder, and more distant, until the last of it was a film of frost on wet stone, and then not even that, and the water where it had crouched was only water.

The ember settled. The lantern's glow faded to ordinary orange.

"Well," said King Aldric Vell, from the chapel doorway, in a cracked and human voice. "That's that, then."

Bram discovered she was crying. She hadn't noticed starting. She wiped her face with the back of her hand, impatient, furious at herself, and found it changed nothing, and gave up, and let it happen.

Seven years of praying into silence. Seven years of believing herself forsaken. And now the truth of it: no abandonment at all. Just a shepherd eaten by wolves while the flock slept, and a flock—including her—too blind to look up. The Shepherd was not coming back. Nothing was coming back. The sparks falling on the city above were all that remained of every god the thing had swallowed, scattered like seed on ground that had never been asked whether it wanted planting.

"Grief's honest work," said a voice nearby, and she turned to find old Marta, the net-mender, one of the handful who'd escaped the raid, watching her with the unsentimental kindness of the very old. "Better than the other kind. The other kind's when you don't know what you're grieving." She nodded at Bram's empty hands. "You know. That's worth something. Not much. Something."

Bram laughed—a wet, broken sound, but real. "You always talk this much, Marta?"

"Only to people who've earned it." The old woman patted her arm with fingers like driftwood and shuffled off toward the fires the survivors were rebuilding.

* * *

Evening found Bram sitting alone on the chapel steps, or what served for steps—the drowned quarter had no true stairways, only the bones of one, and she sat on a fallen column with the black water lapping six inches below her boots.

The king was upstairs in the refugee hall, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, listening. Actually listening—to Marta's ledger of the burned, to the tunnel-keeper's account of the raid, to a boy of maybe fourteen describing the inside of an Inquisition wagon. Voss stood behind him and did not speak, and her silence had a new quality to it, heavy and deliberate, like a woman rehearsing a confession she intended to deliver publicly. Tomorrow, Bram thought, or the day after. Voss was many things, but she was not a coward, and the reckoning coming for Vell would need her testimony more than anyone's.

Doss was still in a cell. That thought sat in Bram's chest like a stone. He had opened the gate; he had been caught opening it; and whatever mercy the new order offered, it would reach the undercity slowly. She had sent word up with the first party of survivors. She would keep sending word until someone listened.

But that was tomorrow's weight. Tonight there was only the water, and the ember—Yara had set it in the chapel's old brazier, and it burned steady and low, and already the surviving fire-keepers were gathering around it the way people gather around a newborn. The vein wasn't dead. Sef had been right about that too: dragon-fire did not extinguish, it only waited for hands willing to tend it. There would be a keeping again. There was, in fact, a keeping now, and Bram suspected—with a tired, rueful certainty—that her name was already on whatever roster the survivors kept, whether she'd agreed to it or not.

Her gift was gone. She tested it one last time, the way you press a bruise, reaching for the old hum behind her sternum, the sense of knots and luck and the Shepherd's far-off attention.

Nothing. Clean nothing. Just a woman, forty-one years old, with scarred hands and a bad knee and no magic at all, sitting in the dark at the bottom of the world.

And underneath the grief—genuine, honest, hers—something else. Small. Stubborn. Almost embarrassing in its simplicity.

Relief.

She had spent seven years being a locked box, and the lock was broken now, and the box was empty, and empty meant *open*. Anyone could walk in. Anything could begin. She had nowhere left to hide, and for the first time in her life that felt less like a threat than like a door.

Boots on wet stone behind her. She knew the stride before she turned—too quick, too light, always half-running even standing still.

Yara dropped onto the column beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. For a while neither spoke. Below them the ember glowed; above, faint through fathoms of stone, the bells of Vell rang on, though by now they'd found something closer to a rhythm.

"The sparks are still falling up there," Yara said finally. "All day. People are saying—" She hesitated. "People are saying things are *happening* to them. The baker on Cordwain Street levitated his own cart. A fisherman's daughter talked to a drowned man and he answered. It's not going away, Bram. It's settling. Into everybody." Her voice dropped. "Mostly into me, though. I can feel it. Like I'm the deepest well and everything drains downhill." She looked sideways, and in the ember-light her eyes were frightened and hungry at once. "What am I supposed to do with all of it?"

Bram considered the question. She considered giving the easy answer, the comforting one, the lie.

What she said instead was: "Come with me tomorrow. There's someone you need to meet. Half-blind, older than sin, keeps a ledger of every soul the purges took—and she taught me the one lesson that matters, the one I want you to learn before you decide anything." She turned to face the girl fully. "Because power kept is power corrupted, little storm. And what you're holding isn't yours to keep."

Below them, the ember flared—as if in agreement, or as if something in it had heard, and remembered, and was waiting to see what the girl would choose.
