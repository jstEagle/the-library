## 20. Yara's Hands

The sparks came down like slow snow.

Yara felt them before she saw them—little warm weights settling on her skin in the days after the unbinding, each one a pinprick of heat that sank through her and did not burn. She stood on the palace steps with the crowd roaring around her and felt the first one land between her shoulder blades like a hand. Then another on her wrist. Another behind her ear, where her mother used to kiss her goodnight, back when she still had a mother's face to remember.

She didn't tell anyone at first. She wasn't sure she could explain it. It was like being rained on from the inside.

Bram noticed anyway. Bram always noticed. She caught Yara flexing her fingers by the fire in the Hollow three nights later and said nothing for a long moment, just watched the way the flames leaned toward the girl like sunflowers finding the sun.

"They're landing on you," Bram said. Not a question.

"Some of them." Yara held up her palm. A mote of light sat there, no bigger than a grain of barley, pulsing gently, patient as an egg. "Most of them went everywhere else. The baker on Cordwainer Street has one now—he doesn't even know it. His bread rises better than it ever did. Old Tammi who sweeps the fish market, she can find anything that's lost. Lost keys, lost cats, lost people." Yara closed her fist and opened it again, and the spark was still there, waiting. "But some of them came to me."

"How many?"

Yara had counted. Counting was the only thing that made the feeling manageable, the way knowing how deep water was made swimming less frightening. "Forty-one."

Bram went very still. In the seven weeks since the rite, since the thing wearing the crown had guttered out against the dragon-fire ember and the king had woken weeping in his own bed like a man released from a long illness, Yara had learned to read all of Bram's stillnesses. This one was fear.

"That's not a gift," Bram said quietly. "That's a congregation."

* * *

The trouble started small, the way trouble always did.

A woman came to the market stall where Yara helped old Pem sort charms—not magic charms anymore, just knots and beads and dried herbs, honest craft—and grabbed Yara's wrist hard enough to bruise.

"You're the girl," the woman said. Her eyes were wet and hungry at the same time. "The one from the fire. They say you pulled a god apart with your bare hands. They say the power went into you."

"I didn't pull anything apart," Yara said, and tried to pull her arm free. "And I don't have bare hands, I have these ones, and they're busy."

"My son died in the purges." The woman's grip tightened. "Two years ago. If there's power loose in the world now—if the dead can be—" She stopped. Her face did something terrible and hopeful. "Can you bring him back?"

And there it was. The question Yara would hear, in one form or another, every day for the rest of that season. Can you bring him back. Can you heal my eyes. Can you make my husband stop drinking, my daughter stop grieving, my luck turn, my debts vanish, my enemies suffer. The city had spent years learning to fear the gifted, and then in a single week it had learned to want them instead, and wanting, Yara discovered, was worse. Fear kept its distance. Wanting climbed through your window.

"I can't raise the dead," she told the woman, gently, because the woman's hands were shaking. "Nobody can. What fell out of the crown wasn't that kind of power. It was just—light. Little pieces of light. They make bread rise and kettles sing and lost things get found. That's all."

"Then give me one." The woman's voice cracked. "Give me a piece. You have forty of them. You won't miss one."

*How does she know about the forty?*

That was the moment Yara understood the shape of the problem. Not the power itself—the counting. People were counting what she carried. And things that got counted got weighed, and things that got weighed got fought over.

She looked down at the woman's chapped hands, still wrapped around her wrist, and felt the sparks inside her stir like birds hearing a whistle. One of them pressed up against her skin, eager, willing to go. They were generous, these little lights. They'd been locked in a god-corpse under a throne for decades; of course they wanted out. Of course they wanted hands.

It would be so easy. One spark, freely given, and this woman would go home lighter. Maybe her grief would sit differently in her chest with a little warmth underneath it. Maybe that was worth something.

Maybe it was worth everything, given away right.

Or maybe it was the first coin of a new treasury, and everyone in Vell would learn very quickly whose hands minted it.

Yara pried the woman's fingers off, one by one, and said, "Not like this. But come back in a month. I'm going to try something. If it works, you won't need me to give you anything—you'll already have your own."

The woman stared at her. "My own?"

"Everyone's got a door," Yara said. It was something Sef used to say, back when Sef was alive and keeping her ledger of the burned: *every soul's got a door, most just never learned where the hinges are.* "Somebody just has to show you where it opens."

* * *

She found Bram on the roof of the Hollow's entrance tunnel, sitting where she always sat at dusk, watching the city above through the grate. The dragon-fire glowed below them, steadier now, tended in shifts by the survivors who'd chosen to stay underground even after the Edict collapsed—even after Voss stood in the Grand Square and testified for six hours about what she'd built and what it had eaten. Some people needed the dark a while longer. Nobody rushed them.

"I know that look," Bram said without turning around. "You've decided something."

"How do you do that?"

"Hedge-witch trick. We can smell decisions. They smell like burnt sugar." She scooted over on the ledge. Yara sat down beside her. Below, the fire painted their shadows long against the tunnel wall, two shapes leaning together like a single figure.

"The sparks," Yara said. "Everyone wants them. And I could give them out—I could be the only person in Vell who decides who gets the gift and who doesn't. I could hand-pick the good ones. I could make sure nobody bad ever gets any." She picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. "And in ten years I'd be exactly what the crown was. Just smaller. Just nicer about it."

Bram was quiet for a while. "Your grandmother would've said that," she said finally. "Sef. About doors and hinges. She said it to me the night I came down here with a broken prayer-bead and nothing else." She turned, and in the firelight her scarred face looked almost soft. "So what are you going to do instead?"

"Teach."

The word came out before Yara had fully decided it, which was how her best decisions always arrived—like sneezes. "I'm going to teach. Not give. Teach. Show people where their own door is and let them open it themselves. Everyone finds their own spark. Everyone owns their own piece. Nobody holds anybody else's." She was talking faster now, the idea growing legs as it ran. "There's a ruined granary off Cordwainer Street, the one with the good roof. Pem says the owner will let it go cheap because it's haunted, but it's not haunted, it's just got a draft that whistles. I'll fix the draft. Benches, a stove, chalkboards. I'll start with the ones who already feel it—the baker, Tammi, the woman's daughter maybe, kids mostly, kids always feel it first—"

"Stop." Bram held up a hand.

Yara stopped, braced.

"You've thought about the Inquisition problem?" Bram asked. "Because the last school of the gift in this city ended with everyone in it chained to a pyre. There are people upstairs who haven't heard yet that the Edict is dead. There are people who informed. There are people who were informed on. You're going to gather the exact population that got people killed and put them in one room with a whistle-draft and a chalkboard."

"Yes," Yara said.

"And if someone denounces the school? If some clerk who survived the reckoning decides to rebuild his career on a new list?"

"Then I'll be loud," Yara said. "That's the whole point, Bram. I spent my whole life trying to be quiet. Living in a flue. Smothering myself with wet blankets so the hounds couldn't smell me. Being quiet nearly got me killed a dozen times, and being quiet is what let the crown eat everybody, because quiet people are easy to swallow one at a time." She felt the sparks rise in her chest like a tide coming in, forty-one small fires all agreeing with her. "If they come for the school, they'll come for a school full of people who aren't hiding anymore. That's different. That's completely different."

Bram looked at her for a long time. Then she laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut.

"When did you get smarter than me?"

"About four minutes ago. Enjoy it, it might not last."

* * *

The granary smelled of dust and old wheat-ghosts and, faintly, of rat. Yara scrubbed the floor herself, on her knees with a bucket and lye, because she wanted every board to know her before anyone else stood on it. Pem hauled benches. Tammi swept. The baker—whose name was Odo, and whose spark lived in his forearms and made his hands warm as fresh loaves—built the stove in two days flat, humming the whole time, and when he lit it the fire burned a shade too bright and a shade too gold, and everyone pretended not to notice, and everyone smiled.

They argued for three evenings about what to call it. *The Gift House* sounded like a temple. *Yara's School* sounded like a cult with a chalkboard. *The Cordwainer Institute* sounded like somewhere accountants went to die. In the end Tammi solved it, as Tammi solved most things, by refusing to call it anything at all. "It's the school," she said. "What else would it be?" And that stuck, because honest things rarely need fancier names.

The first morning, eleven people came. The second morning, nineteen. By the end of the first month the benches overflowed into the aisle and Odo was building more, and Yara stood at the front of the room with chalk dust on her sleeves and her heart hammering, looking out at faces that ranged from eight years old to eighty, and understood that she had never in her life been this frightened or this certain at the same time.

Her method was Bram's method, though Bram would deny the compliment. Cage it first, she taught. Name it. A thing named is a thing that answers to you. The baker's heat was called *Ovenheart*, and once he'd named it, it stopped flaring when he was angry. Tammi's finding-sense was called *Homeward*. A stuttering boy named Pell discovered his spark only worked when he was laughing, and they named it *Gigglefire*, and he cried with relief because for eleven years he'd thought he was broken instead of built strange.

"But here's the rule," Yara told them, every session, until they could say it in their sleep. "Power kept is power corrupted. Power shared is power survived. You don't hoard it. You don't sell it. You don't decide who else deserves theirs. You learn yours, and then you help the next person learn theirs, and that's the whole religion. That's it. No gods required."

A girl in the third row—sharp-faced, maybe nine, with a spark sitting behind her eyes like a held breath—raised her hand. "What if somebody uses theirs to hurt someone?"

Yara had known this question was coming. She'd rehearsed answers on the walk to the granary every morning for a month.

"Then the rest of us will know exactly who they are," she said. "Because they'll have learned it here, in front of witnesses, in the daylight. That's the difference between us and the crown. The crown hid in the dark under a throne and ate for decades before anyone saw its shadow. We're doing this in the light, together, where everything shows." She tapped the chalkboard, where she'd written the rule in letters a child could read. "Hiding is how it rots. So we don't hide."

The girl nodded slowly, satisfied, and Yara exhaled and hoped it was true.

* * *

On the evening the school turned two months old, Yara walked home through the rebuilt market with her pockets full of apples people kept pressing on her—teachers apparently attracted fruit the way magnets attracted iron—and stopped at the corner of Cordwainer Street where the crier's post still stood, the same post where, years ago, a man with a brass bell had read out the King's Edict and she had hidden in a doorway with her heart slamming, listening to her neighbors' names read like a death roll.

The post was bare now except for notices about road repairs and a lost goat. Someone had carved a small mark into the wood at shoulder height: a circle with a line through it, the sign of the old Hollow refugees, meaning *safe road, safe house.* Somebody else had carved it again, lower, for shorter arms. Somebody else again had added a little sun above it.

Yara put her palm flat against the carving. Under her skin, forty-one sparks hummed contentment, and somewhere across the city she knew—she simply knew, the way she knew rain was coming—other sparks hummed back. Odo's in his bakery. Tammi's in the fish market. Pell's in a room where someone was probably telling jokes. Dozens now. More than dozens. The count changed daily, and she'd stopped needing to know the number.

She thought about the woman with the dead son, who had come back in a month like promised, and hadn't gotten a miracle, but had gotten a seat on a bench and, eventually, a spark of her own that did nothing grander than keep her hands warm on cold mornings. She'd cried the day it settled in. "It feels like company," she'd said. Yara had understood exactly.

Footsteps behind her. She didn't turn; she knew the uneven gait, left foot favoring the knee that Doss's boot had broken during the raid years ago, before Doss was Doss-the-forgiven, before he started showing up at the school every fourth day to sit in the back row and take notes like the worst student in history.

"Bram says dinner," he said. "She says if you're late she's eating your share, and she says it in a way that means it."

"Tell her I'm coming." Yara stayed a moment longer, looking up Cordwainer Street, where lamplighters were working their way along and windows were filling with gold. Above the rooftops, the bells of Vell began to ring the evening hour—for no god, only for the city, the way they rang now, the way everyone had agreed they should.

Then she frowned.

The bells rang six strokes, and on the seventh stroke, something answered. Far off, past the river, past the walls, out in the hills where nothing should have been—a sound like a bell answering a bell, low and wrong and patient. The sparks in Yara's chest all went silent at once, forty-one small fires holding their breath.

Doss had gone pale. "Tell me you heard that."

"I heard it," Yara said slowly. Her hand drifted to the carved sign on the post, the safe-road mark, and rested there. "And I think we'd better tell Bram the world's got one more lesson to teach us."

Somewhere out in the dark, the far bell rang again.
