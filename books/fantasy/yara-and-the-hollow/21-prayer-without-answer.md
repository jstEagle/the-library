## 21. Prayer Without Answer

The fire had been burning for eleven days when it finally stopped trying to die.

Bram noticed it the way she noticed everything now—slowly, without urgency, the way a body notices a wound closing. She'd come down the worn steps of the old cistern stair before dawn, as she did every morning, carrying a bucket of rendered fat and a bundle of dried rush-pith that Sef's ledger said was good for feeding a flame that remembered being a dragon's breath. The ember they'd carried out of the raid, out of the dying, out of Sef's cupped and bloody hands, sat in its iron cradle at the center of the Hollow like a heart someone had finally put back where it belonged.

For ten days it had guttered. For ten days it had flared and sunk and flared again, moody as a sick child, and every time it sank Bram felt something in her chest sink with it—not fear exactly, not anymore. Something quieter. The feeling of watching an old animal breathe.

But this morning the flame stood straight. It burned without flicker in the still air of the Hollow, tall and gold-shot with green at its base, and when Bram knelt beside it to feed it the fat, the heat that came off it was not the heat of wood or oil. It pressed against her skin like attention. Like something looking back.

"There you are," she said.

Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat and fed the fire, and the flame took the offering with a sound almost like swallowing, and Bram settled onto the stone beside the cradle with her bad knee cracking, and for a long while she simply watched it burn.

* * *

The Hollow was not what it had been, but it was more than it had been, which was a strange thing to say about a drowned quarter full of refugees and ghosts. There were sixty-one people living down here now. Bram knew because she kept the count herself, chalked on the wall of the old counting-house in her own blunt mercenary hand: names, not numbers. Sef had kept a ledger of the burned. Bram kept a ledger of the living. It seemed the right way to honor both.

They had come slowly at first—a trickle through the Deep Road, through Kessil's dark water, each one paying whatever toll the river-thing asked and arriving hollow-eyed with their missing pieces. Then, after Voss stood up in the Assembly Hall above and said the words *I built it, I burned them, look at what I built*, after the Edict collapsed like a rotted tooth pulled clean, they had come faster. Some were Hollow-born, returning to places they'd fled years ago. Some were gifted, hiding no longer. And some—this was the strangest thing, the thing Bram was still getting used to—some were ordinary folk who had heard that sparks of something divine had settled into ordinary hands all across Vell, and who had come down to learn what to do about it.

Yara taught them. That was the part that still caught in Bram's throat sometimes, watching it.

She'd come up from the chapel school two evenings past, mud to the knees, hair escaping its braid in every direction, and dropped onto the bench beside Bram with the boneless exhaustion of a girl who had spent eight hours teaching grown men and women how to hold a spark without squeezing it to death.

"Marta set her sleeve on fire again," Yara reported.

"Again?"

"She keeps *grabbing* at it. I told her. I told her a hundred times. You don't hold a spark like a coin, you hold it like—" Yara made a shape in the air, fingers curved, open. "Like a bird that might stay if you're patient."

"Did she listen?"

"No." Yara grinned, soot-smeared and seventeen going on forty. "That's why I like her. Stubborn ones learn best."

Bram had looked at her then—at this girl who had paid a river-thing her mother's face, who had stood in the dark beneath the throne with her magic uncaged and roaring, who had given a devouring god nowhere left to hold—and felt the old ache, the one that used to be grief and had slowly become something else. Pride was too small a word. Gratitude was too small a word. Whatever it was, it lived in her chest where the Shepherd used to live, and some mornings she woke up and it was there before anything else, and she thought: *well. So that's what replaced you.*

Not bitterly. Just factually. The way you'd note the weather.

* * *

Doss stayed.

He'd never actually announced it. He'd just kept coming down. First to help clear the flooded galleries, hauling stone with the other able-bodied survivors until his sergeant's hands blistered and split. Then to repair the ventilation shafts, because he was the only one among them who understood the old garrison architecture well enough to know which walls were load-bearing and which were lies. Then just—to be there. Evening after evening, sitting by the fire with his scarred forearms on his knees, saying little, until one day Bram realized the cot in the east gallery had his blanket on it, and his razor on the shelf, and his boots under it, squared off neat as parade ground, and that he hadn't slept upstairs in the city in three weeks.

"You could have your life back, you know," she told him one night, when the others had drifted off to their galleries and the fire had burned down to its low green-hearted hum. "Above. They'll pardon you. Half of them want to give you a medal for opening that gate."

"Don't want a medal."

"You could teach at the garrison. Reform it from inside. That's what the Assembly keeps asking."

"They can keep asking." He turned his hands over, looked at the palms as if reading old maps there. "I spent twenty years learning how cities work from the top down. Oaths, chains of command, who reports to whom. You know what I learned down here?" He nodded at the fire. "How they actually work. From underneath. Somebody ought to know both, and I'm the only fool who does."

"That's not the real reason."

"No," he agreed easily. "It isn't."

Bram waited. Doss was a man who filled silences eventually; twenty years of friendship had taught her that patience beat questions.

"The last person I gave my oath to," he said finally, "was wearing a crown with something dead inside it, and I couldn't see it. I looked right at him. I saluted him." His jaw worked. "I don't trust myself to swear things anymore, Bram. Not to institutions. Not upward." He looked at her, and in the firelight his face was older than his years and younger than his sins, somehow both at once. "So I'm swearing downward instead. To this. To the people in the galleries. If that's not worth anything anymore, tell me, and I'll go."

Bram reached over and took one of those scarred hands, the way soldiers do, grip to grip.

"It's worth everything," she said. "It always was. We just forgot which direction it pointed."

* * *

The city above changed slowly, the way ice changes—imperceptibly hour to hour, undeniable week to week. News filtered down with the supply carts: the Inquisition's records opened to public audit, the long terrible arithmetic of the purges read aloud in the Assembly Hall, name by name, until clerks wept and had to be relieved. Voss herself, gray and unbowed, testifying for nine straight days. The gallows outside the Inquisition fortress dismantled and the timber given to the Hollow for shelving and school desks, which Bram thought was either very wise or very grim and had decided not to decide.

Some days the news was ugly. A magistrate who'd informed on his neighbors hanged himself rather than face the reckoning. A mob in Tanner's Row nearly lynched a former hound-handler before Yara's students—*children*, armed with nothing but composure and small contained lights—walked into the crowd and made them stop. Accountability, Bram was learning, was not a door you walked through once. It was a corridor you walked down every day, and some days it stank.

But other days: the fever-charm workshops reopening openly, hedge-witches registering their craft like carpenters register joinery. The first honest market stalls since the Edict, luck-knots sold without shame, without hiding, hung out in the daylight like washing. And the bells—the bells rang differently now. Not for the king. Not for any god. Just at dawn and dusk, seven strokes, for the city itself, for everyone in it, and Bram had heard more than one refugee stand still in the streets when they rang and cry without knowing why.

She went up sometimes. Once a week, maybe, to buy salt and lamp-oil and the rush-pith, to walk the market with her hood down—her hood *down*, that was still new, still startling, walking through a crowd with her face bare and nobody's eyes sliding away. Children stared at her prayer-bead sometimes, the broken strand of it, the single surviving bead she wore at her wrist. They didn't know what it was. That was a mercy. That was the whole point of everything they'd done.

* * *

On the eleventh morning, kneeling by the fire, Bram prayed.

She didn't mean to. That was the thing about seven years of habit—it lived below thought, below intention, in the tendons. Her hands were busy with the fire-feeding, her mind quiet, and somewhere between one breath and the next the old words rose up her throat like water finding an old channel: *Shepherd, who walks ahead of us in the dark country, keep my feet—*

She stopped.

Not because it hurt. That was the surprise, standing there with the words half-formed and the fire breathing gold-green light against her face: it didn't hurt. For a long time—years—the unfinished prayer had been a wound, a door held shut with her whole weight. But doors, she was learning, could be walked through and closed behind you, and what lay beyond them could be grieved and released, and the release could feel like this: light. Empty and light.

She let the words fall away unfinished.

*Shepherd,* she thought—not praying, just talking, the way you talk at a grave, the way you talk to the dead who cannot answer and never could again—*I found what ate you. We cut it loose. It's done. I'm not going to spend the rest of my life knocking on a door with nothing behind it.*

The fire crackled. The heat pressed against her like attention, and for one wild half-second Bram wondered—if divine sparks had settled into ordinary folk all across the city, if power had scattered like seed into forty-one chests and a thousand more besides—whether some last far-flung fragment of her god had settled somewhere too. Into a baker's hands. Into a child. Into the fire itself, maybe, the dragon-fire, which had always been the closest thing this world had left to a god's own breath.

And then she let the wondering go, too. Because it didn't matter. That was the freedom of it, the strange hard grace of it: it didn't matter where the fragments landed. What mattered was what people did with them. Whether they hoarded or shared. Whether they caged them or taught them. Whether the power became another crown or stayed what Yara was making it—a bird that might stay, if you were patient.

Bram finished tending the fire. She chalked the morning's count on the living-ledger—sixty-one, all accounted—and climbed the stairs toward the smell of porridge and the sound of Yara arguing cheerfully with Marta about sleeves.

* * *

It was three days later, in the deep quiet after midnight, that the bell rang again.

Bram was awake because old soldiers are always awake at the worst hours, sitting by the banked fire with tea gone cold in her hands, when the sound came down through the waterlogged stone of the Hollow like a finger drawn along the spine of the world. Low. Far. Out past the river, past the walls, out in the hills where nothing should be. A bell answering a bell—and the city's dusk-bells had rung hours ago, and nothing answered them, and nothing had rung out in the hills in all the weeks since Voss's testimony, since the unbinding, since the parasite guttered out against the dragon-fire ember.

Except once. Except that night on the safe-road, when Yara had come down pale and grave and told her: *the world's got one more lesson to teach us.*

Now it rang again. Closer.

Bram set down the tea. Around the fire, heads were lifting—one by one, the light sleepers, then the rest—sixty-one faces turning toward the sound with the same expression she could feel on her own face. Not panic. Something older than panic. The look of people who had survived one devouring god and knew, in their bones, that the world did not make only one of anything.

From the east gallery, quick footsteps. Yara appeared with her braid half-done and her eyes wide and bright, and behind her, buckling his sword-belt with steady hands, Doss.

"Bram," Yara said. The sparks in her chest—Bram could see them, faintly, the way you see heat-shimmer—asleep no longer, all of them awake and listening. "It rang twice tonight. Twice. And the second time—"

"I know." Bram was already reaching for her coat, for the staff she'd taken up in place of the sword she no longer carried, for the single prayer-bead at her wrist. "I heard."

Out in the hills, in the dark, the bell rang a third time.

And this time, unmistakably, from everywhere at once—from the fire, from the stone, from the water in the drowned galleries, from the beads of every charm in the Hollow—the answer came back.
