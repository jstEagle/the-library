## 22. The Last Light Carried Up

### Yara

The children came down in a line, each one holding the rope with both hands like Sef had taught them to hold it—like it was a living thing that might change its mind.

Yara went first, of course. She always did. Six years of descending into the Hollow had worn her own path through the dark, and she knew every handhold and every place where the old stairs had crumbled into the drowned stone below. She carried a lantern in her free hand, though she didn't strictly need it. The fire down there answered her now, when it was in the mood.

"Mind the fourth step," she called back over her shoulder. "It's a liar."

Behind her, eleven students clambered down the stair cut into the foundations of the world. They were an odd congregation: the cooper's daughter whose skin glowed faint blue when she slept; a dockhand's son who could hear iron singing; twins from the Weavers' Row who finished each other's charms without meaning to; a boy named Pell who was, as far as anyone could tell, entirely ordinary except for the fact that moths loved him with an almost religious devotion. Yara had argued for weeks that he should be allowed in the class anyway. *Power isn't only what you can do,* she'd told Bram. *It's what you're near.* Bram had given her that long look, the one that meant *you've been listening*, and Pell had come.

"Is it true there's a dragon down there?" whispered Maret, the cooper's daughter, who was eight and took nothing on faith.

"There's dragon-fire," Yara said. "That's better. A dragon would just sit on it."

"Would not."

"Would too. That's basically all dragons do." She grinned at the girl's scowl. "You'll see."

They came down out of the stairwell and into the great vaulted dark of the Hollow, and Yara stopped, because she always stopped here, because she never wanted to forget what it did to her the first time.

The drowned pre-city opened beneath them like the inside of a bell. Streets older than Vell ran off into black water, their roofs long gone, their walls furred with pale moss that drank the firelight and gave back a soft green glow. Bridges arched overhead where no river had ever flowed—or where rivers had once, before the first city sank and the second city was built on top of its bones. And at the center of it all, at the heart of the flooded square where the refugees of three purges had made their homes among the standing stones of some forgotten temple—

The fire.

It wasn't large. That was the thing no one believed until they saw it. After all the stories, after the songs the market singers had started making about the Last Flame and the Girl Who Carried It, people expected a bonfire, a beacon, a pillar. What burned in the old temple basin was a flame no bigger than a cooking pot, red-gold and patient, guttering sometimes in a way that made everyone's chest tighten, but never going out. Around it, the survivors had built low benches of salvaged stone. Someone had planted herbs in cracked urns. Someone else had hung wind-chimes made of spoon-handles that caught the updraft and rang thin and sweet.

Yara watched her students see it for the first time. She remembered being eleven, half-drowned in a sewer with a strange grim woman holding her wrist, seeing this same light and thinking: *oh. I'm not the only warm thing in the world.*

"Come on," she said softly. "She's waiting."

Bram sat on the third bench, exactly where she always sat, mending a net by lamplight with the unhurried competence of a woman who had decided that if the world insisted on continuing, she would continue along with it, competently and on her own terms. Her hair had gone fully gray now, which she claimed was the rite's doing and Doss claimed was simple aging and which Bram refused to discuss further. Her hands moved steadily. The broken prayer-bead still hung at her throat—the Shepherd's bead, the last piece of him, worn smooth now from six years of her thumb.

"You're late," Bram said, without looking up.

"The tide was high. Pell got stuck."

"I did not get *stuck*," Pell said with dignity, arriving damp.

Bram looked up then, and Yara saw her count them—eleven heads, all present, all whole—and saw, the way she always let herself be seen in these small unguarded seconds, that the counting was a prayer. That it had been, ever since the night the Hollow bled and Sef died holding a tunnel open with her two bare hands and the last of her strength, so that a girl with loud magic and a woman with none could run deeper into the dark.

"Sit," Bram said. "All of you. Yes, near the fire, it doesn't bite. It's the only honest thing left in this city."

They settled onto the benches in a rustle of nervous energy. Maret edged closest to the flame, close enough that its light turned her face gold, and gasped when it leaned toward her—an inch, no more, the way a cat leans toward a warm hand.

"It *knows* me," she breathed.

"It knows warmth," Bram said. "So do you. Lesson one starts there."

* * *

### Bram

She had taught this lesson forty times now, and it never got easier, and she had decided that was the sign it was worth teaching.

"Six years ago," Bram began, laying the mended net across her knees, "there was a law in Vell that said if you carried even a spark of the gift, you were to surrender yourself for cleansing. Do any of you know what cleansing meant?"

Silence. Even the youngest of them knew. Their parents had told them, or the streets had, or the new memorial wall above the fishmarket had—three hundred names carved in slate, growing longer every year as the audit commissions confirmed another batch of the disappeared.

"It meant fire," Bram said. "Not this kind." She nodded at the dragon-flame, burning calm and red-gold in its basin. "A colder kind. I watched it from rooftops. I sold charms too small to register, and I kept my head down, and I told myself that staying invisible was survival. And for seven years before that—" her thumb found the prayer-bead, an old habit worn smooth as riverstone, "—I told myself something worse. I told myself my god had abandoned me, so I owed the world nothing but my own hide."

Maret raised her hand. Eight years old and already trained by the new schools to ask the dangerous questions. "Did he? Abandon you?"

"No." Bram let the word sit. "He was eaten. There was a thing under the palace wearing the king like a coat, and it fed on bound gods, and mine was one of them, and I spent seven years grieving a murder I mistook for silence." She looked around at their faces—frightened, fascinated, too young for most of this and old enough for all of it, which was the condition of nearly everyone born in Vell these last twenty years. "I'm telling you this not because I enjoy my own tragedy. I'm telling you because everything I learned, I learned the expensive way, and you're going to learn it for the price of an afternoon."

Pell shifted. "What's the lesson?"

Bram held up one finger. "Power kept is power corrupted." She looked at the boy who heard iron sing. "Joren. When your gift first came, what did you do with it?"

His ears went red. "Hid it."

"And when hiding stopped working?"

"...Used it. To get things. Free bread off the baker's rack, when his scale was in earshot. Small stuff."

"And how long before small stuff felt like *stuff*?"

He stared at his boots. "Three weeks," he admitted.

"Three weeks." Bram nodded. "That's fast, but it's not unusual. A gift locked in one person is a door with no handle on the inside—it can only push outward. Hoard, take, defend, enlarge. Every tyrant this realm has produced started as somebody's clever child who learned that their spark worked better when nobody else could see it." She let that land. "Now look at the other bench."

The students turned. On the far side of the fire, the evening shift of the Hollow was gathering: Old Tam the tunnel-warden warming his ruined knees; the sisters Vetch passing a kettle between their cupped hands, heating it by turns because neither could manage it alone; a former Inquisitor's clerk named Adde, who had defected during the Reckoning and whose small, tidy gift for finding lost things was now used exclusively to reunite flood-refugees with their belongings. Ordinary folk, most of them. A few sparks among them, settling gently since the Unbinding had shaken divinity loose like rain from branches—but mostly just people, drawn to the fire because the fire shared itself freely and they had learned to do the same.

"The sparks that fell after the rite didn't choose lords," Bram said. "They scattered. Most of them thinned out into nothing—into luck, into good harvests, into the feeling you get that you should take the long way home tonight. But where they pooled, they pooled in *communities*. In people who taught each other. Because that's the shape divinity takes when it isn't hoarded." She picked up the kettle from the Vetch sisters, poured water over leaves into six chipped cups, and handed them down the bench to her students. "Power shared is power survived. That's the whole curriculum. Everything else is technique."

Maret wrapped both hands around her cup and frowned into the steam. "But if everybody shares, what if someone doesn't? What if someone keeps?"

Bram met Yara's eyes across the fire.

"Then you tell them the story of the man under the crown," Yara said, "and you keep telling it until they believe you. And meanwhile—" she shrugged, that fierce eleven-year-old shrug grown into a seventeen-year-old's easy confidence, "—you make sure the sharing is better than the keeping. That's why we teach in the market now instead of hiding. Hiding is how the Inquisition happened. You can't burn what everyone can see."

"Above us right now," Bram added, "your school is a room over a bakery with a sign on the door. *The Gifted and the Ordinary, Taught Together.* Half the city thinks it's scandalous. The other half sends their kids."

"Which half are your parents?" Yara asked the students, and there was laughter, and the lesson broke apart into cups and questions and Pell showing Joren how the firelight made the moths dance, and Bram sat back on her bench and let the noise wash over her like a tide she had finally stopped fighting.

Her hands were empty. They had been empty for six years—the rite had taken everything, her gift, her god's last whisper, the cold reservoir of power she'd hoarded through decades of mercenary work. Some mornings she woke reaching for the Shepherd the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore. But her hands were not empty of other things. They knew how to mend nets, bind wounds, teach frightened children to breathe through the first flare of their gift. It turned out that a life could be full of work and love and grief and still have room left over. Nobody had told her that. She suspected nobody had told anybody, which was why the world kept producing kings and Inquisitors—people who had never learned that belonging wasn't a thing you seized but a thing you built, badly, together, and rebuilt again every morning.

Above them, muffled through sixty feet of stone and drowned history, the bells of Vell began to ring.

* * *

### Yara

The evening bells. Not for vespers—for the city. That had been Voss's last official act before she dissolved her own office: striking the god-hours from the calendar and replacing them with plain hours, marked by plain bells, ringing for nothing holier than the fact that people were alive underneath them and deserved to know the time.

Yara stood at the mouth of the stairwell and listened to them come down through the stone, and thought about the girl she had been, six years ago, crouched in a chimney flue with a kettle singing her location to the whole street, running across rooftops with hounds behind her, believing—with the absolute conviction available only to orphans—that she would die alone and that alone was simply what she was.

Then a grim gray woman had grabbed her wrist in a sewer and said, *"Can you swim or do I throw you?"* and everything after that was family.

Bram appeared beside her, following her gaze upward, toward the sound.

"You'll be late for your own class tomorrow if you keep standing there listening," Bram said.

"They're my favorite bells."

"You say that about every bell."

"They're all my favorite bells." Yara bumped her shoulder against the older woman's arm. Below them, the students were being collected one by one by parents and aunts and the occasional neighbor, the procession reforming for the climb, lanterns bobbing. Maret paused at the bottom step and looked back at the fire, small and stubborn in its basin, and Yara saw the girl memorize it—the way you memorize a thing you intend to return to. The way Yara had, once.

"She'll be trouble," Bram observed.

"The best kind." Yara smiled. "Like somebody else I know."

"Hmph." But Bram's mouth twitched, and she put her arm briefly around Yara's shoulders—a gesture that had taken four years to arrive and now came easily—and together they watched the last lanterns dwindle up the stairwell toward the bells, toward the market, toward the ordinary lit windows of a city slowly learning what its own name meant.

The fire burned on behind them. It would burn long past them both; Sef had said so once, and Sef had never once been wrong about fire. Somewhere above, a thousand households were lighting their own smaller flames for the evening, and the divine rain that had fallen through the realm was settling into soil and bread and song, and no one wore a crown that anything else could wear.

Yara took a breath of the deep warm air, tasting smoke and stone and the green smell of the moss.

"Same time next week," she said. "Bring more cups. We're getting a new student—Adde found her. Feral little thing, lives in the aqueduct, sets things on fire when she cries."

Bram sighed the sigh of a woman whose rest was permanently scheduled to begin next year. "Of course we are."

"But you'll teach her anyway."

"I'll teach her anyway," Bram agreed, and reached up, and rang the spoon-handle chimes once, softly, a sound like a small silver prayer going up through the dark to no one—to everyone—and the fire leaned toward the sound, and the bells above rang on for the city, and the last light climbed the stairs ahead of them, carried up in twelve pairs of ordinary, gifted, human hands.
