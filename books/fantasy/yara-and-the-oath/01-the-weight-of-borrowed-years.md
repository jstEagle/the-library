## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The rain came down the way it always did in Thornfell: sideways, mean, and smelling faintly of iron, as though the mountains themselves were bleeding somewhere above the cloud line. Jude Yarrow pulled the oilcloth tighter around their shoulders and kept walking, boots finding the smuggler's road by feel more than sight. The road wasn't on any map worth trusting. It wound along the spine of a ravine where the river Vask's little sister—locals just called her the Sigh—chewed at limestone and swallowed whatever the winter sent down to her. In three days it would reach the lowland markets. In two hours, if the weather held its temper, it would reach the toll.

The toll was a woman named Brannagh, and she owed Jude a memory.

Not that she knew it yet. That was the trick of the trade. Nobody ever knew they owed until the moment Jude named the price, and by then they were usually too curious—or too desperate—to walk away. Curiosity was the coin that never debased. Rain fell on everyone equally, but curiosity fell hardest on people who lived their whole lives in one narrow valley with one narrow road and one narrow life, and Thornfell was nothing but narrow.

Jude smelled woodsmoke before they saw the fire. The toll camp sat under an overhang of black rock where the ravine pinched tight enough that a child could hold it against an army. Two wagons, a cookfire hissing under a canvas lean-to, and Brannagh herself standing at the edge of the light with a crossbow that had seen better decades, its stock worn smooth by other hands, other wars.

"Road's closed," Brannagh said.

"It's raining."

"Road's *closed*."

Jude stopped at the border of the firelight, hands loose and visible, hood back so the woman could see a face designed by nature or long practice to look harmless—young-ish, forgettable, the kind of face a witness would struggle to describe an hour later. That was not a gift; that was craft. The gift was something else entirely.

"You've got a sick goat," Jude said. "Third pen from the left, the brown one with the crooked ear. Bloat, maybe worse. Your boy tried to dose her with gin this morning because he heard it from a tinker who heard it from nobody, and now she won't stand."

Brannagh's crossbow dipped an inch. "Who told you that?"

"No one told me. I watched you pass through Harrow's Ford last week, when I was trading there." This was true, more or less. Jude had been trading there. What they'd been trading was harder to explain, and Jude did not intend to explain it. "I can tell you how to save her. And I can give you something better than a cure for a goat."

"Better than—" Brannagh laughed, short and hard, the laugh of a woman who had been offered many things on this road and believed in none of them. "And what would that be?"

"The summer you were seventeen." Jude let the words come slow, letting the rain fill the pauses. "The boy with the freckles. The one who worked the ferry before the ferry washed out. You never told anyone about him. Not even your husband, especially your husband. He kissed you on the water stair and you can still feel it sometimes when the river's high, and you have spent twenty years telling yourself you imagined how good it was."

The silence that followed had weight. Rain drummed on canvas. Somewhere up the ravine, the Sigh muttered over its stones.

"You're a mind-reader," Brannagh said at last, and there was no admiration in it. There shouldn't have been. Mind-readers were a nuisance. What Jude was was considerably worse, and considerably rarer.

"I'm a memory-thief," Jude said. "There's a difference. A mind-reader only knows what you know. I can *give* it back to you. All of it, whole, the way it happened—the smell of the water, the freckles, the way your heart went stupid in your chest. Better than it lives in your head now, actually, because I'll hand it back fresh instead of faded. Memory's like wine. Left alone it turns to vinegar. I keep mine decanted."

"And what does it cost me?"

"A night's shelter, dry ground, passage past your toll tomorrow when the caravan comes through, and one thing in return. One memory of yours I choose. Small. Nothing you'd miss until you went looking for it." Jude smiled, and made sure the smile reached their eyes, because trust lived in eyes even when it died everywhere else. "That's my price. That's always my price."

Brannagh studied them for a long moment. Rain ran off the crossbow's limb and dripped from the bolt's steel head into the mud. Then she jerked her chin toward the fire.

"Get in out of the wet, thief. If you're lying about the goat, I'll put a bolt in your leg and let the ravine have the rest of you."

---

The goat, as it turned out, could be saved, and was—Jude talked Brannagh's boy through stomach-tubing her while Brannagh watched from the pen's gate with her arms crossed and her suspicion slowly drowning in relief. Afterward there was stew, heavy on barley and light on meat, and after that, the trade.

They did it sitting knee to knee across the cookfire, which had burned down to a red heart of coals. Jude took Brannagh's hands in theirs, palms up, and asked her to think of the freckled boy. To think of him hard. To hold nothing back.

"What do you feel?" Jude asked.

"Warm," Brannagh said. Her voice had gone strange, distant, a woman looking through a window at weather twenty years gone. "Warm and stupid."

"That's the door. I'm coming through now. Don't fight me and it won't hurt."

It didn't hurt. It never hurt, if the holder didn't fight. Jude closed their eyes and slid into the shape of another person's past the way a swimmer slides into dark water—no splash, barely a ripple, and then weightlessness. Inside, Brannagh's memory bloomed around them: evening light going copper on the river, the groan of the ferry rope, a boy's laughter cracking in the middle because he was fifteen and his voice couldn't decide what it wanted to be. The taste of stolen plums. The terrifying, holy clumsiness of a first kiss, teeth clicking, both of them laughing, neither letting go.

Jude took it all. Carefully, gently, lifting the whole of it out of her the way you'd lift a sleeping child from a bed—one hand beneath the shoulders, one beneath the knees—and folded it into themselves, into the deep vault where they kept everything they'd ever stolen. Then they turned and carried it back the other direction, and pressed it home again, fresh-polished, every color brightened, every sound sharpened, the plums tasting of plums instead of the idea of plums.

Brannagh gasped. Her eyes flew open and flooded, and she laughed and wept in the same breath, gripping Jude's hands hard enough to grind bone.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, *him*. I'd lost—he'd gone thin in me, like a coin worn smooth. I couldn't remember his voice. Now he's—" She stopped, wiped her face with her wrist, and looked at Jude with something close to awe and something close to fear, which was exactly the balance Jude worked thirty years to strike. "What did you take from me? Just now. What did you take as your fee?"

"I haven't taken it yet. I'll do it while you sleep. You won't feel it, and you won't know what's gone unless you go hunting for it, and you won't go hunting, because why would you? You don't miss a stone you never knew was in your shoe."

"That's a wicked bargain."

"It's a fair one. Ask anyone I've traded with. Well—" Jude stood, stretching, joints popping in the fire's warmth. "Ask anyone I've traded with twice."

They slept in the wagon bed among sacks of dried eel, and in the grey hour before dawn, while Brannagh dreamed of freckles, Jude knelt beside her and took their fee: a small, sunlit afternoon from her girlhood, an hour of skipping stones on a becalmed river with a cousin whose name Jude chose not to learn. It came away clean, leaving no scar, no hole, only a softness where a sharpness used to be. By morning Brannagh would remember the cousin vaguely, love her distantly, and never once wonder why the day itself had gone missing from between all the days around it.

That was the rule. Jude's rules, carved into them by a dead woman's dying instruction, worn into them like a groove worn into a staircase:

*Never keep.* Take only what you're paid to take, and pay yourself only what you must, and never, never build a hoard out of other people's lives.

*Never feel.* Borrowed joy is borrowed. Grieve nothing that isn't yours. Want nothing that isn't yours. The memories are goods. Goods move. Goods get sold.

Jude broke the second rule every single day and called it discipline anyway. Because here was the truth they admitted to no one, least of all themselves, in the small hours when the fire died and the rain kept on: they *did* feel them. Every memory they carried, they felt. The freckled boy's kiss still lived warm behind Jude's ribs, and the plums still tasted of July, and somewhere in the vault an old king's last words waited in the dark like a coal that refused to go cold—a memory Jude should have sold years ago, had tried to sell a dozen times, and could not, because some things you steal and then discover they have stolen you right back.

But that was a thought for the road ahead. For now there was rain, and a toll paid, and a caravan due at midday carrying salt and tin and, if the gossip at Harrow's Ford held true, news from the eastern passes that made traders lower their voices.

Jude sat up in the wagon bed, watched the grey light come up the ravine, and counted what they carried the way a miser counts coin in the dark. Three hundred and eleven borrowed lives' worth of moments, give or take. A dead queen's coronation. A fisherman's drowning, survived. A wedding that ended badly and a funeral that ended well. And beneath all of it, oldest and heaviest, wrapped in layers of lesser thefts like a jewel packed in wool:

The king of Cael, dying, whispering about a Door.

Jude had never been to Cael. Few people had, outside its borders—it kept itself close, wards thick as castle walls, maps that charged a toll of forgetting just to look at them. But the dying king had come to a mountain shrine where young Jude had been hiding from a Warden patrol, and he had mistaken a thief for a priest, and priests were supposed to hear confessions, weren't they? So he confessed. And Jude, who should have slipped away, stayed and listened and then—hungry, frightened, fourteen years old and breaking the first rule within a year of learning it—took.

The king's last memory. His confession entire. It burned in Jude's keeping like ice that burns, like a brand that heals, and every few months Jude woke at night certain someone was following the heat of it across the world.

Today the feeling was stronger than usual. Today, when Jude stepped down from the wagon and looked east up the drowned grey throat of the ravine, the air felt *watched*.

"Caravan's late," Brannagh said, joining them under the lean-to. She looked rested in a way Jude suspected she hadn't rested in years. She also looked at Jude sideways, the way you look at a knife you're glad to own and careful to store. "You'll travel with us past the split, like agreed?"

"Like agreed."

"Then eat. And thief—" She hesitated. Whatever she wanted to ask—*what else do you carry? is any of it mine? could you bring back my mother, just for a minute, just her voice?*—she swallowed it, which was wise. People who asked those questions never liked the answers. "The boy. The freckled one. Did he live? Do you know?"

Jude considered lying. Lying was easier, and kindness, and habit.

"He lived," Jude said quietly, because it was true—they'd checked, years ago, in another market town, another life. "He runs a ferry still. Different river. He married. He has daughters." One of whom, Jude did not add, had sold Jude a memory of her father teaching her to read, and in the background of that memory, always, a man humming while he mended nets, a man who still sometimes looked west toward mountains he'd never explained wanting to see.

Brannagh turned away fast, but not fast enough to hide her face breaking open, and Jude stood in the rain and felt the familiar ache set up shop behind their sternum—the ache that was supposed to be against the rules.

*Never feel.*

Around midday the horns sounded down the ravine, and the caravan came: eight wagons, forty mules, sixty-odd travelers in oilskins, and at the rear of the column, walking with the loose, exhausted stagger of people who had walked a very long way without stopping—

Refugees. Dozens of them. Maybe hundreds, strung out along the road like beads off a snapped cord, and not one of them, when the caravan guards questioned them, could say where they had come from.

Jude stood at the roadside and listened to a grey-haired woman answer, for the fifth time, the simplest question in the world.

"Where are you from, mother?"

The woman's brow furrowed. She looked east, toward the high passes, toward the place her feet clearly remembered and her mind could not follow.

"I don't—" she said. "East. From... east."

Above the ravine, behind the clouds, where stars would be gathering for the night even through the rain—

Something went dark.

* * *

The first star vanished at dusk, and Jude only noticed because they had been counting.

Counting stars was another of the old woman's rules—not a rule, really, a comfort, a rosary. *Find the Shepherd, find the Ox-Eye, find the three Sisters strung like a necklace over the northern peaks,* she'd say, the woman who taught Jude the gift and the discipline both, back when Jude was small enough to be carried out of burning houses. *However far you wander, the sky keeps the same ledger. As long as you can read it, you're never wholly lost.*

So Jude counted, out of habit, standing at the tail of the halted caravan while the refugees shuffled past in the rain. Shepherd. Ox-Eye. Sister, sister—

Sister.

Jude blinked rain out of their eyes and looked again. The third star of the necklace was simply gone, the way a word goes from your mouth when you forget what you were saying. Not clouded. Clouds dimmed; they didn't delete. The patch of sky where it hung was black and clean and empty as a picked pocket.

"Sera," Jude whispered—the star's old smuggler-name, the name thieves gave it because it rose faithful over every fence-line in the borderlands—and as if the naming had been a summons, the second Sister winked out.

Then the Ox-Eye. Then the Shepherd, mid-count, like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

The caravan had gone quiet. Mule-drivers stood in the traces staring upward. One of the guards dropped his lantern and didn't stoop for it. Along the whole length of the column, faces tilted back into the rain, watching the sky empty itself from horizon to horizon, star by star by star, the great ledger closing, until there was nothing overhead but black.

And then, far to the east beyond the passes—beyond the passes, where no road went and no map bothered—came a sound. Jude felt it in their teeth before they heard it with their ears: a vast, crystalline shattering, drawn-out and delicate, like the whole world's glassware falling at once. The border wards of Cael. Everyone in the caravan felt it; several people moaned. The wards had stood for nine hundred years. They had been less than an hour dying.

The grey-haired refugee stopped in front of Jude. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and long roads, and her eyes were wet, though whether from rain or grief she likely could no longer have said.

"We had a country," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't to Jude, particularly. It was to the dark, to the road, to whoever might listen. "Didn't we? We had a country and it had a name."

Jude opened their mouth to say *Cael*, and stopped, because the answer arrived unbidden and complete and terrible: not just the name but the shape of it, the blue of its high summer skies, the smell of the capital's cedar docks, the sound of its bells counting the hours, the face of its king grown old and afraid. Nine hundred years of a nation rose in Jude's mind like water filling a vessel—because Jude carried a piece of its heart in their vault, and around that anchor-stone, everything else had snagged and held.

Every soul on this road had emptied like cups tipped out.

Except one.

The woman was still waiting. The caravan was still silent. Somewhere east, in the ringing aftermath of the shattering, Jude could almost believe they heard footsteps—many, unhurried, inevitable—already beginning the search for what remained.

"Yes," Jude said, and their voice came out steady, which surprised them, because inside they were screaming. "We did. It was called Cael."

The woman repeated it like a prayer being born: "*Cael.*" Tears cut clean lines down her rain-wet face. "Yes. Yes. Oh—thank you. Thank you, child. How did you—"

"Don't thank me," Jude said. Already turning, already reaching for the wagon, for the oilcloth bundle, for the hoard. "Pray no one finds out I know."

Because the Wardens would come. Of course they would. You did not unmake a kingdom without sweeping afterward for crumbs, and Jude Yarrow, standing in the rain under a sky with no stars, was holding the biggest crumb in the world.

The rules said run. Jude ran.
