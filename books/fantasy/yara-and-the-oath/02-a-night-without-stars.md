## 2. A Night Without Stars

The rain had stopped an hour past midnight, and the road had turned to something between mud and glass. Jude walked it the way a thief walks a floorboard that might creak—heel first, weight rolled slow, listening with their whole body for the sound of things going wrong.

Behind them, Thornfell's hills rose black against a sky thick with cloud. Ahead, maybe six miles as the crow lied, lay the border stones of Cael: a line of leaning granite markers half-swallowed by gorse, each one carved with wards so old the masons who cut them had been dust for nine hundred years. Beyond those stones was a kingdom Jude could not enter and could not stop thinking about.

They counted their hoard the way other travelers counted coins. Not aloud. Never aloud. But in the dark behind the eyes, where the stolen memories lived in their little lacquered boxes of light: the miller's daughter's first kiss, warm as bread. A fisherman's terror in a storm off the Sable Coast, salt and screaming rope. Forty-one years of other people's yesterdays, carried like contraband because that was exactly what they were.

*Never keep,* Jude reminded themselves, the rule worn smooth as a river stone from handling. *Never feel. Take, trade, pass on. The moment you love a memory that isn't yours, it starts loving you back, and then you're not a thief anymore—you're a graveyard.*

They were good at the rules. They'd had to be. It was the nights like this one—quiet, washed clean by rain, the whole world holding its breath—that made being good at them feel like drowning slowly in a very beautiful lake.

Jude crested the rise above the border stones and stopped.

Something was wrong with the sky.

Not the clouds. The clouds were doing what clouds did, drifting fat and grey across the dark. But through every tear in that cover, where stars should have been—where stars *had* been an hour ago, because Jude checked, Jude always checked, a habit learned from smugglers who navigated by them—the holes in the world showed nothing at all.

Not overcast. Not fog. Nothing.

A starless patch is just weather. But this wasn't a patch. Jude turned a slow circle, and everywhere the clouds thinned or broke, the same absence stared back. It was like looking up into the eye of a corpse. The kind of nothing that has been *arranged*.

"Right," Jude said softly, to no one, because talking to yourself on the Thornfell roads was safer than talking to anyone else. "That's not terrifying at all."

And then the wards went.

It began as a sound Jude felt in their teeth before they heard it—a high, crystalline ringing, like a wineglass singing at the edge of shattering. The border stones lit from within, all eleven of them down the line of gorse, granite glowing the pale blue-white of old enchantment waking up for the first time in centuries. For one heartbeat the whole border was a necklace of burning jewels, beautiful enough that Jude forgot to breathe.

Then it broke.

There was no explosion. That was the horror of it. The wards didn't fight; they simply came apart, all at once, the way frost comes off a window when the sun finally gets its teeth in. Light ran along the line of stones and guttered out, and Jude heard—not with the ears but somewhere deeper, somewhere the gift lived—a sound like a country exhaling after holding its breath for nine hundred years.

The last of the blue light died. The stones were just stones again. Leaning, lichened, ordinary.

Jude stood very still on the wet road with their heart going like a trapped bird and thought: *Every map I own is now a lie. Every toll I've ever paid, every gate I've ever slipped through—gone. There is no border anymore because there is no Cael to be bordered from.*

Their hand drifted, without permission, to the inner pocket where the oldest memory rode against their ribs. The king's confession. They didn't open it—they never opened it on the road—but they pressed two fingers to the pocket the way a gambler touches a lucky coin.

*Whatever just happened,* Jude thought, *you are somehow at the bottom of it. And whatever's at the bottom of it is now worth more than my life.*

They started walking. South, away from the dead border, toward the lowlands, because a thief's first instinct when the world breaks is to be elsewhere when it finishes falling.

They hadn't gone a mile when they met the refugees.

* * *

They came out of the dark like a wound bleeding people.

Jude smelled them first—woodsmoke and wet wool and fear-sweat, the particular sourness of humans who have been walking too long without knowing why—and then the vanguard resolved out of the night: a farmer with a scythe he clearly didn't know how to hold, its blade still caked with chaff. A woman carrying a child who was far too big to be carried, both of them past exhaustion into that glassy, mechanical state where the legs simply do what they're told. An old man leading a horse with no saddle, whispering to it constantly, the way you'd talk to a person.

Dozens of them. Then scores. A column strung out along the road into the darkness behind, shuffling south, away from a border that no longer existed.

Jude stepped off the road onto the verge, hood up, hands visible, the universal posture of *I'm not worth robbing.* The column flowed past like dirty water around a stone.

"Where's everyone headed?" Jude asked the farmer, keeping voice flat and friendly, market-day pleasant.

The man blinked at them. His eyes were the worst part—not wild, not panicked, but *empty in a specific place*, like a room from which one piece of furniture has been removed and the floorboards underneath are lighter than the rest.

"Away," he said.

"Away from where?"

The farmer's mouth worked. Jude watched him reach for the answer and find nothing there—not refusal, not secrecy, but a genuine hole. His brow creased with the effort, and sweat stood out on his forehead despite the cold.

"I don't—" He laughed, short and awful. "We were woken by the bells. Or we weren't woken at all, we just started walking, all of us at once, the whole valley. And I can't remember what valley." His grip shifted on the scythe. "I know I have a wife. I know it the way you know arithmetic. Two and two. I have a wife. But I can't see her face, friend. I can't see her face and I can't remember her name and I'm walking away from wherever she is because everyone else is walking."

"The bells," Jude said carefully. "Whose bells? What village?"

"It had a—" The farmer closed his eyes. Opened them. "It had a name."

He moved on. They all moved on, that terrible slow flood, and Jude stood on the verge and let them pass, cataloguing the way they catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how they kept from screaming. A girl of perhaps ten clutching a wooden bird with one wing. Twin brothers walking in perfect step, neither speaking. A priest in the faded vestments of some order whose symbol Jude knew and whose name—

Whose symbol Jude knew. Whose name would not come.

Jude reached for it and felt the gap like a missing tooth. They knew the order existed. They knew its vestments, its vows, its little brass sun-wheel. But when they tried to say its name, the thought slid sideways, smooth and frictionless, into nothing.

"Cael," Jude whispered.

The word sat in the air. Whole. Intact. Weighted like a struck coin.

The woman with the child passed close enough to hear, and her head snapped around, eyes suddenly ferociously alive. "*What?*" she said. "What did you say? Say it again."

"Cael," Jude repeated. "You're coming out of Cael."

She stared at them, and tears started down her face with no expression attached to them at all, as if her grief had been severed from her ability to feel it. "I don't know what that word does to me," she said. "But it does *something*. Why does it do something?"

"I don't know," Jude said, which was a lie, because they were beginning to know, and the knowing was a cold hand closing around their spine.

The column passed. The road emptied. The night got very quiet again, except for the distant creak of cartwheels and the old man's endless murmuring to his unsaddled horse.

Jude stood alone in the middle of a road that led to a country that no longer existed, and did what they had trained themselves their whole life never, ever to do.

They opened their own memories.

* * *

Here is what Jude Yarrow knew about memory, better than any Warden, any priest, any scholar: it has texture. A real memory lives in the mind like a stone lives in a streambed—settled, worn to fit, threaded through with everything that grew around it. You can feel its edges. You know which parts are yours.

Stolen memories are different. They sit on top. Smooth, self-contained, slightly cool, like river-polished pebbles laid in a row. Jude had spent twenty years learning the difference by touch, the way a blindfolded merchant learns true coin from clipped.

So they went down into themselves, past the neat rows of pebbles, to the streambed beneath. To the memories that were actually theirs—which, if Jude was honest, numbered pitifully few. A mother's hands kneading dough, flour to the wrist. A fence named Maren laughing at them when they were small and full of schemes. The first time they'd stolen a memory, aged nine, terrified and electrified, and the taste of the stolen thing: apples, someone else's autumn, someone else's mother calling them home.

Jude looked for the rest. The border crossings of Cael. The capital's spires seen from the ridge road. The customs officers bribed, the taverns, the names, the faces of the Caelish handlers who'd taught them which wards to slip and which to respect. Twenty years of professional history in the kingdom north of Thornfell's hills.

It should have been a shelf of stones. Familiar, settled, threaded-through.

Instead Jude found the shelf intact—and understood, with a slow vertiginous drop of the stomach, why the refugee woman wept without feeling, and why the priest's order had lost its name.

Everything of Cael was *there*. Perfect. Pristine. Suspended.

And utterly alone.

That was the tell. Memory doesn't survive in isolation; it survives by weaving, by hooking itself into a thousand neighboring threads—the smell of a city hooked to the smell of another city, a friend's face hooked to the season you met them, every recollection buttressed by every other. That was what the erasure had done, Jude realized. It hadn't burned anything out of the world's minds. It had cut the threads. Pulled Cael out of the weave of human remembering the way you'd lift a single stitch from tapestry, leaving the fabric whole and the picture changed.

Everyone's memory of Cael had been unhooked, left floating, and then dissolved like smoke because a memory with nothing anchoring it cannot hold its shape.

Except.

Except in a mind where the threads *were* held—by force, by craft, by twenty years of deliberate, disciplined, unnatural keeping.

Jude's hands were shaking. They looked at them shaking in the starless dark, these hands that had never once trembled on a job, and they understood.

The miller's daughter's kiss: intact. The fisherman's storm: intact. And threaded among the pebbles, unharmed, every scrap of Cael Jude had ever walked through, crossed, robbed, or loved. Because Jude's mind was a vault built to hold memories separate from the self. Sealed. Thread-cut by design. The very thing that made them a monster among decent people—the inability to let what they took become part of them—was the reason the erasure couldn't touch what they carried.

The Wardens—or whoever they were, whoever had snuffed the stars like candles between wet fingers—had unmade a kingdom from every mind in the world.

Except one.

"Oh, you absolute fools," Jude breathed, and didn't know whether they meant the Wardens or themselves. "Of all the thieves in all the world."

Because it wasn't just the scenery they carried. It wasn't just roads and spires and the smell of the capital in autumn. It was the pocket against their ribs. The oldest memory. The one taken from a dying man in a plague-dark chamber while his executioner stood witness—a confession about a Door, and a bargain, and a price paid in the only currency kings have left when everything else is spent.

Jude had stolen it for leverage, years ago, the way you steal anything: because it was valuable and because it was there. They had never listened past the first few minutes. You didn't listen. Listening was how memories got hooks in you.

Now they pressed their hand flat against the pocket and felt, or imagined they felt, the faint warmth of it through the leather. Somewhere behind them, a kingdom's worth of grief was wandering a dark road unable to remember what it grieved. Somewhere ahead, whatever power had switched off the stars would figure out, sooner or later, that one thread remained uncut.

And when they figured it out, they would come looking for the carrier.

Jude turned south and walked, fast now, no longer caring about heel-first silence, mud pulling at their boots. Behind them the border stones leaned in their gorse like headstones in an abandoned churchyard. Above them the sky stayed blind.

*Resolve*, they told themselves, because fear was a luxury and panic was a death sentence on the smuggler roads. *You get to wherever is safest, you find four walls and a locked door, and then—for the first time in your miserable life—you listen to the whole of what you stole. All of it. Even the parts that hook.*

The rain began again, fine and needling, and the road ahead was black as spilled ink, and Jude Yarrow—last keeper of a murdered country—ran south into the dark with a king's dying words burning against their ribs like a coal that refused to go out.
