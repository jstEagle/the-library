## 3. What the King Confessed

The waystation had been a shrine once, before the road it served stopped existing.

Jude found it at dusk, half-swallowed by the hillside, its roof caved in on one side like a skull that had taken a careful blow. The old gods' niches still lined the eastern wall, empty except for rainwater and the nests of small birds. Someone had carved a pilgrim's blessing over the door—*Rest, and be remembered*—but the letters had been worn to shallow ghosts by weather and indifference. Jude read them twice, then made themselves stop reading, because blessings were just words, and words had never once kept anything safe.

They built no fire. Fire drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing Jude could not afford tonight. Instead they wedged themselves into the deepest niche—the one that had probably held a god of thresholds, given its position—and pulled their coat around them like a second skin. The rain came down outside in grey ropes, drumming the collapsed roof, filling the shrine with a sound like distant applause for something terrible.

In the dark, with the cold working into their bones, there was nothing left to do but take out the memory.

* * *

Jude's rules were simple. They had kept them for twenty years, through three kingdoms' worth of smuggler roads: never steal what isn't offered or abandoned; never keep longer than the trade requires; never, under any circumstances, feel what you carry.

The third rule existed because of this memory.

It sat in Jude's mind the way a stone sits at the bottom of a riverbed—heavy, patient, wearing its own smoothness into everything around it. They had carried it eleven years. Stolen it from a dying man in a plague-ward in the city of Cael itself, back when Cael was a name on maps and a kingdom with taxes and borders and a king everyone pretended to love. Jude had been twenty-three and starving, and the man in the bed had been so far gone he'd mistaken Jude for a priest. He had *offered*. That was the thing Jude told themselves, all these years, whenever the memory woke and turned over in the dark. He had opened his mind like a gate and said *take it, someone has to carry it*, and so Jude had taken it, because taking was the only prayer Jude knew how to answer.

Eleven years without looking inside. Eleven years of carrying a sealed jar and refusing to shake it.

Tonight, with the stars missing from the sky and a country erased behind them, Jude finally pried the lid off.

* * *

The memory opened the way all stolen memories do—not gradually, but all at once, as if the intervening years were a door that had only ever been latched, not locked. Jude was suddenly not in a ruined shrine in Thornfell. They were in a bed that smelled of camphor and lilies, beneath a canopy embroidered with stars, and the man dying in that bed was—

Old. That was the first shock. In the histories they taught in schoolrooms across three kingdoms, King Aldric of Cael was always depicted in his prime: broad-shouldered, golden-bearded, a coin-profile of certainty. But the man whose eyes Jude looked through was gaunt and yellowed, sixty pounds lighter than his statues, with hands like folded paper resting on the coverlet. Plague had gotten into him the way rot gets into timber—from the inside, quietly, long before anyone thought to look.

The room was dim. Candlelight moved on the walls. And there was someone else in the memory, standing at the window with her back turned—a woman in armor the color of stormclouds, her sword belted at her hip, her posture the rigid posture of someone holding herself together with discipline alone.

*Serapha,* the king's voice named her, from somewhere far away and deep in the failing body. *Come away from the glass.*

*I would rather watch the road, majesty.* Her voice was low and rough, a voice used to commands and battlefields, softened now into something almost tender. *In case anyone comes who can help.*

*No one is coming who can help.* A breath that was nearly a laugh. *That is rather the point of a confession.*

She did not turn. Jude, watching through the dying man's eyes, saw her reflection in the black glass instead—the face beneath the short-cropped hair, young still, younger than Jude had expected, with a scar cutting through one brow. A face that meant nothing to Jude, eleven years ago, when this memory first settled into them like swallowed lead.

A face that meant everything now, after two nights ago, when a knight with no past had looked at Jude across a firelit clearing and said, *I keep dreaming of a thief in a grey coat. Tell me if I find him, because I think I'm supposed to protect him.*

Jude's hands shook in the dark of the shrine. Inside the memory, the king's chest rattled with each breath, and the plague-light burned feverish along the edges of things, and Serapha stood at the window like a drawn blade waiting to fall.

*You brought me the writ,* she said. *I don't understand it.*

*No.* The king's eyes moved to the ceiling, where the canopy's embroidered stars hung in their stitched constellations. *I don't suppose you do. Sit, Serapha. What I am about to say must be carried by exactly one person in this world, and I have chosen you because you are the only soul in my court who has never wanted anything from me except orders.*

She sat. She sat on the edge of the bed like a soldier taking a knee, and the king gathered himself, and Jude—who had been a thief for six years by then, who had thought they understood every shape a secret could take—felt the memory begin to open like a wound.

*There is a door,* Aldric said, *beneath the White Mountains. Older than the kingdom. Older than the gods your grandmother prayed to. My grandfather found it, and my father sealed it, and I—I have spent thirty years learning why it must never open.*

The candle flames bent, though there was no draft. Jude watched through borrowed eyes and felt the temperature of the room change, felt something vast lean close against the walls of the world, the way a great cat leans against a curtain.

*What is behind it is not a monster,* the king said. *Monsters can be killed. It is older than killing. It is a god, or the thing gods were frightened of before there were gods, and my grandfather did not defeat it—he bargained with it. He bound it beneath the mountain and bound the binding to the land itself. Every stone in Cael, every boundary-marker, every ward and border-charm: they are not protections. They are stitches. The whole kingdom is a bandage wrapped around a wound, and the wound has been bleeding through.*

*Then we reinforce it,* Serapha said. *Give me the order, majesty, and I will—*

*You cannot fight a leak, child.* His hand crept across the coverlet toward hers. *And it has been leaking faster every year. The plague is not plague. You know that now, though the physicians will not say it. It began the season the eastern wards cracked. It is the wound, bleeding through into us. And I have run out of ways to bind it that do not cost more than Cael contains.*

He was quiet a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped to almost nothing, the voice of a man confessing not to a knight but to whatever listened beyond candles.

*There are others. Beyond our borders. An order older than any crown—they call themselves Wardens, and they keep prisons of a kind no king may see. They came to me last winter. They offered me a choice, and I want you to understand that I chose, Serapha. Whatever comes of it, I chose. No one forced this on Cael but its own king.*

*Majesty—*

*A bargain.* The word came out of him like a tooth being pulled. *They cannot kill what lies beneath the mountain. Nothing can. But they can unmake the land it is bound to—unpick every stitch, dissolve every boundary, erase Cael from map and memory and star-chart, until there is no anchor left for the binding to hold to. The god would drift loose in a world that had forgotten how to be anchored to. Adrift, it sleeps. Bound to nothing, it cannot wake. That is their mercy, Serapha. That is the mercy I bought with my kingdom.*

Silence. In the memory, the rain had begun against the high windows, and it sounded exactly like the rain falling on the ruined shrine where Jude lay shivering, and for one vertiginous moment the two rains became the same rain, eleven years collapsing into a single night.

*Erase,* Serapha said at last, and her voice was very flat, which Jude would learn later was what she sounded like when she was closest to breaking. *You mean the people.*

*I mean everything.* The king's breath caught. Held. Continued, because he was still, barely, a king. *Their names. Their graves. Their griefs. They will not suffer, because there will be no they. They will scatter into other lives like water into sand, and no one—not even they—will mourn a country that never was. Except—*

He stopped. And here, in the deepest chamber of the memory, the part Jude had never reached in eleven years of not-looking, the dying man turned his head on the pillow and looked directly at the doorway of the sickroom—at the shadowed alcove where a starving thief crouched behind a screen, unseen, unnoticed, stealing air and silence and, without meaning to, everything else.

*Except,* the king said, and smiled, and it was the worst smile Jude had ever seen, *except whoever carries my confession out of this room.*

* * *

Jude surfaced from the memory gasping, back in their own body, back in their own cold niche in a dead god's house, with rain hammering the ruins and their heart going like a fist on a door.

That wasn't possible. Memories didn't work that way. A stolen memory was a fixed thing, a snapshot, amber around an insect—the thief took what was there and nothing more, and the dead man couldn't have known, couldn't have seen—

But kings died slowly, in rooms full of priests and spies, and maybe Aldric had known about thieves the way he'd known about Wardens. Maybe he had felt the theft like a cool wind through a hot room, and spent his last strength choosing not to cry out. Maybe the whole confession—the Door, the god, the bargain—had been left out on a table like bait, or like a letter addressed *to whom it may concern*.

*Someone has to carry it,* he'd said.

Or maybe that was the fever talking. Jude pressed their palms hard against their eye sockets and tried to make the arithmetic come out clean. Because here was what the memory changed, laid end to end:

The Wardens had erased Cael two nights ago. The stars over the borderlands had gone out; the refugees on the road had walked past Jude unable to say where they had come from, their mouths opening on the name of a home that was no longer anywhere. But Jude still remembered Cael. Every road, every market bell, every smuggler's path through the hills—and now, thanks to eleven years of cowardice, Jude also remembered *why*.

An anchor. Ilse had said it later, though Jude wouldn't hear that word for months yet—but even now, alone in the dark, the shape of it assembled itself with horrible clarity. The Wardens had unpicked the stitches. They had dissolved the kingdom into forgetting. And yet the binding held, because somewhere in the world one vessel of memory remained intact, one small stubborn hoard of a stolen country that the erasure could not reach, because erasure takes what minds remember freely, and Jude had never been given a choice about any of it.

Cael was not gone. Cael was asleep in Jude's head, curled around a dying king's confession like a knife wrapped in cloth.

Which meant that every Warden between here and the coast was hunting a single person. Which meant the hounds on the pass tomorrow would not be searching travelers at random. Which meant—

Outside, down the slope, a sound cut through the rain. Not thunder. Thunder rolled; this rang. A bell, or something shaped like one, tolling from the direction of the pass, and with it a light—grey-white, wrong, the color of moonlight strained through ash—moving among the trees in long unhurried sweeps, the way a man sweeps a lantern across a field looking for a lost coin.

Jude went flat in the niche and stopped breathing, and the memory lay in them like a coal, too hot to hold and impossible to put down.

Wardens. Not sweeping the pass for refugees. Sweeping for a carrier.

The light swung nearer. Somewhere below, a voice like dry paper said words that the rain should have drowned but somehow didn't, words that slid into Jude's ears as if spoken directly into the bone:

*"The anchor remembers. Find what remembers."*

Jude closed their hand around the iron pendant at their throat—an old smuggler's charm, worthless, the only thing they owned that had never been stolen from anyone—and made themselves think, fast and cold, while the light came up the hillside toward the shrine where a dead god's empty niche held one living thief.

They had until the light reached the door to decide whether to run, hide, or feed the hounds something worth finding.

The king had chosen them, if the fever hadn't lied. Fine. Then Jude Yarrow would choose too—right now, in the dark, with the grey light climbing the wall.

The light touched the threshold.
