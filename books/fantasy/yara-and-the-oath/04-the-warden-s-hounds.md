## 4. The Warden's Hounds

The pass had a name once—Caelmen's Throat, the smugglers called it, because it swallowed caravans and coughed out bones—but names had gone strange since the stars died. Now the mountains on either side rose black and toothless against a sky the color of wet slate, and the wind that funneled through carried no smell of anything at all. That was the worst of it, Jude thought as they crouched behind a fallen boulder furred with grey lichen: not the cold, not the dark, but the emptiness where scent should be. Pine. Snowmelt. Woodsmoke from some shepherd's fire. All of it scrubbed away, as though the world itself had been picked clean by hands too thorough to leave a fingerprint.

Jude pulled their collar higher and counted travelers.

The refugee column below moved in the slow, shuffling way that people moved now, when they walked toward a place they could not name and fled a place they could not remember. Forty souls, maybe fifty. A woman with a loom-shuttle still tucked into her belt like she'd forgotten to put it down—or like her hands remembered and she didn't. Two brothers leading a mule whose packs were empty; Jude could see the shape of it, the way the straps hung loose. They were carrying nothing but the habit of carrying.

And at the head of the column, waiting where the pass narrowed to a throat within the throat, stood the Wardens.

Three of them. Grey cloaks that drank the light rather than reflected it, hoods thrown back to show faces that were faces the way a mask was a face—correct in every proportion, wrong in every particular. They hadn't bothered with weapons. Wardens never did. Their hands hung open at their sides, palms forward, patient, and around each one's shoulders coiled something that might have been shadow and might have been smoke and was neither.

Hounds.

Jude had seen them work before, back when the world was whole, back when a Warden's hound was a rare thing seen only at royal assizes, leashed and muzzled and used to sniff out perjury among the great houses. Those hounds had been small. These had grown fat on a country's worth of forgetting.

One of them lifted its head—a motionless grey shape resolving suddenly into a long muzzle and eyes like holes bored through to the other side of the sky—and tasted the air above the refugee column.

Jude went very still.

*Never keep, never feel.* The rules they'd lived by since they were small enough to fit inside a flour barrel. Never keep what you steal longer than you must. Never feel what you steal while you carry it. A memory worn close to the heart leaves fingerprints, and fingerprints are how you get found. Somewhere beneath Jude's ribs, wrapped in seven layers of misdirection and false trails, sat the oldest thing in their hoard—the king's dying confession, heavy as a coin of lead—and somewhere above it, lighter than breath, sat everything else they'd ever taken and not yet spent.

Fifty travelers between them and the Wardens. Three grey shapes. And hounds that could smell a stolen memory the way dogs smelled fear.

*Walk away*, said the part of Jude that had survived twenty years of Thornfell's roads. *There are other passes. There are always other passes.*

But there weren't. Not anymore. Jude had spent three days watching roads unspool behind them like thread pulled from a hem, watching maps go blank at the edges, watching a bridge over the Sorrel River stand in midair with nothing under it because the river had been Cael's river and Cael was gone. Every road east ran through this pass. Every road to the Door of Returning—if the king's confession spoke true, and Jude's skin crawled with the certainty that it did—ran east.

Below, the first traveler reached the Wardens.

*

They worked gently. That was the obscene thing about it.

The Warden at the center raised one open palm, and the woman with the loom-shuttle stopped as if she'd walked into a warm bath. The hound flowed off his shoulder like water pouring uphill and pressed its muzzle to her temple—not touching, never quite touching—and Jude watched her face go through a series of small expressions, the way a pond goes through ripples when a fish rises underneath. Surprise. Concentration. A brief, involuntary smile, as if something pleasant had been lifted out of her. Then blankness, smooth and total, and the Warden stepped back and gestured her through with something almost like courtesy.

She walked on. She didn't look back. She couldn't have said what she'd lost, because losing it had taken the knowing of it along with everything else.

*A memory of her mother*, Jude guessed, watching the second traveler step up. *Or her wedding day. Something soft enough that she won't miss it until winter.*

That was how the Wardens hunted the carrier. Not with blades, not with chains. They skimmed every traveler who passed, lifting the topmost memories like a pickpocket working a crowd, and if one of those skims came back tasting of theft—if it came back with the specific tang of a memory that had been handled, held, carried too long in someone else's keeping—the hound would know, and the hunt would begin, and there would be no walking away then.

Jude's hoard was a market-day of handled things. A soldier's last letter home. A bride's nervous rehearsal of her vows. A child's counting song in a language that had just stopped existing everywhere else on earth. If a hound got within ten feet of them—

The third Warden turned its head.

Not toward the travelers. Toward the slope. Toward the boulder. Toward the grey lichen shaped, if you squinted, like a person trying very hard to be stone.

Jude didn't wait to learn whether it had truly seen them or whether it was only casting its gaze across the landscape the way a fisherman casts across still water. They rolled left, low, letting the boulder take their silhouette, and slid down the scree on their heels with both hands out for balance, stones hissing around their boots, until the slope dumped them onto the goat-track below and the goat-track dumped them into a fold of the mountain where the wind couldn't find them.

Think. Jude pressed their spine against cold rock and made themselves think the way they'd taught themselves to think in Thornfell's back rooms, where a moment's panic meant a knife between the ribs.

Three Wardens at the narrows. Travelers being skimmed one by one, gentle as sheep-shearing. The column would take an hour to pass. Maybe two. Which meant the Wardens would be occupied, which meant the narrows themselves would be guarded by exactly no one, which meant—

Which meant Jude could simply walk past fifty grieving strangers and three monsters and out the far side, if they could get past the hounds.

Because the hounds wouldn't be looking at faces. The hounds would be looking at *weight*. And Jude, carrying the heaviest secret in a dead kingdom, was the single loudest thing for a hundred miles in any direction. Walking past a Warden with the king's confession under your heart was like walking past a sleeping wolf with a bleeding wound. Sooner or later the nose would lift. Sooner or later the eyes would open.

Unless there was something louder nearby.

Jude looked down at their own hands, scarred, steady, and felt the old familiar shame rise up and make room for something harder. Because they knew exactly what they were going to do, and they'd known since the moment the third Warden turned its head, and the fact that they were already grieving for it didn't change a single thing.

That was the truth about survival that no one sang songs about. It wasn't cleverness. Cleverness was just the shovel. The digging was done with something meaner.

*

The decoy had to be good. Half-good would kill them faster than none at all.

Jude knelt in the lee of the rocks and opened the outermost layer of their hoard—the way another thief might open a locked case, fingers moving in patterns learned young, breath slowing until the heartbeat dropped to something the hounds' noses would barely register. The outermost layer was where they kept the cheap things, the things taken for bread-money and passage: a cooper's pride in his first barrel, a widow's memory of dancing, a boy's terror and delight the first time he stole an apple and got away with it.

And one other thing. One thing Jude had been carrying for six days without spending, telling themselves they were saving it, telling themselves it was too valuable to trade, when the truth was simpler and uglier: they'd kept it because it was beautiful.

A memory of first love. Not theirs—never theirs, that was the rule, *never feel*—but a ferryman's, a broad-shouldered man with salt in his beard, remembering a girl on a dock at dusk with her hair full of wind and her hand halfway to his and the whole golden evening balanced on the point of that half-reach like a drop of honey on a blade's edge. Sixteen years old, both of them. He'd thought of it every night for forty years, the ferryman had told Jude, laughing, crying a little, paying gladly. *Best trade I ever made, giving it to you. Some things you carry so long they get heavy enough to sink you.*

Jude had promised themselves they'd spend it well. On food, on a boat, on a bribe that bought a life.

Instead they were going to feed it to a hound.

It took an hour to prepare. Jude built it the way a cook builds a dish designed to be smelled from another room: they layered it, folded it, wove threads of *theft* through it—the ferryman's memory had been freely given, which made it nearly scentless to a hound, so Jude had to season it, had to rub it against the texture of stolen goods, wrap it in the ghost-smell of a dozen memories they'd taken by force or trickery over the years. Then they wrapped the whole thing in urgency and secrecy, the flavor of *this is the treasure, this is what I'm hiding*, until it gleamed in their inner sight like bait made of glass and gold.

Then they wrapped the real cargo deeper. Seven layers down, behind false walls and dead ends, behind a shell of nothing-at-all, the king's confession sank into silence like a stone into deep water. It wasn't perfect. Nothing was perfect. But a hound that found the decoy would stop hunting, because hounds stopped when they were fed.

The hard part wasn't the making. The hard part was standing up, brushing the scree dust from their knees, and walking toward the wolves.

*

Jude came down into the narrows openly, hands visible, gait tired, the picture of one more refugee in a land of refugees. The trick was not to hide. Hidden things drew hounds the way meat drew flies. Visible things, ordinary things, were beneath notice—and Jude intended to be noticed exactly once, at exactly the right moment, by exactly the right nose.

The column had thinned. Most of the travelers had passed through the gauntlet and stumbled onward into the eastern dark, and only a handful remained: the two brothers with the empty mule, an old man leaning on a staff, a girl clutching a doll with one arm missing. The Wardens processed them without hurry. Time, for creatures like these, was probably a different substance altogether.

Ten feet. Jude kept the count automatic, the way they kept all counts, because numbers were safe and safe things kept the fear boxed up. Eight feet. The nearest hound hung from its Warden's shoulders like a grey scarf, inert, and Jude walked past it close enough to see that it wasn't smoke at all up close—it was fine grey filaments, thousands of them, drifting, and each filament ended in something like a barb.

Four feet.

The hound's head turned. Its nostrils—two vertical slits that opened like book-covers—parted, and Jude felt the pull begin: a faint tugging behind the sternum, delicate as a fisherman testing a line. *There you are,* the pull said. *What are you carrying, little thief? What's riding so heavy in that hollow chest?*

Now.

Jude let the decoy surface.

They brought it up the way they'd bring a lamp up a cellar stair—one layer, two layers, the ferryman's golden evening rising through the dark of them, seasoned with theft, dressed in secrecy, glowing—and the hound struck.

It poured off the Warden's shoulders like a sheet coming off a bed, hit the ground without sound, and crossed four feet of stony ground in a way that ground should not permit. Its muzzle touched Jude's temple, and the world went white and rippling, and Jude felt the decoy torn out of them in one long exquisite agony—because that was the thing nobody warned you about, giving up a memory you'd carried a while hurt like a tooth coming out, root and all—and then it was gone, and the hound reared back with the ferryman's dusk-lit dock glowing along its filaments like light through a jellyfish, and it *fed*.

Jude staggered. Caught themselves. Kept the gasp small.

The nearest Warden drifted closer. Up close, its face resolved into details Jude wished it hadn't: pores, faintly. Stubble, faintly. As if someone had described a human face to a sculptor who'd never seen one. "What did the hound find?" it asked. The voice was pleasant. That was somehow worse than everything else.

"Nothing," Jude said. They let their voice crack the right amount, let exhaustion do half the work. "I don't—there was a dock, I think? My wife—" No. Careful. Lies needed bones. "My husband. There was a dock. Was there a dock?" Let the confusion show. Confusion was honest-looking; grief even more so.

The Warden studied them for three seconds that lasted a season. Behind it, the hound swallowed the last bright thread of the ferryman's girl-on-the-dock, and its barbs dimmed, and it settled back onto its master's shoulders like a sated cat.

"A love memory," the Warden said. "Stolen." Its head tilted. "You are a thief."

"I'm a survivor," Jude said, and put every ragged year of Thornfell into the words. "Everyone out here steals or starves. You know that. You made the starving."

Something flickered across the borrowed face. Not anger. Interest, perhaps. Or the memory of interest, retained like a specimen in a jar.

"Yes," the Warden said softly. "We did."

And then it raised its hand—not the open palm of the skimming, but two fingers, pressed briefly to its own lips, and then, before Jude could flinch, pressed to Jude's forehead. Cold. Colder than the rock, colder than the vanished stars. Not a taking. A *marking*. Jude felt it settle behind their ear like a splinter of winter, small and precise and absolutely certain to remain.

"For the next checkpoint," the Warden said. "Thieves pass through many checkpoints, carrier-of-small-things. This will save us time."

*I'm marked.* The words rang in Jude's skull like a bell in an empty church. *Every Warden from here to the edge of the world will know my face, my weight, my shape. You stupid, clever, lucky fool—you bought an hour and sold yourself forever.*

"Thank you," Jude heard themselves say, humble, grateful, dying inside with great efficiency.

"You may go."

Jude went. Past the second Warden, past the third, past the old man with the staff who was weeping silently at whatever soft thing had just been lifted out of him, out through the narrows into the eastern dark where the road—if it still deserved the word—unspooled toward the lowlands and the fog and, God willing, the Door.

They walked fast. They didn't run. Running drew eyes.

Only when the pass was a grey memory behind them and the fog of the lowlands closed around their shoulders like a damp coat did Jude allow themselves to stop, hands on knees, breath sawing. The mark behind their ear pulsed once, twice, like a second pulse learning the rhythm of the first. Cold. Patient. A leash they couldn't see, attached to hands they couldn't fight.

They had escaped. They had drawn no blood, shed none, spent nothing but a dead ferryman's most beautiful evening, and they told themselves that was mercy, that the man would never know, that the girl on the dock would shine on somewhere in a grey cloak's hollow head instead of sinking Jude in some winter to come.

They told themselves a lot of things, bent double in the fog with winter lodged behind their ear.

Then they straightened, because the fog ahead had shifted, and the shift had a shape, and the shape had shoulders, and the shoulders belonged to someone standing very still on the road ahead with a sword at her side and no memory in her eyes at all—just instinct, naked and aimed at Jude like a drawn bow.

"You," the stranger said. Her voice was roughened, unused, a blade needing oil. Her hand found the sword-hilt without looking at it. "I've been dreaming about you."

Behind them, far up the pass, a hound lifted its head and began, silently, to follow the mark.
