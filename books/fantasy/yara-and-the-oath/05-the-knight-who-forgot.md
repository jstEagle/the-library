## 5. The Knight Who Forgot

She woke to crows.

Their voices came first, a ragged argument somewhere above her, and then the smell—wet ash and iron and something sweeter beneath it, like flowers left too long in a closed room. She opened her eyes. The sky was the color of old pewter, low and heavy, and against it the crows wheeled in slow circles over a field of what she took, for one merciful moment, to be dark grass.

It was not grass.

She lay on her back among the dead. Men in grey cloaks, men in the rotted blue of soldiers whose uniforms had been fashionable two generations ago, and others in no uniform at all—farmers with scythes still in their fists, as if they had come out to cut wheat and found war instead. The fog had come in from the east without hurry, patient as tide, and it softened everything: the bodies, the broken wainwheels, the splintered pike staves standing at drunken angles like a forest that had died mid-scream.

She sat up. Her body protested—a deep, symmetrical ache, the ache of someone who had been fighting very hard for a very long time—and something clattered beside her. A sword. A longsword, its grip worn to the shape of a hand, its blade notched but clean. Her hand was on it before she had decided to reach for it, closing around the leather with the easy certainty of long habit.

*Whose habit?* she thought. *Mine?*

She looked at her own hands. Calluses, yes—the right calluses, in the right places, sword calluses layered over older ones. A scar across the left knuckles, white and old. A newer wound along her forearm, stitched with neat soldier's stitches gone brown at the edges. She wore mail under a tabard, and the tabard was blue, and where a sigil should have been there was only a smooth absence, a patch of cloth slightly less faded than the rest, as though an emblem had been unpicked thread by thread by someone who cared about doing it properly.

She searched herself. No papers. No ring. No coin. No name sewn into the collar, which soldiers' collars sometimes bore, though she could not remember how she knew that. Nothing. Just the sword, a whetstone in a belt pouch, half a heel of black bread wrapped in oilcloth, and—

And a face.

Not a face on anything. A face behind her eyes, vivid as a brand: sharp-boned, watchful, young and old at once, with the particular expression of someone who has spent a lifetime deciding whether to run or charm their way past whatever stands between them and the door. Dark hair pushed back impatiently. Eyes that did arithmetic even while they laughed. And a coat—a long thief's coat, dark wool, many pockets, the cuffs frayed from use.

She did not know the name that went with the face. But she knew, with a certainty deeper than memory, deeper than knowing her own name—which she reached for and found nothing, a socket with no tooth, a door with no room behind it—that this person mattered. That this person needed protecting. That protecting them was, if not the whole of who she was, then at least the part worth keeping.

The crows settled on a dead man's chest, three of them in a row, and regarded her with flat gold patience.

"All right," she said aloud. Her voice was rough, unused, but it worked. "All right."

She said other things after that, just to hear them. *My name is—I am a—I come from.* Each sentence broke off at the same cliff edge. She tried prayers and got nothing, tried counting and got as far as numbers go, which is nowhere. The fog thickened around her. Somewhere out in it, water moved—a river, or a marsh draining into one—and the sound was the loneliest sound she had ever heard, though she could not have said what she measured it against.

So she stood up. She picked up the sword because her hand insisted. She wrapped the bread in her cloak because hunger, at least, was honest. And she began to walk toward the west, because when she faced that way the face behind her eyes grew warmer, closer, like a coal held near the cheek—and when she turned back toward the east it cooled.

Instinct, she thought. Or instruction. With nothing else in her head, she decided those were the same thing.

She walked out of the field of the dead, and the crows watched her go without comment, and the fog closed behind her like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

Three days later, in the fog-choked lowlands south of the drowned border, she killed her first man since waking—or the first she remembered killing, which might not have been the first at all.

He came at dusk, out of the reeds along a causeway that crossed a black mere. She heard him wrong—one heartbeat too late—and that frightened her more than he did, because some buried part of her knew she should have heard him ten heartbeats out, should have known his weight and his weapon from the rhythm of his steps. Instead there was only a sudden rustle, a shape resolving out of the murk, and a crossbow coming up.

Her body answered without consulting her. Sidestep, inside the arc, blade drawn and rising in the same motion, the flat cracking against his wrist, then the point at his throat before the bolt had finished burying itself in the mud where she'd been standing. The whole exchange lasted less time than it took to describe, and afterward she stood there breathing hard, staring at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who had borrowed her arms.

"Please," the man said. He was young. Reedy. A poacher's lean frame under a scavenged quilted jack, and eyes red-rimmed from weeping or wind. "Please—I wasn't—I saw your fire, I thought you'd have food—"

"You drew a crossbow on me."

"I draw it on everyone!" It burst out of him, half sob, half laugh. "Everyone draws on everyone now, don't they? Since—" He stopped. His mouth worked. She watched him try to finish the sentence and fail, watched the confusion flood in behind his eyes, the same confusion she had been drowning in for three days, and something in her chest went cold and quiet.

"Since when?" she asked carefully.

"I don't—" He frowned. "There was a country. Wasn't there? There was a country east of the wards, and it's—it's like reaching for a stair that isn't there. Everyone's doing it. Stopping mid-sentence. You can see them fall into the gap." He looked at her tabard, at the smooth unpicked place where its emblem had been, and his frown deepened. "Yours is gone too."

"Do you know what it was?" she asked. "The emblem?"

"No. Do you?"

"No."

They stood a moment in the failing light, two strangers holding the same question between them like something hot. Then she stepped back and let the point drop away from his throat, and he exhaled shakily and did not run, which told her something about how alone he was.

"There's a village," he offered, rubbing his wrist. "Half a day west, if the fog lets you keep a heading. They're taking anyone who can work or fight. People are saying—" He lowered his voice, though there was nothing out in the reeds but waterbirds settling. "People are saying the grey ones did it. The Wardens. That they swept through and unmade a whole kingdom and now they're sweeping again, looking for something. Someone." His eyes flicked to her sword. "You fight like a knight. Whatever you were. They're looking for knights, they say. Pulling memories right out of people's heads on the roadside, hunting for someone they call the carrier. So maybe don't fight like a knight. Maybe don't be what you were."

"What was I?" she asked, and hated how the question cracked in the middle.

But the boy only shook his head, gathered his crossbow, and melted into the reeds, and she stood alone on the causeway with the mere breathing black beside her and the fog pressing in like a second, softer army.

That night, over a fire she built small and screened, she dreamed of the face again—and this time the dream gave her more.

A courtyard. Banners going grey, their colors bleeding out like ink in rain. The person in the thief's coat running—always running in these dreams—and turning once to look back at her, and saying something she could never hold onto, the words dissolving even as she strained for them. But this time, in this dream, she saw the coat clearly. Saw the mended tear at the left shoulder, stitched with red thread that didn't match. Saw the way its wearer moved, light on the balls of the feet, a dancer's economy, a pickpocket's silence. Saw them glance down a certain street in a certain town and choose the alley instead of the gate.

When she woke, she knew two things she hadn't known at dawn. She knew the person in the coat traveled west, toward the mountains, because in the dream they always ran west. And she knew, with a knight's certainty she couldn't source and didn't need to, that the Wardens would find them eventually unless someone faster found them first.

She kicked dirt over the fire and walked west.

* * *

The lowlands taught her patience. Fog was a country unto itself, with its own borders and laws, and it did not surrender a road until you were already standing on it. She learned to navigate by the lean of moss, by the sound of hidden streams, by the way her own unease sharpened or slackened depending on whether she faced the direction of the dream-coal. Twice she passed gibbets with bodies on them, and both times the bodies wore grey, and neither time did she stop, though the second time she paused long enough to close the dead Warden's eyes—an act of mercy she performed before she could ask herself why she owed mercy to the enemy, and which troubled her all afternoon.

On the fifth day she found the aftermath of a search.

A carter's camp beside the road, scattered, still smoking. Three travelers sitting in a row on a fallen log with the blank, patient look of sheep, while a figure in grey walked down the line of them with one bare hand hovering over each bowed head. She watched from cover, thirty yards off through the fog, her breath held so completely that her heartbeat seemed loud enough to betray her.

The Warden was ordinary-looking. That was the worst of it. A tired woman of middle years with ink-stained fingers, moving down the line like a tax collector, murmuring questions. *"Where are you from? Where are you from? Where are you from?"* And with each question her hand dipped, and each traveler flinched, and each traveler afterward sat straighter and calmer and emptier, and when she reached the last one—a girl of perhaps twelve—the girl opened her mouth and said, in a voice wiped smooth as river stone:

"I'm from here. I've always been from here."

"That's right," the Warden agreed gently, and moved on.

Sera's hand had found her sword hilt without her permission. She made herself let go. There was one Warden visible, which meant at least two more she couldn't see; there were three civilians who would die in the first exchange of any fight she started; and there was, beneath all the arithmetic, the simple cold fact that she did not know why she wanted to kill this woman. Instinct said *enemy*. Instinct also said *protect the person in the thief's coat*, and instinct had been the only compass she owned since the crows woke her, and compasses could be planted. Someone had put the face in her head. Someone had stripped her name and her sigil and sent her walking west. If instinct was a leash, then whoever held it might be leading her exactly where they wanted.

But then she watched the Warden pause at the edge of the camp, lift her head like a hound scenting, and turn her gaze—not toward Sera's hiding place, but west. Toward the mountains. And murmur, to no one: *"Close. Getting close."*

Leash or no leash, they wanted the same thing she wanted. Which meant she could not afford caution anymore.

She waited for full dark, and then she went west faster than caution would allow.

* * *

She caught the smell of the fire an hour past midnight: woodsmoke and something cooking, faint under the fog. She circled it the way her body insisted on circling—downwind first, a wide arc, reading the ground—before she finally stood at the edge of a hollow and looked down at the small defiant flame and the figure hunched beside it.

The coat was unmistakable. Dark wool, many pockets, frayed cuffs, the mended tear at the left shoulder stitched in red that matched nothing. The person wearing it was younger than the dream-face, or the dreams had aged them, or grief had done its work quickly in the weeks since the stars went out—they were thin, hollow-eyed, feeding strips of dried fish to a fire that wanted very little excuse to die.

Sera's chest did something complicated and painful. Recognition without memory: her body knew this person the way a lock knows its key, and her mind stood outside the door with empty hands.

She could have walked away. She thought about it honestly. She was a weapon with no owner, a knight with no oath, no king, no country, possibly no name at all—and following this stranger meant following the pull in her own chest into a hunt she did not understand, against enemies she could not measure, for reasons that amounted to a feeling. Every sensible instinct said find a village, take honest work, rebuild from the ground up.

Instead she stepped into the firelight, because the feeling was all she had, and because the person in the coat looked up with eyes that went wide and afraid and—beneath the fear, briefly, involuntarily—glad.

"Don't," the stranger said, scrambling up, one hand already inside that coat, holding something Sera couldn't see. "Don't come any closer. I know what you are. I felt you on the road for two days—you walk like a Warden's hound, you breathe like one—"

"I don't know what I am," Sera said. Her voice came out lower than she intended, rougher. "I woke up five days ago in a field of the dead with a sword and no name and your face in my head. I've been dreaming about you every night since. Running. Always running west." She spread her empty hands, sword still sheathed, palms open in the way that meant *I am not attacking you yet*. "So either I'm your enemy, in which case you should kill me now, while I'm telling you the truth. Or I'm the only person left alive who knows you exist—and I think you know what that's worth, with the grey ones pulling memories out of children on the roads."

The firelight moved on the stranger's face. Fear, calculation, exhaustion—and under all of it, that flicker again, quick as a fish: recognition. Wanting, almost, to believe.

"You're a knight," the stranger said slowly. "Or you were. I can see the ghost of the sigil on your tabard. Someone picked it off." A beat. "Someone picked *you* off."

"Yes."

"And you dreamed of me."

"Every night."

The stranger stared at her for a long moment. Out in the fog, far off, a horn sounded—low, single, patient. The stranger's face went white. "That's the third one tonight," they whispered. "They're sweeping the lowlands. They're closing the net, and they're getting closer, and I have to move, I have to move *now*—"

"Then move," Sera said. "West. Toward the mountains."

"How do you know I'm going west?"

"Because in the dream," Sera said, drawing her sword at last—not to threaten, but simply because the horn had sounded and her hands no longer asked her permission about anything, "you always run west. And I always follow."

The stranger looked at her—at the sword, at the empty sigil, at the open hands—and made a sound that was half laugh and entirely despair. "This is insane," they said. "I don't know you. You don't know you. This is the worst idea either of us has ever had, and I have had some genuinely catastrophic ideas."

The horn sounded again. Closer.

"But you're coming anyway," Sera said. It wasn't quite a question.

The stranger snatched up their pack, kicked dirt over the fire, and for one heartbeat stood close enough that Sera could see the exact color of eyes that had lived behind her eyelids for five nights. Something passed between them that neither of them named—the lock finding its key, or the leash finding its hand, or just two lost things recognizing the same darkness.

"Keep up," the stranger said, and ran west into the fog.

Sera followed. It was, she reflected as the dark swallowed them both, the only thing she knew how to do.
