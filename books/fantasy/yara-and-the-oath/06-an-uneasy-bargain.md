## 6. An Uneasy Bargain

The fog in the lowlands did not move the way fog should. Jude had crossed these marshes a dozen times in their life, back when the lowlands belonged to somewhere, and the mist had always drifted with the wind off the salt flats. Now it sat still as held breath, pooling in the hollows like something poured, and when Jude waded through it, it parted around them slowly, reluctantly, closing again at their heels.

They had been running for two days on stolen sleep and half a heel of black bread. The decoy memory they'd fed the Warden's hound was burning through the creature's mind by now—a false Jude, walking east toward the coast, humming an old threshing song—and every hour it bought them was an hour their legs paid for. The mark the hound had left on their wrist still ached, a grey weal like a brand made of cold. When Jude pressed a thumb to it, they could almost feel the thing thinking beneath their skin, patient as a hook in water.

So they did not hear her until she wanted to be heard.

"Stop."

The voice came from behind and above at once, which should have been impossible, and Jude dropped flat without deciding to, rolling into the reeds, hand finding the knife at their belt out of habit older than thought.

She stood on the drowned causeway above them, and the fog gave way around her like a court yielding to a queen. A knight's sword—though the scabbard was scorched and the crossguard bent—hung at her hip. Her armor was mismatched, salvaged, a cuirass that fit her poorly over a gambeson stiff with old blood that wasn't hers. Her hair was cropped short and wet with mist. And her eyes, when they found Jude through the reeds, were the worst part: not hard, not hostile. *Lost.* The eyes of someone standing at the edge of a country that no longer existed, looking for a border crossing that had been torn up overnight.

"You," she said. "You're the one from the dreams."

Jude stayed low. "There's a lot of me going around lately."

"The thief in the grey coat." She came down the causeway steps without hurry, boots ringing on stone that hadn't rung for anyone else—the fog swallowed even the sound of her own footsteps, but not hers, as though the world had already decided to forget everything except her. "I dream of you three times a night. You're always walking away. And I'm always—" She stopped, jaw working, as if the next word were a stone too heavy to lift. "I don't know what I am. That's why I followed you."

"I didn't ask you to."

"No." Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, more like grief wearing a smile's borrowed clothes. "You didn't. That's how I know it matters."

* * *

They circled each other for an hour before either admitted it was a conversation.

Jude kept moving—along the causeway, into the ruins of a tollhouse whose roof had fallen in, through stands of dead alder—and the knight followed, matching pace, never threatening, never retreating. It was, Jude thought grimly, exactly how you'd herd something you didn't want to spook. Except Jude couldn't tell who was herding whom.

At last, in the tollhouse, with rain starting to needle down through the broken rafters, the knight sat down on a fallen beam across from them, laid her sword across her knees, and said: "Sera."

"What?"

"My name is Sera. Or—I think it is. It came to me this morning, standing in the shallows, and it felt like mine the way a key feels like a door. I don't remember earning it. I don't remember anyone giving it to me. But it's mine." She looked at Jude with that terrible open honesty. "Do you know it?"

Every instinct Jude owned screamed *lie*. Say no. Say you've never heard it, never seen her, never—

"No," Jude said, and hated how carefully the word was placed. "Should I?"

"I don't know. That's the whole of my problem." She turned her hands over, palms up, studying them as if they might confess something her mouth wouldn't. "These hands remember things. This morning I broke down a deadfall that would have taken four men and a mule, and I don't remember learning how. Yesterday I saw smoke on the ridge and I was moving toward it before I'd decided to—toward the danger, not away from it. My body knows a war. I just can't find its name."

The rain thickened. Somewhere far off, too far to be real, a horn sounded—one long note, then silence. Both of them went still. Both of them knew what horns meant now. Wardens swept the passes; Wardens pulled memories out of travelers like splinters; Wardens were hunting something they called *the carrier*, and Jude carried enough to hang a kingdom on.

"You're being hunted," Sera said. Not a question.

"Everyone's being hunted. There's nothing left to be but hunted or hunter."

"That's not what I mean." She leaned forward. "The grey-cloaks came through the lowlands yesterday asking after you. Asking after *the carrier*. They showed a token—a hound's mark, cold iron. I saw it. And I saw the man holding it flinch when he touched it, like it bit him." Her eyes came up. "You're marked. I can see the weal from here."

Jude's hand closed over their wrist, too late to be anything but confirmation.

"So here's what I can't explain," Sera went on, and her voice dropped, roughened, became almost private. "Everything I am says walk away. Find some lord, some garrison, some remnant of an order that will give me a banner to stand under and orders to follow, because that's all I have—instinct and muscle-memory and a name that fell on me like weather. Everything I am says a smuggler with a Warden's mark is trouble wrapped in trouble." She paused. "But I dreamed of you before the stars went out. Before any of this. I dreamed of you walking away from me, and it felt like losing something I couldn't name, and I woke up angry about it, and I have been angry about it ever since, and I do not *know why*."

"Maybe you dreamed wrong," Jude said. "Maybe I'm not worth the anger."

"Maybe." She shrugged, and the motion was so weary and so human that something in Jude's chest cracked along an old fault line. "But I've walked three days through fog and drowned ground on a dream, and I've killed two of the grey-cloaks' hounds on the way—they were pulling a fisherman's childhood out of his skull while he begged—and I find I don't regret it. So either I'm a fool, or I'm a fool who used to be something better."

* * *

Night came down like a lid. They built no fire—fire drew eyes—but sat in the tollhouse dark, sharing the heel of bread and a skin of sour wine Sera produced from her pack, and the silence between them changed texture. It stopped being two strangers enduring proximity and became something more dangerous: two people listening to each other breathe.

It was Jude who broke first. Jude, who had rules carved into them like commandments: *never keep, never feel, take only what you can pay for.*

"Why me?" they asked quietly. "Out of everyone in this ruined world. Why does your gut say *him*? *Her*?" They gestured vaguely at themselves, at the ambiguity they wore like armor. "You don't know me. I'm a thief. I steal the past out of people's heads and sell it for passage and I have never once lost sleep over it. Whatever you're remembering, whatever you're dreaming—it isn't worth your sword."

"Probably not," Sera agreed, and passed the wineskin back. "Tell me about Cael."

The name hit Jude like a slap. They hadn't said it aloud since the night the stars died. They hadn't dared. Saying it felt like spending it.

"I don't know what you mean," they said, too fast.

"You said 'Cael' in your sleep. Twice." Sera's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "And your face, when you said it just now—you looked the way I feel. Like someone said a prayer you'd forgotten you knew."

The rain filled the pause. Jude stared into the dark and felt the oldest stolen memory shift against their ribs like a coal refusing to cool. The king's confession. The Door. The bargain. All of it sitting inside them, the only copy in a world where the country itself had been unwritten—and now this woman, this *nothing-remembering* woman, was asking for pieces of it without knowing what she asked.

If Jude gave her Cael, they gave her the reason Wardens were tearing the roads apart. If they refused, they sent her back into the fog alone, carrying nothing but instinct and a name that had landed on her like a bird that couldn't find its tree.

And beneath both those thoughts, quieter and more damning: *she trusted you before you earned it. Nobody has done that since you were nine years old.*

"There are things I could show you," Jude said at last. Their voice came out strange, rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in years. "Not tell. Show. I have... a trade. I take memories. Keep them safe. Carry them. Some of them are from a place that doesn't exist anymore—which means, right now, I'm the only place it does exist."

Sera had gone very still. "Show me how?"

"It doesn't work like showing. It works like—" Jude searched for words and found only smugglers' words. "Like passing goods across a border. I put it in your hands. Your mind holds it a moment, like a stranger's coat. Then it comes back to me. You'll remember seeing it. You won't get to keep it." They hesitated. "That's the rule. Never keep. Never feel. If I break it for one person, I break it forever."

"And what do you want in return?" Sera asked. No coyness in it. Straight trade, straight terms, the grammar of soldiers and smugglers alike. "Nothing's free. You said yourself you never lose sleep."

Jude looked at the sword across her knees. Scorched scabbard. Bent crossguard. A blade that remembered wars its owner couldn't.

"Wardens are sweeping the high road, the salt road, and probably the river road by now," they said. "They've got hounds that smell memory like sharks smell blood. I'm marked. I'm slow. I need to reach the mountains before the passes close, and I cannot fight my way there alone." They met her eyes. "Your sword. Your back. Until the Door."

"What Door?"

"A door." Jude almost laughed. "That's the other thing I'd be showing you."

Sera considered them across the dark, and Jude watched her weigh it—not cynically, not suspiciously, but the way a person weighs a rope bridge: knowing the far side might not hold, choosing to step anyway.

"Fragments," she said finally. "Pieces of this place. Cael. In exchange for my sword until we reach wherever we're going." She extended her hand, palm open, across the space between them—an oath-offering if Jude had ever seen one, though neither of them said so. "And if your fragments are lies, if you're feeding me a story to use me—"

"They're not lies. They're worse than lies. They're true things that no one else alive remembers." Jude took her hand. Her grip was calloused and warm and steady, and it undid something in them that they'd spent twenty years nailing shut. "That's the bargain. Your steel for my ghosts."

"Your ghosts for my steel." Sera's mouth twitched. "Gods, we sound like a ballad. A bad one."

"The worst ones are always truest."

* * *

Jude had never given a memory to someone who mattered. It was a thought that arrived uninvited, midway through the first fragment, and it shook them worse than the Wardens had.

The mechanics were simple; the feeling was not. Jude cupped their palms around a shard of stolen Cael—the harvest festival in the capital's lower city, lanterns strung between the eaves, cider pressed from apples that grew only in the king's orchard, children dancing on the shoulders of giants—and breathed out, and let it cross the gap between their hands like a moth.

Sera gasped.

Her eyes went wide and unfocused, fixed on a middle distance that contained an entire evening thirty years gone. Jude watched her live it—the lantern light moving behind her irises like sunset under water—and watched her face do things faces weren't supposed to do while borrowing another person's joy. Wonder. Recognition. Grief. She laughed once, softly, at something no one in the tollhouse could see, and the laugh cracked in the middle and became something else, and Jude looked away because watching it felt like theft, and for once in their life they were on the wrong side of the taking.

Then it was done. The shard returned, settling home against Jude's ribs, and Sera blinked in the darkness, wet-eyed, furious, radiant.

"That was—" She stopped. Started again. "Someone's whole happiness. Someone danced at that festival. Someone kissed a girl under the third lantern from the well, and I could *feel* how the cider tasted, and none of it exists. None of the people exist. They were erased and this—" she pressed her fist to her sternum— "*this* is all that's left of them, and it lives in *you*."

"Now you understand what I carry."

"Is that why the Wardens want you?"

Jude weighed the truth and found it too heavy to lift all at once. "Partly."

"You're lying."

"I'm rationing." Jude met her gaze steadily. "There are true things I'll give you when I trust you with them. Right now you've given me a name and a sword-arm and three days of following a dream. I've given you a festival. We're not even yet, knight."

Something moved behind Sera's eyes—hurt, maybe, quickly mastered—and Jude watched her decide to accept the terms, and hated themselves for making her.

"One more," Sera said quietly. "Please. Not tonight. But before we reach the mountains. One more piece of it. I need to know what I'm fighting for, and I think—" her voice caught, steadied— "I think I need to remember what it feels like when a place is worth grieving."

Jude nodded, throat tight. "One more."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The fog lay over the marshes in pale sheets, and far to the north, where the mountains waited, the sky was doing a thing Jude had never seen it do: a faint grey shimmer, like starlight trying to remember itself, like the erasure fraying at its edges—or like something on the other side of the world pressing its face against the fabric of the sky.

Sera saw it too. Her hand went to her sword.

"That's new," she said.

"Yeah," Jude said, and stood, and felt the mark on their wrist pulse once, cold as a knock at a door. "And I don't think we're the only ones who noticed. We move now. While the fog's thin."

They gathered their things in silence, two shadows fitting themselves to the dark, and as Jude stepped out onto the drowned causeway, Sera fell in beside them—close, closer than strangers walked, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched.

Neither of them named it. Neither of them would, for a long time yet.

But when the first hound bayed in the distance—long, cold, and unmistakably behind them—Sera's hand found the small of Jude's back, guiding rather than pushing, and Jude let it stay there, and together they ran north into the greying dark, toward mountains full of snow and secrets, carrying between them one festival, one promise, and the last unburned truth of a kingdom that no longer existed.
