## 7. Roads That No Longer Exist

The road to Carrowmere ended in a wheat field.

Not a ruined road, not a flooded one—ended, the way a sentence ends, with a full stop Jude could feel through their boot soles. One moment the packed clay ran ahead of them, rutted by cart wheels, lined with leaning fence posts; the next it simply was not there, and the wheat stood where it should have been, gold and indifferent, swaying as though it had grown in that spot for forty years.

Sera stopped beside them and said nothing for a long while. She had learned that about herself in the weeks since the lowlands: when the world behaved impossibly, her body went quiet first and her questions came after, arriving like soldiers at a muster, late but in good order.

"It was here," she said finally. "On your map."

"It was on every map." Jude crouched and pressed two fingers to the earth where the clay should have continued. It was loam. Rich, turned loam, smelling of rain and manure, as if a farmer had plowed over the road a generation ago. "I walked this road when I was eleven. There was a shrine at the bend back there. St. Verity of the Broken Seal. Travelers left wax tablets with their debts written on them, asking her to unmake what they owed." They laughed, a small ugly sound. "We asked her to unmake things too, in Thornfell. Different debts."

"And now?"

"Now the shrine is a plum tree." Jude pointed. It stood alone in the field, heavy with fruit no one would pick, because no one remembered why a tree would stand in the middle of a wheat field or whose grave fed its roots. "The road is a field. The field is probably going to be something else by autumn. Cael isn't destroyed, Sera. That would be simpler. Cael is being *edited*."

They broke camp an hour later without discussing whether to go on. There was nothing to discuss. The map in Jude's pack—a real map, ink on calfskin, bought from a dying cartographer in the borderlands who had charged double because he could feel something wrong with his own country and didn't know what—showed the King's Road running straight as a sermon from the pass to Carrowmere, three days' walk, waystations every league. But when Jude held the map up against the horizon, the ink had begun to move. Not smudge. *Move.* The line of the King's Road crawled like a worm on a hook, thinned, and dissolved into blank parchment even as they watched. The cartographer's careful hills remained. His rivers remained. Only the roads were going, eaten outward from the capital like frost retreating off glass.

"Smuggler routes don't get eaten," Jude said, rolling the map away before Sera could see how badly it had degraded. "Know why?"

"Because no one wrote them down."

"Because no one *officially* wrote them down." Jude tapped their temple. "They live here. A Warden can erase a kingdom's records, its wards, its roads, its names on maps. They can pull a memory out of a farmer's head like a splinter. But they can't follow a path that only exists in the minds of people who were never supposed to be on it. My routes were taught to me mouth to ear, in the dark, by a woman who'd have cut my tongue out if I'd repeated them to a tax collector. The Wardens can't erase what they never knew was written." They paused, hearing themselves, and the humor drained out of their voice. "Except they're starting to guess. And I'm the last book left standing."

* * *

They went east by ways that were not roads. Down a dry streambed where the stones still bore the polish of water that had been diverted upstream two hundred years ago specifically so that smugglers would have a private corridor; through a hollow called, in no language currently spoken, the Kettle, where the fog pooled thick enough to drink and the trees grew in a ring around a clearing that had never once been searched; along the spine of a hedgerow older than the kingdom itself, planted by some pre-Caelish hand, hawthorn and blackthorn woven so tight a horse couldn't push through and a man could walk its top like a wall if he'd been trained young. Jude had been trained young. They moved along it now with the particular economy of someone returning to a body of knowledge stored below thought, feet finding footholds their mind had forgotten knowing.

Sera followed. She was better at it than she should have been—her balance was a soldier's balance, her silence a killer's—but she kept looking at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who occasionally did impressive things.

"You're quiet," Jude said, near dusk.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet *differently*. Thinking-quiet instead of empty-quiet."

Sera considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, which Jude had privately begun to find unbearable and wonderful in equal measure. "I keep almost remembering," she said. "Not things. Directions. Like I'm standing in a house with all the doors painted onto the walls, but I know which ones used to be doors. When you took the lowland route today instead of the valley—I knew you were right before I knew why. Something in me has opinions about terrain." She looked west, where the sky was doing something bruised and beautiful over country that had forgotten it was a country. "Do you think I'm from Cael?"

"I think everyone we've met since the stars went out is from Cael, and none of us can prove it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Jude agreed. "It isn't." And then, because the light was failing and because lying to her had started to cost more than the truth: "I think you might be worse than from Cael. I think whatever you were, you were important enough that someone made sure your memory was taken clean. Refugees got smeared. You got scoured. There's a difference, and the difference frightens me."

Sera absorbed that the way stone absorbs rain. Then she said, "You could look."

Jude's whole body flinched. "No."

"You take memories. You've said. You could take mine and see."

"The rule is *never keep*, and the second rule is *never feel*, and taking yours breaks both." They said it fast and flat, a door slammed. "Besides. If I reached into your head, Sera, and found out you were the kind of person who'd have hunted thieves for a living—what then? Would you want to know? Would I be able to look at you the same?" They dropped down off the hedgerow into the long grass. "Come on. We're close to the only door in this country that hasn't been rewritten yet."

* * *

Maren lived under a hill.

That was how Jude had always thought of it, and thinking of it now, walking the last mile through alder scrub with the evening thickening around them, they felt the grief arrive ahead of schedule—the way it always did with home places, waiting for you at the gate before you were ready. The hill was a burial mound from some age before naming, and sometime in the last century a fence named Maren Oduya had tunneled into it and made a house of the dead: a round earthen room, warm in winter, cool in summer, its walls shelved with goods that had never legally existed. Salt without the king's stamp. Silk without a customs mark. Letters of passage in six hands. A fence's paradise, half larder, half library of the untaxed world.

Jude knocked on the turf door in the old pattern—two, pause, three—and heard, from within, the scrape of something heavy being moved aside, and then Maren's voice, cracked as old varnish:

"If you're a Warden, I'm a deaf old woman and this is a root cellar."

"If you're Maren, I owe you eleven coppers and a lie about a horse."

Silence. Then the bolts—three of them, iron, older than the mound—slid back one by one, and the door opened onto lamplight and the smell of tallow, dried herbs, and pipe smoke, and a small brown woman with a face like a walnut and eyes that went from Jude's face to Sera's sword to the road behind them and back again with the speed of a woman who had survived forty years of smuggling by counting exits before greetings.

"Well," Maren said. "Look what the tide brought in." She stepped back. "In. Quickly. You're letting the night see you."

* * *

Inside, the hill swallowed sound. Maren set out bread, hard cheese, a crock of honey gone sugary at the edges, and poured something into clay cups that tasted of smoke and plums and possibly several laws broken in sequence. Sera ate like a soldier, watching the door. Jude ate like someone forgetting to eat, turning the cup, turning the cup.

"You came the low way," Maren said. It wasn't a question. "Past the Kettle."

"The King's Road is gone."

"Gone where?"

"Gone. Eaten. The map is eating itself from Carrowmere outward. The shrine at Verity's Bend is a plum tree. Maren—" Jude set the cup down. "What do you remember about Cael?"

The old woman smoked for a while. The lamp guttered and steadied.

"I remember there's a word I can't say," she said at last. "It's on the tip of everything. I go to name the country this all belongs to and my mouth makes a shape and nothing comes out. I remember taxes, so I know there was a government. I remember a war levy, twenty years back, and widows crying in my doorway, so I know there were soldiers and a cause. I remember the color of a uniform—green and silver—but I can't tell you whose." She tapped ash into a shell. "It's like a house where they've taken the furniture but left the shadows on the floorboards. You can see where everything *was*. You just can't say what it was." Her eyes came up, sharp as a billhook. "And you can. Can't you, child. Look at your face. You're carrying the whole house around in your head, and it's eating you alive."

Jude hadn't known it showed. "Something like that."

"Mm." Maren leaned forward. "I'll tell you what else I remember, and you tell me if it's true, because I've been doubting my own skull for a month now and it's a poor way to live. I remember a thief-child. Little thing, all elbows, came up my hill in the dead of winter with frostbitten fingers and a stolen memory in their pocket they were too soft to sell—some mother's memory, a lullaby, I think, or a kitchen. You wouldn't trade it, wouldn't keep it, just carried it around like a hot coal till I told you: *child, you can't save people by holding their pain for them.* Do you remember that?"

The hill was very quiet. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the alders, and beneath it, deeper, the mound held its old bones and said nothing.

"I remember," Jude said, and their voice came out strange, thin, younger than they were. "You gave me soup with too much pepper in it. I cried into it and pretended it was the pepper."

"It was partly the pepper."

"It was partly the pepper." Jude laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle, and for a moment—one unguarded, unforgivable moment—they were eleven years old under this hill again, orphaned twice over, holding a stranger's lullaby because throwing it away felt like murder and keeping it felt like theft. Maren reached across the shelf of goods and took their wrist, grip strong as a snare, and did not say anything comforting, because Maren had never in her life said anything comforting, and somehow that was the most comforting thing in the world.

When Jude could speak evenly again, Sera was watching them with an expression they refused to examine.

"There were others," Maren said, releasing them, brisk again, businesslike, which was its own kindness. "After the stars went out. Wanderers coming up the low way, lost as lambs. They stand in my door saying *I'm going to*—and then nothing. The place they were going has fallen out of their heads mid-sentence. One woman sat right there and cried because she had a sister and she knew she had a sister the way you know a scar, but the sister's face was smooth water." The old woman's jaw tightened. "Whatever was done, it wasn't finished. It's still happening. The erasing. It spreads like damp through a wall."

"I know." Jude pulled out the map and unrolled it on Maren's table, weighting the corners with salt blocks. In the lamplight the damage was worse than that morning: the roads around Carrowmere had vanished entirely, and the blankness was creeping up the river valleys, and the capital itself—Jude touched the spot where the city's name had been, and the parchment there was smooth and pale as an egg, the ink not faded but *unwritten*, as if the cartographer had never put pen to it at all. "The Door of Returning is somewhere under the capital. I'm the only creature alive who knows that, and I only know it because I stole it from a dying king. Every day I carry it, I'm the anchor that keeps the truth from sinking. And every day, the erasure eats a little more of me to get at it. Maren, when I forget the route past the Kettle, when I forget your face—"

"You won't forget my face." Maren said it with the flat certainty of prophecy. "Faces planted deep come up again. Like weeds." She rose, joints crackling, and began pulling a pallet from a niche in the earthen wall, and blankets that smelled of lavender and decades. "Sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow you'll want your wits, because I'll tell you something else for free, and free is the most expensive thing I sell." She fixed Jude with those billhook eyes. "Three nights ago, grey cloaks came up the valley asking after travelers. Asking after *one* traveler. They paid in coin that had no country stamped on it—blank coin, child, money minted nowhere—and they described you." Her voice dropped. "They knew your trade. They knew what you carry. Someone talked, or something leaked, and the hounds are casting upwind of this hill."

Outside, far off across the darkened fields where a road used to run, a dog began to bark—not the ordinary complaint of a farm dog, but a high, sustained, unnatural keening, the sound of an animal that could smell something its instincts had no cage for.

Maren banked the lamp. "Sleep fast," she said.
