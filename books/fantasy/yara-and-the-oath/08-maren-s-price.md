## 8. Maren's Price

Sera woke before dawn to the sound of the kettle and did not know, for three heartbeats, what the sound was.

That was how her mornings went now: a small eternity of blankness, a body in a strange bed with someone else's calluses on her palms, and then memory of the last weeks arriving like late guests—Jude, the fog-choked lowlands, the bargain struck somewhere between a drawn sword and an offered hand. The cellar house beneath Maren's fence-shop smelled of dried yarrow and lamp oil and old turnips, and it was, Sera had decided, the safest place she had ever slept, though she could not have said why safety should feel like this: low ceilings, dirt floor, a door that opened onto a smuggler's road instead of a keep.

She rose quietly. Jude still slept by the cold stove, curled toward the wall like a child braced for a blow, one hand beneath their coat—the coat with its forty hidden pockets, its hoard of other people's yesterdays. Even asleep, they guarded it. Even asleep, they looked like someone rehearsing flight.

Sera found the kettle and poured. Her hands knew the motions. That was the strangest thing about her condition, she had learned: her body remembered everything her mind had lost. It knew which way to stand in a doorway. It knew to sleep facing the entrance. It knew the weight of a sword the way a riverbed knew water, and when she picked up the blade she'd carried out of the lowlands—a plain soldier's arming sword, nothing noble in it—her wrist rolled through a guard she had no name for, smooth as breathing.

*Who taught me that?*

The question came every morning. Every morning it left without an answer.

Above her, footsteps crossed the shop floor. Maren, up early again, moving crates. The old fence slept four hours a night and claimed sleep was a habit for people who hadn't lived interesting lives. Through the boards Sera heard the grind of a trapdoor hinge, the soft thud of contraband being shifted—salt-wrapped charms, unlicensed maps, things that had belonged to Cael before Cael stopped belonging anywhere.

She climbed the narrow stair with two cups and found Maren in the grey light, small and iron-backed, white hair bound in a knot so tight it pulled her eyebrows into permanent skepticism.

"You let the tea steep too long," Maren said without turning. "You always do. Your thief does it right."

"My thief," Sera repeated, and handed over a cup anyway.

Maren took it, sipped, made a face that conceded nothing. "They're not yours, girl. Nothing in that cellar belongs to anyone. That's rather the point of the trade." But her eyes, when they flicked to Sera, were not unkind. "You dream about them again? The one in the coat?"

"I dream about the coat." Sera looked into her own cup. "A road at night. Rain. Someone running and I'm following, and I don't know if I'm chasing or guarding."

"And which is it, awake?"

Sera didn't answer. Outside, the fog was lifting off the lowlands in slow rags, and the road below the shop lay empty and silvered with dew, and she thought: *empty*. She thought: *why do I keep checking whether the road is empty, why does my spine know the word 'sightline' before my mouth does.*

Maren watched her the way old traders watch scales settling. "Your body remembers your name even if you don't," the old woman said. "I've seen soldiers come back from wars with less inside them than you've got, and more often than not the hands still know the work. It's the heart that takes longer to fill back up." She drained her tea in one swallow. "Now make yourself useful. Wardens were seen at the crossroads yesterday evening. Grey cloaks. Three wagons."

Sera's cup stopped halfway to her lips. The word *Wardens* landed in her like a stone down a well—no splash she could hear, but a long time falling.

"How far?"

"A day's walk, maybe. Maybe less, if they've got hounds." Maren was already moving, pulling back the rug by the hearth, setting her shoulder to the trapdoor. "Which means your thief has until midmorning to decide whether that king-memory of theirs is worth our lives, because those cloaks don't sweep passes for exercise."

Down in the cellar, Sera stood over Jude and watched them sleep another moment—the only moment all day they would look unburdened—and then set a hand on their shoulder.

Jude came awake the way a snare comes tight: instantly, silently, eyes open and fixed on hers, breath held. Then recognition arrived, and something behind their eyes performed the daily labor of deciding to trust her.

"Wardens," Sera said. "At the crossroads."

Jude sat up. Whatever softness sleep had lent them burned off like the fog outside. "Then we're gone before the kettle's cold. There's a shepherd's cut through the hills—two days to Vask if we move at night—" They stopped. Looked around the cellar, at the shelves of salvaged and stolen and remembered things, at the ladder to the shop above where an old woman was already repacking crates with the unhurried efficiency of someone who had been raided before and intended to be raided unsuccessfully again. "Maren won't come."

"Maren won't come," Sera agreed.

"She never comes. She says the roads are for people with somewhere to be and she's spent sixty years making sure she isn't one of them." Jude was dressing as they talked, fingers flying through buckles, checking pockets with a touch so habitual it was nearly tender. "We can't make her. We shouldn't ask."

"You're asking anyway."

"I'm telling you the shape of it so you know I'm not pretending otherwise." They looked up, and for a heartbeat Sera saw the thing under the competence—not loneliness exactly, but its twin, some close cousin of it. "She half-raised me. One winter when I was small and feral and selling memories I was too young to have stolen properly. If the Wardens take her because of what I carry—" Jude's jaw worked. "Go. Get to the road. I'll be behind you before you've counted ten."

Sera didn't go. She stood in the doorway of the cellar and counted ten, and Jude was true to their word, slipping up past her with the coat buttoned to the throat and the ghost of a boyhood in their eyes, and together they emerged into a morning that had gone wrong while their backs were turned.

* * *

The fog had not lifted after all. It had only learned patience.

It stood in the fields now in pale columns, and through the columns, on the road, came the wagons—three of them, grey-canvassed, wheel-rims wrapped in felt so they rolled almost silently, and walking beside them a dozen figures in grey cloaks whose hems moved wrong, as though the fabric were remembering wind from somewhere else.

And ahead of the wagons, low against the ground, came the hounds.

Not dogs. Sera's body told her that first, before thought caught up: these were hounds the way a knife was a sword—reduced, concentrated. They were the color of wet slate and they cast no shadows, and where they walked, the dew on the grass went grey and stiff, like breath frozen on glass.

"In the cellar," Sera breathed. "Now."

"They'll search the shop regardless—" Jude started.

"Then they find us searching it too. Two travelers hiding from a sweep looks like guilt. Guilt gets questions." She was already moving, pulling Jude back down the stairs, her mind assembling the plan with a fluency that frightened her more than the hounds did. *Kill the lamp. Bar the inner door. Put the thief behind me and the shelf between us and the door, sightlines broken, one approach vector, chokepoint at the top of the stairs.* Words she had never learned were marching through her head in ranks, and she let them march.

They killed the stove. They killed the lamp. Grey light seeped down through the floorboards in thin blades, and in that light Sera positioned herself at the stair's foot with her sword loose in its scabbard, and put Jude in the dark behind the yarrow shelves.

"If it goes wrong," Jude whispered, "you run. You don't have anything they want. You're the only person in this cellar who can just *leave*—"

"Sera."

"What?"

"That's my name. You gave it to me. I'm keeping it." She heard them exhale in the dark. "Stubborn knight."

"I'm not—" she began, *anything*, and then the trapdoor above them opened.

Light fell down the stairwell like a dropped coin. A silhouette stood against it, cloaked, and behind the silhouette the shop floor filled with boots—she counted five, six, seven by their sounds—and a voice spoke from above, mild and terrible, a voice used to being obeyed in rooms where people had already lost.

"We're looking for a carrier," the voice said. "Someone down there is carrying more than they should. Come up, and we'll only take what we've come for."

Nobody answered. In the silence Sera heard Maren's voice upstairs, dry as sand: "Take what you like, there's nothing here but an old woman and her turnips," and heard, worse than any threat, the sound of a hound's claws on her floorboards, ticking like rain.

Boots started down the stairs.

Sera drew.

She did not decide to draw; her hand decided, and she followed it down into the motion like stepping into fast water. The first Warden came off the last step into her guard and her pommel met his temple with an economy that appalled her—no anger in it, no fear, just geometry. He folded. The second came low and she checked his wrist against the stair-rail and heard bones give, and the third hesitated at the top, and in that hesitation she understood something that would stay with her the rest of her life: *I have done this many times.*

Not practiced. *Done.* Her body carried whole campaigns in its sinews. Somewhere behind the fog of her missing years, women and men had died reaching for her, and her hands had learned everything their deaths taught.

"Up here!" someone shouted above. "The carrier's below!"

Then the hound came down the stairs, and fighting men were one thing; the hound was another. It flowed around her blade like water around a post, and she felt its attention settle on her—not teeth-first, but *tasting*, the way a memory-thief might taste a stranger's past. Cold crawled up her arm where its nearness passed. It was drinking something from her. Searching her for—

Behind the yarrow shelves, Jude swore softly and did something Sera heard rather than saw: a whisper, a tearing sensation in the air, and the hound stopped. Turned. Its slate head swung toward the shelves, nostrils working, and it lunged—not at Jude, but at the air beside Jude, jaws closing on nothing, on something only it could see.

"There," Jude gasped. "There's your carrier—take it, take it, it's good, it's a wedding, it's forty years of a marriage in Thornfell, take *that*—"

The hound drank. She watched it happen: the grey animal shuddering, swelling faintly, fed. And Jude knelt in the dark feeding their own stolen goods to a monster with tears standing in their eyes, because a decoy was cheaper than a grave, and every memory they gave away was a piece of someone else they'd sworn to carry.

The hound finished. Sat back. Went slack and stupid, gorged.

And then the trapdoor filled with grey cloaks, and the voice at the top said, regretfully, "Mark them both," and pressed two fingers toward the stairwell, and light leapt from them like a snapped wire and struck Sera across the collarbone before she could raise steel.

It didn't hurt. That was the worst of it. It wrote itself into her instead—a cold sigil blooming under the skin of her chest, a brand of attention. *Known,* it said. *Tracked. Findable.* And through the painless burning of it she heard Jude scream her name, heard steel ring as they came up the stairs swinging a lamp-iron with no skill and total commitment, and heard, above everything, Maren's voice cut through the shop like a whip:

"Well," the old woman said, "you've broken my stairs."

* * *

What happened next, Sera's memory kept in fragments, the way shattered glass keeps the window.

Maren, at the top of the cellar stair, hauling the trapdoor shut and dropping the counterweighted shelving unit across it—an old fence's trick, sixty years in the building, tons of salvaged oak and iron coming down on its greased runners to seal the stairway like a tomb.

Maren's voice through the boards, conversational: "There's a back way, children. Behind the salt charms. Move."

Wardens shouting. The shriek of whatever tool they used to eat locks. Sera dragging the dazed first Warden's cloak off him because her hands said *disguise* even as her heart said *run*, and Jude wrenching a shelf of salt-charms aside to reveal a crawl-tunnel into the hillside, breathing earth-smell and old rain.

And Maren, meeting Jude's eyes once through the gap where the trapdoor had been, saying: "Not a word, little wren. Not one word. Go."

*Little wren.*

Sera heard it and did not understand it, then. She filed it the way her body filed guards and distances. Later it would be the most important thing she carried.

They went into the tunnel on their bellies, and behind them the lock-shriek ended in a crash as the Wardens breached the sealed stair, and Maren's shop became noise—overturning furniture, commands, the tick of hound-claws—and Sera, crawling in the dark with Jude's heel in her hand, made herself count the turns instead of listening. Left at the fork. Down where the roots hung. Toward the grey thread of daylight.

The explosion, when it came, came through the ground itself.

Not fire. Something colder: a pressure-wave of *absence*, rolling up the tunnel, and where it passed, the walls went smooth and featureless as river stone, as though the hill were forgetting its own rocks. Sera flattened herself and felt it wash overhead, and heard Jude make a sound she had never heard a person make—a sound like a name being torn out of a book.

When they reached the tunnel-mouth and turned to look back, Maren's shop was not burning.

It was *unhappening*. The roof settled inward with no smoke, no heat, timbers going grey and then translucent and then simply agreeing to have never been. The Wagons on the road stood empty. The Wardens were already walking away, unhurried, their work complete, and where the shop and the woman in it had been there was now a smooth-scoured hollow in the hillside and a signpost pointing nowhere, and Sera understood, with a horror that bypassed her mind entirely and lived in her knees, that the Wardens had not killed Maren.

They had erased her. The way Cael had been erased. The way the stars had gone out. *Quarantine,* some buried instinct whispered. *They quarantine what they fear.* And Maren—whoever she had been beyond this hollow, whatever sixty years of fences and favors and half-raised thieves she had filled with it—was now a gap in the world wearing the shape of a garden.

Beside her, Jude did not scream. That was somehow worse. Jude stood in the tunnel-mouth with the morning wind pulling at their coat and made no sound at all, and their face did the thing Sera had seen on soldiers' faces in dreams she couldn't remember: the closing. The door shutting on the burning room so the rest of the house could go on standing.

"She called you wren," Sera said quietly. Because it was all she had.

Jude flinched as if struck. "She shouldn't have said that. Not out loud. Never out loud." Their voice was flat, scraped hollow. "That name—it's mine. The real one. From before I ever stole anything. Nobody living knows it. Nobody was supposed to—" They stopped. Pressed the heel of a hand against their eyes. "She gave it to us like a purse of coins. So we'd know the payment she made. As if we could spend it."

"Sera—"

"Don't." They stepped out of the tunnel-mouth onto the open slope, putting distance between their body and her comfort, and stood looking down at the hollow that had been a home. "Don't tell me it wasn't my fault. I know the arithmetic. I've done it a hundred times on a hundred roads—one death against what I carry, one life against a kingdom, and the numbers always say walk on. That's the rule. Never keep, never feel, never stop moving." A breath that shook despite everything. "But she *knew* me, Sera. Before all of it. She knew me when I was small enough that someone might have loved me for free. And she died—or worse—for a secret I forced her to keep by existing on her doorstep." Their hand found the coat, the pockets, the hoard, gripping it like a drowning sailor grips spar. "Every one of these is somebody's life I took to survive. And today the bill came due and it wasn't my name on it."

Sera came and stood beside them. She did not touch them. Some griefs you cannot lean on a shoulder for without spilling.

"The sigil they put in me," she said instead. Practical. Present tense. The language her body spoke best. "They marked me. Which means they can find us—which means we need distance and we need it now, or her price buys nothing."

Jude looked at her then, and something in their expression broke sideways—from grief into its ugly cousin, the one that sharpens. "Vask," they said. "Two days' hard walk. There's a guide in Vask who knows the way to the Door. And there are brokers there who buy what Wardens sell, which means somewhere in that city is the answer to why they're erasing everyone who ever knew me." They started down the slope without waiting, coat-hem snapping in the wind, and threw the last words back over their shoulder like a thrown knife:

"And when I find out who gives the orders, Sera—I'm going to feed every one of them to their own hounds. Memory first. Name last."

Sera followed, the cold sigil burning under her collarbone like a compass needle, and behind them the hollow in the hillside lay open to the sky, holding nothing, mourning nothing, having never been.

But she kept the words. *Little wren.* Tucked where no Warden's hound could drink them out.

Some prices, she was learning, you paid forward.
