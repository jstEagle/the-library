## 9. The Memory Market

Vask announced itself by smell before it ever showed its walls: tallow smoke and brine from the drying flats, horse lather and hot iron, and beneath it all a sweetness Jude had never learned to stop hating — the smell of memories going stale, like fruit left too long in a closed room.

They came down off the salt road at dusk with Sera half a step behind, the way she'd learned to walk now, close enough to matter and far enough to deny. The city rose out of the fog in tiers, lamplit, its walls patched with the masonry of six conquered centuries. No king's banner flew over it. No banner at all. Vask belonged to no crown because Vask had learned early what crowns cost, and had decided the price was always paid by someone else.

"Smaller than I imagined," Sera said.

"You imagined it?"

"I don't know." Her hand moved to her sword-hilt, that habitual touch she performed instead of scratching an itch she couldn't name. "I think I dreamed it once. A city on a hill, and bells."

"There are no bells in Vask. They're illegal. Something about how they carry on the water and let assassins time their work." Jude said it lightly, watching her face. "You couldn't have dreamed it. You don't remember anything."

"That's what dreaming is," Sera said, and walked on down the road before Jude could decide whether to laugh or be afraid.

* * *

The market opened at moonrise, because memory kept badly in daylight. It filled the old salt-warehouse district like floodwater — lanterns strung on rigging lines between the warehouses, barges moored bow-to-stern forming floating stalls, and everywhere the crowd, moving in the slow reverent shuffle of people in a temple. Which was what it was, Jude thought. Vask's one true church. You could buy bread anywhere. Here you bought the years you'd lost, or never had, or wished you hadn't.

The rules were older than the Wardens, older than Cael itself, maybe. Jude knew them the way they knew the weight of a coin: you never touched a memory bare-handed; you never bought grief without tasting it first; you never, under any circumstance, sold the same memory twice, because the second buyer would find the edges worn and come looking for the seller's throat.

Jude had lived by a fourth rule of their own devising since childhood. Never keep. Never feel. Take what you need, pass it on, walk away clean.

They were about to break all three.

"Stay close," they told Sera. "And whatever you see being sold — don't taste anything I don't hand you. Some of these brokers lace their stock with longing. You buy a summer afternoon and wake up three days later still trying to live in it."

"And you know this how?"

Jude didn't answer. Some questions were just accusations wearing polite clothes.

The first broker they found was a fat man named Doss who dealt in ordinary stock — first kisses, dead mothers' voices, the smell of a house you grew up in. He sat behind a table of glass vials arranged in straw like eggs, each one holding a curl of light that moved when you looked at it, the way a sleeping animal breathes.

"A guide," Jude said, without preamble. "Someone who knows the White Mountains. Someone who can get me to the Door of Returning and doesn't ask why."

Doss's eyes went flat and careful. "No such person sells themselves in this market. People who go to the Door don't come back to complain about the service."

"Then someone who knows someone."

"Everyone knows someone." He rubbed his thumb along a vial, absently, the way other men touched lucky coins. "But knowing costs. What are you carrying?"

Jude set two vials on his table. The first held a fisherman's memory of his daughter learning to swim — warm, sunlit, harmless as bread. The second was older, a farmer's recollection of planting an orchard he'd never live to sit beneath. Good stock. Clean-edged. Jude felt nothing looking at them, which was the point, which had always been the point.

Doss tasted the orchard, eyes half-closed, tongue pressed to the roof of his mouth where the memory dissolved. "Sweet," he admitted. "Bit rustic. Who's your guide, then? Ask for the Ferryman. Salt Gate side, blue lamp. Tell him Doss sent you and that you pay in years, not coin." His gaze slid past Jude then, and sharpened. "Though if it's rare goods you're after—"

"I'm not after rare goods."

"Shame. Because there's rare goods tonight." Doss leaned forward, lowering his voice into the register brokers used for the really valuable cruelties. "New consignment came in yesterday. Warden-grade. Provenance sealed. There's pieces in it that shouldn't exist anymore — things from a country that isn't on any map."

The warehouse noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater.

"Cael," Jude said.

Doss shrugged elaborately, a man declining to say aloud what everyone already knew. "There's a stall at the far end. Red lamp. Go look, if you've the stomach. And if you've the purse." He smiled with too many teeth. "Warden provenance doesn't come cheap."

Jude walked away at a normal pace. It took everything they had.

* * *

Sera caught up at the edge of the barge-row, where the crowd thinned and the canal water carried reflections of lanterns like scattered coins. She'd been watching them, Jude realized. Not the market. Them.

"What did he say?" she asked.

"He says there's a guide. Blue lamp, Salt Gate."

"That's not all he said."

Jude looked out at the water. Somewhere behind them the market murmured on, hundreds of small transactions, years changing hands, lives being edited by strangers. "There's stock for sale tonight that shouldn't exist. Memories out of Cael."

Sera went very still. He remembered that stillness from the lowlands, from the night the grey-cloaked riders passed within thirty feet of their fire and Sera had stood unmoving as a fence post until the hoofbeats faded. It wasn't courage exactly. It was the stillness of something built for war, holding itself back. "Memories of Cael," she repeated slowly. "But everyone's gone. Everyone forgot. The country's erased."

"Not from my head." Jude heard their own voice come out harder than intended. "And apparently not from wherever this consignment came from. Which means somebody kept pieces. Somebody *sold* pieces. And there's exactly one person in the world whose memories of Cael should exist outside their own skull."

"You."

"Me."

They stood in silence while a barge slid past, its deck stacked with sleeping passengers, each one clutching a little clay token stamped with the broker's mark — proof of purchase, redeemable at waking.

"If those memories are real," Sera said at last, carefully, "and they're yours... then whoever's selling them took them from you."

"They can't have taken them from me. I'd know. Taking feels like—" Jude stopped. They had been taken from, once. Only once, when they were small, by a fence who'd decided payment was owed. They remembered the absence better than the thing absent: a hole shaped like a mother's voice, humming. "I'd know," they said again, less surely.

"Unless," Sera said, and didn't finish, because neither of them needed her to.

Unless you can steal from a thief. Unless the rules of the market only protect the people who play fair. Unless someone had been walking behind Jude for years the way Jude walked behind the world, lifting things through pockets no one checked.

The red lamp burned at the far end of the row, past the food stalls and the money-changers, in a corner of the market where the lanterns hung lower and the crowd moved quieter, as though approaching something holy or diseased.

"Wait here," Jude said.

"No," said Sera.

"It might be dangerous."

"Yes," said Sera, and fell into step.

* * *

The red-lamp stall was smaller than the others — a single barge, a single table, a single broker. She was old, thin as winter reeds, wrapped in shawls the color of dried blood, and she did not call out her wares. She simply sat, hands folded, letting the stock speak for itself. And the stock spoke. Even at ten paces Jude could feel it, a pressure against the mind like weather changing.

Twelve vials. No more. Each one labeled not with a description but with a number, and beneath the numbers, in a fine clerk's hand: *provenance verified.*

Jude approached alone. Sera stayed at the rail of the neighboring barge, arms folded, radiating the patient menace of a drawn bow.

The old woman's eyes were milk-pale but they tracked perfectly. "Buying or selling?" Her voice was dry paper.

"Browsing."

"Nobody browses here." She gestured at the vials with one knuckle. "These are not summer picnics, girl. These are artifacts. Every piece verified intact, unedited, straight from the source. Collectors have been fighting over lot seven since sundown."

Jude looked at the vials. Read the numbers. Stopped breathing.

Lot four glowed a particular gold — the gold of late afternoon through dust motes, of a specific kitchen in a specific house in a town called Harrow's Bend in a country that no longer existed. Jude knew that gold. Had lived inside that gold. It was the afternoon their mother had taught them to gut a fish on the back step, laughing because Jude had named the fish first, given it a name and a whole biography, and then cried when it came time to use the knife.

Jude had never sold that memory.

Had never traded it, never spent it, never even shown it to another living soul, because it was one of perhaps five things in their hoard that were actually theirs — not stolen, not borrowed, but grown in their own ground. The rule was *never keep*, and they had broken it exactly five times in their life, and this was one, and here it was in a glass egg under a red lamp with a price chalked beside it in a currency of years.

The floor of the world tilted very slightly. Jude gripped the table's edge.

"You recognize it," the old woman observed. Not a question. Brokers of her grade missed nothing; it was how they stayed alive and rich.

"No," Jude said.

"Liar." She said it without heat, almost kindly. "You've gone white as the canal foam. That's recognition. That's *grief*." She leaned forward, and the shawls shifted, and underneath them Jude saw the faint grey collar-mark of a former Warden acolyte, half-faded, half-scraped away. "Interesting thing about this consignment," she continued conversationally. "It came with documentation. Sealed provenance, like I said. And the provenance lists the original owner."

The market noise receded further. Somewhere Sera shifted at the rail.

"Does it," Jude managed.

"Mm. Lot four's owner is listed as J. Yarrow. Thief. Age nine at time of extraction." The old woman folded her hands again. "Extraction date's the part that should interest you, dear. Because it wasn't last month. Wasn't last year." Her milk-pale eyes held Jude's with terrible gentleness. "It's dated fourteen years ago. Whoever pulled this piece has been carrying it — keeping it fresh, keeping it *pristine* — for fourteen years. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the market to be ready. For Cael to fall. For a certain kind of buyer to come looking." She tapped the chalked price. "Or perhaps just waiting for you. Consignments like this aren't sales, child. They're *messages*. Someone wants you to know they have more. Someone wants you to follow the trail."

Jude's hands had gone cold. Fourteen years. Fourteen years ago they had been nine years old, newly run, newly gifted, newly hungry — and someone had been standing behind them even then, in the dark of some Thornfell alley or waystation, lifting pieces through pockets they hadn't known they had. Every job since. Every trade. Every clean getaway. All of it watched. All of it *shopped from.*

"How much for lot four?" they heard themself ask, because the alternative was to scream.

The old woman named a figure. It was obscene. It was most of what Jude had carried out of Maren's burning cellar and half of what remained of the fisherman's warmth and every clean year they'd banked for the crossing. It was designed to strip them bare.

"That's not a price," Jude said. "That's a hook."

"All prices are hooks." The old woman smiled with genuine warmth, which was worse. "But you'll pay it. Or you won't, and you'll spend the rest of your short hunted life wondering who's holding your childhood. Either way, someone profits. That's Vask."

Jude counted out the vials one by one onto the scarred wood. Watched fourteen years of savings leave. Felt nothing, because feeling was the rule they'd never broken, and breaking it now, here, would kill them faster than any Warden.

The old woman wrapped lot four in black cloth and slid it across. Then, as Jude's fingers closed on it, she added, softly:

"The rest of the consignment's spoken for. Sold this morning, all eleven remaining lots, to one buyer. A collector. He keeps rooms above the Chandler's Exchange, in the tall house with the grey door. They call him Ossian." She paused delicately. "He deals in lost things. And he asked — asked specifically, mind — to be told if anyone came asking after lot four. Especially anyone matching your description."

Behind them, at the rail of the neighboring barge, Sera had gone rigid, her hand locked white-knuckled around her sword-hilt, staring at the wrapped vial in Jude's hand as though she'd seen a ghost wearing Jude's face.

"Sable," she said — no, not *Sable*, the word came out wrong, warped, dredged up from somewhere below her missing years — "your name. Your real name. I don't know how I know it, but I know it, and that woman just said it out loud, and I've never heard it before in my life."

The red lamp hissed. The canal carried the lantern-light away in pieces, like a country dissolving.

Jude looked down at their own childhood, wrapped in black cloth, and understood that the trap had not been ahead of them on the road at all. It had been waiting here, patiently, for fourteen years — and it had just sent its invitation.

Above the Chandler's Exchange, in the tall house with the grey door, someone was waiting to talk about everything Jude Yarrow had ever stolen.

Including, apparently, things Sera had no right to remember.
