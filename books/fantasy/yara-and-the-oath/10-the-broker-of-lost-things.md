## 10. The Broker of Lost Things

The free city of Vask did not believe in silence. Even at the hour when honest folk slept, its streets murmured—dice rattling in back rooms, a fiddler sawing away somewhere above a tannery, water running through channels cut into the cobblestones like veins. Sera walked those veins and let them carry her toward the harbor district, where the fog came off the black water thick as wool and the lamps burned halos around themselves.

She had left Jude sleeping in the loft of a chandler's shop on Salt Row, wrapped in a sailcloth blanket, one hand curled near their temple the way people curl their hands when they dream of holding something. She had watched them for too long before she left. That was the trouble with her now: her memory was a house with all the furniture missing, and every new thing she noticed filled an empty room and made it feel lived in.

*Protect the person in the thief's coat.* That was the only instruction she had ever been given—the one instinct that had survived whatever unmade her—and she was beginning to suspect it was not enough. Because protecting Jude meant knowing what Jude was carrying. And because there were things she needed to know about herself that no one else would tell her.

Ossian would tell her. For a price.

She found the broker's door where Ilse's directions said it would be: behind a fishmonger's stall that sold nothing but eels, down a stair that smelled of brine and old smoke, through a corridor lit by lamps turned low so the flames looked drowned. The door itself was iron-banded oak, and it opened before she knocked.

"You've been followed for three streets," said a voice from within, dry as paper. "Come in anyway. They'll lose you here. Everyone loses everything here."

* * *

The room beyond was larger than the building should have allowed, and it was full of light—not lamplight, not daylight, but a pale, sourceless glow that rose from hundreds of glass vials racked along the walls from floor to ceiling. Each vial held a shimmer, a slow silver turning, like moonlight stirred into oil. Memories. Sera's skin prickled. She had seen them before, in the market stalls of Vask's upper city, hawked beside spices and silks, but never this many, never arranged with such reverence. It was not a shop. It was a reliquary.

Ossian sat behind a desk of black wood, a small man gone soft at the edges, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of weak tea. He wore a coat that had once been expensive and now was merely comfortable. He gestured to the chair opposite him without looking up from the ledger he was annotating.

"The knight," he said. "Or the woman who used to be a knight. I'm never sure which to charge."

"Charge me for both." Sera stayed standing. "You have memories that belong to Jude Yarrow."

"I have memories that belong to *me*, girl. I paid for them." He closed the ledger at last and looked up, and something in his gaze sharpened from merchant to appraiser. "But yes. I know whose they are. Or rather—I knew, before I bought them. Now they're just stock. Beautiful stock. Childhood stock is rare. Most thieves sell other people's pasts; almost no one sells their own."

"They didn't sell them." Sera kept her voice level, though her heart had begun to knock against her ribs. "Jude has rules. *Never keep.* Someone took these."

"Someone did." Ossian leaned back until his chair creaked. "And now we arrive at the interesting part of the evening, which I confess I've been looking forward to since my runner told me a swordswoman with no name had started asking after Yarrow's memories. Do you know who I bought them from?"

"Tell me."

"A Warden." He savored the word, letting it sit between them like a coin on a tongue. "A defector, technically—though defection implies loyalty abandoned, and the Wardens are loyal to nothing but the erasure. Grey cloak. Grey eyes. Hands that shook, though I pretended not to notice. She brought me eleven vials. Eleven pieces of a thief's own life. And she named her price very specifically." He smiled, and it was a thin, unpleasant smile. "Not coin. Information. She wanted to know if anyone had come asking after the Door of Returning."

Sera's hand drifted to the hilt at her hip—an old habit, or perhaps not a habit at all, perhaps a memory wearing the shape of a habit. "And what did you tell her?"

"That no one had." Ossian spread his hands. "I am a broker, not a martyr. But then, a week later, someone did come asking. You. So you understand my position."

"I understand that you're going to tell me about those vials," Sera said quietly, "and then you're going to give them to me, because the alternative is that I report to certain parties in this city that your inventory includes stolen goods taken by the Wardens, and Vask's guilds take a very old-fashioned view of Warden property."

For a moment the only sound was the faint silver whisper of the vials, hundreds of memories breathing in their glass. Then Ossian laughed—a short, genuine bark of surprise.

"Oh, you're good. You're better than good. Where did you learn to negotiate like that?" His smile faded into something more careful. "That's a real question, knight. Not a rhetorical one. Because I've met a hundred soldiers in my time, and none of them stood the way you stand. None of them held a threat the way you hold a blade—like they'd held a thousand before and expected to hold a thousand after." He tilted his head. "You really don't remember anything? Your name, your service, your—"

"My *nothing*. Say what you know."

He studied her for a long moment, and Sera felt the appraisal settle over her like cold water. Somewhere in the racks behind him, a single vial flared briefly brighter and dimmed, as if the memory inside had dreamed.

"All right," Ossian said. "Here's what I know, freely given, because I suspect you'll learn it worse elsewhere. The defector—Ilse, she called herself, though I doubt it's her true name—brought me the vials along with a letter of provenance, which is unusual for Wardens, who generally don't document their crimes. The letter described each memory. Date, subject, condition." He pulled a slim folio from the desk drawer and set it on the wood between them, not opening it. "I read it. All of it. And there was an entry near the end that wasn't about the thief at all. It was about the person the thief was traveling with. A description. A name."

The knocking in Sera's chest became a drum.

"It said," Ossian went on, softly now, watching her face the way a man watches a fuse burn, "*Serah Vaile. Knight-Captain of the King's Justice. Executed the sentence upon His Majesty Aldric IV in the plague winter. Present at the binding. Recommended for the erasure detail. Status: memory-wiped, deployed.*"

The room went very far away and very close at once. Sera heard her own breath, ragged, and the whisper of the vials, and underneath both a sound like the sea heard through stone—her blood, her pulse, the tide of a body that remembered what the mind could not.

"No," she said. It came out small. She made it come out again, louder. "No. I protect them. Whatever I was—I—" Her hand was on her sword now, actually gripping it, though she had no enemy to draw against except the words hanging in the drowned lamplight.

"Knight-Captain of the King's Justice," Ossian repeated, merciless as a ledger. "You were the king's executioner, Serah Vaile. And the king died in the plague winter. Before the stars went out. Before the country vanished." He laced his fingers together atop the desk. "I don't know what Jude told you about themselves. Thieves rarely tell the whole truth—it's bad for trade. But consider: why does a memory-thief carry a king's confession? Why do the Wardens hunt them across half a broken map?" His tea-colored eyes were almost kind. "Consider what it means that the king's executioner woke with no memory and one instinct—to find the thief. Consider whether that instinct was yours, or whether it was *installed*."

"*Installed*—"

"Wardens do not make mistakes, knight. When they wipe a mind, they leave exactly what they mean to leave. If you woke dreaming of Jude Yarrow, it is because someone wanted you to find Jude Yarrow." He leaned forward. "You may be a leash. You may be a knife. You may be bait. I've seen all three dressed as allies."

Sera turned and walked to the nearest rack of vials because her legs demanded motion or she would shatter standing still. Up close she could see the memories turning, each one a little world: a wedding, a funeral, a first snowfall, a face. Silver, patient, alive. One of them—she knew it without knowing how she knew—held a boy running through orchard rows with rain in his hair. Another held a woman's hands teaching small fingers to pick a lock. Another held a king, dying, saying words that had unmade a kingdom.

*You were the executioner.*

She tried to reach for the fact the way she reached for everything now, groping in a dark house for furniture that wasn't there. Nothing came. No face of a king. No plague winter. No blade in her hand descending—or refusing to descend. Only the word *executioner*, sitting in her empty rooms like a stranger's trunk, already unpacked.

"I don't remember doing it," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"Of course not. That was the point of wiping you." Ossian had risen and come to stand a few paces off, hands folded, a merchant again, selling her the truth one piece at a time. "But here is what I can offer, since you came all this way. I don't deal in secondhand rumor. The provenance letter names the memories. Eleven vials. I will show you the rack. You may look at any of them—but looking costs, and taking costs more, and I warn you now, knight: some of what the thief lost is not kind. People are not kind, especially to themselves, especially in childhood. You may learn things about your companion that change what protecting them means."

"Show me."

He hesitated. "There's one more thing. The last item in the provenance letter. It isn't a memory at all." He drew out a final sheet, unfolded it, and read aloud, and his voice went quiet and strange, the voice of a man reading scripture he didn't believe:

"*Item twelve: one sealed writ, recovered from the royal vault at Cael before erasure, bearing the king's seal and the following order: Should the Door be threatened, find Serah Vaile. She will know what she swore.*" He lowered the page. "It's unsigned by any Warden hand. Which means either the Wardens kept it as insurance—" his eyes lifted to hers "—or someone inside the erasure wanted you to exist. Wanted you *here*. Standing in my shop, asking my questions."

The drowned lamps guttered. In their racks, the memories turned and turned, patient as stars.

Sera thought of Jude asleep in the chandler's loft, hand curled near their temple, dreaming of holding something. She thought of the way trust had risen in her like water finding its level, unearned, inexplicable, *installed*—and for the first time since waking on that battlefield with nothing but fog and purpose, she was afraid. Not of the Wardens. Of herself. Of being a key someone else had cut, fitted to a lock she couldn't see.

"Keep your writ," she said at last, and was proud that her voice did not shake. "Keep your vials. But you'll answer one question honestly, Ossian, or I swear by whatever I used to swear by, this whole reliquary comes down around your ears."

"Ask."

"When Jude learns what I was"—the word tasted of ash and iron—"what do you think they do? Run? Or—"

"Ah." Ossian tucked the writ back into his folio, and his expression, for just a moment, lost all its merchant polish and became simply tired, simply human. "That depends on the thief, doesn't it. And on whether love survives being a lie." He moved to the door and held it open onto the brine-and-smoke corridor, the murmur of Vask beyond. "They're awake, by the way. Have been since sundown. Followed you here from Salt Row and waited outside the eel stall for the better part of two hours." He nodded past her, down the dark of the stairs. "So I suppose you won't have to imagine the conversation at all."

At the mouth of the alley, half-dissolved in harbor fog, stood a slight figure in a thief's coat, hood back, face pale as the vials' silver light. Jude Yarrow looked at her across the distance—at the knight who had sworn to protect them and might have been built to destroy them—and Sera saw the exact moment the question formed in their eyes, sharp and small and terrible:

*What did he tell you?*

She began to walk toward them. There was nowhere else in the world to go.
