## 11. What You Did Before

The safehouse above the dyer's shop smelled of vinegar and old wool, and Jude could not stop looking at Sera's hands.

They had walked out of Ossian's parlor in silence, down the crooked stairs of the Broker's Row, through streets where lamplighters were lighting lamps against a dusk that no longer belonged to any kingdom. Sera had said nothing. That was the worst of it. She had taken the truth Ossian handed her like a coin she'd been owed all along, and she had said nothing, and now here they were in a room with one candle and two chairs and a silence wide enough to drown in.

Jude had spent a lifetime reading hands. Smugglers' hands, liars' hands, grieving mothers' hands at the memory markets where they sold their dead children's first steps for bread money. Hands told you what mouths would not.

Sera's hands lay open on her knees, palms up, as if she were waiting for someone to put something in them. Or take something out.

"You knew," Jude said. It came out flat, ugly. "Some part of you knew."

"I remembered nothing." Sera's voice was steady the way a drawn bow is steady. "I still remember almost nothing. But when he said *executioner*—" Her fingers curled, slowly, into fists. "My body knew. The way it knows a sword grip. There was a room. I can almost—there was a window with rain on it, and a bed, and someone breathing wrong, and I was standing very straight because I thought standing straight was kindness."

"Stop." The word tore out of Jude before they chose it. They stood, and the chair went over behind them, and the candleflame shivered. "Stop talking."

Sera looked up at them. In the candlelight her face was the face from the lowlands, from the road, from every mile since the fog had given her to Jude like an answer to a question Jude hadn't asked. She didn't flinch. That made it worse.

"He was dying," she said quietly. "Wasn't he. Your king. That's what you carry."

"You don't get to call him that." Jude heard their own voice crack and hated it, hated how much of them it showed. "You don't get to claim him now. You don't get to stand in his deathbed and call it mercy. I've *seen* his last hour, Sera. I've carried it in my skull for six years. I know what his breath sounded like. I know what his hand weighed when he gripped my wrist—he gripped my wrist, did your body remember that too? Did it remember that he begged?"

"Begged for what?" And there it was, finally, a fracture in her steadiness—a hairline crack running through the knight. "Tell me. If I did it, I have the right to know what I did."

Jude laughed, and the laugh was a bad sound, a wound-sound. "The right. Gods. You want rights. Fine." They crossed the small room in three strides and stopped close enough that she would have to lean back to look up at them, and she didn't lean back, and somehow that was the thing that broke something loose in Jude's chest, hot and awful.

"The plague," they said. "The Grey Sweat. The one that took a third of Cael in a single summer. You remember it? No, of course you don't—you don't remember anything. Well. I do. I stole a physician's memory off a caravan in the Year of Ash, and I have seen the plague wards from the inside. I have seen what the Sweat did. And I have seen—" their voice dropped, and they hated that too, hated the reverence creeping back in even now, even furious, "—what the king found when he followed it upstream."

Sera had gone very still. "Upstream."

"The Wardens made it." Jude spat it like a pit. "Grey-cloaked servants of the realm, keepers of its wards, and they brewed the Grey Sweat in a cellar under their own citadel and loosed it on their own country. Why? Because of what the king had bound. Because the binding was failing, and the king wouldn't renew it—the price had gone too high—and so the Wardens decided the bargain needed *encouragement*. A kingdom drowning in its dead will agree to anything. Even him. Even the Door." They were shaking. They could feel it in their jaw, their teeth. "He found out. He confronted them. And they told him: open the Door willingly, or we will make your people beg you to. And then they made them beg anyway, just to be thorough."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the dyer's dog barked twice at nothing and went quiet.

"So he asked for a sword instead," Sera said. Barely a whisper.

"He asked for the only person in Cael whose oath outranked the Wardens' chain of command. His Kingsblade. The one soul sworn to him personally, past law, past council, past everything." Jude's hands had closed without their noticing; they opened them deliberately, finger by finger, the old discipline, never keep, never feel, but the discipline slid off this like rain off oilcloth. "And you did it. You stood straight because standing straight was kindness, and you did the one thing he asked of the whole world, and then the Wardens erased everything so that no one would ever know what they'd done. So that his confession would die with the country. Except it didn't. Except it landed in *me*, a gutter-thief on the wrong road, and I have spent six years carrying a dead man's last breath like a coal I couldn't put down, and now I find out the hand that held the sword belongs to the woman sleeping three feet away from me."

The silence after was enormous.

Sera rose. Slowly, the way soldiers rise—no wasted motion, weight balanced—and for one black moment Jude thought: *she'll draw on me,* and some animal part of them braced for it, welcomed it even, because pain with edges was simpler than this.

She didn't draw. She stood in front of Jude with her empty hands at her sides and her eyes wet and blazing at once, and she said, "Say the rest of it."

"There isn't more."

"There is. I can see it sitting behind your teeth. Say it, thief. Whatever verdict you've reached, say it to my face or stop sharpening it in the dark."

Jude wanted to hit her. Jude wanted to walk out into the Vask night and sell every memory of her face to the first broker with coin and never feel this again—that was the old way, that was the rule, *never keep, never feel*, and look where keeping had gotten them. Look where feeling had gotten them. Standing in a dyer's garret wanting to strike a woman who had killed the only king Jude had ever mourned.

"You should have known," they said instead, and their voice came out low and shaking. "Whatever they took from you, whatever they burned out—your hands remembered the oath. Your body stood straight for him. Some part of you has been walking toward that room since the day you woke in the mud. And you let me believe you were just—lost. Just a lost knight who happened to dream of me. You let me trust you."

"Yes," Sera said.

The simplicity of it staggered them. She didn't defend herself. She didn't explain. She stood in the wreck of everything Jude had believed about her and said *yes* like a door opening.

"I let you trust me knowing I might be the worst thing you'd ever meet," she said. "Because I had nothing else to give you. Do you understand what it is, waking with no name and no past and one certainty in your bones—one, Jude, one—that the person in the thief's coat must not come to harm? I have followed that certainty through fog and battlefield and a city full of men selling pieces of you, and I would follow it into the White Mountains tomorrow, and if you tell me the certainty was a lie the Wardens left in me like a hook in a fish's mouth, then I will cut it out and follow you anyway. On choice alone." Her jaw tightened. "But I will not apologize for killing him until you tell me what he asked for. Not the manner of it. The words. What did the king say, at the end, to the thief holding his wrist?"

Jude turned away. Went to the window. Below, Vask glittered its lawless glitter, free city, memory market, a place that had never belonged to Cael and so had never been erased, and Jude thought about how the whole world could forget a country and the country would still be true.

"He said," Jude began, and stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, because some things deserved quiet. "He said, *I am the lock and the key and the door is me.* He said the god underneath—the thing the founders bound beneath the capital, the thing the whole kingdom was built on top of like a lid on a well—it spoke to him through the binding. Offered him things. Showed him his dead. And he was so tired, Sera. Six years I've carried this and I still don't know if what he felt at the end was wisdom or just exhaustion wearing wisdom's clothes. He said, *if I open it, we are lost. If I hold it, I am lost. Find me a third road.* And then the Sweat was in his lungs and there wasn't time for third roads, and he looked at you—" Jude's throat closed. They made themselves finish. "He looked at you and he said, *you were never meant for this. Forgive me for making it yours.*"

Behind them, a sound. Small. He would have called it nothing, once—never keep, never feel—but Jude knew the sound a person makes when grief arrives in a body that has no room prepared for it.

When Jude turned, Sera had both fists pressed against her mouth, and she was crying the way Jude imagined she'd done nothing else in her whole armored life: silently, furiously, like a breach in a wall being held by main strength.

"I remember the rain on the window," she said through her hands. "That's all. Rain on glass and the weight of the sword and thinking—I remember thinking *let this be the last kind thing anyone does in this country.*" She lowered her hands. Her eyes were terrible and clear. "They took even that from me. They took the one act of my life that mattered and left me a shell that dreams of strangers."

"They took it from everyone." Jude heard their own fury change key—not cooling, never cooling, but turning, the way a river turns at a rock, finding new direction without losing any of its force. "That's the design. Don't you see it yet? They didn't kill him twice. They killed him and then killed the meaning of him. Every person in Cael died twice—once in flesh, once in memory—and the second death is the one that lets the Wardens sleep at night. As long as no one remembers what they did, they did nothing. As long as the confession burns out in one thief's head, the plague was an act of gods and the erasure was mercy." They laughed again, and this time it wasn't a wound-sound; it was colder, and better. "Except they missed. Six years of hounds and brokers and grey cloaks, and they missed, because a cutpurse on the Thornfell road steals the way other people breathe."

Sera wiped her face with the heel of her hand, a soldier's gesture, rough and unceremonious, and something in Jude's chest turned over at the sight of it and they let it turn.

"You're not going to sell it," she said. "The confession. You could. Ossian would pay a kingdom's ransom. Buy yourself a life in some city that never existed on any map worth having."

"I considered it," Jude admitted. "For about ten seconds in his parlor, while he was telling you what you were. Ten whole seconds of freedom, laid out like a banquet." They shook their head. "But here's what I know about stolen things, Sera. I've handled ten thousand of them. A stolen memory doesn't heal anyone. It sits in you like a stone in a shoe—weight without ground. The king's confession wasn't given to me. It fell to me. And a thing that falls to you isn't property. It's a task."

"A task," Sera repeated.

"The Door of Returning opens for the anchor. That's me—that's what Ilse will confirm, if we ever find her, that's what the High Warden's hounds have been hunting. And everyone wants me to believe the only choice is open it or don't. Restore Cael or keep the proof. But that was never the real choice." Jude crossed back toward her, and this time when they stopped close, it wasn't fury that held them there. "The real choice is whether the truth comes back *whole*. Whether Cael returns remembering the Grey Sweat and the cellar under the citadel and the king's last words—or returns the way the Wardens wrote it, clean and noble and lied about. The Door gives back what the anchor holds. And the anchor holds everything. His confession. The physician's plague-wards. All of it."

Understanding moved across Sera's face like weather. "You're not deciding whether a kingdom lives. You're deciding what it wakes up knowing."

"I'm deciding whether justice gets a body," Jude said. "Law won't touch the Wardens—they *are* the law, or they were, before they unmade the thing they served. But truth outside the law, truth loose in ten thousand returned memories, truth that every midwife and miller and soldier of Cael wakes up holding—*" they spread their hands, thief's hands, quick and scarred, "*—that, no grey cloak can pull. You can't arrest a country for remembering."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then she did something Jude had never seen her do: she knelt. Right there on the dyer's floorboards, one knee down, head bowed, in the old form—the oath-form, the one Jude had seen ghosts of in a hundred stolen ceremonies and never expected to witness living.

"That's not necessary," Jude said, uncomfortable.

"It is." She stayed down. "I broke an oath six years ago and didn't know it. Let me make a new one while I know exactly what it costs. Jude Yarrow—thief, carrier, anchor—I can't swear to restore your kingdom. I don't know yet if it should be restored; I've met one village of survivors who say some doors stay shut for good reasons. But I swear this: the king's confession reaches the living. Whole. Whatever it unmakes, whoever it burns—including me, including you. Truth first. Everything else second." She lifted her head, and the candle caught the wet tracks on her face and the iron underneath them. "On my name. Which I'm told I'll be getting back."

Jude stood there a long moment with their heart doing something complicated and unwelcome in their chest. Then they reached down and took her forearm—the soldier's grip, wrist to wrist, the one her body would know even if her mind didn't—and pulled her up.

"Then we need Ilse," they said. "Tonight, before the Wardens trace Ossian's ledger to this address. She's the only one who knows how the ritual actually works, and if I'm going to pour a kingdom through my own skull, I want the instructions from someone who's read them." They were already moving, snuffing the candle, gathering the thin bundle of their belongings with hands that had stopped shaking somewhere in the last hundred heartbeats. "Vask's undercity has a gate the fence-folk call the Weep. Ilse will be near it. Defectors always are—they go where the water runs dark."

Sera buckled her sword on in the darkness, and the soft click of the strap was the loudest thing in the room.

"And if the High Warden finds us first?" she asked.

Jude paused at the door, one hand on the latch, and smiled in the dark where no one could see how little it reached their eyes.

"Then he'd best hope he catches me before I remember everything," they said. "Because I'm starting to think the most dangerous thing in Cael isn't the god under the capital." The latch gave. Night air poured in, cold and smelling of dye-vats and rain. "It's a thief with nothing left to lose and a country's worth of receipts."

Below them, the streets of Vask waited—and somewhere in their depths, moving fast, grey cloaks were already asking questions in the language of pulled memories, and the first of them had just learned the word *Yarrow*.
