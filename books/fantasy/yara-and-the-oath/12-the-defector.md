## 12. The Defector

The undercity of Vask had a smell all its own—tallow and wet stone and the sweet rot of dye that had soaked into the foundations before anyone living was born. Jude had been down here twice in their life, both times to trade, both times with a knife loose in their sleeve, and the third time felt no different for being the most important.

Sera walked half a step behind them, close enough that her shoulder nearly brushed theirs at every turn. She hadn't spoken since they'd left Ossian's house. The revelation sat between them like a corpse neither would look at directly: *she had been the king's executioner.* Jude could feel her silence working at it the way water works at stone, patient and without mercy.

"Left," Jude said quietly. "At the cistern."

"I see the mark." Her voice came out flat as slate. "Chalked arrow, merchant's code?"

"Smuggler's. It means *the buyer is armed.*" Jude turned down a passage so low Sera had to duck, its ceiling weeping moisture that caught the light of their shuttered lamp and threw it back in trembling threads. "Which is why you're going to let me do the talking."

"I was going to suggest that myself."

"You've been suggesting it with your posture for an hour." They paused where three tunnels met, listening. Above them, muffled through forty feet of Vask's bones, the city went on with its evening—cart wheels, a drunk singing, someone beating a rug with the steady violence of the truly furious. Below, there was only the drip of water counting out seconds no one wanted counted. "Ilse Venn. Warden defector. The broker's note said she'd meet us at the drowned chapel if we came alone."

"Sara—" Sera stopped. Corrected herself, jaw tightening. It was still happening, he knew; she kept reaching for a name that wasn't hers and finding nothing there. He watched her hands flex once at her sides, sword-calloused fingers closing on air. "We are not coming alone. We're coming together. That's not the same thing, and if this is a trap, I want it said plainly: I will kill my way out of it."

"I know," Jude said. "That's why I brought you."

* * *

The drowned chapel had been some free-city god's once, back when Vask had gods instead of markets. Now its nave stood flooded to the ankles, black water perfectly still except where their steps broke it into rings, and the altar at the far end held nothing but a single candle burning behind glass, sheltered from drafts that didn't exist.

The woman waiting for them sat on the altar steps like a pilgrim too tired to pray. Grey cloak, but worn wrong—the hood pushed back, the clasp undone, the whole garment hanging on her like something shed rather than worn. She was perhaps fifty, hollow-cheeked, with the grey eyes Jude had learned to dread in the pass above Thornfell. But hers were different in one crucial way. Wardens looked *at* people the way appraisers looked at goods. This woman looked at them the way a surgeon looks at a wound she made herself.

"You brought the knight," she said. Not a question.

"The broker talks too much," Jude said.

"He does. It's how I afford him." Ilse Venn rose. Up close she moved like a person carrying something fragile and heavy, arms held slightly away from her body. "Serahilde Vane. Captain of the King's Third. Sworn sword of Cael." She inclined her head—not mockery, Jude realized. Something older and more formal. A salute to a rank that no longer existed in any memory but theirs. "You won't remember me either. Few did. I kept the ledgers."

"What ledgers?" Sera's hand had gone to her sword hilt. Jude couldn't blame her. Being named by a stranger when you had no name of your own—it was like being handed a mirror that showed someone else's face.

"The erasure ledgers." Ilse turned and lifted the candle-glass, and the light swung across the flooded nave, catching things Jude hadn't seen at first: chalk marks on the pillars, hundreds of them, columns of tallies in a cramped precise hand. Names, maybe. Or numbers standing in for names. "Every ritual has a cost, and every cost gets recorded. I was the one who wrote them down. Someone had to, or the Wardens would have convinced themselves they'd done nothing at all."

Jude's mouth had gone dry. "You're going to tell us what happened to Cael."

"I'm going to tell you what happened *to you.*" She set the candle down and faced them, and Jude saw that her hands were shaking, very slightly, and that she was letting them shake. "Because you've been walking around thinking you're a thief who stole something valuable and got lucky. You think you're hiding from fate." Her voice dropped, and the water carried it, soft and enormous, into every corner of the chapel. "Thief. You're not hiding from anything. You *are* the fate."

* * *

They sat on the altar steps, the three of them, because Ilse insisted and because her legs gave out halfway through standing anyway. She talked with her eyes closed sometimes, the way people recite things they've repeated to themselves ten thousand times in the dark.

"The king bound something under his capital. You know this much—you carry his confession, don't you? I can smell old memory on you the way a hound smells blood." She didn't wait for confirmation. "What the confession doesn't say—what the king himself may not have fully understood—is what binding requires. A god can't be tied with chains or wards alone. Chains rust. Wards fail. What holds a thing like that is *attention.* Witness. A country full of people who remember it exists, remember what it did, remember the price of the binding—that remembering is the lock itself. Every mind in Cael was a tooth in the mechanism."

"So the plague," Sera said slowly. "The one the king asked me to end him during—"

"Was engineered. Yes. By us. The Wardens weren't destroyers, not at first. We were wardens in truth—a brotherhood sworn to maintain bindings across generations. And the king's binding was failing, because the kingdom was forgetting. Wars, plagues, prosperity—people stop telling the old stories. Each generation the lock grew weaker. So a faction decided on a radical maintenance." Her laugh was terrible and brief. "If attention is the lock, they reasoned, then make the attention absolute. Erase everything else. Unmake Cael entirely—every map, every memory, every border—and leave behind a single wound in the world's skin. One place where something was taken. People can't stop wondering about a hole. Curiosity is the strongest attention there is."

"But it needs an anchor," Jude said. Their own voice sounded far away. They already knew. Some part of them had known since the stars went out, since they'd found themselves the only mind in the world carrying Cael whole. "A country unmade needs somewhere to be written while it's unwritten. Somewhere to keep the original."

Ilse opened her eyes and looked at them with an expression that was almost tender and almost pitying and entirely without comfort.

"An anchor of memory," she said. "Living, mortal, mobile—so the unmade country could be carried away from its own location, its own borders, its own grave. Someone who could hold a nation inside a single skull and walk." She spread her hands. "Do you understand yet, Yarrow? The ritual needed a vessel. And the ritualists needed someone whose gift was taking memories that were never given—who had spent a lifetime learning to hold other people's pasts without drowning in them. They didn't find you after the fact. You were *selected*. Years ago. Every smuggler road you ever worked, every trader who sold you a memory cheap—they were all arranged. Your whole career was a harvest."

The water dripped. Somewhere far above, the drunk had stopped singing.

"They fed me memories," Jude said. Their voice came out strange, thin, stripped of the smuggler's easy cadence. "My rules. Never keep, never feel. I thought those were *my* rules. I built my whole life on—"

"On discipline we installed. Yes." Ilse didn't flinch from it, which Jude hated and respected in equal measure. "You were the best vessel we ever prepared. And then the night came, and the ritual ran, and the anchor took the weight." She leaned forward. "But here is what the High Warden will not admit, even to his own order, and why he hunts you with hounds instead of simply killing you. The anchor isn't just storage. The Door of Returning opens only for the anchor. Only the one who carries Cael can choose to bring it back—or let it stay dead forever. You're not evidence they need destroyed, thief. You're a key with legs. And keys get *used.*"

* * *

Sera had gone very still. Jude knew that stillness now, had learned its grammar in weeks of travel: it was the stillness of a soldier doing arithmetic about a battlefield.

"If they capture the anchor," Sera said, "they open the Door themselves. Force the choice."

"There's a rite for compelling it. It kills the anchor in the process—the country returns, and the vessel burns out like a wick." Ilse shrugged, and the gesture was horrifyingly casual. "Acceptable losses. They've already erased millions. One more thief won't trouble the ledger."

"And if the anchor dies another way?" Jude asked. "If I just—if the last stolen memory burns out, like it's been doing—"

"Then Cael is gone past any returning. The Door seals. Whatever's bound under the capital stays bound by nothing but habit and distance." Ilse studied them. "Which brings us to the question you've been circling since you walked in, and I'll answer it before you have to ask, because I've watched enough people drown in it. *Yes*, the king's confession says the Door must never open. *No*, I don't know if that's the whole truth. I kept ledgers, not councils. But I'll tell you what made me defect." She pulled back her sleeve.

Her forearm was a lattice of scars, fine and white, deliberate—rows and rows of small cuts, each one precise as script.

"Every name I recorded for the erasure, I cut into my own skin afterward. When the ritual took everyone's memory of them, the ink in my books went blank too—did you know that? Ink forgets along with the world. Chalk tallies vanish off pillars. So I wrote them where the ritual couldn't reach." She rolled the sleeve down with careful, ritual slowness. "Four thousand names, thief. I remember none of them. I only remember that I promised. That is what I am now: a woman covered in promises she can't read."

She rose, joints cracking, and retrieved the candle.

"The High Warden reached Vask two days ago. He knows the word Yarrow now—he'll know your face soon, and after that he won't need hounds, he'll need armies. You have until dawn, perhaps less, before this undercity stops being a maze and becomes a trap." At the edge of the candlelight she paused, grey eyes finding Jude's. "One more thing, and then I'll show you the way out that even the smugglers don't use. The rite that compels the Door—it requires the anchor alive but *empty*. Everything you've stolen, everything you carry—the first thing they'll strip is the king's confession. Do you understand what that means? The confession isn't just proof. If the living are ever to renegotiate the binding—to hold the god with something better than a dying man's guilt—then the confession has to survive you. It has to get *out*. Into many minds. Into the world."

"That's not possible," Jude said. "Stolen memories die with me. They always—"

"Everything dies with its keeper," Ilse said. "Unless it's told. You've spent your life trading other people's pasts like coin. Consider, before dawn comes, what it would mean to spend one."

She guttered the candle between finger and thumb, and the dark of the drowned chapel closed over them like water.

* * *

They climbed for a long time. Ilse led them up service stairs slick with mold, through a wine cellar whose racks held bottles older than kingdoms, out through a coffin-maker's yard where the shrouds hung drying like pale ghosts in the windless dark. She spoke little, pointed much, and at the final door—a grate into a dyer's alley two streets from their lodging—she stopped.

"They'll come at first bell," she said. "Wardens don't raid at night; superstition. First bell, when the honest are awake and the dishonest are asleep." Her hand found Jude's shoulder, briefly, and the grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "Anchor. Key. Thief. Yarrow. Whatever you are—you were also a child on a smuggler road once, before we found you. Try to remember that the harvest wasn't all they planted. Something in you grew wild anyway. I've watched you walk. Wild things don't grow in vessels."

Then she was gone, down into the dark, a grey shape folding itself into greyer shadow, and Jude stood in the coffin-yard with the shrouds stirring overhead and Sera silent beside them.

"She lied about one thing," Sera said at last.

"Oh?"

"She said you were selected years ago. That your whole life was arranged." Sera's voice was low, rough. In the dark Jude couldn't see her face, only the outline of her against the lesser darkness, and the gleam of her eyes. "Maybe true. But I've fought beside you. Bled beside you. Watched you give Maren's memory back to the road like burial rites." Her hand closed on Jude's arm, hard, deliberate. "Whatever cage they built you in—you're the one who decides what walks out of it. I've known soldiers shaped entirely by their orders. You're not that. Don't you dare start believing you are."

Jude wanted to answer. What came out instead, barely voiced, was the truth underneath everything:

"I'm the only copy of a whole country, Sera. Four thousand names on her arms. Millions in my head. And I'm *burning.* Every day I wake up and something's dimmer. What kind of fate is that? What kind of key melts before the door?"

"The kind that walks faster," Sera said.

Above the coffin-yard, past the shrouds and the chimneys and the sleeping city, the sky was clear and starless as it had been every night since the world lost Cael. Jude looked up at it for a long moment—at all that smooth, empty black, the sky of a world with a hole in it—and felt the stolen hoard shift inside them like tide, like snow-load, like something vast turning over in shallow sleep.

First bell was three hours away. The High Warden was already in Vask.

"Get your sword," Jude said quietly. "We're leaving tonight."
