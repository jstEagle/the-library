## 13. Ambush at the Salt Gate

The bells of Vask rang wrong.

Sera had learned the city's language in three days—the market bells that meant rain coming off the flats, the harbor bell that meant a ship flying no colors, the small cracked bell by the tanneries that some child rang for no reason but joy. She knew none of this from memory. Memory was a country she'd been locked out of, its windows shuttered, its doors painted over. She knew it the way she knew how to hold a sword: her body remembered what her mind could not.

So when the bells began to ring—wrong, all wrong, high and fast and overlapping like birds startled from a single tree—her hand was on her hilt before her mind finished forming the question.

Jude looked up from the map they'd been hunched over in Ilse's borrowed room above the salt-seller's shop. Candlelight moved on their face, and Sera saw the moment they understood it too. Thieves heard bells the way knights heard them, apparently. As weather. As warning.

"That's not the alarm pattern," Jude said quietly. "That's every bell at once."

Ilse was already moving. The defector had a way of moving that Sera distrusted on principle—efficient, silent, a woman built out of exits—but there was no time to distrust anyone now. "Salt Gate," Ilse said, pulling back the shutter an inch. Beyond it, Vask's crooked streets were filling with grey. Not fog. Grey cloaks. Wardens, dozens of them, flowing down from the ridge road like water finding its level, and among them moved shapes that made Sera's stomach clench: the hounds. Not dogs. Something older than dogs, something with too many joints and eyes like wet slate, noses that drank memory instead of scent.

"They found us," Jude said. Not a question. Their voice had gone flat, the voice of someone folding up everything fragile inside themselves and putting it away where it couldn't rattle.

"They found *me*," Ilse said. "You two can still—" She stopped. Looked at the map. Looked at Jude with an expression Sera couldn't read, guilt or arithmetic or both. "The White Mountain road goes through the Salt Gate. It's the only way out that isn't watched yet. If I go down and make noise in the undercity, draw them toward the harbor—"

"No," Sera said.

"Sera." Jude's voice, careful.

"You said it yourself. They want the carrier. They want—" she gestured at Jude, at the hoard of a dead kingdom carried behind those tired eyes, "—that. Ilse is the one who betrayed them. They'll peel her open like a letter."

"And if they catch all three of us," Ilse said, already wrapping her hands in strips of dark cloth, "they get everything. The anchor, the defector, and the king's own executioner." Her eyes flicked to Sera, and there it was again, that word Ossian had given her like a knife between the ribs: *executioner*. A title for a woman she didn't remember being. "I've been running from them for six years. I know how to run. Let me do the thing I'm good at."

Sera wanted to argue. She searched herself for the argument and found instead something stranger: certainty. Not trust—she still did not fully trust Ilse, might never—but a bone-deep knowledge that this was how it would go, that some plans are simply true before they happen. She'd felt it once before, she realized. Standing somewhere green, somewhere with banners, watching a king she loved decide to die. She couldn't see the scene. She could only feel its shape, like a footprint left in her.

"Go, then," she said, and hated how steady her voice was.

Jude caught Ilse's sleeve. For a moment the thief and the traitor stood forehead to forehead, and Sera looked away, because some griefs are private even in front of witnesses. Then Ilse slipped out the window and down the drainpipe like water running backward, and was gone into the smoke-colored streets.

* * *

They went down through the salt-seller's cellar, into tunnels that smelled of brine and old stone, following Ilse's chalk marks toward the gate district. The city was screaming around them now—not the people, not yet, but the architecture itself, doors slamming, shutters banging, the whole rickety organism of Vask trying to close its pores against the grey tide. Somewhere a crier was shouting something about curfew, about shelter, and then stopped mid-word.

Sera walked with her sword drawn. She hadn't decided to draw it. That was the thing about herself that frightened her most these days: the parts of her that acted without asking permission. Her feet chose cobblestones that gave the least sound. Her eyes sorted crowds into threats and non-threats without her consent. Whoever she had been before the erasure, she had been built in a forge, and the forge-marks were still on her.

At the mouth of the tunnel, Jude stopped her with a hand on her arm.

"The gate yard," they whispered. "Look."

Through the grate, the Salt Gate of Vask showed itself: a squat arch of black granite in a wall the color of old teeth, the mountain road visible beyond it climbing into cloud. And filling the yard between, forty strong at least, stood the Wardens. Not searching. Waiting. Arranged in a crescent around the gate like a hand cupped to catch falling water.

"They're not hunting the undercity," Sera breathed. "They're herding."

"Ilse's noise bought us an hour, maybe less. They'll sweep the districts until we break for the gate, and they'll be here when we do." Jude pressed their forehead against the cold grate. In the grey light their face looked hollowed, provisional, like a sketch of a person someone might rub out. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. That's the whole trade."

"There isn't," Sera said, "and you know it."

She did know it, suddenly and completely, the way she'd known the shape of the king's death. Some truths arrive whole. The Wardens had sealed every road but this one because they wanted exactly this: the carrier funneled into a kill-yard. Unless the kill-yard became something else.

"Jude." She turned to face them, and the words came up from somewhere below memory, from the bedrock under the flooded country of her past. "When I hold that gate, how long does it take you to reach the tree line?"

"Don't." Jude's face did something complicated. Fear, fury, and underneath both, the tenderness neither of them had ever named aloud, the thing that lived between them like a candle flame cupped in two hands. "Don't you dare say what you're about to say."

"How long?"

"A quarter hour if the road's clear. Longer if the hounds pick up my trail. Sera, you can't hold a gate against forty Wardens. You don't even remember how you fight. Your body remembers, maybe, but bodies tire, bodies bleed—"

"Yes," she agreed. "Bodies do."

"You owe me nothing!" The words cracked out of Jude sharp as struck flint. "You don't know me. You met me in a ditch and trusted me for reasons neither of us can explain. Whatever oath you're keeping, whoever you were—you don't owe your life to a thief who stole a kingdom's last breath and carries it like a purse!"

Sera took their face in her hands. Their skin was cold from the tunnel damp; their eyes were wet and furious and more honest than anything else left in the world.

"I don't know who I owe," she said softly. "I don't know my name, or my mother's face, or why my hands know eleven ways to kill a man in armor. But I know you. Not your history—I know *you*. The person who fed a decoy memory to a hound rather than let it touch a farmer. The person who cried over Maren and called it dust in their eye. Whatever I was before, whoever I served, I am standing here now, and now is the only country I have. And in the country of now, you are worth a gate."

"Then I'm worth two gates," Jude snarled, "because I forbid—"

"You can't forbid me. You're not my king."

The silence after that was enormous. Somewhere above, the bells were still ringing their wrong ringing, and the tunnel dripped its slow salt tears, and Jude stared at her as if she'd spoken in a tongue from before the world.

"I wasn't going to say that," Sera whispered. "I didn't know I was going to say that. It just—it came out of me like it had been waiting."

And then, like a door blowing open in a storm—

* * *

She remembered.

Not everything. Not the green field, not the banners, not the king's face. But a fragment, a splinter driven deep enough to survive the flood: kneeling on stone, a sword laid across her palms, a voice above her saying words she felt rather than heard—and beneath the words, her own name, spoken by her own mouth, sworn like blood:

*"Serahilde Vance, knight of Cael, my sword is yours until truth and I part ways."*

Serahilde. Serahilde Vance.

It hit her like cold water, like a wall, like sunrise. She staggered. Jude caught her elbows. "What? What is it?"

"My name." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Jude, my name is Serahilde. Serahilde Vance. I was a knight of Cael. I swore an oath—to him, to the king—and the oath said *until truth and I part ways*, and I never understood until this moment that the oath was telling me the future. Truth and I parted ways. The Wardens saw to that. But the oath—" she gripped Jude's shoulders, "—the oath isn't broken. It's *waiting*. An oath to truth doesn't die when the truth is stolen. It waits for the truth to come home."

Jude was crying now, silently, furiously, wiping their face with the heel of their hand like a child angry at weather. "If you die holding that gate, I'll be carrying your name too. Do you understand? I'll spend the rest of my run remembering a woman I knew for two weeks, and it will hurt worse than losing the whole kingdom, because at least I never *loved* the kingdom."

The word hung between them. *Loved.* Neither of them reached for it. Neither of them stepped away from it.

"Then carry it well," Serahilde said. She kissed them once, brief and fierce, tasting salt that was not from the sea. "A quarter hour. Count them for me. And Jude—when you teach the road to someone someday, teach them that the Salt Gate held."

* * *

She went up out of the tunnel into the grey light of the yard, and she did not sneak.

Later—if there was a later—she would understand that this was the choice that mattered. She could have gone quietly, picked her moment, fought the guerrilla's fight. Instead she walked out into the open ground before the Salt Gate, drew her sword, and let the light run down the blade, and raised her voice in a challenge that came from a country that no longer existed:

"I am Serahilde Vance, knight of Cael! This gate is held!"

The Wardens turned. Forty faces under grey hoods, unreadable as slate. One of them, taller than the rest, with a voice like dry paper, said: "The executioner. The king's own blade, guarding the thief who stole his confession. How the mighty forget themselves."

"I remember myself fine," she said. "Better today than yesterday."

The hounds came first.

They poured around the flanks of the crescent, those wrong-jointed things, and Serahilde discovered what her body knew that her mind had lost: that hounds of memory fear iron that has been sworn. Her blade sang a note she felt in her teeth, and where it touched them, they came apart like smoke refusing a wind. She killed three before the Wardens understood, and their confusion was a weapon too—they had expected a woman with no past, and got instead a knight with one name and total conviction.

Then the crescent closed, and there was no more thinking, only the oldest arithmetic: steel, distance, breath. She fought the way water fights, giving nowhere and yielding nothing, using the gate's stones at her back, making each Warden pay a toll of pain for every step into her circle. A blade opened her arm. She broke a wrist with her pommel. Someone threw a net of woven shadow and she cut through it and the cutting cost her—a line of fire along her ribs—and she laughed, actually laughed, because the pain was proof. Proof she was real. Proof the erasure had not taken everything, would not take everything, because a body that bleeds is a body that happened.

Behind her, through the arch, she heard boots on the mountain road. Fading. Climbing. Jude, running for the tree line, alive, alive, alive.

*A quarter hour,* she thought, and counted.

The tall Warden came for her personally in the ninth minute. He fought like a bureaucrat, efficient and joyless, and he spoke while they circled, soft and reasonable as a confession: "Open the Door for us, knight. Give us the carrier, and we'll let you keep whatever name you've salvaged. You were mercy once. Be mercy again."

"I was obedience once," she answered, parrying, gasping. "I'm told it looks similar. It isn't."

In the twelfth minute her left leg folded—something in the knee, wet and wrong—and she went down on one knee in the yard of the Salt Gate, sword still up, because a knight kneels to no one but her oath.

In the thirteenth minute, the Wardens parted, and through them came figures carrying something wrapped in chains and canvas, and the canvas was stained dark, and Serahilde knew before they unwrapped it, knew by the way her heart simply stopped pretending.

Ilse.

Alive. Barely. Chained in bindings that glimmered like frost, her face grey, her lips moving—counting, Sera realized. The madwoman was counting minutes too, buying time with her capture, shouting numbers into the Warden's ears so their hounds would chase the sound of her thoughts instead of the trail on the wind.

"She led us a merry dance," the tall Warden said. "Six years of it. But everyone comes to the gate eventually. Everyone comes to the gate." He crouched to Serahilde's level, and his hood tilted, considering her, the way a man considers a tool that has outlived its use. "You've spent yourself for a thief. Was it worth it, knight of a dead country?"

Serahilde Vance looked up at him through blood and sweat and the grey light of a world with no stars, and she smiled, because she had finally, finally remembered enough to know exactly what she was.

"You tell me," she said. "When the Door opens—and it will open—when Cael comes home and every stolen thing returns to its owner, you'll be standing in the yard of everything you unmade. And I want you to remember that the gate held. Fifteen minutes. A knight, a bad knee, and the truth." She spat red onto the granite. "Count on it."

The tall Warden straightened. Behind him, the hounds had gone still, heads cocked toward the mountain, toward the tree line, toward the fading warmth of a trail already lost in snow and distance. Too late. A quarter hour, spent like a coin.

He sighed, dry paper settling. "Take them both," he said. "The defector to the questioning rooms. The knight—" a pause, almost gentle, almost pitying, "—the knight we may still have use for. Obedience leaves marks, Serahilde Vance. We know how to read them."

They bound her where she knelt, and dragged Ilse beside her, and as the grey tide swallowed the Salt Gate yard, Serahilde did the only thing left that was hers to do.

She began, aloud, in a voice cracked and bleeding but unbroken, to speak the litany of the road behind her—the pass at Thornfell, the waystation, Maren's cellar, the fog-choked lowlands, the free city of Vask—every step of the route to the Door of Returning, spoken into the air, into the stones, into the ears of any hound forced to listen:

"...and from the village that isn't there, east by the split rock, three days to the white mountains, and at the top of the white mountains, the Door..."

Let them hear it. Let them chain it. Words spoken aloud are harder to steal than words kept silent—and somewhere on a snowy slope, a thief was counting minutes, and a kingdom was waiting to come home.
