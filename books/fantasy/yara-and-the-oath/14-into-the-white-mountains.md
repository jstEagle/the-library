## 14. Into the White Mountains

The snow began an hour after they left the split rock, and it did not fall so much as arrive—descending all at once out of a sky the color of old pewter, thick as wool, silent as confession. Jude pulled their collar up and kept walking, and behind them, unhurried as tide, the mountain swallowed their tracks.

They had been climbing for two days. The fog-choked lowlands were a week behind them, Vask and its burning markets farther still, and Ilse's words had followed them the whole way like a second set of footprints: *A country unmade needs an anchor of memory, and you are it.*

Jude had carried other people's pasts for thirty years without ever once feeling one wear against their own bones. Now theirs were going. They could feel it the way you feel a tooth loosen—not pain, exactly, but a wrongness, a small thing adrift in its socket.

"Maren," they said aloud, testing. The name sat strangely in their mouth. Warm, familiar, shaped like a cellar full of dried apples and lamp oil and a woman's laugh like a gate hinge. But when Jude reached for the face that went with the name, their hand came back empty.

*Maren.* A fence. An old friend. Someone who had died buying them time in a cellar, whispering something at the end—

Whispering what?

The snow filled in where the thought had been.

* * *

Sera noticed first. Of course she did; she was a knight even without her name, and knights are trained to watch for weakness in the line.

"You stopped walking."

"I didn't." Jude looked down. Their boots had indeed stopped, planted in six inches of fresh powder, while Sera stood ten paces ahead with the wind pulling at her grey cloak. How long had they been standing there? There was snow *in* the prints. Minutes, then. Maybe more.

"Third time today," Sera said quietly. She came back to them, closing the distance with that economical stride of hers, the one that wasted nothing and apologized for nothing. Her sword rode her hip like it had grown there, though she still could not remember learning to use it. "This morning you asked me twice which fork we'd taken. And yesterday—" She hesitated.

"Tell me."

"Yesterday you called me by name. My real one." She said it carefully, the way you handle something that might be holy or might be broken glass. "*Seraphine.* You said you'd found it in a stolen memory of a muster roll. But Jude—that was before we ever talked about the muster rolls. You didn't find my name. I think you're losing things, and the losses are showing you gaps you're filling wrong."

The wind moved through the pines above them with a sound like the sea. Jude opened their mouth to deny it, because denial was the oldest tool in a thief's kit, worn smooth from handling—and found they had nothing to put in the denial's place. They couldn't remember whether it was true.

"Yes," they said instead. "All right. Yes."

Sera nodded once, and did not do any of the soft terrible things people do when someone confesses to dying. She only took their pack off their shoulders and slung it over her own beside hers, adjusting the straps without asking, and said, "Then we go faster."

That was why Jude trusted her, they thought. That was the whole of it, the mystery they'd never been able to explain since the day she'd cornered them on the lowland road with a drawn sword and no memory and somehow ended it standing closer than enemies stand. She didn't waste motion on mercy. She simply picked up your pack and made the road shorter.

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow of a rockfall, a bowl of stone with a single wind-bent pine leaning over it like a conspirator. Sera got a fire going small and hot, the way soldiers do—Jude knew that much was true, at least; they remembered knowing it, which was more than they could say for most things now.

"Teach me the route," Sera said.

Jude looked up from their hands. "What?"

"You heard me." She fed a branch to the flames and watched it catch. "You told me once that you never keep anything. Never keep, never feel. Rules of the trade. But you've been keeping this route in your head since Thornfell—the smuggler roads, the passes that aren't on maps anymore, the way through the white mountains to the Door. It's the last map of Cael that exists, Jude, and it exists inside a person who is coming apart." She met their eyes across the fire. Her face was all planes and frost-reddened skin, and her eyes were the color of a winter river, and they did not look away. "So teach it to me. Every turn. Tonight, and tomorrow night, and every night until you don't have to remember it anymore because I do."

"That's—" Jude started, and stopped, because the objection they'd been about to raise was *that will hurt*, and they realized with a lurch that they could not remember precisely why hurting had been a rule. Never keep, never feel. Whose rule? Their own. Made when? Made *why*?

There had been a reason. There was always a reason. Thieves didn't build walls around their hearts for the architecture.

"Jude." Sera's voice, low. "Where did you go?"

"Somewhere there isn't a map," Jude said, and laughed, and the laugh came out cracked down the middle. "Fine. All right. Teach you the route. We'll do it properly." They dragged a stick through the snow, clearing a patch down to the frozen grit beneath, and began to draw. "From the lowlands north, the road everyone remembers ends at a burned tollhouse. After the tollhouse there is no road. You go by the river until the river goes under, and then you trust the stones—the cairns, the old ones, waist-high, older than Cael. Smugglers raised them before there was a kingdom to smuggle out of. Left at the third twin-cairn, always left, even though it looks wrong, especially because it looks wrong—"

Sera watched the lines appear under the stick. Then she said, "Again."

"What?"

"Draw it again. Slower. I'm not memorizing a picture, I'm memorizing your hands making it." And then, with the ghost of something that was almost humor but too disciplined to commit: "I have no memories, Jude. Apparently I learn like a child does. Repetition."

So Jude drew it again. And again. The burned tollhouse, the drowning river, the lying cairns. The pass at Thornfell behind it all, the waystation, Maren's cellar—Maren, whose face was gone now, whose name was a coat with no one in it—and forward, upward, into the white mountains, three days east by the split rock, and at the top of the white mountains—

"The Door," Sera said, when Jude's stick paused.

"The Door," Jude agreed, and drew it: a tall shape, a threshold, and then, because their hand seemed to know something their mind had misplaced, they scratched a second mark beside it. Small. Like a person standing near.

Sera looked at that little figure for a long time. "Who's that?"

"I don't know," Jude said honestly. "I think—I think I used to know."

The fire ticked. Snow hissed softly at the edges of the hollow, falling into the dark beyond their little island of light, and Sera reached across and put her hand flat over the drawing, erasing it with one motion, and said, "It doesn't matter. From tonight it matters less. Say the route to me in your sleep if you have to. Wake me and say it. I'll learn it the way I learned to fight—with no idea how, and well enough to kill for it."

Something in Jude's chest turned over—not a memory, those were mostly gone or going, but the *shape* of one, the socket where a tooth had been. They thought: *she says things like that and doesn't notice what they mean.* They thought: *I am being erased, and she is writing me down anyway.*

"Start again from Thornfell," they said, and lay back against the stone, and began.

* * *

The next morning Jude woke having forgotten the taste of bread.

Not the word. The word was fine, squatting in its dictionary slot. But somewhere between sleep and waking, the memory of it—the warmth, the tear of crust, butter going liquid into the holes—had been scooped out of them like pulp from fruit. They sat very still with their hands around a tin cup of melted snow and tried to grieve and found that grief requires specifics, and the specifics were already melting off the mountain.

"It's starting faster," they told Sera on the slope. The wind was hard in their teeth, driving ice crystals sideways, and they had to shout a little, which was almost a mercy because it meant the words didn't have room to shake. "Ilse said the erasure consumes the anchor. I thought that meant when I open the Door. I think it means *now*. Every mile closer, the price runs ahead of me, collecting."

Sera walked beside them, rope coiled over one shoulder for the icefield ahead, matching their pace without seeming to look at them. "Collecting what, exactly? What did you lose?"

"Bread." Jude laughed, and hated the sound. "The smell of my mother's hands. I checked this morning—there was something there about lavender and flour and now there's a hole shaped like it. Yesterday it was Maren's face. Tomorrow it might be your—"

They stopped. The wind shouted around them.

"It won't be you," Sera said.

"You can't know that."

"No." She turned and faced into the wind, into them, and pulled her hood back so they could see her whole face doing it—refusing to shelter, refusing to let the weather or the truth be faced alone. "I can't know that. So here is what we do. Everything you want to keep, you give to me. Not the route—the route I have, I drilled it half the night, ask me the third twin-cairn and watch me answer. *Everything else.* Your mother's hands. Whatever Maren whispered. What bread tastes like, before it finishes going. Tell me all of it, every night, everything you're afraid to lose, and I will hold it. I'll speak it back to you. I'll be your—"

She stopped herself. But Jude had spent thirty years reading the endings of other people's sentences, stealing them straight out of unguarded heads, and so they finished it for her, gently:

"Your archive."

"I was going to say *your witness*," Sera said, with dignity, in the wind.

"Witness," Jude agreed. "Yes. That too."

And that evening, in the lee of another rockfall with another small fire, Jude began the strangest theft of their life: a theft in reverse. They gave. They pried loose everything that still remained and handed it across the fire in words—lavender and flour, the weight of a copper coin earned at nine years old, the exact sound of rain on the waystation roof, Maren's last whisper (*it was your childhood name, they'd told Sera once, but the name itself had already been eaten, and saying so made Sera's jaw go tight with a fury she filed away for later, for Wardens*)—and Sera listened the way she fought, completely, and repeated each piece back until it lived in two heads instead of one.

"You understand," Jude said, deep in the blue part of the night, "this is how the erased village survives. The elder told us—they tell their stories aloud every night, and so they persist. You're making me a village of one."

"I'm making you two," Sera said. "Go on. You hadn't finished the coin."

* * *

On the fourth day the trees gave out. Above the treeline the world simplified itself to white and grey and the blue-black teeth of ridgelines, and the cold stopped being weather and became a presence, an animal walking beside them, breathing on their necks. Jude's thoughts slowed in it, thickened, and the holes in them widened to match. They forgot the name of the capital of Cael for an hour and got it back and lost it again. They forgot which eye of theirs had been scarred—reached up to touch the brow above it and could not remember receiving the wound, could not remember the face it belonged to.

At midday they sheltered behind a cairn the height of two men, ancient, crusted in rime, and Sera made them stop and drilled the route one more time, standing toe to toe in the screaming wind, shouting it call and response like a sergeant with a raw recruit.

*"After the third twin-cairn?"*

"Left!"

*"Even though?"*

"It looks wrong! Especially because it looks wrong!"

*"And at the top of the white mountains?"*

And Jude opened their mouth, and nothing came out.

For three full seconds—a long time, in wind, on a mountain, with everything at stake—Jude Yarrow stood on the roof of the world and did not know what they were walking toward. The word *Door* floated somewhere out of reach. The purpose of the climb, the kingdom in their keeping, the king's confession burning in its vault of stolen years—all of it whited out, snow-blind, gone.

Sera saw it happen. Jude watched her see it, watched something move behind the winter-river eyes—not fear; she didn't seem built for fear—but a grimness settling like a load-bearing beam.

Then she gripped Jude's shoulders, leaned close, and said into their ear, slow and clear, each word hammered flat so it would carry:

"At the top of the white mountains, *the Door of Returning.* And you are the key. Say it."

"The Door," Jude said. Their voice sounded far away. "I'm the key."

"Say it again."

"The Door of Returning. I'm the key."

"And I'm the one who gets you there." She held them at arm's length and studied their face with the thoroughness of someone checking a blade before battle. "Every time you drop it, I'll pick it up. Do you hear me? You've spent your whole life carrying what belongs to other people. For once in your miserable thieving life, let someone carry you."

The wind tore the moment's silence apart before Jude could decide what to say. Which was probably just as well. They had never been good at the sentences that mattered.

They climbed on.

* * *

Near dusk, cresting a shoulder of ice-glazed scree, Jude stopped—not because they had forgotten to walk this time, but because the land ahead had done something impossible.

Below them, tucked into a hanging valley where by every law of altitude and season there should have been nothing but wind and stone, lay a village.

Smoke rose from its chimneys. Lamplight glowed warm in its windows, gold as yolk. Fields lay under snow in neat furrows; a dog barked somewhere, small and bright in all that silence. It looked like a woodcut from a storybook, a place remembered rather than built—and Jude felt the hair rise on their arms beneath three layers of wool, because the valley was *familiar*, and familiarity was nearly extinct inside them, and anything that survived the erasure deserved to be feared.

"Sera," they said. "Do you remember the elder? The one who begged us not to open the Door?"

Sera had gone still beside them, hand drifting to her sword-hilt out of an instinct older than her memory. "We haven't come to any village yet."

"We have now." Jude watched the lamplight flicker, and felt the last of the daylight fail, and heard, faint on the wind from the valley below, the sound of many voices rising together—one voice, then ten, then all of them, speaking in unison some rhythm too distant to parse. Stories, they thought. The nightly telling. The rite that keeps the erased alive.

"They're remembering out loud down there," Jude said. "And I need to hear what they remember—before I forget which parts of me are worth saving."

They started down toward the lights, and behind them, patient as erosion, the mountain filled in their tracks.
