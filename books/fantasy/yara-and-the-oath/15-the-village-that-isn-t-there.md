## 15. The Village That Isn't There

The snow had stopped lying to them by the third day.

Sera noticed it first as a wrongness in the light—how the drifts held no shadows, how the pines stood in white silence like a congregation waiting for a hymn that never came. Then she understood: the tracks behind them were filling in too fast, smoothing over, as if the mountain itself were tidying away the evidence that anyone had ever walked here. As if the world were trying to forget them the way it had forgotten a kingdom.

Jude walked ahead, and Sera counted their steps the way she had learned to count everything now—because Jude was forgetting, and someone had to keep the ledger.

"Forty paces to the stream bend," she said. "You told me that this morning. You said the stream bends left, and past the bend there's a cairn with a red stone, and past the cairn the trail splits, and we take the low road."

Jude didn't turn around. "Did I?"

"You did. You said your mother showed you the cairn when you were seven, and that she lied about the red stone being a piece of the old king's crown, and that you believed her until you were nine, and that you still think of it as a crown-stone anyway."

A pause in the snow-hush. Jude's shoulders moved—something that was almost a laugh and almost its opposite. "I said all that?"

"You did. This morning, while the tea was heating. You said it twice, because the first time you said it you were smiling, and I think you wanted to make sure the smile got said along with the rest."

They walked on. The wind came down the white shoulders of the mountain carrying nothing—not the smell of woodsmoke, not the resin of the pines, not even the mineral breath of ice. A wind scrubbed clean of the world. Sera had begun to hate it the way one hates a person who keeps interrupting.

"Tell me the rest," Jude said quietly.

So she did. She told them about Maren's cellar, the smell of tallow and turnips, the way the old fence had hummed a lullaby in a language neither of them knew and pressed Jude's hand at the end and called her by a name that wasn't Jude. She told it the way she had told it every night since, and every night she watched Jude receive their own life like a letter from a stranger, and every night something in her chest tore a little further along its seam.

She had her own name back now. It had come to her at the Salt Gate, in the crash of iron and the red bloom of her own blood on the snow, *Sera*, arriving not as a memory but as an act—arrived the way a sword arrives in the hand, all at once and forever. She remembered her oath. She remembered the king's face. She did not remember killing him, and some nights she lay awake trying to decide whether that absence was mercy or a wound that hadn't finished opening.

The cairn appeared in the late afternoon, exactly where Jude's morning-self had promised. The red stone sat in it like a dropped jewel.

"Crown-stone," Sera said, touching it.

Jude stared at it for a long time. "I don't remember her showing me. But I remember that I told you. Is that—" Their voice snagged. "Is that how it goes? You keep the shape of a thing after the thing is gone?"

"I don't know," Sera said. "But I'll keep saying it until you don't need the shape anymore."

They took the low road.

* * *

They smelled the village before they saw it: woodsmoke, real woodsmoke, and beneath it bread, and beneath that the particular animal warmth of people living close together through winter. Sera's hand went to her sword on instinct. Smoke meant chimneys, and chimneys meant the Wardens had missed something, or hadn't come yet, or—

"There's nothing on any map," Jude murmured. "There's nothing on *any* map anymore. But I know this valley." They went very still. "I stole a memory here once. A cooper's memory. The cooper of a village that—" Their brow furrowed, that new furrow Sera had learned to dread, the one where a thought went in smooth and came out snagged. "A village that isn't there."

The houses appeared out of the whitefall one at a time, low and stone-rooted, roofs bowed under snow. No banners. No wards. No wards at all—Sera felt their absence like a missing tooth, the way she had felt the sky's absence the night the stars went out. A village with no wards on the edge of a broken world, and yet standing.

A woman met them at the well in the village square, a broad-shouldered woman with flour to her elbows, and she looked at them the way no one had looked at them in weeks: *directly*, with recognition, with the frank appraisal of one human being sizing up two more.

"Travelers," the woman said. "You'll be wanting supper and a roof, and you'll be wanting to earn both, and that's fine. Everyone earns here. That's the whole of the law." Her eyes moved to Jude's face and something flickered there—not recognition, Sera thought, but its cousin. Grief's cousin. "You'll come to the telling tonight," the woman added, and it was not a request. "Everyone comes to the telling. That's the other half of the law."

"What telling?" Sera asked.

The woman wiped her hands on her apron. "The telling," she said, as if the word were a door she was surprised they couldn't open.

* * *

The hall was low-beamed and fire-bright, and it was full—not with the desperate hush of refugees, Sera realized, but with the settled warmth of people who had done this every night for a long time. Farmers and coopers and a smith with burn-scarred forearms and children wrapped in wool, all of them arranged in the firelight with the ease of ritual. On the wall behind the hearth hung a wooden board, and carved into the board, in a hundred different hands, were names. Hundreds of names. Some were worn smooth as river stones from being touched.

An old man sat nearest the fire. He was small and neat as a folded letter, with white hair braided at the temples in a style Sera almost recognized and couldn't place, and eyes the color of a winter sky at dusk. The room quieted when he raised one hand.

"We are the people of Harrowmere," he said, and the words had the worn cadence of something said ten thousand times, "in the vale of Harrowmere, in the east of Cael, which was, and is, and—"

"—will be," the room answered, every voice together, children and elders, "so long as one of us remembers."

Sera's breath stopped.

Cael. The word dropped into her like a stone into a well, and the well was so deep she never heard it land. She felt Jude go rigid beside her, felt the thief's hand find her sleeve and grip it—not for support, she thought. To keep from running. Or to keep from weeping. With Jude the two had always been close cousins.

The telling went on for hours. The cooper told of the cooper's trade, and the cooper was his grandfather, and he told it in his grandfather's cadence, with his hands shaping the staves in the air. The smith told of the forge and the fire that had taken her father's arm and the wedding that had been held in the forge anyway because her mother had said love doesn't wait for buildings. A girl of perhaps ten stood and told of a dog named Bristle who had once stolen an entire wheel of cheese and hidden under the church, and the whole room laughed, laughed with the full-throated ease of people who had heard it before and loved it anyway, and the girl glowed, and Sera understood.

They weren't telling stories. They were *building*. Every night they laid the same stones, in the same order, and the village stood on them—not on its foundations, which the Wardens had burned out of the world, but on the telling. The names on the board were not a memorial. They were a load-bearing wall.

"They're erased," Jude breathed against her ear. "Like Cael. Like the refugees on the road who couldn't say where they came from. But they're *holding*." The thief's eyes were wet and wide and burning. "How are they holding? The erasure doesn't leave residue. It doesn't leave—"

"It leaves what people refuse to put down," said the old man.

Sera started. He had crossed the room without sound, small as he was, and stood before them with his dusk-colored eyes moving from her face to Jude's with terrible gentleness.

"Forgive an old man his eavesdropping," he said. "I am Tammas. I was— I am the reeve of Harrowmere. We noticed you at the well. Strangers don't come here. The road forgot itself years ago; that's why the Wardens never found us. You can't hunt what you can't remember." He smiled, and the smile was kind and sorrowful in equal measure. "But you came anyway. Which means you are either lost, or you are looking for something, and lost people don't grip their sword-hilts the way you do, child."

"We're passing through," Sera said.

"No one passes through," Tammas said mildly. "There is no through. There is only here, and the Door." He said the word the way one says a name of the dead.

Jude made a small sound. Sera's hand closed over theirs.

"You know it," the old man said, watching them. "Not as a rumor. You know it the way I know it, which is to say you carry something that remembers it." His gaze settled on Jude, and deepened, and then—Sera saw it happen—went wide. The old man swayed as if the floor had tilted under him. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, child. What is it you're carrying?"

"I don't know what you—"

"The king," Tammas whispered. "I can see him. Not your face—you wear it well, whatever your craft is, thief, and no offense meant, we've all stolen to survive—but behind you, under your skin, the shape of a dying man's voice. I sat at that man's council table for twenty years before I retired to my orchards. I would know his cadence in a storm." His hand came up, trembling, and hovered near Jude's chest without touching. "You have his last words in you. You have the confession."

The hall had gone quiet around them. The fire popped. Somewhere a child's voice was still mid-story about the dog Bristle, trailing off as every adult face turned.

Sera stepped half in front of Jude, an old reflex from a life she couldn't fully remember. "Whatever he has, it's his."

"It's *ours*," Tammas said, and there was iron under the gentleness now. "It's the last true thing left of a country, and it's walking around in the chest of a stranger on the one night in ten years that a stranger has walked into my hall." He closed his eyes. When he opened them, the dusk-color of them had gone hard as slate. "Sit with me," he said. "Both of you. And then I am going to ask you for the hardest thing I have ever asked anyone, and I need you to hear all of it before you answer."

* * *

He took them to his house at the edge of the square, a low stone cottage with an orchard sleeping under snow behind it—apple trees, Jude said faintly, apple trees, I remember apples, I stole an orchard once, whose orchard, whose—and sat them by his fire and poured a cider so dark it was nearly black, from apples that should not have existed, that existed only because a hundred people said they did.

"The Wardens came to Harrowmere in the first hour," Tammas said. "I know, because I was awake. Old men sleep badly. I saw the sky go wrong, saw the wards of the world crack like river ice, and I ran for the village bell, and I rang it, and I shouted the truth into every window: *the stars are dying, the country is being unmade, and if you want to survive it, you must hold on, you must speak, you must not let go.*" He looked into his cup. "I don't know why I knew. Perhaps the reeve's office comes with certain knowledge. Perhaps someone warned me once and I've forgotten the warning and kept the shape of it, as your friend says." A ghost of a smile at Jude. "By dawn the village was erased. Every map, every mind beyond this valley, every record. The people woke up hollow. Some of them wandered. Some of them are still wandering; you saw them on the roads, the ones who couldn't say where they came from." His voice frayed. "But some of them heard the bell. Some of them held on to the sound of my voice the way a drowning man holds a rope. And every night since, we tell. And every morning, we are still here. Not on any map. Not in any mind but our own. But *here*."

"And you want to know about the Door," Jude said.

"I want to *beg* you about the Door," Tammas said, and the iron in him bent, and Sera saw that he was not as strong as his voice. "The king's confession—I heard the half of it that was public, before the end. The bargain. The binding. I don't know what he told you in his last hours, thief, but I know what he told his council, and I know what my granddaughter told me before the erasure took her, because she served in the deep archives, and she said the Wardens' quarantines were not cruelty. She said they were the only mercy left."

"Mercy," Sera said. The word tasted like ash. She remembered a plague, remembered orders, remembered a king who wept. She remembered—no. She didn't. She remembered the shape of remembering.

"Sit," Tammas said again, and this time it was a command. "Listen to an old man who has spent ten years learning what forgetting is for."

He told them about the years before. About the thing the king had bound—not a beast, he said, not a demon out of the old stories, but something older and stranger, something that had lived in the deep places of Cael since before Cael had a name, something the first kings had fed and the later kings had chained. "And it was *leaking*," he said. "That's what my granddaughter found in the archives. The binding was old and it was failing, and everything that went wrong in the last years of the kingdom—the blight, the madness in the border lords, the plague that came from nowhere—leaked out of it, like water through a cracked dam. The king knew. The king had always known. And when he understood the dam would fail in his lifetime, he—" Tammas's hands shook. "He made his bargain. And the Wardens, whatever else they are, whatever else they did, they looked at a failing dam and a kingdom full of people downstream, and they chose to move the people out of the flood's path by taking the flood's *reason* to exist. You cannot unmake a quarantine without unmaking what it quarantines. So they unmade the country instead. All of it. Everyone in it, scattered and hollowed, but *alive*, and free of the thing that would have eaten them."

"Alive and empty," Sera said. "You call that mercy?"

"I call it the only door that wasn't worse," Tammas said. "I have had ten years to think about it, knight, ten years of telling, and I will tell you what telling teaches: that memory is a wall and memory is a weight, and the same stones do both jobs. The Wardens chose to drop the wall on everyone at once. It was monstrous. It was also the last resort of people who had run out of good choices, and I will not pretend otherwise, because pretending is how the dam cracks in the first place." He set down his cup and leaned forward, and his dusk eyes held Jude's with a ferocity that belied his smallness. "But you. You carry the confession. You carry the king's own voice, his last words, the thing he spent his dying breath to say. And you mean to open the Door of Returning. To pour it all back. To bring Cael back—*all* of Cael, back to its borders, back into its binding, back into the shape the dam was built to hold." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Child. I am begging you. Some erasures are mercies. Some doors are locked for the sake of what's behind them, not what's in front. I have buried a granddaughter whose name I keep alive by saying it every night, and I would say it every night until I die rather than bring back the world that fed the thing that killed her. Do you understand me? We are not a village that was stolen. We are a village that was *spared*, and we have made a life of the sparing, and it is a hard life and it is a *good* life, and you would take it from us to satisfy a dead king's guilt."

The fire cracked. Outside, the snow-wind moaned once against the shutters and went quiet, as if listening.

Jude sat very still. Sera watched their face and saw the war move through it like weather—grief and fury and doubt, and beneath all of it the thing Sera had come to know better than she knew her own returned name: the thief's terrible, stubborn love for what was lost.

"I stole a memory in this valley," Jude said at last, very quietly. "Years ago. A cooper's memory of his daughter learning to walk. I never sold it. I never keep, it's my rule, but I kept that one, for a while, because it was—" Their voice caught. "It was the first beautiful thing I ever held. And I gave it back. I walked three days out of my way to give it back, because keeping it felt like theft in a way the rest never did." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and burning. "I don't know if that matters to you. I don't know if it makes me the right person or the wrong one to carry this. But I didn't take the king's confession to sell it, and I'm not carrying it to satisfy his guilt. I'm carrying it because it's *true*, and everything else has been taken, and if I let the last true thing burn out of me then the Wardens win even if I never see their faces." They drew a breath. "But I hear you. I want you to know I hear you. And I don't know what I'm going to do."

Tammas studied them for a long moment. Then the old man nodded, slowly, as if a debt had been acknowledged.

"That," he said, "is the first honest answer I've had from a stranger in ten years." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the carved board that hung even here, in his own house, a smaller board with fewer names. He touched one name at the top—*Lira*, worn nearly smooth—and turned back.

"Stay the night. Tell with us, if you like; the telling is open to anyone who means it. And in the morning—" He hesitated, and Sera saw the fear move through him, the fear he had been living inside for ten years. "In the morning, I'll give you something. A thing my granddaughter left, hidden in the orchard, for whoever came carrying the king's voice. She said someone would come. She said I'd know them by the weight they carried." His dusk eyes went to Jude, and then, unexpectedly, to Sera. "She said there would be two. A carrier and a sword. She said the sword would have to choose."

Sera's hand had gone to her hilt without her deciding to. "Choose what?"

Tammas looked at her with a sorrow so old it had gone smooth, like the names on the board.

"Whether some doors," he said, "should stay locked—even by force. Even against the person you—" He stopped himself. Smiled the terrible kind smile. "Sleep well, knight. The morning will ask enough of you."

* * *

They were given a room under the eaves, narrow and clean, with a quilt that smelled of cedar and apples. The telling went on below them for hours; Sera lay awake listening to the murmur of it, the rise and fall of a hundred voices laying stones in the dark, building a country out of breath.

Beside her, Jude slept badly, turning and murmuring, and once—Sera was nearly sure—said a name that wasn't theirs, a small name, a child's name, and wept without waking.

Near dawn, Sera rose and looked out the frost-furred window at the orchard, at the buried rows of impossible apple trees, and saw, between one blink and the next, a figure standing among them. Small. Still. Watching the house.

By the time her eyes cleared the frost, the orchard was empty, the snow unbroken.

But the door at the foot of the stairs, the one that had been shut when they went up, now stood open a hand's width, and from the dark below came the smell of turned earth, and a voice like a bell rung underwater, saying a word she did not know in a language she was almost certain she had spoken once, before.

Sera took her sword from the wall and went down.
