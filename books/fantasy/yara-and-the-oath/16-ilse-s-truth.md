## 16. Ilse's Truth

Jude woke to the smell of turned earth and the sound of Sera's boots on the stairs.

They had slept badly in the elder's house, in a bed that smelled of apples and lamp oil, and their first waking thought—the thought that came before any other, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be—was that they could not remember Maren's face. They knew they had loved her. They knew it the way you know a sum is correct without being able to show the working. *Maren died for me. Maren whispered my name.* But the face above those facts had gone soft-focus, like a portrait left out in weather, and Jude lay still under the rough blanket and grieved a person who was becoming a fact.

Then Sera came up the stairs with her sword drawn and her knuckles white on the grip, and said, "The cellar's empty. The door at the bottom—it isn't a door anymore."

"It isn't a door anymore," she repeated, as if saying it twice might make it less true. "It's a threshold. There's no wood, no hinges. Just... standing dark. And the voice was coming from it. It said—" She stopped, jaw tight. "It said something in Old Caelic. I don't know how I know it's Old Caelic."

Jude sat up slowly. Somewhere in their chest, the king's confession turned over like a sleeper disturbed. "Show me."

* * *

The cellar of the village that wasn't there was cold enough to make breath visible, though it was nearly spring outside, or what passed for spring in the White Mountains. The villagers had gathered at the top of the stairs, and the elder stood at their head with her lantern held high, and none of them would come down.

Jude understood why when they saw it.

Where the door had been—and Sera swore it had been a door, oak and iron, shut fast when they'd gone up—there was now simply an absence shaped like a doorway, cut into the earthen wall. Not black. Absence. Looking at it was like looking at the place where a star should have been on the night the stars went out. The eye kept sliding off it. Behind Jude, one of the village children began to cry without noise, tears running down a slack face, and the mother clamped a hand over the child's mouth as if even that small sound might be an invitation.

And from the absence came the smell of turned earth, rich and wrong, the smell of something buried so deep and so long that its grave had become soil, and beneath the smell, faint as a pulse, the bell-underwater voice.

"It's stronger here," Jude said. Their own voice sounded far away. "It's been following us since Vask."

"Since the Salt Gate," Sera said. "Maybe since the pass." She hadn't put the sword away. Jude doubted she could have if she tried; her arm would have had to be broken first. "Ilse would know what it is."

"Ilse," Jude said, "is tied to a chair in the granary with three of the elder's best knots, and every reason in the world to lie to us."

"She saved your life at the gate."

"Ilse let herself be captured at the gate," Jude said. "There's a difference, and I've had a lot of time on the road to think about which kind of person volunteers for Wardens' ropes."

Sera looked at them—a long look, level, the look of someone who had once executed a king and was not easily moved by the arithmetic of sacrifice. "Then let's go ask her," she said, "and this time we'll know if she's lying, because now we know what question matters."

* * *

The granary was warm with stored grain and lamplight, and Ilse sat in the chair they had carried her to, her captured Warden's grey cloak folded neatly beside her as if she were already half-undressed for execution. Her wrists were raw where the ropes had been. She had not tried to work them loose. That, more than anything, had kept Jude awake.

She looked up when they came in, and her eyes went first to Jude, then past them, to the sword at Sera's hip, and something in her face settled, the way a house settles when a weight it has carried too long is finally set down.

"You found the cellar door," she said.

"You know about it."

"I know about everything down here. I helped design the quarantine lines through these mountains thirty years ago, before I understood what I was drawing." She flexed her bound hands. "Untie me, Yarrow. Not because you trust me. Because you need what I know, and what I know is too large to say through rope burns."

Jude didn't move. "Start talking. If a word of it smells like Warden doctrine, Sera will decide what happens next, and she's been looking for an excuse since Vask."

Ilse glanced at Sera. "Yes," she said softly. "I imagine she has. Very well. Ask me the question first. The real one. The one that's been eating you since the elder begged you not to open the Door."

Jude had rehearsed questions all night, turning them over like stolen coins, checking the weight of each. Now, facing Ilse's steady grey gaze, only one remained.

"Why did the elder say some erasures are mercies? What's behind the Door of Returning that made her beg?"

Ilse closed her eyes. When she opened them again, she seemed older—not tired exactly, but emptied, a vessel poured out for a purpose at last.

"Cael is still there," she said.

The words landed in the warm grain-smelling silence and lay there.

"The kingdom. The land itself. Every field, every wall, every orchard. Nothing was destroyed—you can't destroy a country that size in a night, not even with the rituals we had. It was *folded*. Taken out of the world's memory and tucked away, whole, like a letter sealed and put in a drawer. Everyone in Cael when it happened—the living, the dying, the newborn—they're all still there. Walking their streets. Baking their bread. They just don't remember anywhere else existing, and no one outside remembers them." She paused. "You remember them. Because you're carrying the anchor."

"The king's confession," Jude said. Their mouth was dry. "I know this part. The Door brings them back. The Door of Returning—"

"—opens the drawer," Ilse finished. "Yes. And everyone comes home, and the maps mend, and the stars over Cael return, and it will be the most beautiful thing anyone alive has ever seen." Her voice cracked, very slightly, on *beautiful*, and Jude realized with a lurch that Ilse had wanted this—had defected, had betrayed her order, had endured capture and ropes and Jude's suspicion—for this. "I want you to understand that I want it. I was born forty miles from the Caelish border. My grandmother sang songs in a language that stopped existing when the stars went out, and she cried every night without knowing why. I have spent thirty years wanting this."

"But," Jude said.

"But," Ilse said, "the drawer was locked for a reason. And the reason was never us."

* * *

She told it plainly, which was somehow worse than if she had told it in whispers, with candles and portents. Plainness made it undeniable.

"The king of Cael was the finest binding-mage of his line," she said. "That was what kings *were*, in Cael—not conquerors, binders. The royal bloodline existed to hold things shut. And three generations before the erasure, his grandfather bound something. A god. The old kind, the earth-and-hunger kind, the kind that grew under orchards and asked for planting in ways you don't want to imagine." Her eyes flicked toward the window, toward the buried rows in the frozen ground. "It was bound under the palace. Under the throne itself, because the strongest seal is a living oath renewed by a living hand, and each king renewed it at coronation, and each king passed the burden down."

"The confession," Jude whispered. In their chest the stolen memory stirred, the old king's last words, which they had carried for years without understanding more than fragments. "He talked about a bargain. About roots. I thought he was raving—he was dying, I thought—"

"He was dying of the binding, not of poison. The plague the Wardens engineered was engineered to reach him *because* the binding was consuming him faster than it should have, because the god had learned, over generations, to pull. It pulled strength out of the king's line like water down a drain. He was the last link strong enough to hold it, and he was failing, and he knew that when he failed, the thing under the throne would wake with nothing but a thin seal between it and four million people." Ilse leaned forward against her ropes. "So he sent word to the Wardens. Not asking to be saved. Asking to be killed cleanly before the binding broke from inside him—and then, because a dead man can't renew an oath, asking us to fold the whole kingdom away with the god still asleep inside it. Quarantine, not destruction. Seal the drawer with everything in it. Let the world forget Cael rather than let the world inherit what was under Cael."

"And Sera," Jude said. The words came out of them flat and strange. "Sera was his mercy."

"Sera was his mercy," Ilse agreed, gently, watching the knight's face. Beside Jude, Sera had not moved, had not breathed visibly; she stood like something carved, and her silence was louder than the bell-voice under the earth. "He chose her himself. His own oath-guard. He told her what she was carrying and why, and she wept, and she did it, and then we erased her knowing along with everyone else's, because that was the price of the mercy—that no one, least of all the king's executioner, should have to live remembering it."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The lamp flame bent and straightened. Somewhere outside, the village bells—those stubborn nightly bells, rung by people who persisted by telling their stories aloud—began the dusk peal, and Jude flinched at the sound, hearing in it the drowned-bell under the mountains.

"That's not all," Jude said at last, because they had learned, in a lifetime of trading in other people's truths, to hear the shape of what is being withheld. "That's the history. Tell me the design. Tell me what happens when I open the Door."

Ilse's composure finally wavered.

"When the Wardens folded Cael away," she said, "they needed an anchor. A single thread of true memory left loose in the unmade world, holding the fold closed from outside—like a knot in a thread you keep pinched between two fingers so the seam doesn't slip. The anchor carries the king's confession because the confession is the keystone: the reason, the justification, the *truth* of the erasure. As long as the anchor exists and remembers, the fold holds." She met Jude's eyes. "You are the anchor, Yarrow. You've probably worked that out. You're the only person in the world who remembers Cael, because you were meant to be the only person in the world who remembers Cael. Your whole life—the stealing, the roads, the survival—was the ritual wearing you like a glove."

"Then I open the Door," Jude said, "and the seam slips, and the fold unfolds, and Cael comes back."

"And the anchor is spent," Ilse said. "The knot released. The thread pulled free. And everything the fold was holding shut—" she spread her raw hands, helpless, "—comes back with it. The god wakes under a restored palace, in a kingdom full of returned and rejoicing people, with no binding renewed in three generations and no king left alive who knows how. Yarrow, restoring Cael doesn't save it. Restoring Cael is setting the table. The Door doesn't bring them home. The Door lets the thing under the throne come home *to them*."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the bells finished their peal, and the evening went very quiet.

"There has to be another way," Sera said. It was the first thing she had said since *his mercy*. Her hand was on her sword hilt, and her voice had the flat calm Jude had heard exactly once before—at the Salt Gate, holding it alone. "A binding renegotiated. The confession says—I remember fragments now, I remember *him*—" her voice caught and steadied, "—he believed it could be done. Renegotiated, with the truth known. Public. That's why he wanted the confession kept, not erased entirely. Why hide the reason if you never intend the reason to matter?"

"Which is why the anchor carries it," Ilse said, and for the first time she smiled, thin and fierce and sad. "Yes. The king gambled, in his last hour. He gave the Wardens their quarantine, and he hid a seed inside it—the confession, entrusted to a thief, because a thief would carry it where no Warden would think to look. He gambled that someday someone would stand at the Door with the truth in hand and find a third road: not eternal erasure, not blind restoration, but *renegotiation*. A new binding, sworn not by one dying king but by a whole living nation that knows what sleeps beneath it." She looked at Jude. "But a gamble is not a plan, Yarrow. The third road may not exist. And the Door is openable tonight, by you, and the easy way leads home, and everything in you that has lost Maren and Vask and your own name is screaming to take it."

Jude stood in the lamplight with the king's confession burning in their chest like a coal wrapped in cloth, and understood, finally, the full shape of the choice that had been walking beside them since the night the stars went out.

Loyalty said: *open the Door. Bring them home. Four million people are breathing right now in a folded drawer, baking bread that doesn't exist, and every day you hesitate is a day you choose your caution over their lives.*

Truth said: *the dead king trusted you with his reasons, not just his request. Open the Door blind and you honor his plea while betraying his warning. You'll trade a quarantine for a feast.*

"We don't open it," Jude said slowly, "until we know how to renegotiate the binding. We don't open it until the truth of what's under the throne is public—in the restored kingdom, sworn aloud by people who choose to stay knowing. Which means..."

"Which means," Ilse said, "the confession has to come out of you *before* the anchor is spent. Copied. Spread. Taught. Every rite the village knows, scaled to a nation." She exhaled. "And the only place in the world where the mechanics of that are written down is the Warden archive under the High Warden's seat. Where I defected from. Where they took my sister hostage when I ran."

The fire popped. Sera drew a slow breath, and Jude watched her arrive, visibly, at the same place they were arriving: that the road forward led directly back through everything that hunted them.

Outside, far under the snow and the buried orchard, the drowned bell rang once—soft, patient, almost fond—and the horses in the village paddock began, all together, to scream.

Ilse lifted her bound hands.

"Untie me now," she said. "And ride light. The High Warden knows where we are. She always did."
