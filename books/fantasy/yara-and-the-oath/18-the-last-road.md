## 18. The Last Road

The mountain did not want them.

Jude had known it from the first switchback, from the way the wind came off the white shoulders of the range not as weather but as intention, a long cold exhale that seemed to say *turn back* in a language older than any kingdom. The snow lay deep and blue-shadowed above the tree line, unbroken by any track, and where their boots punched through the crust the holes filled almost at once, as if the world were quietly erasing them even now, even here, even one step at a time.

They climbed anyway.

The king's voice went ahead of them into the thin air—Sera's voice carrying it, rather, pitched low and steady against the wind, the confession unwinding line by line like rope paid out down a cliff face. *I bound what should never have been bound. I made my people the lock. I could not bear to turn the key.* She did not stumble over the words. Jude had taught her them twice, three times, and each time she took them into herself the way she took everything: completely, without flinching, as if her body had been built for holding things other people couldn't carry.

Jude walked beside her and forgot.

It happened gently, which was the cruelest part. Not like the hounds pulling memories out of travelers on the pass—that had been violence, teeth and cold and the sick lurch of something torn loose. This was softer. This was a tide going out. They would be thinking of Maren's cellar, the smell of lamp oil and dried apples, the old woman's laugh like a gate swinging—and then they would reach for the next detail and find nothing there, a smooth blank wall where a life had been. They knew Maren had mattered. They knew the name still, because Sera kept saying it. But the face was gone. The voice was gone. Somewhere behind them, down the white slopes, a woman had died buying them time with a name on her lips, and Jude could no longer remember what she had looked like when she said it.

"Tell me about Vask," Jude said, because that was how it worked now. That was the litany. You asked, and Sera answered, and between the asking and the answering you got to keep a thing a few hours longer, the way you cup water in your hands knowing it will run through.

Sera didn't break stride. "The memory market opens at dusk. Lanterns strung between the stalls like captive stars—before the real ones vanished, I mean. A broker named Ossian wears rings on every finger and lies with beautiful manners. There was a woman selling roasted chestnuts near the Salt Gate who gave you an extra handful and wouldn't take payment." She glanced sideways. "You cried about the chestnuts. You said it was the wind."

"It was the wind."

"It wasn't."

Jude laughed, or something close to it, and the sound cracked in the dry air. Below them the lowlands had disappeared entirely beneath cloud; above them the pass narrowed toward a col of black stone where, if Ilse had told the truth, the Door waited in its shelf of rock like a wound that had learned to wait. Two days' climb. Maybe less. Behind them—far below, invisible now but never far enough—the High Warden came on.

Jude could feel him the way you feel a storm front. Not footsteps. Pressure. A vast patience moving uphill through the snow, unhurried, because he did not need to hurry. He had erased a country. He could erase a thief. All he had to do was arrive.

* * *

They sheltered that night in a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, backs to the stone, sharing a block of travel bread that tasted of nothing. Sera built no fire; fire would show. Instead she wrapped her cloak around them both, and in the dark, with her breath fogging, she began the litany again—not the king this time, but Jude.

"Your name is Jude Yarrow," she said. "You were born in Cael, in the river quarter of the capital, in a house with green shutters that your mother repainted every spring whether it needed it or not."

"I don't remember the shutters."

"You do. You told me. The paint was the color of new leaves and your mother let you stir the bucket and you got it on your nose and she laughed so hard she had to sit down on the step." Sera's voice was even, unhurried, a hand on a rope. "Your father mended nets. You stole your first memory at seven years old, by accident, from a ferryman—you brushed his wrist reaching for a dropped coin and suddenly you knew what it felt like to drown and be pulled out, and you cried for a day, and he never knew why the little Yarrow child wept."

"That's mine," Jude whispered. "That one's still mine."

"Then hold it. Hold it hard." She shifted closer. "Maren had a cellar full of contraband and kindness. She remembered you as a child, before the roads took you. She called you—" Sera paused, and Jude felt her choosing, carefully, whether to spend the name. "She called you *little wren*. And when the Wardens came through her door she said it one last time, and then she bought us the length of a hallway with her life, and it was enough. It was exactly enough."

Tears froze on Jude's cheeks before they could fall properly. They didn't wipe them away. Wiping away would mean acknowledging, and acknowledging might mean examining, and examining might mean losing the shape of the grief itself, and grief was all they had left of her.

"Sera." Their voice came out small. Smaller than they'd ever heard it. "What do I look like?"

She was quiet a moment. In the dark, in the cold, with the mountain listening.

"You look like someone who has spent their whole life giving other people their pasts back," she said at last, "and never once believed they deserved one of their own. You have grey eyes. You have a scar on your left eyebrow from a knife fight in Thornfell that you won by talking instead of fighting. When you're frightened—and you are frightened now—you press your thumb against two fingers, like this." She found their hand in the dark and pressed their thumb against their fingers, showing them their own habit. "And when you look at me like you're looking at me right now, I forget, briefly, that I ever lost anything at all."

The wind screamed across the mouth of the hollow. Neither of them moved.

"I'm forgetting myself," Jude said. "By tomorrow I won't know my own face in the ice. By the Door I'll be nobody walking beside you. Why does that not make you leave?"

"Because I spent half my life as a sword with no memory and I know what's on the other side of forgetting." Her voice roughened. "Someone carried me. Someone spoke me back into the world when I had nothing. You think I'd walk away now that the debt runs the other direction?" She shook her head, and Jude felt the motion more than saw it. "No. You don't get to be nobody, Jude Yarrow. I won't allow it. I'll say your name until the mountain is sick of it. I'll say it at the Door itself. Whatever the anchor must be spent on, it will not be spent on being *unremembered*."

* * *

They climbed through the second day in a silence broken only by the litany.

It became a rhythm, a heartbeat for the ascent: Sera speaking, Jude holding on. The king's confession. Maren's cellar. Vask's lanterns. The chestnut woman. The green shutters. The ferryman's drowned-and-saved terror. Each memory spoken aloud, given twice—once into the air where the mountain could hear it, once into Jude where it could burn a little longer. And all the while the pressure behind them grew, patient and immense, and Jude began to understand with a terrible clarity that they were not outrunning the High Warden at all.

They were racing themselves.

By midafternoon Jude could no longer picture the waystation where they'd hidden with the king's memory burning in their chest. By late afternoon the face of the broker Ossian dissolved mid-thought, leaving only the word *rings*. They tested themselves constantly, the way you probe a tooth: Maren—gone, a name without a body. Vask—a smear of light. The king's confession—still there, barely, held only because Sera spoke it hourly, pouring it back into them like water into a leaking cask.

And then, crossing a wind-scoured shoulder of black rock where the snow had been stripped to naked stone, Jude stopped, and looked at their own reflection in a sheet of ice glazed over a hollow, and did not know the person looking back.

Grey eyes. Yes. A stranger's eyes. A stranger's sharp-boned, wind-burned face. They raised a hand; the stranger raised a hand. Somewhere inside, a faint horror rang like a bell in an empty house—but even the horror felt secondhand, a story they'd been told about themselves.

"*Jude,*" Sera said, and caught their shoulders, and turned them from the ice. "*Look at me.*"

They looked at her. That, at least, remained. Whatever else the erasure devoured, it did not seem to touch her—or perhaps it simply hadn't reached her yet, and the thought arrived and passed like a cold draft.

"Say it with me," she said. "All of it. Every piece I've given you. Out loud, together, until we reach the Door. If the mountain wants to take you, it can work for every syllable."

So they spoke together, climbing, two voices braided against the wind—her steady and theirs fraying, but braided nonetheless. The confession. The names. The small bright worthless precious things: green shutters, chestnuts, a laugh like a gate swinging. And Jude found, as the light slanted long and red across the snow, that the words had changed weight. They were no longer only a hoard being defended. They were an offering being prepared. Every memory spoken aloud loosened its roots in them a little, and Jude let it happen, deliberately now, feeling the way each one grew lighter, more portable, less *theirs* and more *true*—truth being the one thing that could survive outside them.

If the anchor must be spent, then let it be spent well. Let every stolen year buy back a kingdom. Let the last thing Jude Yarrow ever did be the first generous thing they'd ever done with what they'd taken.

* * *

They crested the col at dusk.

The Door stood in a shelf of black stone at the head of a cirque of peaks, and it was not a door in any way the word had ever meant before. It was a seam in the world—a vertical line of pale gold light, thin as a knife cut, taller than three men, set into bare rock with no frame, no hinges, no handle. Snow fell around it and did not land. Wind reached it and stopped. The air within arm's length of it was perfectly, terribly still, and standing before it, Jude felt every remaining memory in their chest lean toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"There," Sera breathed.

But Jude was already turning, because the pressure that had followed them for days had stopped being distant.

He came up the final slope without hurry, a tall figure in grey, unbothered by the cold, snow parting around his boots as though it knew better than to touch him. His hood was back. His face was calm and ageless and kind in the way that disasters are described as kind, afterward, by survivors trying to make sense of them. He stopped twenty paces away, at the edge of the stillness, and looked at Jude with an expression of genuine, almost gentle regret.

"The anchor walks to the door on its own feet," the High Warden said. His voice was quiet, and it carried perfectly in the dead air. "In four hundred years of the craft, that has never once happened. Do you know why, thief? Because every anchor before you understood what you refuse to. If you open that Door, you do not restore Cael. You release what sleeps behind it. Everything the quarantined god touches, it unmakes—not with malice, but the way water unmakes sugar. Your kingdom comes back *inside its hunger.*"

"Liar," Sera said, hand on her sword.

"I have no reason to lie." He spread his hands, sorrowful, reasonable. "Ilse told you half a truth, because Ilse loved your king and hated what we did and could not bear the whole of it. Here is the whole: the binding is failing regardless. It was always going to fail—the king knew, which is why he asked for mercy. Open the Door, and it fails today, on your terms, with your confession shouted from the rooftops and the living gathered to renegotiate. Keep it closed, and it fails in a generation anyway, unwitnessed, unprepared. Those are your choices, thief. Not restoration versus ruin." His pale eyes held Jude's. "*Ruin now, witnessed, versus ruin later, alone.*"

"And if I walk away?" Jude asked. Their voice sounded very far away to themselves, thin as the last thread of a spider's web. "You offered once before. Walk away, stay anonymous, keep surviving."

"And I offer it still. My final offer, and it is honest: turn south, and you keep whatever fragments remain of yourself. A nameless life in some warm city. Years of them. The Door stays sealed another lifetime." He tilted his head. "Survival, thief. The thing you have traded kingdoms of memory to protect. It is yours. All you must surrender is everyone else."

The wind resumed around them, screaming, as if released from a held breath. Sera's hand was iron on Jude's arm. And Jude Yarrow—who had spent a lifetime keeping, hoarding, surviving, whose every rule had been *never keep, never feel*, whose whole art was letting go of other people's pasts before those pasts could cost them anything—looked at the Door, and at the Warden, and at the knight who had spoken them back into existence all the way up the mountain.

They pressed their thumb against two fingers, one last time.

"No," Jude said. "Survival without truth is just a longer erasure. You taught a whole country that lesson, Warden. I'm the only one who learned it."

And they stepped past him, into the stillness, toward the gold.
