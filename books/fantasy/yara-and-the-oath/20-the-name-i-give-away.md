## 20. The Name I Give Away

The first memory to leave Jude was not the king's.

It was a baker's, stolen in a border town whose name Jude no longer owned the right to remember—a woman's memory of kneading dough at dawn while her mother sang off-key, flour ghosting up into lamplight like slow snow. Jude had carried it for eleven years without once tasting it. Now it tore free of them like a hook from flesh, and Jude felt it go, felt the shape of warm bread and a dead woman's off-key voice pass out through their palm and into the Door, and they understood with terrible clarity that every memory after it would hurt worse, because they had broken their own rule at last: somewhere on this road, without noticing, they had begun to keep.

*Never keep, never feel.* Their father's rules, or their mother's, or their own, invented young in some alley they could no longer picture. The hoard had always been cargo. Coin. Passage. A thief does not weep over the purse.

But the baker's memory was gone now, and Jude was weeping.

* * *

The Door stood open, and it was not a door.

It was a threshold cut into the white mountain's crown, a gap in the world roughly the size of a person, filled with neither light nor dark but with an attentive nothing—a nothing that leaned toward Jude the way a starving thing leans toward warmth. Beyond it, if the king's dying words were true, lay the binding: the vault beneath the vault, the knot the king had tied with his own life and then begged a knight to cut, because he could not bear to untie it himself and could not bear to leave it tied for what would come after him either.

And behind Jude—ten feet, then eight—the absence came on.

The High Warden did not walk. That had been the horror of it on the ascent, watching the grey figure descend the switchbacks to meet them: it moved the way frost moves across a window, arriving everywhere at once without seeming to travel. Where it passed, snow did not melt or crunch; it simply stopped having been disturbed. Birds mid-flight above the pass hung suspended, then dropped, unremembered by their own wings. And its voice, when it spoke, arrived already inside Jude's skull, patient as arithmetic.

"You are spending yourself," it said. "I can feel you thinning from here. It is a remarkable sensation—like watching a candle decide to be a flame no longer. Walk away, carrier. Keep the hoard. Keep your name. There are roads enough south of here that no Warden will ever trouble; anonymity is not so high a price for survival. You have paid worse."

Jude's hand stayed against the threshold. The lock beneath their palm turned another degree, hungry, asking.

"I have spent my whole life trading other people's pasts," Jude said. Their voice came out cracked and small in all that white. "You know what nobody ever told me about stealing memories? What they never warn you about?"

"Enlighten me."

"That you end up carrying everyone's deaths and no one's life." Jude laughed, and it came out wrong, wet. "I have held a hundred people's last moments. I know how kings die and how farmers die and how children die, and I have never once known how anyone *lived*, because living doesn't weigh enough to steal. Survival without truth is just a longer erasure. You taught me that. You erased a whole country, and I'm the only proof it happened, and you've been hunting me across three provinces to make the proof stop walking around."

"The country was a wound. We closed it."

"The country was *people*." Six feet. Frost-quiet. Jude could see Sera now in the corner of their eye, sword drawn, standing between them and the advancing absence with her weight low and her breath smoking, and she did not remember her own name yet—only remembered it now in fragments, in flashes, *Sera, Sera, the gate at Vask, I held the gate*—but her body knew its work. Her body had always known. "And I am done being the only one who remembers them. Take a look at what I'm holding, Warden. Go on. Look inside the thief."

For the first time, the High Warden hesitated.

* * *

The hoard opened like a chest underwater.

Jude did not push the memories out. That was not how the giving worked—they had learned the mechanics of theft at nine years old, the cupping of hands, the gentle extraction, but giving was different, giving was a door opening from the inside—and so instead they simply unlocked themselves, everything, all of it at once, and let the Door read them the way a customs officer reads a manifest.

A midwife's memory of a birth in a burning village, both mother and child surviving against every law of the body. A smuggler's knowledge of forty-one ways over the Thornfell passes, thirty-seven of which no longer existed anywhere but in Jude. A soldier's last sight of his brother's face. A drunkard's memory of his daughter's wedding, which he'd sold for wine and mourned sober ever after. A child's memory of a red kite, string burning through small fingers as it climbed. Maren's cellar, the smell of apples and lamp oil, her crooked smile saying *you were a skinny little thing, always stealing crusts from my counter—I kept them for you, you know, after a while. I liked being robbed by you.*

Gone. All of it, going, the Door drinking them down and the lock turning, turning, and with each memory that left, Jude felt lighter and less, a manuscript with pages lifting out one by one into wind.

And beneath the cargo, beneath the coin, the keel of them: the king.

The confession rose last, because it was oldest and heaviest, a stone at the bottom of a well rising slow through darkening water. Jude had carried it since before they understood what it was—stolen in a deathbed room they'd crept into at fourteen for something easy, a noble's embarrassment, a minister's bribe, and found instead a dying man gripping a knight's wrist and saying, *remember this even if no one else can.*

Now it surfaced, and the whole white mountainside heard it.

*My name is Aldric IV, and I am the last king of Cael, and I am dying of a wound I asked for.* The voice poured through the threshold and out into the air itself, into the snow, into the bones of the pass, and Sera went rigid as though a hand had closed on her spine, because she knew that voice, she had knelt to that voice, she had—that was coming, that part was coming—

*I bound a god beneath my capital. Not conquered. Bound. It came with the land, older than the land, and my ancestors fed it oaths and festivals and it slept, mostly, and I thought myself cleverer than my ancestors. I starved it of ceremony. I told myself it was mercy, modernity, that a kingdom need not feed its old fears to be safe from them. It woke hungry. It ate the plague into being—you have heard of the weeping years, whoever hears this; the Wardens will have told you it was quarantine, necessity. It was my arithmetic. My hunger, borrowed.*

*The Wardens came to me with two mercies and called them one. Kill the god, they could not—no more could my ancestors; binding was all binding was for. But they could erase the kingdom entire, every mind, every map, every debt the land owed and was owed, and in the erasure the god would sleep unfed forever, because a god sleeps when nothing remembers to fear it. That was their offer: forget me, forget yourselves, forget the god, and live. Unremembered safety. I refused it, because I was a coward twice over—I could not bear to release the binding and face what I had woken, and I could not bear to sign my people's names away. So I chose a third road. I asked my knight to kill me, that my death, freely confessed, might stand outside the erasure as a splinter of truth—a seed. A binding renegotiated needs a truth spoken aloud, freely, by the one who bound. She gave me mercy. I give you this. Hear it. Argue with it. Renegotiate. Do not simply obey, as I obeyed my own fear. That is the whole of my confession, and I die asking your forgiveness for making you inherit it—*

The voice ended. The memory tore free.

Jude felt it go like a tooth leaving the jaw, and staggered, and the Door drank the confession down—and then, wondrously, gave it back. Not to Jude. To the air. To the pass, to the snowfields below where the erased survivors' village still told its nightly stories, to the wide world where maps waited blank and minds waited hollowed. The truth, once given freely, did not stay locked in any single keeper. It scattered like seed. Somewhere down in the lowlands, a shepherd who could not have said what country grazed his sheep paused, frowned, and said aloud, "Cael," tasting the word, and the word agreed with him.

The lock under Jude's palm shuddered—and did not open.

Because a binding renegotiated needs a truth spoken *and a price paid*, and the truth had been paid out, but the price was still climbing out of Jude like blood from a deep cut, and the Door was not finished with them.

* * *

"No," the High Warden said.

It was the first thing it had said that sounded like a voice instead of a verdict.

Jude turned, swaying, nearly empty now—the hoard down to embers, the last common memories guttering out—and saw the absence stopped four feet away, frost-still, and Sera planted before it with her blade bare and her eyes streaming, because she had heard everything, she had heard her king ask for her hand and her mercy, and she was not attacking, she was doing something stranger: she was speaking.

"—your oath was to protect the realm," she was saying, low and fierce, tears freezing on her jaw, "and the realm is *people*, and you erased the people, so tell me, Warden, tell me to my face whom you served—" and her sword began to shine, faintly, the way steel shines when it remembers being sworn, and the High Warden's attention split, half on the anchor at the Door, half on the knight who should have been nothing but a hollow instrument and was somehow, impossibly, an argument.

"Knight," it said. "You were ours. We made you forget the sin so you could live."

"She made me forget," Sera said, "so I could choose again," and she struck—not at the Warden, there was nothing solid to strike, but at the *absence* it cast, cutting the shadow of it clean from the snow, and where her blade passed the frost-stillness broke and birds fell upward, startled back into their own wings, and ten feet of mountainside remembered how to be ordinary ground.

The High Warden moved toward her.

Jude did not think. Thinking was for people with futures they were hoarding. Jude simply reached into the last chamber of themselves—the one they had been saving, saving without admitting they were saving it, the chamber that held not stolen goods but the only possessions that had ever truly been theirs: a name, *Jude Yarrow*; a childhood of alleys and crusts and cold; a mother's face they had guarded for twenty years like a coal in cupped hands; the memory of the first time they'd stolen, aged seven, and the shame, and the strange bright freedom after—and poured it all into the lock.

*Here,* they thought, offering themselves up like coins to a blind toll-taker. *Not just what I took. What I am. Take the whole purse.*

The Door accepted.

* * *

It did not hurt, exactly. It was more honest than pain. It was subtraction.

The name went first, and Jude watched it go—felt the word *Jude* loosen from their selfhood like a burr from wool and slide away into the mechanism, and with it the shape of their mother's voice calling it across a courtyard that no longer existed anywhere, and with it the courtyard, and with it—

—the alleys. Gone. The crusts stolen from Maren's counter—Maren, who was that, why did the name ache like a healed break?—gone. The first theft, the shame, the freedom: gone. Their own face, which they had checked in a Vask mirror only weeks ago and barely recognized: gone, and worse, the checking, the habit of checking, the whole continuous thread of *I, I, I* unraveling stitch by stitch into the lock, and Jude stood at the threshold of a restored kingdom with empty hands and an empty interior and thought, distantly, with the last coherent part of themselves: *this is what I did to a hundred people. This is what it feels like from the inside. Oh. Oh, I am sorry. I am so sorry.*

The lock turned fully.

The Door became, briefly, a door—wooden, iron-banded, absurdly ordinary, the kind found in any farmstead in any foothill—and then it opened inward onto green.

Not the nothing. Green. Grass running down a mountainside that had been snow a heartbeat before, wildflowers opening in fast-forward, a sky filling with stars that had been missing since autumn, constellations clicking home one by one like a ledger balancing. Below, the fog-choked lowlands exhaled; rivers remembered their names; a city rebuilt itself in the minds of its own fleeing citizens, who stopped on the roads and turned around and said *home* in a language that had, until this moment, been only rhythm to them. Maps, in a hundred cartographers' shops across three nations, blistered and reinked themselves in the space of an eyeblink, borders crawling back like scars fading.

And beneath the green world, deep, deeper than roots, something vast turned over in its sleep—tasted the truth of the king's confession riding the wind, tasted the renegotiation implicit in it, oaths owed and owing, a kingdom returned and therefore a kingdom's fealty restored, feeding, feeding, *fed*—and settled, sated not with fear but with honesty, back into its long bound dark.

It stayed bound. That was the miracle no one had dared hope for: the god did not rise. It only dreamed, and its dream, for the first time in centuries, was not of hunger.

The High Warden stood very still amid the returning spring.

Around it, the world it had unmade reassembled itself, and Jude watched—nameless, hollowed, kneeling in new grass—watched the absence begin to shrink, because an erasure requires an emptiness to fill, and the emptiness was full now. Full of Cael. Full of bread-kneading and red kites and a drunkard's daughter dancing and a king's last brave cowardly human words.

"You have unmade our work," the Warden said. Its voice was smaller now. Arithmetic losing count.

"No," Jude said—or tried to say, and found they had to reach for the words, that speech still worked but ownership of it was strange, a borrowed coat. "You made a quarantine. Someone else made a cure. Turns out it was just—telling the truth out loud. Cheapest magic there is."

The High Warden looked at them for a long moment. And then—it did something Jude had not imagined it capable of, something that would take years to understand: it inclined its head. Fractionally. The way one craftsman acknowledges another's work.

"The anchor survives," it said. "Spent, but surviving. That was not in the design." A pause, frost forming and reforming. "Neither was the knight." And then the absence folded inward like a letter being unsent, and the grey went out of the air, and the pass was only a pass, snowmelt ringing in the stones, and the war—for that day, at least—was over.

* * *

Sera found them there, kneeling in the grass where snow had been, staring at their own hands as if the hands belonged to a stranger, which was precisely the problem: they did.

She was bleeding from a dozen shallow cuts where the Warden's edges had grazed her, her oath-break sword still bare in her fist, her face wrecked and lit, and when she reached them she did not embrace them, because she had seen the look in their eyes and understood what it meant before they could find words for it.

"Do you know me?" she asked quietly.

Jude searched. There was warmth there, an instinct, the way a hand knows fire—but the history, the bargain on a fog-road, the fragments of Cael traded for her sword, the cellar, the market, the mountains—all of it was smooth blankness, a wall where shelves had been.

"No," Jude said. The voice that came out was theirs and not theirs, unowned, a stranger's instrument. "Should I?"

Sera sat down in the grass beside them. She sheathed her sword with shaking hands. Above them the returned stars burned, all of them, every constellation home, and somewhere far below a shepherd was shouting the name of his country at the sky, and a hundred thousand people were waking to lives they'd lost without knowing they'd lost them, grieving and rejoicing over memories that had never been theirs to carry.

"Yes," Sera said. "As it happens, you should. And since you don't—" she took a breath, wiped her face, looked at this nameless stranger who had bought a kingdom with everything they were, "—someone has to tell you. So. Once, on a rain-slicked road in Thornfell, there was a thief in a green coat, and I put a sword to their throat, and they offered me a deal I couldn't refuse—"

Jude listened. It was, they would think later, the strangest theft of all: someone stealing their life back into them, story by story, freely given.

"—and the first thing you said to me," Sera went on, "the very first thing, before the deal, before anything, was—"

She stopped.

"What?" Jude asked. "What did I say?"

Sera looked at them—at the emptied stranger in the grass beneath the returned stars—and something in her face broke open, tender and terrified at once.

"You said," she said, "*you look like someone worth remembering.*"

And in the hollow place where a name used to live, something stirred. Not a memory. A seed.
