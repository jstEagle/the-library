## 21. After the Stars Return

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

Sera knew this because the innkeeper in the lowland town of Harrow's Ford said it three times that morning, to everyone who passed his door, as though repetition could make the miracle stick to the world like mortar. *Tuesday*, he said, beaming, *the stars came back on a Tuesday.* He had no way of knowing what day it had been before—no one did—but he had found an old almanac in his cellar, water-stained and half-eaten by mice, and the almanac had settled the matter for him, and he clung to that small certainty the way a drowning man clings to driftwood.

Sera understood that impulse better than anyone alive.

She stood in the market square of Harrow's Ford and watched people remember. It was not a gentle thing to witness. An old woman dropped a basket of apples and began to weep, not because the apples were lost but because she had suddenly recalled her son's face—a son who had been erased and returned and was now, impossibly, standing at a stall across the square selling rope, older than she remembered him being, because five years had passed inside the erasure and none of them had lived them. A man knelt in the mud and pressed his palms flat against the cobblestones as though checking whether the ground itself were real. Children ran through the streets shouting names—the names of streets, of neighbors, of a kingdom—and each name landed like a struck bell, ringing out over rooftops that had been nameless only yesterday.

Cael existed again. The word itself had weight now, had borders and a king's tomb and a history that stretched back further than any living memory. Maps were already being redrawn; Sera had seen a cartographer in the next town working by candlelight through the whole night, weeping as he inked coastlines he had never known he'd lost.

And in the middle of all this returning, one person remained unreturned.

* * *

Sera found Jude—or the person who had been Jude—eleven days after the Door closed, sitting on the sea wall at Greyhaven, watching the tide come in with the patient blankness of someone watching weather.

She had been searching since the mountain. She had carried Jude down from the white slopes herself when the anchor-spent body finally gave out, had wrapped them in her cloak against the killing cold, had talked to them the entire descent even though they did not answer, did not seem to hear, did not seem to know their own eyes were open. She had told them about Maren's cellar and the smell of lamp oil and dried plums. She had told them about Vask and the memory market and the broker Ossian with his rings and his soft voice. She had recited the king's confession word for word, though she had never heard it spoken aloud until Jude gave it to her, and now she carried it the way soldiers carry shrapnel—too deep to cut out, too much a part of her to wish it gone.

At the foot of the mountains she had left Jude in the care of the village that wasn't there—the village that was there again now, its survivors weeping and laughing and telling their stories anyway, because telling stories was the only rite they trusted. And then she had gone looking, because the healers said the mind might mend if given time and familiar things, and Sera intended to be the most familiar thing left in the world.

She had checked every town along the Thornfell road. She had asked after a thief in a long coat, a memory-stealer, a person with clever hands and wary eyes. No one knew the description. No one had seen such a person. Of course they hadn't. The person she described had been made entirely of stolen years, and every one of those years now belonged back where it came from.

But she kept dreaming of the sea wall. Not a memory—she had never been to Greyhaven—but a dream, the kind of dream that had once led her across fog-choked lowlands toward a stranger in a thief's coat. Whatever thread had first tied her to Jude Yarrow, it had survived the spending of everything else. So she followed it south, past the salt marshes, into the grey smell of brine and kelp, and there they sat, exactly as the dream had promised: small against the vast slate of the ocean, hands loose in their lap, coat still long, still patched at the elbows, still smelling faintly of woodsmoke and old paper.

They turned when her boots rang on the stone. Their eyes were Jude's eyes—dark, quick, always measuring exits—and yet they were not, because behind them there was nothing now. No hoard. No ledger of other people's yesterdays. Just a person, looking at a stranger.

"You've been walking," Sera said. It wasn't a question. There was road dust to the knee, and their boots were ruined.

"Have I?" The voice was Jude's too, but lighter, unburdened, like a bell with the clapper removed. "I suppose I have. I woke up in a village full of people who cried when they saw me. They fed me and called me *anchor* and *savior* and I didn't know which one was my name, so I left." A pause. The tide sucked at the stones below. "Do you know which one is my name?"

"No," Sera said honestly. She sat down on the sea wall beside them, leaving a careful hand's width of space. "Neither do I."

That seemed to satisfy them. They looked back at the water.

"I keep almost-remembering," they said after a while. "There's a shape in me where something used to sit. Like a bruise you can't stop pressing. When I try to look at it directly, it's—" They flexed their fingers, the clever hands, the thief's hands, reaching for something that was no longer there to steal. "It's gone. But the shape of it hurts."

"I know," Sera said. "I remember what that's like."

"Do you?" They glanced at her, and there—in the corner of their mouth, in the tilt of the head—was a ghost of the wariness that had once been armor. "You have a name, though. You got it back at a gate, holding a line against men in grey. You screamed it so loud the gulls left the cliffs." They frowned, pressing at the bruise again. "Why do I know that? I wasn't there."

"I told you," Sera said. "On the mountain. While I carried you down."

Their brow furrowed. "Did you? I don't remember."

"No," Sera agreed softly. "But some part of you does. That's why I'm here. I'm going to keep telling you, until the part that listens is bigger than the part that's empty."

* * *

They stayed in Greyhaven because it was the last town on the road and because neither of them seemed able to leave the sea. Sera took a room above a chandlery that smelled of tar and hemp, and the innkeeper, a broad woman named Doss with forearms like hawsers, asked no questions beyond whether the knight would be wanting breakfast and whether her quiet friend ate fish. Both answers were yes. It was the simplest transaction Sera had been part of in years, and she nearly wept over the fish stew.

The nightly telling began that first evening.

Sera had learned the shape of it from the village in the white mountains—from Elder Brannic and his circle of survivors who had refused to let themselves be forgotten, who gathered every dusk and spoke their histories aloud into the cold air so that the words would take root in the children before sleep could wash them away. *A thing told nightly cannot be erased,* Brannic had said. *Memory is a fire. It wants feeding.*

So she fed it.

She started small, because she was learning the terrain of Jude's emptiness the way she had once learned battlefields—one careful step at a time, testing for what held. She told them about rain on a smuggler road in Thornfell, about a toll-gate keeper named Hesk who took payment in first loves and paid in safe passage. She watched Jude's face as she spoke, and when she reached the part about the rules—*never keep, never feel*—something flickered there, a muscle-memory of wry self-knowledge, gone as fast as it came.

"That sounds like a lonely way to live," they said.

"It was," Sera said. "That's the point of the story. Keep listening."

Night after night. She told them about the waystation and the king's confession, how a dying man had whispered a bargain into the ear of a stranger who stole memories for bread, and how that stranger had carried the weight of a whole country without knowing what it was. She told them about the Wardens' hounds in the pass, and the decoy memory fed to a grey beast with too many teeth, and the mark it left—a cold spot between the shoulder blades that ached before danger. She touched that spot now, lightly, through the cloth, and Jude flinched and then stared at her hand as though it had performed magic.

"How did you know that was there?"

"You told me. In the mountains. You were trying to teach me the route in case you forgot it." Her throat tightened. "You were forgetting Maren even then. You kept saying *Maren* like you were tasting the word, trying to hold the flavor. And then one morning you said, *who is Maren?* and I thought my heart would crack down the middle."

"Maren," Jude repeated. The name meant nothing to them. Sera saw that clearly, and it hurt, and she let it hurt, because Brannic had taught her that too: grief was part of the rite. You did not skip the sad verses. The sad verses were the load-bearing walls.

"Maren kept a cellar under a false floor in a house that smelled of lamp oil," Sera said. "Maren cheated at cards and won anyway. Maren remembered you as a child, before you were a thief—remembered a kid with quick hands who could pick a lock at six and cried at songs. Maren died buying us twenty minutes with a crossbow and a bottle of burning spirits, and the last thing Maren said was your real childhood name." She stopped. "Which I'm not going to tell you yet."

Jude looked up sharply. For a moment the old alertness blazed through the blankness, and Sera's chest ached with recognition. "Why not?"

"Because it isn't mine to give," Sera said. "It died with Maren, and it belongs to whoever you decide to become. When you choose a name—if you choose one—it should be yours. Freely taken. Or freely given." She smiled, thin and tired. "You'd understand the distinction. You spent your whole life learning it."

* * *

Some nights the telling went badly.

One evening, four days in, she described the memory market in Vask—the lantern-lit stalls, the brokers weighing vials of other people's childhoods, the moment Jude had glimpsed their own memory for sale, a thing they had never sold, and understood that someone was carving pieces off them in the dark. She had expected recognition, or at least the ache of the bruise-shape. Instead Jude stood up abruptly, knocking the stool over, and walked to the window, and stood there shaking.

"I can't," they said. "I can't listen to this. It's like hearing someone describe a room I've never been in and knowing—I *knowing*—that I hung the pictures myself." Their voice cracked. "Who was I? What kind of person lives like that? Stealing? Selling? Marked by monsters, hunted across a kingdom, and for *what*—"

"For survival," Sera said quietly. "For passage. For bread. And in the end, for all of us." She rose and stood beside them at the window, not touching, though every part of her wanted to. Below, the tide was coming in over the stones, black water silvering at the edges. "Listen to me. I know what you were. I know worse—I know what *I* was. I was the king's executioner, Jude. I killed him with my own hand because he begged it, because he couldn't bear to live with what he'd bound beneath Cael, and then the Wardens answered his death by erasing everyone he'd ever ruled. Two tools. That's what we were. The difference is that you broke your tool-nature by giving everything away, and I broke mine by breaking an oath. Neither of us comes out clean. But both of us come out *true*."

Jude's reflection stared at hers in the dark glass—two ghosts superimposed on the sea.

"The stars came back," they said at last, very quietly. "Everyone in this town keeps saying it. *The stars came back on a Tuesday.* Everyone has someone to remember them back into the world. Mothers, sons, kingdoms." They turned, and their eyes were wet and furious and lost. "Who remembers me back?"

"You're looking at her," Sera said.

* * *

The rite settled into a rhythm, the way rites do.

Each evening after supper, Doss's common room emptied of fishermen and Sera lit the lamp by the window seat, and Jude sat, and she told. Some nights it was one story; some nights three. She told them about Ilse the defector, and the quarantine, and the god bound beneath the king's throne—a god of unmaking, or perhaps of forgetting; even Ilse hadn't known its true nature, only that the king had chained it and the Wardens had smothered the kingdom to keep the chains quiet. She told them how the confession, once public, had let the surviving Wardens renegotiate the binding—new anchors, freely offered instead of stolen, a dozen volunteers from the village that wasn't there who now took turns holding the lock. Justice, served outside any law, by ordinary people choosing to remember.

She told them about the Door of Returning, and the High Warden's offer—*walk away, keep surviving, anonymous forever*—and how Jude had refused it. How they had poured a lifetime of stolen years into the lock, and then their own name, and then their own past, until the Door opened and Cael poured back into the world like light into a cracked jar.

Each time she finished that part, Jude was silent for a long while.

"I don't feel brave," they said once. "I feel like a hollowed gourd. Everyone out there—" they gestured at the window, at the town, at the whole resurrected kingdom beyond it— "they light fires for me. Brannic's village sings about the Anchor. There's supposed to be a mural going up in the capital. And I sit here feeling like the space where a person used to be."

"That's because you are," Sera said. "A little. The rest grows back. It's growing right now, every night you listen." She hesitated, then added, "Yarrow grows back from nothing, you know. Cut it to the root, burn the field—it returns. Farmers hate it. Healers love it. It's the weed that refuses erasure."

Jude looked at her strangely. "Why do you say it like that? Like the word matters."

"Because it will," Sera said, and would say no more.

* * *

On the twelfth night, something changed.

Sera was mid-story—she was describing the ambush at the Salt Gate, her own story, the one she told least often and therefore needed most to tell: how she had held the gate alone while Ilse was dragged away, how somewhere in the blood and noise her own name had come back to her not as a memory but as an act, *Sera, Sera, Sera*, each sword-stroke a syllable. She was describing the sound of the gate hinges screaming, and Jude was listening with their chin on their fists, the way they always did, when Jude interrupted.

"Sera."

"Yes?"

"When you tell these stories. About the thief." They paused, and their clever hands—the hands that had once picked locks at six, though they didn't know it—turned over in their lap, palm up, as though waiting to receive something. "When you talk about them. Your voice changes. Did you know that? It gets softer. Careful. The way people talk about—" They stopped. Started again. "The way you talk about someone you—"

"Tide's turning," Sera said, which was a coward's answer, and she knew it, and Jude knew it, and mercifully—for now—let it go.

But that night, after the lamp was out, Sera lay awake in the chandlery's narrow bed listening to the sea, and she admitted the truth to the darkness: she loved them. She loved the person who had been Jude Yarrow, and she loved this nameless stranger who was becoming someone new, and she suspected—with the particular terror of a woman who had lost her whole self once and rebuilt it from a single instinct—that those two loves were the same love, wearing different coats.

And she suspected something else. Something she had noticed three nights ago and told no one.

Jude was beginning to remember. Not the stolen years—those were gone forever, returned to their rightful owners. But the *shape*. The bruise was fading, and underneath it, new flesh. Small things. Yesterday Jude had hummed a snatch of song while mending their coat, a smuggler's tune from the Thornfell roads, and had stopped, startled, not knowing where it came from. This evening, when Sera described Hesk the toll-keeper, Jude had said, *"He cheated. He weighted the scales. I remember being angry about it"*—and then gone white, because they shouldn't have remembered anything, and yet they did.

Memories were returning. Not borrowed ones. New ones, or perhaps very old ones—the ones Jude had been born with, buried so deep beneath the hoard of stolen years that even the anchor's spending hadn't reached them. The child with quick hands who cried at songs. The person under the thief.

Tomorrow, Sera decided, she would tell them about the yarrow plant properly. About weeds that grow back. About names chosen freely. Because tomorrow, she suspected, they would ask her the question she had been waiting for—the question that was really a door of its own, smaller and kinder than the one in the mountain:

*Who am I allowed to become?*

And Sera, knight without an oath, keeper of a story that was still being told, would answer: *Anyone. Anyone at all.*

Outside, the stars burned steady over Greyhaven, and the tide came in, and somewhere in the dark the sea wall held, as it had held for a thousand years, against everything the water could bring.
