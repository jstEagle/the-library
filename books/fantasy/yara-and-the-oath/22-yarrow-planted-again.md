## 22. Yarrow, Planted Again

Spring came to Greyhaven the way forgiveness came—slowly, and from several directions at once.

Jude felt it first in the stones of Sera's little house above the sea wall, which held the sun's warmth long after dusk and gave it back through the night like a memory that refused to burn out. Then in the smell of it: salt and thaw and green things pushing up through cracks, stubborn as gossip. Then, last, in themselves. There was a lightness where the hoard had been, an emptiness that was not loss but room. For twenty years they had carried other people's pasts like stones sewn into a coat, and now the coat was gone, and they kept reaching for the weight of it and finding only their own thin body underneath.

A stranger's body. A stranger's face in the mirror each morning, unfamiliar as a passing traveler.

They were sitting on the doorstep with a cup of chicory cooling in their hands when Sera came up the path from the market, carrying a paper cone of roasted chestnuts and something small and green tucked behind her ear like a soldier's plume. She had taken to wearing flowers there, absurdly, gloriously, as if she were daring the world to comment. Nobody commented. You did not comment on Sera, formerly the king's executioner, currently keeper of another person's entire life, who whetted her knife each evening by lamplight with the calm of a woman saying her prayers.

"You're staring at your hands again," she said, sitting down beside them without ceremony. Their shoulders touched. That was how they sat now. Neither of them had ever once remarked upon it.

"I keep hoping they'll tell me something," Jude said.

"Hands are liars." She pressed a chestnut into their palm—their right hand, which had learned to peel them again two weeks ago as if the skill lived somewhere deeper than remembering. "Ask me instead."

* * *

This was the rite they had made between them, the one the erased villagers of the hidden valley had practiced for generations and which Sera had carried down from the mountains like an ember cupped in both palms: each night, the telling. Not all of it. Never all of it at once. A story poured out whole would scald; a story given in measures could be drunk.

"Tell me about the yarrow plant properly," Jude said, because that was what she had promised, and Sera was exacting about promises in the way of someone who had once broken the largest one ever sworn and never intended to again.

She took the flower from behind her ear and laid it across her knee. White petals gone a little bruised at the edges from the day. Feathery leaves, silver-green, soft as a secret.

"Yarrow," she said, "grows where nothing else consents to grow. Rubble. Roadside. Old battlefields—it came up over the graves at Redwater within a season, whole fields of it, white as surrender flags." Her fingers moved over the leaves while she spoke, the way they moved when she was telling the hard parts of the story, steadying herself against something solid. "Soldiers used to pack their wounds with it. Still do, in the highlands. They call it soldier's woundwort, knight's milfoil, old man's pepper—a hundred names, because everyone who ever bled near it gave it a new one."

"It's a weed," Jude said.

"It's a weed the way hope is a weed." Sera looked at them then, grey eyes steady, and Jude understood that she had chosen this lesson first for a reason, out of all the things she might have told them about who they had been. "You can pull it, burn it, pave over it. It comes back. From a root fragment the length of your finger, it comes back. Whole meadows of it, from almost nothing."

The wind came off the water and stirred the flower on her knee.

"That's why I chose it," Jude said slowly. Not a question. The knowledge arrived the way their peeled chestnuts arrived—out of some stratum of self that the Door had not consumed after all, or perhaps had grown anew, like a root fragment working its way up through rubble. "When you asked me, at the end. What name I wanted written nowhere, so no one could take it from me. I didn't know why I picked it. But I knew it was mine."

"You knew," Sera agreed. "That's the thing about you. About either of us, maybe. Everything else got spent—the hoard, the name, the childhood, all of it, poured into that lock like blood into a wound. But the choosing stayed. The choosing is the part that can't be stolen." She smiled, small and crooked. "Believe me. I've checked. I've been trying to give you back your own story every night for a season, and every morning you wake up having chosen, again, to be here. That's not memory. That's something memory doesn't have a word for."

Jude turned the chestnut over in their palm. Below them the tide was coming in, unhurried, doing the work it had always done.

"Tell me the part about Maren," they said. "I want to grieve her properly this time. Last night I only had her secondhand, from your telling, and it went through me like water through a net."

So Sera told it: the cellar in the border country, the smell of lamp oil and dried apples, the old fence who had half-remembered a thief-child named nothing much and died buying that child an hour's head start. And this time, because Sera told it slow, and stopped twice, and let the silence hold the worst of it, the grief found somewhere to pool instead of drain away. Jude wept for a woman they could not remember and would now never forget, and Sera sat close and did not say *there, there*, because she was not a liar even in kindness, and when it passed she simply began the next part, as the village elders had taught her: *and then, and then, and then.*

* * *

Cael had been back in the world for eight months now, and the world was still deciding what to do about it.

Maps had rewritten themselves overnight—cartographers in three kingdoms woke to find coastlines they had never drawn inked onto their charts in their own hand. People remembered their mothers' faces, their debts, their songs, their grudges; the returning dead of memory walked through thousands of lives at once, and Greyhaven, being nearest the Door and strangest with it, had become the place where the returned came to ask questions. Pilgrims, mostly. Historians. A surprising number of lovers separated by the erasure who had woken in different countries holding the same half of a song.

And petitioners. Always petitioners, now, because the king's confession was public—Sera had seen to that, standing in the restored hall at Cael Adair before the assembled remnant court and reading the deathbed words aloud in a voice that did not shake until the very end—and the confession had changed everything. The god beneath the mountain remained bound. But the binding was no longer a secret maintained by murder and erasure. It was a covenant, renegotiated in the open, tended by living hands. Some nights Jude lay awake and marveled at the shape of it: that truth, loosed into the world, had proven stronger than any ward. That the Wardens' great crime had failed not because anyone fought it with a sword, though Sera had certainly tried, but because one thief had been too greedy—or too lonely—to ever stop keeping what they stole.

"You're smiling," Sera said.

They were walking the sea wall at dusk, their evening habit, the chestnuts long finished. Jude hadn't noticed the smile arrive. It did that more and more lately, appearing unbidden like a neighbor's face in a doorway.

"I was thinking that I won," they said. "In the end. The Wardens offered me survival forever, anonymous, and I turned it down, and everyone said I'd sacrificed everything. But look." They gestured—at the wall, the lights coming on along the harbor road, the flower behind Sera's ear, the whole improbable spring. "The High Warden kept her power and lost the world. I gave up my name and got one back. Chosen, this time. Nobody survives their own story, Sera. But I got to keep mine."

"Some of it," Sera corrected gently. "The parts I tell you."

"The best parts, then." Jude bumped her shoulder. "You make me sound better than I was."

"I make you sound true," Sera said. "You were a smuggler and a thief and you kept a dying king's confession warm for twenty years while every power in creation hunted you for it, and you never sold it, never spent it, never once considered giving it up even when keeping it was the loneliest thing a person could be. If that sounds better than you were, then the fault is with whoever you were comparing yourself to."

They reached the bend in the wall where the old lighthouse stood dark—no one had relit it since the return; there was argument in town about whether it should guide ships or stand as memorial—and Jude stopped, because there was something they had been turning over for days the way one turns a stone in the pocket, and tonight it had worked its way smooth enough to speak.

"There's one more thing," they said. "About my gift. It came back. Not all of it—I'm not the thief I was, thank every star. But I can still take, if I touch skin and mean it. Ilse thinks it's because the taking was never really mine to spend. The Door took the coin, not the hand."

Sera went very still, the way she did. Not fear—Jude knew her fears now, all of them, told nightly like beads on a string—but attention.

"And?" she said.

"And I've been thinking about the rules." Jude looked out at the darkening water. "Never keep. Never feel. Those were my rules, and I hid behind them for twenty years, and they kept me alive and they kept me empty, and I built my whole self on the idea that a stolen thing is a spent thing. Take and pass on. Never let it take root." They turned to face her. The lighthouse rose black behind her against the first stars, and the wind pulled at her hair, and Jude's heart did what it had done for months now whenever he looked at her too long, which was behave like a root fragment refusing to accept that it had been paved over. "But there's one kind of theft I never tested. One experiment left."

Sera's breath fogged faintly in the cooling air. "Jude."

"Freely given," Jude said. "That's the variable. Every memory I ever stole was taken against the grain—fear, bargaining, desperation. What happens if it's offered? Does it burn out like the rest? Does it stay? I need to know, Sera. Because there's exactly one memory in the world I want, and it isn't mine, and I am asking, formally, with my chosen name as surety—" their voice cracked, absurdly, on the formality of it, and Sera huffed something that was almost a laugh, almost a sob— "whether you will give me yours. The one where you met me. On the smuggler road, in the rain, before either of us knew anything. You remember it. You remember everything. And I never will, not firsthand. Unless."

They held out their hand. Bare palm up, the way you offered a trade on the Thornfell roads a lifetime ago. The gesture felt like an heirloom.

For a long moment Sera didn't move. The tide breathed below them. Then she said, quietly, "You understand what you're asking. That memory is the most valuable thing I own. I've kept it polished like armor. There were nights in the mountains when it was the only proof either of us existed."

"I know."

"And if it burns out—if taking it spends it the way everything else was spent—you'll have cost me the only copy."

"I know." Jude's hand stayed steady. It had learned steadiness in worse places than a sea wall at dusk. "But here's what I think, and I've thought it since the valley. Your memory of me doesn't live in your head, Sera. It lives in your telling. Every night you pour it back into me a little at a time. If the original goes into my hand and stays there—then for the first time in my life I'll be keeping something on purpose. Not hoarding. Keeping. Like a seed in the ground instead of a stone in a coat." They swallowed. "And if it burns out anyway—then you'll tell it to me again. You're good at that. You've had practice."

Sera laughed then, really laughed, wet-eyed, and pulled off her glove, and set her bare hand in theirs.

"On purpose," she said. "Kept. Gods help us both." She bowed her head slightly, the old formal gesture of a knight yielding ground, repurposed entirely. "Take it, then, thief. And mind how you carry it. It's heavier than it looks. It's got a whole future folded inside it."

Jude closed their fingers around hers.

The taking felt nothing like it used to. No cold sliding, no picklock click of a soul's latch. It felt like warm water rising; like a door opening from the far side; like the moment on a smuggler road when a stranger in a wet coat looks up and decides, against all sense and all rules, to trust you.

Rain on grey stone. A figure in a thief's coat, wary, competent, so desperately alone. The strange certainty arriving before any reason for it: *protect this one.* And underneath it all, unmistakable, the thing Sera had carried polished through plague-remembrance and betrayal and snow:

Hope. Hers, freely given, rooted and growing.

Jude opened their eyes. The memory sat inside them, whole and warm and burning nothing, staying exactly where it was put, and they knew—with the deep unarguable knowing of a plant that has found soil—that it would keep. Some things took root when they were planted honestly. Some things were seeds.

"Well?" Sera searched their face, her hand still in theirs, her composure hanging by the thread she allowed it. "Does it stay?"

Jude Yarrow—who had been a thief, an anchor, a nameless stranger, and was now, finally, simply this—drew her close under the dark lighthouse and the returned stars, and answered the way the story deserved, the way the nightly rite demanded, beginning it again as all stories must begin:

"It stays. Now listen—I'll tell you how we met."
