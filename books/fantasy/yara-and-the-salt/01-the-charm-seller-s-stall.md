## 1. The Charm Seller's Stall

The tide came in twice a day, and so did the lies.

Orsen Kavanagh had learned to time both. The morning tide brought the fish-wives and the net-menders up from the harbor steps, smelling of brine and herring blood, and they wanted charms for luck before their boats pushed off. The afternoon tide brought everyone else — lovers wanting fidelity knots, mothers wanting fever-wards, old men wanting to hear, one more time, that their dead wives were at peace.

The lies were the same every day: *I can't really do anything, friend. What I sell is tradition, nothing more. Old words in a pretty bottle. Comfort, mostly.*

Comfort sold well in Greyharbor. Truth would have gotten Orsen burned.

They set out the stall at first light, as they had for twenty years — a plank table on trestles at the corner of Salt Row, beneath the leaning sign of the Gilded Finch, which was neither gilded nor a finch but had been painted by Orsen's predecessor and never corrected. Charms in rows. Wards on strings. Bundles of dried rowan tied with red thread. Little glass vials of harbor water that Orsen had, very quietly, whispered to.

That was the part no one could ever know. The whispering.

Because here was the truth under twenty years of careful lies: the charms worked. Not all of them, not always, but the ones Orsen spoke over while pretending to arrange the stock — those worked. A fever-ward murmured to with the right voice kept a child's burning down through the night. A luck-knot addressed properly turned an arrow aside, once, on the marsh road, and Orsen still dreamed about the sound it made skipping off bone.

And it wasn't only charms. That was the worse part. The worst part stood at the end of Salt Row right now, tail low, ribs showing through patchy brown fur, watching Orsen with the particular patience of a dog who has learned that some humans are worth waiting for.

"You're early," Orsen said, barely moving their lips.

The dog's ears came up. *Cold,* she said, in the way dogs say things — not words, exactly, but meanings that arrived in Orsen's mind wearing the shape of words, because that was the only shape Orsen could give them. *Cold. And the fish man kicked.*

*The fish man kicks everyone,* Orsen answered silently. Aloud, they said, "Fresh water, that's a copper," to no one, because a customer was drifting near, and the customer was everything.

This was the arithmetic of Orsen's whole life. Every conversation with every animal happened in the narrow space between two human heartbeats, in the pause where a buyer looked down to count coins. Twenty years of practice had made Orsen a virtuoso of the half-second. They could murmur comfort to a sparrow with a broken wing while their mouth asked after the mackerel catch. They could listen to a horse describe its abused flank and nod along as if the carter were complaining about grain prices.

It was a way to live. It was not living, quite.

The morning passed in its usual rhythm. Mrs. Aldithy bought a hearing-ward for her deaf husband and did not notice that Orsen held it a moment too long, lips pressed to the glass, breath warm. A boy named Pip bought a luck-charm for his first crew signing and paid in a coin so worn the king's face was a rumor; Orsen whispered to the charm anyway, properly, because Pip was eleven and the sea did not care about luck, only about weight and weather, and the charm might tip one wave his way.

*Small one goes to sea,* the harbor gulls reported from the rigging overhead, gossiping the way gulls did, ceaselessly and about everything. *Small one goes to sea, small one smells of bread, the bread will be dropped, the bread will be ours.*

"Leave his bread alone," Orsen muttered, and then caught themself, and coughed loudly, and turned the mutter into "—leave room for the larger sizes, madam, yes, the blue thread is stronger."

Mrs. Aldithy squinted at them. "Talking to yourself, Kavanagh?"

"Best conversation in the market," Orsen said, and smiled the smile they had worn for twenty years, the one that sat on the face like a mask sits on a nail.

She laughed and moved off. The dog at the end of the row thumped her tail once against the cobbles.

By midday the crowd thickened. Greyharbor market on a fair day was a river of noise — hawkers crying oysters, a fiddler somewhere near the Tide Gate, the clang of the smith, the endless argument of gulls — and inside all of it, threaded through it invisible, was the other market, the one only Orsen heard. Cats trading territory reports. Rats discussing which cellar had been trapped. A carthorse two streets over telling another carthorse, at length and inaccurately, about a wolf it had definitely seen. A whole hidden country of voices layered under the human one, and Orsen the only soul in Greyharbor who could hear it, which sounded like a gift until you understood it meant Orsen walked through every day of their life translating silence into company and back again.

Loneliness, Orsen had found, did not always look like loneliness. Sometimes it looked like routine. Sometimes it looked like a well-kept stall and a friendly smile and a reputation for being a bit odd, a bit quiet, harmless — harmless above all — and it went home every night to a single room above a chandler's shop and ate a single portion and spoke aloud only to say *closed* and *thank you* and *that'll be four coppers*, until the speaking part of them got so starved it whispered to moths.

There was a moth in the stall's awning now. Orsen had told it, earlier, that the lamp flame was hot and it should reconsider. The moth had not listened. Moths rarely did.

*Hungry,* said the dog, when the crowd thinned past the midday swell.

"I know." Orsen broke off a corner of the honey-bread they'd been saving and let it fall, apparently carelessly, beside the table's leg. The dog took it with the delicacy of a creature who understood that visible gratitude got strays culled. She was getting better fed than most strays, this one. Three weeks she'd been coming to the stall. In three weeks Orsen had learned: she'd belonged to a fisherman lost in the winter storm; she hated the smell of tar; she thought gulls were liars (correctly); and her name, in whatever name-shaped thing dogs carried inside themselves, was something like *Wave-Brown-Waiting*. Orsen called her Brownie, because humans needed short words, even when the humans were just one person hiding behind a stall.

"What do you make of her?" Orsen asked silently, tilting the chin toward a woman across the square.

The woman had appeared maybe ten minutes ago and had not yet approached the stall, which made her unusual. Most customers were drawn in by the wares or the smile or the simple fact of standing still in a moving crowd. This one stood at the mouth of Salt Row like someone consulting a map only she could see. Grey traveling cloak, salt-white hair cut blunt at the jaw, boots that had walked farther than Greyharbor — Orsen could read a boot like a ledger. At her belt hung jars. Small ones, stoppered with wax, and they caught the light strangely, the way water catches light when something lives in it.

*Smells of stone and salt,* Brownie sent. *Old salt. Deep salt. Under-the-world salt.* A ripple ran through the dog's hackles. *Not-here salt.*

"That's either poetry or a warning."

*Both,* said Brownie, who was very smart for a dog.

The woman crossed the square. Up close she was perhaps fifty, weathered the way driftwood is weathered — grayed and grooved and somehow harder for it — and her eyes did something Orsen had almost never seen eyes do in twenty years of selling charms.

They weren't looking at the merchandise.

They were looking at Orsen's hands.

"Protection ward," the woman said. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to wind. "The real kind."

"They're all real, mistress. Tradition, old words, comfort—"

"Don't." The word wasn't sharp. It was tired, the way a door is tired on a house nobody visits. "Don't do the speech. I've crossed three realms. I know what a lie sounds like when it's wearing an apron."

Orsen's heart performed a maneuver best described as falling downstairs. Their face, trained for twenty years, did nothing at all. "Mistress, I—"

"The ward." She set two silver coins on the table, which was roughly what the ward cost, and roughly eight times what it was worth, and both of them knew the coins meant something other than price. "The strongest you have. And I'll stand here while you make it."

Every instinct Orsen owned screamed at once, in different languages, all saying the same word. To refuse was safe. To refuse was twenty years of survival doing exactly what twenty years of survival was built for. But the woman's eyes stayed level, and there was something in them Orsen recognized with a shock like cold water — the same look Orsen saw in their own mirror. Someone carrying a secret so long it had become a spine.

And underneath the fear, treacherous and warm, rose the oldest ache of all: *she knows, or she guesses, or she hopes, and either way she is looking at me like I am not alone.*

Orsen chose a ward. The deep green glass one, the good work, the one they'd made on a sleepless night last month and spoken over properly — not the mumbled fake-blessing they gave strangers, but the full whisper, the true one, the words that came from somewhere below Orsen's breastbone in a language they had never learned and could not forget. Protection wards made that way actually protected. Orsen had sold it cheap precisely because it frightened them.

They wrapped their hands around it now. Behind the shelter of the table, below the sightline of the square, their lips moved. Barely a breath. *Stand firm. Turn aside what means harm. Hold, little one, hold like a dike holds, hold like a promise—*

The glass warmed between their palms. It always did that. It was the thing Orsen could least explain away.

The woman watched. Not the hands — Orsen noticed — the mouth. Reading lips the way Orsen read boots.

When Orsen set the ward on the counter between them, the glass was faintly glowing, and stopped the moment it left their skin, and the woman nodded slowly, like a scholar confirming a citation.

"There it is," she said softly.

"Mistress, I don't know what you—"

"My name is Yara." She took the ward, and instead of pocketing it, hung it around her own neck, where it rested against her chest like something returning to a socket. "You're going to want to hear what I have to say. Probably tomorrow. Possibly tonight, if the crown moves fast, and the crown always moves fast." She glanced up at the Tide Gate at the end of the square — the great arched customs gate where, twice a day, the sea itself flowed beneath the city's threshold. Riders were gathering there. Crown riders, in dove-gray, with the new seal on their banners that hadn't been there last month.

Something in Orsen's chest went cold ahead of the autumn air.

"Come back at closing," Orsen heard themself say, and hated how thin it sounded, and hated more that it was *yes*. "If you like. For the — for follow-up care. We offer that. With purchases."

Yara almost smiled. It transformed her face the way light transforms a cliff. "Follow-up care," she repeated. "Aye. That'll do."

She walked away toward the Tide Gate, toward the gray banners, unafraid in a way that seemed less like courage and more like having already survived the worst thing.

Brownie leaned against Orsen's leg. *You shook,* she observed.

"I'm aware."

*Good shaking or bad shaking?*

Orsen looked down at their hands. They had sold ten thousand charms in this market and spoken honestly to no one in twenty years, and a stranger with salt in her hair had read their lips in broad daylight and walked away smiling, and the sky had not fallen. Not yet. There was still the rest of the day, and the closing of the stall, and the decision that waited at the end of it like a stair in the dark.

"Ask me at sundown," Orsen said.

The afternoon crawled. Orsen sold a fidelity knot, two fever-wards, and a jar of harbor water to a skeptical sailor, whispering to each one in the gaps between heartbeats, and every whisper felt different now — less like hiding, more like practice. The gulls reported that the gray-bannered riders had posted a paper on the Tide Gate and stood beside it in pairs, and that everyone who read it walked away differently than they'd walked up.

*Paper says fear,* Brownie sent, when the wind brought scraps of the crowd's murmuring up Salt Row. *Everyone tastes of fear.*

At sundown the market emptied the way it always did, stalls folding like wings, the river of noise draining toward supper and hearths. Orsen packed the stock with hands that were steady in the way of hands gripping a rail. Across the square, the last light caught the paper on the Tide Gate, white as a sail, unreadable from here and unmistakable anyway.

The dog pressed closer. The moth finally found the lamp. And at the mouth of Salt Row, patient as the tide, Yara stood waiting in the shadow of the Gilded Finch, salt-white hair going gold at the edges of the dying light.

Orsen blew out the lamp.

Twenty years, they thought. Twenty years of small charms and careful lies.

Then they lifted the latch, and stepped out from behind the stall.
