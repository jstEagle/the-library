## 2. Proclamation at the Tide Gate

The heralds came at low tide, which everyone in Greyharbor knew was the coward's hour. Anything posted at low tide would be half-sunk by evening, paper gone soft and illegible, and you could tell yourself for days afterward that you'd only imagined what it said.

There were three of them, riding out along the cobbled spine of the Tide Gate with a trumpeter whose notes cracked in the salt wind. They nailed the edict to the old mooring post where ship-chandlers pinned their notices, and beneath it they hung something that made the whole market go quiet: an iron cage, empty, sized for a person.

Orsen stopped mid-transaction, a copper charm for colic still warm from the polishing cloth in their palm.

"Read it," said the fishwife across the stall, because Orsen was nearest and she'd never learned her letters.

So Orsen read it aloud, in the flattest voice they could manage, letting the words come off their tongue like someone else's cargo:

*By order of His Majesty's Crown and Council: all persons harboring or practicing unlicensed gifts — beast-speech, hedge-craft, salt-working, fire-tongue, second-sight, or any art of the old covenant — shall surrender themselves to the appointed hunters for covenant audit within fourteen days. Those who comply will be examined and, if found harmless, released. Those who conceal themselves do so against the peace of the realm and will be taken.*

*Harmless*, Orsen thought. *Released.* The words had the smoothness of well-worn stones, and about as much warmth.

"It's an audit," said the cooper's wife behind them, too loudly. "Just an audit. Honest folk have nothing to—"

"Honest," snorted the eel-man two stalls down, and his eyes went not to the edict but to the charm seller's awning, red as a wound against the grey sky.

Orsen set the copper colic charm down on the counter and slid it toward the fishwife without meeting her gaze. "On the house," they said. "For the little one."

She took it quickly, the way people take things when they've decided not to wonder what else you might be hiding. Then she gathered her basket and went, and she didn't say thank you, and that was how Orsen knew the market had already changed.

* * *

By noon, Greyharbor had emptied of its warmth the way a hearth empties when the wind finds the flue.

It wasn't the crowd thinning — markets stayed busy; fear is excellent for commerce, if you sell the right goods. It was the quality of the looking. All morning, Orsen watched neighbors turn into surveyors of each other. Old Fen at the rope stall, who had traded jokes with Orsen for eleven years, suddenly couldn't hold their eye. The girl who sold cockles whispered to her mother and both of them looked over, then away, then back again, like gulls circling something dead on the sand.

People came to the stall anyway. That was the bitter joke. They came for charms — protection wards, mostly, though nobody said what they wanted protecting *from*. A tanner bought a ward against ill wishing and paid double without haggling, and as Orsen wrapped it he leaned close and said, "You're not gifted, though. Are you? Only asking." And laughed. And waited.

"I sell trinkets," Orsen said. "Polished brass and wishful thinking."

"Right," said the tanner. "Right." He didn't sound convinced, but he left, and Orsen supposed that counted for something.

That was the arithmetic of a hunt, Orsen thought. Nobody had to accuse anybody. You just gave every stranger's glance a question mark and let the market do the rest. Twenty years Orsen had lived in this city, and it had taken one sheet of paper and an empty cage to make all twenty years feel like a costume they might be stripped of by supper.

They worked through the afternoon with the mechanical calm of long practice: hands moving, voice level, the small lies falling into place like coins into a purse. *Blessed under a waning moon. Soaked in chapel-water, very nearly holy. My grandmother's recipe, from the eastern valleys.* None of it magic. None of it the true thing. The true thing sat folded up small inside Orsen's chest, wings tucked, silent, the way it had been silent since they were nine years old and learned what happened to children who talked to animals out loud.

Around midafternoon, a dog trotted past the stall — some terrier-mix with salt-stiffened fur and a limp — and paused, ears up.

*Cold today,* it observed, in the way dogs do, all smell and simple appetite. *Warm where you stand. Why is your heart beating so fast?*

*Because I am about to die of fright,* Orsen answered silently, and smiled at nothing, and the dog wagged its tail at the smile and limped on, satisfied.

And Orsen stood there in the middle of their careful life, holding a rag over a brass sun-charm, and realized their hands were shaking.

Not from the dog. From the cage. From the image that would not leave: themselves inside it, hoisted at the Tide Gate, while the whole market came to look at the freak who spoke with beasts. Fourteen days. The crown said *audit*, but audits did not need cages, and cages did not get built overnight. Someone had been planning this before the ink dried.

*Those who conceal themselves do so against the peace of the realm.*

Concealment. As if the silence hadn't cost anything. As if Orsen had chosen it out of vanity instead of survival. Twenty years of swallowing every word, of biting back answers when the gulls argued overhead, of pretending deafness to the whole chattering world — and now the crime wasn't the gift, it was the *hiding* of it, as though the hiding weren't the crown's own doing, taught to children with stories of pyres and purges until silence grew into the bones like salt into timber.

Orsen closed the stall early. First time in eleven years. Fen at the rope stall noticed — of course Fen noticed — and Orsen watched the older woman's face work through something complicated before settling into a nod so carefully neutral it was itself an accusation.

*Good luck to you,* Fen's face said. *Whatever you are.*

* * *

The Tide Gate after dark was a different country. By day it was commerce and noise; by night it became bone-white stone and black water, the great arches standing over the channel like the ribs of something the sea had eaten and not finished. The edict had been reposted on fresh paper — the crown's men had thought of the tide, apparently, second time around — and lantern light made the letters jump.

Orsen hadn't meant to come here. They'd told themselves they were walking home, and home was the other direction, and their feet had simply disagreed. Now they stood under the arch reading the words again, alone, and let the truth of them settle into the gut like cold ballast.

Fourteen days minus one.

A gull roosted on the cage's iron dome, feathers puffed against the wind. *Bad perch,* it remarked to no one. *Rattles. Smells of blood, old blood, other birds' blood.* It shifted, resettled. *Why do humans build boxes for grief?*

"You don't want to know," Orsen murmured, before they could stop themselves — a whisper, barely breath, but a whisper in the open air, aloud, at night, under the arch where anyone might—

"They don't," said a voice behind them. "Want to know, I mean. Gulls. Terrible confidants. Everything goes in one ear and out the other beak."

Orsen turned fast enough that their heel skidded on wet stone.

Yara stood at the edge of the lantern light with her hands pushed into the sleeves of her coat, and the wind coming off the channel pulled at her dark hair, and she looked — not amused, exactly. Something more careful than amusement. She wore the same salt-grey clothes as before, the same worn satchel, and the protection ward Orsen had sold her hung openly at her throat, brass catching the light.

"How long have you been standing there?" Orsen's voice came out steadier than their pulse deserved.

"Long enough." She stepped forward, unhurried, boots quiet on the wet cobbles. "The gull asked why humans build boxes for grief. That's a question worth answering, actually. We build them because we can't bear to look at what we've done, so we cage it instead." Her eyes flicked to the iron dome above them, then back. "I'd guess whoever wrote that edict knows exactly what a covenant audit involves. And I'd guess it isn't ledgers."

"You followed me."

"I waited." Yara tipped her head at the arch. "You came to me. Which tells me more than following ever could."

Orsen's mouth was dry. Every instinct honed over twenty years screamed at once: deny, deflect, laugh it off, sell her another charm. But the words wouldn't arrange themselves. Perhaps because there was nowhere left to file them. The market had closed its face to Orsen today; the edict had named concealment a crime; and here, in the dark, under the rattling cage, stood a woman who had watched a charm seller talk to a stray dog and had come back anyway — had come back *because of it*, apparently — wearing the ward she'd bought like a promise she hadn't explained yet.

"The dog," Orsen said. "Three weeks ago. You saw."

"I heard." Yara let that land. "Saw nothing. Heard everything. You asked it where its person was, and it told you it didn't have one anymore, and you told it—" she paused, and her voice lost some of its edge, softened into something almost embarrassed on Orsen's behalf, or maybe her own, "—you told it the harbor wall had good crevices out of the wind, near the bakery chimneys, and that if it waited there mornings, someone would eventually drop a sausage."

Orsen remembered saying it. Remembered the ache of saying it — kindness with nowhere to put itself, spilling out in the only direction that had ever felt honest.

"You heard that," they said, "and you bought a ward from me."

"I heard that," Yara said, "and I knew the edicts were coming, and I needed to know who in this city still had the real thing before the crown swept it all into cages." She lifted the ward at her throat. "This is brass. Pretty brass. It couldn't stop a determined cat. What you have doesn't need props."

The wind funneled through the arches and made the lanterns gutter. Below, the channel breathed against the pilings, in and out, patient as everything the sea did.

"What do you want from me?" Orsen asked. Not brave. Just tired, and past pretending tiredness was anything else.

"To start?" Yara glanced up at the edict, its letters pale in the dark. "I want you to understand your fourteen days aren't fourteen days. Audits take time to organize. Cages take time to fill. The crown posts a deadline so the frightened ones come in first and the slow ones get taken last — but people like us, people who've been hiding a long time, we don't go quietly and we don't go early. Which means the hunters will stop waiting. Ten days, maybe less, before they stop auditing and start sweeping. Greyharbor first. It's a port. Ports are where the gifted hide, because ships need luck and sailors know it."

"'Us,'" Orsen repeated. "You said 'people like us.'"

Something crossed Yara's face then — quick, swallowed, like a fish turning under ice. "Did I?"

"You know I heard it."

For a moment he thought she'd deny it. Then she reached into her satchel, slow and deliberate, showing her hand the way you show an empty hand to a frightened animal, and drew out a small clay jar no bigger than a fist. It was sealed with wax the colour of old bone, and through the unglazed clay, faintly, impossibly, Orsen saw a glow. Orange-red. Ember-light. Warmth in a jar on a cold night.

"This is salt-work," Yara said. "Forbidden where I come from. Which is nowhere, now, because where I come from decided I was forbidden instead." She turned the jar in her fingers and the ember-glow swam inside it like something sleeping. "It's also the reason I'm standing in front of you instead of running. Because whatever this is—" the glow pulsed once, dimmer, like a breath drawn with difficulty, "—it's dying. And I think you're the only person in Greyharbor who might help me understand why."

Orsen looked at the jar. At the light moving behind the wax seal like a heartbeat seen through a ribs. At the woman holding it out — not offering it, exactly. Showing it. The way you show a wound to prove you're not lying about the fight.

Every thread of sense Orsen owned said: walk away. Say *trinkets and wishful thinking*, one more time, and go home to the narrow bed above the stall, and spend ten remaining days deciding whether to run or to bury the gift deeper and hope the crown's nets dragged shallow.

But the gull on the cage shifted its feet and muttered, drowsily, *cold, bad perch, why does the small sad human smell of lightning-about-to-happen*, and Orsen thought: *twenty years of being the small sad human. Twenty years of lightning kept in a jar of my own.*

"What is it?" they asked. "In the jar."

Yara's shoulders came down half an inch. It was the first thing about her that had seemed young.

"Not here," she said, glancing once at the dark water, the dark windows, the whole listening city. "And not tonight. Tonight, go home, count your stock, decide what you can carry. Tomorrow, if you're willing, come to the salt sheds past the tanneries at dusk. There's an old drying floor nobody uses." She tucked the jar back into the satchel, and the light vanished as if swallowed. "If you don't come, I'll understand. If you do—" she hesitated, and for a heartbeat looked as frightened as Orsen felt, which was somehow the most convincing thing she'd done all night, "—if you do, bring nothing you'd hate to lose."

"That's a terrible sales pitch."

"I'm not selling." Yara stepped back into the dark beyond the lanterns, and her voice came out of it one last time, dry as driftwood. "Believe me, charm seller, neither of us is selling anymore."

Then she was gone, footsteps fading along the Gate, and Orsen stood alone under the arch with the edict and the empty cage and the gull, and the wind came off the channel carrying salt and the far-off reek of the tanneries, and somewhere out in the black water a seal surfaced and blew once, a sound like a sigh the sea had been holding for years.

*They're lying about the audit,* the seal said, to no one, to the water, to anyone with ears to hear. *The boats with the iron cages came up the coast last week. We smelled the fear on them from miles out.*

Orsen went very still.

*How many boats?* they asked the dark.

But the seal had already gone under, and the tide was coming in, and fourteen days had just become fewer than that.

Orsen turned toward home, walking fast, already counting what they owned, and what they could carry, and what they'd hate to lose — and finding, to their surprise and no small terror, that the answer to the last question was finally, after twenty years, beginning to change.
