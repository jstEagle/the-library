## 3. Salt in the Wound

The boat smelled of bilgewater and old salt, which was the only reason Yara had bought it. Salt kept honest things honest and dishonest things quiet, and a hull that had spent thirty years in the sea had soaked up enough of it to hold a secret or two.

She had moored it under the rotting pier at the east edge of Greyharbor, where the tide went out far enough each night to strand the crab-pots and the smugglers' memories alike. It was not a home. She had told herself for six years now that she did not need a home, that a salt-witch needed only a place to keep her jars dry, and she had almost made herself believe it.

Tonight she sat cross-legged on the deck with the lantern turned low and waited for the charm seller to find her.

She had left the invitation folded inside the ward she'd bought three days ago — a scrap of paper with nothing on it but a tide table and a circle drawn in salt, which any witch could read and no magistrate could arrest. *Under the broken pier, when the water leaves.* Whether Orsen Kavanagh could read salt was another question. Most people couldn't. Most people looked at a line of white grains and saw spilled dinner.

Yara had watched the charm seller for two weeks before she'd approached the stall, and everything she'd seen had unsettled her in ways she hadn't expected. The gulls that landed near the awning and stayed. The cart-horse that turned its head to follow Orsen through the crowd as though listening to a voice no one else heard. And the dog — thin, brindled, half-feral — crouched in front of the stall one grey morning while Orsen leaned down and murmured something into its ear, and the dog's whole body changed, fear draining out of it like water from a pulled plug.

Yara knew what that was. She had been taught what that was, before they took her name away.

So she waited, hands loose on her knees, breathing the smell of brine and tar, and told herself she wasn't nervous. Nervous implied there was something to lose.

*You always have something to lose,* her grandmother said in her memory, in that voice like gravel rolled in honey. *The trick is deciding what it will be before someone else decides for you.*

Footsteps on the pier above. Hesitant ones. A pause at the gap where the planks had rotted through, then a soft curse — Orsen's voice, low and careful even when swearing — and then the scrape of boots finding the ladder rungs.

"You came," Yara said, without turning around.

"I nearly didn't." Orsen dropped the last few feet onto the deck, caught their balance against the mast, and straightened with the wary dignity of a cat that had not meant to slip. In the low lantern light they looked younger than they had across the market stall. Tired, too, in the way of people who have been holding their shoulders still for twenty years so no one notices them. "Your note was — I don't read salt. But you drew the circle widdershins-inward, and there's exactly one person in Greyharbor who does that."

"My grandmother's habit. You knew her?"

"I bought fever-charms from her when I was fourteen." Orsen lowered themselves onto the crate Yara had cleared for them, movements economical, gaze moving over the deck the way a market-stall keeper's gaze moved over a crowd — cataloguing exits, weighing weights. "She never drew it inward for me."

"She only drew it inward when she wanted to talk about the thing you don't talk about." Yara let that sit. "You spoke to that dog."

Orsen went very still. Lantern light caught the line of their jaw, tight as a knot pulled hard. "I don't know what you mean."

"Orsen." Yara reached beside her and picked up the jar.

It was a modest thing, fist-sized, blown glass gone cloudy with age, stoppered with wax and bound in copper wire gone green. Inside: a bed of grey salt, fine as flour, and resting on the salt an ember no bigger than a thumbnail. It glowed faintly — not orange, not red, but a deep bruised gold, like sunlight remembered rather than seen. As she held it up, the glow pulsed. Once. Then again, slower.

"It's dying," she said. "I've kept it alive for six years with everything my art knows, and it is still dying. Do you understand what you're looking at?"

Orsen's eyes had gone to the jar and would not leave it. When they spoke, their voice came out strange, half-strangled. "It's warm. I can feel it from here. Like standing near a hearth." A pause. "No. Not a hearth."

"No," Yara agreed softly. "Not a hearth."

"Dragons are stories." Orsen said it too quickly, the way people say things they've spent years making themselves believe. "Old stories. Northern stories. Nobody in Greyharbor has seen one in—"

"In ninety-one years." Yara set the jar between them on the crate, and the ember pulsed again, and this time she saw Orsen flinch as if it had spoken their name. "Ninety-one years since the last confirmed sighting. Sixty since the covens stopped pretending the high-flame rites did anything. My mother's generation called them tradition. Mine calls them fairy tales. And this—" she tapped the glass, gentle, the way you'd touch a sleeping thing you were afraid of waking, "—this is the last ember of dragon-fire south of the Ashreach, and it is going out, and when it goes out I genuinely do not know what happens. Neither does anyone. That's the part that keeps me up at night. Not that it's dying. That nobody knows what dies with it."

The tide chuckled out beyond the pier, hollowing under the pilings. Orsen stared at the ember, and their face did something complicated — wonder pulling one way, terror the other, meeting in the middle and canceling into a kind of blankness Yara recognized because she wore it herself for the first year after her exile.

"How do you have it?" Orsen asked.

"Because I was supposed to guard it. That's why they exiled me."

And there it was, out of her mouth before she had decided to let it out, six years of silence cracked open by one tired charm seller's question. Yara looked at the water instead of at Orsen.

* * *

Her grandmother had taught her the first law of salt before she'd taught her letters: *salt remembers*. Every crystal had been water once, had held the shape of the sea, and if you knew how to ask, it would tell you where it had been. Salt-witchery wasn't power over the sea. It was memory of it, borrowed and returned with interest.

The covens of the Saltmarsh Reach kept three oaths. Keep the tides' records. Keep the ember. Keep silent about both.

"We weren't dragon-priests, whatever the songs say," Yara said. She had drawn her knees up, arms wrapped around them, the posture of a much younger girl. She hated that she couldn't seem to sit any other way when she told this story. "We were librarians. Archivists. The covenant — the real one, the old one — it needed keepers on both sides, and when the dragons stopped coming, someone had to hold the receipts. That was us. Nine generations of my family fed that ember salt-blessings every solstice. My turn was coming. I'd been named Ember-Warden-elect. Do you know what an honor that was? Do you know how hard I worked for it?"

Orsen said nothing. They had the good grace to say nothing.

"The year I was to take the oath, I found the ledgers were wrong." Her voice came out flat now, scraped clean. "The ember's decline isn't natural decay. It tracks a curve. Someone had been skimming the blessings — siphoning flame off the ember for decades, small amounts, hidden amounts, enough to work wonders the covens hadn't legitimately done in a century. Storm-calling. Curing blight. Little miracles, very profitable miracles, sold quietly to landholders who paid well and asked nothing. And the ember paid the interest. It's been paying it for eighty years, and everyone in coven council either didn't know or — worse — did."

"You reported it," Orsen said quietly.

"I reported it to the First Tide-Mother herself, who thanked me for my diligence, praised my sharp eyes before the full convocation, and exiled me eleven days later for falsified charges. Unsanctioned workings. Theft of coven materials. They salted my name out of the rolls — there's a rite for it, you know. They speak your name into a jar of dead sea-salt and bury it, and after that no coven working will answer you. You become, formally speaking, a woman who never existed." Yara smiled, and knew it wasn't a good smile. "They were thorough. My own mother signed the roll."

The lantern hissed. Out in the dark, a buoy bell clanked once, twice, patient as a clock.

"They were protecting themselves," Orsen said. It wasn't a question.

"Some of them. Some of them honestly believed the ember's decline was fated and my accusations were grief looking for a villain. It doesn't matter anymore. What matters is—" she picked up the jar again, held it so the ember's dying gold lit her palm from below, "—I ran with this. Stealing it was the only crime I'm actually guilty of. If the covens won't guard the fire, I will. Alone, apparently, for six years, getting nowhere, watching it fade a little more every winter. And then three days ago a charm seller in a shabby market stall talked a stray dog out of biting a child, and lied to a city guard with a seagull's help — yes, I saw that, the gull that flew in the man's face at exactly the right moment, don't look surprised — and I thought: *there*. There's the other half of the covenant sitting under a market awning selling love charms to fools."

Orsen had risen halfway during the story, and now stood at the rail with their back to her, hands gripping wood. Their shoulders were doing the thing again — held perfectly still, holding something in.

"The covenant needs two voices," Yara said to their back. "That's what my grandmother's oldest scrolls say, the ones I took when I ran. Fire gives, and the given is answered. The dragons lent their flame to human hearths, and humans answered with remembrance — names, songs, oaths spoken aloud, generation after generation. The ember doesn't die because the magic fails. It dies because *no one answers anymore*. The answering was a job. There were people whose job it was — oath-keepers, scattered across the three realms when the practice went underground. And the scrolls say—"

She stopped. Took a breath.

"The scrolls say the answering must be spoken by those born with the covenant's voice. Beast-speakers. People who can hear the old tongue in living creatures' throats. Which is a forbidden gift, hunted for generations, driven so deep underground that most who carry it don't know what they carry." She stood, slowly, jar cradled in both hands like an offering. "Orsen. You talked a dog out of its fear. You asked a gull for help and it *helped*. You are not a curiosity. You are the last functioning half of a covenant older than the crown, and the other half is dying in this jar, and I am asking you — begging you, actually, and I have not begged anyone in six years — to help me find the oath-keepers before midwinter. Before this goes out. Before we learn the hard way what dies with it."

Silence, except the sea.

Then Orsen turned, and their face was wet, and their voice when it came was low and shaking and furious:

"Do you know what they do to beast-speakers? Have you ever watched a gifted person burn? Because I have. I was nine years old, and the woman they burned had healed a farmer's cow, that's all, that was her whole crime, and the whole town came out with pies and flags like it was a fair, and my parents held my hands so I wouldn't run to her, because they knew. They knew what I was. And they taught me to smile at those festivals, Yara. They taught me to clap." Their breath tore. "Twenty years. Twenty years I have swallowed every word that wasn't aimed at a human mouth. You come to me with a dying coal and a story and you ask me to be *loud*."

"Yes," Yara said.

"It will kill me."

"It might." She would not lie to them; lying was the covens' sin, not hers. "Or staying silent will kill something larger. I don't know which. I've done the arithmetic six different ways and every sum comes out terrible, and I am asking anyway, because you are the only sum I've got."

Orsen laughed — a cracked, awful sound — and dragged a hand down their face, and for a long moment just breathed, staring at the ember pulsing in the jar between them like a slow second heart.

"Why me?" they finally whispered. "Really. Out of everyone."

"Because yesterday," Yara said gently, "when you thought no one was watching, you said good morning to the rats behind your stall. Nobody says good morning to rats, Orsen. Nobody hears them to say it to."

Orsen closed their eyes.

When they opened them, they didn't say yes. Yara hadn't expected them to, not yet; hope was a thing you had to approach sideways, the way you'd approach a wounded animal. What Orsen said was, "If I help you. If. Where would we even start? You said scattered across three realms."

"I know one name," Yara admitted. "An orchard-tender, north past the marsh country, in the old ash country where the trees grow only where dragons were said to have wept. Maren, they call her, if she's still alive, and she'd be the first test anyway — if Maren turns us away, the others will too."

"Marsh country." Orsen looked out at the black water, and Yara watched the war happen behind their eyes — fear against longing, twenty years of silence against whatever that ember's warmth was doing to their skin. "There are patrols on the marsh roads already. New edict posted at the Tide Gate tonight. Did you see it?"

"I saw it."

"Then we'd be traveling into a hunt."

"Yes," Yara said. "We would."

Orsen was quiet a moment longer. Then they reached out — hesitantly, the way they must have reached toward the dog — and laid two fingers against the glass of the jar.

The ember flared.

Not much. A breath's worth, a heartbeat's worth — but brighter than Yara had seen it in six years, gold flooding the little prison of salt, and Orsen snatched their hand back as if burned, staring, and Yara felt her own breath go out of her in a rush, because in six years of every blessing, every rite, every desperate solstice working, the ember had never once responded to anyone. It had only faded.

"It knows you," she whispered. "Orsen. It *knows* you."

"That," Orsen said unsteadily, backing toward the ladder, toward the shore, toward the ordinary world they were already leaving, "is exactly what terrifies me."

But they didn't say no. They climbed the rotten ladder into the dark, and Yara sat alone with her stolen fire, and found — to her surprise, to her suspicion, to something dangerously close to gladness — that she believed them.

Two days later, at dawn, a folded scrap appeared under her door, drawn in salt on paper instead of grain: a single word, widdershins-inward.

*North.*

She was packing within the hour. And by the time she reached the tide gate with her jars strapped to her back, the square was already filling with hunters in crown grey, and a crier was reading names off a list — and the third name on the list, spoken flat into the morning crowd, was Kavanagh.
