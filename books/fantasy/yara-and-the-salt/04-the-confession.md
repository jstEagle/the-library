## 4. The Confession

The knock came at the stall an hour before dawn, and Orsen knew before opening the door that the world had changed shape overnight.

They had slept badly, in the narrow room above the chandler's where they'd lived for eleven years. The bed was still made. The kettle still sat on its hook. The little tin of dried lavender they kept for sleep-charms still smelled of nothing at all, because it was only lavender. Everything in the room was exactly as ordinary as Orsen had spent two decades making it, and none of it felt like theirs anymore.

Down in the market square, someone was hammering. Not building-hammering — the flat, patient rhythm of nails driven into a posted board.

Orsen dressed in the dark and did not light a lamp.

* * *

By full light, the edict was everywhere. It hung from the Tide Gate in letters tall enough to read from a boat. It was pasted to the fishmonger's shutters, to the customs house, to the door of the chapel where Orsen had been baptized and subsequently taught, very gently, that what lived inside them had better stay unnamed. *By order of the Crown,* the broadsheets said, *all persons possessed of unlicensed gifts shall present themselves for covenant audit within the fortnight. Sheltering or concealing a gifted person constitutes treason against the realm.*

Covenant audit. Orsen read the phrase four times and admired, in a distant way, how clean it sounded. As if the gift were a ledger entry. As if you could hand over twenty years of silence at a counting desk and receive a stamped receipt.

The market did not empty. That was the terrible part. Greyharbor needed its market; herring didn't care about edicts. But the warmth went out of it between one bell and the next. Conversations thinned when Orsen passed. Mrs. Oduya, who had bought a luck-knot every solstice since Orsen took the stall, looked at them a half-second too long and then looked away, and Orsen understood that the looking-away was the message: *I don't know you. I never knew you.*

Good, Orsen thought. Be quick about it. Learn fast. The ones who learn slow end up in the audit wagons too.

They opened the stall anyway, because closing it would be a confession of its own. They sold a wind-charm to a cooper. They sold a fever-thread to a woman with a sick child and did not meet her eyes. Their hands did the work while their mind ran through the arithmetic of flight: how much coin in the floor-cache, which roads out of the city were watched, how long before the hunters worked down the list of charm-sellers and knocked on a certain chandler's door.

It was nearly dusk when Dagny came.

Orsen should have known. Should have known the moment they saw her face — gray, scrubbed, eyes red-rimmed and darting like a gull's before a storm. Dagny was a net-mender off the *Sable Tern*, forty years old, salt-burned hands, a laugh like a hatch cover slamming. Two weeks ago she had leaned across the stall and whispered that her boy was hearing the whales talk back, clear as bells, and could Orsen — just this once, just quietly —

And Orsen, against every rule they'd kept for twenty years, had listened. Had given her a charm for the boy and a warning wrapped in kindness: *don't let him answer where anyone can see.* And then — God help them — Orsen had done the thing they never did. They had knelt by the harbor steps that evening and spoken one soft sentence to the gulls, asking them to keep watch over a small boy who talked to whales. A single indulgence. A single loosening of the mask, because Dagny had cried, and because her boy was seven, and because Orsen had been seven once too.

Dagny stood at the stall now with her hands flat on the wood, and she couldn't look up.

"They came to the Tern last night," she said. Low. Fast. "Whispering-men. Asking after unnatural things. There's a reward, Orsen. Twenty crowns for a name." Her throat moved. "Twenty crowns is a season of mending. It's the winter's coal. It's —"

"Dagny."

"I haven't told them anything yet." Her voice cracked down the middle. "But they'll come back. They said they'd come back. And I thought — I thought you should have time. To go. Because I might be weak, Orsen, I might be weak when they ask me again, and I wanted you to hear it from me and not from the knock on your door."

There it was. Delivered like a fish wrapped in paper: honest, stinking, impossible to send back.

Orsen looked at this woman they had tried to help and found, searching themselves, no rage. Just a cold spreading clarity, like standing in seawater up to the ribs. She wasn't evil. She was afraid and poor, and fear plus poverty was the whole engine of every betrayal ever committed in Greyharbor. Orsen had known that when they gave her the charm anyway. They had simply gambled her poverty against her decency, and lost, and the stake had been their own life.

"Go home," Orsen said quietly. "Sell my name if you have to. Buy the coal."

"Orsen —"

"Don't come back to the stall. Whatever happens. Don't." They began stacking the trays, quick and methodical, hands steadier than they had any right to be. "And tell your boy—" The words caught. Orsen swallowed and finished. "Tell him the whales are worth listening to. Tell him somebody said so."

Dagny wept. Orsen did not watch her do it. Some things you owed people the privacy of.

She slipped away into the thinning market, and Orsen stood alone among the charms, doing sums. Coin cache: eleven crowns, three shillings. Roads north and east: watched by now, surely. Harbor: sealed for the audit, boats being inspected at the chain. Friends: a category Orsen discovered, taking inventory, had been allowed to go dangerously empty for twenty years.

Except one.

* * *

They took almost nothing. That was the strange part, how little there was to take. The coin cache. A knife. Their grandmother's ring, which had never been magic and never needed to be. Orsen stood a moment in the doorway of the stall with its neat rows of knots and threads and bottled breaths, all of it lies, all of it craft, all of it twenty years of careful small work — and then pulled the shutter down and walked away without hurrying, because hurrying drew eyes.

The hunters came at moonrise. Of course they did. Whispering-men worked at night; shame kept better hours after dark.

Orsen watched from the shadow of the chandler's alley as six of them took the stall apart with professional thoroughness. Lantern light swung. Wood cracked. Someone laughed. A sergeant held up a fever-thread to the lantern, turning it, and Orsen felt something twist in their chest — not grief exactly. Grief was for things mourned. This was more like watching your own skin nailed to a board and picked over.

"Charm-seller," the sergeant said, loud enough to carry. "Where's the seller?"

"Gone ahead to his lodgings," said one of his men.

"Then we'll go to the lodgings."

Which meant the chandler's room was already lost, which meant the sewers were already the only road left, which meant — Orsen turned the arithmetic over one final time and found the sum had changed. Every calculation they had run since dawn assumed a life to preserve. The lodgings gone. The stall gone. The name, by tomorrow noon, in Dagny's trembling mouth and then in the crown's ledgers: *Kavanagh, gift of beast-speech, unlicensed, twenty years concealed.*

Nothing left to hide behind.

And underneath the terror, faint but unmistakable, something in Orsen's chest *unclenched*. Like a fist they hadn't known they were making, opening finger by finger.

"Right," they murmured to the dark, to no one, to themselves. "So that's that."

They went down through the chandler's cellar, where the old storm-drain grate had rusted loose years ago and the chandler had never once asked why the lodger knew about it.

* * *

The sewers of Greyharbor were older than the city above them, and truer. Down here the tides still ruled; the channels filled and emptied twice a day with the sea's indifferent honesty, and everything the city pretended about itself got flushed out to the bar twice daily as well. Orsen moved through the waist-deep dark by touch and memory, one hand on the slick wall, breathing through the mouth.

Rats kept pace along the ledge. They always did — rats had followed Orsen since childhood, drawn to the voice the way moths drew to lamplight, though Orsen had never dared speak to them down here where echoes carried. Tonight, alone in the dark with nothing left to lose, the discipline cracked open like ice in spring.

*Not tonight,* they told the rats, silently, in the wordless way that was barely speaking at all. *But thank you for walking with me.*

The lead rat stopped. Turned. In the blackness Orsen felt its attention settle on them, bright and total, the way only a small prey-animal's attention could be — everything it had, aimed at you at once.

*Long time,* the rat said. Its voice was dry leaves and water. *Long, long time, soft-voice.*

Orsen stopped dead in the channel, water pulling at their knees, and something broke and healed in the same instant. Twenty years of silence, and the first creature to address them by their gift's own name called them *soft-voice*, casually, as if no edict had ever been written anywhere.

"Yes," Orsen managed. "A long time."

*Riders came to the gate at dusk,* the rat said, matter-of-fact, because rats traded news the way merchants traded salt. *Horses and iron. They watch the tide gate now. All gates. They will watch until the bells stop.*

"All gates?" Orsen's stomach dropped. The tide gate. Where they had arranged — where they had *hoped* —

*One person waits there anyway,* the rat said. *Smells of salt-stone and storm. She sits in the arch where the wind funnels. She has been there since the lamps were lit.*

Orsen stood in the sewage-dark of the city that had raised and was now hunting them, and laughed — a short, cracked, astonished sound that echoed down thirty yards of brick.

"Then show me the long way around," they said aloud, "and I'll owe the rat-kind a debt."

The rat chittered, which Orsen somehow understood was laughter too.

*This way, soft-voice. Mind the third junction. The water bites.*

* * *

They surfaced an hour later through a culvert fifty yards south of the Tide Gate, mud to the eyebrows, boots ruined, every possession they owned in the world slung in one soaked bundle. The night wind came off the sound carrying salt and the first spit of rain. Orsen crouched in the culvert's mouth, dripping, and looked up at the great stone arches of the gate — and there, exactly where the rat had said, sat Yara.

She was cross-legged under the arch with her hood up, and beside her, resting in her lap like a sleeping cat, was a jar. Even at this distance, even in the dark, Orsen could see the glow. Faint. Ember-red. Alive.

Yara's head turned before Orsen made a sound. Salt-witch senses, or maybe she'd simply been listening for footsteps in the water the way Orsen had been listening for boots on stone.

"You look," she said, "like something the harbor rejected."

"It did, actually." Orsen hauled themself up onto the cobbles and stood there streaming, shivering, grinning like an idiot, unable to stop. "They took the stall. They're at my lodgings. By morning every hunter in Greyharbor will know the name Kavanagh."

"And you're smiling."

"I am smiling because—" Orsen gestured at themself, at the mud, the bundle, the whole sodden ruin of their former life. "Because this is everything I own. Do you understand? A knife, a wet coat, and my grandmother's ring. Twenty years of hiding myself in plain sight, of making myself so small and so dull that nobody would ever look twice, and tonight a good woman sells me for the price of winter coal, and I feel *lighter* than I have since I was seven years old." The laugh escaped again, ragged at the edges. "I've got nothing left to hide behind. There's nothing between me and the gift anymore except open road."

Yara studied them for a long moment, rain starting to stipple the stones between them. Whatever she saw, she didn't flinch from it.

"The roads are watched," she said. "The harbor's chained. The marsh country starts forty miles north, and it's the only way that isn't a checkpoint."

"Then we walk north."

"In this." She nodded at the sky, where the rain was thickening toward weather.

"In this."

Yara rose, tucked the glowing jar inside her cloak with the tenderness of a woman shielding a candle, and looked down at the water boiling black beneath the gate. When she spoke again her voice had that other register in it, the one from the night she'd shown Orsen the ember — old grief wearing patience like a coat.

"There's a story I haven't told you yet," she said. "About what that fire is, and who owes it what, and why a salt-witch walks the world alone. You'll want it before the marsh. It changes what the road means."

"Then tell me on the road."

Yara glanced back once at the city — at the lantern-glow of the hunt moving through it, street by street, patient as tide — then pulled her hood low and stepped out into the rain beside Orsen, heading north, toward the marshes, toward whatever the ember in the jar was burning down to reach.

"One more thing," she said, as the Tide Gate fell behind them. "Your brother. The hunter. If he's on the list-keepers' side of this, he'll have your name within the week."

"He's had my name for twenty years," Orsen said quietly. "He's just never had a reason to use it."

The rain closed over the road, and the marsh country waited ahead, dark and wide and full of voices.
