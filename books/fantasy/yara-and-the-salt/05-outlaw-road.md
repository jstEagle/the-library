## 5. Outlaw Road

The marsh began where the road gave up pretending.

One mile north of Greyharbor's tide gate, the cobbles crumbled into mud and reeds, and the world went soft at the edges — water stitched through grass, grass stitched through fog, everything holding hands all the way to the horizon. Orsen had lived twenty years within the sound of harbor bells and had forgotten, until now, that land could be this quiet.

They walked side by side, Orsen and Yara, boots sucking at the causeway, packs light because there had been nothing to pack. Behind them Greyharbor sat under a sky bruised with smoke — hunters' fires, someone said. Ahead of them lay three hundred miles of drowned country between them and the first of Yara's oath-keepers.

"You're limping," Yara said.

"I'm walking."

"You're walking wrong in a way that will slow us down by nightfall." She stopped, set down her pack, and dug out a twist of salt-stiffened cloth. "Sit on that stone. Ankle."

"There's no time—"

"There is exactly enough time," she said, "if you don't break yourself doing it."

Orsen sat. It was strange, being told what to do by someone smaller than them, stranger still to find they didn't mind. Twenty years of keeping everyone at arm's length had left no practice in this — in letting a woman with salt-white streaks in her black hair kneel and pull off their boot with brisk, unceremonious hands.

The ankle was already swelling, a plum-colored bloom above the sock line. Yara hissed through her teeth. "Twisted on the sewer stairs?"

"Caught it on a grate. In the dark."

"In the dark, running from men with iron nets." She packed wet salt against the swelling, muttering words Orsen couldn't catch — or maybe didn't need to; the cold that spread through their ankle was language enough. "The hunters will have dogs."

"They'll have dogs," Orsen agreed.

"And you can talk to dogs."

It wasn't a question anymore. It hadn't been since the tide gate, since Yara had stood in the dark with her salt jar glowing faintly like a coal wrapped in gauze, and said, *I've watched you charm a stray dog into guarding your stall for two hours for nothing but conversation. You're coming with me, beast-speaker, whether you know it yet or not.*

"Yes," Orsen said. Out loud. To another person. The word felt like stepping off a dock.

Yara tied off the bandage and looked up at them, and there was something in her face that Orsen was learning to read: not pity, not awe, just attention — the particular attention of someone who knew what it cost to hide a self. "Good," she said. "Because I can't hear them at all, and we're about to cross the best-informed country in the realm."

* * *

They made camp that first night on a heron rookery island — a hummock of black willow rising out of the shallows, unreachable except by a causeway of stones only Yara could find in the dark. She built no fire. She laid out strips of dried eel and a hard gray cheese, and they ate in the blue dusk while the marsh sang around them: frogs, bitterns, the endless small machinery of water.

Orsen realized, somewhere in the middle of the cheese, that they were listening the old way. The way they had as a child, before Tammas, before the word *gift* became a word like *heresy*. Head tilted. Whole body open. Not straining — just receiving, the way other people received sunlight.

A night heron dropped onto the branch above them, folding its gray wings with an air of profound grievance.

*Fish,* it said — not in words, exactly; it had never once occurred to young Orsen that animals used anything so clumsy as words, more a shape of meaning, a cold ripple of image and intent — *fish, mud, fish, mine, go.*

*I'm not taking your fish,* Orsen answered, and then froze, because they'd answered. Out loud in their own head, in the open, without flinching, without checking over their shoulder for a brother who might be watching.

The heron cocked one yellow eye. *Two-legs. Sitting. Not fishing. Waste.*

"We're traveling," Orsen said aloud, and Yara looked up sharply, and the heron ruffled, offended.

*Traveling is not a thing,* the heron said. *There is here. There is water. There is fish.*

"It doesn't understand journeys?" Orsen murmured to Yara, who had gone very still.

"What did it say?"

"That we're wasting its time by existing away from a fishing spot." Orsen laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty as a gate hinge. When had they last laughed? "Yara, it thinks in — it doesn't think in *roads*. There's just here. Just water and fish and now."

"Herons don't migrate through here?"

"Some do. But ask it where riders are and it'll tell you where riders *are*, not were, not will be." Orsen turned back to the bird, feeling along the shape of its mind the way you'd test river ice with one foot. *Have two-legs come through the water-road? Many, on horse-flesh?*

*Riders.* A flare of something — not fear exactly; herons kept fear small and efficient, a coin spent only when necessary. *Riders came on the shell-road yesterday-sunrise. Dogs. Dogs smell loud. They stopped at the drowned chapel-place and turned up the bank toward the burning trees.*

Orsen's whole body went cold. "Burning trees?"

*Trees that burn when cut,* the heron said impatiently. *Where the old one lives. The fruit-woman. Smoke yesterday-evening, standing up tall against the stars.*

That was impossible. Maren's orchard was a week north, if that — they hadn't even reached the marsh's far edge. Unless the crown had sent riders ahead by the coast road, unless the hunt wasn't following them but *preceding* them, cutting the throat of every thread that led out of Greyharbor.

"Orsen." Yara's voice came flat and careful. "Your face has gone white as my salt. What is it?"

"The orchard," Orsen said. "Maren's orchard. Riders burned it. Yesterday evening."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere out in the dark a bittern boomed, once, twice, like a mallet on a sunken door.

"They can't have known," Yara said finally. "We left Greyharbor two days ago. Nobody knew where we were going except—" She stopped.

"Maren," Orsen said. "And whoever Maren talked to. And whoever watches Maren." They thought of the old woman among her weeping trees, pressing the covenant-shard into Yara's palm, saying *go north, go quickly, trust the marsh.* Trust the marsh. As if she'd known the hunters would come behind them like a tide.

"She sent us ahead on purpose," Yara whispered. "She bought us time with her orchard."

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, fog lying on the water like a second sky. They broke camp in silence, and Orsen found that the silence had changed texture overnight: it wasn't the silence of two strangers sharing a road, it was the working silence of people mid-task. Yara navigated by means Orsen couldn't follow — touching her salt jar through its wrapping every hour, reading some inner compass. Orsen listened.

By midmorning they had crossed out of the deep marsh onto the long shingle flats where the causeway firmed and the herons stood in ranks in the shallows like an honor guard. And Orsen, instead of hunching their shoulders and shutting the door on the voices as they had done every day for twenty years, walked down to the waterline and asked.

*Road-watchers,* they said to a gaunt old heron fishing the shallows with monastic patience. *You who stand so still and see so much. Have riders passed northward?*

*Many riders,* the heron said. *Yesterday and the day before. They ride the shell-road and they stop at every bridge and every inn and they show a paper.* A flicker of image: white rectangle, red seal, held up to faces. *The paper makes the two-legs point north. The paper points the riders north too. Everyone pointing north.*

North. Where the second oath-keeper waited — Brother Ilex, Maren had said, in his flooded chapel beyond the marsh. North, where the hunters were thickening like frost.

*Are there riders between here and the great north road?*

*Four,* the heron said promptly. *At the stone bridge. Sleeping in the sun. Their horses are unhappy. Horses are always unhappy. Stupid animals.*

Orsen almost smiled. *Thank you, elder.*

*Do not thank me. Bring fish next time.*

They reported back to Yara, who had been watching them work with an expression Orsen couldn't quite name — hunger and grief holding hands.

"Four at the stone bridge," Orsen said. "Sleeping. We could go around, through the fen proper — the heron says the ground will carry us if we walk where the reed-beds grow thickest, single file, on the old peat-cutters' paths."

"You asked a bird for a scouting report," Yara said.

"I asked a bird for a scouting report."

"Twenty years," she said softly, half to herself. "Twenty years you carried this, and they used you for nothing better than selling love-charms to gullible fishermen." Then, brisker, shaking it off: "Around, then. Through the fen. How sure are you of the path?"

"I'm not sure of anything. But the heron is." Orsen met her eyes. "It knows this water the way you know your salt. Every channel, every soft place. If I ask, it'll tell us true. Animals don't lie, Yara. That's the thing nobody understands about them — they *can't*. There's no room in them for it."

Something shifted in Yara's face then, a door opening onto a lit room. "No," she said slowly. "They can't lie." She gripped Orsen's arm, hard. "Then they can't lie about me either. About the covens. About why I was exiled — the version the covens tell and the version that's true. You could ask. You could find out what really—"

She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Orsen understood that she had shown them something she hadn't meant to, and that the kindest thing was to look at it and step past it.

"When there's time," Orsen said. "When we're not running. Ask whatever you need answered."

Yara nodded once, not trusting her voice, and turned to lead the way into the reeds.

* * *

The fen crossing took the rest of the day and all of Orsen's nerve. The paths were barely suggestions — ridges of firmer peat winding between pools that lay dark and still as oil. Twice the heron's directions saved them outright: *left at the dead pine, the pretty water eats horses and two-legs both.* Once Yara's salt saved them, sprinkling a handful across a stretch of skin-water that rippled wrong and went still beneath it.

They talked as they walked, because walking all day with someone does that, cracks the seals.

"I had a sister once," Yara said, apropos of a heron's comment about frog populations. "Coven-born. When they cast me out she didn't say goodbye. She sent a jar of my favorite salt with the expulsion order. That's the kind of people they are — cruel in the shape of kindness." She glanced at Orsen. "You had family. Before."

The question underneath the question. Orsen watched a marsh harrier quartering the far reeds and let the answer come up slow.

"A brother. Tammas. Older by two years." The name came easier than expected; it had been rusting in them so long it had worn smooth. "When we were children, I talked to his falcon. Made it return to his fist when he'd failed to call it for a week — he was terrified he'd lose his place in the falconry levy. I did it to help him. He thanked me. And then when the first purges came, when gifted folk started disappearing from the fishing villages, he went to the magistrates and told them what his sibling could do." Orsen stepped carefully over a black pool. "He was twelve. They gave him a commendation. A little silver badge, hunter's apprentice. He's worn it ever since, I'm told."

"Told by whom? You've been hiding."

"Animals talk. Gulls gossip worse than fishwives. I've heard him described a hundred times over the years, always secondhand, always from a beak or a paw." A bitter half-laugh. "My entire knowledge of my own brother comes through the gift. He took my family and the gift took his place."

Yara was quiet for several steps. "Maybe he was twelve and scared."

"He was twelve and scared," Orsen agreed. "I've had twenty years to decide how much that matters. Some days it matters completely. Most days it doesn't matter at all." They looked at her. "Why? Do you forgive your coven?"

"No," Yara said. "But I remember being twelve and scared, and I try to hold both things at once. It's exhausting. I recommend it anyway." She pointed ahead. "Is that our exit, or are we walking into the pretty water that eats people?"

Orsen asked the reed-warbler, who was scornful of the question but answered, and it was the exit, and they climbed out of the fen onto firm ground as the light went long and gold behind them.

* * *

They made a cold camp in the lee of a drystone wall, and it was there, in the last light, that the partnership got its true seal.

Orsen had gone to the wall's edge to ask the rabbits about the night watch — the rabbits knew everything within a quarter mile and cared about nothing, which made them ideal informants — when the hare came.

It came fast, low, ears flat, straight across the open field as if drawn on a wire, and it stopped three feet from Orsen and its heart was hammering so loudly Orsen could feel it in their own chest.

*Riders,* the hare said. *Riders off the shell-road, cutting across the heath. Four horses. Dogs. Coming this way, fast. Coming for the smell of you — the wind carries you, two-legs, the wind carries you like smoke.*

Orsen was moving before the meaning finished landing, back to the wall, voice low and level — years of practice at sounding calm in a market making itself useful at last.

"Hunters. Off the road, on the heath, four horses and dogs. Less than a mile. The wind's been carrying our scent to them all evening."

Yara was already on her feet, already unwrapping her salt jar, and Orsen saw her hesitate over it — saw the flinch, the memory of Maren's burning orchard priced against every use of forbidden craft — and then saw her put the flinch away like a tool in a kit.

"How long?"

"Minutes. The hare says the dogs are unsure — our scent is tangled with a hundred others from the market — but they'll resolve it soon."

"Then we split the difference. You get us a path; I get us a blind." She was scanning the ground, and her eye caught on the drainage ditch running along the wall's far side, deep, water-filled, choked with reeds. "There. We go in the water, up the ditch, north. Cold, miserable, and invisible. Can you keep the animals from giving us away? Rabbits bolting, birds going up — hunters read the country like a page."

For a moment — just a moment — the old fear rose in Orsen, the lifetime-deep reflex of *hide the gift, smother it, swallow it whole.* Then they looked at Yara, standing in the failing light with her illegal fire in a jar, betting her life on them, and the reflex broke like thin ice.

"Yes," Orsen said. "Get in the water."

What followed was the strangest quarter-hour of Orsen's life, and the best. Chest-deep in ditch water, teeth chattering, they opened themselves fully for the first time since childhood — not a whisper, not a stolen conversation, but the whole roaring chorus of the living world, and Orsen stood in the middle of it like a conductor walking up to an orchestra.

*Rabbits — stay down. Stay down until the horses pass, then run *south*, make them chase your south-running, make them waste their noses.* The rabbits, blessedly literal, complied. *Lapwings — when I say, lift screaming from the far field, all of you at once, draw every eye east.* *Hare — run along the wall south, drag your scent across theirs, muddy the page.* And the country, which had seemed empty, revealed itself to have been full all along, dozens of small minds turning willingly in Orsen's hand, not commanded — never commanded, animals didn't work that way — but *asked*, and asking, Orsen discovered, was a thing the gift did better than any charm or spell. Asking was its native tongue.

Through it all Yara worked her salt in the ditch water, and the water around them went mirror-flat and gray-lightless, and the scent of them sank, she murmured, into the salt's cold keeping.

The riders came through in a clatter of hooves and lantern-glow, four of them, hounds ranging ahead and finding nothing but a hundred false trails and a field of shrieking birds going the wrong way. One rider cursed. Another laughed. Orsen, submerged to the chin, watched the lanterns swing past close enough to count the rivets, and felt no fear at all — felt instead something so new it took a moment to recognize as joy.

The gift was not a secret. It had never been a secret. It had been an instrument waiting twenty years for someone to pick it up.

The lanterns dwindled. The lapwings settled. The hare flopped in the grass, theatrically exhausted, and informed Orsen that it expected compensation in clover, terms negotiable.

Orsen surfaced, gasping at the cold, laughing — actually laughing, helpless with it — and Yara hauled them up the ditch bank by the collar, and for a heartbeat they knelt there together in the wet grass, soaked and alive and unseen, and Yara's grin matched theirs, wide and disbelieving.

"Partners," she said. It sounded like an oath.

"Partners," Orsen agreed.

* * *

They walked north through the falling dark, wringing out what could be wrung, and the marsh gave way to rough grazing and the first stony hints of the high country. The drowned chapel, Brother Ilex's chapel, lay a day ahead — through country, the hare had warned, where the riders were gathering thicker, as though something ahead mattered to the crown even more than two fugitives.

Near midnight they crested a low rise, and Yara stopped.

Below, in a fold of the hills, light glimmered — not torchlight, not campfire. Something paler, steadier, rising off what looked like water. Water, in the middle of dry hill country, glowing faintly silver-blue, and around it a structure's dark bones: roof fallen, walls half-sunk, a bell tower leaning like a drunk against the sky.

"The chapel," Yara breathed. "It's true, then. Flooded — and *lit*." Her hand found her salt jar, and Orsen saw the glow inside had changed, pulsing quicker, brighter, answering something below.

And Orsen, opening the gift out of habit now rather than courage, listened to the country ahead — and found it strangely, utterly silent. No rabbits. No owls. No fox-song, no rat-whisper. For half a mile around the drowned chapel, every animal mind had gone quiet as held breath.

Except one. Deep beneath the water and the stones, something old was awake. And it had noticed them.

It spoke a single word into the dark, in a voice like a bell rung underwater, and Orsen staggered on the rise as the meaning arrived:

*Come.*
