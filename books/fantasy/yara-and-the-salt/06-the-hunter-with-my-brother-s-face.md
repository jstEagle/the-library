## 6. The Hunter with My Brother's Face

The heron had told us about the riders, but it was the crows who gave me my brother's name.

We had cut east out of the marshes two days past, following the old drover's road toward the ash orchard Maren kept somewhere in the hills above the Vell. Yara moved through the country like water finding its level — always knowing which hedge hid a gap, which farmstead would sell bread to strangers and ask no questions. I followed in her wake with my hood up and my heart doing its usual work of pretending it wasn't pounding.

It was near dusk when the crows came down to walk the fence rail beside us. They do that, sometimes, when they've decided you're interesting. Twenty years of keeping my mouth shut in a crowded market, and out here on an empty road I'd started letting myself answer them — small things, greetings, the bird-equivalent of gossip. *Big boots in the village,* one said, hopping along the rail. *Big boots, red cloaks, and they took the miller's boy.*

I stopped walking.

Yara got three steps before she noticed and came back, salt-gray braid swinging. "What."

"Crows," I said. "Red cloaks at Crossway Ford. They took someone." The words felt like they belonged to someone else. "They're asking after a charm seller."

"How far?"

"Half a day behind us. Maybe less." I swallowed. "Yara. The crow says their captain wears a grey badge. A wolf's head under crossed lines."

She went very still. She'd seen my reaction to nothing, these last days — a dog's bark, a red cloak at distance — and she'd never asked why. Now she did, quietly, the way she asked everything. "Who is he?"

"My brother," I said, and heard how the word broke in the middle.

* * *

His name is Tammas.

He is four years older than me and for the first twelve years of my life he was the best thing in it. That's the part nobody tells you about betrayal — that it has to be carried by someone you loved, or it isn't betrayal at all, just cruelty, and cruelty is easier to survive.

When we were children in Greyharbor's Tanner's Row, Tammas taught me to gut fish, to read weather off the harbor water, to whittle a whistle from willow that actually whistled. He called me *Ossie* in the way older brothers call younger ones, as an insult and a crown at once. And he was there, the first time, when the gift showed itself.

I was seven. A gull with a broken wing had fallen into our yard, and I knelt in the muck and held its hot trembling body, and I didn't speak aloud — I never needed to — and the gull went still and looked at me, really *looked*, the way nothing looks at a child, and then it stood up out of my hands and flew, crooked but flying, over the rooftops toward the sea.

Tammas saw the whole thing from the doorway. He was eleven. He ran to me, grabbed my shoulders, shook me once, and his eyes were enormous.

"You *talked* to it."

"I didn't—"

"You did. Ossie. You *did*." And he laughed — this wild, delighted laugh — and swore himself to secrecy with all the solemn ceremony an eleven-year-old could muster, spitting in his palm and making me spit in mine. *Blood and salt,* he said. *Nobody knows but us.*

For six years, nobody did. It was ours. My strangeness, my secret, my one impossible beautiful thing — and Tammas carrying it like a treasure, puffing with the weight of it, the keeper of his little sibling's miracle.

Then the winter he turned seventeen, our father's lungs finally drowned in the tannin dust, and there was no money, and the crown's stipend for sons of Kavanagh men who'd served in the coastal levy came due — but only, the clerk said, tapping the ledger, for sons of good character. Sons without taint. There was a new word in the world that year. *Taint.* You could hear it spreading through Greyharbor like damp through plaster, whispered in shops, preached in chapels: the gifted were tainted, untrustworthy, unclean. The hunts hadn't started yet. But the ground was being prepared.

I didn't know any of this. I was thirteen and I thought the world was made of fish guts and willow whistles.

What I know now — what I've spent twenty years refusing to know — is that Tammas went to the magistrate's office on a grey Tuesday and told a man in a red collar that his sibling spoke with animals. I know because I was there when the hunters came, because I hid in the coal cellar while they turned our house over, because I heard my mother's voice crack on his name: *Tammas. Tammas, what have you done.*

I heard him answer. I've never stopped hearing it.

*"The right thing."*

They didn't find me. Our mother, whatever else she was or wasn't, packed me out the cellar grate before dawn with bread in my pockets and didn't look at me, and I never saw her again either. Tammas took the levy stipend, and then the hunter's training, and then the hunter's cloak. And I took a new name and a stall in the market and twenty years of silence, and between us we built two entirely different lives out of the same broken thing.

* * *

"Your brother," Yara said now, sitting across the dead fire in the shelter of a hollow way, "is the captain hunting us."

"The crown's new hunt needed a face," I said. "Someone who knows how the gifted think. Someone clean." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's what they'll have called him. Clean blood. He reported his own family, so he must be trustworthy."

"And you're sure it's him? The badge—"

"A wolf's head under crossed lines. He designed it himself, when they promoted him. Drew it in the frost on our window, the winter before—" I stopped. "Yes. I'm sure."

Yara fed a twig to the dead fire without lighting it, just holding it, some salt-witch habit of keeping her hands busy. "Then we go around."

"We should go around."

"But you want to see him."

I didn't answer, which was an answer.

She studied me for a long moment. Out here her exile-witch's face softened sometimes, and I was learning to read it — the way I read everything except people. "Orsen," she said. "Seeing him serves nothing."

"Knowing where he is serves everything. He's the blade pointed at us. At Maren, if Maren's real. At every oath-keeper we find." I heard my own voice harden and was surprised by it. "And the crows say he's at Crossway Ford, interrogating villagers, half a day behind us on the only road north. So either we run blind, or somebody asks the birds exactly where he sleeps tonight."

Yara put the twig down. "Somebody," she repeated.

"I'll do it. You keep watch on the road."

"It's dangerous."

"Everything's dangerous." I pulled my knees up. "That's rather the theme."

* * *

We doubled back at dawn, which is the kind of thing that sounds brave until you're doing it, at which point it mostly feels cold and foolish. Yara found us a place above the ford village — an abandoned byre on the hillside, its roof half fallen, its door hanging open like a slack jaw. From the loft, through a gap in the rotting boards, you could see the whole of Crossway Ford: one street, a mill, a green gone brown with frost, and perhaps forty people going about their morning badly, the way people do when fear is sitting on their chests.

The red cloaks arrived mid-morning. Six riders and a wagon. And at their head, riding a big grey gelding like he'd been poured onto it, a man in a hunter's longcoat with the wolf's head at his throat.

Twenty years, and I knew him instantly. Not from his face, though the face was his — broader than I remembered, weathered, a scar through one eyebrow he hadn't earned when I knew him — but from the way he sat the horse. From the set of his shoulders. Some part of me had learned to recognize my brother the way you recognize a voice through a wall, and that part woke up now and screamed, and I put my hand flat against the boards and breathed until it shut up.

*Say something,* it begged. *Just call his name. Just once. See what happens.*

I knew what happened. I'd seen it happen in a coal cellar when I was thirteen.

Below, the interrogation began.

They'd set up at the well, and the villagers were brought to them one at a time — not dragged, I noticed, not yet, but escorted, gently, the gentleness worse than force somehow, because it meant everyone could pretend. Tammas dismounted and did not sit. He stood with his hands clasped behind his back and asked his questions, and even at this distance I could hear the shape of his voice, that low evenness he'd had even at fifteen, talking me down off the barn roof the time I fell.

A woman first — the innkeeper, by her apron. Then the smith. Then a very old man who leaned on a stick and answered too long and was led away from the line, and my stomach dropped, until I saw they only put him back at the end of it, patient as sheepdogs.

Then the miller's boy.

He couldn't have been more than ten. The crows had said *they took the miller's boy*, and here he was, fetched to the well in his father's shadow, small and square-shouldered and terrified. Tammas crouched down to his level. Even across the distance I could see him do it — the deliberate folding down, making himself small, making himself safe.

And I knew exactly what was coming, because I'd been that boy's age once, and Tammas had crouched down to me a thousand times, and every single time it had meant *you can tell me anything.*

"—heard it talk back," the boy was saying, high and thin, the wind carrying fragments up the hill. "The dog. It talked back to the charm seller and the charm seller laughed and the dog *answered*, honest, sir, like words—"

My hands had gone numb on the boards. The market. Three weeks and a lifetime ago. A stray dog, a moment of weakness, one whispered exchange about scraps and rain — and it had reached Crossway Ford ahead of me, grown in the telling, become the edict's first proof.

Tammas was quiet a long time. Then he asked the boy something I couldn't catch, and the boy nodded, and Tammas put his hand on the boy's head — briefly, lightly, the exact gesture he used to give me, ruffling my hair, *good lad, Ossie, good lad* — and stood, and said something to his riders, and two of them began going door to door.

"They're searching," Yara breathed beside me. We'd crept into the loft together an hour ago; she'd watched in silence, but her silence had a different weight now. "House to house. Why search if they have the boy's testimony?"

Because testimony isn't proof, I thought. Because he wants proof. Because Tammas Kavanagh does not move on a rumor — he moves on certainty, always has, ever since a grey Tuesday when he decided with a boy's terrible thoroughness that he was *certain* about me.

"He's careful," I said.

"He's thorough," Yara corrected softly. "Careful protects people. Thorough just takes longer."

Below, the boy was released to his father, who gathered him up with the desperate fierceness of a man handed back his own heart. And Tammas watched them go, and I watched Tammas, and I saw him turn toward the hills — toward us, though he couldn't know it — and stand there a long moment, looking north along the road we'd come by, with his hand resting absently at the wolf's head badge.

And then, clear across the frost-brown air, faint but unmistakable, I heard him say to his sergeant:

"Charm sellers travel light and sleep rough. Sweep the high pasture. Check every byre."

* * *

Yara had us out the back of the byre before I'd finished flinching, moving fast and low through the bracken, down the hill's blind side, away from the road, away from the ford, away from my brother's thoroughness. We didn't stop until a stream lay between us and the village, and then she stopped us anyway, turning to face me with her jaw set.

"You heard him. High pasture. Every byre." Her eyes searched mine. "How close were we?"

"Close enough to hear him give the order." I was shaking now, the delayed shaking, the body catching up. "Close enough to smell the horses."

"Orsen." She waited until I looked at her. "You went quiet back there. After he touched the boy's head. What happened?"

What happened was that I had watched my brother be gentle and cruel in the same gesture, to a stranger's child, wearing the same hands he'd once laid on me, and I could not find the seam between the two. What happened was twenty years of a story I'd told myself — *he chose the law, he chose it easily, he never looked back* — developing a hairline crack.

"He warned them," I said slowly. "Did you see it? He questioned the whole village, house to house, slow as syrup — and then sent us warning. *High pasture.* If he wanted us caught, he'd have circled wide and said nothing."

"Or he's clever," Yara said. "Clever hunters announce searches they've already covered."

"Maybe." I looked back up the hill, at the smoke standing straight from the ford village chimneys, ordinary and domestic and searched-through. "You didn't see his face when he looked north."

"I saw enough." She shouldered her pack. "We lose the road today. Cut cross-country to Maren's. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

But that night, wrapped in my cloak under a holly tree while Yara slept, I took out the memory and examined it the way I'd examine a charm for flaws. The crouch to the boy's level. The light hand. The questions asked slowly, giving everyone time — time to hide things, time to coach children, time for the guilty to slip away northward while the sweep crawled through its motions.

And underneath all of it, one detail I hadn't let myself look at directly: when Tammas had said *check every byre*, his voice hadn't been flat. There'd been something in it. Something I'd spent twenty years telling myself my brother no longer contained.

He'd sounded, just for a breath, like a man hoping the search would fail.

I lay under the holly tree and hated him, and hated how much I didn't, and understood with sudden terrible clarity that the worst thing the hunt could take from me was not my freedom.

It was this — the not-knowing. The wound I could keep as a clean wound, simple and cauterized, *my brother chose the law*. If he faltered, if there was anything left under the red cloak, then everything reopened, and I would have to feel all of it again: the coal cellar, my mother's cracked voice, the spitted palms and the oath of *blood and salt, nobody knows but us.*

Sleep took its time. When it finally came, it came with crows in it, wheeling.

They found me again two days later, on the third morning of cross-country travel, dropping out of a white sky onto a stone wall we'd paused to eat beside. One of them — the same one, I was almost sure, the fence-rail gossip from before — walked the wall toward me with something urgent in its strut.

*Riders,* it said. *Riders on the road behind, red cloaks, moving fast — but listen, listen — they turned aside. Turned at the shrine road. Went the long way round the mere. The tall one made them. The tall one made them go the long way.*

I sat with the bread going stale in my hand.

"Tall one," I said. "Grey badge? Wolf's head?"

*Wolf's head,* the crow agreed, bobbing. *Made them ride the long way round. Half a day lost. He watched the road the whole time, like he was waiting for something to get away.*

Yara was watching my face. "What."

"He detoured," I said. "He cost himself half a day. On purpose." My voice sounded strange, far away. "Yara — he knows. Or he suspects. And he's buying us time, or herding us, and I don't know which, and I don't know which is worse."

Behind us, far to the south, a smudge of smoke rose against the pale sky. Too thick, too black, too steady to be a chimney.

Yara stood up slowly, shading her eyes, and I saw her count the direction against our map of the hills, and saw her arrive at the answer a full breath before she said it.

"That's north," she said. "That's the orchard. Someone's burning Maren's trees."
