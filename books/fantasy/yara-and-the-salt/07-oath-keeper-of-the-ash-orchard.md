## 7. Oath-Keeper of the Ash Orchard

The trees were wrong, and Yara knew it before she could name why.

She had grown up on coastlines, where everything grew crooked and salt-bitten, bent by wind into shapes like old women telling secrets. These trees stood in the lee of a nameless hill in the high marsh country, and they grew *toward* something. Not the sun — the sun was a pale smear behind three days of cloud — but toward each other, branches interlacing so thickly that the orchard's heart was a dim green cathedral. And they grew out of ash. She could see it in every footprint she left: not soil, not loam, but fine grey powder that had once been something else.

"Orsen," she said quietly. "Look at your boots."

Orsen looked. They had been looking at everything since the flats — at shadows, at ridgelines, at the far-off dust of riders who never quite resolved into pursuit. They looked at their boots the way a person looks at a snake that has already bitten them once.

"Ash."

"Ash." Yara crouched and let a handful run through her fingers. Salt-witches learned to read what the land was made of; salt was only the beginning of it. This wasn't hearth-ash or kiln-ash. It was too fine, too uniform, too *old*. It crumbled between her fingers like something that had given up entirely on being solid. "This is bone-ash. Old. Very old."

"Whose bones?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She stood, brushing her palms on her coat, and felt the familiar prickle along her forearms that meant salt in the air — but wrong, diluted, as if the sea had passed through here centuries ago and left only its memory dissolved in the ground. "Trees don't grow in ash. Nothing grows in ash. Except—"

Except these trees were heavy with fruit. Small, dark, bitter-looking things clustered along every branch, and the smell coming off the orchard was astonishing — honey and smoke and something underneath both, something mineral and cold, like licking a stone in winter.

Orsen stopped walking.

Yara watched them do it — watched the moment their whole body went still, the way it did when something spoke to them that she couldn't hear. Their head tilted. Their lips parted slightly. Twenty years of hiding that habit, Yara thought, and it still lived in them like a second heartbeat. On the road north they had begun to let it show, first in flickers, then openly: pausing to trade words with a marsh-pony, arguing with a heron about fishing rights, listening to things Yara would never hear. Every time, Orsen flinched afterward, waiting for the blow that no longer came. Every time, something in their shoulders sat a little straighter.

"What are you hearing?" Yara asked.

"The trees." Orsen's voice came out strange. "They're not... they don't sound like animals. Animals think in wants. Food, warmth, fear, sometimes just noise for the joy of noise. The trees sound like—" They searched for it. "Like people remembering a song they've forgotten the words to."

Yara's chest tightened. Her grandmother had sung songs like that. Songs with holes in them where the words had been burned out, and the covens sang the holes anyway, because the shape of a missing thing is also a kind of prayer.

"Keep walking," she said. "Carefully."

The orchard deepened around them. The light went green and underwater. Somewhere ahead, woodsmoke — real smoke, fresh, which meant a hearth, which meant a person, which meant either salvation or a trap, and after twenty years of selling charms in Greyharbor Yara had long ago stopped believing those two things couldn't be the same.

Then the trees opened, and an old woman was standing among them holding a pruning knife, watching them with eyes like frost over black water.

"You're late," the woman said.

* * *

Her name was Maren, though she offered it the way a miser offers a coin — reluctantly, and with a look that said she expected change to be demanded. She made them sit on a stone bench beneath the largest tree while she cut deadwood, and she did not stop cutting while she talked, as if the conversation were a branch that needed managing.

"This orchard stands on nine graves," she said. "Nine dragons. They came here in the last great dying, four hundred years past, when the fire began to gutter the first time. They lay down here together, and they wept, and where their tears fell, my trees took root. Ash makes poor soil. Grief makes better."

Yara kept her face still. Inside, something old and cold turned over. The covens had fragments of this — the Lamentation, they called it, sung at midwinter in a language half the witches couldn't pronounce anymore. But the covens told it as story. Maren told it as *address*, the way you speak of a neighbor who lives next door.

"You're the orchard-tender," Yara said. "The one the salt-stones point to."

"I'm the one who rakes the ash." Maren's knife whispered through a dead limb. It fell, and Yara noticed — really noticed — that the wood of the fallen branch glowed faintly at the cut ends, a low coal-light, like something banked but not extinguished. "And you're the exile. Yara Venn, of the Saltmarsh covenant, cast out under the Third Oath for working the forbidden tides. And this is the beast-speaker from Greyharbor whose name is currently worth more than mine." Her frost-grey eyes moved to Orsen without malice and without mercy. "News travels faster than horses when the news is frightened enough."

"We've come because—" Yara began.

"No." Maren held up one knuckled hand. "You've come because the stone sent you, or you wouldn't have found this valley. But you don't know yet what you've come *for*. So we'll do this properly." She set down her knife at last, and the absence of its sound made the orchard suddenly very loud — leaves moving without wind, a low hum Yara realized was coming from the roots, from the ground, from everywhere at once. "Stand up. Both of you. Walk to the well."

There was no well that Yara could see. But Orsen was already standing, and there was something in their expression Yara had learned to trust on the road north — the look of someone hearing instructions from a world that mostly ignored her kind.

"There's a door in the hillside," Orsen said slowly. "Behind the third tree. There's water behind it, and the water is asking who we are."

"The water asks everyone," Maren said. "It doesn't ask twice."

* * *

The door was old stone, older than anything Yara had seen outside the drowned chapels of the northern coast, and it stood open as if it had been waiting. Behind it, stairs cut into living rock descended to a round chamber, and in the chamber's floor lay a pool no wider than a cartwheel, black and utterly still.

The air down there tasted like the jar.

That was Yara's first thought, and her second thought was that she was being ridiculous, and her third thought was that she was not being ridiculous at all, because when she leaned over the pool she saw it — deep down, at the bottom of the black water, a faint red glow, pulsing slow as a sleeping heartbeat. Dragon-fire. Not in a salt jar this time. In the earth itself.

"It's dying too," she whispered.

"Everything is dying," Maren said from the stair. "That is rather the point. Now. The water will ask who you are. Answer it truly, and it will know you. Answer it falsely, and it will drown you in your own lie — I've raked the ash of two such liars off my orchard floor, and they made the worst soil I ever tended. Beast-speaker. You first. That is how it must be."

Yara watched Orsen go to the pool's edge. Watched them kneel. Watched their reflection look back up at them — and then watched the reflection *move differently*, tilting its head, listening to something in the water that wore Orsen's face.

Orsen spoke. Yara caught only fragments, the words half-swallowed by the chamber's acoustics: *"...hid it... twenty years... sold charms that weren't charms because real ones would have killed me..."* A pause. Then, quieter, so quiet she almost missed it: *"...I'm tired of it being small."*

The pool flared. Red light climbed the walls like dawn arriving underground, and for one heartbeat Yara saw images move in the water — wings, vast and slow, a shape of fire folding itself around a small figure standing in a market square — and then it sank back to black, and Orsen sat back on their heels shaking, alive, *known*.

"Your turn," Maren said.

Yara approached the pool the way she had once approached her coven-mother after the trial: knowing the truth would cost her, and knowing that lies would cost more. She knelt. The water showed her her own face, then showed her the night she had worked the forbidden tides, then showed her the faces of the coven elders turning away from her one by one, and asked, in a voice that was not a voice but a pressure against her skull:

*Who are you?*

"An exile," Yara said. And then, because the pressure did not lift, because the truth under the truth was demanded: "A keeper. Last of the keepers. I carry an ember in a salt jar and I have carried it for six years and I don't know if I've been protecting it or just hoarding grief." Her hands were fists on her knees. "I want to give it somewhere to go."

The pressure lifted.

The pool went dark, and warm, and somehow that was worse than any judgment — the terrible gentleness of being believed.

* * *

Maren gave them bread and bitter tea in the orchard's heart, under the great tree, and only then brought out the stone.

It was the size of a fist, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, and when she peeled the cloth back Yara's breath left her in a rush. It was covenant-stone. She had seen drawings in the coven archives, charcoal rubbings of a thing no living witch had touched: grey shot through with veins of red fire, the same veins, the same pulse, as the ember in her jar. But where the archive drawings showed a whole and shining relic, the shard in Maren's hands was cracked nearly through, dull, its fire-thin veins guttering like lamps running dry.

"There were twelve of these once," Maren said. "Twelve oath-keepers, twelve stones, one covenant. Each stone holds a piece of the speaking — the old words, split apart so no one mouth could own them all. Mine holds the first word." She turned it over. The red light moved inside it, weakly, like blood through a failing heart. "I was going to say it was treasure. Everyone always assumes treasure. Look at it properly, girl. What does it look like?"

Yara looked. "A wound."

"It looks like a wound," Maren agreed, "because it is one. When you take this, you take the burden of remembering a word that four hundred years of people have worked very hard to forget. You take the knowledge that the fire in it is *measured* now — I have watched this stone dim for sixty years, and I can tell you within a season when it goes dark forever, and it is soon. You take the fact that hunters burn orchards now. That is the inheritance. Not the stone. The weight."

She held it out. To Yara. Not to Orsen.

Yara stared at it. All her life she had thought of herself as dispossessed — cast out of the coven, stripped of tide-rights, carrying someone else's sacred duty in a pickle jar like a vagrant with stolen goods. She had imagined, in her worst and best nights, that finding the oath-keepers would feel like coming home. Like a key turning. Like inheritance finally delivered.

It felt like this instead: a cracked, dying stone, held out by a woman who had spent her whole life raking ash, and the weight of it already settling into Yara's shoulders before her fingers even closed.

"Why me?" she asked. "You heard the pool. Orsen's the heir. The covenant's voice runs in their blood."

"Because the voice speaks," Maren said, "but the hands must carry." Her frost eyes went to Orsen, and softened by perhaps one degree, which Yara suspected was the most the old woman had softened in decades. "And because the stones choose their bearers, and this one has been reaching toward the sea for thirty years. Toward salt. It knew you were coming before your grandmother was born, girl. We are all just catching up to what the fire decided long ago."

Yara took the shard.

It was heavier than it should have been. It was cold, and then abruptly warm, and through her salt-touched palms she felt it *recognize* her — a shudder running up her arms like the first wave of a tide coming home, and with it, unbidden, a single syllable rising in her mind. Not in any language she knew. But she knew it anyway, the way she knew the pull of the moon on water.

One word. Of many. The first brick of a bridge that had to be rebuilt entire, and it was already almost too much to hold.

"How many more?" Orsen asked quietly.

"Two keepers live that I know of," Maren said, wrapping her tea in cloth, businesslike again, the giving done. "One keeps vigil in a chapel the sea took — Brother Ilex, if he hasn't drowned in his devotion, which he may have. The other..." She paused, and for the first time her knife-hand trembled slightly as she gathered her tools. "The other is in the capital. In the archive. Which means the crown holds the last keeper's stone, whether they know what they hold or not. And the crown has recently developed such an appetite for knowing things about gifted folk."

The orchard hummed around them, low and grieving and patient.

"So we go north," Yara said. The stone pulsed against her ribs where she'd stowed it, in time, she realized with a chill, with her own heartbeat. "To the flooded chapel first."

"Yes," Maren said. "Go quickly. And when you see riders on the road behind you—" she did not look at either of them, but bent to examine a branch, pruning knife whispering, "—do not come back here. Whatever happens, whatever you hear behind you, do not turn around. Some inheritances are meant to be carried away from the burning."

Far off, down the valley, a dog began to bark — and Orsen went still, head tilting, listening, and Yara watched the color leave their face as they heard whatever it had to say.

"Riders," Orsen said. "More than we heard on the flats. Coming fast."
