## 8. What the Covenant Was

I slept badly in the ash orchard, which was fitting, because the trees themselves looked like they had been sleeping badly for a hundred years.

They grew in rows that followed no human logic — curving, wandering, as if planted by something that walked on wings and wept where it landed. The bark was grey-white, streaked with dark veins that might have been mineral or might have been old sorrow. And the fruit, small and bitter-smelling, grew only at the bases of the trunks, low to the ground, exactly where tears would fall.

Maren woke us before dawn by setting a kettle over a fire that burned without smoke.

I sat up too fast. "Your fire's wrong," I said, before my manners caught up with me.

"It's not wrong," Maren said, not turning around. "It's borrowed." She fed a twist of dried apple-wood to the flames and they accepted it the way an old dog accepts a hand — gratefully, and with a kind of grief underneath. "Everything here is borrowed. That's rather the point."

Yara was already awake, already salt-eyed and wary, sitting with her back against a tree and the covenant-stone shard from last night cupped in both palms like water she was afraid to spill. She hadn't explained what happened between her and Maren in the orchard's deep rows after dark. She'd only come back to our bedroll of straw with her face doing something complicated and said, *Tomorrow. He needs to hear it from her.*

So: tomorrow. Which was now.

Maren poured three cups of something that smelled like pine and punishment and handed them around, then lowered herself onto a root with the care of a woman whose joints had outlived several kings. Up close she was impossibly old in a way that had nothing to do with wrinkles. Her eyes were the color of the orchard's bark. When she looked at you, you felt read.

"Orsen Kavanagh," she said.

"Just Orsen." The surname still tasted like a door closing.

"Mm. We'll see." She sipped. "You told me last night you didn't believe in dragons. You said it kindly, the way people say things when they think they're comforting an old fool. So today I'm going to tell you a story, and you're going to listen the way you listen to animals — which is to say, completely, and against your own good sense."

Yara made a sound that was almost a laugh. I glared at her. It had no effect whatsoever.

"The fire," Maren began, "is older than the world's memory of it."

* * *

She talked as the light came up through the weeping branches, and I will set it down here as she gave it, because some things should be carried whole.

In the beginning — not the beginning of everything, but of this arrangement, this *agreement* — the northern ranges were full of dragons the way a sky is full of weather. They were not monsters. They were not gods. They were long-lived and warm-blooded in a way nothing else has ever been, and their fire was not a weapon first. It was a metabolism. It was how they thought. A dragon's flame, banked in its chest, turned slow and bright, and everything near it grew toward it, the way plants grow toward sun.

The first humans who came north into the cold came half-dead, starving, freezing in passes that the dragons had shaped with centuries of wingbeats. And the dragons watched these small soft creatures huddle at the mouths of caves and die in the tens and hundreds each winter, and the eldest among them — Vessith's grandmother's grandmother, though names blur that far back, Maren said, even for dragons — did something no dragon had done before.

She opened her chest-fire and let a little of it out.

Not flame. Not destruction. An ember. A coal of living warmth that would not die so long as it was tended and remembered, and which could sit in a human hearth and make a human house the warmest place in a frozen world.

"She didn't do it out of love," Maren said flatly. "Don't romanticize it; the dragons never did. She did it because the humans were dying in sight of her, winter after winter, and a mind as long and slow as hers could not look away forever. Pity, if you want a word. But pity with conditions."

The conditions became the covenant.

Fire for remembrance. Warmth for witness. Each ember given into a human hearth came bound to an oath, spoken aloud in the old tongue, renewed each generation: *We will keep your name. We will keep your shape in story and stone. We will speak to you and for you, so that no dragon sleeps unremembered.* In exchange the fire lived, and spread, and every hearth lit from an ember carried a thread of the same oath, and every child born in firelight was born inside the covenant whether they knew it or not.

"And they knew it, once," Maren said. "Everyone knew it. There wasn't a village in three realms without its fire-tale, its carved dragon on the lintel, its night each year when the whole settlement went out to the high places and *spoke the names*. Not prayer. Conversation. That was the covenant's genius and its fragility — it ran on speech. Dragons don't need worship. They need someone talking *to* them, and about them. Attention, freely given, in words. Fire is fed by being witnessed. Forget a dragon and its flame gutters. Forget them all—" she gestured, one thin hand taking in the orchard, the cold morning, the whole grey sky— "and you get this."

"What, an orchard?" Yara asked quietly.

"A world where the fire is dying," Maren said. "This orchard grows where a dragon named Serevaneth wept herself out, four hundred years ago. She had kept vigil over a valley of humans for six hundred years, and then the valley forgot her — plague took the speakers first, then the stories, and within two generations the children used her name as a curse word for anything large and frightening. She came here, to the ridge above the valley she'd warmed, and she cried until there was nothing left in her but salt and seed. Everything grows here. Nothing burns. That is what forgetting makes."

I looked at the dark veins in the bark and understood, all at once and unwillingly, that they were not veins at all.

"The crown didn't cause this," Maren went on. "The crowns never do; they're just the wind that strips the last leaves. The forgetting started small. One village stopped the naming-night because of a bad harvest. One kingdom decided dragon-stories frightened trade. Then the covens—" she nodded at Yara— "your grandmothers' grandmothers sealed the salt-pacts and called the work of remembering superstition. Then the church of the ledgers came, and wrote down that fire was fuel and dragons were livestock, badly documented. And the embers began to gutter, one hearth at a time, so slowly no one alive ever saw the difference. You cannot miss what dies across ten generations. You can only inherit the cold."

"How many are left?" Yara asked. "How much fire?"

"One waking dragon. Perhaps two sleeping past waking, in the deepest ranges, if the stone has kept them. And embers — scattered, hoarded, mostly misunderstood. Salt-witches kept some, thinking them relics. Your jar, exile. My orchard's root-warmth. A few hearths in the far north where the very old still mutter names over the coals at night without knowing why their grandmothers taught them to." Maren's eyes came to rest on me, and stayed. "And one other thing remains. The thing the covenant cannot die without, and cannot live without. Its voice."

* * *

I should have seen it coming. Yara probably had; she'd gone very still beside me, the way she does when she's braced for weather.

"When the covenant was strong," Maren said, "it wasn't only spoken at fires. It was spoken everywhere, to everything, because the oath binds all living warmth — the dragons lent their fire to the world's hearths, and hearths mean every place life gathers. So there were those who carried the voice outward. Who could speak to beast and bird, not as a trick, not as a charm, but as the covenant speaking through a human throat. Reminding the world, creature by creature, that it was loved in words. They were called fire-speakers once. Later, when the fire thinned, just beast-speakers. Later than that—" and here her voice did not soften so much as sharpen— "later than that, they were called freaks, and charlatans, and witches, and they learned to shut their mouths or hang for it."

The kettle hissed. Somewhere above us, a wren sang, and I heard it the way I have always heard everything — as speech, as meaning, as a small brown voice saying *territory, territory, come then, come then* — and for the first time in my life I flinched away from hearing it.

"No," I said.

"Yes," said Maren, with terrible patience.

"I'm a charm-seller. I talk to dogs. I've talked to horses and rats and one extremely opinionated goose. That's all it is. A quirk. Nobody in my family—"

"Every family breaks somewhere," Maren said. "Yours broke twice. Sit still and listen, because this is the part your brother's blood comes from."

I sat still. It cost more than I can say.

"Three generations back," she said, "there was a Kavanagh woman named Edith, a fire-keeper in a northern valley that still held one true ember and one aging oath. She had the voice — full, unbroken, the real inheritance — and she spoke the covenant's renewal on the naming-night as her line had done for nine generations before her. But Edith's daughter married out of the valley, into the lowlands, into a family of respectable people who found the old words embarrassing, and the daughter let the naming lapse. Not out of malice. Out of *mortification*, which has killed more sacred things than malice ever managed. The ember was moved to a hearth where no one spoke over it. The oath went unrenewed. And the voice passed down anyway, generation to generation, thinner each time, like a river forced under stone — until it surfaced again in a child in Greyharbor market who talked to strays and thought it was only a strangeness."

My hands were shaking. I put the cup down before I spilled it.

"That's not possible," I said. "If the voice runs in my family, why me? Why not—"

And then I stopped, because I understood, and understanding was worse than not knowing.

"Why not Tammas," I finished, barely aloud.

"Gifts don't divide evenly," Maren said. "He got your mother's level head and your father's stubbornness and none of the voice. You got the whole river. And a boy who hears nothing where his sibling hears everything will find a reason, any reason, to explain the silence. Most of them choose resentment. Some choose law. Law gives the silence a shape you can salute."

Yara's hand found my wrist under the table of roots. I didn't pull away. That surprised me more than anything else that morning.

"So it's real," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "The gift. It's not a curse, not a trick, not a — it's an *office*. It's the covenant's own voice, and I'm the last one carrying it, and the fire is dying because—" Because the speakers stopped speaking. Because Edith's daughter was embarrassed. Because twenty years ago a child in Greyharbor decided survival meant silence, and the world got exactly as silent as they chose to be. "Because people like me shut up."

"Don't drown in it yet," Maren said briskly, and I realized she'd been watching me go under and had thrown me a rope made of rudeness. "Awe is useful. Terror is useful, briefly. Guilt is useless, and self-importance is worse. You didn't break the covenant, child. You inherited a broken one. The question — the only question — is whether you'll carry the voice forward or let it end in the last Kavanagh throat that ever muffled it."

"And if I refuse?" I asked. "If I just keep selling charms? What happens?"

Maren looked at the orchard. At the weeping trees, the tear-dark bark, the fruit growing low where grief fell.

"Then within your lifetime there will be no dragons anywhere," she said simply. "Not dead — worse. Unwitnessed. Gone out like rooms no one enters. And the cold will come the way it always comes: gradually, and then as a surprise. Humans survived the last cold age poorly. This one we'll survive having chosen."

* * *

I walked out alone into the rows afterward. I needed air, or distance, or possibly to be somewhere no one could watch me come apart politely.

Here is what I knew, walking under those grieving trees, and what I had known since the tide gate, if I'm honest, and maybe since the stray dog pressed his head into my hand while hunters walked past twenty feet away:

The gift had never once felt like mine. Every conversation I'd ever had with a beast had felt like standing in a doorway while something vast spoke through the gap — through *me* — and I'd spent twenty years trying to shut the door quietly. I thought the door led out of my life, into danger. It had never occurred to me that it might lead inward. That the voice wasn't leaking into me from outside. That it was a room in the family house, my room, furnished by nine generations of speakers, and I was the first heir in a century to stand in it and call it empty.

A heron dropped out of the marsh-grey sky and landed on the low branch ahead of me, folding itself up like a closed umbrella.

*River-walker,* it said, in the flat patient tone herons use. *Old one says come back. Old one says the stone sings louder when you're near.*

"Does it," I said aloud, and heard my own voice crack on the second word.

*You're loud,* the heron observed. *Since yesterday you're very loud. The marsh heard you in your sleep.*

I stood in the ash orchard, in the tears of a dead dragon, and laughed until it turned into the other thing, and the heron waited through all of it with the sublime indifference of herons, and when I finally straightened up I was not the same person who had walked into the rows.

I was terrified. Properly, bone-level terrified. Not of hanging — I'd carried that fear so long it had worn smooth as a river stone, almost comfortable. This was new. This was the terror of mattering. Of a debt nine generations wide arriving at my single narrow account. Of a dying fire looking at me, specifically me, and waiting for words.

When I came back to the fire, Yara and Maren had the map out — the salt-scratched hide with its three realms and its scatter of marked places. Yara looked up at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod once, the way you nod at someone stepping off a ledge who has decided to fly.

"There's a cave in the northern ranges," Maren was saying, tapping the hide. "Two weeks' walk, less if the marsh cooperates. The stone will sing stronger as you close. She'll be there. The last waking one. Vessith, oldest of the old."

"And she'll talk to me?" I asked.

Maren smiled, and it was the first smile I'd seen on her, and it was heartbreaking, because it was hopeful.

"She's been waiting sixty years for anyone to knock," she said. "But understand this, Kavanagh — Vessith doesn't grant audiences. She takes stock. She will know within a breath whether the voice in you is real, and if it is, she will not ask you gently. Dragons are dying, and dying creatures don't waste breath on gentle."

Yara rolled the map. I picked up my cup. Above the orchard the sun finally cleared the ridge, and every weeping tree threw a long shadow east, all pointing the same direction, like a signpost written in grief.

North, then. Toward the last fire, and the first true test of a voice I had spent my whole life refusing to raise.

The heron lifted from its branch, circled once overhead, and called down cheerfully: *Loud one walks north! Loud one walks north!*

By nightfall, I had no doubt, the entire marsh would know.
