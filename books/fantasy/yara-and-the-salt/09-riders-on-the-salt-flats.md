## 9. Riders on the Salt Flats

The salt flats began where Maren's orchard ended, a white plain so wide it looked like the sky had fallen and forgotten how to rise. Three days' walk, Yara said, before the marsh country swallowed us again. Three days of nothing to hide behind.

I hated it immediately. Twenty years of market stalls had taught me that crowds were cover — a hundred bodies between you and the thing you feared. The flats offered no such mercy. A rider could be seen from a league off out here. So could a fugitive.

"Walk in my tracks," Yara said, not turning around. She moved across the crust like she'd been born on it, each step placed with a precision I couldn't read. "The salt remembers weight. If we're lucky, it forgets ours by morning."

"The salt *remembers* things?"

"Everything remembers things, Orsen. You of all people should know." She glanced back at me, and there was something almost like a smile at the corner of her mouth. "You just have to ask."

The covenant-stone shard rode against her breastbone on a cord, and every few hours she'd press her palm flat over it, eyes going distant, listening for whatever it whispered. It had led us true from the ash orchard. It would lead us, she said, to water — and where there was water deep enough and old enough, there might be a dragon.

Behind us, Maren's orchard shrank to a smudge of green on the horizon. I kept looking back anyway. Something about leaving it felt like leaving a lit candle in an empty house.

"You're doing it again," Yara said.

"Doing what?"

"Worrying at the world like a dog with a bone. Save your teeth. You'll need them."

We made camp that first night in a shallow fold of the land, no fire, sharing dried fish and hard bread. The wind came off the flats carrying voices — or what I'd spent twenty years pretending weren't voices. A vole somewhere near my boot, muttering its small anxieties about owls. Gulls far inland, arguing over a dead fish. And beneath it all, faint as breath on glass, something else: a low, slow sound with no animal shape to it, coming from the north, from under the earth itself.

I didn't tell Yara about that one. Not yet. Some things you want to be sure of before you say them aloud, because saying them makes them real.

* * *

The second day, the riders found us.

I heard them before I saw them — hooves, but wrong, muffled, wrapped. Hunters who wanted to be heard would never wrap their horses' feet. These meant to arrive without warning, which meant they already knew exactly where we were.

"Down," I said, and pulled Yara into a trough between two salt ridges, flat against the crust. Above the ridge line, six shapes crested the distant rise, dark against the pale sky, fanning out in a pattern I recognized from the edict's diagrams posted at the Tide Gate. Crown hunters. Sweep formation. They weren't chasing us. They were *herding* us.

"They've been tracking since the orchard," Yara breathed. Her hand was already at the salt jar at her belt — the little jar with the dying ember inside, the one thing she guarded closer than the covenant-stone. "Someone talked. One of Maren's neighbors, maybe. Or—"

"Or they followed us out and doubled back to cut the road north." My mouth had gone dry. "Yara. There's no cover on this flat. None. We can't outrun horses."

"No," she agreed. Her voice had gone very quiet, very level, the way it did when she was standing at the edge of something. "We can't."

The riders swept wider, closing the arc. Through the ground I felt their horses — confusion, mostly, and thirst, and a dull trained patience. Horses don't hate. That's men. But the horses knew their riders wanted something badly, and it made them nervous, and their nervousness bled down through their hooves into the salt and up into my palms where I pressed them flat against the crust.

One of the riders shouted something. Too far to catch the words, but I caught the tone. Command. The formation tightened.

"Yara," I said. "What are you doing?"

She had knelt. Both palms flat on the salt, head bowed, lips moving — not words I knew, or words anyone knew anymore, maybe. The air around her changed. It's hard to describe except that the light went thin, like the skin of the sky had been stretched too tight, and every hair on my arms stood up.

"Yara. What are you *doing?*"

"What I swore I'd never do again." She looked up at me, and her eyes had gone white as the flats themselves, salt bleeding up through the color until there was nothing left but mineral and light. "Hold on to me."

"I don't understand—"

"Hold. On."

I gripped her shoulder. She exhaled, long and slow, and spoke one word into the salt.

The world went blind.

Not dark — blind. White. A whiteness that came up out of the ground itself, the whole plain flaring like a struck match held to the eye of the sky. I heard the riders scream — the men, I mean; the horses screamed too, different, higher, more frightened than angry. Six hundred paces away and the light reached them anyway, poured into their eyes whether they were shut or not, because it wasn't light anymore, it was the memory of light, every dawn the salt had ever reflected, given back all at once.

Then it was gone, and the flats were just flats again, gray-white and silent, and Yara sagged against me like a dropped marionette.

I caught her. Her skin was cold and gritty, crusted with salt that hadn't been there a moment before, as if the ground had tried to make her part of itself and she'd refused at the last instant.

"Can you walk?"

"Ask me in a minute." Her voice was a rasp. When her eyes opened they were brown again, but rimmed red, bloodshot to the corners. "They'll be blind for hours. Maybe a day. The horses longer."

"And you?"

"Oh, I'll pay for it." She laughed, and it wasn't a funny laugh. "That's the arithmetic of salt-magic, Orsen. Nothing borrowed. Everything paid. Help me up."

I got her arm over my shoulders and we went north across the flats, stumbling, half-carrying each other, while behind us six crown hunters sat their horses and wept and cursed at a sky that had done them no harm.

It should have felt like victory. It felt like watching someone burn a bridge while still standing on it.

* * *

We walked through the night. By the gray hour before dawn, Yara could move on her own, though she leaned on me when she thought I wouldn't notice. We came off the flats onto hardpan and scrub, and there, at last, we let ourselves stop.

She sat down heavily against a leaning stone and took out the ember jar. In the pre-dawn dimness I could see it clearly for the first time: a coal that wasn't quite a coal, glowing a deep sullen orange, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. Slower than when I'd first seen it in Greyharbor. I didn't say so. She knew.

"That word you spoke," I said. "On the flats. What was it?"

"A name." She turned the jar in her fingers. "The salt has a name. Everything old has a name, if you know how to ask. I called it, and it answered, and now—" She stopped. Started again. "When the covens exiled me, they made me swear three oaths. Never teach the craft. Never speak a name unbidden. Never call the salt to do harm." She looked at me, and her face in the gray light was stripped bare of everything clever and wry she usually wore. "Today I broke the third. Maybe the second. The salt blinded men, Orsen. Men. The covens drew the line at dragons and drowned villages, and I crossed it to save one charm-seller who two weeks ago was nobody to me."

The words landed harder than she probably intended. Or maybe she intended exactly that.

"Why?" I asked. It came out smaller than I meant it to.

She was quiet a long moment. Out on the hardpan, some night bird made its one note, over and over, patient as a clock.

"Because I watched my coven sit on their hands for forty years while the fire died," she said finally, "because oaths were more comfortable than action, and I told myself exile was the price of my honesty when really it was the price of my cowardice — I left before I had to choose." She closed her hand around the jar. "You're the first thing since that made choosing feel worth the cost. Don't make it strange."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"Good." She tucked the jar away. "Because it gets worse. The hunters will report what happened on the flats. Blinding light out of bare ground — there's only one craft does that. The crown will know a salt-witch is traveling with the beast-speaker. They'll know it's me. My name goes on the edicts beside yours."

"So we're even," I said. "Two names on a list."

"We're not even. You lost a stall. I've lost—" She gestured vaguely southward, toward everything. "Doesn't matter. It's done. The question is what we do next, and the answer is the same as it was: north. Find the stone's whisper. Find the dragon, if there's still a dragon to find."

There was. I was nearly sure of it. That low slow sound under the earth had been clearer all day, and now, sitting in the dark with Yara bleeding guilt and defiance in equal measure, I let myself say it:

"There's something north of here. Big. Old. Its breathing comes up through the ground like it's coming through water." I hugged my knees. "I think it's sleeping. Or trying to. I think it hurts."

Yara stared at me. Then she laughed — a real one this time, short and disbelieving — and pressed her palm to the covenant-stone at her throat.

"It's been pulling us north for three days," she said. "I thought it was the stone leading. It was the stone *answering.*"

* * *

We learned what the cost looked like on the fourth morning, when a crow dropped out of a cold sky and landed on a fence post not ten feet from us, and looked at me, and said: *Burned. All burned. The green woman's trees. Smoke for two days.*

I stood very still. Crows exaggerate — it's their nature, they're gossips with wings — but not about fire. Fire is the one thing crows respect.

"Which trees?" I asked aloud, and Yara went rigid beside me, because she'd learned by now that when I spoke to empty air, the air answered.

*The weeping ones,* the crow said. *The crooked rows. The old woman stood in the gate and would not leave and the soldiers carried her out and she watched. She watched the whole time. She did not cry until the last tree fell.*

"Maren," Yara whispered.

*They asked her where the beast-speaker went,* the crow went on, hopping along the post, feathers ruffling in the wind. *She said she was old and her memory was older and both were failing. They burned the orchard anyway. Retribution, the tall one said. The tall one with the ledger-voice. He gave the order and then he rode to the far side of the smoke so he would not have to watch.*

My stomach turned over. *The tall one with the ledger-voice.* I didn't need a name. Some part of me had known since the Tide Gate that my brother's shadow would find me eventually; I'd just hoped to be past the horizon before it arrived.

"He didn't burn it," I heard myself say. "He ordered it and then he hid from it."

"Is that better or worse?" Yara asked quietly.

"I don't know. I keep waiting for it to become one or the other."

The crow cocked its head. *The old woman walks north,* it added, almost as an afterthought. *Alone. Carrying seeds in her apron. She says trees can be replanted. She says the ones who burned them forgot that.*

And then it lifted off the post and was gone, a black comma against the white sky, and Yara and I stood in the wind and let the weight of it settle on us.

"She lied for us," I said. "An old woman with nothing left to lose, and she lied to armed men for two strangers, and they took her life's work for it."

"Yes."

"And she's walking north. With seeds. At her age. Because she thinks the covenant is worth replanting."

"Yes." Yara's jaw was set. "So we don't get to stop either. That's what it costs, Orsen. Choosing each other means other people pay the bill, and the only way to honor it is to keep moving and make the choice mean something."

I thought of my stall in Greyharbor, the careful lies, the small charms, the twenty years of silence. I had told myself hiding was harmless. Hiding had a body count after all — it just collected slowly, in orchards and embers and old women's tears.

"North, then," I said.

"North." Yara shouldered her pack and pressed her hand once more to the covenant-stone, and her expression shifted — listening, straining. Then she went still in a way I'd learned to recognize.

"What is it?"

"Orsen." Her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "The stone isn't whispering anymore. It's *singing.*"

Far to the north, under the hardpan and the scrub and the roots of the mountains just beginning to show their shoulders against the sky, something vast turned over in its sleep — and I felt it the way you feel a door open at the far end of a house: a change in the pressure of the air, a draft from a room you thought was sealed.

It knew we were coming.

And behind us, days back but gaining, the tall hunter with the ledger-voice rode north through the smoke of a burned orchard, following our trail and his own conscience, neither of which he could put down.
