## 10. The Grieving Dragon

The stone sang for two days.

Not words — I want to be clear about that, because everything that came after has made people assume the covenant-stone spoke to us in sentences, like a magistrate reading charges. It didn't. It sang the way a struck bell sings, one note held so long you stop hearing it and start *feeling* it, a vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the wet dark of the ear. Yara carried it wrapped in wool inside her coat, and still we both heard it, and by the second morning we had stopped pretending it was only in our heads.

We climbed out of the marsh country into hills the color of old iron, where the scrub gave way to bent pines and the pines gave way to nothing at all, just bare shoulders of granite with snow in their creases. The wind came off the high passes smelling of stone and distance. Nobody followed us up there. Even the crown's hunters, I think, believed the northern wastes were empty — which was true, in the way that a locked room is empty.

Yara walked ahead because she could read the stone's pull better than I could, angling left when it wanted left, stopping dead once for a full quarter hour while it shifted its song to a lower register, then turning north again. I watched her do this and thought, not for the first time, that exile had built her into something with no wasted motion at all. Everything about Yara was necessary. It made me ache a little, and I didn't examine why, because there was a dragon ahead of us, allegedly, and I needed all my courage in one place.

"Tell me again what Maren said," I called to her. "Exactly."

"She said," Yara answered without turning, "that the stone would take us to what's left of the fire. And she said—" here she did pause, not for the stone but for herself— "*be gentle with her. She has been alone longer than your whole nation has existed.*"

"Her."

"Maren said *her*. Vessith. The name is older than the word for name, or some such thing. Old women in orchards love saying things like that." But Yara's hand had gone to the pocket where the stone slept, and stayed there, and I understood that the irreverence was armor, the same way my small talk had always been armor, the same way twenty years of selling luck-charms to gullible fishermen had been armor. We were both of us people who had learned to joke on the approach to wounds.

*

We found the cave on the third morning, and I will tell you honestly: we nearly walked past it.

It was a seam in the mountainside, half-screened by a fallen slab of granite furred with grey lichen, breathing out air that was warm — warm, up here above the treeline, in a wind that could strip paint. The warmth was the first wrong thing. The second was the quiet. The mountain has a sound, if you listen long enough: water moving under stone, the tick of frost, the small business of birds. Inside the seam there was none of that. The silence had weight. It pressed on the ears like deep water.

Yara unwrapped the stone. The singing stopped — cut off clean, the way a singer closes her mouth at the end of a song rather than letting it fade. Then the stone crumbled. Not dramatically; it didn't blaze or shatter. It simply went to salt in her palm, grey grains sliding between her fingers, its work done.

"It brought us home," Yara whispered, and I heard her voice crack on the word, and pretended I hadn't.

We went in.

The passage opened the way a throat opens, narrowing first and then widening into a dark so complete that for a moment I thought we had gone blind. Then the dark moved.

I felt it before I saw it — a displacement of air enormous and slow, the kind of pressure change you feel when a ship passes in a fog, and beneath it a heat rising from below like summer rising off a road. My eyes adjusted to a light I couldn't locate, a dull red suffusion coming from everywhere and nowhere, and shape assembled itself out of the darkness the way continents assemble out of the sea.

She was dying. That was my first coherent thought, and it arrived with total certainty, the way you know a burned house is a burned house. Vessith lay coiled in the cavern's heart like a banked coal — that was the phrase that came to me and would not leave, banked coal, fire kept barely alive under ash — and her scales, which must have been mirror-bright once, were matte and grey as slag, cracked in long seams through which the faint red light breathed in and out. In and out. Each breath took ten of mine. Her ribs moved like hills settling.

Her head was vast beyond sense, resting on the stone floor, and her eye—

Her eye was open. It was the size of a cartwheel and the color of a candle seen through horn, and it was watching us, and had been from the moment we entered, and I understood with a lurch that everything slow about her was choice, not weakness. She was conserving. She had been conserving for a very long time.

Yara sank to her knees beside me. I heard her whisper something in a language I didn't know — salt-witch cant, maybe, or older — and I felt the dragon's attention slide past me to her, weigh her, and return.

To me.

*You are the one who speaks.*

I don't know what I expected the voice of a dragon to sound like. Thunder, probably. What I got was worse, in a way: it sounded like a person. Tired, courteous, precise. It didn't come through my ears at all. It came through the same door the dog in Greyharbor had spoken through, the door I'd kept barred for twenty years, except that now the door stood open and the whole house was full of her voice.

"Yes," I said aloud, uselessly, and then, fumbling, reaching for the other way of speaking, the way I'd used on herons and wolves and one terrified mule: *Yes. I speak.*

*So few left who do,* she said. *I had begun to think the door itself had rotted shut. Come closer, small speaker. My sight is fire, and my fire is low.*

We crossed the cavern floor toward her, and the heat grew until it was like standing in an open oven door, and still she was cold compared to what she must have been. I could feel the absence of her heat the way you feel a missing tooth. Up close her scale-slag rose higher than Yara's head, and the seams between plates were clogged with grey ash, and I understood that the ash was her own body, shedding, failing, the fire eating its own hearth because there was nothing else left to eat.

"What happened to you?" Yara asked. She meant it kindly. The dragon received it like a knife.

*What always happens,* Vessith said. *We were forgotten.*

*

Here is what I learned in that cave, in the space of an afternoon that felt like a year, and I set it down plainly because someone has to:

Dragons do not die of wounds. They do not die of age, or at least not on any schedule a human clock could measure. They die of forgetting. Fire is memory; memory is fire; the covenant was never a contract between species the way the ledgers in the capital describe it, two parties trading goods across a table. It was closer to a heart. Humans remembered the dragons — told their stories, spoke their names at hearths, wove them into songs and oaths and the little prayers of ordinary days — and that remembering fed the dragons' fire the way breath feeds ours. In exchange, the dragons lent their flame outward: to forge, to warm, to light the long winters of the young world.

When humans stopped telling the stories — slowly, then suddenly, then by edict — the flame began to starve.

*I felt the first forgetting like a cold wind,* Vessith said. Her voice had settled into a rhythm, the rhythm of someone telling a thing they have told themselves ten thousand times alone. *One hearth going dark in a fishing village. One child's song unsung. Small things. Then the small things became decades, and the decades became law, and men wrote books explaining that we had never existed at all, and the books were remembered better than we were. Do you understand what that is, speaker? To be argued out of your own fire? To lie in the dark and feel yourself becoming a story no one tells, and then a story no one knows, and then—*

She paused. The red light in her seams guttered, actually guttered, flinched.

*Then nothing. My kin went out like candles. Not slain — extinguished. Some of them chose it, in the end. It is gentler than starving. I did not choose it, because I am stubborn, and because I am the keeper of the covenant's ember, and a keeper does not put down her charge because the keeping hurts.*

"And the ember," Yara said softly, "is why Maren sent us."

*Maren,* Vessith said, and something moved through her — not warmth exactly, but the memory of warmth, grief's ghost. *Little green tender. She still keeps the orchard? The trees still grow where we wept?*

"They grow," Yara said. "They burned, three days ago. The crown fired them looking for us."

The silence that followed was the longest yet. The great eye turned from Yara to me and back, and in it I watched something I had no name for — a being deciding, with the last of her strength, whether hope was worth the expense.

*Then time is shorter than I feared,* she said at last. *Good. Desperation clarifies.*

*

I want to tell you about the moment the speaking changed.

For the first hour, I had been translating — not consciously, but structuring, tidying, doing what I had always done, standing between her voice and the world like a customs officer. When she said *forgotten*, I rendered it in my head as *neglected*. When she said *kin*, I softened it to *family*, as if family weren't the more frightening word. Twenty years of habit. Twenty years of making the gift small so the gift would go unnoticed.

And then Vessith said, gently, with something that in a human face would have been a smile:

*You are still hiding, even now. Even in here. Why, speaker? Who taught you that your door was shameful?*

And I — I don't know how to describe what happened except that something in me sat down, like a traveler setting down a pack carried too long. My brother's face in the doorway when I was nine, listening to me talk to the barn cat. The schoolmaster's strap. The neighbor women crossing the street. Twenty years of charm stalls and careful lies, and every one of those years I had told myself I was hiding to survive, which was true, and had also been, all along, a way of agreeing with everyone who ever said the gift was filth. I had swallowed their verdict and called it prudence.

"No one," I said, "taught me. They just taught me to bury it. And I let them. Because buried things don't burn."

*And yet here you stand in the last fire in the world,* Vessith said, *asking it to save you.*

"I wasn't asking it to save me," I said. "I was asking—" and stopped, because the honest answer rose in my throat like a swallow of hot water. "I was asking if there was anything left worth being. If the thing I am, underneath the hiding, was ever anything but a curse with my name on it."

The eye regarded me. The cavern breathed. Somewhere far above, wind moved over the mountain mouth, and I heard it twice — once with ears, once through the stone, through her, the way you hear a drum through a floor.

*Small speaker,* she said. *Listen to me. Your gift is not a curse and it is not a trick and it is not a strange appetite to be fed in alleys when no one watches. It is a sentence of an old language, and you are the only living mouth that can pronounce it. When your ancestors broke faith with mine, the covenant lost its voice — and the gift passed down through the breaker's line like a debt, waiting for one of that line to have the courage to speak instead of hide. You are not an accident. You are an heir.*

"The Kavanaghs helped break it," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "That's what the stone showed Yara. That's what the ledger says."

*Yes.*

"Then I'm heir to the breaking too."

*You are heir to the mending,* she said. *That is the mercy of debts. They can be paid by anyone willing to carry them.*

I looked at Yara. She was watching me with an expression I hadn't seen on her before — not pity, not awe. Recognition. The look of someone watching another exile decide to stop walking away from the thing they were exiled for.

"Ask her," Yara said quietly. "Ask her what you've been afraid to ask since the orchard."

So I asked. *What happens to the ones who speak? What does it cost?*

And Vessith was silent for so long that I counted forty of her breaths, eight hundred of mine, and the red light rose and fell, rose and fell, and at last she said:

*Come here, and I will show you what it costs to be forgotten. And then you will understand why I cannot answer the other question yet. There is a bargain in me, small ones, and a hard one, and I will not spring it on you until you have seen everything.*

I crossed the last few yards of stone. I laid my palm against her scale, expecting slag-cold, and found instead a heat like a heartbeat, faint and stubborn and unbelievably lonely.

And she opened her memory to me the way you'd open a window in a sealed house, and I fell into centuries.

*

I came back to myself on the cavern floor with Yara's hands on my shoulders and my own voice hoarse from weeping. I won't tell you everything I saw. Some of it belongs to her, and some of it I genuinely cannot render — how do I describe the taste of a medieval sky, the weight of a thousand wingbeats as music, the specific grief of outliving the child who named you? But I will tell you the thing I brought back, the thing that changed me, because it is the hinge on which everything after turned:

Being forgotten does not hurt dragons the way a wound hurts. It hurts the way drowning hurts — slow, systemic, a pressure closing from all directions at once. Every forgotten story was a cup of water in the lungs. Vessith had spent four hundred years drowning politely, refusing to thrash, because thrashing would frighten the humans she still loved.

And in all that time, in all that dark, do you know what she had wanted? Not worship. Not sacrifice. She had wanted what I had wanted behind my charm stall in Greyharbor, talking to stray dogs in whispers, keeping my one true self folded up small as a love letter:

She had wanted to be spoken to. Known. Once, openly, by anyone.

"Yara," I said, when I could speak. She was still kneeling beside me, and her face was wet too, though I hadn't seen her cry. "I'm done hiding."

"I know," she said. "I watched you stop about a minute ago."

I laughed — actually laughed, lying on the floor of a dying dragon's tomb, and the laugh echoed strangely off the vast ribs of the cavern, and somewhere in the dark Vessith made a sound like distant rockfall that I understood, finally and completely, was also laughter.

*Now,* said the last dragon in the world, and the play was gone from her voice, and the ember-light in her seams gathered itself and steadied. *Now I will tell you the bargain. And you must understand, speaker, before I say it, that I would spare you if I could — and that I cannot, because you are the only one who can carry it.*

She drew a breath that pulled the heat from half the cavern, and began.

"One word of the covenant remains unspoken. It cannot be written, only said, and it cannot be said by me, nor by your salt-witch, nor by any oath-keeper Maren gathers. It must be spoken by a Kavanagh — the blood that broke the trust — freely, at the gathering, before midwinter. Or the fire goes out, and everything I am becomes ash and story, and your world grows colder by one miracle forever."

Behind us, at the cave mouth, the wind changed direction — carrying, faint and unmistakable on the freezing air, the smell of horses and woodsmoke and oiled leather.

Riders. Coming up the mountain.

Vessith's eye found mine in the dark.

"You're going to need to tell me," I said, "which of us gets to break that news to my brother."
