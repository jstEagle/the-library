## 11. Vessith's Bargain

The cave breathed.

That was Yara's first thought when they crossed the threshold — not *dragons live here*, not *we've found it*, but simply that the cave breathed, slow and vast, the way a sleeping thing breathes, and the stone walls moved with it the way a chest moves under blankets. The air was warm. Not hearth-warm. Body-warm. Warm the way salt shallows are warm at dusk in the southern coves where she'd learned her craft, before the covens took the warmth away with everything else.

Ahead of her, Orsen had gone still in the way they went still around animals — weight settled, hands loose, eyes soft. Yara had watched them do it with marsh ponies and herons and once, absurdly, with a hostile gull. But this was different. This wasn't a trick of posture. This was Orsen standing in front of something enormous and being *recognized*.

And the dragon — because it was a dragon, because Maren's covenant-stone had sung in Yara's palm for three days straight until they reached the mountain and there was no longer any doubt — the dragon lay in the deep of the cave like a banked coal.

That was the image that stuck, and never left: a coal. Vessith was grey-scaled and vast, big as the tithe barn back in the cove villages, big as the chapel at Greyharbor, and she lay curled so tightly around herself that Yara couldn't tell at first what was wing and what was tail. Between two foreclaws, cupped like something stolen or prayed over, glowed a light no bigger than a fist.

An ember. The same ember Yara carried in her salt jar — but a thousand times older, and dying by the same arithmetic.

"Vessith," Orsen said. Not loudly. The name came out of them shaped strangely, syllables bent into a rhythm Yara didn't know, and she realized with a chill down her spine that Orsen hadn't spoken Common at all. They'd spoken *dragon*. First try. Like a door opening onto a room they'd never entered but had always known the layout of.

The great head lifted. It took a long time — the motion had the creak of something that hadn't moved in years, scales grinding against scale, and the ember-light swung and threw shadows like fleeing birds across the ceiling. One eye found them. It was molten gold going to ash at the rim, and it was ancient, and it was *tired*, and when it fixed on Orsen the whole cave seemed to lean in.

Then the eye moved to Yara.

She felt it like a hand on her sternum. Every story she'd ever heard about dragons said you didn't meet the gaze; you kept your eyes on the ground and your palms open and you hoped. She met it anyway. Something in her — the same stubborn thing that had gotten her exiled, probably — refused to look away from the last waking dragon in the world while it decided whether she was worth its remaining fire.

"You," Vessith said.

Her voice didn't come through the air so much as through the stone, through the floor, up through the soles of Yara's boots into her teeth. It was female and vast and cracked at the edges, like a bell that had been rung too long in weather.

Yara's mouth was dry. "Me."

"The salt-witch." The nostrils flared, drawing a slow tide of air across them both. "I smell your craft. I smell your oath-breaking." A pause, and then something that might have been, in a smaller creature, amusement: "Good. Oath-keepers who have never broken anything are useless."

* * *

They made camp inside the cave mouth, because Vessith told them — in that voice through the bones — that the hunters' patrols did not come within a league of the mountain anymore. *They think the mountain is dead,* she said. *Let them.*

Orsen sat close to the dragon, cross-legged on the warm stone like a child at a grandmother's knee, and the two of them spoke in that rolling old tongue for hours while Yara built a small fire out of scrub pine and pretended not to eavesdrop and failed completely. She caught fragments only. Orsen's accent improved as they went, hesitation smoothing into fluency, and every so often Vessith would make a sound low in her chest that was unmistakably laughter, and Orsen would flush in the ember-light like someone caught laughing in church.

It should have made her jealous. It didn't. What it made her feel was worse and stranger: hope. Real hope, the dangerous kind, the kind she'd spent four years strangling in its sleep because hope was how the covens got you, hope was how exile broke people — they hoped, and then the letters never came, and then the hoping killed them slower than any working could.

She fed twigs to her little fire and watched the last waking dragon watch her partner, and she let herself hope anyway.

Near midnight, Vessith called them both close.

The ember between her claws had brightened since sundown. Yara understood why before she'd worked out how to say it: company. Attention. Being remembered. The dragon was burning better tonight than she had in years because two humans were looking at her, and that fact was so sad and so revealing that Yara had to look at her own hands for a while.

"You carry my sister's flame," Vessith said to Yara. "In salt."

"Ashenveil's ember." Yara drew the jar from her pack. Through the packed white crystals, the mote of fire still pulsed, faint, patient. "The covens were supposed to keep it lit forever. My grandmother's generation let it gutter. Mine inherited the jar." She set it on the stone between them. "I stole it when they cast me out. Seemed wrong to leave it with people who'd forgotten what it was for."

"And do you remember what it is for?"

"I remember the stories. Fire lent to hearths. Dragons remembered in return." She shrugged, hating how small the gesture sounded. "Stories aren't keeping, though. Keeping is work. Nobody wanted the work."

"No," Vessith said. "Nobody ever has. That is the whole history of my kind and yours, salt-witch, in one sentence. Well done."

Orsen laughed — a startled, tired sound — and even Yara felt her mouth twitch. Then the gold-and-ash eye turned to Orsen, and the amusement drained out of the cave like water through sand.

"Heir," Vessith said. "Do you understand yet what you are?"

"I'm starting to," Orsen said quietly.

"Then let us finish the lesson, because we are running out of time, and I am running out of flame, and I am done — do you hear me — *done* with watching my fire die politely." The ember flared. Heat rolled off it in a wave that dried Yara's eyes. For one instant the great grey body seemed lit from within, veins of orange showing through the scales like fire behind shutters, and Yara saw — really saw — what a dragon was when it burned properly, and understood in her marrow what the world would lose when this one went out.

Then the light sank again, and Vessith was an old coal in a cold grate, and she spoke her bargain.

* * *

"I will give my flame," she said. "Not lend it. Lending is what broke us. I will pour what remains of Ashenveil's line-fire into the covenant-stone, and the stone will hold it, and the speaking will reforge the bond — if, and only if, the words are spoken true, by the right mouths, before midwinter."

"How many mouths?" Yara asked.

"Four kinds. The keeper of growing things — your orchard-witch has given you her shard already, so her part is done. The keeper of the words, who must speak the longest portion — your drowned monk, if he still lives, and he will, monks are weeds. The keeper of the salt, who binds the working to the world." The eye swiveled to Yara. "You. Whether your covens approve of you or not is between you and them. The salt does not care about their politics."

Yara's throat tightened. "And the fourth?"

Vessith was quiet a long moment. Outside, wind moved along the mountain's flank with a sound like slow breathing, and the little fire Yara had built popped its pitch pockets, and the dragon looked at Orsen with all the weight of a hundred years of patience.

"The voice," she said. "The covenant's own voice. The beast-speaker. You, heir. That part we knew already, you and I. Your gift is not a talent. It is an office. When the words are spoken, the covenant will need a tongue, and yours is the only one left."

"That part I can almost bear," Orsen said. Yara heard the fear under the steadiness and loved them for it. "Speaking in the square, or on a mountaintop, in front of whoever comes — I've stopped pretending I can hide. Fine. Me. But you said four kinds of mouth, and you've named three of mine and one of hers." Their jaw worked. "Who speaks the word of the Kavanagh?"

The cave breathed. In, and out.

"There it is," Yara said softly, mostly to herself, because she'd been turning the ledger pages over in her mind since the capital, since Orsen had read aloud the names of their own ancestors with a voice gone flat as slate. She'd hoped the dragon wouldn't confirm it. Hope, again. Stupid, brave hope.

"The covenant is a circle of oaths," Vessith said. "Each oath answers another. Fire answers memory. Memory answers salt. Salt answers the voice. And the voice—" the ember dimmed, as if the telling itself cost fuel— "the voice answers the breakers. The covenant cannot be reforged clean, because it was not broken clean. It was broken by a family that swore to guard it and chose crown and coin instead. Kavanaghs held the eastern watch for nine generations. Nine generations of fire-wards and dragon-roads and sworn memory, and then Aldric Kavanagh signed the Severance and took his payment in titles, and his children hunted the very roads their grandfathers had walked beside wing."

"So it has to be a Kavanagh," Orsen said. Their voice had gone somewhere flat and far. "To unmake what we made."

"It has to be a Kavanagh *willingly*," Vessith corrected, and there was iron under the weariness now. "A coerced word seals nothing. A shamed word seals nothing. The breakers' blood must choose to stand in the circle and speak the answering word with an open throat. That is the price the covenant sets for its own mending. The wound closes only from the hand that cut it."

"And if no Kavanagh will?" Yara asked, though she knew.

"Then the flame goes into the stone and waits, and I die, and the stone waits for another age of the world, and the waiting may be long enough that there is nothing left in the world to mend."

Silence. The wind. The pop of pitch.

Orsen was staring at their own hands — the same hands that charmed dogs and calmed cart-horses and had, two nights ago, signed *your brother knows the mountains, he'll guess the pass* in frost-breath against the cave wall while they talked strategy. Yara watched them arrive at it, watched the arithmetic land.

"Tammas," they said.

"You told me yourself," Yara said gently. "He's the crown's hunter. He posted the edict. He's been chasing us across half a realm."

"He reported me when we were children." Flat. Reciting. "He stood in our mother's kitchen and told the village warden what I could do, and I was sent away, and he stayed, and now he hunts people like us for a living." Orsen looked up, and their eyes were wet and furious. "And you're telling me the world ends — *stays* ended — unless that man stands in a circle and chooses us."

"I'm telling you," Vessith said, "that the covenant is crueler and wiser than any of us, and it has chosen him whether you have or not."

* * *

Later, when Orsen finally slept — curled against the dragon's foreleg like a child against a hill, and Vessith's tail had curved around them so naturally neither of them seemed to notice it had happened — Yara sat at the cave mouth with the salt jar in her lap and did the math she'd been avoiding.

Midwinter. Six weeks, maybe less, with the storms coming early. They needed Brother Ilex and his flooded chapel, wherever exactly that was. They needed a fourth oath-keeper beyond the salt, because the covenant-stone's song named four keepers and they had two and a half. They needed to survive the crown's hunt, which now had a face Yara had seen only in Orsen's nightmares and old family stories, and they needed that face — that man — to do the single thing his entire life had been built to avoid doing.

Find Tammas Kavanagh. Tell Tammas Kavanagh. Ask Tammas Kavanagh to commit treason against the crown he served and treason against his own conscience in the same breath, in front of witnesses, before midwinter, or the last dragon in the world dies alone in a cave that breathes.

"Impossible," she said to the jar.

The ember pulsed. She chose to believe it agreed.

Behind her, deep in the dark, Vessith's voice reached her one last time through the stone, so low she felt it more than heard it.

"Salt-witch. You bound yourself to this path when you stole that ember. Do you regret it yet?"

Yara looked at Orsen, asleep against a creature out of the oldest stories, face slack and young, the gift finally out in the open where it belonged. She looked at the ember in the salt, small and stubborn and refusing, after all these years, to go out.

"Not yet," she said. "Ask me at midwinter."

And far to the north, past the flats and the burned orchard and the drowned chapel road, a hunter woke in a cold tent, pulled on his boots, and gave the order to ride for the mountains.
