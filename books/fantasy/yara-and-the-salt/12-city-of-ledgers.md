## 12. City of Ledgers

The capital did not look like a place that had broken a covenant. It looked like a place that had never needed one.

Orsen saw it first from the crest of the Ossuary Road, where the pilgrim track they'd followed for nine days crested a ridge of frozen clay and the whole city unfolded below: white walls, slate roofs furred with frost, and above it all the Lantern Spire of the Royal Archive, its beacon burning green — archive-green, the color of records and rules and every name ever written down by men who believed writing a thing down made it true.

"You're doing the face," Yara said.

"What face."

"The one where you're about to say something bitter and then decide to say nothing instead." She adjusted the pack on her shoulders. Salt crystals clicked inside it like slow applause. "Say it anyway. It's cheaper than keeping it."

"Fine." Orsen nodded at the spire. "That building is going to have my family's shame in it. Filed somewhere between tax rolls and grain tariffs, probably. And I'm walking in through the front door."

"That's not bitterness, that's just accurate." Yara started down the road without waiting. She walked like someone who had decided long ago that hesitation was a luxury, and she dressed the part too — the salt-witch grays scrubbed out of her clothes, replaced with the dun wool of a midland herb-wife, her jar of dying dragon-fire wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the pack where no inspector would think to look. "Besides. It won't be filed between anything. A Kavanagh confession from the old betrayal? The archivists will have kept that one close. Shame is valuable paper."

Orsen had stopped arguing with that particular logic somewhere around the ash orchard, where Maren had shown them trees that grew only from a dragon's grief and told them, in a voice like wind through dead branches, *the covenant has three voices left in the world and you are carrying two of them*. Since then Orsen had simply been doing what was next. Walk north. Cross the flats. Survive the ambush — no, don't think of the orchard burning, not yet, not while there was still work that required steady hands.

Find the names. That was what mattered. Brother Ilex had given them the words of the covenant but not all the speakers; the final oath required seven voices, and four of the seven belonged to families scattered so thoroughly by the crown's old purges that only the Archive itself remembered where their bloodlines had landed. Ledger-keepers wrote everything down. Even the things they swore to forget.

* * *

Getting inside was insultingly easy, which was the point. Yara had explained it on the road: nobody guards the door everyone believes is beneath notice. The Archive ran a public reading room for clerks, surveyors, and anyone with legitimate trade who needed to consult land grants. You needed only a sponsor's seal, clean hands, and the patience to look bored.

They had none of those things, strictly speaking. They had a seal Yara had pressed in salt-ice off a merchant's dropped writ outside a coaching inn — it would hold for exactly as long as no one looked twice — and Orsen had clean hands because Orsen had spent twenty years learning how to be unremarkable in every particular.

The disguise was the cruelest part. Greyharbor charm-sellers wore color; that was the trade. So Orsen went as something else entirely: a junior clerk of the Surveyor's Office, gray coat, ink-stained cuffs applied that morning with a careful thumb, spectacles of plain glass that turned their eyes into something small and squinting and harmless. Hair bound back. Shoulders drawn in. Twenty years of practice at being furniture, put to use at last.

At the reading room desk, a clerk with a nose like a hatchet examined the seal, examined Orsen, and found both adequately dull.

"Section?"

"Covenant-era land compensations," Orsen said, in the flat voice of a person reciting a task assigned by someone else. "Pre-Dissolution grants. My supervisor wants the dispositions cross-referenced before the solstice recess."

Hatchet-Nose grunted, stamped a slip, and pointed down a corridor that smelled of cold stone and lamp oil. "Third gallery. Touch nothing with red tags. Red tags are sealed by warrant of the Crown."

"Of course," Orsen said, and did not look at Yara, who had peeled away ten minutes earlier toward the map room with her herb-wife basket and her harmless face.

The third gallery of the Royal Archive was a long throat of shelves, lit by green-shaded lamps that made every document look faintly drowned. There were perhaps four other people in it, all older than winter, all asleep in the way of career clerks — present, breathing, seeing nothing.

Orsen found the index within the hour.

It was worse than expected, and Orsen had expected a great deal. The covenant purges had not been hidden; they had been *administered*. Volumes upon volumes, each spine labeled in tidy capitals: **OATH-BREAKERS AND DISSOLUTION OF THE DRAGON ACCORDS — PROCEEDINGS, SUBSCRIPTIONS, DISPOSITIONS.** Not a shameful secret locked in a vault. A filing category. A job some man had done, and gone home from, and slept after.

Orsen pulled the volume marked for the northern provinces and carried it to a reading table at the far end of the gallery, where the lamp was weakest and the sightlines were worst, and opened it.

And there was the name. Their own name, in an ancestor's hand — or rather, in an archivist's hand copying an ancestor's confession, and that distinction mattered, Orsen realized, because the original voice underneath was still audible somehow, bleeding up through the transcription like a stain through fresh paint.

**Confession of Edric Kavanagh, wainwright of Harrow Fen, taken the 9th of Deepmonth.**

*States that his grandfather kept the speaking, and his father, and that he himself has heard his daughter's child talk to the yard geese in a manner unnatural. States he wishes it rooted out of his line for the good of his soul and the safety of the realm. Names given under his own hand.*

Below, in a different ink, the disposition: the child questioned. The child found gifted. The child remanded to the crown's custody for correction.

The child's name was Tammas.

Orsen sat very still for a long time. A lamp guttered somewhere down the gallery and no one relit it. Somewhere overhead, in the rafters where the warm air rose, a mouse moved along a beam, and Orsen heard it the way they heard everything now — clearly, without flinching — a small dry mind full of crumbs and fear of owls. *Nothing here,* Orsen told it, without words, without sound. *Nothing here but paper.* The mouse's heartbeat settled, and it moved on, and Orsen envied it with a completeness that bordered on worship.

Because here was the shape of it, finally, in the archivist's merciless hand. It had not begun with Tammas. It had begun generations back, with a wainwright who was afraid, whose fear had been recorded and rewarded, and the reward had curdled down the family like rust down a nail until it reached a boy named Tammas Kavanagh who had done exactly what his family had taught him to do for three generations: he had heard something unnatural in a child, and he had reported it, and he had been told he was good.

*He didn't choose the law over blood,* Orsen thought, and the thought arrived not as forgiveness but as something colder and more durable — as architecture. *He never once in his life got to see that they were different things.*

Their whole childhood reorganized itself around this fact. Tammas teaching them cards. Tammas standing between them and their grandfather's temper. Tammas at fourteen, watching Orsen talk a lame cart-horse back onto its feet in the yard, and going pale, and going quiet, and going to the magistrate — because that was the only door his upbringing had built, the only action his inheritance contained. He had not betrayed them out of hate. He had turned them in the way you'd douse a fire in a barn. He'd probably wept. He'd certainly been praised.

Somehow that was worse. Somehow that was also, unbearably, something Orsen could hold.

"I'm sorry," Orsen said to the volume, barely aloud, to a dead wainwright who could not hear. "Not for me. For you. Someone should have asked why you were so frightened."

A page whispered behind them.

Orsen closed the book too late. Habit died hard; twenty years of hiding did not go quietly. But when they turned, it was only Yara, standing at the end of the row with her basket on her arm and her face carefully arranged into clerk-boredom, which on her fit like a borrowed glove.

"The maps room has a copy of the oath-keeper dispositions," she murmured, sliding into the seat across the table, opening her own volume as cover. "Cross-referenced against parish rolls. I have three names already. Surnames Ashling, Corve, and — " she paused, and her eyes came up over the edge of the page, " — and one more thing. Gallery's about to change shift. There's a man doing the rounds who isn't a clerk."

Orsen's pulse performed a small cold maneuver. "Hunter?"

"Worse. Uniformed, but wrong for a hunter — hunters dress like debt collectors. This one dresses like a man who owns his boots." She turned a page she hadn't read. "Tall. Fair hair going gray at the temple. He checked the red-tag shelf for eleven seconds longer than a clerk would."

The gallery's other occupants began, slowly, to gather their things. Shift change. Lamps were being snuffed one by one down the long throat of the room, and the dark came walking toward them shelf by shelf, and with it, footsteps — unhurried, even, the footsteps of a man who had walked every corridor in this building a thousand times and knew the sound of every other set of feet in it.

Orsen knew those footsteps too. That was the obscenity of it. Twenty years, and some animal ledger inside them still matched that gait instantly, helplessly, to a boy coming up the stairs to share supper.

"He can't see my face," Orsen breathed.

"No," Yara agreed. "So don't give him your face." She stood, tucked her basket close, and became in one motion a tired herb-wife with an errand. When she spoke again it was barely air. "Reading room, west stair, before the doors lock at dusk bell. I'll have the names copied by then. Don't read anything else. Don't *talk* to anything else — I know you, and there are ravens in that roof, and you at a moment like this will want to ask them things."

Then she was gone down the aisle, and Orsen was alone with the drowned lamp and the open volume, and the footsteps reached the head of the gallery.

Orsen bent over the book. Spectacles down the nose. Gray coat, stained cuffs, the posture of furniture. Let the man walk the length of the room and find nothing but a junior clerk drowning in pre-Dissolution land grants, which was punishment enough for anyone.

The footsteps came. Slowed. Stopped.

Orsen did not look up. Looked up exactly as much as a bored clerk would — a flick, an assessment, back to the page — and caught him in that flick: taller than memory had made him, broader through the shoulders, the fair hair cropped short now, a crown badge at the collar that had not existed when they were children. Tammas Kavanagh, Hunter of the Crown, standing eight feet away in a green-lit gallery, looking at the shelf.

Looking, Orsen realized with a slow vertical dropping sensation, at the exact volume Orsen had returned to the wrong slot. Two spaces off. A clerk's error. Nothing.

Tammas reached out and slid the volume back into its place. His gloved fingers rested on the spine a moment.

"Ashling," he said, to the shelf, in a voice pitched for no one — and Orsen's heart stopped and restarted wrong. "Corve. Drennmere. If it's the oath-keepers you're after, clerk, the parish rolls lie. The dispositions don't."

Silence. Down the gallery, a door closed. The last lamp but theirs went out.

Tammas turned. For one instant his gaze crossed Orsen's bowed head, the spectacles, the gray coat — and Orsen watched, from behind plain glass, something move through their brother's face. Not recognition. Recognition would have meant arrest, alarm, the crash of a world. This was smaller and stranger: a flicker like a man passing a stranger who smells, for half a breath, like home.

He walked on. His footsteps faded down the gallery, even and unhurried, and turned at the far end, and were gone.

Orsen sat without moving until the dusk bell rang across the city, flat and bronze through high windows. Then they stood, legs unreliable, and walked to the west stair, and did not run, because running was a thing people noticed.

Yara was waiting at the door with the copied names folded small in her glove, and she read Orsen's face in one glance, the way she always did, and her jaw tightened.

"He saw you."

"He saw a clerk." Orsen pushed through into the blue evening, into the cold and the crowd and the smell of coal smoke, and walked, fast, saying it out loud at last because if they didn't say it they would carry it like a stone. "But he gave us the third name anyway, Yara. Drennmere. He knew why I was here, or guessed, and he said it out loud to an empty shelf, knowing I'd hear. He falsified nothing, signed nothing, risked nothing *provable* — and gave us the name."

Yara was quiet for half a street. Snow had begun to fall, fine as sifted flour, catching in her hair.

"That's not a gift," she said finally. "That's a man splitting himself in half and hoping neither piece bleeds out. And one of those pieces is sworn to hunt you."

"I know." Orsen pulled their collar up. Ahead, the city gates glowed with lantern-light, and beyond them the north road waited with its three remaining names and its storms and its hunters. "I know what he is. But Yara — I read what he came from. Three generations of Kavanaghs feeding their own children to the crown because they were taught that love looks like reporting the fire before it spreads." They exhaled, and the breath hung white between them. "I'm not forgiven. I'm not forgiving, either. Not yet. But I understand him now, and I'm telling you, that frightens me more."

"Why?" Yara asked.

"Because a man that split," Orsen said, "is a man who can break either direction. And we've just shown him our face without showing him our face, and midwinter is nine days out, and if he decides the clerk in the third gallery was his brother —"

Behind them, back inside the white walls of the Archive, a bell began to ring. Not the dusk bell. Something faster, thinner, repeated — the sound an institution makes when it has discovered an error in its ledgers.

Yara started to run. Orsen ran with her, and above the capital's rooftops the snow thickened, and somewhere behind them, in a green-lit gallery, Tammas Kavanagh stood before an empty shelf, holding a slip of paper with a clerk's forged seal pressed into its wax, deciding which half of himself he was.
