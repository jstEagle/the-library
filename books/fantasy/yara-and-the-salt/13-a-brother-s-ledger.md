## 13. A Brother's Ledger

The report sat on Tammas Kavanagh's desk for three days before he touched it.

It was not a long report. That was the cruelty of it — how little paper it took to end a life. *Charm seller, Greyharbor market, third stall from the Tide Gate. Suspected beast-speech. Witness statement attached. Recommend audit and detention.* A clerk's neat hand, a hunter's seal, and beneath it all the name of the accused, written in ink that had not yet dried when Tammas first read it.

*O. Kavanagh.*

He had told himself, in the three days since, that it was a common enough name. Half of Greyharbor was Kavanaghs by blood or by marriage. He had told himself that the initial could stand for anything. Orla. Osric. Old Man Kavanagh who sold rope down by the fishmongers' row and had died ten years ago anyway.

He had told himself these things the way a man tells himself the tide won't come in.

Because he had known. He had known since the edict went up at the Tide Gate, since he'd stood in the crowd in his crown-black coat and watched the market empty of warmth, watching faces go careful and shut. He had known because his orders that day had included a list of licensed charm-sellers, and one name on that list had made his stomach drop through the floorboards of the world.

*Orsen Kavanagh.*

Twenty years. Twenty years since he had stood in their parents' kitchen with his hand shaking around a candlestick while Orsen talked to the half-dead gull on the windowsill, cooing to it in that soft wrong language, and Orsen had looked up at him with such trust — such stupid, unguarded trust — and said, *You won't tell, will you? You promise?*

And Tammas had promised.

And then their father had found out anyway, months later, and there had been shouting, and their mother crying into her apron, and the whole house bending under the weight of a secret too heavy to hold. And when the local magistrate came asking questions — gently, at first, the way they did back then, before the audits and the pyres — Tammas had been seventeen and terrified and so desperate to be good, to be clean, to be anything other than the brother of a witch, that he had answered honestly.

*Yes, sir. I've seen them speak to animals. Yes, sir. It's true.*

He had told himself it was for Orsen's own good. That surrender was safer than discovery. That the old ways were lenient — a fine, a marking, a watch placed on the family. And maybe that had even been true, back then. Orsen had run instead, vanished into Greyharbor's sprawl, and the fine had fallen on their parents, and the mark had fallen on Tammas, and the years had done what years do: stretched the wound thin over everything, until he could almost pretend it wasn't there.

Almost. Until the edict.

* * *

"You're staring at that report like it insulted your mother," Sergeant Vell said from the doorway.

Tammas closed the folder. Too fast. Vell's eyebrows went up a fraction, which for Vell was a shout.

"Greyharbor charm-seller," Tammas said. "Beast-speech allegation. Single witness."

"The confession?" Vell came in, dropped into the chair across the desk without being asked. He was a broad, comfortable man with the kind of face that made villagers trust him right up until the moment he put the irons on. "Good one, that. Woman came in weeping, gave up the whole thing. Name, stall, the lot. Says she bought a ward off the gifted one and then saw them talking to a stray dog like it was answering."

"Like it was answering," Tammas repeated.

"That's the phrase." Vell leaned back, laced his fingers over his belly. "You know the stall?"

"I know Greyharbor."

"You know *the stall*, though." Vell's voice stayed pleasant. His eyes did not. "Third from the Tide Gate. You grew up what, two streets over? Kavanagh's a harbor name if ever there was one."

"My family's from Greyharbor. That's hardly evidence."

"No," Vell agreed. "It's hardly evidence. It's a coincidence, is what it is. Like how you volunteered for the Greyharbor posting yourself, and how you've walked past that stall four times this week and never once gone in, and how you've got that look on your face like a man doing sums he doesn't want the answer to."

Tammas kept his hands flat on the desk. Steady. He had learned steadiness the way other men learned prayer — daily, desperately, and never quite believing it would save him.

"What would you have me do, Sergeant? Walk in and arrest my—"

He stopped. The word hung there. *Cousin,* he'd meant to say. The lie was already halfway out and he'd fumbled it, and Vell watched him fumble it with the mild patience of a man watching a drunk miss a doorframe.

"Your cousin," Vell finished for him. "Aye. Your cousin."

"It's not against procedure to recuse oneself from a case involving kin."

"It's not against procedure," Vell said, "for me to note in the ledger that you didn't."

* * *

That night Tammas walked the market district alone, out of uniform, hood up against the drizzle. He told himself it was surveillance. He was good at telling himself things; it was the only gift the gods had given him, and he'd used it profligately for twenty years.

The stall was shuttered. Had been shuttered for two days now, according to the watchers' reports — reports he had signed off on, routing them to himself, burying them in the middle of the week's stack where no one would look twice. The canvas was tied down against the wind. Someone had left a wilting bunch of dock-leaf greens against the post, an offering or an apology, he couldn't tell which. Greyharbor folk were like that. They'd inform on you to the crown and leave flowers at your stall the same afternoon.

He stood across the lane in the dark and let himself remember, because he was tired of not remembering, and tiredness makes honest men of us all eventually.

Orsen at nine, crouched in the alley behind the tannery, surrounded by cats that should have scattered and didn't, sitting in a ring around them like small furry congregants while Orsen preached something in purrs and clicks. Tammas, twelve, standing at the mouth of the alley keeping watch, feeling — this was the part he never let himself feel, the part under all the others — feeling *proud*. Feeling like his sibling was something marvelous and he was the keeper of the marvel.

Their father finding out. The gull. The kitchen. The way Orsen had looked at him after the magistrate's visit — not hateful. That was the worst of it. Not hateful. Just *done*, the way a door is done, closing on a room no one will use again.

*You won't tell.*

*I won't tell.*

He had told. And every year since, the law had grown teeth, and every year his single honest answer had grown heavier, until now the law wanted fire and his one sentence wanted blood, and here he stood in the rain outside a shuttered stall doing arithmetic no ledger could balance.

*If I turn them in, I'm the brother who burned their own kin twice.*

*If I don't, I'm a crown hunter who falsified a report in the middle of a magic hunt, and if it surfaces — and it always surfaces — they'll want to know why. And then they'll pull the thread, and the thread leads to Orsen anyway, and I'll have damned us both.*

There was a third option, the one he kept not looking at directly: find Orsen first. Warn them. Put them on a northbound cart with coin in their pocket and be done with it — let the crown hunt a ghost. But the edict had teeth now, and teeth meant checkpoints, and checkpoints meant descriptions, and Orsen had their mother's face, which Tammas saw every time he shaved.

He turned back toward the garrison, and that was when he saw the dog.

It was the same dog — he was nearly sure of it. The grey-muzzled stray from the witness statement, sitting in the shadow of the shuttered stall as if it had an appointment. It watched him approach with an attention no stray should waste on a stranger.

Tammas stopped. Felt foolish. Spoke anyway, quietly, to the empty lane.

"If you can understand me — and I'm aware of how this sounds — tell them to run. Tell them to go north and keep going. Do you understand? Go on, then. Shoo. Carry the message."

The dog stood. Stretched elaborately, the way dogs do when they intend to ignore you. Then it trotted off down the lane toward the water, pausing once to look back at him with an expression he chose, against all sense, to read as *message received.*

He walked back to the garrison feeling lighter and more damned than he had in twenty years.

* * *

In the morning he falsified the report.

It was almost disappointingly easy. He struck the surname — clerical error, transcription mistake, the witness had given a first name only and some eager clerk had matched it against the license rolls. He wrote *identity unconfirmed; subject may have fled Greyharbor prior to edict; recommend reduced priority pending further intelligence.* He signed it, sealed it, and slid it into the dispatch pile with hands that did not shake, because he had spent twenty years practicing steadiness and this, at last, was what it was for.

Vell read it over his shoulder at breakfast and said nothing for a long time.

"Unconfirmed," Vell said finally.

"Clerical error."

"Mm." Vell buttered his toast with terrible calm. "You know what happens to the man who files that report, if it comes out."

"I know what happens to the woman in the cell, if it comes out."

"And which are you, Tammas? The man or the woman?"

Tammas looked at him. Vell was many things — lazy, fond of his dinners, casually cruel in the way of comfortable men — but he was not stupid, and he was not, Tammas realized with a lurch, entirely his friend.

"I'm the officer who corrected a clerical error," Tammas said.

"Aye." Vell stood, gathering his bowl. "Well. For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope your cousin runs far and runs fast." He paused at the door. "But I'll be honest with you, Kavanagh, because you've always been straight with me. There's riders come in overnight. Crown's not satisfied with strays and stall-keepers anymore. They're saying there's talk of dragons. Talk of a salt-witch seen on the north road with an unknown companion. And they're saying whoever finds them writes their own ticket." He smiled without warmth. "So your clerical error had better stay buried. Because if the crown finds them first — and finds out you knew — it won't be a cell for you. It'll be the pyre beside them."

He left. Tammas sat with his cold tea and understood, with perfect clarity, what he had done.

Not saved anyone. Not yet. Maybe not ever. He had simply chosen — clumsily, treasonously, twenty years too late — which side of the ledger his soul would be entered on. The ink was wet. The debt was real. And somewhere north, his sibling was running with a salt-witch and a dying flame, toward a thing the crown was already hunting, and Tammas Kavanagh had just made himself the only wall between them and the fire.

He pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him. Dipped his pen.

To the crown's archive, a request: *all records pertaining to the covenant, the oath-keepers, and the Kavanagh family line.* If he was damned, he would at least be damned informed. He would learn what his family had broken, generations back. He would learn what his brother — what Orsen — was walking toward.

And when the order finally came down — and it would come, Vell had made that plain — the man who carried it would decide, in that moment, whose side the law was on.

Outside, the rain cleared. Far to the north, over the mountains he could not see, something old was waking or dying, and Tammas set down his pen, and prayed — for the first time in twenty years — to a god he had no right to address:

*Let me reach them before the fire does.*
