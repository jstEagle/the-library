## 14. The Drowned Chapel

The sea took the chapel a hundred years ago and never gave it back.

Yara felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the water's taste, salt braided with something older, something that had been saying the same words for so long they had soaked into the stone. She stood at the tideline where the marsh gave out into grey shallows, and Orsen came up beside her with their hood pulled against the wind, and neither of them spoke for a moment, because some places ask for silence the way some people do.

"There," Orsen said.

The bell tower rose out of the water like a drowned man's hand — just the top third of it, the belfry arch open to the sky, no bell in it anymore. Below the waterline Yara could make out the pale suggestion of rooftops, the long spine of a nave lying just under the surface, green with weed. The tide was out. It would come back. Everything here moved to the tide's schedule, including, she suspected, the monk.

"He lives *under* it?" Orsen said.

"He keeps vigil," Yara said. "There's a difference."

She knew about Ilex before she knew his name. Every salt-witch knew about the drowned chapel, even exiled ones — especially exiled ones, because the covens used it as a cautionary tale. *This is what devotion costs,* the elders said. *This is what happens when you love something more than you love your own skin.* A monk who walked into the sea twice a day when the tide went down and walked back out again before it returned, carrying nothing, taking nothing, year after year after year, until the story wore smooth and nobody remembered what he was keeping vigil *for*.

Yara remembered now. She wished she didn't, because remembering meant admitting what the covenant had cost people, and she had spent six years trying not to count.

The covenant-stone shard rode warm against her breastbone, wrapped in oilcloth under her shirt. Maren's shard. Maren, whose orchard had burned three days ago because of them, because Yara had torn the salt off an ambush on the flats and left a signature in the sand wide as a shout. Maren had pressed the shard into her hands through the smoke and said, *Go north, girl, and stop apologizing.* Yara had not stopped apologizing. She had just started doing it silently.

"Low tide lasts maybe two hours," she said. "We go now or we wait for tomorrow's."

Orsen looked at the water, then at her. In the week since the flats, since the fire on the horizon that neither of them had talked about properly, something had shifted between them — not softness exactly, but weight-bearing. Trust loaded like a beam across two shoulders. Orsen's beasts had gotten them through the marsh; Yara's salt would get them across the shallows.

"How deep?"

"Chest-high at the lowest. The floor of the nave might be dry-ish, if the stories are right." She was already pulling off her boots. "The stories say there's a door under the waterline. Say he opens it from inside."

"And if the stories are wrong?"

Yara smiled, and hated that it felt good to smile. "Then we drown in a church, and someone will write a very confused ballad about us."

* * *

The water was colder than it had any right to be in autumn. It found every seam of her, climbed her ribs, made her breath go shallow and high in her chest. Yara kept one hand skimming the surface, reading it the way she read everything — by taste, by pressure, by the small electrical arguments between salt and fresh. The chapel's flooded interior had its own weather. The water in there was *old*. It had been standing still for generations, holding its shape around the walls like a cupped palm, and it did not want to be disturbed.

*Easy,* she told it, in the wordless grammar of her craft. *Easy. We're expected.*

Whether the water believed her or simply got bored, it let them through.

The door was real. Oak gone black as bog-iron, ringed in coral, with a latch worn smooth by one hand touching it ten thousand times. Yara hauled on it and it opened inward with no resistance at all — the hinge oiled, recently, impossibly, forty feet under the sea — and they spilled into air.

Not dry air. Damp, cold, tasting of candle smoke and brine and, underneath it all, faintly, of incense that had burned so long it had become part of the architecture. But air. The nave stood open above them like the rib cage of a whale, vaulted stone rising out of black water, and along the edges of the flood someone had laid a path: flat stones, mortared, raised barely above the surface, winding between drowned pews toward the altar end where a light burned.

A man sat on the top step of the altar dais, which was the only dry ground in the whole church. He was thin the way driftwood is thin — weathered down to the essential grain. His robe had once been white. His hair had once been brown. He held a book in his lap, an old one, swollen with damp but lovingly handled, and he did not look up until they were three stones from the dais.

"You're late," he said. "By about sixty years."

"You knew we'd come?" Orsen said.

"I hoped." He closed the book with care, both hands, the way you close something you're afraid of breaking. "Hoping is most of a vigil. Sit, if you can find a dry inch. I'm Brother Ilex. You'll be the salt-witch, and you—" his eyes found Orsen's face in the candlelight and stayed there a moment too long— "you'll be the voice."

Yara's hand went to the shard under her shirt without her willing it. "Who told you about us?"

"No one." Ilex smiled, and it was a gentle expression worn soft by disuse. "The gulls did. They've been talking about a beast-speaker walking north out of Greyharbor for a month. Gossip travels faster than riders, if you have the ears for it. I don't. But the gulls perch on my belfry and I've learned to read their tempers." He turned his palms up, an open gesture. "Maren sent word too, before the burning. A moth, carrying a scrap of prayer-paper. Old trick of hers. She always did trust insects over people."

"Maren's alive," Yara said. It wasn't a question, but it wanted confirming, needed it, the way a wound needs pressure.

"Alive, furious, and replanting. Ash is good for orchards. She'd tell you so herself, at length." Something eased in Yara's chest that she hadn't known was clenched. Ilex watched her the way a man watches a rope he's tied his own life to. "You broke your oath on the flats to save her work. She knows. She wants you to know the coven will call it exile-breaking, and she wants you to know she doesn't care. Salt-magic was never meant to be pretty. It was meant to be *kept*, and you kept it."

"That's not what it felt like."

"It rarely does." He rose — slowly, joints announcing themselves — and gestured at the flooded nave, the drowned pews, the patient black water pressing against every window. "You want to know why I live in the sea. Everyone does, eventually. So. Sit. This is the part of the story where sacrifice gets named plainly, and I've found it goes easier sitting down."

* * *

They sat on the dais steps. Ilex set a battered kettle on a little iron stove — dry fuel, hoarded, precious — and made tea from something that tasted mostly of kelp and forgiveness. The candles guttered and steadied. Outside, muffled by forty feet of sea, the tide had begun its slow return; Yara could feel it building against the chapel walls like a held breath, hours away still, but coming.

"The covenant," Ilex began, "was spoken. That's the thing everyone forgets, because forgetting is the whole disease. It wasn't carved. It wasn't written first. Dragons don't read, and men didn't write yet — it was *said*. Voice to voice. Flame to breath. The words existed in mouths, and mouths die, so the words had to be carried." He tapped the swollen book in his lap. "My order carried them. We called ourselves the Rememberers, which embarrassed the dragons considerably — they thought it was sentimental. But that was the bargain, you understand. Fire for memory. They lent their flame to our hearths, and we promised, in plain words, out loud, to remember them. Not to honor them. Not to worship them. To *remember*. To keep their names in living speech, generation after generation, the way you keep a banked coal alive by breathing on it."

"And when the order died out," Orsen said quietly.

"When the order was hunted out," Ilex corrected. "Different verb, worse ending. Two hundred years ago the crowns decided that spoken magic was the worst kind — you can't confiscate a voice, only silence it. My brothers and sisters were burned for carrying the words. The last prior hid this book in the chapel and drowned himself rather than give up its location, which I have always considered an excessive interpretation of duty." He said it dryly, but his thumb stroked the cover once, and Yara saw the grief under it, ancient and unfixed. "I am the last. I came here thirty years ago because the words needed a mouth, and mine was available, and I thought vigilance would be enough."

"And it wasn't."

"It bought time. Time is not nothing." He looked at Orsen, and his voice changed — dropped its teaching cadence, became direct, almost blunt. "But hear me now, beast-speaker, because this is the reason you crossed the marsh, and I won't have you hear it softened. Vessith's ember can reforge the covenant. The stone says so; Maren's shard confirmed it; the words exist and I carry them. But the old words require a speaker who is *bound* — bound by blood to both sides of the broken promise. The Kavanaghs helped break it. Their ancestor's signature is in this book, in ink that predates the crown, next to the phrase *let our line be answerable*. Which means the final speaking cannot be done by me, though I will be there to lead it. It must be done by a Kavanagh. And it must be done aloud, at full power, unshielded, with a dragon's flame moving through the words as they're spoken."

The kettle ticked. Water lapped somewhere in the dark of the nave.

"I don't understand the danger," Yara said, though a cold part of her already did.

"Don't you?" Ilex's kindness was terrible, the way mercy is terrible — total, unhurried. "A dragon's flame is a century of dying compressed into a gift. When it passes through human speech, it burns the channel clean. Sometimes that means a scorched throat. Sometimes it means a life shortened by years. And sometimes—" he spread his hands— "sometimes the channel doesn't survive the passing. The record is honest about this, because my order believed honesty was also a kind of remembering. Three of the four speakers who renewed the covenant in past ages died within the hour. The fourth lived to ninety and complained about her joints. There is no way to know in advance which speaker you are getting. There never has been."

Silence. Yara looked at Orsen and saw her own fear reflected and refused — she watched Orsen refuse it, watched the jaw set, watched the eyes go somewhere flat and decided, and understood with a lurch that Orsen had already done this arithmetic, alone, somewhere on the road, and had not shared the sum with her.

"No," Yara said.

"It's not yours to refuse," Ilex said gently.

"It's not theirs to *offer* either—" She was on her feet without remembering standing. "Six years I've been exiled for less magic than fits in a thimble. Six years, for a ward worked wrong one time, and the whole time the covens lectured me about *cost*, about proportion, about how a witch must weigh what she spends. And now you sit there in your drowned church and tell me the price of saving every dragon alive is possibly, probably, *maybe* the person I—" The sentence hit a wall she hadn't known was there. She stood in front of it. "No. Find another way. Make Tammas Kavanagh speak it, he's got the blood too, let the hunter burn for once—"

"Blood alone isn't enough," Ilex said. "It needs a voice the beasts will answer. It needs the covenant's heir. You know this. The stone knows this. You've felt it choosing him since the ash orchard." He waited until she met his eyes. "And you know, better than anyone in this room, that magic never asks permission about cost. It only asks whether you're willing."

Yara sat back down. It was the hardest thing she'd done since the flats.

Orsen spoke for the first time in minutes, and their voice was very quiet and very steady. "Ask me the question properly. If you're going to name the sacrifice plainly, then name it plainly. Don't dress it as destiny."

Ilex regarded Orsen for a long moment, and then inclined his head, monk to stranger, as if to a fellow professional.

"Orsen Kavanagh," he said. "Will you carry the words of the covenant to midwinter, knowing that speaking them may kill you? Knowing that no one — not I, not the stone, not the dragons themselves — can promise otherwise?"

The candle flames leaned, all of them, toward Orsen, as if listening.

"Yes," Orsen said. No hesitation, and Yara realized the hesitation had happened days ago, miles ago, in the dark of some camp, and that she had slept through it beside them. "Yes. On two conditions. First — you teach me the words until I could say them drunk, concussed, or terrified. All the weeks we have. Second—" and here Orsen's voice cracked, just once, and repaired itself— "when the time comes, you let her stand where she chooses. You don't put her behind a wall for her own good. I've been behind walls my whole life. They don't protect anybody. They just delay the grieving."

Ilex looked at Yara. Yara looked back, and could not speak, and nodded, and hated nodding, and would not have unsaid it for anything.

"Agreed," Ilex said softly. Then he opened the book, and the smell of centuries came off the pages — smoke and tallow and dragon-warmth, faint as a memory of summer. "Then we begin tonight. Tide comes in at moonrise; we'll have six hours sealed in with the words, and I warn you, the pronunciation is brutal on human teeth."

* * *

Later — much later, while Orsen bent over the book by candlelight, mouthing syllables that made the flooded nave hum like a struck bowl — Yara climbed the spiral stair to the belfry, because she needed air that hadn't been breathed by a martyr.

The sea had come back while they worked. It lay black and enormous under a half-made moon, and far off to the south, where no lights should be, she saw torches. Many. Moving along the coast road in a column, unhurried, the way hunters move when they are certain.

She counted, out of bad habit. Forty, at least. More than a raid. A procession.

Behind her, on the stair, Ilex's voice: "Riders?"

"Hunters. A day out. Maybe less."

He came up beside her, unsurprised, unsurprising. "Then we leave at low tide and take the pilgrim path through the fen. There's a causeway the horses hate." He paused. "You should know — I sent word ahead, when the gulls told me you were coming. To the third keeper. She'll meet you at midwinter, or not at all, depending on what the torches do between here and there."

"You *what*?"

"I hoped," Ilex said again, mildly, "and hoping is most of a vigil. The rest is making sure hope has somewhere to land." He looked south at the torches, and his old face in the moonlight was calm as the drowned stone below them. "They'll burn the chapel, you know. When they find it empty. They always burn what they can't hold. Let them. The words aren't in the walls anymore."

Below, from the dark of the nave, Orsen's voice rose — halting, careful, wrong in a hundred places and unmistakable in one — speaking the first sentence of a promise older than kingdoms, and out over the black water, every gull on the drowned coast lifted at once into the moonlight, crying, answering, calling it back.
