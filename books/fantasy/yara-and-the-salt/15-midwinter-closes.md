## 15. Midwinter Closes

The storm came down on us like something with intent.

One hour we were climbing the shepherd's track through the Grey Spine, Ilex grumbling about his soaked robes, Maren leaning on her covenant-stone staff and refusing all offers of help. The next hour the sky turned the color of wet slate and simply *let go* — snow so thick it erased the mountain, the path, the difference between up and down.

"We make the pass before dark," Yara shouted over the wind, "or we don't make it at all."

We didn't make the pass.

I knew it before anyone said it. The ravens had told me that morning — *white road closing, white road closing, turn back, soft-things* — but the pass was the only way north to Vessith's range, and behind us the hunters' fires were visible at night like a string of orange teeth. There was no turning back. There was only forward, into weather that did not care about crowns or covenants.

We found shelter by accident, which is how most true things find you. A lean-to of stone and old timber, half-swallowed by the slope — a drover's rest, abandoned a generation ago, still holding a ghost of peat-smoke in its walls. Yara got a fire going with salt-work I'd stopped flinching at weeks ago. Ilex prayed quietly in a corner, water dripping off his hem. Maren sat with her back to the stone and slept the instant, unashamed sleep of the very old.

I stood at the mouth of the lean-to and listened to the mountain.

This was the part no one warned me about, when my gift stopped being a thing I carried and became a thing that carried me. Twenty years of clamping my jaw shut against the voices of beasts, and now the world poured into me every waking hour — the marmots' alarm under the snow, a fox's patient hunger on the ridgeline, the slow mineral indifference of goats. It was like being deaf for two decades and then hearing not just sound but *every* sound, all at once, forever.

"You're doing the face," Yara said behind me.

"What face?"

"The one where you're listening to something and deciding whether to tell us."

"The wind," I said. Then, because lying to her had stopped working somewhere around the salt flats: "And wolves."

She came to stand beside me. Snow whirled past the gap in the stones like a crowd pressing at a door. "How many?"

"A pack. Nine, ten. They've been following our scent since the treeline." I wrapped my arms around myself. "They're hungry, Yara. This storm has killed everything they usually eat. And they can smell four warm bodies in a hole in the rock like we smell the fire."

"Can you talk them around?"

"To what? We have no meat. We have barely bread."

"You could try honesty," she said. "It worked on me."

I looked at her. Her face was lit orange from the fire behind us, wind-burnt, beautiful in the way weathered things are beautiful, and she was asking me — casually, as if it were small — to do openly what I'd been beheaded for doing in whispers.

"They'd hear me," I said. "Not sense me, not guess. *Hear.* A human voice coming out of the dark, speaking wolf. If any hunter is within five miles of this mountain—"

"I know what it costs."

"It's different for you. Your magic is your craft. Mine has been a death sentence since before I could write my own name." My voice cracked on the last part, and I hated it. "If I call out into that dark, I'm not hiding anymore. Not ever again."

Yara was quiet a moment. Snow ticked against the stones.

"Orsen," she said gently, "the crown posted your name in three realms. You fled through a sewer. You rode north with a salt-witch, a monk, and an orchard-tender older than the edict. You are carrying a shard of covenant-stone toward the last dragon in the world." She touched my shoulder. "You have been not-hiding very badly for a month now."

I laughed despite myself — a short, startled sound the wind took away.

Behind us, Ilex spoke from his corner without opening his eyes. "In the chapel," he said, "we held that speech was the first sacrament. Before prayer. Before song. The old texts say the covenant was spoken, not written — that the world itself was talked into being." He opened his eyes, mild and terrible. "I have guarded words my whole life, child. I would very much like to hear someone use them."

Maren, asleep or pretending it, murmured: "Tell the wolves the truth. It's all any of us have left."

* * *

I went out into the storm at moonrise, when the clouds tore open long enough to show a hard white sliver of it.

The cold was total. It found every seam in my clothes and made itself at home. Ten steps from the lean-to the firelight vanished behind me and there was only snow, blue-black and endless, and somewhere in it, eyes.

I didn't make myself comfortable. I stood in the open, deliberately visible, the way you'd stand in a doorway of a house where you meant to have a difficult conversation. And I opened the door in my chest that I'd kept barred for twenty years, and I spoke — not with my throat first, but with the whole of me, the way beasts speak, posture and scent and meaning braided together, and then, after, with my throat too, aloud, into the night:

*I see you.*

The pack answered from three directions at once. They'd ringed the lean-to while we slept — of course they had, they were professionals at this and we were amateurs at surviving. Their voices rolled through the snow, layered: the big brindled lead female, steady and suspicious; a young male all nerves and appetite; an old scarred one at the rear who mostly wanted to sleep.

*Four soft-things,* the lead female said, circling. *Thin. Slow. Storm keeps them in a den with a fire. Why come out, speaker?*

"Because I'd rather meet you than bleed," I said aloud, teeth chattering. "Because you're starving, and I know it, and I won't pretend otherwise."

A ripple through the pack. Hunger-talk, sharp and bright as ice cracking.

*Meat,* said the young one, and came a pace closer, hackles up, and his mother snapped at him — *not yet, fool, listen* — and the strange thing, the thing that undid me a little, was how ordinary it sounded. A family arguing in the dark. My family had argued worse, and with swords.

"There's no meat here," I told them. "But I'll give you the truth instead, and you can decide if it fills you. South of this mountain, men are hunting my kind. Men who burn whatever speaks as I speak. In time they will come to these slopes with dogs and fire, and they will not stop at your elk, your hares, your dens. Crowns are greedy things. They take until nothing is left."

*Men,* the old wolf said, and there was a weight of years in it. *Men burned the low forest when I was young. True talk.*

"If you guard this pass tonight," I said, "and if you and yours keep the high country when we're gone, then when the world turns — and it is turning, I swear it by fire and stone — those who remember will remember you. Your names will be known. Your part in it will be told." I swallowed, and gave her the only coin I truly carried. "You will not be forgotten."

Silence, except the wind.

Then the lead female came out of the snow fully into the moonglow, close enough that I could see her breath and the frost in her whiskers, and she studied me with yellow eyes that had seen a hundred winters starve her kind and spare them anyway.

*Forgotten,* she said, tasting the word. *That is the worst hunger. That is the one that kills when the belly is full.* She sat, right there in the snow, deliberate as a queen taking a throne. *We will hold the pass, speaker. Go back to your fire.*

And the pack settled around the lean-to like a living wall, nine shadows sinking into nine snowdrifts, and above us the young male threw his head back and sang at the torn sky — not a hunt-song, not a warning. Just a song, for the joy of a full-dark night and a promise kept, and I stood there shaking with cold and something else entirely, listening.

I had hidden my whole life from exactly this. From being heard.

It felt nothing like danger. It felt like coming up for air.

* * *

The hunters came two nights later, when the storm broke.

We knew before we saw them. The ravens had been shrieking since dawn — *steel on the south trail, steel, steel* — and the wolves' mood had changed overnight from watchful to bristling. I woke with the pack's tension already in my blood and crawled to the lean-to's mouth into a world scoured clean and blinding, every drift edged in blue shadow, the air thin as glass.

Yara was already awake, packing. Ilex stood with his staff. Maren leaned on hers, eyes sharp and ancient.

"How many?" Yara asked.

"Twelve riders. Two days' climb behind us, one day now — the storm slowed them less than we hoped." I flexed my hands, trying to get feeling back into them. "They have dogs."

"Dogs you can talk to."

"Dogs whose handlers will notice when their dogs stop pointing at us and start pointing at rocks." I grimaced. "It buys hours, not days. And Orsen-speaking to their hounds in front of twelve hunters—" I shook my head. "That's a bonfire, not a candle."

"Good," said Maren, and I stared at her. She smiled with all six of her remaining teeth. "Child, we are carrying a dragon's ember to midwinter. Do you imagine there is a version of this where we arrive quietly?"

Ilex laid a hand on my arm. "There is a saying in the chapel: a lamp hidden under a bowl lights one table. A lamp in a window lights a street. A lamp on a hill—" he shrugged, water dripping from his sleeve even now, in this dry frozen air, some habit of the drowned chapel he never lost, "—lights everything it touches."

"The pass closes behind us tonight," Yara said, squinting south. "Snowmelt, then refreeze. By tomorrow this slope will be a sheet of ice no horse crosses. We just need until tonight." She looked at me, and I saw it in her face before she said it — the question, and the answer she already knew I'd give. "Orsen. Can the pack hold twelve riders for one day?"

I looked south, down the long white throat of the valley, where the wind carried faint and getting-fainter the jingle of harness and the mutter of men who thought they were the only clever things on this mountain.

Twenty years I'd whispered. Twenty years of charm-trinkets and careful lies, of watching my own gift like it was a wound that might reopen. And now here I stood on a frozen rooftop of the world with a salt-witch, a monk, and an old woman who believed in me more than I believed in anything — and behind me, waiting for my word alone, nine wolves who had accepted my promise as if promises from my mouth were worth something.

Maybe they were. Maybe that was the whole covenant, right there. Someone has to speak first.

"No more hiding," I said.

I stepped to the edge of the slope, filled my lungs with air cold enough to cut, and let my voice roll down the valley in both tongues at once — the beast-speech beneath it like a second heartbeat — calling the pack to the hunt-line, naming each wolf as I named each rider, telling them where to harry and where to herd and where to let the horses smell death on the wind and refuse the trail entirely.

Behind me I heard Yara begin her salt-work, the hiss of it, the sea-smell rising impossibly in the high snow. Ilex began to speak the old words under his breath, keeping time like a drummer. Maren laughed.

And far below, at the head of the steel column, a rider raised a hand to halt the march, and turned his horse, and looked up the slope — straight at me, across half a mile of blinding white, and even at that distance I knew the set of those shoulders, the angle of that hat, the brother-shaped absence of him.

Tammas lifted something to his lips. A horn, or maybe just his hands.

The pass closed behind us like a door. The mountain held its breath.

And Tammas Kavanagh, crown hunter, my brother, began to climb.
