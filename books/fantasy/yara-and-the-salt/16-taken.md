## 16. Taken

The snow came in off the pass like something with intent.

Tammas Kavanagh stood at the mouth of the command tent and watched it sift down over the hunters' camp — thirty men and women in crown grey, braziers guttering, horses stamping — and thought, not for the first time that week, that he had built his entire life into a shape that could hold exactly one conviction at a time. For twenty years the conviction had been simple: the law is the wall between people and the fire in their blood. He had reported his own sibling to prove he believed it. He had hunted strangers for a decade to keep believing it.

Then an edict had gone up on the Tide Gate boards, and a charm seller with careful hands and their mother's jaw had walked back into his life, and the single conviction had split down the middle like river ice in spring.

"Riders report them holed up in the pass," said Corporal Ennet, ducking into the tent, shaking snow from her hood. "Wolves harried the vanguard all morning. Wolves, sir. Organized. That's the beast-speaker's work."

"I know what it is."

"The Warden wants them taken alive if possible."

"The Warden wants them taken *spectacularly*," Tammas said. "There's a difference, and you should learn it before your career depends on it."

Ennet blinked at him. She was young enough that her loyalty still looked like belief rather than habit, and he found he couldn't look at her too long.

He had known, since Greyharbor, since the edict went up and he'd read the name *Kavanagh* half-formed under some hunter's thumbnail — he had known the charm seller was Orsen before he'd had proof. Twenty years since the day he'd stood in their parents' kitchen and told a crown clerk, in a voice that didn't shake because he'd practiced it not shaking, that his little sibling could talk to animals. Twenty years since the door of the house had closed behind Orsen for good, and their mother had stopped setting a third place at table without ever once saying why.

He'd told himself it was mercy, what he did then. If the crown took Orsen gently, through family, they'd be audited and registered instead of hunted. That was the arithmetic he'd carried for two decades like a stone in his boot: *I saved them from worse.*

Now he was camped in a mountain pass with thirty hunters and orders to bring in the most famous fugitive in three realms, and the stone had migrated from boot to chest.

"Sir?" Ennet said. "Your orders?"

Tammas looked out at the snow. Somewhere up that white throat of the mountain, his sibling was sitting by a fire with a salt-witch and a monk and whatever was left of a dragon's ember, and every hour he delayed was an hour closer to freedom.

And every hour he delayed was an hour in which Captain Voss might stop waiting for him.

"Light touch," he said. "No horns until we see them. I'll go ahead."

"You'll— alone, sir?"

"They trust no one in grey, Corporal. They have reason to trust me even less, but they *know* me, and knowing is the only currency I've got." He pulled his cloak pin loose so the crown sigil hung hidden beneath wool. "Keep the company at the treeline. Anyone comes down that pass without me beside them, you do nothing. Nothing. Am I understood?"

Ennet's eyes flicked — quickly, but not quickly enough — toward the far end of the camp, where Captain Voss's tent stood with its banners staked stiff in the wind.

"Understood, sir," she said.

* *

Orsen heard him coming before he saw them — of course they did; the whole mountain probably heard itself being listened to.

He came around the shoulder of rock where the pass narrowed, hands open and empty, and there they were: five figures around a fire tucked into a hollow, and the sight of them hit him like a fist under the ribs. The salt-witch, Yara, on her feet instantly, salt crystals already crawling up her forearms like frost given opinions. Brother Ilex rising slower, water dripping somewhere under his robes even here, even in the cold. And Orsen — older than the last time Tammas had seen those eyes across a kitchen table, thinner, wind-burned, standing with a kind of steadiness Tammas had never once seen in the child who used to smuggle sparrows indoors and argue with the cat in a language the cat visibly understood.

"Hello, Orsen," Tammas said.

Yara's hand closed on Orsen's shoulder. "You know this one."

"He's my brother," Orsen said. Flat as slate. "Tammas. Come to arrest me properly this time? Last time I was nine and you only confiscated the hedgehog."

"I came to warn you." He kept his hands where they were, palms out, and made himself say the next part plainly, because plainness was the only thing he'd ever been able to offer this family. "Voss is two miles behind me with thirty hunters and a writ that doesn't say *audit* anymore. It says execution. Yours. The Warden signed it yesterday — they're not sending gifted folk to registration anymore, Orsen. They're making examples. There's a pyre being built in the capital square right now, and your name is carved on the stake."

Silence, except the wind and the fire and the drip-drip of Ilex's chapel-soaked robes.

"You're lying," Yara said.

"I falsified my reports for six weeks so you wouldn't be found," Tammas said. "Ask your wolves. Ask any bird in this pass. I'm the only person who has ever turned you in, Orsen, and I am telling you now that everything after me is worse. You have maybe two hours before Voss loses patience and stops caring about my name on the writ."

"And why," Ilex said quietly, "would a crown hunter damn his career for us?"

Because the ledger doesn't balance, Tammas thought. Because twenty years ago he'd traded his sibling for a principle and discovered, somewhere in the long grey middle of his life, that the principle had never once kept him warm. Because last month in the archive he'd read his own grandfather's name in the covenant-breaking records and understood, finally, that the Kavanaghs hadn't just helped forget the dragons — they'd taught the whole realm how.

"Because I'm done," he said aloud. "South road is watched. East road is watched. But there's a drover's track over the Shoulder, west — it won't take horses but it will take you, and Voss won't find it in this snow for two days. Go now. Don't thank me. Don't forgive me either; I haven't earned the first and I'm not asking for the second."

Orsen stared at him for a long moment. Something moved in their face — not forgiveness, nothing so clean. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone seeing the shape of a wound they'd carried so long they'd mistaken it for their own body.

"You falsified reports," Orsen said slowly. "For six weeks. For me."

"For the record I believed in when I was nineteen and stupid about it," Tammas said. "Go."

They went — Yara first, dousing the fire with a twist of her hand, salt hissing against coals; Ilex gathering his small bundle of written words like a man carrying eggs. Orsen paused at the edge of the hollow, one hand resting briefly on the rock where a raven sat hunched against the wind, listening to something Tammas couldn't hear.

"Come with us," Orsen said. It wasn't warmth. It was arithmetic — the same cold mercy he'd offered twenty years ago, handed back across the counter. "If Voss finds out what you've done—"

"Someone has to stand in front of you and look official while you run. That's the only use I've got." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Besides. Family dinners were never our strong suit."

Orsen held his eyes one heartbeat longer. Then they turned, and the mountain swallowed them westward, and Tammas Kavanagh stood alone in the falling snow and permitted himself, for exactly four breaths, to feel what hope felt like.

Then he walked back down the pass toward the camp, and the four breaths ended.

* *

Captain Voss was not at the treeline. Captain Voss was at the head of a column of twelve riders, already moving up the pass, and the look on his face when he reined in beside Tammas was the look of a man doing sums he'd finished hours ago.

"Hunter Kavanagh," Voss said. "Enjoying the weather?"

"My orders were to hold position—"

"Your orders were countermanded at dawn. Warden's courier reached us before your little scouting trip." Voss's breath steamed. He was a heavy man with a heavy face, and he wore authority the way other men wore armor — carelessly, because it had never once failed him. "Strange thing, though. My scouts found tracks going *west* over the Shoulder. Five sets. Fresh. And strange thing number two — every report filed from this hunt for six weeks has been wrong in the same direction. Always one valley east. Always one day late."

Tammas felt the ground tilt under him, though his boots didn't move. "Captain—"

"I wrote to the archive," Voss said. "Pulled your file. Did you know there's a discrepancy in it? A falsified requisition dated the night the Greyharbor fugitive escaped the city. Small thing. The kind of small thing a man does when he's already decided which side of a wall he's standing on." He gestured, lazily, and two riders closed in on either side of Tammas's horse. "So here's where we are, Kavanagh. Your fugitives are on a track my men can't ride. But the Warden needs an example by solstice regardless, and it turns out we have one in camp already. The brother of the realm's most wanted beast-speaker. A Kavanagh — of *that* Kavanagh family — caught conspiring with the very magic he was commissioned to hunt."

"You can't—"

"Can't what? Can't arrest a hunter?" Voss smiled, and it was the worst thing Tammas had seen all year, because it wasn't cruel. It was administrative. "I'm not arresting you, Kavanagh. I'm *promoting* you. Into the story. 'Crown hunter betrays his oath to shield the witch-blood.' The Warden will love it. The broadsheets will love it. And the pyre gets a traitor instead of a ghost, which is better theater anyway."

The world went very simple, the way it did before violence. Tammas's hand drifted toward his sword, and both flanking riders noticed, and Voss shook his head almost kindly.

"Twelve of us, son. And even if you were fast — and you're not — who would you save? They're gone west. Best thing you can do for them now is exactly what you're told." He turned his horse. "Bind his hands. Use cord, not iron — I want him presentable. We ride for the capital within the hour."

As they cinched the cord around his wrists, coarse and final as anything, Tammas looked up the pass one last time. Snow filled the drover's track's mouth already, erasing it. His sibling was out there somewhere under the white silence, running toward midwinter with a dragon's ember and two oath-keepers and a word that could remake the world — or so the old texts claimed, the ones he'd spent his career calling superstition.

Twenty years ago he had told himself he was saving Orsen from worse.

Now the worse had a date attached, and the date was his own execution, and the only thing he'd actually saved anyone from was himself.

* *

They gave him a cell in the capital's deep ward — not a criminal's cell but a ceremonial one, high-vaulted, iron-brazered, positioned so that processions of visiting officials could be shown the traitor like an exhibit. The Warden herself came down on the second day, a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes like audit columns, and she was almost gentle about it, which was somehow more terrible than Voss's smile.

"You understand," she said, "that it isn't personal. The realm requires a fire. Solstice night, the old turning of the year — symbolically apt, the people respond to it. You'll be given a speech beforehand. Confession of treason. Very moving, I'm told, when it's sincere, and yours will read as sincere because some of it will be." She set a paper on the cell table: the order of proceedings, his name printed in tall black letters above the word *STAKE*. "The witch, when we catch her — and we will catch her; the storm closes the passes within days — will burn the following week. You've bought her perhaps a fortnight. I mention it only because the condemned always ask, and I find honesty shortens the grieving."

When she'd gone, Tammas sat on the cot's edge and did the arithmetic he'd been doing his whole life, one last time.

A fortnight. Midwinter fell in eight days. If Yara and Ilex got Orsen over the mountains — if the storms closed behind them instead of on them — the pair would reach the capital around the solstice, because Orsen would come. Of course Orsen would come; the child who argued with cats had grown into an adult who argued with empires, badly armed, gloriously stupid, faithful past all sense. They would walk into the square where their sibling burned, because that was what love looked like in the Kavanagh family: arriving too late with insufficient weapons and staying anyway.

Unless.

He lay back on the cot and stared at the vaulted ceiling, and for the first time in twenty years he let himself think the whole thought without flinching: the covenant was real. The old words were real. Ilex carried them in a drowned book; the salt-witch carried a shard of covenant-stone; and the beast-speaker whose gift Tammas had reported at nine years old was, by every text the archive possessed, the voice the covenant had been waiting for. A Kavanagh had helped break it. Perhaps a Kavanagh was meant to help mend it — just not the one everyone assumed.

On solstice night there would be a pyre in the capital square. Fire, wood, crowds, guards — and a prisoner at the center of it all whom nobody guarded against, because what threat could a bound man possibly pose?

Tammas began, quietly, to plan.

Somewhere above him, beyond the vaults and the frost-furred stones, a raven landed on the barred window slot and looked down into the dark with one bright eye, and waited, patient as a promise, to hear what he would say.
