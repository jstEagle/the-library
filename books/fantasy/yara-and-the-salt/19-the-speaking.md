## 19. The Speaking

The pyre was still burning when I reached it.

I had imagined this moment a hundred times in the cell below — rehearsed it against the dark the way a child rehearses a confession — and in every imagining the square was silent, the crowd a blur of faces, my voice steady as a bell. In reality the square was screaming. Hunters were shouting orders no one obeyed. Yara was somewhere behind me with Vessith's ember cupped in salt and both hands, and Brother Ilex was chanting words that made the air taste like lightning about to decide, and Tammas had turned his sword on men he'd eaten breakfast with, and none of it was silence at all.

It was better than silence. It was alive.

The pyre they'd built for me stood at the center of Tide Gate Square, stacked oak and pitch and a platform of black iron where the condemned were meant to kneel. It had caught properly now — the executioner's torch had done its work before Yara's diversion tore through the square — and the flames climbed twenty feet into the midwinter dark, throwing heat across my face like an open hand.

I should have been afraid. Some part of me noted, distantly, that fear was probably the appropriate response to standing at the base of your own funeral fire while armed men converged from three directions. But the gift was awake in me the way it had never been awake in twenty years of hiding, and what it gave me instead of fear was *listening*.

I could hear everything.

The horses in their panic, screaming wordless questions at their riders. The crows on the rooftops, delighted — crows are always delighted by human catastrophe — calling *fire-fire-fire* to each other like children at a festival. The dogs breaking their chains in the alleys. The rats beneath the cobblestones, flowing toward the square in rivers because the rats had carried my messages for weeks and word travels fast underground: *he's out, he's out, the speaker is out.*

And underneath all of it, faint as a pulse under skin, something older. Something vast and slow and dying.

Vessith.

*Come,* she said, or the ember said for her, the flame in Yara's jar guttering low and gold. *Come to the fire, little voice. We will finish this together or not at all.*

"Orsen!" Yara's voice cut through the din. She was fighting her way toward me through the crowd, salt-white light bleeding between her fingers, her braid half-undone and her face lit from below like a woman carrying dawn. "The hunters are regrouping — we have minutes, maybe less—"

"Then we don't waste them." I turned to the pyre.

The heat pushed at me. The flames roared their animal roar, hungry and mindless, wood consuming itself because that was all fire knew how to do alone. And I understood, suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, why the covenant had been spoken here — not in temples, not in archives, but at fires. Every hearth in every house in Greyharbor held a splinter of the same flame. Every candle, every forge, every lantern. When humans forgot the dragons, they didn't stop using fire. They just stopped *thanking* it.

That was the forgetting. Not absence. Ingratitude.

Ilex reached my side, robes soaked to the knee from the flooded chapel he'd waded out of two days ago, his old voice steady as bedrock. "The words must be spoken over open flame," he said. "And they must be meant. The old tongue forgives a poor accent. It does not forgive a lie."

"I know."

"You do not know if it will kill you."

"I know," I said again, gentler. "Brother Ilex. You told me in the chapel. You've told me four times since. I heard you the first time." I looked up at the fire eating the sky above the platform where I was supposed to have died. "A life hidden was never fully lived. Whatever happens when I speak — I'll have been more myself in one minute than in twenty years."

He studied me with eyes gone pale and watery with age, and then he did something I didn't expect. He smiled. "Your grandmother would have liked you," he said. "She said the same thing, the night she taught me the words. Now. Kneel at the fire's edge. All of us. A covenant is not a monologue."

Yara knelt on my left, setting the salt jar down between us. Inside it, Vessith's last flame burned no bigger than a thumbnail — but it burned *upward*, impossibly, straining toward the great fire like a plant toward sun. On my right, Ilex folded his ruined hands. And beyond him, sword still drawn, blood on his cheek from a blow I hadn't seen land, my brother dropped to one knee in the ash.

Tammas.

He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the hunters across the square — a dozen of them now, forming a line, Captain Voss at their head with her crossbow already raised — and his jaw was set in the shape he used to make before schoolyard fights he knew he'd lose. Protecting my back with his body. The boy who reported me. The man who falsified a report to save me. Both of them, kneeling in the same ash.

"Tamm," I said.

"Don't," he said hoarsely. "Whatever you're going to say to me, say it after. If there is an after. Speak, Orsen. *Please.* Twenty years I've waited for you to use your voice. Don't stop now because of me."

* * *

Captain Voss's voice cracked across the square like a whip. "In the name of the crown! The condemned will step away from the pyre. All who aid the gifted are guilty of treason. This is your only warning."

The crowd — gods, the crowd. Half of Greyharbor had come, drawn by the chaos, by the rumor running street to street faster than any rider: *the charm seller lives, the witch broke the square, there's a dragon.* They stood packed between the hunters and us, and they did not move. A fishwife I'd sold a luck-charm to six years ago stood with her arms crossed like a wall. The baker whose dog I'd spoken with on my last morning in the market. Children on their fathers' shoulders.

Nobody stepped away. Nobody stepped forward either. They just *watched*, hundreds of eyes, and I realized that whatever happened in the next minute would happen in front of witnesses. That was the point. That had always been the point. Magic done in cellars dies in cellars. Magic done in daylight becomes a memory, and memories become stories, and stories become permission.

"Loose on my mark," Voss called, drawing her bow.

"Orsen," Yara breathed. "*Now.*"

I put my hands flat on the scorched stones at the pyre's edge. The heat seared my palms and I let it — pain is just the body paying attention, and I wanted every part of me attending. I opened the door I had kept locked since I was nine years old, since the day my brother's face went white in our mother's kitchen and the word *witch* entered our family like a knife left in a wound.

I spoke in the old tongue.

I won't write the words here. Not because they're secret — Ilex says secrets are how covenants die — but because they don't survive translation onto paper. They weren't designed for paper. They were designed for breath, for fire, for the space between one living throat and another. What I can tell you is what they *meant*, which is the only part that matters:

*We remember you. We thank you. Come back to us.*

Three sentences. That was the whole covenant. That was what generations of Kavanaghs had helped bury under ledgers and law and shame — a thank-you, owed and never paid, until the debt grew so old the creditors began to die.

My voice came out rough at first, rusted, a hinge unused for decades. The first phrase cracked in the middle. The second came easier. By the third, something strange was happening in my chest — the gift wasn't leaking out of me the way it always had, in whispers and stray conversations with dogs. It was *pouring*. The words carried farther than sound has any right to carry. I heard them bounce off the Tide Gate itself, off the harbor water, off the bell tower, and each surface gave them back changed, amplified, multiplied.

And the fire answered.

Not the pyre — the pyre was just wood. But inside the flames, something else woke. Gold threads I hadn't seen before, running through the orange like veins through a leaf, and the heat changed character, stopped being hunger and became *attention*. The whole square felt it. I saw it hit the faces in the crowd — that involuntary lean-forward, the way people turn toward a name being called.

Above the northern ranges, miles away, something enormous exhaled.

"Again," Ilex commanded, joining me, his ancient voice weaving under mine like a bass note under a melody. "The words want voices. Give them more."

Yara's voice came next — clear and salt-bright, singing more than speaking, the cadence of the covens' oldest laments repurposed at last for praise. The salt jar flared white. Vessith's ember rose out of it, floating, trailing sparks, drifting toward the pyre like a homing bird.

Four voices, then. No—

Five.

The fifth voice stumbled. Cracked. Started over.

Tammas.

He was crying; I could hear it in the texture of the sound even without looking, the wet gravel of a man singing through tears. His accent was terrible. He got half the phrases wrong. It didn't matter. The old tongue forgives a poor accent; it does not forgive a lie, and there was no lie in him. There was only a brother who had spent thirty years trying to protect his family the only way the world had taught him how, and who had finally, catastrophically, learned a better way.

A Kavanagh voice, speaking the covenant. The family that broke it, helping mend it. Somewhere in the ledger-halls of the capital, I suspected, every record of my family's shame had just quietly become obsolete.

The crossbow bolt came anyway.

I heard it leave the string — the gift caught the *twang* and read it faster than thought — and I heard Tammas move, and I heard steel ring off steel as his sword batted the bolt aside in a shower of sparks, and I heard the crowd roar, and none of it touched me. Because the ember had reached the pyre.

Vessith's last flame touched the great fire the way a single word touches a silence.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The flames stood still, every tongue of them frozen upright, and the whole square held its breath with them.

Then the fire bloomed.

There is no other word for it. It opened. Colors poured out of it that fire has no business knowing — deep violet, sea-green, the gold of honey held up to light — and the heat reversed, washing outward gentle as a summer wind instead of searing inward. Sparks rose and did not fall. They hung in the air above the square, thousands of them, arranging themselves, and I understood with a jolt that they weren't sparks anymore. They were letters. Old-tongue glyphs, burning slow and patient, spelling the covenant across the sky over Greyharbor in letters big enough to read from the harbor mouth.

*We remember you. We thank you. Come back to us.*

Written in fire, in front of a thousand witnesses, where no edict could ever unwrite it.

The hunters broke. Not all at once — Voss held her line for a long moment, crossbow shaking, staring up at the burning sky with an expression I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe — but one by one, bows lowered. One man dropped his entirely. Another fell to his knees, though not, I think, in surrender. In the crowd, people were reaching up toward the drifting embers as if catching snowflakes, and where the embers touched bare skin they left no burn, only a warmth that glowed briefly gold and faded.

And far to the north, so distant it was almost imagination, a sound rolled down out of the mountains.

Every creature in the square heard it. Every human too, gifted or not. A cry — long, ragged, immense — the sound of something that had been holding its breath for three hundred years finally letting it go.

"She's waking," Yara whispered. Her hand found mine in the ash, salt-crusted and trembling. "Orsen. She's *waking*."

"Not just her." I was listening past the cry, past the crowd, past my own hammering heart, listening the way I'd been afraid to listen my entire life. The gift stretched north like a hand opening finger by finger. "Do you feel that? Under the mountains. Under the ice. They're stirring — all of them — the sleepers, the ones we thought were stone—"

The ground shivered. Gently, the way a great ship shivers when its engines wake.

Tammas pulled me back from the pyre as a section of it collapsed in a bloom of colored fire, and for a moment the three of us — brothers, witch, monk — stood in the falling gold-light with our arms around each other like survivors of a shipwreck, laughing or crying or both, while above us the covenant burned its slow sentence across the winter sky.

Then the bells started.

First the Tide Gate bell, then Saint Verren's, then every bell in Greyharbor ringing wild and unsanctioned, and beneath the bells the crowd began to cheer — not the polite cheer of spectators but the raw, ugly, joyful roar of people who had just watched the impossible happen and decided, collectively, instantly, irrevocably, that they were on its side.

Voss lowered her crossbow. Looked at it. Looked at the sky. And threw it into the harbor.

"They'll send soldiers," Tammas said into my ear, and there was no despair in it, only the flat clarity of a soldier doing arithmetic. "The capital can't ignore this. Within days — maybe hours. And Orsen—" he gripped my shoulder, turned me to face him. His eyes were red-rimmed and blazing. "When they come, they won't be hunting a charm seller anymore. They'll be hunting the person who woke the dragons. You understand? Everything changes tonight. Everything."

Above the rooftops, the burning letters pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat.

And out of the north, riding the wind down the mountain passes, came an answer no one in that square had heard in three hundred years:

Wings.
