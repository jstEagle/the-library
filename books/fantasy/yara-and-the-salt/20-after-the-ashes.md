## 20. After the Ashes

I did not sleep that night. I don't think anyone in the capital did.

The square smelled of wet ash and salt and scorched pine, and the crowd that had gathered to watch a witch burn refused to disperse. They stood in knots around the pyre's black bones, speaking in the hushed, careful voices of people who have witnessed something they cannot yet find a shelf for. A baker's wife touched the ashes with two fingers and then pressed them to her own forehead, like a blessing, or an apology. Somewhere near the fountain, a child was asking her mother whether the dragon had been real, and the mother — I heard her clearly, because the night had gone very quiet after the wings passed over — said, "Real as rain."

Vessith's ember had gone into Orsen's hands at the last, and the last flame had gone up with the covenant words, and now there was nothing left in the brazier but heat-shimmer and history. But above us, for one long minute before the wings faded northward, the sky had held a shape I had only seen in the salt-scrying of my grandmother's bowl: vast, deliberate, unhurried. A dragon in flight over a human city. My whole life I had been told it was impossible. My whole life, it turned out, had been waiting on my friend's voice.

Orsen sat on the steps of the old customs house with a blanket around their shoulders — someone from the crowd had put it there; three someones, actually, layered like offerings — and watched the square the way a swimmer watches the shore they never thought to reach. Tammas Kavanagh stood beside me, his sword still drawn, though there was no one left to point it at. His fellow hunters had scattered when he turned the blade. Two had yielded. One had run. The rest had simply stood very still, as men do when the ground shifts under their certainties.

"You should sit," I told him.

"I've been sitting for twenty years," he said. "Inside my own head. I'd rather stand."

Which was, from Tammas, practically a love letter to humanity, so I let it go.

The soldiers came an hour before dawn — not the crown's riders, but the city garrison, four hundred strong, filing into the square with torches and pikes. The crowd went silent. Tammas stepped forward alone, sword sheathed now, hands open, and I saw him draw himself up into the officer he had been trained to be and then deliberately set that man aside, piece by piece, the way you might take off armor that no longer fits.

"My name is Tammas Kavanagh," he called out, in the carrying parade-ground voice I imagined he used for inspections. "Yesterday I was a hunter of the gifted under edict of the crown. Last night I broke that edict, and I would break it again, and here is why—"

He told them. Not about magic, exactly, or dragons, or covenants — he told them about the ledger. About what the crown had known for three hundred years and hidden: that the fire in every hearth was borrowed, that the warmth their children slept beneath was a loan against a promise nobody had kept. He told them the fire was dying because we had forgotten to say thank you. He told them a charm seller from Greyharbor had remembered on behalf of everyone in that square, and that remembering had cost more than any tax or tithe ever levied.

And the garrison — this is the part I will tell my grandchildren, if the covens ever let me have grandchildren — the garrison *listened*. A captain near the front lowered her pike. An old sergeant took off his helmet. And when Tammas finished, the silence held for ten heartbeats, fifteen, and then somewhere in the ranks a voice said, quietly, "My mother was taken in the first audit. Rellin. She mended nets." And another voice answered, and another, and the pikes came down along the line like wheat bending before wind, all the way to the end of the square, without one order being given.

That was when I understood the edict was already dead. It just didn't know it yet.

* * *

The crown's answer arrived three days later, and it was not soldiers.

It was a clerk.

He rode into the capital in a mud-splattered courier's coat with a sealed writ and an expression of a man who had drawn the shortest straw in a very long bundle, and he read the proclamation from the same Tide Gate steps where the hunt had been announced. I stood in the front this time. I wanted to see his face.

"In light of recent... events," he read, and his voice cracked on *events*, "and following consultation between His Majesty's advisors and His Majesty's—" a pause, a swallow, "—advisors, the edict of covenant audit is hereby rescinded. All persons detained under its provisions are to be released. All hunts recalled." He lowered the parchment. Behind his eyes I could see the whole rotten machinery of it: the palace waking to the fact that you cannot burn a witch when forty thousand people have watched her walk out of the flames talking to the sky, and half of them have started leaving bowls of milk on their doorsteps for the crows, just in case the crows are listening now.

"Is that all?" someone shouted.

The clerk looked at the crowd — the same crowd, grown larger, stranger, braver — and then did something no crown functionary had done in living memory.

He bowed.

"There is also," he said, "a request. His Majesty requests an audience. With the beast-speaker." A beat. "At the speaker's convenience."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Three hundred years of fear, dissolved into *at the speaker's convenience*, and the word *request* sitting in a royal sentence like a fish in a fruit tree. Beside me, Orsen went very still. Later, at the tavern where we'd taken rooms, they turned the writ's copy over and over in their hands.

"They want to meet me," Orsen said. "Me. The person they tried to burn."

"People want to meet the weather now, too," I said. "You're both beyond arguing with."

But I saw what it cost them to smile, and how long it took.

* * *

The news from the north came in pieces, each one less believable than the last, and all of it true.

A drover arriving from the mountain passes swore she had seen firelight on the peaks on two consecutive nights — not lightning, she insisted, not beacon-fires, but *moving* light, circling, like lanterns carried by something patient and enormous. A shepherd from the high valleys brought down a shed scale the size of a dinner plate and would not sell it at any price; he was going to give it to his church, he said, or start one. In Threllmark, so the traders claimed, three dragons had risen from the glacier fields in a single morning, and the sound of their waking had cracked the ice on Lake Vorn from shore to shore.

They were waking. All across the northern ranges, in the deep cold places where the last of them had gone to gutter out alone, the dragons were lifting their heads to a world that had finally, belatedly, learned their names.

I felt it too, in my own way. Salt-witches work with what endures — preservation, memory, the long patience of crystals — and something in my craft had shifted, the way a tide shifts before you can see it move. My jars of warding-salt, packed tight for years, had loosened overnight. When I cast a circle now, it closed sweeter, cleaner, as if the world itself had stopped holding its breath.

Brother Ilex noticed it before I did. We found him on the tavern roof at dawn — he had never quite lost the monastic habit of praying at strange hours and stranger elevations — with his face turned north and tears running freely into his beard.

"They're answering the words," he said simply. "Every covenant spoken truly is heard twice, Yara. Once by the parties, and once by everything that waited."

* * *

The letter from my coven came a week later, carried not by rider but by gull.

I should explain that. Salt-witches keep gulls the way other folk keep pigeons, and mine found me in the capital because salt knows salt, and because Mother Ondine had always had a flair for the dramatic delivery. It dropped a scroll wrapped in oilcloth onto the table between me and Orsen as we ate breakfast, screamed once — gulls are incapable of good news quietly — and wheeled off southward.

I knew the seal before I broke it. White wax, pressed with the nine-toothed comb: the mark of the Tidemothers, my mother-coven, the women who had walked me to the tide line seven years ago and told me not to look back.

*Yara of the Outer Shallows,* the letter began, formal as a funeral, and my stomach did the old drop, the exile-drop, the body remembering every door that had closed. Then:

*We write to inform you that the ban which removed you from our circle has been examined in light of the covenant restored, and found to have been, in the judgment of the assembled mothers, wrongly laid.*

*We do not ask your forgiveness. Forgiveness is yours to give or withhold, and we have no claim on it. What we ask is smaller and harder: come home, if home still means anything to you, and let us see what seven years have made of you.*

*Also enclosed is the recipe for storm-broth you requested at nineteen, which Ondine has been hoarding out of spite and now surrenders under protest.*

*The sea remembers you.*
*O., for the Nine.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, because Orsen was making a face that said *well?* louder than words could.

"'The sea remembers you,'" I repeated, and my voice did something embarrassing on the second word, so I stopped and folded the letter and looked very hard at my breakfast.

"Yara." Orsen leaned across the table. Twenty days ago I had met them behind a market stall full of lies, a person folded up small enough to fit inside their own secret. Now they looked at me with the steady attention of someone who had spoken with a dragon and lived. "Go home."

"After midwinter," I said. "There are oath-keepers still scattered — Maren's shard points to two more, and Ilex can't travel alone, and—" I ran out of excuses and heard how thin they'd sounded even as I made them. "All right. The truth is I don't know if I can walk back through that door. Exile fits a person, eventually. Like a stone fits a riverbed."

Orsen turned their cup slowly on the table. "I spent twenty years fitting myself to a hiding place," they said. "And then one day the hiding place burned down, and it turned out I'd been a hearth all along, just boarded over." They looked up. "Stones can leave riverbeds, Yara. That's how you get gems."

"That's terrible."

"It's a beast-speaker proverb. I invented it this morning. Give it time."

I laughed until the tavern keeper looked over, and then I laughed a little more, because I could. Because the door ahead of me was open, and for once, so was the one behind.

* * *

Small mercies. That was the shape of those weeks, once I learned to look for them. Not one great healing — the world doesn't work that way, and neither do people — but mercies in the small plural, arriving like tide in the night.

The audit-rolls were burned publicly in the capital square, and I was there, and I watched a woman named Rellin — net-mender, mother of the sergeant who had first lowered her voice in the garrison — throw the page with her own name onto the fire and warm her hands at it. Hunters' garrisons across three realms stood down or disbanded; some of the hunters, Tammas told me grimly, were having a harder time than anyone deciding who they were without the hunt, and he did not exclude himself.

In Greyharbor, word came, the market had reopened under new unwritten rules: charms sold openly, prices posted, no more whispered bargains in back alleys. A charm-sellers' guild had formed in a week. It had, apparently, immediately gotten into a price war with the saltwives over warding-work, which Orsen found hilarious and I found professionally offensive.

And on a clear cold morning, standing on the capital's north wall, I saw one.

Not a shape in cloud, not a story in a trader's mouth. A dragon — young by the set of the wings, or perhaps just small, or perhaps there is no small — crossing the dawn from the mountains toward the sea, unhurried, its hide catching the low sun in colors I have no trade-names for. Below us on the road, carters pulled up their teams and stood on their benches to watch. Nobody screamed. A child waved.

The dragon banked, wide and easy, and I would swear by salt and covenant both that it dipped one wing toward the wall before it went on south. Toward Greyharbor, maybe. Toward whatever was next.

"They're curious," Orsen said beside me. Their gift had been growing louder since the Speaking — not burdensome, they insisted, but insistent, the way hearing is. "It asked the gulls about us. Whether the speakers were friendly."

"And?"

"The gulls said yes." A pause. "Gulls are flatterers."

We stood a while longer in the cold, and I thought about doors, and tides, and how the world had been remade not by decree but by forty thousand ordinary people deciding, in the space of one burning night, that they would rather live in a world with dragons in it. The crown had rescinded the hunt because the hunt had become impossible — you cannot make people afraid of a thing they have begun to leave milk out for. The edict hadn't been defeated. It had been *outgrown*.

"Midwinter's past," Orsen said at last. "Maren's shard wants to move. I can feel it pulling — northeast, toward the coast. There are keepers out there who don't know yet what happened here. Someone should tell them the waiting's over."

"Someone," I agreed. "Us."

"Us," they said, and it wasn't a question anymore. It hadn't been since the marshes.

Ilex joined us on the wall an hour later with bread and hard cheese and news: a ship in the harbor, bound north, its captain reportedly so devout in her new dragon-fever that she was offering free passage to "any soul on covenant business." And Tammas came at noon, out of uniform for the first time since I'd known him, carrying instead a leather satchel of maps and a commission he'd drafted himself, titled, in his own severe hand: *Protector of the Covenant — Provisional, Population One.*

"Population one," I said.

"Every institution starts small," he said, with the ghost of a smile. "Even the crown did."

So: the shard pointing northeast, a ship at anchor, a monk packing his vigil into a carpet bag, a soldier learning a new trade, a witch with a letter from home folded against her heart and seven years of riverbed-stone beginning, grain by grain, to loosen. The dragons waking in the white countries. The world, remade in mercies small enough to hold.

We sailed on the morning tide.

And forty miles out from the capital, where the coast bends toward the deep water, the sea around us suddenly went silver — not with fish, but with salt rising unbidden from the hold-barrels, my own craft answering something before my mind caught up — and every gull within a league went silent at once, and Orsen gripped the rail, eyes wide, and said:

"Something big is coming up from the deep. And it's *singing.*"

The water off the stern began to glow.
