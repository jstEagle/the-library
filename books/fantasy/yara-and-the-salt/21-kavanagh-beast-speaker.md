## 21. Kavanagh, Beast-Speaker

The commission seal came off my coat easier than I expected.

I'd imagined it fused there — wax and oath and twenty years of service grown into the wool like a second skin. But it peeled away with a thumbnail's pressure, leaving only a pale circle where the crown's colors had been, and I stood in the barracks doorway holding it in my palm like something small and dead.

Sergeant Vell watched me from across the yard. She didn't say anything. She'd been there at the solstice, sword drawn, standing in the smoke while a dragon's shadow crossed the sun over Greyharbor Square. Whatever she'd decided about me in that moment, she'd had three weeks to live with it, same as everyone.

"You could wait for the formal discharge," she said finally. "Let them take it off you in front of witnesses. Make them look at what they're losing."

"They're not losing anything," I said. "That's rather the point."

She almost smiled. "Kavanagh."

"Vell."

"The northern garrisons are reporting wakes. Dozens of them. Dragons coming out of the ice like it's spring." She said it flatly, the way she reported everything, but her eyes weren't flat at all. "Somebody's going to have to figure out how people and dragons are going to share a country again. And it won't be the men who spent last year hunting children through the marshes."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm going."

* * *

Orsen was waiting for me at the edge of the ash orchard.

I'd sent word ahead — through channels I still half-expected to be illegal, though the edict had collapsed into paper and apology two weeks after the solstice. A boy on a mule, a verbal message, nothing written. *Your brother asks to come. He brings no sword.* It felt strange, asking permission from someone I'd once arrested. It felt stranger still that Orsen granted it within the hour.

They stood among Maren's burned trees — or what remained of them. The riders' retribution had come here months ago, before any of us understood what was waking, and the orchard had worn its scars openly since: black trunks, leafless branches, ground still smelling faintly of old smoke. Except that wasn't true anymore either. Walking up the cart track, I'd seen it before I let myself believe it. Green shoots at the base of every dead trunk. New wood climbing the old like ivy, tender and impossibly fast, as if the trees had been waiting all this time for permission.

Dragons had wept here once, Yara told me later. The trees grew where their grief fell. Now something else was falling on them, and they were growing again.

"You came," Orsen said.

"You asked me to."

"I asked you to come unarmed. You brought a horse, provisions, and that ridiculous red traveling cloak." Their mouth twitched. "Very martial. Very intimidating to the apple shoots."

I looked down at the cloak. It was maroon. It had always been maroon. "It's practical."

"It's hideous." They said it lightly, but we both heard what sat underneath it, because we were Kavanaghs, and underneath everything was where we lived. "Come on. Yara's cooking. If you insult the salt-work she'll turn you into a pillar of brine, and I'll pretend to be very sad about it."

* * *

We ate in Maren's old kitchen, under a roof half-collapsed and patched with new thatch, at a table that had survived every fire and frost the orchard had ever suffered. Yara cooked over a hearth where a jar of dragon-fire sat on the mantel, glowing steady and gold, no longer dying. Ilex said grace in a language none of us knew and all of us somehow understood. The food was simple — root stew, dark bread, apples from the first of the new growth, small and tart and so alive they hurt to eat.

I kept waiting for the moment when someone would say the thing. The thing that needed saying. It hung over the table like weather.

It was Ilex who broke first, gently, the way he did everything. "Tammas. You gave up your commission. That is a large thing. Tell us how it was done."

"Badly," I said. "Then properly."

Yara snorted into her cup.

"I wrote my resignation the night after the solstice," I said. "Wrote four versions. The first one blamed the crown. The second one blamed the edict. The third one was just the word *sorry*, forty times." I turned my cup on the table, watching the firelight move in it. "The fourth one told the truth. That I had enforced an unjust law with diligence and skill, and that diligence and skill in the service of cruelty are worse than incompetence, because they make the cruelty efficient. That I had known it was unjust. That I had known since the beginning."

"And did they accept it?" Ilex asked.

"They accepted it. They also offered me a promotion." I let that sit. "Command of the new Office of Draconic Affairs. Which is either a very generous gesture or a very clever way of putting a leash on the man who turned his sword on his fellow hunters in front of three hundred witnesses. Possibly both."

"And you refused," Yara said. Not a question.

"I refused the office. I'm not refusing the work." I looked at Orsen then, because this part was for them, and I was tired of arriving at the hard parts sideways. "Someone has to stand between the dragons waking in the north and the garrisons that spent a year learning to fear magic. Someone the soldiers will listen to, because I'm one of them. Someone the covenant can trust, because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because I owe it. Not as payment. Payment implies the debt can be cleared. As maintenance. Like tending an orchard. You don't finish. You just keep doing it."

Orsen set down their spoon.

"There's something you should know," they said, "before you swear yourself to anything. Before you decide what kind of tending you're offering." They took a breath, and I recognized the breath — I'd seen it across a childhood of shared rooms, the inhale before a confession or a dare. "When you reported me. Twenty years ago. To Father. When we were children and I talked to the yard dog and you told him what I could do."

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the fire-jar seemed to dim.

"I've thought about it every day since the edict posted," Orsen went on. "Every single day. Why you did it. Whether you knew what Father would do. Whether you knew he'd beat me half to death and call it correction, whether you watched, whether you—"

"I watched," I said.

Orsen flinched. So did I, hearing myself.

"He told me to leave the room," I said. "And I stayed. Because some part of me — the part that had already learned to love the law more than my own brother — needed to see justice done. That's what I called it. Justice. My sibling had a forbidden gift, and Father was the head of the household, and the law of the house was the law, and I stood there and watched him break you and I thought: *this is correct.*" My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "I was eleven years old, and I was already lost, and I want you to know that I know that. I don't say it to be forgiven. I say it because you deserve the whole accounting, not the comfortable parts."

Orsen was staring at the table. Their hands were flat on the wood, pressing down, the way they pressed when they were keeping themselves steady.

"Why?" they asked finally. Just that. "Why did you love the law more than me?"

I had carried the answer for thirty years without ever saying it aloud. It came out smaller than I expected.

"Because the law never left," I said. "You did. After Father — you went quiet, and then you went careful, and then you went to Greyharbor and became someone who wasn't quite my sibling anymore, someone who flinched when I raised my hand too fast, someone I couldn't reach. And the law was always there. Every morning. Same rules, same shape, same certainty. I married it because it was the only thing in my life that didn't flinch away from me." I shook my head. "That's not an excuse. An eleven-year-old doesn't get excuses. But you asked why, and that's the truth of it, and I've owed you the truth longer than I've owed anyone anything."

Silence. Yara reached across the table and covered Orsen's hand with hers, and didn't say a word, which was the wisest thing anyone did that night.

"I'm not going to tell you it's all right," Orsen said at last. Their voice was rough. "Because it isn't. What you did — standing there, watching, calling it justice — that lives in me. It's part of why I hid for twenty years. Part of why I nearly died on that pyre believing a hidden life was the best I could hope for." They lifted their eyes to mine. "But I watched you turn your sword on your own hunters to save me. I read your falsified report in the archive — yes, I found it, don't look surprised, I found everything in that archive. I know what it cost you. And I know you resigned a career rather than pretend the last year didn't happen." They exhaled slowly. "So here's what I can give you, Tammas. Not absolution. Absolution would be a lie, and we've had enough lies between us to fill a ledger. What I can give you is this: a seat at this table. Work worth doing. A chance to spend the rest of your life being something other than what you were. Whether that's enough — whether it ever becomes enough — that's not mine to promise. It's yours to earn, one day at a time, probably forever."

"That sounds exhausting," I said, and my voice came out wrecked.

"It is," Orsen said. "Welcome to family."

Yara laughed — a short, wet sound — and refilled all our cups, and Ilex murmured something in the old tongue that might have been a blessing or might have been *about time*, and the fire in the jar burned steady and gold, and nobody forgave anybody, exactly. We just stayed at the table. Sometimes that's the whole of it. Sometimes staying is the sacrament.

* * *

I swore the oath at dawn, in the orchard, among the growing trees.

Not to the crown. There was no crown in it, no seal, no wax. Yara had drafted the words with Ilex over two nights of argument, borrowing from the old covenant's structure because, as she put it, "the ancients understood that protection oaths fail unless they name what you're protecting *from* and *for*." The final text was seven sentences long. Ilex spoke each one and I repeated it, kneeling in the wet grass with the green shoots brushing my hands.

*I swear to stand between the waking and the fearful. To hold the line where old wounds meet new wonder. To answer to the covenant, and to no crown that forgets it. To guard not the powerful but the vulnerable — scale and skin alike. To keep honest accounts of my own failures. To lay down any command that contradicts this oath. And to teach, always, before I fight.*

When it was done, Orsen stepped forward and did something no one had warned me about. They pressed two fingers to my forehead, the way our mother used to when we were small and feverish, and spoke a word in the beast-tongue — low, resonant, a word that seemed to arrive through my bones rather than my ears.

"What does it mean?" I asked.

"It means *kin*," Orsen said. "In the language every creature understands. From today, the wolves know it. The crows know it. Anything with wings or teeth that hears your name will hear that word behind it." They smiled, and it was the smile of someone who had finally stopped hiding, wide and unguarded and lit from inside by a fire that would never gutter again. "You wanted to be the covenant's protector. Congratulations. Now everything in the world knows whose side you're on."

"That seems tactically unwise."

"Everything worth doing is tactically unwise," said Yara, shouldering her pack. "Come on, protector. Vessith's sending word through the stone — there's a hatchling wake in the northern passes, and the garrison at Coldmere is spooked enough to start shooting at shadows. You wanted work." She tossed me a folded paper sealed with salt-crystal instead of wax. "Here's your first commission. Try not to arrest anyone."

"No promises," I said, and swung onto my horse, and rode north with my brother's word humming in my bones and my old life burning down behind me like a field after harvest — cleared, at last, for planting.
