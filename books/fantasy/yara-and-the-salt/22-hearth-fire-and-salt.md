## 22. Hearth-Fire and Salt

The gulls woke me before the sun did, which was fitting, because they'd been arguing about me.

"—told him, I told him, the tall one leaves out fish heads on Thursdays—"

"Thursdays. It's Thursday, you absolute barnacle-brain."

I lay a moment longer in the loft above the stall, listening to the two of them squabble on the new roof beams, and felt the particular happiness of being argued about by gulls. Two years ago I'd have pressed my pillow over my ears and pretended I couldn't hear. Now I answered them.

"It's Wednesday," I said through the floorboards. "Thursday's tomorrow. Patience."

Silence. Then, delighted, scandalized: "He *talks back*."

"He always talks back. Why do you think the crows like him?"

I got up, dressed in the grey half-light, and went down the ladder into the stall that Yara and I had built with our own four hands on the ashes of the old one — literally so; the foundation stones were quarried from the burned market row, and if you knew where to look you could still find scorch-marks on the underside of the lintel like old scars that had decided to become decoration.

The new market of Greyharbor smelled of sawdust and salt and frying dough, because it was half-built and fully alive, scaffolding laddering up between finished shops, hammers going off all down the row like an irregular heartbeat. My charm-stall occupied the corner plot, wide enough now for two tables: mine on the left, hung with wards and blessings and small honest magics, and Yara's on the right, where she worked salt the way a smith works iron — coaxing it, singing to it low, drawing shapes out of white crystal that made people stop in the walkway and forget what they'd been walking toward.

She was already there when I came down, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of my old cloak. She didn't look up.

"You're talking to gulls again."

"They started it."

"Everything starts with you talking to something." But she was smiling at her salt. On the table between us, a line of crystals had grown overnight into the shape of a wave mid-curl, delicate as lace, and I knew without asking that it wasn't for sale. It was for the window. For looking at. For remembering what the sea had almost taken from both of us and hadn't.

I opened the shutters. Morning came in.

* * *

The first customer was a cat.

Not, strictly speaking, a customer. Mackerel — a vast orange tom with a torn ear and the self-importance of a minor duke — sat himself on Yara's table at precisely the hour he sat there every morning, and stared at me until I gave him the dried sprat I kept under the counter for exactly this purpose.

"*Your tribute is late,*" he said, around the fish.

"Your tribute is on time. Your *arrival* is early."

"*Time is a construct of the ones who feed us.*" He ate with terrible dignity. Behind him the fishwives were already laughing; everyone on the row knew Mackerel, and everyone knew I talked to him, and nobody crossed the square anymore to avoid seeing it. That was the part I still couldn't get used to. Not the magic — the magic had never changed. The *watching*. Two years ago a whispered word to a stray dog had nearly cost me my life. Now I held conversations with a fat cat in front of forty people, and a fishwife's daughter leaned on her mother's cart and listened like it was a story.

Which, I supposed, it was. Everything was a story now. The whole city had agreed to live inside a better one.

Mrs. Ferrow came next, for her hearth-ward, same as every month, and complained about her lodger, same as every month, and paid in plum jam, same as every month, because we had long since stopped pretending either of us wanted coin for this transaction. Then the cooper's boy for a charm against splinters, which I made him watch me weave, because the crown's new guild-school sent examiners around and I had promised Magister Ilex — *Brother* Ilex still, stubbornly, though the chapel had been drained and reconsecrated twice over — that I would demonstrate openly whenever asked. Education, he said, was vigilance done gently.

Then a woman I didn't know, travel-worn, with a northern accent and a salt-jar wrapped in wool held against her chest the way you hold something breathing.

"I was told," she said carefully, "there's a witch here who works salt. And a beast-speaker. I was told — " and here her voice did the thing voices do when they've been holding fear a long way and can finally set some of it down — "I was told you help."

"We help," Yara said, coming to stand beside me. "What's in the jar?"

And the woman drew back the wool, and inside, banked down in its bed of grey crystal, glowed an ember of dragon-fire — small, guttering, but alive, and my own chest tightened the way it always did, because I remembered the first ember Yara had ever shown me, in a cellar, in another life, and how close that little flame had come to being the last one in the world.

"A wake-site," Yara said softly. "In the north?"

"The high passes past Coldmere." The woman's hands shook. "It came to my grandmother's hearth sixty years ago, when the fires were leaving. We kept it. We never knew why, only that we must. And now the dragons are waking and the fire wants to go *home*, and I don't know the way, and the roads are full of — of people coming to look at dragons, and some of them are kind and some of them are not, and I was told—" she stopped, steadied herself, "—I was told you were the ones who carried the last ember once. That you'd know."

I looked at Yara. Yara looked at me. Somewhere behind us a hammer rang, and the gulls screamed their opinions, and Vessith's shadow — vast, unhurried, beloved — slid across the rooftops on her morning circuit of the harbor, and every person in the market paused to watch her pass the way you pause for a passing bell, except that what it rang now was not mourning but *morning*.

"Yeah," Yara said. "We know the way."

* * *

We closed early, which caused comment, because the charm-seller and the salt-witch never closed early. I left a sign — GONE NORTH, BACK BY THAW, ASK MACKEREL IF URGENT — and Mrs. Ferrow promised to feed the cat, and Mackerel promised to extort her mercilessly, and I told him not to frighten her, and he pretended not to hear, which is a cat's version of assent.

We rode out through the tide gate where everything had ended and everything had begun. The guards there saluted us now, which I would never get entirely comfortable with; one of them, a round-faced young man who'd been a child during the hunt, asked shyly if it was true I could speak to anything alive, and I said mostly true, I still couldn't get a straight answer out of oysters, and he laughed like I'd given him something.

Past the gate, the flats opened out, silver and enormous. The salt flats where Yara had once blinded hunters with forbidden light, where guilt had ridden with us and been slowly, mile by mile, worn down into something else. She reined up at the edge of them and just looked, for a while, the way she always did.

"My coven sent word again," she said, apropos of nothing. "Third time this year."

"I know. You keep showing me the letter."

"I keep showing you the letter so you don't think I'm hiding it." She turned the paper over in her gloved hand. *The exile is lifted. Come home when you're ready. The sea misses its daughter.* "They don't understand that this is home now. I keep writing it and they keep not understanding, and eventually one of us will have to visit in person and shout about it over dinner."

"Family," I said, "is like that."

"Speaking of which." She nodded back the way we'd come, and I turned, and far off on the road behind us — too far for any ordinary eye, but I'd have known the set of those shoulders from a hundred leagues — a single rider had paused at the tide gate, and raised a hand, and turned north onto our road.

Tammas didn't hurry to catch us. He never did. He rode at his own pace, a few hundred yards back, the distance he'd chosen and maintained for two years: near enough to come if we called, far enough that no one could say the covenant's sworn protector traveled under his brother's shadow. Some evenings at camp he closed that distance. Some mornings he opened it again. We had stopped trying to read it as anything but what it was — a man learning, at fifty yards a day, how to be family to someone he'd once handed to the fire.

He'd resigned nothing, actually. He'd been *reassigned*, loudly and publicly, by a crown that understood optics better than its edicts had — sworn protector of the covenant, first of the name, with a new badge struck in covenant-stone and everything. He wore it awkwardly. He wore everything awkwardly. It was one of the things I'd learned to love about him again, loving him the way you love weathered wood, knowing every crack and what made each one.

"He'll want to know why we closed the stall," Yara said.

"He'll have read the sign."

"He'll interrogate Mackerel."

"God help him."

She laughed, and put her heels to her horse, and we went out across the flats with the winter sun low and gold and Tammas's dust plume rising patient behind us, and above, very high, where most eyes would see only a dark mote against the brightness, Vessith wheeled once — a slow acknowledgment, an elder's nod — and turned north ahead of us like a drawn thread pulling three small lives along the pattern they'd always been meant to make.

* * *

We made the marsh country by dark, because of course we did; the road north ran through the marshes, and the marshes were where everything between Yara and me had turned from flight into journey, back when herons were intelligence-gatherers and shame still rode on my shoulder like a second passenger.

The herons remembered me.

"*Grey-coat,*" they said, dropping out of the dusk one by one to stand in the shallows around our camp like a parliament in grey feathers. "*You came back. Nobody comes back.*"

"I live here now. Near here. Near-ish."

"*Near-ish,*" repeated the eldest heron, in the tone of one filing the word away for later mockery, and then, businesslike: "*There are riders ahead. Many. But they are not hunter-riders.*"

"How many is many? And how do you know they're not hunters?"

"*Because,*" said the eldest, with immense patience, "*they are riding slowly, and stopping at every village, and the children run alongside them, and one of them is carrying a thing that hums.*"

Yara and I looked at each other over the fire. Covenant-stone hummed. Oath-keepers carried covenant-stone.

"How many villages ahead?" Yara asked.

"*Three days,*" said the eldest heron, and then added, because even herons had learned which side their bread was buttered: "*Bring fish next time. The tribute economy of this marsh depends on you.*"

* * *

I couldn't sleep that night, and it wasn't the cold ground or the coming trouble. It was the noise. The good noise.

The marsh at night was a congress: voles running messages under the tussocks, an owl conducting her audits, frogs holding forth on matters of theology, and somewhere off east, faint but unmistakable, the deep slow dream-speech of a young dragon sleeping in the high passes — a hatchling-wake's fire, talking to itself in its sleep the way children do, saying fragments of words older than any of us. The covenant's voice, everywhere now, in everything, and me in the middle of it like a man standing in a bell-tower while all the bells rang.

Twenty years I'd kept quiet. Twenty years pressing the gift down flat, muffling it, feeding it scraps in cellars and alleyways, terrified of the day it would be heard. And now the world was loud with it and nobody wanted me silent. The strangest grief of my life was discovering that being heard took practice — that I flinched, still, sometimes, when a stranger caught me mid-conversation with a horse. That trust, once you finally got it, had to be worn in like boots.

Yara sat down beside me at the fire's edge, salt-crystals chiming softly in her pack.

"Loud night," she said.

"Very."

"You're smiling."

"I'm allowed."

"You're allowed," she agreed, and leaned her shoulder against mine, and we watched the fire eat down to embers — real fire, ordinary fire, humble fire, the kind that had once been lent to human hearths by creatures so old that forgetting them had nearly killed them, and which now, in every hearth in every house from Greyharbor to Coldmere, burned a little brighter than physics strictly allowed, because somewhere a dragon was awake and remembered, and remembering was the whole of the bargain.

"Orsen," she said, after a while. "Those riders with the humming stone. If it's what I think it is — more oath-keepers, come looking for us —"

"Then the covenant keeps growing."

"And if it isn't?"

I listened east, where the hatchling dreamed, and the dream-speech curled through the dark like smoke with meaning in it, and one phrase surfaced clear, the way it had every night this week, patient as tide:

*Said aloud. Said together. Said Kavanagh.*

"That's tomorrow's problem," I said, "and I'll tell you what I told the gulls: Thursday's tomorrow, and patience."

"*That makes no sense,*" said Yara.

"No," I admitted, and fed another stick to the fire, and watched the sparks rise toward Vessith's wheeling shadow, and was, for perhaps the ten-thousandth time since the pyre, glad — down in the bone-marrow, where hiding used to live — that I no longer had anywhere left to be invisible, and no reason left to want it.

North of us, the stone hummed. South of us, the harbor slept. Beside me, my family.

"Come on," I said, standing, and offered Yara my hand, and spoke to the dark marsh all around us at once, out loud, in my own voice, the way the whole world could hear: "Let's go see who's looking for us."
