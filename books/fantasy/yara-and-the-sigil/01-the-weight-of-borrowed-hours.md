## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Hours

There were three rules to the trade, and Kestrel Delacroix had learned them all the hard way, which was the only way anyone learned anything worth knowing in Vell.

Rule one: never steal a whole life. A memory taken whole left a hole shaped like a person, and holes that size drew questions the way a dropped coin drew hands.

Rule two: never be remembered. The best thief in the city was the one who existed nowhere—not in ledgers, not in gossip, not even in the corner of a widow's eye.

Rule three, which was really rule one wearing a different coat: know what a thing weighs before you lift it.

Grief, for instance, weighed more than love. He'd learned that early. Love came away clean, like a ripe plum from its stem; grief grew roots, and pulling it free always tore something.

He stood across Lantern Row from Maren Ostrey's shuttered house and watched candlelight gutter behind the glass, and he reminded himself of all three rules at once, because tonight he would break the spirit of rule one if he wasn't careful, and rules kept him alive.

Maren had been a widow for eleven months and four days. Kestrel knew the count because grief had a texture, and hers had changed with the seasons—he'd been watching her house since spring, back when her sorrow was still wet clay, too soft to handle. Now it had dried into something he could grip. Eleven months of mourning had hardened it into a shape: the smell of a gray coat on a peg, the sound of a second teacup set down unused, the particular way lamplight fell on an empty half of a bed. That was what he'd take. Not all of it—never all of it—but the weight of it, the daily ache of it, enough to fill two charms and leave her lighter without leaving her less.

He crossed the street in the space between one patrol lamp swinging past and the next. His boots made no sound; he'd paid a cobbler in the memory of his mother's lullabies for soled leather, back when he still had lullabies to spend, and the silence they bought had saved his skin more times than he could count. Which was the problem, wasn't it. He couldn't count them anymore.

The latch gave to a touch of pressure from his knuckle, not magic, just knowledge—Vell's locksmiths had exactly seven tricks between them, and Kestrel knew every one.

Inside, the house smelled of tallow and dried lavender and something underneath both, something like a room where no one has spoken in months. He climbed the stairs on the balls of his feet. Maren slept in the narrow bed beneath the window, her breath coming slow, her face turned toward the wall where a coat hung that no one would ever wear again.

Kestrel sat in the chair beside her—the visitor's chair, the empty chair—and pressed two fingers to his own palm, where the sigil lived under his glove like a coal banked in ash.

It never woke for this part. It never woke for anything, not once in five years, and some nights he told himself that meant it had gone dormant, dead scar tissue, nothing but an old burn with a story attached. Other nights he suspected it simply didn't care about small thefts. Whatever it was—and he did not let himself think about whose hand had sealed it into his—it waited for larger things. He was grateful for its silence. Magic left traces. Traces got people burned.

He leaned close, breathed out slow, and *took*.

The taking felt, from his side, like drawing thread from a spool. From hers it would feel like nothing—a dream she wouldn't remember, an evening slightly less heavy than the one before it. The grief came up through his fingers in strands: the coat's smell, first, wool and cedar and the ghost of pipe smoke; then the teacup's clink; then the long gray hours of waking at dawn expecting warmth beside her and finding sheets gone cold as river stones. He took those hours specifically, the eleven o'clock hour when she woke and lay listening to a house with only one heart in it, because that hour repeated nightly, and grief like that replenished itself. Take it and tomorrow it grew back, a little thinner each time. She would sleep better. She'd never know why.

He did not take the face. There was always a face at the center of a grief, and he never touched it. Take the face and you took the person, and the widow who woke having forgotten why she mourned would tear the city apart looking for the reason, and the reason would be missing, and missing reasons made the kind of noise Inquisition wardens came running to.

The threads wound around his palm—metaphorically, mostly; the sigil stayed cold—and he cupped them, compressed them, worked them the way a charmkneader works wax, until the whole of that gray hour sat in his cupped hands no bigger than a walnut, glowing faintly the color of old pewter.

Two charms' worth. Maybe three, if he stretched it thin, but thin charms cracked, and cracked memories leaked, and a buyer who suddenly smelled pipe smoke that wasn't there started asking questions. Rule three.

He slipped down the stairs, paused at the door to look back once at the dark shape of the bed. "Sorry," he murmured, which was ritual, not apology. He said it every time. He wasn't sure to whom.

* * *

The merchant's name was Corvin Hale, and his treasure was not gold.

Kestrel found him at the Gilded Scale, Vell's most self-consciously expensive supper club, where Hale sat alone over a plate of quail he hadn't touched, staring at a woman across the room with the naked, unguarded longing of a man who believed himself unobserved. The woman was not his wife. The woman was, if Kestrel read the room correctly, someone Hale had loved twenty years ago, before money and marriage and the great gray accumulation of a respectable life.

First loves were delicate work. They braided themselves into everything—a song, a street corner, the specific bitterness of coffee drunk at nineteen—and you could not pull the braid without unraveling adjacent strands. But Hale had done the work for him, bless him: he'd spent twenty years cordoning that love off, keeping it in a locked room of himself, visiting it only nights like this when quail went cold and wives stayed home. Isolated love. Preserved in its own brine. It lifted like a stone from shallow water.

Kestrel waited in the coat corridor, in the shadow between two lanterns, and when Hale came for his cloak Kestrel brushed past him in the manner of a man slightly drunk and very sorry, one hand steadying itself briefly against the merchant's chest.

"Beg pardon—"

"Quite all right," Hale said, already dismissing him, already turning back toward the dining room and the woman in the amber dress.

The theft took three seconds. Kestrel walked out the front door with a fistful of someone else's nineteen years glowing warm against his sternum, and behind him Corvin Hale stopped mid-stride, frowned faintly at the woman in amber, felt—for just a moment—that something lovely was slightly out of reach, and then ordered another bottle of wine and went home to his wife.

That was how it should go. Clean. Painless. A subtraction so gentle the sum never noticed.

Kestrel told himself that four times on the walk to Ropewalk Stair, which was three times more than usual, and he knew what the extra telling meant. He was getting tired. Tiredness made the trade feel like what it was instead of what he pretended it was. He ate a roasted chestnut from a brazier to occupy his mouth so it couldn't keep apologizing.

* * *

The gray market lived in the Hollows, under the ribs of an aqueduct the city had abandoned two hundred years ago and never officially noticed it still depended on. By day it was a fog of tarps and pigeon coops. After the ninth bell it became something else: stalls lit by fat candles, vendors selling charms of luck and forgetting and borrowed courage, and above it all the low stone arches sweating moisture that caught the light and dripped like slow rain.

Kestrel moved through it the way he moved everywhere—present, then absent. He nodded to the eel-woman. He stepped around the dream-peddlers, whose product competed with his and whose quality was criminal. And he came, at last, to the stall at the far end, where Odile sat behind a curtain of hanging keys that chimed when customers parted them, though no customer ever touched them by accident twice.

Odile was seventy if she was a day, blind in one eye, sharp as a paper cut in both. She took the grief-charm first, held it to her good eye, turned it.

"Mm. Widow's sorrow, the hourly kind." She set it on black velvet. "Clean work. You've gotten better at leaving the face."

"I always leave the face."

"You left a thumbprint on the edges last month. Charmseller in Saltrow asked me whose grief this was because he swore he recognized the coat it smelled like." Her good eye rose to him. "You are getting careless, kitten."

"I'm getting tired," Kestrel said, which was truer and therefore worse, and Odile received it with the flat non-surprise of a woman who had heard every confession in the city and filed them all alphabetically.

She counted out coin for the first charm, held the second—the first love—to the light, and made a sound low in her throat that might have been appreciation or indigestion.

"Twenty years old, this one. Kept sweet. Merchant?"

"Hale. Corvin."

"Hm." She paid for it too, and Kestrel watched his week's living vanish into his coat, and hated, as he always hated, how little other people's hearts went for. A grief: nine marks. A love: fourteen. Enough to eat. Not enough to stop.

"You have a visitor's question waiting," Odile said, as he turned to go.

"I don't like the sound of that."

"No. You wouldn't." She sorted her keys, not looking at him, which was how Odile looked at you hardest. "Someone came asking after charms of local make. Fine quality, she said. The kind sold by a thief who works Lantern Row and the supper clubs and never leaves a name." A pause, keys clicking. "She described your hands."

Kestrel's mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with fear of wardens. Wardens didn't ask. Wardens kicked doors.

"Described them how?"

"Slender. Careful. A pale scar across the right palm." The keys went silent. "I told her I sell trinkets to fools, kitten, and she smiled at me with all her teeth and left a silver mark on the counter anyway. Silver. Nobody pays silver for nothing unless they're buying the answer they didn't get."

He made himself breathe. His right palm itched inside its glove, and he caught himself before he scratched it, before he acknowledged it, because acknowledging things was how things got noticed.

"Wardens?" he asked.

"Wardens don't smile," Odile said. "This one smiled."

He walked home the long way, doubling back twice along the canal, watching reflections for followers. The Hollows' candles thinned behind him. Overhead, past the aqueduct's broken arches, the night sky hung low and hazy, and Vell's border wards hummed their constant note at the edge of hearing—the sound every citizen of the city stopped hearing at age three and never quite stopped feeling, the hum that meant *safe*, that meant *contained*, that meant the dark beyond the walls stayed politely outside.

Kestrel climbed to his rooms above a shuttered dyeworks, barred the door, and lit one candle.

Then he pulled off his right glove.

The sigil sat in his palm as it always had: a knotwork star, fine as engraving, old as his earliest memory—or rather, occupying the place where his earliest memory should have been, because there was a Yara-shaped hole in his childhood and he had learned, over five long years, not to look directly at it. Five years since she'd pressed this mark into his flesh and vanished. Five years of silence from the thing in his hand.

He stared at it now, thinking of silver coins and smiles with all their teeth, and told himself the familiar lie: *no one knows. No one has ever known.*

The sigil flared.

Heat shot up his arm like a struck bell rung through bone, and Kestrel bit down on his own wrist to keep from crying out. The light leaking between his fingers wasn't firelight and wasn't candlelight; it was the color of the sky at the exact moment before snow, and inside the glare—for less than a heartbeat, less than the time it takes a door to close—he saw her face.

Yara's face. Older than he remembered. Frightened in a way she had never once been frightened in front of him.

And she was saying something. Her lips moved around words the light swallowed whole, but the shape of them was unmistakable, the shape every frightened mouth makes in every language ever spoken:

*Run.*

Then darkness. The candle still burning. His own ragged breath. The wards' distant hum, steady as ever.

Kestrel sat very still in the small room above the dyeworks, holding his burning hand like a man holding a bird that had flown back from the dead, and somewhere across the city, though he could not yet know it, something ancient at the edge of Vell's borders shuddered in its long sleep—and began, softly, to crack.
