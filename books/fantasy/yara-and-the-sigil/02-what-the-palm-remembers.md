## 2. What the Palm Remembers

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Kestrel could move.

He sat in the narrow room above the dyeworks with his palm turned up toward the guttering light, watching the sigil the way a man watches a snake he thought was dead. It lay quiet now, that silvered knot of lines in the meat of his hand, faint as an old scar, ordinary as anything. It had been ordinary for five years. Five years of forgetting to remember it, which is a skill like any other, and he had been very good at it.

Then it had burned. Then she had come.

*Run,* her mouth had said, and the word still hung in him like smoke in a closed room, settling into his clothes, his hair, the back of his throat.

He flexed his fingers. Nothing. The skin was cool, faintly ridged where the lines crossed, and if he pressed his thumb against the center—as he did not, as he absolutely did not—it would feel like pressing a bruise left by a blow he couldn't recall taking.

"Five years," he said aloud, to hear something human in the room. The candle flame leaned as if listening. "You had five good years. You absolute—"

He didn't finish the sentence. Talking to the mark felt too much like talking to her, and he had promised himself at nineteen that he was done with that particular way of going mad.

Outside, Vell was doing what Vell always did after midnight: settling. The dyeworks below exhaled its sour smell of alum and old madder through the floorboards. Somewhere east, the border wards hummed their one long note, so constant that no one in the city heard it anymore except in the moments before sleep, when all the sounds you'd stopped noticing came back at once. Kestrel had learned to take comfort in that hum. Wards meant walls. Walls meant rules, and rules meant there were ways to live inside them without being seen.

He wrapped his hand in a strip of linen—not because anyone would see the mark, but because covering it was a habit older than his ability to explain it, and habits were the only family he had kept.

There was work to do, and work did not ask questions.

* * *

The widow Maren lived on Candlewick Row in a house that smelled of tallow and stopped clocks. She was ninety if she was a day, and she had been sitting up waiting for him, because widows who wanted to sell always sat up waiting, dressed in their best, with tea gone cold beside them, as though what they were about to do were not selling at all but something closer to a funeral arrangement.

"You'll be careful with it," she said. Not a question.

"I'm always careful." Kestrel sat across from her and set his hands flat on his knees, linen wrap hidden beneath his sleeve. "Tell me the rules before we start. Say them out loud. It makes the memory sit still."

She nodded, gathering herself. "Only the night of the funeral. Only him. Not the forty years before. And—" Her voice caught on the hook of her own grief, and she steadied it the way old women do, with practice. "And you leave me the part where he laughed. I don't care what happens to the rest, but I keep the laughing."

"That's not how it works," Kestrel said gently. "I can't cut around a piece in the middle. But—" He watched her face begin to close, and hurried on. "But I can copy instead of lift. It costs you nothing but a headache and me a great deal more, and you'll keep everything. The grief too."

For a moment he hoped, the small hopeless way he hoped whenever someone chose the harder kindness for themselves.

The widow Maren looked at her cold tea and shook her head. "Take it out. All of it but the laughing, if you can't manage even that. I have carried him thirty years, young man. I would like to know what it is to set him down."

So he took it. He took the funeral and the illness before it and the terrible ordinary morning when she had found him still beside her, and he took it carefully, the way you draw a splinter—slow, along the grain, with the whole of your attention on the place where flesh resists. Grief comes out heavy. It has weight the way water has weight, and it does not want to leave the body that has grown around its shape.

She wept while he worked, quietly, without drama, tears running into the seams of her face, and when it was done she blinked at him with wet, astonished eyes and said, "Oh. Oh, it's *quiet* in here," and touched her temple like a woman hearing silence after decades of bells.

"The laughing?" he asked.

She frowned, reaching. "There was... he laughed at our wedding. In the rain. I remember rain."

"That's what's left of it," Kestrel said, hating himself in the old familiar way, the low-grade hatred of a man who has gotten good at necessary cruelty. "It grows back, sometimes. Memories want to regrow around what's missing. Give it a year."

She paid him in coin and half a fruitcake and did not remember, by the time he reached the door, why her eyes were red.

* * *

The merchant's first love cost him less. First loves are shallow water; they come away clean, barely bleeding. Aldous Vey, cloth factor, forty-two years old and bored to marrow-rot with his own success, wanted to *feel* something again, and had heard from a friend of a friend that there was a boy in the Sallow Quarter who could sell you your own youth back—and better than that, could sell you someone else's.

"It was a girl named Iselle," Vey said, lying back on his own chaise in his own study among his ledgers and his leaded glass. "I was seventeen. She was a cooper's daughter. Nothing happened, that's the thing, nothing ever happened, and then her family moved downriver and I married Prudence, and I have wondered for twenty-five years what would have happened if—"

"If," Kestrel agreed, and put two fingers to the man's temple, and drew.

Iselle came out bright as a new coin. Sunlight on a river dock. The smell of sawdust and tar. A girl with flour on her forearms laughing at some joke whose words dissolved in the extraction but whose laughter stayed, intact, perfect, a little bell of a thing. Kestrel sealed it in wax the color of dusk, pressed the sigil-hand lightly to the surface—never the marked palm itself, never the bare mark, only the backs of two fingers, a superstition he maintained with religious rigor—and murmured the binding under his breath.

Vey would wear the charm for a month, feel seventeen again in stolen increments, and eventually the wax would thin and the memory would fade back toward wherever sold memories go, and he would pay through the nose to renew it, and this was the business, this was the whole gray engine of it.

Kestrel had three rules. He recited them to himself on the stairs out of Vey's house, the way other men said prayers. Never steal a whole life—a life leaves a hollow shaped like a person, and hollow people are noticed. Never sell a memory to the one who lived it twice running—the mind learns the taste of theft. And never, ever, be remembered.

Three rules, five years, zero mistakes. He was twenty-four years old and the best in Vell at being no one, and if there were nights when the competence curdled into something colder—if there were nights when he climbed through his own window and sat in the dark and understood with total clarity that no one in this city of eighty thousand souls could pick him out of a lineup of one—he told himself that invisibility was safety, and safety was the thing, the only thing.

His palm itched under the linen.

He ignored it. He was, as established, very good at ignoring things.

* * *

Odile kept her shop behind a chandler's on Rope Walk, down a stair that smelled of tallow and river rot, behind a door with no sign on it that opened for those who knocked in the pattern. Inside it was warm and gold-lit and crammed to the ceiling with the merchandise of other people's pasts: charms strung on copper wire like strange fruit, jars of pressed sensations labeled in Odile's crabbed hand (*the smell of your mother's house*, one said; another, *being forgiven*), and, in the back room nobody entered, a locked case for the things Odile bought but could not sell, which Kestrel had learned early not to ask about.

"You look like something a cat brought in and regretted," Odile said by way of greeting. She was sixty, sharp-faced, iron-gray hair pinned with whatever was nearest—a pencil tonight—and she appraised everything in the world, including him, with the same expression: mild interest tempered by the certainty that it was worth less than claimed.

"Widow's grief," Kestrel said, setting the sealed jar on her counter. "Deep vintage. Thirty years' steep. Funeral-grade."

Odile held it up to her lamp. Something in the light moved inside the wax, slow, like a fish under ice. Her eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch, which from Odile was applause.

"And this?"

"Cloth man's first love. Shallow, but sweet. It'll move fast."

"Mm." She set both aside and poured two cups of the chicory swill she insisted was coffee, pushing one at him, and Kestrel understood, with the small cold dropping sensation of a floorboard giving way, that Odile only fed him when something was wrong.

"You've heard something," he said.

"I've heard things." She drank, watching him over the rim. "I've heard, for instance, that a certain warden-sergeant with expensive boots has been in twice this week asking after charm-stock. Not confiscating. *Asking.* Taking notes on provenance. That's not how a purge starts; purges start with fire. That's how a hunt starts. They're mapping the trade."

"They map it every winter. It's tradition. They draw their charts, we move our stalls, spring comes."

"This isn't charting." Odile set her cup down with a click precise as a lock turning. "This is a man following a thread. And here's the second thing, and you're going to hold very still while I say it, because I've been deciding all evening whether to say it at all."

Kestrel held very still.

"There's been a stranger in the market. Three days now. Well-dressed, gray coat, keeps his gloves on indoors, which in my experience means either a lord or a leper or a liar, and he's none of the first two. He's not buying stock. He's buying *talk*. And the talk he's buying—" She paused, and he realized Odile, who bargained with death itself without blinking, was reluctant. "The question he's asking, the question he's paying silver for, is whether anyone knows the whereabouts of a thief who steals with his hands. A thief who works memory. And here's the phrase that made me pour you coffee, boy: he asks after *the marked thief*. Marked how, you think? Marked where?"

The chicory in Kestrel's cup had gone cold, or perhaps he had; the two were difficult to distinguish. Under the linen, against every law of his personal religion, his palm began—faintly, horribly—to warm.

"Gray coat," he said. His voice came out level, which he counted as the day's single triumph. "Gloves?"

"Gloves. Young face. Old eyes. The combination I don't trust." Odile leaned forward. "Now you're going to tell me why my shop got ten degrees colder when I said the word *marked*, and why you've been wearing linen on your right hand for two days, because I count, Kestrel. Counting is my whole profession."

"There's nothing to tell."

"Wrong answer. Try the true one; they sound different."

He looked at her. Twenty years of fences and smugglers and liars, and Odile remained the only person in Vell who had ever asked him a true question and waited, without threat, for the true answer. It was the closest thing to love he had experienced since—

Since the burning. Since the flash of her face. Since *run.*

"If I show you something," he said slowly, "you will forget it immediately afterward, because I am going to make you forget it, and you will hate me, and then you will thank me. Agreed?"

Odile's eyes narrowed to knife-cuts. "Agreed," she said, in the tone of a woman agreeing to nothing forever.

He unwound the linen.

The sigil lay open to the lamplight, silvered lines in the cup of his palm, and it should have been nothing—an old scar, a birthmark, a bit of ink from a misspent youth. But the lamp-flame bent toward it. Just slightly, just unmistakably, the way grass bends toward water under the ground, and Odile went very still, and Kestrel watched thirty years of professional composure fail on her face one layer at a time.

"That's not possible," she said. "That mark. I've seen that mark. In the old days, before your time, in the *back room* stock—there are charms in that case, boy, sealed thirty years, that carry the edge of that exact—"

"Stop." His fingers were already moving, two fingertips to her temple, gentle as setting a bone. "Thank you for the coffee. The soup was fine. We talked about wardens and weather. Sleep on it."

The extraction of an hour's conversation was nothing; it came away soft as bread. He sealed it in plain gray wax, crushed it, swept the crumbs into his pocket. Odile blinked, refocused mid-sentence onto a different sentence entirely, and continued smoothly:

"—and that's why you never buy stock off a man who names his prices in rhymes. Now get out, it's late, and take my advice with you: whoever's hunting a marked thief, don't be home when he calls. You have a bolt-hole?"

"I have several."

"Sleep in the farthest one." She turned back to her shelves, dismissing him, and then, with her back still to him, added quietly: "You've been the closest thing I have to a son these five years, Kestrel, which is a sentence I apparently decided to say just now for no reason at all. Get out of my shop."

He got.

* * *

Rope Walk at three in the morning was a corridor of fog and mooring-smell, lamps haloed in damp, the river somewhere to his left making its endless argument with the pilings. Kestrel walked the long way, the way he'd taught himself years ago: no straight lines, doubled-back turns, a mirror-check at the cooper's yard. No one followed. No one ever followed. Being no one had that advantage.

But the palm burned now. Not the flicker-flash of two nights ago, not the sudden white heat and the face and *run*—this was slower, lower, a coal refusing to die, warming the linen wrap like a held coin. And with the warmth came images that were not memories, because he had never owned them: a doorway of black stone standing alone in a field of grass that moved against the wind; nine stars in a pattern no sky above Vell had ever held; a voice saying words in a language that his ears refused but his bones understood, and the bones said, clearly, in the plain speech of marrow:

*It is starting again. You are late. You are five years late.*

"No," Kestrel said to the fog, to the river, to the mark. "Whatever this is, no. I didn't agree to anything. Whatever she—" His voice cracked and he shut his mouth on the rest and walked faster.

He turned onto Sallow Street and stopped.

Across the way, half in shadow between two lamp-posts, stood a figure in a gray coat. Gloves. A young face beneath the hood, turned toward him with the mild patience of a man who has been waiting exactly as long as it suited him to wait.

"Well," the stranger said pleasantly, into the fog and the river-sound and Kestrel's entire collapsing world. "There you are. I've been looking everywhere for the man with the mark."

And in Kestrel's wrapped palm, the sigil flared silver through the linen, bright enough to throw the shadows of his own finger-bones up the wall behind him—a beacon, a banner, a lit window in a dark street—while somewhere beyond the city's humming wards, at the edge of the world, something ancient shifted its weight, and the first crack widened, and the hum changed note.
