## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards sang at dusk.

Most people in Vell had stopped hearing them years ago—the way you stop hearing a river outside your window, or your own heartbeat—but I never had. Maybe it was because of what lived in my palm. Maybe it was because I'd spent five years listening for anything that might mean the world was about to notice me. Either way, every evening when the sun dropped behind the Spireward Hills and the border wards took up their watch, I heard it: a low harmonic thrumming through the cobblestones, through the bones of the houses, through the fillings in my teeth. The sound of a city saying *mine* to everything inside its walls and *none* to everything beyond.

That night, the song was wrong.

I noticed it first as a sourness, a note bent flat, like a lute string someone had loosened while the player wasn't looking. I was crossing Tanner's Row with a dead man's last good memory folded into a wax charm in my coat pocket—a ferryman who'd wanted to forget his brother's drowning face, and had paid me half his savings to take it—and I stopped mid-stride, one boot on wet cobbles, and listened.

The ward-song stuttered.

Then it caught again, thinner, like a candle flame guttering in a draft.

Around me, the evening crowd flowed past without pausing. Fishwives hauling their barrows home. Apprentices with ink-stained fingers and cheap wine on their breath. A lamplighter working his way down the row with his long brass pole, flame to wick, flame to wick, each lamp blooming gold against the gathering dark. None of them heard what I heard. None of them ever did. That was the arrangement of my life: I heard everything, and no one heard me.

I told myself it was nothing. Wards hiccuped sometimes—old magic, older than the city, and even old magic got indigestion. I told myself that all the way down Tanner's Row, all the way across Gullmarket Square where the gray market was setting up its nightly stalls behind shuttered fronts, all the way to the crooked doorway of Odile's shop, where I meant to sell the ferryman's grief before it went stale in my pocket.

I was halfway through haggling her up from twelve silver to fifteen when the sky broke.

* * *

It began as a sound I will hear until I die, however long that turns out to be. Not thunder. Thunder rolls; this shattered. It was the sound of glass breaking, if the glass were the size of the horizon—if some enormous hand had closed around the dome of the world and squeezed until everything crystalline in it gave way at once. It came from everywhere. It came from below the ground and above the clouds and inside my own skull, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the ward-song rose to a single sustained shriek and then

stopped.

Silence rushed into the space where the song had been, and silence, I learned that night, has a sound of its own. It sounds like falling.

The lamps in Odile's shop flickered—not dimmed, exactly, but *hesitated*, as if the flames themselves were unsure whether they were still permitted to burn. Odile's hand froze over her coin drawer. She was a small woman, sixty-odd, with a face like a walnut and eyes that had seen every kind of trouble Vell could brew and priced most of it. In the thirty years she'd run her shop of mislabeled curiosities and unlabeled charms, I had never once seen her look afraid.

She looked afraid now.

"Kestrel," she said quietly. "What was that?"

"I don't know."

And then, because the truth arrived a half-second later and I have never been able to stop my mouth from telling it: "The wards. The border wards just fell."

Odile crossed herself with two fingers, an old habit from a country neither of us had ever seen. "Fell? Wards don't fall, boy. They're woven into the bedrock. They've stood since before the city had a name."

"They fell." My voice came out flat and certain, and I hated how certain it was. "I felt it go through the floor."

We went to the door together. Above the rooftops of Gullmarket, above the chimney smoke and the gulls wheeling home to the harbor cliffs, the sky was doing something the sky does not do.

The stars were going out.

Not fading—*going out*, one by one, in no order I could read, like windows being shuttered by unseen hands in a house the size of heaven. First the bright ones, then the middling ones, then whole constellations winking away between one breath and the next, until there was nothing left overhead but black. Not cloud-black. Clouds have texture, depth, the gray suggestion of something behind them. This was a black with nothing behind it at all. A black like the inside of a closed eye. A black like the moment before you're born or after you die.

The moon lasted longest. It hung there fat and coppery over the harbor, the last lit thing in the heavens, and then it thinned like a coin rubbed between fingers, and then it was gone too, and Vell stood beneath a sky with no stars in it for the first time in the memory of anyone living.

Somewhere out in the square, a woman began to scream. Then another. Then, like the ward-song's death had opened a door in everyone at once, the whole city began to scream, or pray, or both.

* * *

Panic moves through a city the way water moves through cloth—capillary, unstoppable, finding every seam. Within minutes Gullmarket Square was a single heaving organism of torchlight and shouting. Stalls overturned. A wine merchant's cart went up in flames when someone knocked a lantern into it, and nobody even tried to put it out; fire seemed suddenly like the smallest of the night's problems. People pressed toward the temple steps clutching children, coins, chickens, whatever their hands happened to be holding when the sky died. A man knelt in the middle of the chaos, naked to the waist despite the cold, beating his chest and crying out that the gods had blinked and found us wanting.

And through it all, cutting against the current like knives through wool, came the wardens.

They moved in squads of six, lantern-poles high, white sashes luminous in the torchlight. The Inquisition didn't panic. Panic was for people with something to lose besides orders. I watched them fan out along the square's edges with the practiced geometry of men who had drilled for exactly this—not this catastrophe specifically, but *unrest*, the general animal fear of crowds—and I watched them start locking things down. Gates. Bridges. The harbor chain would already be rising, I knew; I'd seen it raised once during the plague year, and the groan of it had haunted my sleep for a week.

"Curfew," a warden bellowed near Odile's door, voice amplified by a speaking-trumpet etched with suppression runes. "By decree of the Grand Inquisitor: return to your homes. Doors barred by the ninth bell. Anyone abroad after curfew is subject to questioning." He paused, and the pause was worse than the words. "Questioning may include examination for unlicensed arts."

Unlicensed arts. Four words that had always hung over Vell like weather, and tonight they had teeth.

I drew back from the doorway. My heart was doing something complicated and unpleasant against my ribs.

"Boy." Odile's hand closed around my wrist—around the wrist just below the palm I kept wrapped, always, in linen bandages I claimed were burns from a forge accident. Her grip was astonishingly strong. She was looking at me with those walnut-face eyes, and what was in them now wasn't fear of the sky. It was fear for me. "Your hand," she said. "Has it done anything tonight?"

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the bedrock, all the way down to wherever the fallen wards had gone.

Because here is the thing I hadn't said. Here is the thing I'd been carrying since the shriek and the silence, since the first star winked out like a snuffed candle:

The sigil had flared.

At the exact moment the ward-song died—at the precise instant, not a breath before or after—the mark burned into my left palm had ignited like a coal blown upon. White-hot, searing, alive. And in the half-second of that burning, I hadn't just felt pain. I'd *seen*. A flash behind my eyes: a door, vast and dark, standing open somewhere beneath the world. Light pouring out of it like water from a cracked dam. And a voice—or not a voice, but the memory of a voice, five years buried—saying words I couldn't quite catch, in a tone I knew better than my own.

Yara's tone.

"No," I lied to Odile. "Nothing. It's just a scar."

She held my gaze for a long three seconds, and I understood that she didn't believe me, and that she loved me enough to let the lie stand. "Get off the streets," she said instead. "Not your room—they'll sweep the tenements by midnight, they always do. Take the back way to the tannery loft. And Kestrel—" she pressed the fifteen silver into my hand, closing my fingers over the ferryman's payment I'd somehow forgotten to collect, "—whatever's coming, don't let them see the palm. You hear me? Whatever happens to this city, that comes first."

"It's just a scar," I said again, softer.

"Of course it is," said Odile, and turned back to bolt her shutters, and did not look at me again.

* * *

I went the long way, because the short ways were full of wardens.

Vell without starlight was a city I didn't recognize. Every street I'd memorized by the shape of rooftops against the sky was suddenly illegible; every shadow had lost its anchor. I moved by touch and memory and the low-grade animal cunning that had kept me fed and free since I was eleven years old, hugging walls, slipping through gaps in the panicked crowds, breathing through my mouth so the smoke wouldn't choke me. Twice I passed warden patrols close enough to count the buttons on their coats. Both times I became furniture. It's a skill I'd refined over years—shoulders rounding, face slackening, eyes going dull, the whole architecture of a person rearranging itself into something too boring to remember. Never steal a whole life. Never be remembered. Those were my rules, and rule two had kept me alive through every purge and roundup the Inquisition had ever conducted.

But tonight, under the starless sky, with the sigil still radiating a faint heat through its linen wrappings like a banked fire, rule two felt less like a discipline and more like a countdown.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing, walking through the dying light of Gullmarket: the wards had fallen at the exact moment the sigil flared. Not approximately. Exactly. Five years that mark had slept quiet as a stone, and the night it woke was the night the sky went black. Coincidence was a currency I'd traded in for most of my life, and I knew counterfeit when I held it.

Something had happened to the wards. Something had happened to the stars. And the same something had reached across whatever distance separated them from my left hand and *lit me up like a signal fire*.

Which meant that whatever was coming for Vell—Inquisition lockdown, Thorn Court, gods, devils, the plain mechanical collapse of ancient magic—it already knew where to look. Or would soon. A flare like that, in a city full of wardens with detection runes and suppression trumpets and a Grand Inquisitor who'd built her career on finding people exactly like me?

For five years I had survived by being no one. Tonight, for the first time, I understood that being no one was no longer on offer. Somewhere in the dark above the broken wards, or in the dark below the broken streets, something had said my name—or rather, had said the name written in my skin, which was not mine, which had never been mine, which belonged to a woman who had walked out of my life and taken the explanation with her.

I reached the tannery loft a little past the ninth bell. The stairs smelled of lime and rot and the particular sourness of fear-sweat that had soaked into the building from a hundred hiding fugitives before me. I barred the door, wedged a chair under the handle, and sat down on the floor with my back to the wall because I didn't trust my legs.

Through the loft's one grimy window, the starless sky pressed down on the city like a lid.

And in my palm, under the linen, the sigil pulsed. Slowly now. Patiently. Like a second heartbeat. Like something that had waited five years and could wait a little longer, but not forever.

Like a key that had just felt its lock turn somewhere far away.

I unwound the bandage. I know—I could give you a dozen reasons, and none of them would be true. The truth is I had to look. Some part of me had to confirm that the flash I'd seen, the door, the light, the voice, hadn't been terror playing tricks.

The sigil lay on my palm exactly as it always had: a spiral of interlocking lines, fine as spider silk, dark as old ink, in a pattern no scholar had ever matched and no warden had ever seen. Except—

Except that tonight, faintly, in the deep black of the starless night, it was glowing. A pale silver shimmer, barely brighter than breath on cold glass. And as I watched, the glow pulsed once, twice, and settled into a rhythm.

A rhythm I recognized, because I'd felt it under my ear once, years ago, falling asleep against someone's shoulder in a house that no longer existed.

The sigil was beating in time with a heart. And the heart, whatever else had happened to it, whatever distance or darkness or death lay between us, was still beating too.

Outside, boots on cobblestones. A patrol, moving slow, checking doors. I wrapped the palm, killed the candle, and sat in the dark listening to my own name approach down the street—one I'd never given them, spelled in a language only one person had ever known.

The knock, when it came, was polite. That was the worst part.

"Open in the name of the Inquisition," said a voice through the door. "We know you're awake. We saw the light."
