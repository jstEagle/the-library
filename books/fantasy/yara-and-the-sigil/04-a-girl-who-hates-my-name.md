## 4. A Girl Who Hates My Name

I had been waiting in the alley for three nights, and by the third night I had stopped hating myself for it.

That was a lie. I hated myself constantly, efficiently, the way my mother used to fold linen—sharp corners, no wasted motion. I hated that I'd spent my coin on this city instead of leaving it while the gates still worked. I hated that the stars had gone out and nobody in Vell seemed to know how to be afraid properly; they just milled about in the dark with lanterns held too high, like the light was a question and the sky owed them an answer. Most of all I hated the compass.

The compass sat in my palm now, half of it, cracked down the middle like a skull someone had split to look inside. It had been my mother's. It had never once pointed north, not even when she was alive to lie about it. It pointed at things she wanted found. For five years after she vanished it had pointed nowhere at all—the needle lying flat, dead, sulking—and then, four nights ago, it had twitched like a sleeping man hearing his name.

It pointed down a narrow street off Tanner's Row, through a door with a bad lock, at a man who sold grief in glass vials.

I knew what he looked like before I ever saw him. Mother's journal had one page with writing that wasn't ciphered, just scrawled fast and angry, ink torn through paper: *If anything happens to me, find Delacroix. He has what matters.* And beneath that, smaller, as if added later against her own will: *He is not what you think. Neither am I.*

I'd had five years to imagine him. In most versions he was older, soft, a fat spider sitting in a web of other people's stolen hours. In none of them did he have hands like that—quick, careful, a thief's hands but gentle ones—and in none of them, when I finally saw him through the crack in his shutters, candlelight on his face while he talked to himself like a man rehearsing an argument he kept losing, did he look *afraid* in a way that made my chest hurt.

So I hated him extra for that.

* * *

He ran from the knock. Of course he ran. I watched him come out the back of the building into the alley where I stood, moving quiet as spilled water, wrapped palm tucked against his ribs, and I thought: *there he is, the reason my mother is gone, and he runs from wardens the way a rat runs from fire, and he doesn't even run beautifully.*

I put the knife to his throat.

To be fair to me, I meant to say something better than what came out. I had rehearsed. I had lines prepared, cold ones, questions shaped like knives. What came out was:

"You're smaller than I thought."

His whole body went still—not the frozen terror of a man with steel at his neck, but something worse, something practiced, a stillness he could hold for minutes if he had to. His eyes slid sideways toward my voice without turning his head. Even cornered, he was doing the arithmetic of escape.

"Then you have the advantage," he said. His voice was low, roughened, the voice of a man who hadn't spoken aloud all day. "Because I don't know you."

"No," I said. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point of you."

The knife pressed a bead of warmth to his skin. He didn't swallow, didn't flinch, which made me angrier. Men should flinch. Men should know they were going to die and behave accordingly.

"Kestrel Delacroix," I said, and watched the name land on him like a blow he'd been expecting for years. Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, yes, but under it, guilt with a specific shape. Guilt with a woman's shape. Good, I thought. Let it hurt.

"And you're her daughter." He said it quietly. Not a guess. The compass in my pocket chose that exact moment to shift against my hip, its needle swinging, and I realized with a sick lurch that it wasn't pointing at him from across the room anymore. It was pointing straight up through my own coat, through my own hand holding the knife, at the throat beneath the blade. Pointing so hard it trembled.

"You knew about me."

"I knew she had a daughter she talked to the way people talk about the sea." A muscle jumped in his jaw. "Sable. She said your laugh sounded like a door opening somewhere good."

"Don't." The word tore coming out. "Don't you dare quote her to me. You don't get to carry her around in your mouth like a charm you're saving to sell."

"If I wanted to sell her," he said softly, "I'd have done it years ago. Her memories would fetch more than everything else I've ever taken combined."

I turned the blade. Just enough. Enough to make him understand the conversation had ended.

"Five years," I said. "She walked out of our house on a Tuesday. She kissed my forehead and told me to mind the stove, which she never did, because she was the kind of mother who forgot stoves and remembered the names of every star in the sky, and she walked out, and she never came back, and the last person to see her—" I leaned close, close enough to smell lamp oil and fear-sweat and something underneath it, faintly sweet, like the air before lightning, "—was a thief with a burn scar on his hand. So here's how tonight goes. You tell me what happened, or I open your throat, and then I take whatever's in your pockets, because I've been eating hard bread for a week and I'm told grief sells well in this city. Maybe I'll sell yours."

For the first time, he flinched.

Small. Barely a breath of movement. But I'd grown up watching my mother read people the way scholars read dead languages, and she'd taught me without meaning to, and I saw it: the flinch wasn't at the knife. It was at *sell your grief*. Somewhere behind those gray eyes a ledger turned a page, and the entry was ugly.

"There's no memory of that night to sell," he said. "She took it. That's what she did. She took things and left nothing, that was always—" He stopped himself. Closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "You want the truth? Here's the truth. I woke up three streets from the harbor with a hole in my head where six months used to be and her mark burned into my palm, and I have spent five years trying to decide whether she saved my life or ruined it, and I don't know, and I'm sorry, but I don't *know*, and if you kill me you'll be killing the only other person in Vell who loved her badly enough to still be angry about it."

The alley went very quiet. Somewhere above us the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

My hand shook. I hated that most of all.

"She marked you," I said. "She gave you the sigil."

"She gave me something," he said. "Whether the mark is a gift or a leash depends on the day."

And then the night broke open.

* * *

Boots first. Many boots, moving in the coordinated rhythm I'd learned to dread in the last four days—wardens, a full file of them, sweeping the alleys off Tanner's Row with lanterns hooded except at the seams, spilling thin blades of light along the wet cobbles. Since the stars died, the Inquisition had tripled the patrols. They said it was to prevent panic. Everyone in Vell knew it was to prevent *questions*, because panic asks small questions—where do we go, what do we eat—and questions ask big ones, like *what did the wards actually hold back, and who built them, and why did the Inquisition swear up and down until Tuesday that they'd hold forever.*

"Contact!" A voice, ahead and to the left. Light bloomed.

Kestrel moved before I finished deciding what I was going to do. That's the thing I'll never fully forgive or forget—he grabbed my wrist, the knife-hand, and instead of fighting him I let him pull me, because his grip was certain and the light was coming and some animal part of my brain had already decided to trust the thief my mother trusted, and I despised that animal part immediately.

He shoved us both into a recess behind a rain barrel, into shadow and the smell of rot and old tallow, and pressed his wrapped palm flat against the wall.

"What are you—" I started.

"Hush," he breathed. "Or pray. Whichever works faster."

The light swung into our alley. Three wardens, then a fourth, lanterns high, and I felt Kestrel's hand change against mine—not gripping anymore, doing something, *humming* something, a vibration too low to hear and impossible not to feel, like standing near a struck bell. The wall beside us seemed to thicken. The darkness deepened from shadow into something with weight and intent, a pocket of not-being-seen, and I understood with a jolt that this was what my mother had died protecting or fled from or both: not tricks. This. A small, deliberate miracle, performed by a man crouched in filth with a stranger's wrist in his hand.

The wardens' light passed over us. Passed *through* us. One of them paused, frowned at the rain barrel, kicked it lightly, and moved on, and the sound of the boots faded toward Tanner's Row like a tide going out.

We stayed crouched there a long moment anyway. His breathing was ragged. Mine matched it without permission.

"That was blood magic," I said. It wasn't an accusation. It came out almost reverent, and I hated myself for the reverence. "Hiding. That's forbidden art."

"So is everything I do before breakfast." He released my wrist carefully, the way you'd set down something borrowed. In the thin light I saw him flex the wrapped hand, wince. Whatever he'd just done had cost him. "Your turn to talk fast, since we're apparently having a night together. You followed me. How?"

I should have lied. Instead I pulled out the compass.

It lay in my palm between us, half-moon of cracked brass, and the needle strained toward him so violently it chattered against the glass like a trapped insect. He stared at it, and I watched his face do several things quickly and settle on grief, and I thought: *you recognized it. You know whose it was.*

"Her finder-compass," he said hoarsely. "She used it to locate lost things. Lost *people*." He laughed, a short broken sound with no humor in it. "It's pointing at me."

"It's been pointing at you for four days. Since the stars went out." I closed my fist around it, needle biting skin. "Which means either you have something she lost—"

"—or I'm something she lost," he finished.

The silence between us filled up with rain that hadn't started falling yet, that heavy waiting air, and I looked at this man—this thief, this liar by trade, this keeper of my mother's mark—and felt the fury in me collide with something else, something worse, which was the terrible suspicion that he might be telling the truth, and that the truth was worse than betrayal. Betrayal I knew how to carry. Betrayal had edges. This was just a hole, the same hole Tuesday had left in me five years ago, wearing a stranger's face.

"Why would she hide your memory?" I asked, and my voice came out younger than I wanted. "Why would she take your six months and give you her sigil and walk out and leave *me*? If she trusted you, if she—why am I finding out my own mother's secrets from a cutpurse in an alley?"

"I don't know." He said it simply, and it rang true, and that was somehow the cruelest thing anyone had said to me in years. "But I think I know where the answer is."

He unwrapped his palm.

I'd seen the sigil in the journal—a sketch, hurried, annotated in cipher. Seeing it alive was different. It sat in the center of his hand like a brand that had decided to become a constellation: lines fine as hair, arranged in a spiral that hurt to follow with your eye, because the eye kept wanting to fall inward. And it was glowing. Faintly, unsteadily, pulsing with that rhythm I'd already learned to recognize from the needle of the compass—a heartbeat's rhythm, slow and stubborn, keeping time with something far away.

"The night the wards fell," Kestrel said, "this lit up for the first time in five years. Right at the moment the stars went out. It's not a coincidence, Sable. Nothing about her was ever a coincidence. She planned like weather systems." He closed his fingers over the light, muffling it. "There's a place under Vell. I've heard whispers in the market—wardens digging, sappers being paid in gold to keep quiet. Something old under the city. I think the wards weren't walls. I think they were a lid. And I think my hand is the key to whatever's under it."

"Or a trap," I said.

"Those aren't mutually exclusive. That was rather her specialty."

Against every instinct, against five years of rage and a week of hunger and the simple human desire to stop hurting, I laughed. It escaped before I could catch it—one short, startled sound—and I saw him register it, saw something flicker across his face that looked unbearably like recognition, like my mother's laugh had lived on somewhere in him and had just come home.

I put the knife away. Not because I forgave him. Because the compass had gone quiet in my pocket, resting, the way it used to rest in my mother's hand when it had found what it was looking for.

"Truce," I said. "Until I decide otherwise. You don't touch my memories—I know what you do, I've seen your charms, I know exactly what you are. And you don't lie to me. Not even kindly."

"Sable." He said my name carefully, testing its weight, and I realized he was the only living person besides me who'd heard it in her voice. "Kind lies are the only kind I know. But for her—for you—I'll learn the other kind."

Above the alley, where stars should have been, the sky hung black and absolute. And down at the end of Tanner's Row, past the shuttered houses and the hooded lanterns, a new sound began—distant, rhythmic, wrong. Digging. The wardens weren't just patrolling anymore.

They were already digging down toward the answer, and we weren't the only ones who knew the way.
