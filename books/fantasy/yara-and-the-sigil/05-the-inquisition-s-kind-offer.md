## 5. The Inquisition's Kind Offer

They came for me at dawn, which was an insult. Everyone competent in Vell knew I kept no dawn hours.

I woke to the smell of cold iron and candle smoke, and when I opened my eyes there were three wardens in my garret, filling it the way stones fill a shoe. The room had always been small—just a pallet, a stove that smoked more than it warmed, and a loose floorboard under which I kept everything worth keeping—but with them in it, it shrank to the dimensions of a coffin. Frost furred the inside of the window glass. The stars were still gone outside, a black ceiling pressing down on the city like a held breath, and the wardens' lantern light made the frost glitter as if the sky's absence had been ground up and scattered over everything.

The tall one in front wore captain's knots on his collar. He had a face like something carved for a church door—a saint, maybe, or more likely the sinner being trampled beneath the saint's boot—and he stood with the patience of a man who had never once needed to hurry for anyone. His eyes moved around my room the way a moneylender's fingers move across a ledger: itemizing, appraising, finding everything wanting.

"Kestrel Delacroix," he said. My name in his mouth sounded like a sentence being read out before a hanging.

"Depends who's asking," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is one of the few gifts my trade has given me. When you spend years lifting other people's feelings and passing them off as trinkets, you learn to wear your own face like a mask fits over a wound. "The rent's not due till week's end."

"Warden-Captain Brannoc." He did not offer a hand. Wardens never do; hands are for shaking and pyres need both of yours free to struggle. "You'll come with us."

"Is this about the stars?" I sat up slowly, letting my blanket fall, letting him see a thin man in a nightshirt, unremarkable, harmless. Invisibility was my oldest art, older than the stealing. "Because I've got opinions about the stars, Captain, same as everyone. Mostly complaints."

Brannoc watched me perform. I could see him watching, which was the first bad sign—the second was that he let me keep doing it, the way a cat lets a mouse believe in its cleverness right up until the moment the paw comes down.

"You'll want your coat," he said instead. "It's cold below."

That was the third sign. He knew I kept a coat. He knew enough about my mornings—or my lack of them—to know where I'd look first. Which meant someone had talked, or someone had watched, or both, and either way my rules had failed me somewhere along their long, careful length.

I dressed with hands that wanted very much to tremble and were forbidden to. The two lesser wardens flanked the door like bookends, and one of them—young, freckled, trying hard to look bored—kept his gaze fixed on a point six inches above my head. The other looked at my hands.

He looked at my left hand.

I pulled on my gloves. I did it casually, the way any man dresses on a cold morning, and if my heart was hammering against my ribs like something trying to get out, well, the mask held. It has always held. That has been the whole architecture of my life: the mask holds, the floorboard stays loose, nobody remembers my face twice.

But Brannoc watched my gloved hand the whole time, and he smiled without warmth, the way winter smiles at the end of autumn.

* * *

They did not take me to the cells. That frightened me more than irons would have, though I didn't understand why until later. Cells mean uncertainty, and uncertainty means bargaining time. What they took me to instead was a chapel.

The Inquisition had repurposed one of Vell's old star-shrines, back before the stars went out—back when they still bothered pretending their quarrel was only with magic and not with wonder itself. Now the shrine stood in a courtyard behind the warden barracks, and its dome had been stripped of its silvered glass so that the black sky showed through, starless and total, like the inside of a closed eye. Where the altar had been they'd built a fire pit, ringed in iron, with chains above it that hung from a winch. The chains were scorched. The stone around the pit was scorched. Everything was very clean, the way things get when people scrub them often and take pride in their work.

They sat me in a chair opposite the fire pit. Unchained. That too was a message: they didn't think I could run.

Brannoc dismissed the other two to the doorway and poured wine from a carafe into two cups, handing me one as if we were merchants meeting over a contract. The wine was good. That frightened me too. Institutions that burn people do not waste good wine on people they intend to burn.

"Do you know what happened five nights ago?" he asked.

"The wards fell," I said. "The sky went dark. Half the city's praying and the other half's looting."

"And what do you suppose caused it?"

"I'm a charm-seller, Captain. Not a philosopher."

"You're not a charm-seller." He said it mildly, correcting a child's arithmetic. "Charm-sellers deal in trinkets and hope. You deal in memories, lifted clean and sold to strangers hungry to feel something real. Widow's grief bottled in glass. A merchant's first love, worn as a pendant. Don't look surprised—you have a reputation among people who notice such things, and noticing such things is my entire profession." He sipped his wine. "There are perhaps four memory-thieves in Vell good enough to leave no trace. Three of them are dead. Killed in the last five days, one by one, by my wardens, following information I am not at liberty to discuss."

The floor of my stomach opened. I drank some wine so my mouth would have something to do besides confess anything.

"That seems wasteful," I managed. "If they were good."

"They were good," Brannoc agreed. "And they are ash, because the city is frightened, and frightened cities want fires, and I would rather give them the right fires than the wrong ones. You're the last, Master Delacroix. The last thief of your particular talent in Vell." He set down his cup. "Which brings me to my kind offer."

"I haven't heard anything kind yet."

"No? Then listen." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the saint-and-sinner carving of his face went human, which was the most frightening thing he'd done all morning. "The border wards didn't fail. They were released. Something on the other side called them home—or called them down—and whatever that something is, it isn't natural, and it isn't alone. The Inquisition has spent two hundred years burning hedge-witches and root-doctors and children who talk to wells, and I will tell you plainly, thief: it has all been pebbles. We have been plucking pebbles off a road while something vast walks toward us along it, and now the road is open."

"So the great machine admits it's been swatting flies."

"The great machine," Brannoc said, without shame, "wants bigger flies. Survivors. Whatever walked through those wards when they fell—there are people in this city who know things, who carry marks, who hear voices in the dark now. Some of them will try to use what they have. Some already have. I cannot find them by searching door to door; the city's too old and too full of shadows, and my wardens are good men but they are looking for witches and what's coming wears better disguises than that." His eyes settled on me, patient and heavy. "You, however, find people. You find the grief-stricken and the lovesick and the desperate, and you slip into the places where they keep their secrets. You've been doing my work for years, Master Delacroix. I propose you be paid for it."

"Paid how?"

"Immunity. Full and written. Your name struck from every ledger, your face forgotten by every warden in Vell. You keep your trade, your little gray market, your life. In exchange, you bring me names. Magic-users. Marked ones. Anyone whose blood sings since the wards fell." He spread his hands, generous, reasonable, a man offering a leash with a very comfortable collar. "Save yourself, thief. And save the city while you're at it. There are worse careers."

I looked past him at the fire pit. At the clean-scoured stone. At the chains, and at the scorch marks shaped like nothing I wanted to imagine, and I understood at last why they hadn't taken me to the cells. Cells are for people whose answers aren't known yet. The chapel was for after. This whole interview, the wine, the courtesy—it was all a window display arranged in front of the pyre, and Brannoc was showing me both at once so I could choose which one to walk toward.

"And if I decline your kindness?" I asked.

"Then tomorrow night there will be a fire in this courtyard," he said, "and the crowd will go home satisfied, and the city will sleep a little easier, and no one will remember your name except the ledger, which will close." He said it the way other men remark on weather. "I should tell you—we've been aware of you longer than you'd credit. Longer, I think, than you've been aware of yourself."

Something cold walked down my spine. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning we know about the mark."

The words dropped into me like a stone into a well, and I listened all the way down for the splash and heard nothing but depth. Five years I had hidden it. Five years of gloves and bandages and lies about a scalding accident, five years of never letting anyone see my left palm open, five years of the sigil sleeping quiet under my skin like a coal banked under ash—and here was a warden in a stripped shrine saying *we know* as easily as he'd say *we know your shoe size.*

"You're mistaken," I said. "Old burn. Cooking accident."

"There's a woman in Salt Row," Brannoc said, "who sells charms she buys from you. Last month she sold one to a warden's wife—a grief-charm, lifted from some stranger's funeral. The wife wore it for three days. On the third day, the charm began to sing." He watched me. "Memory doesn't sing, Master Delacroix. Memory whispers, weeps, murmurs. But this charm sang, in a language none of my scholars know, and when we traced it back through the seller to the seller's supplier, the trail ended with a thin man who pays in odd coin and wears gloves indoors." He rose, unhurried, and walked to the fire pit, standing at its edge with his back to me, a man warming hands that did not need warming. "And when our scholars sketched what the song might look like, if a song could hold still, do you know what shape they drew?"

I said nothing. The sigil on my palm had begun, very faintly, to warm. It does that sometimes—had done it once before, five nights ago, the exact moment the sky went black—and I had told myself then it was nothing, a scar remembering rain. Now it warmed again, warm as a hand reaching for another hand, and I pressed it flat against my thigh and prayed to whatever starless god was listening that neither of us would notice.

"A circle," Brannoc said. "Interlaced. Seven points, drawn inward, like a key seen from the wrong side." He turned. "We drew it five nights ago. And four hours later, the wards fell. And the man who wears it in his skin is sitting in my chair, drinking my wine, telling me about a cooking accident."

The chair creaked under my grip. Every instinct I owned screamed different directions at once: run, lie, bargain, kneel. The old rules lined themselves up like soldiers—never steal a whole life, never be remembered—and I saw with terrible clarity that both of them were already broken, had been broken for five years, broken by a mark I'd never chosen and couldn't scrub off. I was remembered. I had been remembered the whole time. The whole architecture of my invisibility was a house built over a cellar, and the Inquisition had found the stairs.

"Here is what I think," Brannoc said, conversationally, settling back into his own seat as though we were old friends closing a deal. "I think you don't know what the mark is any more than I do. I think you've carried it for years, hiding it, using its edges—because your thefts are too clean, thief, too deep; ordinary charm-work can't reach the memories you reach—and you've told yourself stories about cooking accidents and luck. I think something gave it to you, or left it in you, and whatever that something was, it's connected to the thing that pulled down our sky." He picked up his wine again. "So help me. Find me the others—the marked ones, the singers, the ones who know—and I will spend my career keeping you from every pyre in Vell. Or refuse, and I will spend my career putting you on one. Either way, Master Delacroix, you are going to be useful. That is not cruelty. That is simply arithmetic."

The sigil burned now. Not painfully—not yet—but with a warmth like a held word, like a name pressed flat between my palm and my thigh, and I thought, absurdly, of Yara. Of her face, the flash of it the sigil had shown me five nights ago, gone as fast as a fish breaking water. Of her daughter, somewhere out in a city going quietly mad, carrying half a broken compass and a grudge with my name on it. Of Odile, who had warned me someone was asking about the marked thief, and how I'd paid her warning no more mind than morning fog.

Everyone had been asking about the marked thief. The whole city, apparently, had been asking. And I had walked through it for five years believing that if I was careful enough, small enough, forgettable enough, the world would let me keep my one secret and my one skin.

"Give me a day," I said.

Brannoc raised an eyebrow.

"To consider," I said. "Properly. You're asking me to sell people, Captain. However it's dressed up, that's the trade. I'd like to at least pretend I thought about it before I said yes."

For a long moment he studied me, and I met his eyes and did the hardest work of my life: I let him see fear, and confusion, and a man genuinely torn, and I buried beneath all of it, deeper than the floorboard, deeper than the well without a bottom, the single flickering truth that I had already decided—decided before he finished speaking, decided maybe five years ago—that I would die in this courtyard before I handed him a single name.

Not because I'm good. Let's be honest with ourselves; I've stolen a widow's grief and sold it twice. But the people Brannoc wanted were *mine*, in the way that everything desperate and hidden and hungry in Vell was mine—I knew their alleys, their needs, the shapes of their secrets. Handing them over would be handing over myself. And besides, some animal part of me, the part that had flared five nights ago when the sky went dark, whispered that whatever was coming wore better disguises than the wardens thought, and that the mark on my hand was not a thing to be traded but a thing to be answered.

"A day," Brannoc said at last. "Come to the barracks by tomorrow's dusk. Ask for me. If you don't come—" he gestured gently at the fire pit, the chains, the clean stone, "—then we proceed by the less pleasant arithmetic."

He stood, and I stood, and he shook my hand—my gloved hand—gripping it firmly, and through the leather and the wool I felt the sigil pulse against his palm like a second heartbeat, and I watched his eyes flicker, just once, with something that might have been confirmation or might have been appetite.

"Until dusk tomorrow, Master Delacroix."

* * *

They let me out into a city I no longer recognized.

It was morning, gray and cold, and Vell moved through it like a body with a fever. Folk clustered at street corners speaking low. Doors had chalk marks on them—warden marks, quarantine marks, I couldn't tell. A flower-seller had abandoned her cart and the flowers were freezing in their bundles, colorless in the starless daylight, and a boy was stealing them one by one and no one stopped him, because what was theft, now, next to a sky that had gone out?

I walked home the long way, taking doors and alleys I'd used a thousand times, testing each shadow like a rotten plank. And everywhere, on corners, on shuttered shops, nailed to the grain-market posts, I saw the new broadsheets, fresh ink, still smelling of the press:

*BY ORDER OF THE INQUISITION: ALL PERSONS BEARING UNNATURAL MARKS SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR INSPECTION WITHIN THREE DAYS. SHELTERING THE MARKED IS TREASON. THE FIRE CLEANSES ALL.*

Underneath, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*A REWARD IS OFFERED FOR INFORMATION CONCERNING A THIN MAN WHO WEARS GLOVES.*

I stood in the freezing street and laughed, quietly, because the alternative was screaming, and a thin man laughing draws fewer eyes than a thin man screaming. They knew my habits, my suppliers, my mark. By dusk tomorrow I would either be their hound or their kindling, and both collars choked.

Which meant the answer was neither. Which meant I had one day to become someone the Inquisition's ledgers didn't describe—to disappear so completely that even arithmetic lost count of me—and there was exactly one person in Vell who knew how to find the marked and the hidden and the desperate, one person with half a compass that pointed at my throat and a blade to match.

She was going to hate this.

I turned down toward the river district, walking fast, and behind me the black sky pressed down on Vell like a lid, and in my glove, patient as a promise, the sigil burned.
