## 6. Memories I Never Lived

My mother taught me that grief is a room you furnish yourself.

She said it the winter before she left, kneeling in front of our little stove with her hands out to the coals, her braid coming loose down her back. *Some people fill it with anger, Sable. Some with silence. Be careful what you put in yours—you'll have to live in it.* I was nine. I thought she was talking about the weather.

I have furnished my grief-room with him. With Kestrel Delacroix and his thief's hands and his careful gray eyes that never quite meet mine. For five years he has been the chair by the window, the lamp on the table, the coat hung on the door of everything I lost. When I dreamed of her—and I dreamed of her every night for the first year, waking with her name shaped on my lips like a stone I couldn't swallow—I dreamed of him too, standing at the edge of the dream, holding something behind his back.

So when I led him up the four creaking stairs to my rooms above the dyer's shop, I made myself look at him directly. Let him see what he'd built.

The stars were still gone. Three days now, and the sky over Vell was a lid of black lacquer, so complete that people had begun leaving candles in their windows just to prove the dark could be argued with. The wards along the border had fallen with them—the city's old protections, the ones that had hummed under the cobblestones since before anyone's grandmother was born—silent as dead bells. Wardens patrolled in threes now. Fires burned unchecked in the Low Quarter because there was no one left who knew how to quench them the old way. And in the alleys, in the spaces where magic used to be afraid to breathe, things had started to move that I didn't have names for.

Kestrel climbed the stairs behind me without a word. He moved like a man walking into a confession booth he'd built himself.

"Sit," I said, when we were inside. I lit no lamps. The room was dim enough already, amber-gray with light from the dye-vats below seeping through the floorboards, and I wanted him uncomfortable. Petty, maybe. But I'd earned a little petty.

He sat on the one stool, elbows on knees, and watched me open the trunk at the foot of my bed.

The trunk had been hers. Iron-banded, older than me, smelling of cedar and something colder underneath—river water, maybe, or the space between stars. I hadn't opened it in five years. I'd told myself it was because I couldn't bear to. The truth was worse: I couldn't bear *not* to, and opening it meant admitting that.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay her journal.

* * *

It looked like nothing. A book the size of my two palms together, bound in leather the color of dried blood, its spine cracked white from handling. No lock, no clasp. When I set it on the table between us, Kestrel leaned forward despite himself, and I saw the thief in him wake—the way his fingers twitched toward it, the way his breath went shallow.

"Don't touch it yet," I said.

His hand stopped midair. "What is it?"

"It's hers." My voice came out flatter than I wanted. I tried again. "Her journal. She kept it her whole life, I think. I found it after—" After. Five years and I still couldn't finish that sentence. "After she went."

"And you're showing me this why?" He wasn't being cruel. That was almost the worst part about him—he asked questions like a man genuinely willing to hear any answer, even the ones that cut him.

"Because I can't read it."

I turned the journal open to its first page and pushed it across the table so the lamplight—or would have been lamplight, if I'd lit one; the vat-light—fell across it.

Kestrel stared. His brow creased, then smoothed, then creased again, like a man watching water try to hold a shape.

"That's not writing," he said slowly. "Or—it is, but it keeps..." He passed his hand above the page without touching. "It keeps moving."

It did. I'd watched it move for years. The characters were slender and angular, like frost ferns or the tracks birds leave on snow, and they never held still. Look away and look back, and the same line had rearranged itself into something else entirely. Once, drunk on cheap wine on the anniversary of her going, I'd stared until my eyes streamed, convinced that if I could only catch the letters between arrangements I'd find her hiding there, laughing at me.

"She wrote it in cipher," I said. "But not a cipher of letters. Watch."

I took a breath. This was the part I hated. This was the part that felt like undressing in front of an enemy.

I closed my eyes and reached for a memory—not one of mine. One of the few I owned outright, bought honestly years ago from a dying ferryman who had no more use for it: the memory of rowing. Just rowing. The pull of oars through brown river water, the creak of the locks, the particular patience of a body doing work it has done ten thousand times.

I let it rise to the surface of my mind, and I pressed my palm flat against the page.

The frost-ferns shivered. Rearranged. And for three heartbeats—only three, it never lasted longer for me—the text resolved into words I could read:

*...the river remembers every boat that crosses it, which is why I never cross the same river twice...*

Then it slid apart again, letters scattering like startled fish.

Kestrel had gone very still. "How did you do that?"

"You can't read it with your eyes," I said. "You read it with what you've taken. Every memory you've ever stolen is a key. Different memories unlock different passages. The ferryman's rowing unlocked that line. A baker's memory of kneading dough opened a passage about flour charms. My mother built it that way deliberately—" my throat tightened around the words, "—so that only someone like her could ever read it. Someone who carried other lives inside them."

"Someone like me," Kestrel said quietly.

"Someone exactly like you." I looked at him across the table, across the book that held whatever was left of her voice, and I hated how much hope was in the room suddenly, how it changed the air pressure. "That's why I brought you here. Not because I forgive you. Because you are the only person in Vell who might be able to read what she left behind."

He looked down at his right hand. At the palm I'd seen bare once, in the alley, blade at his throat—the sigil hidden beneath some glamour most days, but visible then, a knot of silvered lines like a spider's web drawn by someone shaking. It had flared the night the stars died. I'd seen it from across the square, a cold white star burning in his fist while the sky went black above the whole city.

"If I touch it," he said, "and it shows me things—"

"It showed me things," I said. "Little pieces. A kitchen. Her handwriting on shopping lists. Nothing whole." I swallowed. "But you steal for a living, Kestrel. You know how to go deeper than I do."

Something crossed his face—fear, I thought, real fear, the first honest thing I'd ever seen him wear. "Sable. If I open this and it takes me somewhere I can't come back from—"

"Then you'll finally understand what it's like," I said, "losing someone all at once instead of a piece at a time."

It was cruel. I heard it land. He flinched like I'd struck the sigil itself.

But he reached for the book anyway.

* * *

I will tell this part plainly, because I have rewritten it in my head a hundred times since, and plainness is the only honesty left to me.

He laid his marked palm flat against the first page, and the sigil woke.

Not flared—woke, the way a sleeper opens one eye. The silver lines brightened to a low ember-glow, and the glow ran off his skin and into the paper like water into thirsty ground, following the frost-fern characters, filling them, and the whole journal began to breathe. The pages stirred though there was no wind. The air in the room thickened. Below us, faintly, the dye-vats sloshed against their vats' sides as if something large had turned over in its sleep beneath the city.

And Kestrel made a sound I will never forget. Not a cry. Something smaller and worse—a single syllable of recognition, punched out of him, the sound of a man seeing a face in a crowd that he'd spent five years telling himself he'd imagined.

Then his eyes rolled back and he fell sideways off the stool, and I caught the journal before it hit the floor, and I knelt beside him with my heart slamming and did the only thing I could think of, which was to take his hand and fall with him.

* * *

I had expected darkness. What I got was a kitchen.

Not mine. Hers—I knew it instantly, the way you know your mother's footsteps on stairs. A narrow kitchen with a copper kettle and a window looking out on a courtyard where laundry lines crossed like rigging. Rosemary hanging in bundles from a beam. A cat, orange and disreputable, asleep in a bowl meant for rising dough.

And her. Younger than I remembered her, or maybe just younger than I'd allowed myself to remember. Dark hair pinned up carelessly, ink on her fingers, laughing at something. Laughing at—

At a boy. Thin, maybe twelve, hollow-cheeked, sitting at her table eating bread like it might be taken from him. Eating like a boy who had learned that food was a trick played by adults.

I knew that face. I had hated that face for five years, and here it was, young and starving and *alive*, and my mother was looking at it with an expression I had never once seen directed at me—raw, terrified tenderness, the look of a woman watching something she has already decided to die for.

The memory shifted. They always shift; that's the nature of stolen things, they don't sit still in the mind.

A night street, rain coming down in ropes. The boy—older now, fifteen maybe—pressed against a wall, and men in gray coats moving toward him with instruments I didn't want to look at too closely, and my mother stepping out of an alley with her arms spread wide and her shadow spreading wider, impossibly wide, swallowing the street, and the gray-coated men screaming—

A room full of candle stubs. Hundreds of them, each one labeled in her slanted hand with a name. She was teaching him—teaching Kestrel—that a memory properly prepared could be burned like tallow, could give light, could be given away. *Never steal a whole life,* she was saying, and her voice was so clear I could have wept, could still weep, *because a whole life is a person, and we don't sell people, Kestrel. We sell hours. Hours are mercy. Lives are theft.*

He was nodding. Young Kestrel, sixteen, seventeen, solemn as a priest, writing it down. *Never be remembered.* His rules. Her rules. I had spent five years believing he invented his carefulness himself, that his caution was the caution of a guilty man. It wasn't guilt. It was inheritance. He had been keeping her commandments without knowing whose they were.

The memories came faster now, tumbling, the sigil drinking deeper—

A market at dusk, and her haggling fiercely over fish while the boy watched her with an expression I recognized from my own mirror—

A fever, his fever, nights of it, her sitting by his bed grinding willow bark, singing under her breath a song I knew, *the lullaby, she sang him MY lullaby*—

And then the last one. The last one, and I felt Kestrel's hand tighten on mine hard enough to grind bone, because he knew what was coming too.

* * *

Her final night.

Rain again—she always fled in rain, I understood now, rain confused trackers and drowned scent. She was packing. Not frantically; methodically, terribly, the way you pack when you have done the arithmetic and the answer is that you are not coming back. A cloak. Dried food. A knife. The journal, wrapped in oilcloth.

And the sigil—her sigil, burning silver on her own palm, and her looking at it the way you look at a debt.

There was a knock at the door. Soft. Courteous. Three knocks, evenly spaced, like a gentleman calling.

She went still as a hare. Through the window I saw the street below, and the street was wrong—too many shadows, and the shadows arranged too neatly, and in the doorway of the house opposite stood a figure in a long dark coat with flowers blooming out of his lapels where buttons should be. Black roses. Living ones, opening and closing like slow mouths.

I heard her whisper a word I had never heard before but understood completely, the way you understand a wound:

"*Thorn Court.*"

More knocking. Then a voice through the door, smooth as poured wine, patient as rot: *"Yara. We only want what you took. Give it back, and no one needs to be hurt. You know we always keep our bargains."*

She looked at the sleeping loft above her. At the boy in it. And I watched my mother make a decision, and the making of it broke something in her face that I don't think ever healed, wherever she was.

She pricked her palm with her knife. Pressed the bleeding sigil against the journal's cover. Whispered words in the moving language, the frost-fern language, and the sigil *lifted*—peeled off her hand like a brand lifted from meat, trailing light—and she cupped it in both hands and carried it up the ladder to the loft.

To him.

I watched her kneel beside the sleeping boy who had eaten her bread and learned her rules and called her—what did he call her? I strained for it through the memory's haze—and I watched her press the sigil into his small scarred palm, and I heard her say, so softly the words barely survived the crossing:

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry, little wren. You won't remember why, and you'll hate me for the vanishing, and that's the kindest thing I can do for you. If they can't get it out of you because you don't *know*—" her voice cracked, mended, hardened, "—then they can't use you. Live small. Live unseen. And someday, if the stars start dying, you'll know what to do. I've made sure of it. I'm so sorry."

She kissed his forehead. She erased herself from his mind with two fingers drawn across his brow like closing a curtain.

And downstairs, the courteous voice said, *"Yara. We're coming in."*

The memory ended.

* * *

We came back to the room gasping, both of us, tangled on the floorboards with the journal between us still faintly glowing, and the vat-light below had gone strange, and somewhere outside a dog was barking at the starless sky.

Kestrel was crying. He didn't wipe his face or hide it. He just knelt there with tears running into his mouth and said, over and over, barely voiced: "She didn't vanish. She didn't *vanish*. She left. She chose it. She gave me—" He looked at his palm, at the sigil fading back to sleep. "Five years. Five years I thought I'd dreamed her. I thought I'd *invented* her, Sable, I thought—"

"The Thorn Court," I said.

He stopped.

"They took her." My voice sounded far away to me, like it belonged to someone standing in a doorway. "They came for her and she gave you the sigil and walked out to meet them so they wouldn't search the house. So they wouldn't find *you*. And I—" The grief-room I had furnished so carefully, the anger and the blame and the beautiful clean hatred of him—all of it caught fire at once, and burned, and left me standing in an empty room with nothing in my hands. "I spent five years hating the wrong thing."

"Sable—"

"She's alive." I grabbed the journal, shoved it at him, pages riffling. "If she'd died that night, the Thorn Court would have stopped hunting. Ashvine's people—they've been circling Vell for months, I've seen them, the ones with roses in their coats. They're still looking. You don't hunt for five years for a corpse." I was talking fast, faster than sense, building a ladder out of anything. "And the stars went out the same hour your sigil woke. She told you—if the stars start dying, you'll know what to do. She *made sure of it*. There's more in this book. There has to be. Read it again. Read all of it."

Kestrel looked down at the journal in my grip. Then at me. And for the first time since the alley, since the blade and the hatred and the truce neither of us trusted, he looked at me like I was not the daughter of the woman he'd lost, but something rarer—an ally. A fellow survivor of the same storm.

"There's a problem," he said hoarsely. "Every time I touch it, it takes more. It doesn't ask. It *drinks*, Sable. Another reading and I might not come back up with all of myself."

"Then we find another way in." I wiped my face with my sleeve, furious at the wetness, and pulled the journal against my chest where it pulsed faintly, warm as a sleeping bird. "Because I just watched my mother erase herself to save a starving boy who grew up into a cowardly charm-thief, and I am *done* letting other people pay for things alone."

He almost smiled. It was terrible and crooked and true.

Outside, in the street below the dyer's shop, a bell began to ring—not the wardens' alarm, but the deep old bell in the temple district that hadn't sounded in living memory. Three tolls. A pause. Three more.

Neither of us knew what it meant.

But the sigil on Kestrel's palm flared once, briefly, pointing downward—toward the ground, toward the deep places under the city—and the journal in my arms turned cold as riverbed stone.
