## 8. Thorns in the Dark

My mother taught me one lesson about strangers, and she taught it with her hands wrapped around mine, both of us standing in the herb-smell of our little kitchen while rain ticked against the shutters.

"When someone is too kind too quickly," she said, "they are not being kind to you. They are being kind to what they want from you."

I was seven. I remember thinking it was a strange thing for a woman who made her living healing strangers to say. I remember thinking she was wrong.

I have thought about that kitchen every day for five years, and I have never once stopped being wrong about the people I love.

* * *

Lord Ashvine descended the bridge stairs the way water descends—without effort, without hurry, as though gravity were a favor he had personally arranged. Up close he was not what I expected. I had built him out of nightmare-furniture: hooks and teeth, the Inquisition's cruelty wearing a different coat. Instead there was a tall man in charcoal wool, gray-haired, handsome in the way old portraits are handsome, with clean hands and a voice like warmed honey poured slowly.

"There you are," he said again, closer now, and smiled at Kestrel as if they were old friends meeting at a funeral. "And you've brought company. How lovely."

Kestrel's shoulder pressed against mine. He had put himself half in front of me without seeming to notice he'd done it, which was, I decided, the single most infuriating thing about him.

"We're just leaving," Kestrel said.

"You are," Ashvine agreed pleasantly. "Eventually. Everyone is, eventually." He gestured up the street with an open palm, and I saw then that the alley mouths on either side of us had filled with his people—not the three from the stairs but a dozen, two dozen, silent figures in dark traveling clothes, standing with the loose patience of cats outside a mousehole. None of them carried drawn weapons. That was somehow worse. "But the city's gates are locked, the wards are down, and the Inquisition is burning anyone whose shadow falls wrong. It's a terrible night for walking. May I offer you shelter?"

"We know how shelters work in Vell," I said. "You have to pay to get out."

Ashvine turned to me fully for the first time, and something shifted behind his face—the mask adjusting, or perhaps being removed to show me a second, more attentive mask beneath. His eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and they moved over my face like fingers reading braille.

"And you must be Sable," he said softly.

The sound of my own name in a stranger's mouth has always felt like theft. This time it felt like theft committed gently, apologetically, by someone who would return it if only I asked nicely—and asking nicely, we both knew, would cost me everything.

"Yara's daughter," he went on, when I said nothing. "You have her chin. Her stubbornness, I imagine, though I only knew her briefly. Briefly," he repeated, with a small sorrow that fit him so perfectly I understood immediately it had been tailored. "Long ago."

"Then you know where she is."

It came out of me before I could cage it. I heard it leave my mouth and hated myself for the nakedness of it—the hunger, plain and ringing, offered up to this man like an open purse.

Ashvine did not gloat. That was the terrible part. He simply looked at me with those patient gray eyes and let the silence do his work, and in the silence I heard the river under the bridge, and the distant screaming of a city learning to be afraid, and my own pulse, loud and stupid and hopeful.

"I do," he said at last. "Yes."

* * *

They took us somewhere I still cannot map correctly in my memory. There was a covered barge on the black canal, its lanterns hooded, sliding through streets where fires burned unattended. There was a door in a warehouse wall that opened onto a corridor longer than the building could contain—I counted my steps, an old habit from when my mother used to vanish into places like this, and I reached ninety before logic gave up. There was a room at the end with a fire burning in a hearth that had no chimney above it, and chairs, real upholstered chairs, and a table set with bread and cold chicken and a bottle of wine from some southern slope that no doubt hadn't existed since before the Thorn Court fell.

Captivity, done courteously, looks exactly like hospitality. That's how you know to be afraid.

They took our knives at the door—a woman with kind eyes and a scar through her eyebrow, who apologized so sincerely I almost thanked her—and then left us alone with Lord Ashvine and the impossible supper.

"Eat," he urged us. "Please. Interrogation is barbaric and unnecessary. People tell you everything if you simply feed them first. It's why the Inquisition will always fail, you understand—they start with fire, and fire makes liars of everyone."

Kestrel sat rigid in his chair, hands folded over the palm I knew he was hiding. He hadn't touched the food. He was doing the thing I had already begun to recognize as his signature: making himself smaller, duller, a gray man in a gray coat, forgettable as furniture. Five years of practice. But tonight it wasn't working. Tonight Ashvine's gaze kept returning to him with the mild, satisfied attention of a collector confirming a piece is genuine.

"You know what I am," Kestrel said.

"I know what you carry." Ashvine settled into the chair opposite, folding his long body down with the ease of a man in his own parlor. "The distinction matters. You are a thief, Master Delacroix—I hope you don't mind, we did inquire; your reputation in the gray market is quietly excellent, by the way, very disciplined, very careful—and what you carry is rather older than you. Older than me. Older than the Court, though we have kept its memory alive where others let it rot." He spread his hands. "The sigil."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into deep water. I watched Kestrel's knuckles whiten.

"My mother didn't steal anything," I said.

"Didn't she?" Ashvine sounded genuinely curious, which frightened me more than any sneer would have. "Tell me, Sable—and forgive the familiarity, but we are likely to be family of a sort before this is finished—what did your mother tell you about her work? About where she learned the counter-art? About why a woman of her considerable talent chose to live in a warded city full of ordinary people, mending memories for coppers, hiding her light under wardstone and habit?" He leaned forward, firelight pooling in the hollows of his face. "She was ours once. Did she ever say that? The Thorn Court does not exile its daughters lightly. She took something from us when she ran, something irreplaceable, and we have spent nineteen years—forgive me, nineteen years and some months—being very patient about getting it back."

"Nineteen years," I repeated.

It came out flat. But inside me, arithmetic was happening whether I wanted it or not. Nineteen years. And I am twenty-two. And my mother left me in a neighbor's kitchen for one afternoon when I was three, an afternoon the neighbor could never account for afterward, an afternoon my mother never mentioned again except once, drunk on plum wine, laughing and crying at once, saying *the things I do for you, little bird, the things I do*—

I looked at Kestrel. He was looking at the table. He had been twenty-something when my mother vanished, which meant he'd been fifteen or sixteen when—

"How long have you known each other?" I asked him.

"Not now," Kestrel murmured.

"No," Ashvine agreed warmly, "not now. Later, I think, you'll want hours for that conversation. Days, possibly. Which brings me—" he rose, crossed to the sideboard, poured two small glasses of something amber "—to the reason for all this hospitality."

He gave one glass to Kestrel. The other he brought to me, and stood waiting until I took it, because refusing would have been a move and I did not yet know what game we were playing.

"Here is my offer, plainly, because I find plainness disarms people wonderfully." He returned to his chair. "Your mother is alive, Sable."

The room tilted. I heard myself breathing. Somewhere far away Kestrel said my name, and Ashvine talked over him with perfect gentleness, a surgeon's gentleness, a man cutting precisely where it hurts most and least.

"She is alive, she is found, and she can be reached—by us, tonight, if we choose. We have spent nineteen years and a great deal of blood finding what the Inquisition cannot even name. And in exchange, I ask for one small thing." His colorless eyes moved to Kestrel, rested there, fond as a father's. "Him."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me in that moment, because later—after everything—I will want to pretend I hesitated less than I did.

Hope is not a clean feeling. My mother told me that too, though she said it about other things: *hope is the lure on the line, little bird; the fish never sees the hook, only the shining.* And there I sat with the hook in my mouth, tasting honey, hearing the word *alive*, seeing her—not as she had been at the end, gaunt and watchful, checking the window latches twice—but as she'd been when I was small, spinning me in the herb garden, humming, whole.

Alive. Reachable. For the price of a thief I had known for four days and hated for five years.

"He isn't mine to sell," I said.

Ashvine laughed—a real laugh, brief and warm, entirely at odds with everything else about him. "Oh, splendid. Loyalty. I confess I hoped you'd say something like that; the ones who bargain instantly always disappoint me later." He sipped his drink. "But consider the mathematics, child, because your mother would. One boy, marked, hunted by the Inquisition regardless of what you or I decide—he'll burn within the month either way; Brannoc already has his scent, I promise you. Against that: a woman who healed your fevers, who taught you which mushrooms lie, who loved you badly and left you anyway. Alive. Sitting across a table from you by morning." He tilted his head. "What would she want you to choose? Truly? She spent her whole life spending herself for other people. It would be fitting, wouldn't it, for her daughter to spend someone else?"

"That's not—" My voice cracked and I despised it. "That's not how she'd think."

"Isn't it? Then you knew her better than I did." And there, for half a heartbeat, the courtesy thinned and I saw underneath it: something old and cold and terribly patient, something that had waited nineteen years in exile among thorns, and the sight of it dried my mouth better than any threat. "I knew her," Ashvine said quietly, "when she was brilliant and ruthless and mine, before she learned this softer arithmetic you admire so much. People change, Sable. It is our tragedy and our trade. What we take—" he gestured vaguely at Kestrel, at the hidden hand, at the whole trembling apparatus of stolen hours that Vell ran on below its pretty surface "—we keep. Nothing given freely survives. Only what is taken."

"The seal takes nothing freely," Kestrel said.

Everyone in the room went still, including me. Including, for just a moment, Ashvine.

He recovered beautifully. But I had seen it—that flicker, that recalculation, the moment the collector realized the piece knew more than its provenance suggested. He set down his glass with a soft click and regarded Kestrel with new attention, and I understood that until this instant, Lord Ashvine had been negotiating for an object.

Now he was negotiating for a rival.

"Where did you learn that phrase?" he asked.

"Doesn't matter," Kestrel said. "What matters is you're offering her mother's life like it's yours to give. If Yara's alive, and you know where—if you could reach her *tonight*, your words—you wouldn't trade her away for anything. Not even for me." He unfolded his hands at last, and I saw the sigil bare in the firelight, the mark I had spent four days wanting to cut off him and two days wanting to hold, and it was glowing. Faintly. Warmly. Like a coal remembering fire. "You need me too much to spend me. And you need her to believe otherwise, because you need *her* to make this trade. You can't take the sigil, can you, my lord? Something about it. Something it does to hands that reach for it without asking."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere in the long corridor beyond the door, a footman—or a soldier, or whatever the Thorn Court called its courtesies—shifted his weight.

"You see," Kestrel said softly, to Ashvine, but looking at me, "that's the difference between stealing and asking. She taught you the taking half. Someone taught me the rest."

For one suspended moment I thought Ashvine might simply kill us there, over the untouched chicken and the southern wine, and I braced for it with an odd clarity, noticing the pattern of the rug, the scar-woman's reflection in the darkened window, my own heartbeat counting down.

Then Lord Ashvine smiled, and clapped his hands once, gently, like applause.

"Oh, *wonderful*," he breathed. "Nineteen years, and she sent us exactly the right thief." He stood, buttoned his coat, became again the portrait-handsome gentleman. "Keep him another night, Sable. Ask your questions; you clearly have years of them. In the morning, when the Inquisition finds this place—and they will, I've made sure of it—you'll have a choice to make with better information than you have now. I am nothing if not fair." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "She is in the Understar, by the way. Your mother. Where else would a woman go to hold up the sky? Do think on what holding it up has cost her. And on what it might cost her daughter to leave her there."

The door closed. The lock did not click. He was too courteous for that.

* * *

We sat in silence for a long time. The fire burned without shrinking. Outside the impossible windows, Vell lay starless and smoking, and I thought about my mother holding up the sky, and about the word *alive*, and about how Ashvine had given me the truth precisely because the truth was the sharpest blade in the room.

"Why did you do that?" I finally asked. "Turn him down. Buy me time. You could have—maybe he'd have let you go if I'd agreed."

"He'd have let me go to the Understar," Kestrel said. "As luggage." He rubbed his hidden palm, staring at the fire. "Sable."

"Don't." I stood up, because sitting near him felt suddenly like standing on the edge of the hole Ashvine had just opened in my life. "Don't say anything kind. Don't—four days. I've known you four days and I've spent five years hating you, and now there's a man with warm wine telling me my mother is under the world, and you—" My voice was coming apart and I let it, because some things should hurt to hear. "You knew her *nineteen years ago*. Before me. Before the sigil. And you've lied to me with your face this whole time, every hour, that same gray honest face—"

"Sable—"

"*What aren't you telling me?*"

The firelight caught him, and I watched the gray man fall away—watched five years of practiced smallness crack down the middle the way the border wards had cracked, all at once, with a sound like breaking glass. He looked younger. He looked terrified. He looked like someone standing at the edge of a confession he had been carrying so long it had grown into his bones.

"After tomorrow the Inquisition comes," he said quietly. "So. Before that."

He opened his marked hand, palm up, between us. The sigil pulsed like a small captive star.

"You deserve the truth," Kestrel said. "All of it. Even the parts about your mother that will make you hate me forever."

"Try me," I said.

And he took a breath, and began.
