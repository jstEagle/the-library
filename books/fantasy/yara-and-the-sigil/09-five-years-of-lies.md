## 9. Five Years of Lies

The Thorn Court did not chase us.

That was the worst of it. We ran anyway—through the drowned cellar of the old tannery, up a ladder slick with rot, out through a coal chute into the alley behind the chandler's shop—but no one came after us. Lord Ashvine had let us go the way a man opens his hand to let a moth fly, already certain of where it will land. I kept looking over my shoulder for white gloves, for that voice like warmed honey poured over broken glass, and found only Vell unraveling in the dark: shuttered windows, a shrine to the wards with its candles guttering, someone weeping two streets over in a way that had gone past grief into habit.

Sable ran ahead of me. She ran the way she did everything—jaw set, blade already drawn, as if the world owed her a fight and she meant to collect early. The half-compass swung on its cord at her wrist, needle straining toward me with every step, dragging at her like a leash she'd learned not to resent because resentment took energy she needed elsewhere.

We didn't stop until we reached the aqueduct arches at the edge of Candlewick, where the city's old stone went quiet and the dark was honest enough not to lie about what it held. Sable posted herself at the mouth of the arch, back to me, watching the street. I sat down against the cold curve of the pillar and discovered my hands were shaking.

Not from Ashvine. Not exactly.

From what I'd seen in his face when he said her name. *Yara.* He had said it like a creditor. Like a man who had been keeping a ledger for five years and knew, finally, that the debt had walked into his parlor wearing my skin.

"Stop breathing so loudly," Sable said, without turning. "You sound like a bellows."

"You're welcome," I said. "For the rescue."

"You mean the part where you got us captured."

"I mean the part where we're not still in his parlor."

She didn't answer. In the distance, somewhere past the dead wards, something howled—not wolf, not dog, nothing with a shape I wanted to imagine. Five days since the stars had gone out, and the city was learning new sounds every night, filling the silence the sky had left.

I turned my palm toward what little light there was. The sigil had gone quiet after the parlor, after Ashvine's gloved finger had traced it without touching—*the last living key,* he'd called it, *and Yara's final theft*—but I could still feel it the way you feel a bruise: proof of impact, memory written in the body.

And that was the problem, wasn't it. It was always going to come to this. I'd known it since the alley, since her blade at my throat and her mother's eyes full of hate above it. Every road we walked led here, to this arch, this cold stone, this confession I'd been swallowing for five years until it had worn a groove down my throat.

"Sable."

"Don't." She said it flatly. "Whatever speech you're winding up, don't. I want to get off the main streets before dawn."

"I killed your mother."

* * *

The silence afterward was total. Even the distant howling stopped, as if the city itself had leaned in to listen.

Sable turned around slowly. The knife came up—not to my throat this time, just up, an old reflex finding a new reason. Her face in the dark was all angles and held breath.

"Say that again," she whispered, "very carefully."

"She's not alive, Sable." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "She didn't vanish. She didn't run. She died—or something close enough to dying that I've stopped pretending there's a difference—and I was there, and I can't remember any of it, and I need you to understand why before you open my throat. Because you're going to want to. So hear all of it first."

The knife trembled. Just barely. She noticed, and hated that she noticed.

"You were there," she repeated. "You were *there*. She disappeared when I was thirteen. There was no body, no note, no—" Her voice cracked and she crushed it flat, ironing the crack out by force. "The wardens searched. The Court searched. Half the gray market swore she'd fled across the sea. And you're telling me you stood in the same room as her and lived, and I'm supposed to believe you remember *nothing*?"

"That's the truth of it. That's the whole rotten truth." I opened my palm to her, sigil up, like showing a wound to prove it wasn't self-inflicted. "But there's more underneath, and you're not going to like it, and I'm telling you anyway because Ashvine was right about one thing: lies are just debts with better clothes. I owe you this one."

"Then pay it," she said, "and pray I don't take your hand as interest."

* * *

Here is what I told her, under the aqueduct arches, while Vell came apart at the seams beyond our little circle of dark:

Five years ago, I was nineteen and dying. Poison—I never learned whose; a job gone wrong, a client who decided a thief who knew his secrets was worth more dead. I remember the fever. I remember the room above Odile's, the ceiling water-stained into shapes like drowning faces. I remember being certain I would die, and being almost fine with it, because I had built a life out of taking pieces of other people's and none of them had ever been mine to keep.

And then Yara was there.

I had seen her before, in the market, the way everyone had—the memory-witch, the woman who could hold a stranger's childhood in her cupped hands like water and drink it without spilling. I'd stolen from her once. Early, stupid, before I understood what she was. A single memory, three seconds long: a girl laughing in a garden. I'd sold it within the week and never thought twice.

She knew. Of course she knew. She sat beside my sickbed and told me she'd bought it back from the charm-buyer years ago and had been waiting to see what kind of man I'd grow into before deciding what to do about me.

"And what did she decide?" Sable asked. Her voice had gone very quiet. The knife was still up, but her arm was lowering by degrees, the way a storm lowers.

"She decided I was worth saving," I said. "That was her mistake. Or her choice. I've spent five years arguing with myself about which." I swallowed. The groove in my throat ached. "She pulled the poison out of me—but not the way a physiker does. She drew it into the sigil. Into *her* sigil, burned into her palm since before I ever met her. She said the mark was older than both of us, older than the Court, older than the wards—that it was made to hold things. To carry them. And that carrying me through the poison nearly cracked it, which meant it needed a bearer with more years left in him than she had."

Sable had gone very still. Stillness was worse, I found, than the blade. A blade only wants blood. Stillness wants everything.

"She offered it to me," I said. "Her mark. Her key—whatever it truly is. And I said yes, because I was nineteen and grateful and I didn't ask a single one of the questions I should have asked. And then she—"

I had to stop. Not because the words wouldn't come. Because they came too easily, and I wanted, one last time, to feel how much they cost before I handed them over.

"She erased herself," Sable finished, in a whisper. Not a question.

"She erased everything." I pressed my palm against my sternum, as if the hollow lived there, which in every way that mattered it did. "Every memory of her. The sickroom. The garden girl—the girl was her, Sable, I know that now, she was laughing in her mother's garden the day the Thorn Court first came for her. She took it all out of me, clean, the way I take things from other people but *whole*, entire, root and branch, and she fed it into the sigil to seal it shut. Because a key that doesn't remember it's a key is a key nobody looks for. Because if the Court came asking what happened to Yara, the last person to see her couldn't tell them anything, not even under torture, not even to himself. Because—"

"Why?" The word came out of her like something torn. "Why erase *you*? Why not just hide?"

"Because they could have taken the memory out of me anyway. People like her. People like Ashvine." I met her eyes. "Because knowing is a door, and doors can be opened by anyone with the skill and the cruelty. She didn't hide the knowledge, Sable. She destroyed it. She made sure the only place it existed was inside the seal itself, where no one could reach it without paying whatever price she'd rigged into the lock. She didn't vanish on you. She vanished *for* you. For both of us. For the whole godsdamned city, maybe—I don't know, I can't know, that's the thing, that's the whole shape of it. She took my five years so that no one else would lose anything at all."

* * *

For a long moment there was only the drip of water somewhere in the arches, counting out seconds neither of us wanted.

Then Sable threw the knife.

It buried itself in the pillar an inch from my ear with a sound like a snapped bone. I didn't flinch. Some animal part of me noted that I didn't flinch, and filed it away as either courage or numbness, and suspected the second.

"You *let* her," she said. Her voice had gone low and shaking, a kettle about to scream. "You lay there dying, and a woman offered to gut her own life open to save yours, and you said *yes*. You didn't say no. You didn't say *take less*. You didn't say *what about your daughter*, you didn't—she had a *daughter*, Kestrel. She had *me*. Thirteen years old, waiting up with a candle in the window because Mother worked late, Mother always worked late, and one night the candle burned down and she just—wasn't coming home, ever, and I spent five years thinking I wasn't worth staying for."

"Sable—"

"The *compass*." She yanked it off her wrist and hurled it at my chest; I caught it, fumbled it, clutched it. The needle strained against its pin, quivering toward me like a starving thing. "Her half of it. Left on my bed the night she went. I thought—gods, I *thought*—it meant she wanted me to find her someday. I carried it six years. It points at *you*. It's pointed at you since the night you took her hand and let her unmake herself, and I thought it was leading me to her killer, and in a way—" her breath hitched, ugly and raw— "in a way it was. You killed her as surely as any warden with a pyre. You just used *gratitude*."

"Yes," I said.

She blinked. Whatever she'd expected—a denial, a defense, the clever thief's tongue I'd used on her since the alley—it wasn't agreement. The wind went out of her rage for a heartbeat, leaving her standing in the wreckage of it, young and exhausted and thirteen years old again under all that armor.

"Yes," I said again, because the first one deserved company. "I said yes. I'd say it again. Not because I was right. Because I was dying, Sable, and she was offering me the sun, and no one—no one—has ever offered me anything before or since. You want the honest version? Here it is, freely given: I was selfish. I was grateful. I didn't deserve her, and I took her anyway, and I've paid for it every day since in a currency I can't even name, because I wake up some mornings reaching for a face I don't remember and find a burn on my hand instead. She's gone and I'm the grave she dug, and I don't even get to mourn properly because *she stole the mourning too*."

My voice broke on the last word. I let it. She'd asked for honesty; she could have the ugly sounds that came with it.

Sable stared at me. The half-compass lay in my lap, needle trembling, faithful to a fault.

"You should have told me," she said at last. "In the alley. The first hour. Instead you let me drag you around the city believing you'd betrayed her, believing you were just—some thief."

"If I'd told you in the alley, you'd have cut my throat. Don't argue. You know you would have."

"I might still."

"But you won't." I picked up the compass and held it out to her, handle first, the way you return a weapon to its owner. "Because now you know what I know, and you know what Ashvine knows, and there's no one else alive who knows it. Which means we're the only two people in Vell who understand what the stars going out actually means. The seal is failing, Sable. Whatever your mother locked behind it—whatever she thought was worth five years of my life and her whole existence—it's coming loose. And the Inquisition wants the key for their fires, and the Thorn Court wants it for their war, and your mother gave it to a gutter thief because she thought a gutter thief was the safest hands in the world."

Sable took the compass. Her fingers brushed mine, and she flinched as if the touch had burned—then looked at my open palm, at the sigil, and something moved across her face that was almost unbearable to witness. Recognition. Grief. The beginning, terrible and tender, of understanding.

"It's her handwriting," she said quietly. "The sigil. I never saw it up close before. The loops—that's how she wrote her Y's. She signed everything like that, with the tail curling under." She closed her fist around the compass until the casing bit her palm. "You're carrying her signature, Kestrel. On your hand. All this time."

"I know."

"And you're going to help me fix what she sealed. You're going to walk me into whatever dark she hid herself in, and we're going to find out if any of her survived it." It wasn't a question, and it wasn't forgiveness. It was a sentence being passed. "Because I can't forgive you yet. Maybe never. But Ashvine said she might be alive, and if there's even a chance—if there's one grain of her left in that light—then you and I are allies until she's free or we're ash. Do you understand me?"

"Understood," I said.

She turned away toward the street, shoulders rigid, and began walking south along the aqueduct, toward the river, toward whatever came next. After ten paces she stopped, without turning.

"The garden," she said. "The girl laughing in the garden. What did it feel like? When you had it."

"Warm," I said. "Like summer had decided, just for those three seconds, to be kind."

Sable nodded once, sharply, and kept walking. And I followed her into the dark, carrying a hollow shaped like a woman, while somewhere beneath our feet—beneath the city, beneath the wardbones, deep in the Understar's failing heart—the seal groaned in its sleep, and the last starless night leaned down close to listen.
