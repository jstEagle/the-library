## 10. The Cipher of Her Leaving

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and old rain, and I hated it because my mother would have loved it.

That was the shape of grief, I was learning. Not a wound but a lens. Every guttering candle, every crooked beam, every draft that sighed through the walls of Kestrel Delacroix's hiding places turned briefly into her—into the woman who would have pressed her palm to the stone and murmured something appreciative about the masonry's old bones. She had been dead to me for five years, and in one night of stolen revelations she had died again, more thoroughly, and left me holding a journal written in a language of thorns.

I sat at the table with the book open before me and did not touch it.

Kestrel had gone out—to find food, he'd said; to see what the wardens were doing to the city, which was closer to the truth. He'd left me his knife, which was either trust or an apology, and with Kestrel those were often the same thing. The blade lay on the table beside the journal like two arguments I hadn't finished having.

Outside, Vell continued its unraveling. The sky beyond the shuttered window was a black so absolute it looked painted on, no stars, no moon, nothing but the city's own fires reflected off low cloud—a bruised orange glow, like the sky itself had been struck and was healing badly. People said the wards had failed. People said the Inquisition had done it, or the Thorn Court, or God, or the stars had simply decided Vell wasn't worth shining on anymore. I knew better now. Or I suspected better. And suspicion, in my experience, was just grief wearing armor.

The journal had been in my satchel since before the night the stars went out. I had carried it through four cities and eleven years of not reading it, the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out. My mother's handwriting covered page after page—not her ordinary hand, the quick slanted script she used for market lists and bitter little poems, but a cipher, dense and deliberate, all angular strokes and hooked terminals, like frost creeping across glass.

She had called it *thornscript*, once, when I was small enough to ask questions instead of accusations. *It's how witches write when they don't want to be read,* she'd told me, tapping my nose. *Only memory can open it.*

At eight years old, I'd thought that was romantic. At nineteen, holding the book while my mother's killer—or betrayer, or victim, or whatever Kestrel Delacroix actually was—snored fitfully in the next room, I understood it was a lock, and the key was the cruelest possible thing: you could only read her words if you already remembered her.

* * *

Here is what memory-theft looks like from the outside, from the side that loses:

My mother used to buy memories the way other women bought ribbon. A taste of the sea for a homesick dockhand. A dead husband's laugh for a widow who'd begun to fear she'd invented him. I thought it was kindness then. I still think it was kindness—but kindness has edges, and I learned them the winter Yara came home from a job with someone else's wedding day behind her eyes and looked at my father as if he were a stranger wearing his face.

He forgave her. He always forgave her. That was its own kind of erosion. And when he died—fever, three bad days, nothing anyone could have stolen or returned—she changed. Grief made her careless, or brave, or both. She started taking jobs I wasn't allowed to ask about. She started sleeping with a knife under her pillow and a sigil drawn in chalk beneath the doormat. And one morning I woke to find her gone, her room cold, her good boots missing, and a journal wrapped in oilcloth sitting on the table like an accusation.

No letter. No explanation. Just the book, and the silence after.

For five years I had built a fortress of blame around that silence, and Kestrel Delacroix had been the cornerstone—the boy she'd saved, the thief she'd loved like a son or worse, the reason she ran. I had rehearsed his death a thousand times in a thousand alleys. Then last night I had finally put my blade where all those rehearsals lived, and discovered that the man under it wept when he spoke her name, and that hating him was like trying to hate the rain for falling.

So: the journal. The lock. The key.

I turned to the first page and let my memories come forward.

* * *

Thornscript does not yield to study. Odile had told me that once—I'd tracked down my mother's old fence in the gray market two years ago, desperate, and been sent away with pity and a warning. *You can't learn it, girl. It reads you.* What she meant, I understood now, watching the angular strokes swim and slide across the page like minnows, is that the cipher keys itself to association. Each glyph is a question. It wants the memory that answers.

And my mother had built the answers herself, out of me.

The first glyph resolved when I thought of red doors. Why red doors? Because when I was six, my mother had told me that all the important houses in the world had red doors, that you could always tell where magic lived by looking for red, and I had spent a whole summer knocking on strangers' doorways until she laughed until she cried and admitted she'd invented it to keep me away from the actual witch-house on Candlemaker's Row. Red doors. The glyph opened like a hand unclenching, and beneath it, faint as breath on a mirror: *Sable. You will be angry. Be angry well.*

My chest went tight. I kept reading.

The second glyph wanted the sound of rain on the canal boat—we'd lived on a canal boat for one glorious, doomed summer before the tax collectors found us—and the third wanted the smell of burnt sugar, and the fourth, fourth glyph stopped me cold, because it wanted my father's face, and I had not let myself see his face in years, had let it blur deliberately the way you let things blur when remembering is a wound you keep reopening. The glyph sat there on the page, patient as stone, and would not give up its secret until I dragged my father out of the fog I'd buried him in—his crooked smile, his hands rough with rope-work, the way he sang flat on purpose to make my mother correct him—and only then, only when I was aching with him, did the ink resolve into words.

She had keyed her own secrets to everything she knew I would never forget. Everything I would carry whether I wanted to or not. Reading her journal meant walking through my entire childhood with my eyes open, and she had known that, and she had done it anyway, because—

*Because she wanted me to remember her while I read it,* I whispered, and the empty room said nothing, and the candle flame bent as if bowing.

I read until the candle was a puddle. I read through the hour when the city bells should have rung midnight and didn't—no one ringing bells anymore, everyone too busy watching the starless sky and waiting for the world to admit it had ended. I read, and the woman on the pages stopped being a riddle and became my mother again, which was worse, and better, and necessary.

* * *

The middle pages were operational notes, dry as ledgers, and I nearly skimmed them—the Thorn Court, names crossed out and rewritten, routes through cities I'd never heard of. But some phrases snagged like fishhooks.

*They do not want power. They want the war back. There is no profit in peace for men like Ashvine; his courtship is courtesy and his courtesy is a kind of hunger.*

*Ashvine. I had heard the name from Kestrel, hissed like a curse. I filed it among the sharp things.

Then, deeper in, the handwriting changing—smaller, hurried, the glyphs losing their ornamentation as though she no longer had time to make them beautiful:

*I have found the door. Understar is not a legend and not a place, or not only a place. It is the loom. Every ward ever woven in this world draws its thread from there. The stars are its lanterns. And the seal the old witches set—Ashvine calls it a cage, and he is not entirely wrong—is failing. It was made to be maintained. Made to be fed. Memory freely given, freely renewed. No one has tended it in three hundred years.*

*Three hundred years of neglect. Three hundred years of the light going down by fractions so small no one noticed, the way a family doesn't notice they've grown poor.*

*Ashvine wants the seal broken. He believes what was caged there can be bargained with, weaponized, that the old war can be refought and won this time. He has offered me everything. That is how I know I must take everything from him instead.*

I turned the page and my hands were shaking badly enough that I had to flatten the paper against the table, pinning it like something that might fly away.

*There is a way to mend the seal properly. There is also a way to seal it anew, stronger than before, but it requires an anchor. A living anchor. Someone willing. Someone whose memories can feed the lock forever, the way a wick feeds a flame—slowly, endlessly, at the cost of everything the person was.*

*No one should be asked to do this. So I will not ask. I will do it, and I will arrange matters so that no one can follow me, and no one can undo it, and no one I love will ever know enough to feel responsible.*

*Sable. If you are reading this—if your stubborn heart has carried you through every door I built against you—then understand: I did not leave because of the boy. I did not leave because of you. I left because the sky was going dark a hundredth of a degree at a time, and I was the only person alive who knew how to stop it, and stopping it required that I stop.*

*Forgive me the cowardice of sparing you. Forgive me the arithmetic. You were the best of my life and I spent myself so that your sky would keep its lights.*

*But hear the rest, because it matters:*

*Seals are not eternal. Even mine will fray. I have woven warnings into the weave itself—when the seal weakens past mending-by-distance, the outermost lanterns will fail first. The border wards will fall. The stars will go dark, one sky-wide night, and anyone carrying a piece of the key will feel it burn.*

*I am leaving a piece of the key behind. Not by accident. Deliberately. Because if the seal fails, it must be refed from outside, and I cannot do it from inside alone. Look for the marked one. Trust the thief, even when he lies, especially when he lies—he lies the way frightened people pray, desperately and to survive.*

*The sigil knows the way down. Through the wardbones, below the city, below the oldest stones. Follow the burning.*

*Follow the burning, my love. Relight what I lit. And when you find me, I may not be me anymore—but I will know your footsteps. I have memorized your footsteps. They are the last thing I intend to keep.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there after the last legible page. Long enough for the candle to die entirely. Long enough for the orange bruise-glow outside the shutters to shift as the fireless hours passed. I sat in the dark with my mother's voice still echoing through my skull, delivered in the cipher only our shared past could unlock, and I did the thing I had sworn not to do in any alley, at any knifepoint, in front of any man.

I cried. Ugly, doubled-over crying, the kind with no dignity in it, the kind that comes up from somewhere below the ribs. Five years of blame coming out of me like a fever breaking. All that rehearsed hatred for Kestrel Delacroix, all those imagined deaths, and underneath it all the simple unbearable truth: she hadn't chosen him over me. She hadn't chosen anything over me. She had chosen the sky. She had walked into a dying light to keep my stars burning, and she'd done it without telling me because telling me would have made me follow her, and she'd known me well enough to know that.

*Be angry well,* the first page had said.

Well, mother. Here was my anger, burned down to its ash, and in the ash something else was smoldering. Resolve, maybe. Or inheritance. The stars going out wasn't the end of the seal—it was the warning shot. She had designed it that way. Which meant there was still time, a narrow and closing window, in which the seal could be refed from outside.

Which meant the wardbones were real. Which meant the way down existed.

Which meant Kestrel Delacroix, marked thief, carrier of the key, liar-who-prayed-desperately, was currently out wandering a collapsing city with the answer burning in his palm and no idea what it was for.

I wiped my face with my sleeve, took the knife off the table, and the journal, and stood. My legs had gone stiff from sitting; my eyes ached in their sockets. But under the exhaustion, something new was turning over, bright-edged and steady, an ember refusing the dark: not grief anymore. Direction.

The door of the safehouse opened on a whisper of cold air, and Kestrel came through it sideways, the way he always did, like a man perpetually expecting the room to be angrier than when he'd left it. He had bread under one arm and blood on his knuckles and the sigil on his palm was glowing—faintly, steadily, gold as the last coin in a beggar's cup.

He saw my face and stopped. "You read it," he said quietly.

"I read it." I held up the journal, then nodded at his blazing hand. "And unless I'm wrong, that's not a wound anymore. That's a compass." I pulled my cloak from the chair and shouldered past him toward the door, toward the stairs, toward the dark mouth of the city and whatever waited below it. "Come on, thief. My mother says we're going down. And she says you're going to stop lying to me—" I looked back once, letting him see that the fury in my eyes had changed its shape into something far more dangerous, "—because I'm done being spared."

Behind us, far above the starless rooftops of Vell, thunder rolled across a sky with no clouds.
