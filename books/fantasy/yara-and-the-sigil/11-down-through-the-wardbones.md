## 11. Down Through the Wardbones

The entrance to the undercity was not a door. It had never been a door. It was a wound that someone, long ago, had tried to dress as one.

We found it in the cellar of a tannery on Salt Lane, three streets from where Odile used to keep her shop before the fire took it and half the market with it. Sable pried up the flagstone while I held the lantern low, and beneath it there was no staircase—only a hole breathing cold air upward, air that smelled of wet stone and something older underneath, like the smell you find inside a bell that hasn't rung in a hundred years.

"That's not masonry," Sable said, running her fingers along the lip of the hole. The light caught her face from below, hollowing her cheeks, making her look like her mother in a way I wasn't ready for. "Masonry has joints. This doesn't have anything. It's like it grew."

"It did grow." I hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words came out with the certainty of memory—which was strange, because I had no memory of ever learning this. Only the sigil knew, and lately it had begun to speak through me, using my mouth when it grew impatient. "The wardbones were here first. Vell was built on top of them the way a barnacle builds on a whale. We're not entering a building. We're going under its skin."

She looked at me for a long moment. "Say things like that less," she said, and dropped through.

I followed, because following Sable had become, over these past days, the only direction I trusted.

* * *

The descent was longer than it should have been. That was the first wrongness. The tannery's cellar sat perhaps twelve feet below street level, but we climbed down handholds cut into the living rock—or what I kept wanting to call rock, though it gave slightly under my grip the way bark does, the way flesh almost does—and the shaft went down and down until the lantern's circle showed nothing above us but darkness and nothing below us but more darkness, and the air stopped smelling of tannery and began to smell of deep water and iron filings and, faintly, unbearably, of Yara's workshop: rosemary oil and scorched copper.

"Kestrel." Sable's voice came up from below, thin. "Are you smelling that?"

"Yes."

"What is it?"

I didn't answer. Some truths are too heavy to carry while climbing.

At last our feet found a floor, and we stood in a passage so tall the lantern couldn't find its ceiling, and so wide that our small circle of light seemed an insult to it. The walls curved away in both directions like the inside of a throat. And they were carved—I have no other word for it, though *carved* implies a carver and *growing* implies growth, and this was somehow both—the whole surface of everything covered in lines and channels and whorls that ran into one another and branched and rejoined, and every line glowed. Faintly. Barely. A gray-gold luminescence like embers seen through ash, dimmer than they should have been, flickering in long slow pulses.

Like veins. Like a body's veins, with blood still moving in them, but not enough of it.

"The wards," Sable whispered. She had her hand flat against the wall, and I saw the glow brighten faintly around her fingers, recognizing something in her blood. "When Vell's wards were strong, this is what they came from. All of it. Every warden's post, every gate-seal, every charm-seller's protection—it all drew from this."

"And now the stars are out," I said, "and the heart's barely beating."

The sigil answered before I finished speaking. It flared against my palm—not pain, exactly, not anymore; more like a hand closing around mine in the dark, familiar and insistent—and pulled. Not left or right along the passage. Down. Always down. It had been pulling since the morning the sky went black, gently at first, then with the growing insistence of a tide, and now, standing in the dark at the root of the world, I finally understood what it had been trying to tell me all along:

*I know the way home.*

"We go left," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, doubled, as if something old spoke the same words a half-breath behind.

* * *

We walked for hours. Or what felt like hours. Time behaves strangely in the wardbones; the pulse of the walls became our clock, that slow gray heartbeat, and after a while I stopped counting footsteps because the rhythm of my own steps had fallen into time with it, which unsettled me more than I let show.

Sable talked to keep the silence from getting in. She was good at silence, better than me, but even she had limits, and the wardbones pressed against them.

"My mother brought me here once," she said. "I was seven. I'd forgotten until just now—how do you forget a thing like this? She carried me on her shoulders and she said, *Sable, remember this place, because someday everyone will need to know how to find it, and hardly anyone will.*" She laughed, a short unhappy sound. "I thought she meant the cellars under the fish market. I remembered the fish market instead. Seven-year-old logic."

"She brought you down here." Something cold moved through me that had nothing to do with the air. "Before she left?"

"A year before. Maybe two." Sable's jaw set. She had been doing that more and more—setting her jaw against grief the way you set a broken bone, painfully, on purpose. "Which means she knew. Even then, she knew she was going to leave, and she was leaving me a map I was too young to read."

"You're reading it now."

"I'm walking it now," she corrected. "Reading would imply I understand any of it." She lifted the lantern high. The light reached maybe thirty feet before the dark ate it. "Look at this, though. Look."

She had stopped by a niche in the wall—a deliberate hollow, man-sized, and inside it, worn smooth by centuries of hands, a shape like a seat. Above the seat, carved deep and filled with something that still gleamed faintly silver, a line of script. Not Vellish. Not anything I knew. But the sigil knew. It turned over in my palm like a sleeper, and a word surfaced in my mind unbidden, in a voice I had spent five years failing to forget:

*Resting-niches. For the wardwrights, when the work was long. She used to say the old makers slept inside their own machinery, dreaming the city safe.*

"Sable," I said carefully, "what did your mother call these?"

"She didn't. Why?"

"Because I just heard someone who wasn't you describe them." I flexed my hand. The sigil settled, smug as a cat. "It does that sometimes. Feeds me things. It never used to." I looked down the endless passage ahead, glowing its faint dying gold. "Down here it's louder. Like it's closer to its own voice."

Sable studied me with an expression I was beginning to know—the one that weighed whether to be afraid of me. She always chose, in the end, not to be. I never asked her why. I was afraid the answer would be something about her mother, and I couldn't have carried it.

"Then let's hope it stays friendly," she said, and walked on.

* * *

We heard the sappers before we saw them—the particular ring of steel on stone that is nothing like a pickax and everything like a pickax, a sound with cruelty folded into it. Sable killed the lantern and pulled me into a resting-niche, and we fit together there in the dark, her back against my chest, both of us breathing as quietly as fear allowed.

They came around the curve of the passage six strong, Inquisition sappers in their slate-gray leathers, their lamps hooded to slits, and they were doing something to the wall. Chiseling out the glowing lines. Carefully, methodically, the way you'd extract a tooth—one of them catching the chips of luminous material in a leather bag as they fell.

"They're harvesting it," Sable breathed against my collarbone. "The ward-stuff. They're mining the wards."

Of course they were. The wards had fallen, the stars had gone out, the Inquisition had lost its leash on magic and gained, instead, a city full of terrified people looking for someone to burn—and here, underneath everything, was magic itself, lying exposed and bleeding, free for the taking. Brannoc's people wouldn't destroy the wardbones. They'd strip them. Sell the light back to the city a chip at a time.

And then I saw the seventh figure, at the rear of the party, and stopped breathing entirely.

He was not chiseling. He stood apart, hands clasped behind him, and where the sappers worked he simply watched—and the lines of the wall bent toward him. Not physically. They brightened near him, strained toward him like plants toward a window, and where his gaze rested longest they dimmed, going gray as old scars.

"He's drinking it," I whispered. "Sable, he's *drinking* it."

A mage. A live one, working openly with the Inquisition, feeding on the wardbones themselves. Which meant the Inquisition hadn't just learned to hunt magic. It had learned to keep it. To use it. Everything we believed about who hunted whom tilted sideways.

One of the sappers straightened, stretching his back, turning his hooded lamp our way—

And the sigil made its choice for me.

It didn't flare. That would have been loud, obvious, fatal. Instead it did something I had never felt it do: it reached out through the wall, through the dead-gray lines around our niche, and it *hummed*—a note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the marrow—and every line of the wardbones for fifty feet in either direction went dark at once.

Total blackness. The sappers' hooded lamps swung wildly. Someone swore. Someone else shouted about a surge, a dead section, and their officer's voice cracked across them—*keep working, it's the collapse spreading, the whole system's failing, that's why we're here*—and in the confusion of the dark I felt Sable move, drawing me backward, deeper into the niche, and we stood pressed together in absolute night while seven men argued ten feet away about whether the world was ending.

After a long time, the light returned to the walls—slowly, grudgingly, like breath coming back after a fall. The sappers moved on. The drinking mage paused at our niche, tilted his head like a bird considering something, and then walked past, trailing dimness behind him like a torn cloak.

Only when their lamps had faded entirely did I realize I could feel Sable's heart hammering through both our jackets.

"The sigil hid us," she said.

"The sigil hid us."

"It's never done that before."

"No," I said. "It hasn't."

She was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of everything: "Kestrel. Whose side is it actually on? Yours? Its own? Or hers?"

I had no answer. The sigil pulsed once in my palm, warm and patient and utterly unreadable, and pulled again—down, and left, and onward.

* * *

An hour later—we thought; time had gone soft by then—we found the camp.

Sable smelled it first: woodsmoke, old, and under it the sourness of unwashed human beings. We doused the lamp and followed our noses around a final bend, and there the passage opened into a natural cavern where a vein of the luminous material ran thick across the ceiling, pooling the gray-gold light like a moon caught indoors.

Someone had lived here. Recently. A fire ring, swept and cold. Bedrolls, four of them, empty. A cook pot. Boots by the fire, still upright, waiting for feet.

And pinned to the cavern wall with an ordinary iron nail, in handwriting I knew better than my own—because I had copied it, letter by letter, onto charms for five years, practicing it in the margins of ledgers until I could reproduce it in my sleep—was a page of notes.

Cipher. Her cipher. The same shifting script that filled Yara's journal, except these notes were practical, hurried, a supply list and a route: *tallow, rope, the second resting-niche past the singing gallery, do not drink the black water, M. insists on going back for the boy, I have told her no, we will see who wins.* Dated. Dated eleven days ago.

Eleven days ago was five days after the stars went out.

"Kestrel." Sable's voice had gone very careful, very flat, which was how she sounded when she was closest to breaking. "This is her handwriting."

"I know."

"These are her notes. This is her bedroll—that's her patch on the corner, the green thread, she sewed that on when I was—" Sable stopped. Pressed her fist against her mouth. When she spoke again she had rebuilt herself out of rubble, brick by brick, and I understood suddenly and completely why Yara had raised her daughter to be made of stubbornness. "There were four of them. She wasn't alone. And they were headed *down*, the same way we are, eleven days ago—and Kestrel, there are no bodies. If she came through here, she might still—"

"Don't." I said it gently, but I said it, and I watched hope flinch in her face like a struck animal. "Sable. If your mother is down here—if she's been down here since before the seal started failing—then she knows things we don't. About what's waiting below. About what happened to the people who came before us." I took the page off the wall, folded it, and put it inside my jacket, against the sigil, which warmed at her proximity the way it always did. "We follow them. But we follow them as evidence, not as hope. Hope down here will get us killed faster than wardens."

"You're a coward," Sable said, without heat.

"I'm a survivor. There's a difference, and it's kept me alive long enough to stand next to you."

She turned away to check the bedrolls, and I let her believe I was unmoved. But the sigil was pulsing against Yara's folded page like a second heart, urgent now, nearly frantic, pulling harder than it had ever pulled—down through the wardbones, toward whatever waited at the root of Vell—and as I stood there in the trapped moonlight of that buried cavern, a new understanding opened under my feet like a trapdoor:

The sigil wasn't leading us to the Understar.

It was leading us to whoever had taken the fast road first.

Four bedrolls. One patched with green thread. Cold ashes, no bodies, boots left behind—because whoever wore those boots had gone on barefoot into the dark rather than wait.

Sable turned back to me, her face gray-gold and terrible with hope she was refusing to show.

"They went down yesterday's walk," she said. "Maybe two."

The sigil flared, and somewhere far below us—farther than any of those bedrolls could have carried anyone—I heard, rising up through the bones of the city, thin and sweet and wrong, the sound of many voices singing in unison.
