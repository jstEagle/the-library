## 12. Brannoc's Net

The wardbones hummed beneath our feet like a struck bell that never finished ringing.

Sable heard it too. She kept pausing, tilting her head the way she did when her mother's journal spoke to her through the cipher, one hand flat against the pale ribs of stone that arched overhead. The tunnel we'd found—or that the sigil had found for us—was older than anything Vell had built above it, older than the wards, older maybe than the city's name. The walls were not carved. They were *grown*, if stone could be said to grow: long curving struts of white and gray, veined with faint gold light that pulsed in time with nothing so simple as a heartbeat.

"It's getting louder," Sable said. "The humming."

"That means we're going the right way."

"Or it means something up ahead knows we're coming."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I lifted the candle stub higher instead. Wax had run down over my knuckles and hardened there, a small white armor over the hand that bore the sigil. The mark itself was quiet tonight—no burning, no flare—just a low warmth, like a coal banked under ash, pulling gently toward the left-hand passage at every fork. I had learned in the last two days to trust the pull the way sailors trust tide. It had never once led us wrong.

It also made me feel like bait on a very long line.

"Kestrel." Sable's voice dropped. "Stop walking."

I stopped. Ahead of us the tunnel bent, and around the bend came light—not the gold pulse of the wardbones but torchlight, orange and unsteady, and beneath it the sound I should have noticed three turns ago and hadn't: boots. Many boots. The measured, unhurried cadence of men who did not need to run because they already knew where you were going.

A net doesn't chase. A net waits.

"Back," I breathed.

"Too late for back." Sable's hand found mine in the dark, cold fingers closing over the sigil as if she could shield it. "Look."

Behind us, at the mouth of the last junction, torches bloomed one by one like a foul flower opening. Wardens. A dozen at least, in their slate-gray cloaks, iron ward-masks catching the light in blank silver grins. And at their center, taller than the rest, his helm under his arm instead of on his head, stood Warden-Captain Brannoc.

He looked tired. That was the first thing I noticed, and it unsettled me more than the crossbows would have. In the market, in my shop, across the interrogation table with its charming little instruments, Brannoc had always been granite. Now there were grooves around his eyes that hadn't been there five years ago, and his hair had gone the color of wet ash, and he held himself the way old soldiers hold themselves when the war has gone on past all reason and they are the only ones left who remember what started it.

"Master Delacroix," he said. His voice carried down the tunnel without effort, the way voices do in places built to carry prayers. "You've made this difficult. I'll thank you not to make it bloody."

* * *

There is a particular quality to being surrounded underground. Above ground, in a city, being cornered is a geography problem—there is always another alley, another roof, another crowd to dissolve into. Underground, cornered is simply a fact. The stone has decided, before you were born, where you will stand.

I counted anyway. Old habits. Twelve wardens visible, likely four more holding the side passages. Two crossbows cranked and leveled, bolts fletched with warded quills—iron-banded, sigil-scribed, designed to punch through exactly the kind of magic I'd been pretending for two days I didn't have. The purge at the Market of Small Mercies had burned in Vell's memory for forty-eight hours now; every warden in the city knew the marked thief had pulled light out of a firestorm and walked a fence-woman through collapsing flame untouched. There would be no pretending tonight.

"I'm not armed," I said, which was true, and held up both hands, which showed them the sigil, which was perhaps less clever than it felt.

"We know," Brannoc said. "Miss Yara-Sable." He inclined his head toward Sable with a courtesy that somehow made everything worse. "Daughter of Yara the witch. Your mother cost me eleven good wardens over the years, one way or another. I'd hoped you'd be less trouble."

"She usually is," Sable said. "I'm much worse."

Something twitched at the corner of Brannoc's mouth. Died there. He let his sergeants arrange themselves with the quiet efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times, and then he did the thing I dreaded most: he lowered the torch, sat down on a fallen strut of wardbone as though it were a bench outside a tavern, and gestured to his men to hold.

"No pyre?" I asked.

"You've seen what's coming," Brannoc said. Not a question. "You've been under here longer than I have. You've seen the veins going dark."

I had. We both had. For the last day the gold light in the wardbones had been guttering, stretch by stretch, like lamps running out of oil. Whole galleries now stood black, and in the blackness the city above shuddered—wards failing street by street, gates groaning, the plague-ward fires burning green. Vell was a body, and the body was dying, and here we stood inside its skeleton while a man whose job was to burn people like me looked at me with exhausted eyes and asked, in essence, for help.

"The Inquisition wants you," Brannoc said. "The Prelate wants you in a collar by month's end. I'm telling you plainly, Delacroix, as one man who's watched this city rot from underneath to another: I don't care what you are anymore. I care that the stars are out and the wards are failing and children in the Lowfer district are waking up screaming because whatever lived in those stars can see them now." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, torchlight carving his face into ridges. "Help us. Tell us what's under here. Tell me how to stop it. Do that, and I'll keep the Prelate's hounds off you until my authority runs out—and then I'll walk you to the border myself."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I put you in irons and deliver you to the Prelate, and everything under here goes dark while you burn." He said it without relish, which frightened me more than relish would have. "I've stopped enjoying the alternative, Master Delacroix. That's the only mercy I have left to offer you. Take it or don't."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stolen memories: they weigh.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. When I lift a memory from a grieving widow—a single hour of her husband's voice, say—I carry that hour afterward the way a courier carries a sealed letter. It sits behind my breastbone with the others, hundreds of them, thousands, a hoard of other people's brightest and worst. Most fences deal in gems or coin. Odile dealt in charms; I dealt in hours. I knew the weight of every one.

And among them, near the bottom where I kept the things I almost never sold, were three memories I had taken five years ago from a young warden named Tam Brannoc, on the worst night of my life, in a rain-soaked alley behind the Foundling Hospital, to save my own skin.

Tam Brannoc had been the Captain's son. He had been nineteen. He had laughed too loudly and worshipped his father and kept, folded in his breast pocket, a charcoal sketch of a girl he was too shy to speak to. On the night I met him he had caught me lifting a charm from a dying charm-wife, and he'd had his baton raised, and I had panicked, and I had done the thing I swore I would only ever do in pieces, gently, sparingly.

I had taken his whole evening. All of it—the patrol, the alley, the sketch, the girl's name, his father's face saying *I'm proud of you, lad*, said that very dusk for the first time in years. I took it all because I needed cover to vanish, and a man who cannot remember the last three hours cannot testify about them. I sold none of it. I told myself that made it different. That made it clean.

Three days later Tam Brannoc rode out on a patrol he no longer remembered agreeing to and died at the border when the wards first flickered—died confused, they said, asking his fellows why they were riding and where his sketch had gone. They never found the sketch. I had it still, along with everything else, sealed in the vault of my chest like a debt that gathered interest.

Warden-Captain Brannoc did not know I had them. But I knew. And sitting there on the cold stone of the wardbones, watching a father offer me the last mercy he had, I understood with terrible clarity that I was holding a knife made entirely of his son.

* * *

"Captain," I said.

My voice came out wrong. Sable looked at me sharply; she knew my merchant's voice, my liar's voice, my frightened voice. This was none of them.

"There's something you should understand about what I am," I said. "Before you decide what I'm worth."

"Kestrel," Sable whispered. "*Don't.*"

But the sigil was warm against my palm, and the thought had already taken root, ugly and practical and utterly myself: *he will take us in irons, and the Prelate will burn us, and the stars will stay dead, unless you give him a reason to let you walk. You have the reason. You've carried it for five years.*

Mercy, Yara had once told me, in a memory I owned fair and honestly, is the sharpest thing in the world, because no one ever arms themselves against it.

"Five years ago," I said, "your son caught me stealing. Behind the Foundling Hospital. In the rain."

The change in Brannoc's face was slow, like ice cracking across a pond. Every warden in the tunnel had gone absolutely still.

"His name was Tam," I went on, because I had passed the point where stopping was possible. "He was proud of you. That same evening you'd told him—" My throat closed. I pushed through it, because a bargain half-spoken is cruelty without purpose. "You told him you were proud of him. First time since his mother died. He carried your words next to his heart for three hours. Then he met me."

"Don't." Brannoc's voice had gone very quiet. The granite was gone entirely now. Underneath was just a man, old and hollowed, gripping his own helm hard enough to whiten his knuckles. "Thief, whatever you're about to sell me—"

"I'm not selling," I said. "That's the horror of it, Captain. I never sold them. I took them to escape, and I kept them, and I have carried your son's last evening with me every day for five years like a stone in my chest. I know the sketch. I know the girl's name was Maren. I know he was afraid you didn't love him and overjoyed to learn he was wrong. I know how he died wondering what he'd forgotten." I opened my hand. The sigil pulsed once, gold against the dark. "I am the reason your son died confused. And I am also the only place in this world where the last three hours of his life still exist."

Silence. Even the wardbones seemed to hold their hum.

One of the crossbowmen shifted, and Brannoc raised one finger without looking at him, and the man froze.

"You're bargaining with my boy," Brannoc said at last. Each word laid down like a coffin board.

"Yes." I did not look away. He deserved better than a man who flinched. "And I am ashamed of it, and I would do it anyway, because the alternative is that we all die down here in the dark with the truth still locked in my chest. So here is the bargain, Captain, and I'll make it plainly because your son deserved plain dealing and got me instead: you let us pass. You keep your Prelate off us for as long as you are able. And I will give you back every hour of Tam that I hold—not sold, not shown, *given*, whole, the way his own they were meant to be. You'll fall asleep tonight a warden and wake tomorrow remembering your son's laugh from the inside."

Brannoc stood. Slowly, like a man much older than his years. He crossed the distance between us until I could smell the torch-smoke in his cloak and see the wet shine in eyes that had stared down witches and pyres and the end of the world without blinking.

"If this is a trick," he said, "I will build your pyre with my own hands."

"If it's a trick," I said, "you'll never know, because I'll be gone. That's the only kind of trick I'm good at."

For a moment—one heartbeat, two—he was close enough to kill me, and I think he wanted to, and I think some part of him wanted it *because* I was offering the thing he wanted most, because accepting it meant admitting it existed somewhere outside his grief. Then his gaze dropped to my open palm, to the sigil burning there, and something in his face shifted and settled, like a load redistributing on an old pack animal.

"Your mother," he said to Sable, without turning, "once told me magic was just love that learned to carry a blade. I called it witch sentiment. I burned her colleague for saying less." He exhaled, long and shaking. "Stand your men down."

* * *

Giving a memory back is nothing like taking one.

Taking is quick, a fishhook of will, done before the giver feels anything. Giving—Yara's counter-art, the craft Sable had spent three bitter nights teaching me in safehouses and stairwells—is slow. It is a pouring out. You must hold the other person's hands, or press palms with them, or in Brannoc's case grip a stranger's forearm like a fellow soldier, and you must want the return more than you want to keep the weight, and the memory travels the way water finds a dry bed: reluctantly at first, then all at once.

The alley came first. The rain. Tam's young face lit by a ward-lamp, indignant and alive. Then the sketch, the charcoal girl with the crooked smile. Then the dusk before it, and the words, and the way Tam had stood straighter for an hour afterward, taller by exactly the length of his father's approval.

Brannoc made no sound. But his hand on my arm tightened, and loosened, and tightened again, and tears ran down into the grooves around his mouth and dripped off his jaw onto the white bone-stone of the floor, and none of his wardens looked away and none of them spoke, because whatever else the Inquisition was, these were men from a city where everyone had lost someone, and they knew what they were witnessing.

When it was done I felt lighter and emptier and filthier and cleaner than I had ever felt in my life, all at once, which I suspect is simply what it feels like to pay a debt.

Brannoc stepped back. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, once, brutally, the way men do when crying is a thing that happened to someone else. When he spoke his voice was rough as ballast stone.

"Sergeant. Give the thief your lamp. The deep passages flood past the third arch; keep to the right wall where the vein-light's strong." He turned away from me, because he could not yet bear to look at me, and I understood that too was part of the payment. "If I ever see you again, Delacroix, it'll be too soon. If the world ends before you fix it—" He paused. His hand rose briefly, touched his own breast pocket, where five years ago a sketch had been. "Then it won't matter what I do to you. Go."

We went. Sable took my sleeve and steered me past the parted line of wardens, past faces young and old and stunned, past a crossbowman who was quietly, helplessly weeping and pretending it was the dust. At the junction's mouth she glanced back.

"Captain," she said. "The third arch. There's something written on it, in the old cipher. My mother's cipher. It says *the door remembers names*. Bring your men around the long way and you'll live longer."

Brannoc looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, a soldier acknowledging intelligence received, and turned to his men, and we were moving again, down and down, lamp in hand, the wardbone-veins glowing faintly brighter ahead of us as if the city itself approved of what had just happened.

We walked nearly a quarter mile in silence before Sable finally spoke.

"You gave them back. All of it?"

"All of it."

"Do you feel it? The hole?"

I did. Of course I did. Somewhere behind my breastbone, where Tam Brannoc's three hours had sat for five years, there was now a shaped absence, a Tam-sized silence, and I would carry that absence the way I carried everything else. But the strange thing—the thing I couldn't yet say aloud—was that the absence ached less than the possession ever had.

"Yeah," I said. "I feel it."

Ahead of us the passage opened into darkness, and the darkness was moving.

Not water. Not wind. Figures—dozens of them, filing silently through the great gallery beyond, each carrying a hooded lantern of a design I had never seen, each walking with the smooth synchronized grace of people who shared a single mind. And at their head, tall and thin and unmistakably courteous even at fifty paces, a man in thorn-gray paused, lifted his lantern, and inclined his head as though greeting guests he had been expecting all evening.

"Well," said Lord Ashvine, pleasantly. "There you are."
