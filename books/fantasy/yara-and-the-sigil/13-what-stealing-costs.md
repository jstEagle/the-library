## 13. What Stealing Costs

My mother taught me one rule before she taught me to read: *never let anyone hold your hand in the dark.*

She said it lightly, the way you'd say *mind the stove*, but I was seven and Yara never said anything lightly. So when Kestrel Delacroix reached for my wrist in the wardbones—steadying me over a broken stair—I pulled away so hard I nearly fell anyway.

He didn't apologize. He never did. He just kept walking, that burned palm of his tucked against his ribs like a secret he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was a secret at all.

We'd been down in the dark for two days. Two days of breathing stone dust and eating dried plums gone leathery in their paper wrappings, following the pull of his sigil through tunnels that had no right to still stand. The city above us was a rumor now—a weight on our backs, a sound like a thousand distant bells whenever the wards shuddered. Below, everything was quiet in the way of things that are waiting.

I hated him most in the quiet.

Not because of the ambush in the alley, though I still dreamed of it—not the fight, but the look on his face when my blade touched his throat. Not even because of what he'd confessed on the road, that my mother had given him her sigil willingly and wiped the why from his mind like chalk from a slate. No. What I hated him for lived in his pockets.

I'd seen them. The night before we descended, while he slept with his back against a pillar and his coat bunched under his head, three small glass vials had rolled free. Each one held a curl of light, dim as an ember under ash. A widow's grief. A merchant's first love. Small things, cheap things—the inventory of a charm-seller's day.

Except they weren't his to sell. And every one of them meant someone in Vell walking around with a hole where something warm used to be.

* * *

I confronted him at the cold hearth.

The wardbones were threaded with old way-stations, rooms the first builders had cut into the rock, and this one had a hearth big enough to roast an ox though there was nothing left to burn but a few pale roots and the pages of a book some refugee had abandoned decades ago. Kestrel crouched over the little fire, turning a skewer of roots with the absent precision of a man who had fed himself badly for a very long time.

"Show me how you do it," I said.

He didn't look up. "Do what."

"You know what."

"The roots are almost—"

"Kestrel." His name came out wrong, sharp-edged, the way it always did in my mouth. My mother's name for him had been different. I'd found it in the journal, in the cipher: *kest*, she'd written once, *little kest*. A small falcon. She had called him something gentle, and I could not make my tongue do the same. "Show me the theft. All of it. I want to see what it costs."

That made him look up. In the firelight his face went careful, the way it always did when anyone got too near the truth of him. He was handsome the way ruined buildings are handsome—all arches and absence—and I despised that I'd noticed.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because we're going into whatever's making those stars go out, and I don't trust anything about you that I haven't watched with my own eyes." I sat down across the fire from him, deliberately close enough that the heat pressed against my knees. "And because I want to know what you took. From them. From all of them. Every widow and lovesick merchant whose lives you sold by the dram."

Something moved behind his eyes—shame, maybe, or its well-worn costume. "It's not what you think."

"Then show me."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire chewing the roots and, far off, the deep bell-note of the wards flexing. Then Kestrel sighed, set the skewer aside, and drew one of the vials from his coat. He held it up between thumb and forefinger, and the ember-light inside stirred, pressing against the glass like a moth.

"A memory isn't a thing," he said. "It's more like—" He stopped. Started again. "You know how a room smells different after people leave? The memory is the person. The hollow is the room."

"Show me," I said again, quieter.

"It's easier if I show you on something small." He hesitated. "Mine. I'll take one of mine, and you watch. Agreed?"

I nodded, not trusting myself to speak.

He uncorked the vial, turned his left hand palm-up, and pressed the open mouth of the glass against the sigil.

The burn flared white. The firelight bent toward it, every shadow in the room leaning like grass in wind, and I felt the air change pressure the way it does before lightning. And then—through no sense I can name, through something behind my eyes—*I saw*.

A market street in Vell. Rain. A woman laughing under an awning, flour on her hands, and the taste of honey bread, and the specific joy of being twenty years old and certain the rain would pass—

The vision snapped shut. Kestrel sat gasping, the vial in his hand now glowing faintly brighter, and his other hand was pressed flat against his chest as if holding something in.

"What was that?" I whispered.

"My sixth winter." His voice came out roughened. "There was a baker on Candle Lane. My mother bought me honey bread when the rain came. That's all it was. Sixty seconds of being happy." He stoppered the vial. His hand shook, just slightly. "Now ask me about my sixth winter."

"I don't understand."

"I can't answer." He met my eyes across the fire, and his were wet—not crying, exactly; more like a man who has walked into a room and found the furniture gone. "I know it happened. I have the shape of it. But when I reach for it, there's nothing. It's like a tooth-socket. My tongue keeps going back to it and back to it and there's just—" He made a small, terrible gesture, fingers closing on empty air. "That's the cost. That's what I sell. I take the warmest hour someone owns, and they keep the rest of their life around a hole shaped like it."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, impossibly far, Vell rang its frightened bells.

"And the widow," I said. "The grief you took. Do you know what a person is without their grief? It's the proof they loved anyone at all."

"I know." It came out flat and immediate, no defense in it at all, and somehow that was worse than any excuse would have been. "I've known since the first one. You think I don't see them afterward? They come back to the market, weeks later, trying to buy back what they sold. *It doesn't feel like mine anymore,* they say. *The funeral. My husband's voice.* Because I didn't take the memory whole—I took the weight out of it. They remember the facts and feel nothing, and nothing is worse than hurting." He looked down at his scarred palm. "I told myself rules. Never steal a whole life. Never take from the desperate. Never be remembered. As if rules made it clean."

"Why do it, then?"

"Because it was that or starve." And then, quieter, with something raw underneath: "Because when you carry a hole inside you, it helps to know you're not the only one."

I hadn't expected honesty. I had prepared, over two days of dark tunnels, for cleverness—for the silver tongue that talked warden patrols into alleys and talked me out of killing him. Honesty disarmed me completely. I sat with my hands around my own elbows and understood, suddenly, that I was looking at a man who had been robbing himself for years and calling it a trade.

"She hated it," I said. "What you did. She must have."

Kestrel was quiet a moment. "She never said so."

"She didn't have to." My throat tightened. "She spent her whole life putting things back. Did you know that? People brought her their dead and their damaged hours—not to remove them. To mend them. She called it the counter-art. *Giving back*, she called it, when she was being plain about it. She used to sit up nights working on memories the way a seamstress works on torn cloth. I used to fall asleep listening to her whispering to them." My voice cracked on the last words and I let it. Let him hear what he'd helped break. "And her daughter gets a blade across the throat from the boy who sells holes."

Kestrel closed his fist around the vial. For a long time he said nothing, and the fire ate itself lower, and I watched him arrive somewhere I couldn't follow.

"Sable." My name in his mouth sounded foreign, formal, like a word from a dead language. "Swear me something first."

"You're in no position to ask oaths."

"Humor me." He turned the vial over in his fingers. "This is the last one I'll ever sell. The widow's grief. When we get out of here—if we get out—I'm giving it back to her. Her name is Maren. She lives over the chandler's shop on Dyer's Row. I've known where she lives for four years." He swallowed. "I've known for four years and I never went back, because giving it back seemed impossible and keeping it seemed survivable. So swear me the oath first, and then I'll teach you what I should have learned from her instead of stealing from her memory."

"What oath?"

"Never let me take from you." His voice dropped low, urgent, stripped of all its market-day smoothness. "Not a memory. Not ever. Not to save your life, not to save mine. You carry things whole, Sable. Your anger, your mother, all of it—even when it cuts you. I've watched you for months now and you don't have a single hollow in you, and I—" He stopped, and I watched him decide to finish the sentence honestly. "I need one thing in this world I haven't put a hole in. Swear it."

The fire had gone down to embers. In their light his sigil glowed faintly through his skin, my mother's mark, pulsing slow as a second heart.

"If I swear," I said carefully, "you'll give me the counter-art. All of it."

"All of it."

"Then I swear. By the stars that went out and the ones coming back—you will never take from me, Kestrel Delacroix, not one hour, not one breath of one hour, while either of us lives." I leaned forward so he couldn't mistake me. "And if you break it, I will find a way to make you remember everything you've stolen. Every hollow. All at once."

Something like relief crossed his face—quick and naked before he could hide it. "Fair terms."

"Now teach me."

* * *

He started with the widow's grief.

"Not the vial," he said. "The vial's just storage. The work happens here." He laid his bare palm open on his knee, sigil upward. "When I steal, I pull. The mark draws, like a lung inhaling. My mother's art must be the opposite—but I don't know how she did it. I only know how mine works, and I think—" He exhaled. "I think if I can learn to push instead of pull, the rest follows. Will you guide me? You knew her craft. I only knew her hands."

So it became a strange lesson, there in the ruin of a way-station older than the city, the thief and the witch's daughter hunched together over a dying fire.

He placed the vial against his sigil again, but this time he tried to reverse it. I felt the effort like standing near a millwheel forced to turn backward—the air groaned, the ember-light in the glass thrashing—and then the memory surged halfway up his arm and stalled, caught, and he cried out and tore the vial away.

"It won't go," he panted. "It's like pushing rope uphill. The mark knows how to take. Taking is a shape it knows."

"Then change the shape." I thought of my mother at her worktable, lamplight on her hands, whispering to a folded memory like lulling a child. She had never pulled. She had *invited*. "You're forcing it. She never forced anything. She asked. She made the memory want to return." I reached across and, remembering my own rule, did not take his hand—only rested my fingertips above his wrist, hovering over the burn. "Try again. But this time, don't push. Open. The way you open when you steal, but facing outward. Offer it a door instead of a fist."

He stared at me. Then he laughed—one short, broken sound. "That's what she used to say. About doors." Something in his face went young and lost. "I'd forgotten. I gave the forgetting away years ago, I think. Sold it cheap."

"Try," I said gently.

He set the vial down. This time he didn't press it to the sigil—he simply cupped it in both palms, and I watched him do the hardest thing I'd ever seen a person do with their own soul: stop. Stop gripping. Stop taking. His shoulders lowered. His breath slowed. His scarred hand opened like a window.

The ember-light rose out of the glass on its own.

It climbed his wrists, his forearms, sank beneath the skin without a wound, and traveled—I could see it, a warm thread under the surface—up along the old channels of his thefts, back through five years of hollow places, filling each one as it passed. I watched his face while it went. Watched the tooth-sockets of him close over. When the light reached his chest it bloomed there, brief and gold, and faded.

Kestrel sat very still.

"Well?" I asked.

His eyes were streaming. He didn't wipe them. "Honey bread," he said, wondering, wrecked. "Rain on the awning. Flour on her hands. She was laughing at me because I got rain in my boots and I didn't care." He looked at his palm as if it belonged to a stranger. "Sable. It doesn't hurt going back. It *heals* going back."

"That's the whole art," I said, and my own voice failed, because above his hand the sigil had changed—just slightly, just for a heartbeat—the burn-shape softening into something rounder, warmer, closer to the mark I remembered tracing on my mother's worktable when I was small enough to sit in her lap. "That's what she spent her life doing. One returned memory at a time. She believed—" I stopped, steadied. "She believed the world's wards were built from it. From given-back things. Freely returned. That's why they're failing, maybe. Everyone takes, and no one gives back, and the seal starves."

Kestrel looked down at the last vial—the widow Maren's grief, glowing patiently in the glass—and then at me.

"Maren first," he said. "When we reach the Understar. If the seal mends with freely given memory—" He turned the vial in his fingers. "Then maybe it doesn't only want mine. Maybe it wants everything anyone ever stole. Every charm in Vell. Every hollow, filled."

The idea landed in the dark room between us like a struck bell.

But before either of us could speak, the far tunnel breathed.

Not wind. Wind doesn't come in waves, rhythmic, many-throated. Wind doesn't sing.

The sound slid up through the stone floor and into my teeth—a low, layered humming, dozens of voices braided into one note, rising and falling like a tide. Like a choir. Kestrel was on his feet before I was, the vial vanishing into his coat, his face gone bloodless in the ember-light.

"You hear it too," I said.

"It's coming from below." He stared down at the black mouth of the descending stair, where the sigil's glow reflected off wet stone. "Sable—it knows we're here. It's been singing since we entered the wardbones. We just weren't far enough down to hear it until now."

The choir swelled, sweet and wrong, and beneath the fire's last embers I saw the hair on his arms rise, and I reached for my knife, and together we listened to the dark learn our names.
