## 14. The Hollow Choir

The wardbones sang to us before we saw them.

Not with sound, not exactly—it came up through the soles of our boots and settled in the teeth, a vibration like a tuning fork struck somewhere far below and never allowed to finish its note. Kestrel had felt it faintly for hours, a hum he'd mistaken at first for his own blood, then for the sigil waking in his palm. But as they descended past the third ring of Vell's buried foundations, the hum resolved into something with shape. Something with *structure*.

A melody.

"Sable," he whispered. "Tell me you hear it."

She stopped two steps ahead of him on the stair of black stone, her lantern held low so its light pooled around her feet instead of spilling into the dark ahead. She'd learned that trick down here—the dark down here was old enough to be shy of light, or hungry for it; neither of them wanted to find out which. Her face when she turned was pale in the lantern-glow, and there was ash from the collapsed tunnel still ground into the crease of her brow where she'd wiped her hand days ago and never washed.

"I hear it," she said. "It sounds like—" She stopped, frowning, searching for the word. "Like something trying to remember itself."

Kestrel knew the feeling intimately. He opened his left hand without meaning to. The sigil burned there in its usual way—a low coal-glow beneath the skin, the scar of an old brand shaped like nothing and everything, a wheel with too many spokes, a star seen through water. But now the glow pulsed. In time with the melody rising through the stone.

*It knows this song,* he thought. *Or it's part of it.*

They went on down. There was no other direction. Behind them lay the rubble of the passage Brannoc's net had failed to close on them—failed, because the sigil had flared and brought half a mountain of ancient masonry down between Kestrel and the Warden-Captain's face—and ahead, the pull in Kestrel's palm grew stronger with every step, a fisherman's line reeling him gently, patiently, toward whatever waited at the bottom of the world.

The stair ended at a gallery: a vaulted corridor wide enough for six abreast, its walls worked with carvings worn nearly smooth by time and by hands that must have touched them in passing for centuries. Kestrel dragged his fingers along one as he walked. Under the erosion he caught fragments—a procession of robed figures, a tree whose branches were also veins, a door rendered in such loving detail that he half expected to feel hinges. The city above had been built on these bones the way a barnacle builds on a whale, never asking what the whale thought about it.

The melody thickened here. And now he could hear voices in it.

"Wait." Sable caught his sleeve. "Listen."

He listened. Beneath the deep harmonic thrum of the stone itself—beneath what he'd taken for instrument, for architecture—he heard singing. Human singing. Dozens of voices, maybe hundreds, woven together in a chord that had no beginning and no end, each voice holding its single note with a devotion that sounded less like music and more like prayer, or like obedience.

The notes were perfect. That was the horror of it, once he understood what he was hearing. Not one voice wavered. Not one breathed wrong. Every singer held their tone with the flawless steadiness of something that no longer needed breath—or wanted anything else at all.

"That's the seal," Sable said quietly. "The failing seal. My mother's journal said—" She swallowed. "It said the Understar sings while it holds. A song of keeping. When the song falters, the stars go out."

"This isn't faltering," Kestrel said. "This is the most perfect thing I've ever heard."

"Yes." Her jaw tightened. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

The gallery bent twice, following the curve of something enormous beneath the city—some chamber or root-system of power far larger than any architecture had a right to be—and with each turn the singing grew until it filled Kestrel's skull the way water fills a bell. He found himself walking faster, drawn, and had to consciously slow his feet. The sigil pulsed hot against his palm. Twice he caught himself humming a note of the chord before he realized what he was doing and bit it off hard enough to taste copper.

"Don't join in," Sable said, without looking back. She'd noticed too. Of course she had. "Whatever you do."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Everyone does. My mother wrote that the song *wants* voices. It's how the seal eats. It offers you a note, and if you take it—" She didn't finish. Down here, unfinished sentences had begun to feel like mercy.

The last bend opened onto a ledge overlooking the cavern.

Kestrel had prepared himself for many things. He'd spent the whole descent building pictures in his head the way a man builds sandbags before a flood: a ritual site, an altar, Thorn Court agents in their thorn-black livery kneeling over some writhing engine of magic. He'd prepared for blood. For bodies.

He had not prepared for beauty.

The cavern was vast beyond the reach of the lantern-light, but it was not dark. Pale luminescence rose from the floor in slow columns, like heat-shimmer made visible, like the ghosts of auroras trapped underground, and in that light Kestrel saw the choir.

They stood in concentric rings on the cavern floor, hundreds strong, facing inward toward a center he could not yet see. They wore the clothes they'd been taken in—a baker's flour-dusted apron, a dockhand's oilskins, a fine coat with silver buttons gone green, a nightgown. Ordinary clothes. Vell clothes. Their faces were upturned, mouths open, each pouring out its single unwavering note, and their faces were—

Their faces were *empty*. Not slack, not vacant in the way of sleepers. Empty the way a house is empty after the family has moved out and taken everything, even the curtains, even the hooks from the walls. Kestrel looked into those upturned faces and saw no fear, no pain, no longing. Whatever those people had been, they were not imprisoned in themselves anymore. There was simply nothing left inside to suffer.

Except the singing. The singing was magnificent.

"Oh," Sable said. It was barely a sound. Her knuckles had gone white around the lantern's handle. "Oh, gods. It's them. It's the missing."

She was right, and Kestrel had known it would be, and knowing did nothing to blunt it. The disappearances had started weeks ago—not the dramatic vanishings the broadsheets loved, but the quiet kind, the kind that never made the papers because no one reported them. The friend who stopped coming to the taproom. The neighbor whose shutters stayed closed. Vell was full of people no one would miss, and the Thorn Court had understood, with terrible clarity, exactly how full.

"They're not dead," Kestrel said. His own voice sounded obscene against the purity of the chord, a smudge on clean glass. "Look—they're breathing. Fed, maybe. Kept."

"Kept for what?"

He didn't answer, because he already knew. He could feel the answer pressing up through the sigil like a fever. Each of those stolen people had once been full of memory—griefs, loves, first snowfalls, mothers' voices, every small hoarded treasure a life accumulates. And memory was the only currency the Understar accepted. The seal mended with freely given remembering—that truth had been carved into Yara's journal in cipher, and Kestrel had read it and felt it in his bones. But the Thorn Court had no patience for *freely*. They had found another way to fill the seal's hunger. They were feeding it whole people, hollowed and strung like instruments, each empty vessel holding a single sustained offering of what used to be a self.

The seal wasn't being repaired. It was being *played*.

"We have to get closer," Sable said.

"No."

"My mother is down there, Kestrel. Or what's left of her anchor is. I'm not standing on this ledge admiring the view."

"And if you step onto that floor, you'll end up in the next ring over, singing your one note until the sun burns out." He kept his voice low and level, the voice he used in negotiations with frightened clients. "Look at the edges of the rings. Look."

She looked. Between the outermost circle of singers and the cavern wall stood figures that did not sing. A dozen of them, maybe more, dressed in gray-green, faces shadowed under hoods, moving among the choir with slow ceremonial patience. Some carried ewers—watering the singers, or feeding them, or anointing them; at this distance Kestrel couldn't tell. Others carried long hooked poles, and with these they made small adjustments, turning a singer a degree, tilting a chin, tuning them.

Tuning them. Like a luthier walking the rows of his instruments before a performance.

"Thorn Court," Sable breathed.

"Ashvine's people. His gardeners." Kestrel's stomach turned. He thought of Lord Ashvine's voice—courteous, warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because the world arranged itself around his preferences. He thought of the offer Ashvine had made them in that lamplit cellar what felt like a lifetime ago: *the sigil for safe passage*, delivered with the gentleness of someone discussing the weather. He'd thought then that the man's menace lay in what was hidden behind the courtesy.

Now he understood the courtesy was the menace. You did not snarl at things you intended to keep. You tended them.

* * *

They should have withdrawn. Later, lying awake in whatever dark place sleep found them, Kestrel would return to that moment again and again—the ledge, the light, the two of them crouched behind a fallen pillar carved with the tree-that-was-also-veins—and ask himself why they hadn't simply backed away into the gallery and found another path around. He never arrived at an answer that satisfied him. The closest he could come was this: the singing pulled. It pulled at the hollow places in a person the way the sigil pulled at his palm, and both he and Sable had been carrying hollow places for years, and neither of them had ever learned to leave a wound unexamined.

So they crept along the ledge, following its curve downward toward a lower shelf of rock that jutted out over the choir's outermost ring—close enough to see faces clearly now, close enough to count.

And that was the mistake. That was the moment everything in Kestrel Delacroix's carefully rationed life broke open.

Because he counted, and counting meant looking, and looking meant seeing her.

She stood in the third ring, slightly left of center, wearing a shawl he knew—faded indigo wool, moth-eaten at the shoulder, the one she'd worn the day he'd first come to her back room smelling of rain and stolen goods, and she'd looked up from her ledger and said, *You have careful eyes, boy. Careful eyes are worth money down here.* Odile. His fence. The woman who had warned him, five years ago and again three weeks ago, that someone was asking questions about the marked thief; who had taught him prices and silences and which wards in Vell could be bribed with charm-work and which required coin; who had fed him soup on the anniversary of Yara's vanishing every year without once mentioning Yara's name, because she'd understood that grief needs company more than it needs acknowledgment.

Odile stood with her face turned up to the unseen center of the cavern, mouth open, pouring forth a single perfect note, and her eyes were open too, and there was nothing behind them.

Nothing. No recognition. No Odile.

The ledger-keeper, the soup-maker, the sharpest mind in Vell's gray market—all of it poured out of her and into the seal, refined down to one sustained tone, and the tone was beautiful, and that was the unforgivable thing. They hadn't even left her the dignity of discord.

Kestrel didn't realize he'd moved until Sable's hand closed on his arm, hard, dragging him back from the lip of the shelf.

"Stop. *Stop.* What are you doing?"

"She's in there." His voice cracked apart on the second word. "Sable, that's Odile. That's—she took me in when I was nobody, she—"

"Keep your voice *down*—"

"She's *in there* and she's *singing*—" The words wouldn't organize. Grief and rage came up his throat together like floodwater through a broken grate, and underneath them, worse than either, ran the cold knowledge that he had done this. Not him, not with his hands—but his trade. His market. Memory-thieves selling charms to frightened people, hollowing the givers a little more with every sale, teaching a whole city that a soul was a commodity with a fair price. The Thorn Court hadn't invented the theft of selves. They'd just stopped pretending it was commerce.

The sigil flared.

He felt it happen before he could stop it—a surge of heat up his forearm, white light bleeding between his clenched fingers—and the melody stuttered. Across the cavern floor, dozens of empty faces turned. Not all at once. Not mechanically. Worse: gradually, like flowers finding the sun, the nearest singers' heads rotated toward the ledge with a smoothness that had nothing human left in it, though the motion itself was perfectly, horribly human-shaped.

One of the hooded gardeners raised a hand. The heads paused mid-turn, attentive, obedient.

Then a voice spoke from the cavern floor, resonant and mild, pitched to carry without effort, and Kestrel's blood went cold because he knew it. Warm. Courteous. Unhurried.

"Well," said Lord Ashvine, stepping out from between two ranks of the choir as though he'd been waiting there all evening, which Kestrel suspected he had. "The key comes home. And it brings my old friend's daughter with it. How *convenient*."

Below, in the third ring, still singing, still empty, Odile's voice held its perfect note.

Kestrel pressed his burning palm flat against the stone, and the stone sang back.
