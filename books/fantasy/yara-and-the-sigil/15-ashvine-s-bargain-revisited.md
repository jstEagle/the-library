## 15. Ashvine's Bargain, Revisited

The dark beneath Vell did not like fire. We learned that in the third hour of our descent, when the torch I carried guttered sideways, as if the flame were trying to hide its face from something. The light bent. Not dimmed—bent, the way water bends around a stone, and for a long moment my own shadow lay wrong against the wardbone wall, stretched and jointed like a spider's leg.

"Stop that," I whispered to it, because after everything, talking to shadows still felt more reasonable than talking to what waited ahead of us.

Kestrel walked half a step behind me, which had become his habit and my irritation. He said it was so he could watch the tunnels at our backs. I knew it was because he couldn't stand to look at the sigil's glow bleeding up through his own fingers—the pale green light that pulsed now with every heartbeat, faster than it had a week ago, brighter, hungrier. The seal was failing. His hand was the proof, and he carried it the way he carried everything: close against his body, hidden, as if shame could be worn like a glove.

"It should be close," he said. "The pull's changed direction."

"Changed how?"

He turned his palm upward. The sigil's light didn't point anymore—it *swayed*, drifting between two headings like a compass needle caught between magnets. "Like something down here is pulling against it."

I heard it before I understood what he meant. A sound threading through the stone, so low I felt it in my teeth before my ears caught it. Singing. Hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, all holding one note—not a melody, exactly, but a sustained tone that rose and fell with a rhythm too slow and too regular to be anything alive.

"The choir," Kestrel said quietly.

"I know what it is." My mother's journal was wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs, and I pressed my arm against it without meaning to. Three days I had spent deciphering the last pages, using memories she'd left folded into the cipher like flowers pressed in a book. Three days learning that Yara Solenne had walked into the Understar with her eyes open. That she had sealed it from inside. That the stars going out meant her seal was thinning like ice in spring. "We go around it. There has to be another way to the door."

"There isn't." He said it gently, which made it worse. "I've checked the pull three times. Every path bends toward that sound."

* * *

We found the cavern by its smell before its sight: wet stone and old copper and underneath both, something sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in a closed room. The passage opened without warning—a seam in the rock widening into a vaulted hollow where the wardbones arched overhead like the ribs of something enormous and long dead.

And they were there.

The choir stood in rows on the cavern floor, arranged in concentric circles around a raised dais of fused stone. Men and women in the gray clothes of Vell's lower streets. A dockhand I recognized from Saltrow. An old woman still wearing an apron dusted with flour. They stood upright, swaying slightly, faces smooth and empty as eggs, mouths open, singing.

Their voices were the worst part. There was nothing in them—no strain, no effort, no self. Just sound poured out of hollow people the way water pours from a cracked jug.

"They're not dead," Kestrel breathed beside me.

"No."

"Can we bring them back?"

I thought of the counter-art my mother's journal taught in fragments—the slow, painstaking work of returning what was taken. One memory at a time. One person at a time. Years, maybe, for a single soul. "Some of them," I said. "If anyone ever finds them."

He looked at me then, and I saw him understand the arithmetic I was doing: this cavern, times however many like it, times the years it would take to undo it. His jaw tightened. "Ashvine did this."

"Ashvine ordered it. Someone else held them down and took." I had stopped believing in monsters who worked alone somewhere around the third cipher page. Cruelty was always a guild trade.

We might have slipped along the cavern's edge. We might have. But the sigil chose that moment to flare—green light spilling through Kestrel's fingers like dawn through shutters—and every empty face in the choir turned toward us at once.

The singing stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the sound. Two hundred mouths closing together, two hundred blank faces fixing on the light in Kestrel's palm with something that was not recognition and not curiosity but pure, unreasoning *orientation*—the way iron filings turn to a lodestone.

Then, from the far side of the dais, came the sound of slow, courteous applause.

"Lady Sable." Lord Ashvine stepped out from behind the stone platform, brushing dust from the sleeve of a coat the color of dried blood. He looked exactly as he had in the Thorn Court's parlor a month ago: tall, silver-haired, handsome in the way old portraits are handsome—all surface, no warmth, the paint cracked just enough to show the wood beneath. Behind him stood six of his people in thorn-gray, hands loose at their sides, which in Ashvine's service meant ready. "And the thief. How fitting that you found your way here. I would have sent an escort, but you two do so love to make your own entrances."

"We're not staying," I said.

"On the contrary." He gestured, and one of his people brought forward a lantern, hanging it from a hook on the dais. The light spread, and I saw what I hadn't seen before—that the dais was carved, ancient script spiraling inward across its surface, and that the spiral's center was a hole. A doorway down. The threshold. We were closer than I'd dared hope. "You are precisely where you have been going since the night the stars went out. You simply didn't know it yet. May I show you something?"

"No," said Kestrel.

"Yes," I said.

They spoke over each other, and for a heartbeat Ashvine's mouth curved—not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "Lady Sable has always been the reasonable one."

"Don't call me that. Don't use my name."

"As you wish." He inclined his head, gracious as a host at dinner. Then he snapped his fingers, soft as breaking a wishbone, and the choir began to move.

Not toward us. That was the horror of it—they didn't attack. They simply *rearranged*, flowing across the cavern floor in their silent rows until they ringed us completely, a circle of swaying, empty-eyed neighbors, close enough that I could smell the flour on the old woman's apron. A wall of stolen people.

"Please understand," Ashvine said, spreading his hands, "that none of this is hostility. It is... emphasis. Now. What I wished to show you."

He reached into his coat and drew out a small glass vial, no longer than my finger, stoppered with wax. Inside it something silver moved—not liquid, not light, but a slow turning shimmer, like moonlight caught in water and forced to stay.

"Do you know what this is?"

Kestrel knew before I did; I saw it hit him. "That's a memory."

"The finest extraction my artificers have ever produced. Unspoiled. Undiluted." Ashvine held it up to the lantern, admiring it. "It was taken from a woman of considerable power, in a place where memories are the only currency that holds value. Would you like to see whose?"

"You're lying," I said, and my voice came out strange, flat, very far away.

"I never lie, Lady Sable. Lying is a crutch for men without resources." He crossed the cavern—his people parting, the choir swaying aside for him as though he were a stone in a stream—and stopped before me, holding the vial out on his open palm. "I am going to tell you something I have told no one else, because I believe in dealing honestly with the people I intend to deal with. Your mother is alive."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. I heard my own blood. I heard the choir resume its low note, softer now, almost gentle. I heard Kestrel say my name—just *Sable*, sharp, warning—but it came from a long way off.

"She lives within the Understar," Ashvine continued, in the mild tone of a man discussing weather. "When she sealed it five years ago, she did not die doing it, whatever sentimental stories you've constructed. She became part of it. The seal requires an anchor—a living mind vast enough to hold the door shut against what presses on the other side. She is that anchor. Has been, every day since. Sustained. Preserved." A pause, delicately weighted. "*Aware.*"

"Aware," I repeated.

"For five years, Lady Sable, your mother has been conscious inside that door, holding it shut with everything she is. And she is *tiring*. The stars went dark because she is weakening. They will not come back on their own." He turned the vial slowly in his fingers, and the silver light washed his face, making it young and terrible. "This memory was taken from her the night before she descended. Her insurance, I suppose—she hid it where she thought the Thorn Court would never look. She was wrong. It contains, among other things, her final words. To her daughter."

My knees nearly went. I locked them. Somewhere behind me Kestrel had gone very still, and I could feel him wanting to reach for me and knowing I'd break his wrist for it, and loving him a little, in that moment, for the restraint.

"What do you want for it?" I asked.

"Sable." Kestrel's voice, low. "*Don't.*"

"I'm asking him a question."

"You know what I want," Ashvine said, and his eyes went past me, to the thief. "The sigil. Freely given, freely surrendered—I won't take it by force; it wouldn't serve. In exchange: safe passage out of Vell for you both, past the Inquisition, past the wards, anywhere in the wide world you care to live. And this." He weighed the vial in his palm like a coin. "Her last words. Her living face. Proof of life, when all Vell believes her dust."

"And if we refuse?" Kestrel asked.

Ashvine sighed, genuinely regretful, and that was the most frightening thing he'd done all night. "Then I take the vial through that door myself, with the key I already possess—" he tapped his own chest, where something under his shirt gave a faint answering pulse to the sigil's glow, some cold twin, some forged echo "—a lesser key, but sufficient. I break the seal. I let the old war back into the world. And your mother, Lady Sable, spends her final hours watching it happen, knowing her daughter chose a thief over her."

The choir sang. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above us, in a city that didn't know it was dying, a bell rang the hour.

"He's lying about something," Kestrel said to me, quiet, urgent. "Sable, listen to me—he's telling the truth about Yara because the true parts are what make the lie work. If she's alive in there, if she's the anchor, then giving him the sigil doesn't free her. The seal needs an anchor. Take away the key, take away the choice, and he doesn't release her from that door—he just becomes the only one who can decide what happens to her."

"He could get us to her," I said. "He could get us *past*—"

"To what end? So she can watch us hand him the weapon that kills her?" Kestrel stepped closer, and his voice dropped until only I could hear it. "I know what she is to you. I know. But think what she would want. Think what she *did*. She spent herself to shut that door, and you'd hand him the crowbar because he wrapped it in her handwriting?"

"It's her *voice*," I said, and hated how it broke. "It's five years, Kestrel. Five years she's been awake in the dark, and you want me to walk past her because the man holding the door makes a convincing argument?"

"I want you to walk past him," he said, "because the man offering you your mother is the same man who hollowed out a baker to sing in his cave."

I looked at the old woman with the flour on her apron. I looked at the vial, silver and turning, close enough to touch. My mother's last words. Maybe her only words left. Everything in me that was eight years old and waiting at a window reached for it.

And I almost said yes. I want to be honest, since honesty is the only inheritance she left me: I stood in that cavern and I weighed the whole starving world against one woman's voice, and the world lost. I would have fed every star in the sky to that darkness to hear her say my name again.

What stopped me wasn't wisdom. It was smaller than that. It was the flour on the apron.

She'd been baking. Someone had eaten her bread that morning, someone had called her by name, and now her name was gone and her morning was gone and she stood in the dark pouring herself out for a man who called it *emphasis*. If I took the vial—if I bought my mother's voice with the sigil—I would be buying it with her. With all of them. And Yara Solenne, who had burned her own life into a seal rather than let that thing through, would have died twice: once at the door, and once in her daughter's hands.

"No," I said.

Ashvine tilted his head. "Lady Sable—"

"I said no." My voice steadied as I said it, the way a rope goes taut. "You don't get her. You don't get him. You don't get any of it. Find another door."

"There is no other door," Ashvine said softly. "That is rather the point."

"Then you're out of doors."

For a moment—one long, flickering moment—I watched the courtesy drain out of his face the way color drains from a corpse, and what sat underneath was old and patient and hungry beyond anything the Inquisition could have shown me. Then he smiled again, and it was somehow worse.

"Well," he said. "I did promise honesty." He raised the vial between two fingers, and before I could move, before Kestrel could shout, he crushed it in his fist.

Glass rained. The silver spilled—not falling, but *rising*, unwinding upward through the dark like smoke with a shape in it, and for one impossible instant I saw her: dark hair, sharp chin, eyes the exact gray-green of mine, looking straight at me with an expression I had spent fifteen years trying to forget. Not love. Not farewell. *Urgency.*

Her lips moved. No sound reached me—Ashvine's artificers had taken that too, or the air down here ate it—but I read the shape of it, the way you read a shout through thick glass:

*Don't open it. Don't let him—*

And then the silver thinned, unraveled, and was gone, and Ashvine was brushing glass dust from his palm with a small silk handkerchief.

"A pity," he said. "She was always so dramatic."

Kestrel caught my arm. "Sable—"

"I saw her." My voice sounded torn out of me. "I saw—"

"She's alive," Kestrel said, grim, watching Ashvine. "Whatever he wants us to think that proved, it proves she's real, and she's fighting him. Which means he needs us more than he said. Which means we still have cards."

"I do admire optimism in the doomed," Ashvine remarked. He folded his handkerchief with neat little movements, and around us the choir began to sway forward, closing the ring like a noose drawing tight. "Now. Since negotiation has failed, let us speak instead of necessity. The seal weakens hourly, Lady Sable. Whatever you choose, someone must anchor that door—and my key can only carry me *to* the threshold, not through it. Your thief's sigil is the last living key. So you may give it to me, or keep it from me while I hold you both here until the choice no longer matters and I take it from whichever of you is still breathing." He glanced at the choir with something like fondness. "They don't tire. Do you?"

Kestrel's hand was burning. I could see it—the green light flaring up through his fingers, brightening, and the choir faltering as it did, faces twitching, the note in two hundred throats wobbling like a struck bell. The sigil knew its own. It pulled at what Ashvine had stolen, the way a magnet pulls at scattered iron.

And I remembered, all at once, standing in my mother's workroom at nine years old, watching her press a memory into a charm for a grieving widow, her voice in my ear: *Nothing taken stays taken, little bird. What was given can be returned. That is the whole of the art.*

Given. Not taken. Given.

"Kestrel," I said. "The choir. Can you feel them? All of them? They're hers—his takings—and the sigil *wants* them."

His eyes widened. "I can't return two hundred—"

"You don't have to. Just enough. Just *loud* enough."

The first warden of Ashvine's six lunged. I put my knife into the lantern hook's bracket and cut the lantern down, and in the sudden swinging dark Kestrel opened his fist, and the sigil blazed like a green sun.

What happened next, I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe. The choir sang—but differently now, raggedly, two hundred throats cracking as something long-stolen stirred in them, memories rising in them like bubbles through black water. The old woman with the floury apron dropped to her knees and began, horribly and wonderfully, to weep. The dockhand shouted a name—someone's name, a wife's, a daughter's—and the ring of bodies broke apart like a knot coming undone.

"Go," Kestrel gasped, white-faced, swaying, the light guttering in his hand as fast as he spent it. "The dais—the door—"

We ran. Ashvine's voice followed us across the cavern, no longer courteous at all, ringing off the wardbone walls with a fury that shook dust from the ancient stone:

"*You cannot anchor it forever! Nothing can! I will have that door, and I will have your mother's bones for the hinges—*"

We reached the dais. The spiral script lit under our feet, drinking the sigil's glow, and the hole at its center unfolded downward into light—not the green of the sigil but something older, whiter, starlight-colored, pouring up out of the deep like water finding a crack.

Kestrel skidded to a halt at the edge, chest heaving, and stared down into it. I saw his face change. Saw the color leave it entirely.

"Sable," he said. "There's stairs."

"Then we take them."

"You don't understand." He looked at me, and behind him the cavern was chaos—Ashvine's people regrouping, the freed choir stumbling and weeping and clinging to each other in the lantern light, and above it all Ashvine himself striding toward the dais with his cold twin-key burning through his shirt, his composure gone, his patience gone, nothing left of him now but appetite.

Behind his shoulder, at the far edge of the cavern, other figures were arriving. More of them. Dozens. The rest of the Thorn Court, come down through ways of their own.

"Sable," Kestrel said again. "If we go down those stairs, there's no coming back up past him."

I looked down into the starlight rising from the deep. Somewhere down there, in the dark behind a failing door, my mother was holding on with everything left of her, aware, alone, for five long years. And she had looked at me with her stolen last breath and said *don't open it*—

But she hadn't said *don't come*.

I took Kestrel's hand—the burning one, the sigil searing against my palm like a held star—and laced our fingers together.

"Then we don't come back up," I said, and jumped.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the city was deep. That shouldn't be possible. I have decided to stop letting that word govern my life.

Behind and above us, Ashvine's boots struck the spiral dais, and his voice came down the stairwell after us, stripped of every elegant pretense, reduced to the thing underneath:

"The door eats names, thief! Whatever you were, it will *unmake* it! You'll arrive at the threshold less than you are now!"

Kestrel didn't answer. But I felt his hand tighten in mine, and I felt the sigil's light stutter—once, twice—like a heart that had just been given something new to fear.

Far below us, faint and fading and unmistakable, a voice was singing.

I knew the song. My mother used to hum it over her charms, late at night, when she thought I was asleep.

She was still singing. And she was getting quieter.
