## 16. The Door That Eats Names

The wardbones ended where all roads end: at a door.

Not a door like the ones in Vell, with hinges and iron and the scuff of ten thousand boots. This one stood free in the cavern's heart, upright and alone, taller than the gatehouses above us, carved from a stone that wasn't stone—black shot through with veins of silver that moved when I looked away and were always somewhere else when I looked back. It had no frame. It had no wall. It simply was, the way a wound is, the way a promise is.

And it was singing.

Low, beneath hearing, felt in the teeth more than the ears—a note so old it had worn grooves into the rock around it. The hollow choir had sung this song in their cavern of stolen faces. The seal itself was singing it, failing, fraying thread by thread, and every hour the stars above stayed dark another voice went out of the world.

Sable stopped three paces from the door and would go no closer. I understood why. Standing near it was like standing near an open grave in winter—the cold came off it in sheets, and something in my chest kept trying to climb up my throat and leave.

"It knows we're here," she whispered.

It knew I was here. The sigil on my palm had been burning since the last tunnel, not the sharp flare of danger but a steady heat now, a coal cupped in flesh, pulling me forward the way a hook pulls a fish. I raised my hand and the door answered. The silver veins brightened, crawling toward the center of its face, arranging themselves—and I saw, with a lurch, that they were forming letters. Old letters. Older than Vell, older than the wards, older maybe than the idea of writing.

*Speak,* they said. *Pay.*

"Kestrel." Sable caught my sleeve. "What does it want?"

I didn't know how I could read them. But then, I'd never known why the sigil had chosen my palm either; I only knew what Yara had left burning in my skin, and her leaving was written in the same language as this door. Some things you inherit whole, without instructions.

I stepped forward. Sable's grip slid from my sleeve to my wrist and held there, hard enough to bruise.

"Toll," I said aloud, testing the word against the silence. "You want a toll."

The letters shifted. Rearranged. And the answer came not as sound but as understanding, dropped into my mind whole, the way memories come when you steal them:

*A name.*

The cold went through me. Not the door's cold—my own.

Every charm-seller in Vell knows what names are worth. We deal in their shadows daily: a widow's grief has a price, a merchant's first love has a price, but ask anyone to sell their *name*—the word their mother breathed over their cradle, the syllables their friends shout across a square—and they'll laugh at you, or spit, or cross themselves against old superstitions. A name isn't memory. A name is the handle the world holds you by.

I turned the thought over and found, to my surprise, that I wasn't afraid. Or I was, but underneath the fear was something quieter and stranger. Relief, almost. Five years I'd lived as a rumor with a body attached. Never be remembered—that was my first rule, older than any other. The door was only asking me to make my rule permanent.

But Sable was looking at me, and her face told me she'd arrived at the same arithmetic, and hated it.

"No," she said. "No. There has to be another way."

"There isn't." I said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "You heard it. You felt it. It wants a name, and I'm the only one here carrying a key it will accept. Your mother sealed herself inside this thing, Sable. She paid with everything she had. The least I can do is pay the toll."

"The toll is *you*." Her voice cracked down the middle. "If you give it your name, the wards won't know you. Brannoc's records won't know you. *I*—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "People forget names all the time. They're just sounds. But this is different. This is the door eating the sound itself. You'll walk through that and I'll stand here knowing I knew someone, knowing his face, and the word for him will just be—"

"Gone."

"Don't say it like it's nothing."

"I'm not saying it's nothing." I looked at my palm. The sigil pulsed there, patient, ancient, hers. "I'm saying some things are worth less than a sky full of stars. My name is one of them."

She hit me. Open-palmed, across the chest, not hard, the way you hit someone to prove they're still solid under your hand. Then she did it again, harder, and then her forehead was against my collarbone and her fists were knotted in my coat and she was shaking, and I held onto her because there was nothing else in the world to hold.

"I spent five years hating you," she said into my shoulder. "Five years. And it took me until now to figure out that hate was just love with nowhere to go. Don't you dare take the name she gave you into that door like it's trash."

"She didn't give me my name."

"She gave you everything else."

* * *

We made our preparations the way soldiers make them: quietly, without looking at each other too long.

Sable took the broken compass from her pocket—the half that pointed at me instead of north, her mother's work, her mother's stubbornness forged into brass—and pressed it into my hand. "So you can find your way back to being findable," she said. "When this is done. When she's free. Whatever happens in there, you come out the other side, Kestrel Delacroix, or I swear by every star we're about to relight, I will learn necromancy purely out of spite."

"Noted."

"And—" she hesitated, and in the door's cold light her face looked very young and very old at once, Yara's face with the grief sanded deeper, "—when you've forgotten, and I haven't. I'm going to tell you who you were. Every day if I have to. Until the name grows back. Do you understand? Names are just words, and I know a lot of words."

I believed her. That was the terrible thing—I believed her completely, and it made the giving both easier and infinitely harder. Because now I wouldn't only be losing a name. I'd be losing mine and gaining hers: her version of me, curated by fury and tenderness in equal measure, and I would never know which parts she got right.

"Sable." I took both her hands. Hers were cold; the sigil in mine burned warm enough for both of us. "There's something I need you to remember even after the door takes it. Something I need carried outside my own head, in case I lose more than I mean to."

"Tell me."

"Yara didn't leave because of me. Whatever you find, whatever Ashvine told you, whatever the journal says—she chose. She chose me, she chose you, she chose this. If I come out of that door hollowed down to the bone, don't let me believe otherwise."

Her jaw tightened. She nodded once, a soldier's nod, and I loved her then—not the way the ballads mean it, but the plainer, rarer way: as family chosen in the dark.

Then I turned to the door.

* * *

Up close, the black stone drank light. The silver letters circled its center like slow fish, waiting, and the song beneath hearing had changed pitch—it was leaning toward me now, attentive, hungry the way winter is hungry.

I raised my right hand. The sigil flared white.

*I offer,* I said, and the language came out of my mouth in syllables I had never learned, old and round and heavy as river stones. *I pay the toll.*

*Name,* said the door, everywhere at once.

My whole life passed through my head in that instant—not stolen, not given, just *mine*, all of it, unbidden. Kestrel, six years old, starving in the ash-market before the plague year. Kestrel, nine, fevered to the edge of dying while Yara worked her craft over him and the sigil passed from her palm to his like a coal handed hand to hand. Kestrel, learning to steal small things first and then, discovering the true trade, stealing hours and sorrows and first kisses, selling them wrapped in wax paper to people desperate enough to buy back what they'd sold. Kestrel Delacroix. Son of no one. Thief of Vell. Keeper of the last living key.

I could feel the door's patience. It had waited centuries. It would wait out my trembling.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about giving up your name: you think it will be like losing a tooth, one clean absence you can run your tongue over. It isn't. It's like being asked to unlearn the color of your own eyes.

"Do it fast," I told Sable. "Don't let go of my hand. Whatever happens."

"I won't."

"I mean it. Even if I scream. Especially if I scream."

"Kestrel—"

"That's the last time," I said. "Say it again."

She understood. Her grip crushed my fingers white. "*Kestrel.*" A breath. "*Kestrel Delacroix.*" And then, fierce as a brand: "Mine. Whatever the door does, that part's mine, and I'm keeping it."

I smiled. I stepped into the door's shadow, laid my palm flat against the black stone, and spoke my name into it—all of it, every letter, every year it had been shouted across squares and whispered over sickbeds and scratched into wanted-notices in warden barracks. I gave it freely, which mattered; I felt the difference the way you feel a door open versus a door broken.

The stone drank.

Cold first—cold like the space between stars, pouring up my arm, and then the *taking*, and oh, the taking was worse than anything the Thorn Court's hollow magic had ever shown me, because it wasn't theft. Theft leaves a hole. This was *unmaking*, gentle and total, a tide going out of me and taking the shoreline with it. I felt the name lift out of my mouth like a splinter drawn from the heart. I felt it go out of my hands. Out of the shape of my face, which suddenly belonged to anyone, to no one. Somewhere far above, in the city of Vell, ledgers were opening themselves and lines of ink were fading. In a barracks file, a sketch of a marked thief blurred into a smudge. In Odile's shop, in the gray market's ashes, in the minds of every person who had ever spoken to me, a small word was quietly dissolving, and they would wake tomorrow with a soft gap where a man used to fit.

I screamed. I'd told her I might.

And through it all—through the unmaking, the tide, the terrible generosity of it—one thing held: her hand. Her grip on my fingers, crushing, refusing, *anchoring*. She was talking the whole time, I realized dimly. Saying my name over and over into the dark like a spell, like a lifeline, *Kestrel, Kestrel, Kestrel, Kestrel,* spending it faster than the door could eat it, feeding me the sound even as it drained away, and I clung to her voice the way a drowning man clings to a rope he knows won't hold but holds anyway.

The pressure stopped.

I opened my eyes. The door stood open—no swing, no hinge, the black stone simply wasn't there anymore where a doorway should be, and beyond it burned a light I had no name for, because I had just given away most of the names I owned.

That was the strangest part. I reached inside myself, the way I reach for stolen goods, taking inventory—and found the shelves half-empty. Not empty. Half-empty. The door had taken the name and its roots, but the memories tangled around those roots remained, orphaned: I remembered a woman with kind terrible eyes saving my life, though the word for who she was floated loose and unattached. I remembered a girl hitting me in the chest and calling me *mine*. I remembered five years of gray-market nights, a fence named O—someone, a captain with a dead son, a court of thorns. All there. All unlabeled. I was a house where every portrait had been cut from its frame.

But I was still *here*. Still walking. The door had eaten my name and found me, somehow, still nourishing.

Sable stood beside me, gray-faced, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her cheeks, still gripping my hand like it was the last fixed point in creation.

"What's your name?" I asked her—testing, needing to hear that language still worked, that words could still belong to people.

"Sable." Her voice shook. Then, watching me carefully: "Do you know yours?"

I searched. Honestly, thoroughly, the way I'd search a mark's pockets. Nothing. Just a warmth where it used to be, and her voice echoing in the hollow of it—*mine, that part's mine, I'm keeping it*—

"No," I said. "But I know you do."

She laughed, wet and broken, and pressed her forehead to our joined hands. "Good enough," she whispered. "Good enough. Come on. Stars are waiting."

Hand in hand—we did not let go, we would not, I suspected neither of us would let go for a long time—we stepped through together, out of the wardbones' dark and into the light beneath the world.

And the light rose to meet us like a tide, and in its depths, faint and vast and fading, something turned toward us with eyes I had spent five years trying to forget and one hour forgetting everything else—

—eyes I knew.

Eyes that knew me, nameless as I was, and filled with recognition, and grief, and joy, all at once, as Yara's voice came across the impossible distance like a bell struck underwater:

"*Oh. Oh, you foolish, brave boy. You shouldn't have paid it alone.*"
