## 18. The War Beneath the Skin

The Understar did not look like a place. It looked like the inside of a held breath.

We came through the door that had eaten my name, and the world on the other side was a cavern of slow light—vast beyond reason, its walls not stone but something like frozen dawn, layered in strata of gold and bruised violet. Rivers of pale radiance ran along channels carved into the floor, converging toward the center of everything, where Yara hung.

I say *hung*, but there is no honest word for it. She was suspended above a great seam of darkness—a wound in the light, a crack running wider than a cathedral—and threads of brilliance poured out of her body into the fissure the way water pours from a pierced jug. Her hair moved as if underwater. Her eyes were open. They found me across all that impossible distance, and they were still her eyes: tired, wry, unbearably kind.

"Kestrel," she said, and her voice arrived inside my chest rather than my ears. "You brought my daughter."

"I brought myself," I said. "She insisted."

Sable made a sound beside me that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed her hand hard against her mouth.

Then the light behind us changed color, and I turned, and Lord Ashvine stepped out of the doorway we had just come through, unhurried, brushing dust from his sleeve as though he had merely crossed a ballroom rather than followed us through a door that ate names.

"You gave it your name," he observed, with something like admiration. "Clever. Foolish. Both, usually, are the same thing down here." He looked past me to the seam, to Yara, and his courteous face went soft with an old and terrible hunger. "Hello, thief. You've kept what isn't yours for a long time."

"It was given," Yara said.

"It was *taken*." Ashvine's voice did not rise. It never needed to. The whole cavern seemed to lean in to hear him. "The seal was our work, witch. Our great mending, after the war we started and regretted. And you sealed it from the inside with a key you stole from under our hands and hid in the palm of a gutter-thief." His gaze slid to me, and I felt it like a blade laid flat against skin. "Give me the sigil, boy, and I will let the girl walk out of here. Break the seal properly, release what's bound beneath, and the old courts will have their reckoning at last. The stars will burn *brighter* than they ever burned."

"And everything under them will burn too," Sable said.

"Everything under them has been burning for five hundred years," Ashvine replied. "You simply haven't been invited to watch."

* * *

He did not wait for an answer. Perhaps he never intended to; perhaps the offer was courtesy, the way a headsman offers a blindfold.

Light left him like a drawn breath. Not fire, not lightning—something older, a pressure that made my bones hum and my teeth ache, and I threw myself sideways as the place where I'd stood became a crater of black glass. The sigil flared on my palm, hot as a brand, and instinct older than thought made me answer it: I reached into the nearest thing I could find, which was the memory of the crater itself, of the heat, of the sound—and I *pulled*. I stole the moment out of the air, unmade it into a charm, and hurled it back at him as raw force.

It caught him across the shoulder and staggered him one step. One step. He smiled.

"Oh," he said softly. "You're *good*."

"Sable!" I shouted. "Yara—the choir—"

Because they were coming. Down through the strata of light they poured, the hollow ones, dozens of them, the mindless citizens of Vell whose memories the Thorn Court had drunk dry—Odile among them, Odile who had fenced my charms for six years and called me *little fox*—their mouths open, singing. That was the horror of it. They weren't screaming. They were singing, a low sweet chordless drone, and I knew now what they sang: the seal's failing song, the lullaby Yara had woven to hold the dark closed, sung wrong, sung hollow, each note a thread pulled loose.

Sable didn't hesitate. She never hesitated; it was the thing about her that frightened me most and that I loved most, though I hadn't yet admitted the second part even to myself. She drew her mother's journal from her coat—the cipher-pages glowing faintly now, drinking this place's light—and planted herself between the choir and the seam.

"I know the song," she called back to me. "Mama sang it when I had fevers. She changed the words so I'd sleep." Her voice cracked and steadied. "I can sing it *right*."

And she began to sing.

I had no time to marvel. Ashvine came at me again, and this time I stopped trying to be clever.

* * *

Fighting Lord Ashvine was like fighting the memory of a storm. He moved through the Understar's light as if it were water and he had gills. Every strike of his carried weight—not muscle but *certainty*, centuries of it, the confidence of a man who had never once doubted his right to take what he wanted.

I dodged. I stole. I ripped moments out of the air and spent them like coins: the heat of his last blast, the angle of his next, once even a sliver of his own memory—I got inside his guard and tore free a fragment of something old, a garden, a woman laughing, ash falling on roses—and drove it into him like a knife. He cried out, actually cried out, and for one heartbeat his composure cracked and I saw underneath: not a monster. A mourner. A man who had lost so much that taking things was the only way he knew to feel full.

Then he broke my ribs.

I felt them go—three of them, maybe four—a white detonation of pain that lifted me off my feet and slammed me down against a ridge of golden strata. The world went spotty. Through the spots I saw him walking toward me, unhurried again, straightening his cuffs.

"You fight like a thief," he said. "Everything taken, nothing owned. Do you know why brute theft fails here, boy? Because this place runs on gift. The seal was built on sacrifice freely offered. Your stolen scraps—" he gestured contemptuously at the fading charm-light around my fist, "—are counterfeit coin. The Understar knows the difference."

He raised his hand, and the light gathered in it like a tide going out.

And in that moment—flat on my back, ribs grinding, tasting copper—I understood him completely, and understanding arrived not as strategy but as revelation, the way Yara's lessons used to arrive, sudden and warm.

The seal mended only with freely given memory.

Not stolen. Not taken. *Given.* That was why Yara had hung here for five years, pouring herself out. That was why the sigil lived in a palm instead of a vault. That was why Ashvine, with all his power, could only widen the wound—he had nothing left to give. He had spent five hundred years hollowing himself into a perfect instrument of taking, and now, at the end of everything, he was the poorest man in the room.

Yara had known. She had always known. *The price of survival was never meant to be paid alone,* she'd told me once, in a memory I'd kept hidden even from myself. I had thought it a warning. It was instructions.

Ashvine's hand came down.

I didn't dodge. I opened my palm—the sigil blazing, white-hot, joyful almost—and I reached inward, past the fear, past the pain, into the treasury of everything I had hoarded for twenty-six years, and I began to give.

* * *

You cannot imagine what it is to give a memory willingly, if all you have ever done is steal. Stealing is cold; it slides out clean, leaving a smooth-walled hollow. Giving is different. Giving is *bleeding*. Each memory tore free rooted and bleeding, and I fed it into the sigil, and the sigil drank it and poured it into the seam, and the seam—gods, the seam *drank*.

I gave it Yara's face as I'd first seen it: rain, a burning tenement, a woman crouched over a dying boy with her hands full of impossible light. The dark seam shuddered. A thread of gold knitted across it, thin as spider silk.

I gave it the taste of bread Odile used to leave on her counter for me, warm, salted, the first kindness anyone in Vell had ever shown without a price. Another thread. Wider.

I gave it Sable's laughter—which I had only heard three times, and had treasured all three like a miser, replaying them in the gray hours before dawn—and the silk became rope, the rope became a beam.

Ashvine's blast broke over me and I barely felt it, because giving, it turns out, is also armor. The seal's light rose up around me like a mother's arm. He struck again, shouting now, no longer courteous, striking at the light itself, at the threads, and the threads bent but did not break, because they were not mine anymore. Given things cannot be taken. That is their whole power. That is the entire secret, and it cost Yara everything to learn it and was costing me everything to prove.

"Stop," Ashvine snarled, and for the first time his voice sounded young. Frightened. "You don't understand what's *down there*, what she bound—"

"I know," I gasped. Ribs. Blood. Keep giving. "I know exactly what's down there. Same as you. Grief. You're both just grief, wearing crowns."

I gave it my mother's voice, humming, off-key. I gave it the smell of Vell after rain, wet slate and lamp oil. I gave it Brannoc's dead son, the boy's face as I'd seen it in the Captain's stolen grief—no, not that, I couldn't give another man's grief away, it wasn't mine—but I gave the memory of having carried it, the shame and the strange tenderness of it. I gave it the first charm I ever sold and the sick triumph of not being caught. I gave it every night I'd spent invisible, and the loneliness that I had mistaken for safety, and the fear of being seen that I had mistaken for wisdom.

The seam was closing. Half-mended now, a scar of bright rope across the throat of the dark, and Yara above it was dimming with every gift I gave, fading like a candle in wind, and she was smiling.

"Don't," she whispered. "Kestrel. Save some. You must save some, or there'll be nothing left to—"

"There's nothing left worth keeping," I lied, "if the stars stay dark."

I gave her the moment she saved me. I gave her my own name—*Kestrel Delacroix*, the name the door had already eaten, given twice now, given truly—and felt it go out of me like a tooth pulled, leaving a gap my tongue kept returning to. Who was I? Someone. Someone was lying on golden light, bleeding light from an open hand, and someone was singing—Sable, Sable was singing her mother's lullaby with the true words, and the hollow choir was faltering, faltering, some of them weeping without knowing why, Odile's ruined voice catching on a note like a key catching in a lock—

I gave it the plan I'd made for the shop Sable and I would open someday. I gave it the way morning looked through her kitchen window. I gave it hope, which is just a memory of a thing that hasn't happened yet, and the Understar took that too, took it hungrily, and the scar-seam blazed—

And then I reached for the last of it. Sable's face. The ambush in the alley, the blade at my throat, her fury like a small sun, the compass-needle swinging to point at me instead of north. *You* , the compass said. *Her mother's last choice was you.*

My hand shook. The sigil pulsed, patient, waiting.

"No," said a voice behind me, ragged, furious, alive.

Sable dropped from the ridge where she'd been singing, blood on her lip, her mother's journal clutched to her chest, and she seized my wrist with both hands—seized the sigil itself—and her eyes were Yara's eyes, tired and wry and unbearably kind, and she said:

"Not that one. You don't get to give that one away. That one's *mine* to keep."

And before I could speak, before I could stop her, she did the unthinkable—she reached into the seal's own blaze, into the mountain of everything I had just surrendered, and began, with her mother's counter-art and her mother's stubborn hands, to steal it *back*.

The last thing I remember clearly is her face, lit gold, fierce as dawn, saying words I couldn't yet understand:

"You gave enough for two lifetimes, Kestrel. Now it's my turn to be the thief."
