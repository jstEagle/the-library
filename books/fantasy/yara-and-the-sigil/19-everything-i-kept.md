## 19. Everything I Kept

Stealing a memory, I had been taught, was like lifting a coal from a fire with your bare hand. My mother taught me that lesson when I was seven years old, holding my palm open beneath hers, showing me how to grip fast and let go faster, how the burn was the price and the price was always paid by someone.

She never taught me how to steal from light itself.

The seal blazed before me like a wound in the world's skin—a ring of white-gold fire suspended in the black heart of the Understar, and Kestrel knelt at its edge with his ruined palm pressed flat against it, pouring himself out. I could *see* it now, in this place where seeing was the only sense that mattered: his memories leaving him in bright threads, unwinding from his chest like silk pulled from a spool. His mother's kitchen. The first rain he ever tasted. The gray market's lantern-glow. Yara's face.

Yara's face, going into the fire first, because of course it was. He would spend her before he spent himself. He had been spending her for five years without knowing it.

"Stop," I said, and the word came out small, swallowed whole by the vast dark. "Kestrel. Stop."

He didn't stop. Of course he didn't. His lips were moving—counting, I realized. He was counting what he gave away, the way he counted everything, the thief's habit of tallying losses so they couldn't sneak up on him. "...the smell of tallow after the lamps go out... the way Odile says my name wrong on purpose..." Each item a thread of light, drawn out of him and wound into the seal, and the seal drank and drank and the stars above us—I could feel them through leagues of stone, faint as heartbeats—flickered a little brighter with every memory he surrendered.

And Kestrel grew dimmer.

That was the thing no one told you about watching someone die this way. It wasn't blood, it wasn't wounds. It was subtraction. The edges of him were going soft. His shoulders, which always sat crooked with the weight of pretending not to be seen, were straightening—not because he stood taller but because there was less of him to slouch around. His face was smoothing into something generic, something anyone might wear. I had known him for weeks and hated him for years, and I could see it happening: the man I knew was being spent, coin by coin, and the seal would take all of it and still be hungry, because seals made to hold back wars are hungrier than any single life can sate.

My mother had known that. That was why she hadn't come alone. That was why she'd put the sigil on a boy instead of coming down here herself—she had meant for him to carry it *here*, yes, but she must have meant something else too, some other way, some third door I hadn't found in her journal yet—

No. There was no third door. There was only this one, and the boy kneeling in front of it, and me.

I thought about what he'd said. *Not that one. You don't get to give that one away. That one's mine to keep.* He had meant the memory of me—the sound of my laugh, which he'd fed to the fire minutes ago anyway, traitor, liar, beautiful stupid man—and the fury of it cracked something open in my chest, and through the crack came not grief but clarity, cold and clean as well water.

He was right that some things shouldn't be given away.

He was wrong about who gets to decide.

*You gave enough for two lifetimes,* I had told him, already moving, already reaching. *Now it's my turn to be the thief.*

Here is what my mother taught me, in a kitchen that smelled of rosemary and burnt sugar, when I was seven and did not yet understand what she was: that taking and giving are the same gesture seen from different sides. That a memory is not a thing but an *agreement*, a pattern of light held between two minds, and that patterns can be moved. Stolen memories leave hollows—that was true, that was the crime of Kestrel's trade, the thing that made me despise him before I ever met him. But the counter-art, her art, the art she hid from the Inquisition and the Thorn Court and even from me until I was old enough to bleed for it: the counter-art was theft done *rightly*. Theft where the hollow fills again. Where you take with permission, or give without asking, and the pattern is carried rather than destroyed.

The seal held thousands of patterns now. It held Kestrel's whole remembered life, and it was burning them as fuel, wastefully, gloriously, feeding five hundred years of starlight with the taste of one man's childhood.

It didn't need all of them.

That was the mathematics no one had done, because everyone who reached this room arrived willing to die completely. But a fire doesn't burn a whole forest if you feed it branch by branch. A seal didn't need *everything Kestrel was*. It needed enough. And *enough* was a number, and numbers could be negotiated with, and negotiation—haggling with power over a fair price—was the oldest magic in Vell's gray market, older than any sigil.

So I stole from a god.

* * *

The first thing I took back was small. Deliberately small. A thread near the edge of the blaze, thin and cool: the taste of river water in summer, mud and minerals and cold. I gripped it the way Mother taught me—fast hand, soft heart—and pulled.

It fought me. Not maliciously; the seal didn't hate, it simply *held*, the way a river holds a stone. But my hands had been trained on smaller rivers, and I dug in, and the thread came free with a sensation like tearing linen soaked in honey, sweet and terrible, and I turned and pressed it to Kestrel's chest and whispered the word Mother used for returning:

*"Remember."*

The change was instant and invisible and enormous. His breathing caught. His fingers curled against the seal's edge. Somewhere behind his smoothing-over face, a man tasted river water again and knew it was *his*, knew he had been eight, knew he had dared his cousin to drink first.

I went back for more.

The second was the color of the sky over Vell in autumn, that bruised pearl-gray he'd once described to me with actual affection, the sentimental fool. The third was Odile's voice saying *kestrel* wrong on purpose, *kest-rel*, two beats where one belonged. Each one cost me. I could feel the seal noticing me now, its attention turning like a lighthouse beam, and where it touched me I felt my own memories shiver in their moorings, threatened, warned: *give back what you've taken, little thief.*

"No," I said, out loud, to a force that had held back the old war before my grandmother's grandmother was born. My voice cracked and I said it again. "No. You've had enough. You've had *more* than enough. You're greedy, and greed was never the same thing as need, and I know the difference because I grew up poor."

The light flared. Heat washed over me—not burning, worse than burning, a heat that reached inside and started *sorting*, weighing which of my memories would feed it best, and I felt it settle with horrible tenderness on the ones I loved most: Mother's hands braiding my hair. The half-compass warm in my pocket. Kestrel's stunned face in the alley when I'd first held a blade to his throat and he'd said, absurdly, *you have her eyes.*

It wanted those. It would take them if I stayed too long.

So I worked faster. Thread after thread, grip and pull and turn and return, and each time I pressed a memory back into Kestrel I watched another piece of him solidify—the line of his jaw reasserting itself, his crooked shoulders returning, and once his eyes opened and found me and they were *his* eyes again, terrified and fierce, and he mouthed my name like a question: *what are you doing.*

"Saving you, obviously," I said, wrenching free another thread—the smell of candlewax, no wait, not that one, that one was tied to something I didn't understand yet, some grief he kept below the waterline; I left it and took the sound of rain on a tin roof instead. "You'd do it for me. Don't pretend you wouldn't. It's the most annoying thing about you."

He tried to pull his hand from the seal. The seal held him. Of course it held him—it had paid for what it took, hadn't it, in starlight, in purpose, in the ancient bargain that says *feed me and be part of the feeding forever.* And I saw the moment he understood what I understood: that we could not stop entirely, that the stars needed feeding, that somewhere in this equation someone had to remain diminished or everything above us went dark and Vell unraveled like a cut seam.

But it didn't have to be *all* of him.

"Keep your hands on it," I told him. "Keep giving. Just—" and here my voice broke, because the next words were the truest and hardest I had ever spoken, truer than any I'd said over my mother's empty grave, "—just give slower. Let me keep pace. We do this together or not at all. That's the rule. That was always supposed to be the rule."

He looked at me across the blaze, this thief I had hunted and hated and followed to the bottom of the world, and something in his face folded—not surrender, nothing so simple. Recognition. The look of a man who has spent his whole life believing the price must be paid alone, seeing the bill split in half.

"All right," he said. His voice was rough, mostly gone, mostly *returned*. "Together. But I'm keeping the laugh."

"You gave away the laugh, you absolute—"

"I'm keeping it," he said, "because you're going to steal it back. That's how it works now. That's the new trade."

So I did. I reached past the seal's warning heat, past its slow turning attention, and I found the thread of my own laughter—he'd heard it three days ago in a tunnel, when I'd slipped on wet wardbone and sworn in a way Mother would have charged me a copper for—and I tore it loose and gave it home, and Kestrel laughed then, actually laughed, ragged and disbelieving, in the exact shape of the memory, proof returned to its owner.

Above us, through infinite stone, the stars burned steady.

* * *

We might have gone on that way—him feeding, me thieving, the long arithmetic of *enough*—if Ashvine had not begun to scream.

I had forgotten him. That shames me now; it shames me less than it should, because there was a great deal happening. But Lord Ashvine of the Thorn Court stood at the cavern's edge where Kestrel's collapsing memory of the old war had thrown him, and he had picked himself up, and he was walking toward the seal with his beautiful ruined face full of triumph.

"It's weakening," he breathed, and his voice was silk over broken glass. "The anchor dissolves, the seal runs on stolen scraps—oh, this is better than I hoped. It isn't failing. It's *emptying*." He spread his hands, and thorns of shadow bloomed from his fingertips, black roses opening in the gold light. "A few more hours and the door stands open. Little Sable, you've done in an evening what fifty years of my court could not. I should thank you."

He reached for the seal.

He never reached it.

Because the light at the center—the deep light, the oldest light, the layer beneath all the borrowed memories where the seal's original radiance still lived—shifted. Folded. Took on, for one staggering moment, the outline of a woman.

Mother.

Not whole. Never whole again—I will not lie about that, not even in my own head. She was fused to the seal still, half-dissolved, her edges made of fire and her center made of exhaustion beyond anything I have words for. Five years she had hung here, the living anchor, holding shut a door that pushed every second of every day. But Kestrel's flood of offered memory had filled the channels she alone had kept open by force, and for the first time since she sealed herself into the Understar, she had strength to spare.

She spent it all in one motion.

"Ashvine," she said, and my name for her—Mama, Mum, the woman who smelled of rosemary—collided with the title, and both were true, and neither was enough. Her hand came up, trailing light like smoke. "You never understood the seal. You thought it was a lock. It was never a lock."

Ashvine stopped. For the first time since the alley, since the courteous voice and the offered bargain, I saw fear move behind his composure like a fish under ice.

"It's a ledger," Mother said softly. "Everything taken is written down. Everything owed is collected." Her fingers closed—not on him, I want to be clear, she was thirty feet away and dying and she did not touch him—but on the memory of him, the record of him, the long account of Lord Ashvine's centuries: every village hollowed, every mind emptied into the choir, every debt. "And yours," she said, "is overdue."

The stone of the Understar rose up around him like water closing over a sinking thing. Not violent. That was the horrifying mercy of it—gentle, patient, inevitable, roots growing through a wall. Ashvine screamed then, truly screamed, none of the silk left, and he reached toward me—toward *me*, the daughter of the witch who beat him—as the stone took his legs, his hips, his outflung hands.

"The court," he rasped. "The court will come. The court is *older than your seals*, Yara—"

"The court," said my mother, "is welcome to visit you here."

The stone sealed over his face. The screaming stopped. Where Lord Ashvine had stood there was now a shape in the rock like a man entwined in thorns, and I understood that he was not dead—death would have been a release, and the ledger does not deal in releases—and I wrapped my arms around myself and shook, and could not tell for a long moment whether I was horrified or grateful, and decided that being both at once was probably what growing up meant.

* * *

Then it was quiet, and my mother turned to us, and there was so little of her left that I could see the far wall of the cavern through her shoulder.

"Sable." My name in her mouth. I crossed the distance before I decided to, and my hands passed into her light the way hands sink into sunbeams on a dusty afternoon, and it did not matter, because I felt her anyway—cool and certain, the pressure of a palm against my cheek, five years of absence compressed into one impossible touch. "You haggled with it," she whispered, and there was wonder in her, real wonder, the last gift she had left to give. "You looked at the one bargain everyone else accepted as final, and you asked for a better price. Oh, my love. My love. I built the sigil to find a carrier. I never dreamed it would find a merchant."

"Mama, come with us. If the seal has enough now, if it's fed, you can—"

"No." Gentle. Final. The word fell like a coin into an empty bowl. "I am the last payment on an old debt, sweetheart, and I pay gladly. But listen—" and her light leaned close, urgent now, guttering, "—listen, because I am almost finished. The seal needs a keeper's *attention*, not just fuel. Someone must come, sometimes, and count what it holds, and take nothing, and give a little. A pilgrimage. A promise. Yours, or his, or yours together. Will you?"

"Yes," Kestrel said, from his knees at the seal's edge, his hand still pressed flat, his face wet. I hadn't heard him cross the room. "Yes. Every year. Both of us."

"Both," Mother repeated, and the smile in her voice was the one from the kitchen, from the rosemary mornings, from before. "Good. That's the word I hoped for. *Both.* Because the sigil was never meant to be carried alone, and neither was he, and neither, my darling girl, were you."

Her light began to thin. Not vanish—I have to believe not vanish; the seal holds everything, and the seal keeps accounts, and somewhere in its ledger my mother is written down in fire. But it thinned, drawing inward, settling into the deep gold of the seal like sediment into still water, and the last thing she did—the very last, before she became indistinguishable from the light she anchored—was reach out one fading hand toward each of us, and touch our joined ones where they rested on the blazing rim, and say:

"Keep the shop. Keep the trade. Return what was stolen. And when you light a candle—" her voice was nearly gone now, woven through with the seal's low harmonic hum, "—and find yourself weeping for no reason, don't grieve. That's just the ledger, balancing. That's just me, remembering you."

And she was gone into the gold, and the gold held, and through a thousand leagues of stone I felt the stars above Vell burn like nails hammered firm into heaven's roof.

* * *

We climbed for a long time afterward. Neither of us spoke. What was there? We had gone down into the dark carrying every kind of hunger—her location, his absolution, my revenge—and we came up carrying only each other and a promise, which I suspect is the standard exchange rate for miracles.

The wardbones hummed under our feet the whole way, steady as a sleeping pulse. Twice we passed warden lanterns and twice the wardens looked at us—at Kestrel's blazing palm, at my tear-streaked face, at whatever we had become down there—and let us pass without a word. Brannoc himself stood at the mouth of the deep shaft, and he stepped aside, and I will spend the rest of my life deciding whether that was mercy or surrender.

It was full dark when we surfaced through the sapper's tunnel into the ruin of a garden on Vell's north slope, and Kestrel stopped so suddenly I walked into his back.

The sky.

The sky was *full*. Stars past counting, stars in rivers and clusters and reckless scatterings, more stars than I had ever known existed—the city's wards had dimmed them for generations, I realized, filtered them the way sick eyes filter light, and now the wards sang healthy and the heavens poured through unmasked. The whole of Vell lay below us, lamplit, intact, its people pouring into streets and rooftops to stare upward, and somewhere in that sprawl a woman named Odile was waking up in her own mind with a headache and a lifetime of missing months, and somewhere a shop stood empty with dust on the counter, waiting.

Kestrel was shaking. I thought at first it was exhaustion, the crash after the long giving, and I put my hand on his arm.

Then I realized he was looking at his palm.

The sigil still glowed there—but changed. Finer. It had been a brand, a burden, a key. Now it sat in his skin like a signature, delicate as penwork, and as we watched it pulsed once, gently, in time with the stars.

"It didn't fade," he whispered. "It was supposed to fade, wasn't it? Job done, key spent. Why is it still—"

The pulse came again. And with it, faint but unmistakable, rising from his marked palm like scent from a sealed letter steamed open: the smell of rosemary and burnt sugar.

My mother's kitchen. A memory neither of us had given the seal. A memory Kestrel shouldn't have had at all—buried, hidden from himself, hidden by himself, five years ago, on a night I suddenly understood we had never once spoken about.

"Kestrel," I said slowly. "What did you hide from yourself?"

He stared at the sigil, and the stars reflected in his eyes, and when he answered his voice was the voice of a man reading words he wrote in a language he no longer speaks.

"I don't know," he said. "But it knows. And I think—" he closed his fist, opened it, and the sigil pulsed like a heartbeat, like a knock at a door, like something patient asking, finally, to be let in, "—I think it wants to show me."
