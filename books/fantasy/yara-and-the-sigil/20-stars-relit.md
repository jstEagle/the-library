## 20. Stars, Relit

The last thing I remember of the Understar is light.

Not the thin borrowed light of Vell's lanterns, nor the cold arithmetic of starlight I had grown up under, but something older and warmer—a light that seemed to remember me. It poured through the seal like water through a cracked bowl, and Sable's hands were on my face, and she was saying words I cannot recall no matter how many times I turn the hourglass over, and then—

Then there was grass.

I woke on the hillside above Vell with the dawn in my mouth and Sable's fingers laced through mine so tightly that our knuckles had gone the same white as the bones beneath. The earth smelled of rain that had not yet fallen. Somewhere below us, the city was screaming—but it was a different scream than the one we had left behind. This one had joy in it.

"Kestrel," Sable said, and her voice was raw, scraped down to the quick. "Look up."

I looked up.

I have stolen ten thousand memories. I have held a widow's grief in my palm until it hummed like a trapped moth; I have carried a merchant's first love against my ribs for three days while I etched it into charm-glass. I thought I knew what memory weighed.

I did not know what it weighed when it came *back*.

The stars were burning.

Not the handful of stubborn lights that had outlasted the dark—there had been seven, and Odile used to joke they were just holes in the sky's own wards. No. The whole spilled river of them, thousands upon thousands, wheeling above the ruined hills in their ancient constellations, and between them a faint silver seam where the seal now held, bright as scar-tissue, mended with everything I had poured into it and everything Sable had torn back out again.

"They're warm," I said, which made no sense, and Sable laughed—or sobbed, or both—and pressed her forehead against our joined hands, and for a long time neither of us said anything at all. The wind moved through the grass in long slow breaths. Below, Vell's bells had begun to ring, all of them at once, discordant and desperate and alive.

I tried to remember what we had done inside. I reached for it the way I reach for a stolen memory laid into glass, expecting the familiar texture, the weight and grain of it.

There was a shape where the remembering should have been. A smooth place, like the hollow left in a giver after I take their grief. I could feel the edges of it—the way you can feel the socket where a tooth was—but the center would not come. I remembered the door that ate names. I remembered Ashvine's face going from triumph to fury to stone. I remembered Yara, half-dissolved into light, smiling at me with an expression I could not name because the name of it had been part of the price.

Everything else was silver, and silence, and my daughter-of-the-witch's hand holding mine as if I might still be unmade.

"What did you keep?" I asked finally.

Sable lifted her head. There were lines around her eyes that had not been there a month ago, and dirt in her black hair, and she was the most beautiful thing on that hillside, including the sky.

"Enough," she said. "Only enough."

"Sable—"

"Don't." She said it gently, which was worse. "You'll ask me what I kept and what I gave back and how much of you is still in there, and I won't tell you, because if I tell you then you'll start doing sums, and Kestrel—you are *terrible* at sums. You sold a widow's grief for two coppers once. Two. The exchange rate alone should have shamed you out of the trade."

Despite everything—the hollow places, the ache behind my sternum like a healing fracture—I laughed. It came out broken and wet, but it came.

"The widow cried," I said. "When I returned it to her. In your mother's shop, months ago. She wept like—" And there I stopped, because I had hit another smooth place. I knew the fact of it: the widow, the tears, the little shop with the crooked sign. But the feeling had been poured out of me somewhere between the door and the dawn, spent like a coin, and I could not get it back.

"She wept like someone getting back a piece of herself," Sable finished quietly. "Because she was. Because you gave it back. That's the trade now, remember? That's yours."

Above us, the silver seam pulsed once, slow, like a sleeping heartbeat.

*Like Yara*, I almost said, and did not know why.

* * *

We walked down into Vell because there was nowhere else, and because I think we both needed to see whether the city had survived us.

It had. Barely, bleeding, but breathing. The gates stood open for the first time since the night the stars went out—wardens still at their posts, but their posts had changed; they weren't barring people in anymore. Farmers were coming through with carts of early greens, staring up at the sky like it was a miracle, which, I suppose, it was. Children ran ahead of their parents in the streets, pointing at the pale ghosts of stars still visible in the daylight, and every third doorway had a candle in it—not charms, not ward-lights, just plain tallow candles, lit by hand by people who wanted, I think, to be doing something with their fear.

The wards themselves I could feel without touching, the way you feel a fever breaking. They ran through the city like veins of cool water, and at the center of each ward-stone, deep down in the wardbones, the seal sang its mended song. I could hear it faintly through the soles of my feet. It sounded different now. Less like a warning. More like a lullaby sung by someone who has decided to stay.

People looked at us. That was the strangest thing. Five years I had walked these streets practicing the only art that mattered: never steal a whole life, never be remembered. I had been so good at not being seen that sometimes I wondered if I'd erase myself eventually just from practice.

Now a baker dropped his flour-dusted hands to his sides and stared. Now a woman grabbed her son's sleeve and pointed, whispering, and the boy whispered back, and I caught the word on the wind: *the marked thief*. Not as a hunt-cry. As a story. The kind of story children tell each other about the person who went down under the city and came back up with the stars.

Sable squeezed my arm. "They know," she said.

"I know they know."

"How does it feel?"

I turned my marked palm over and looked at the sigil. It had changed. The lines that had burned crimson all my life now shimmered a soft silver-white, and they were no longer confined to my palm—they traced up my wrist in delicate branches, like frost, like rivers seen from very high up, like the seams of a thing that had been broken and beautifully rejoined. It didn't hurt. For the first time in twenty years, it didn't hurt at all. It felt like being held.

"Like being seen," I said. "And surviving it."

She nodded slowly. "That's the whole trick, isn't it? That's what she was trying to teach you." She didn't say *my mother*, but I heard the name anyway, in the space where the words weren't. We had agreed, somewhere in the dark, not to talk about Yara yet. Some griefs need to cool before you can carry them.

We made it as far as the Fountain Square before the wardens found us.

* * *

They came out of the side streets in a quiet crescent, twelve of them, wands drawn but lowered, and the crowd drew back not in fear of the wardens but in fear *for* us, murmuring, and Warden-Captain Brannoc walked through the middle of his own formation like a man coming to settle a debt he'd rather not discuss.

He looked terrible. He had always been a big man, built like a door, but doors don't usually look like someone took an axe to their faith. His gray cloak was scorched along one hem. There was a bandage at his throat. And his eyes—he had eyes like a ledger before, all columns and sums, and now the sums had been done and the total was not one he could carry.

He stopped six feet away. His wardens stopped with him.

"Delacroix," he said.

"Captain." My voice came out steadier than I expected. Beside me, Sable had gone very still, and I knew without looking that her hand was near her knife. Old habits. New world. "Come to collect?"

Brannoc looked at me for a long moment. Then past me, up, at the pale stars hanging in the morning sky.

"When the sky went black," he said, "we had three days before the first thing came through the gap in the border wards. Three days. Do you know what came through?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Neither do I. That's the part that keeps me awake. Whatever it was, it took Sergeant Ilven's entire patrol, and it sang while it did it. Sang like a choir." His jaw worked. "My men have been fighting things out of old wives' tales for a month. I've burned people, Delacroix. People who probably had no more magic in them than a good luck horseshoe, and I burned them because I was afraid, and I told myself it was duty."

Nobody in the square breathed. I could hear a candle guttering in a nearby doorway.

"And then," Brannoc went on, quieter, "three nights ago, my sappers report the wardbones singing. The whole undercity, lit up like a festival, and something moving down there—two somethings, small as mice against that light. And then the stars came back." He looked at me, and his voice did something I had never heard it do: it frayed. "I've read the reports from my men who saw what happened at the market when you pulled Odile Verret out of the fire. I know what you spent down there. My diviners felt it. Every memory you owned, poured into that seal like wine into a cracked cup—and the girl pouring some of it back, which my diviners say shouldn't even be possible, which means somewhere in the world there is still magic that *gives* instead of takes."

He was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, the ledger-voice was back, but the numbers in it were different.

"There will be an inquiry. There always is. Names will be asked for. Magic-users will still be registered, because cities are cities and paper is paper." He rolled his shoulders, and something in him settled, like a man putting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. "But I'm not going to put a leash on the two people who relit the sky. And if anyone in the Inquisition comes asking after the marked thief of Vell, they'll find my signature first, on a document that says you died in the collapse of the wardbones. Officially." The corner of his mouth twitched—it wasn't quite a smile, but it was the closest thing I'd ever seen him wear. "Unofficially, you were never anyone at all. Shouldn't be hard. You spent five years making sure."

"Captain," I said. My throat was tight. "Your son—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and fast, a blade laid flat across a table. Then, softer, with his eyes fixed on some middle distance where a boy had once been: "Whatever you gave down there. Whatever it cost. Keep the accounting private. Some debts a man needs to believe he paid himself."

He turned. Then he paused, half-facing me, the torchlight from a doorway catching the gray in his cropped hair.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning back, "Ilven's patrol was found the morning after the stars returned. Alive. Sitting in a field outside the walls with no idea how they got there, and a song stuck in all their heads." A beat. "It was a nice song."

Then he walked away, and his wardens walked with him, and the crowd in Fountain Square parted around Kestrel Delacroix like water around a stone that had finally, finally stopped sinking.

Sable let out a breath that seemed to empty her whole body. "We're alive," she said, as if testing the words for counterfeit.

"We're alive," I agreed.

"Now what?"

I looked down at my silver-marked palm, then up at the crooked lanes of Vell, the candlelit doorways, the bells still ringing their ragged joy, the stars fading gently into morning the way stars should, patient, certain of their return.

"Now," I said, "I believe someone owes me a visit to a certain shop with a crooked sign. If it's still standing. If Odile—" The name caught. She had been one of the hollow choir; she had been singing the seal's failing song with her mind scooped out and her voice left behind. But Sable had given back what she could in the dark below, working the counter-art desperately while the seal drank, and I had felt—somewhere in the silver, in the parts I couldn't reach—one thread of the song change key.

"Odile is sitting on the shop's front step this very minute," Sable said, "eating apricots and complaining that we took too long. She remembers the fire. She remembers you pulling her out. She does not remember three weeks in February, and she says she doesn't miss them, and she says—" Sable's voice caught, and she laughed to cover it, and did not quite succeed. "She says the shop kept the kettle warm."

* * *

The shop was there. The crooked sign was there. Odile was there, apricots and all, and she hugged me hard enough to crack ribs and called me a fool in three languages and put the kettle on.

And that evening, when the work was done and Sable had fallen asleep in Yara's old chair with the deciphered journal open on her chest, I lit a candle.

Just a candle. Plain tallow, like the ones in every doorway in Vell. I struck the match and touched the flame to the wick, and the small warm light bloomed up between my cupped hands, and—

I wept.

No warning. No grief I could name. Just a sudden flooding sorrow, sweet and enormous, rising out of a hollow place in me the way water rises out of a spring, and I sat there in the little shop with tears running silently down my face, watching a candle flame, and I did not know why.

I searched for the reason the way I had once searched pockets and memories and locked merchant's drawers—professionally, gently, with all my old skill. And I found the hollow. The smooth place. And at the bottom of it, faint as a watermark, one image: a woman's hands, lighting a candle, in a room I almost remembered, turning to smile at someone whose face was—

Gone. Poured out. Spent on the seal, or kept by Sable, or given to the door that eats names. I would not get it back by searching. Maybe I would not get it back at all.

But the sorrow stayed, and it was not a bad sorrow. It was the kind that means something loved existed. It was a wound that proves the body remembers.

The candle burned. Outside, above the crooked sign and the sleeping city, the first stars of evening came out one by one, taking up their posts, keeping the watch they had kept for a month now—bright, and warm, and somehow, whenever I looked at them, familiar.

On the table, the journal lay closed. And tucked inside its back cover—though I would not find it until tomorrow, and it would change everything I thought I knew about the woman who saved me—waited a sealed letter, addressed in handwriting I did not recognize:

*To Kestrel, from himself.*
