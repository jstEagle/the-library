## 21. The Charm-Seller's New Trade

The shop had been a cobbler's once, and before that—a hundred years before that, if the deed Odile procured for us was telling the truth—a chapel to a god whose name had been scrubbed from the stone lintel above the door. We left the scrubbing. Sable's rule, in those first months: leave what the city has chosen to forget. There was enough forgetting in Vell already. She wanted no part of adding to it.

So we hung our shingle beneath the blank lintel—no sigil, no sigil-burned palm pressed in wax, none of the old gray-market marks that meant *memory for sale here*. Just three words in paint the color of dawn through smoke: *Returned Goods Accepted.*

And every morning, before I unlocked the door, I stood in the doorway a moment with my hand flat against the wood and listened. It was a habit from the wardbones, from those long dark miles under the city where you learned that everything made a sound if you were patient enough—the seal humming its one long note, the stone remembering footsteps. The shop made a sound too. Quieter. The sound of forty-odd jars of memory sitting on shelves in the back room, each one faintly warm, each one waiting.

Kestrel called them his ledger of debts. He never said whose debts. I think he counted himself among them.

I unlocked the door.

* * *

Our first client of the day was a widow named Marta Voss, which was fitting, because the last time she had come to us she had not been a client at all. She had been a seller. In the hungry spring after the wards fell—after the stars came back, after Brannoc's wardens walked out of the wardbones with their pyres unlit and their faces gray as old ash—half of Vell had sold pieces of itself to buy bread. Grief sold best. Grief was heavy, and people carried it like a stone they'd forgotten they could set down.

Marta had sold her grief for her husband. All of it. She had walked out of the market stall lighter than air and poorer than sin, and for two months she had lived in a house full of his coats and his cups and his side of the bed and felt nothing at all about any of it, because feeling required memory, and she had traded hers to a charm-seller named Corvin Hale who gave her four silver bits and vanished into the burning of the gray market before anyone thought to ask him what he'd done with what he took.

Corvin's jars had ended up scattered across Vell when the wardens purged the stalls. Some broke. Some were confiscated—those went into the Inquisition's vaults, until Brannoc, in one of the strange mercies the stars' return had worked on all of us, ordered them released to the survivors. And some, by luck or by the stubborn gravity of things wanting to come home, had found their way to a shop with a blank lintel and a sign that said *Returned Goods Accepted*.

Kestrel kept Marta's grief in a jar of smoked glass on the second shelf, wrapped in wool, labeled in his small careful hand: *V., M. — sorrow — 14 yrs — handle gently.*

He always wrote *handle gently* on the griefs.

"Sit where the light is," I told her, and steered her to the chair by the window, because returning was easier in daylight. Kestrel had learned that early, the way he learned everything—with a thief's precision about other people's pain. Darkness made people brace. Light made them soften. You could pour a river back into a person only if they weren't clenching against it.

Kestrel came out from the back room wiping his hands on his apron—he insisted on the apron; he said it made him look like a tradesman instead of a thief, and I privately thought it made him look like exactly what he was, which was both—and sat down across from Marta Voss with the jar between them.

"Do you remember selling?" he asked. He always asked this first.

"No." Her voice was flat and honest. "I remember the money. I remember it was raining."

"That's good. That's enough." He unwrapped the jar. Inside, behind the smoked glass, something moved—not light, exactly, and not smoke, but the way heat shimmer over a road looks like water. Fourteen years of loving a man and losing him, curled up small.

"It will hurt," Kestrel said. "I won't lie to you about that. It hurt when you carried it, and it will hurt when it comes home. But it will be *yours*, and there is a difference between a wound and a hole. A wound closes. A hole just gets bigger."

I watched Marta's face while he said it. I had spent five years watching people's faces for a living too, though mine had been a different trade—I had been reading them for lies, for weakness, for the flicker that said *run*. Now I mostly read them for readiness. Marta was ready. You could see it in how still she sat. People who aren't ready fidget. People who are ready go quiet as churches.

"Yes," she said. "Please."

Kestrel bared his left hand.

The sigil caught the morning light the way it always did now—not the angry ember-glow of the wardbones, but a low steady warmth, like a coal that had decided to be useful instead of destructive. It had changed shape, subtly, since the Understar. Or perhaps it had only settled into its true shape. Yara's lines, Yara's geometry—but threaded through them now, fine as spider silk, a second pattern that I had slowly recognized, over the months, as *Kestrel*: the particular slant of his strokes, the way he crossed things out and rewrote them. The mark of a man drawn over the mark of a witch. Neither erased. Both legible.

He laid his palm over Marta's heart, and I put my hand on her shoulder, because my part in this was the holding-on. That was the counter-art's other half, the half nobody had written down because it seemed too simple to be magic: somebody must witness. Somebody must stay in the room. Memory returned alone could slide right back out of a person, like a stitch through rotten cloth. Memory returned witnessed stays stitched.

The jar emptied. The shimmer rose up through the glass, through Kestrel's palm, into Marta Voss, and her whole body bowed forward like a bow being drawn—

—and then she made a sound I had heard maybe thirty times by then and would never get used to. Not a cry. Something smaller and worse and better: the little *oh* of a woman meeting her own dead husband again, fourteen years of him arriving all at once, the wedding and the sickness and the joke he told badly and the way he died and the way she had sat beside the bed anyway, every day, because love is mostly attendance.

She wept for eleven minutes. I timed it without meaning to. Kestrel held her hands and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that the grief wasn't already saying better.

When it passed, she looked around the shop like a woman waking, and her eyes went soft and fierce at once.

"I remember you," she said to Kestrel. "Not from here. From before. You were the boy who—" She stopped herself. Everyone stopped themselves, eventually, at some version of that sentence. *You were the boy the wardens hunted. You were the boy who burned the market open with light coming out of his hand. You were the boy who went under the city and paid whatever it cost.*

"I'm the charm-seller now," Kestrel said mildly, which was his answer to all versions of it.

"You were a thief," Marta said, but her mouth was doing something complicated. "My husband bought a charm off you once. Said it smelled like rain on hot stone. He kept it in his pocket till the day he—" Her breath caught, fresh, raw, alive. She pressed her hand to her chest like she was checking that the new-old weight was real. "Oh. Oh, it *hurts*."

"I did warn you," Kestrel said.

"It hurts like he's *here*." She laughed, wet and astonished. "You wonderful, terrible boy. How much do I owe you?"

"Nothing. You already paid once. Overpaid." He was already rising, ending it; he never let gratitude pool. Gratitude pooled too long turned into worship, and worship of anything wearing his face made him itch. "Take these instead." He pressed a paper packet of willow-bark tea into her hands—for the headache that always followed a return, the body's complaint about rehousing fourteen years in an afternoon—and walked her to the door.

From the doorway, she turned back. "There are others," she said. "In my street. People who sold things to Hale, to the Delveries, to that smooth-tongued one off Chandler's Row. They think it's gone forever. They think that's how it works." Her chin came up. "Tell them it isn't."

"We'll tell them," I said.

And she went out into Vell carrying her dead husband whole again, and I stood in the empty shop in the smell of willow bark and smoked glass, and felt the thing I felt most days now and still hadn't found a better word for than *inheritance*.

* * *

At midday we ate bread and pickled things at the counter, and Kestrel did the accounts, and I watched him do the accounts, which was among my private entertainments. He kept books the way he'd once kept his rules—scrupulously, obsessively, in a hand so small it looked like the pages were trying to keep secrets even from themselves. Column after column. *Returned: 6. Held: 41. Broken beyond mending: 3.* He had crossed-out lines everywhere, revisions layered on revisions, and I thought, not for the first time, that the sigil on his palm matched his ledgers line for line. Nothing clean. Everything amended. Everything still becoming.

"The Hollowfield woman comes tomorrow," he said, not looking up. "The one who sold her wedding day. I told her it might take two visits. Big ones don't go back in all at once."

"Mm."

"And Odile's bringing the Chandler's Row jars tonight. Three more. One of them's marked *childhood, summer, unnamed*." His pen paused over the ledger. "Unnamed sellers are the hard ones. No one to bring it home to."

"Then it waits on the shelf until someone claims it," I said. "That's the whole system. That's the beautiful stupid thing about it."

He almost smiled. Around us the shop did its quiet noon work: dust motes turning in the window light, the forty-one jars in the back room giving off their faint collective warmth, like a hearth banked for the night. Somewhere below us—far below, past cellars and sewers and the bones of the old wall—the ward hummed its one long note, steady as it had been since the night my mother spent her last strength binding a villain into starlight and letting her daughter walk out of the dark carrying a half-empty thief by the hand.

I had done the arithmetic of that night a hundred times since, and it never balanced. The seal needed memory to mend. Kestrel had poured in nearly everything he was—Yara's face, my laughter, his own name. And I had stolen it back out again, piece by piece, breaking the oldest law of my mother's art because the law was wrong, because *giving* without *keeping* isn't sacrifice, it's just erasure with better manners. We kept enough in the seal to hold the stars. We kept enough in Kestrel to make him still recognizably Kestrel. But there were gaps in him, gaps I could map like a cartographer maps coastlines, and some nights I lay awake listing them: he didn't remember why he sometimes wept at candlelight. He didn't remember the name of the first person he'd ever stolen from, though he remembered the stealing. He didn't remember certain hours of the wardbones at all, and when I described them he listened with the polite attention of a man hearing about a stranger's dream.

He never complained. That was the worst of it. He treated his own hollows like weather—unremarkable, simply there. And I treated mine like debts, and the shop was, if I was honest, partly a debt payment: every memory I helped return to someone else was a proxy for the ones I couldn't return to him.

"You're staring at me like I owe you money," Kestrel said.

"You owe me money constantly."

"True." He closed the ledger. For a moment he looked out the window at Vell—the repaired rooftops, the warded gates standing open for the first time in living memory, the ordinary miracle of people walking around under a sky with stars in it even in daytime, in the sense of a sky that *had* them—and his expression did the thing it did, the brief shadowed fold between his brows, a man reaching for something on a high shelf and finding it just out of reach.

"Candle," he said, apropos of nothing, and frowned deeper. Then it passed, the way it always passed, and he reached over and pinched the bread.

Later, much later, I would understand what that frown had been reaching for. Later I would find the thing that explained it, folded away where neither of us ever cleaned. But that noon I only threw a crust at his head, and he caught it without looking, thief to the bone, and the bells of the Relit rang the hour across Vell, and I was—gods help me—happy. A wary, arithmetic-haunted, entirely unaccustomed happiness. But happy.

* * *

The afternoon brought the kind of client I had come to measure all others against: a man who wanted nothing taken and everything given back, and who did not know yet if either was possible.

His name was Aldous Ferrane, and he was a warden. Formerly. He wore no colors now, just a carpenter's rough coat, and his hands—hands I had seen, during the bad years, dragging charm-sellers toward pyre-carts—were nicked and scarred with honest joinery. He sat in the chair by the window as if it were a tribunal seat and he expected judgment.

"I held the vault keys," he said, without preamble, when Kestrel sat across from him. "During the purge. Confiscated charms. Hundreds of them. When the Captain ordered them released, I carried them out myself, crate by crate, and I—" His jaw worked. "There was one that got cracked. Sloppy handling. Mine. It leaked. Into me."

Kestrel went very still, the way he went still before a lockpick found its tumbler.

"What was in it?"

"I don't know. That's the point. That's the whole cursed point." Ferrane leaned forward, and now I saw it, what I should have seen when he walked in: the shine of someone else's weather behind his eyes. "For eight months I have grieved a wife I never married. There's a house on Tanner's Lane I keep walking past because it feels like *home*. I know the color of a dress I've never seen a woman wear. Sir—madam—" he floundered between us, desperate, "—is there a word for mourning someone you never had?"

"There is," Kestrel said quietly. "It's the same word. Mourning doesn't check its receipts."

He opened his hands, palm up, sigil glowing its low coal-warmth, and I saw Ferrane flinch at it—the old flinch, the trained flinch, the one every man of the purge years wore like a scar—and then, deliberately, with visible effort, unflinch. That was the thing. That was the whole slow revolution of that year, happening one body at a time in a cobbler's-shop-turned-chapel with a blank lintel: people looking at the mark of magic and choosing not to fear it.

Because here was what none of the histories had bothered to record, what the Inquisition's whole edifice had been built on forgetting: most people's experience of magic was not fire and thrones and wars beneath the skin. It was a charm bought in desperation. A memory sold for bread. And now, increasingly, it was this—a quiet room, a warm hand, and something lost being carried home.

"Will it hurt?" Ferrane asked.

"Yes," Kestrel said. "But it isn't yours to carry, and it isn't yours to keep, and I think—Ferrane—I think the woman it belongs to may still be alive somewhere, missing it. If we can find whose it is afterward, we return it properly."

"And if it's mine by then? If eight months counts? If it's grown into me?"

"Then it's yours," Kestrel said simply, "and no one has the right to take what you freely keep. Not even me. *Especially* not me."

They did it there in the window light, and I witnessed, hand on Ferrane's shoulder, and what came out of the cracked charm was not grief after all but courtship—an entire summer of it, a ferry ride and a shared umbrella and a proposal under the eaves of Tanner's Lane, leaking into a stranger for eight months like dye through water. Ferrane wept harder than Marta Voss had, and longer, and when it was done he gripped Kestrel's wrist—sigil and all, gripped it like a comrade's—and said, hoarse:

"They told us you people took. All my service, they told us: witches take, witches hollow, witches feed. Nobody ever said—" his voice broke and rebuilt itself— "nobody ever said your art could run backward."

"It always could," I said, from the shoulder. "Somebody just finally taught it to."

* * *

We closed at dusk. Kestrel counted the till out of habit even though half our clients paid in eggs, mending, and one memorable case in firewood, and I swept, and together we shelved Odile's three new jars in the back room, wrapping each in wool, Kestrel labeling the unnamed one in his small careful hand: *summer, childhood, unnamed — handle gently.*

I lingered over the shelves afterward. It was a vice of mine, reading his labels—little epitaphs for living things, each one a promise. And it was there, wedged behind the jar of a merchant's first love (some debts circled; that one had come home twice), that I found what I found.

A letter. Sealed with plain wax, no stamp, folded flat and shoved deep behind the jars where dust had gathered on it undisturbed—months of dust, by the thickness of it. Dated, in the corner, in handwriting I knew better than my own by then.

His handwriting. Small, careful, revised, crossed-out-and-rewritten.

Dated the night after we came up out of the Understar. The night he could barely remember his own name, the night I fed him back piece by piece everything I'd salvaged. The night he'd sat by my mother's old hearth with a candle guttering and wept without knowing why, and I had been too exhausted to ask.

He had hidden it from himself. I understood that the way you understand lightning—instantly, totally, without needing the mechanics. A man who could steal memories could hide them from his own keeping, could write a letter and bury the writing of it in his own hollows, could leave instructions for a future self he wasn't sure would exist. My mother's trick, used in reverse. Used *mercifully*. And addressed—to my staggering, in the same cramped hand—not to Kestrel at all.

*To Sable. To be given on the anniversary, or when she finds it, whichever comes first. Do not let me read it before then. Please. — K.*

I stood in the cold of the back room with the jars warming my back and the letter warming my hands and the dust of months on my fingertips, and upstairs I heard him call my name, easy and unknowing, asking what was keeping me.

The anniversary was in six days.

I put the letter in my coat, next to my heart, next to the half of a broken compass that still pointed, always, tenderly, uselessly, at him—and I went upstairs to lie to the man I'd sworn never to steal from, which is, I have decided, what family is.

"Coming," I called.
