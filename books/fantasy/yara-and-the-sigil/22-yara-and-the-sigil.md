## 22. Yara and the Sigil

One year, and the stars still burned.

Kestrel had learned to check them the way other men checked their purses—a quick glance upward at the end of each day, an old habit from the season when the sky had been a wound. Tonight the stars hung thick over Vell like spilled salt, and the border wards hummed their low, patient note beneath the cobbles, and the city breathed.

He stood at the counter of the shop that had no name on its door, only a small carved sigil—the mark that had lived in his palm for most of his life—mounted above the lintel where the wardens could see it and do nothing. Those days were over, mostly. Warden-Captain Brannoc kept his word, which was more than Kestrel had ever expected from a man who burned people. The Inquisition had larger worries now: wards to rebuild, a hole under the city to pretend they had always known about, and a population that had seen the stars go out and come back and would never quite trust the dark again.

The shop was quiet at this hour. Sable had gone up to bed an hour ago, leaving the smell of her rosemary tea hanging in the rafters and a half-finished cipher worksheet pinned to the corkboard—she taught now, three evenings a week, teaching children of the gray market how to read memory-script without losing themselves in it. Yara's journal sat in a glass case by the window, open to a page Kestrel could almost read. Almost. Some nights he got whole sentences. Other nights the cipher slid through him like water through fingers, and he suspected that was deliberate—that somewhere in the ink Yara had written something she did not want him to carry.

The candle he was trimming—the one that made his eyes sting lately, though he never let himself wonder why—guttered in a draft from under the door.

And then he saw the letter.

It lay beneath the glass case, half tucked under the journal's stand, as if it had always been there and his eye had simply refused to fall on it until tonight. A single sheet folded three times and sealed with wax the color of old blood, pressed with a thumbprint rather than a signet. He knew before he touched it whose thumbprint. He had spent five years memorizing the lines of Yara's hands, and even now, with half of what she was returned to him, some things lived deeper than theft or gift.

But it was the handwriting that stopped his breath.

His own. The cramped leftward slant of it, the way he crossed his *t*s low and hard, the little hook he put on his *K* because Odile had once teased him that his name looked like it was trying to fly away. He turned the letter over twice. He checked the seal, unbroken. He held it to the candlelight and saw the faint shadow of words within, and his hands began to shake—not with fear, exactly, but with the particular vertigo of standing at the edge of a room in your own house and finding a door you have never once noticed.

*You wrote this,* said a voice in him that sounded like Sable. *Some part of you wrote this, and then took it away from yourself.*

That was a thing he could do now. That was what he was, after everything: a man who could take. Even from himself.

Kestrel sat down slowly on the stool behind the counter. Outside, Vell celebrated—the anniversary had become a festival almost overnight, lanterns strung from the ward-towers, children wearing paper stars on sticks, the whole city determined to be glad out loud. The noise came through the window as a warm blur. He broke the seal.

The paper smelled faintly of smoke and yarrow. Her handwriting was underneath his own—no. Not underneath. His handwriting was *over* hers, written in the spaces between her lines, and when he understood that, he understood the shape of what he had done, and grief arrived ahead of the words like weather before a storm.

*Kestrel,* his own hand said.

*If you are reading this, you survived. I want you to notice that first. Sit with it a moment. You were never good at sitting with things.*

*You don't remember writing this letter. You hid it from yourself the way you used to hide charms in a dead man's coat lining—somewhere clever, somewhere your own searching hands would skip past. I'm writing this the night before we went down into the wardbones, while you slept in Odile's back room and I couldn't sleep at all, and I am going to tell you the last thing, the one thing I made you forget, because you'll need it when the stars are lit and the danger is over and you start wondering what else the fire took.*

*You remember now—I gave most of it back, or Sable stole it home to you piece by piece, stubborn girl, she gets that from neither of us—that I carried the sigil before you. You remember the Understar, and the seal, and how a key must be given freely or it is only a lock. But there's a gap in you. A small one, candle-sized. You've felt it every time you look at a flame too long. Tonight I am going to hand you the missing piece, and you are going to hide it inside this letter, and one day you are going to find it again.*

*Here is what you chose to forget.*

*The night I sealed myself into the Understar, I was not only buying the world time. I was dying. The Court's thorns had been in me for a year by then; Ashvine's poison doesn't kill, it hollows, and there was not enough of me left to hold a seal for more than a decade. Ten years, maybe less. I needed a vessel that could carry the key out into the light and keep it alive—and bring it home when the time came, whole, fed, loved. Loved especially. The seal mends with freely given memory, and a hollow man has nothing to give.*

*I found you in the ash-market with your hands full of someone else's yesterday, eleven years old and already so careful, already apologizing to the memories you took. Do you know what I thought? I thought: there. That one. That boy steals grief like he's carrying it for someone, like he intends to give it back.*

*I cut the sigil from my palm and pressed it into yours, and it hurt you, and you forgave me before I finished saying sorry. And then I named it. We never told you that, did we? Everyone calls it Yara's mark, the witch-sigil, the key. But a sigil isn't a thing, Kestrel. It's a word. A word of power, spoken in the oldest tongue, and the word I spoke over your small burned hand was not my name.*

*It was yours.*

*I named the sigil Kestrel, after the bird that hangs in the wind without seeming to try, watching, patient, kind. In the old language it means: the second chance. I did not choose you to be the key's carrier. I chose you to be the key's meaning. Everything the sigil has done—every door it opened, every star it relit—it did because a boy who returns what he takes was holding it.*

*You asked me once, in the Understar, why I saved you in the ash-market, and I said something clever and didn't answer. This is the answer: I didn't save you. You saved me. You were five years old and you found a hollowed-out witch in the ruins of a war and you gave her back her daughter's face—you don't remember that either, and you don't need to, but it's true—and everything after, all of it, the sigil and the seal and the stars, was just me trying to return the favor.*

*Memory is the only inheritance that grows heavier the longer you carry it, and lighter the moment you share it. So share this one, when you find it. Say it out loud to Sable. Say it to your own reflection if you have to. Let it be heavy in the air instead of hidden in your hand.*

*The candle is guttering and Sable is stirring upstairs and I have perhaps an hour left of being alive in the ordinary way, and I find I want to spend it watching you sleep, which will embarrass you terribly if you ever learn of it. Good. Be embarrassed. It means you're loved.*

*Light the candle, Kestrel. Don't look away this time.*

*—Yara*

*P.S. You hid this letter in the one place in the whole shop your eyes refuse to rest. Clever hands skip past clever hiding places. I taught you that. I'm glad it worked one last time.*

Kestrel read it twice. Then he sat very still, the way the letter had told him to, and let survival sit with him like a guest.

Outside, the festival swelled—a fiddle starting up somewhere near the fountain, a scatter of laughter, a child's voice insisting on one more lantern. The candle beside him burned steadily, throwing his shadow long across the counters and cases and the little glass shrine where a dead woman's handwriting waited to be read by students who would never know her. He looked at the flame.

For a year, he had flinched from candlelight. For a year, tears had come to him at odd hours, salt without reason, and he had built a life around not asking why, the same way he had once built a life around not being seen. Now he looked, and did not look away, and what rose in him was not the grief he had braced for but something stranger—a memory assembling itself out of the flame's small movements, the way a face comes together out of distance.

A woman with smoke in her hair, kneeling in ash. A girl's face held out in two small child-hands like something rescued from a fire. *Here,* the boy had said, not knowing what he gave. *You dropped this.*

"Oh," Kestrel said aloud, to the empty shop, to the year-old letter, to the woman who had planned even her absence like a gift. "Oh, it was me."

The sigil in his palm warmed—not burning, not anymore, just warm, the way a hand is warm when another hand holds it. He spread his fingers in the candlelight and looked at the mark without flinching: the fine silvered lines, the shape everyone in Vell had begun drawing on doors and shutters and the backs of children's hands for luck. They called it the Star-Keeper's mark now. There were songs. One of them was very bad and Sable sang it on purpose to annoy him.

He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, and for a while he simply let the crying happen, the way she'd taught him, giving the sorrow back to the air where it belonged.

The stairs creaked.

Sable came down wrapped in a blanket with her hair loose and her mother's gray eyes soft with sleep, and she stopped when she saw his face, and she did not ask what was wrong, because she could already tell that nothing was. She crossed the shop, blew out the candle—he felt the small darkness like a blessing—and lit another from the banked hearth coals, setting it between them on the counter.

"You found it," she said. "I wondered when. I felt the seal break from upstairs." She settled onto the stool beside him, shoulder against his shoulder. "What did you hide from yourself this time, thief?"

"She named it after me."

Sable went very still. Then she huffed a laugh that was half a sob. "Of course she did. Of course she—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "She named a key to the Understar after a boy who picked pockets in the ash-market. Mother. *Mother.*" And then she was laughing and crying at once, and Kestrel put his arm around her and held on while the anniversary of the stars rang softly outside.

After a long time she said, "Read it to me. All of it. Every word she wrote in her hand and yours."

So he did. His voice caught at the postscript and she caught him, steadying, the way you steady a lantern in wind. When he finished, neither of them spoke for a while, and the candle between them burned the way candles burn when no one is afraid of them.

Then Sable reached across the counter, took the letter gently from his hands, and did something he did not expect. She pressed her palm flat over the page, closed her eyes, and whispered the words of the counter-art—the giving-back, the returning—and when she lifted her hand, the letter was blank.

"What—" His heart lurched. "Sable, what did you—"

"Shh. Look." She turned the page toward him.

In the center of the blank paper, in Yara's handwriting, fresh as the hour it was set down, stood a single line:

*A sigil is a promise. Keep making it true.*

"It wasn't meant to be kept," Sable said quietly. "Not hidden, not cased in glass. Memory isn't a thing you store, Kestrel. It's a thing you spend." She folded the letter once, twice, and handed it back to him. "Tomorrow we open the shop late. Mrs. Halloran comes for her son's voice at noon, and the cooper's girl at dusk. And I think—" she hesitated, and he saw his mother's smile move across her face like weather, "—I think tomorrow we start teaching the returning to anyone who wants to learn. Not just charm-craft. The real trade. Her trade. Ours."

Kestrel looked down at the line in Yara's hand, and then at his own palm, and understood at last what he had been carrying all these years—not a burden, not a debt, not even a gift exactly, but an assignment. A word spoken over a child in the dark, waiting all this time to be answered.

"A sigil is a promise," he said.

The candle flame leaned toward him, companionable as a cat.

And it was then, in the warmth of that small light, with his sister asleep-leaning against his shoulder and the whole celebrating city humming its wards beneath them, that Kestrel noticed the last thing—the thing Yara could not have known, the thing that made the breath go out of him slow and strange:

At the bottom of the page, below her farewell line, new writing was surfacing. Ink rising like a bruise, letter by letter, in a hand that belonged to no one he had ever met—neither hers nor his nor Sable's—slender and sharp and unmistakably alive:

*Thank you for lighting them. Now, if you are quite finished celebrating — the second door is opening.*

Beneath it, drawn with a few economical strokes, was a symbol he had seen only once before: burned into the stone of the Understar's far wall, past the seal, past the place where Yara had dissolved into light—a door he had assumed was decorative, a relic of the old war, shut forever.

It had begun, very softly, to glow.
