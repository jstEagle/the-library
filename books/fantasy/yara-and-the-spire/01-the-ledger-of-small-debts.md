## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The bone told her first with warmth.

That was always how it began—not with sound, not yet, but with heat rising through the graver's handle into her palm, as though the femur had been pulled from a fire instead of a cold-drawer. Ulyana Okafor had carved eleven hundred bones in the Guild of Impossible Things' workshop at Grayharbor, and every one of them had warned her this way: *someone is still home.*

She adjusted the whale-oil lamp, tucked a coil of pale hair behind her ear, and kept working. Around her the workshop breathed in its night rhythm—the tick of cooling tallow, the sigh of the sea against the pilings below, the long slow exhale of the guildhouse settling on its ancient bones of ship-timber and stone. Forty carvers worked these benches by day. By night there was only Ulyana, because Ulyana had made herself the sort of person nobody thought to relieve.

The femur lay across her leather cradle like a sleeping woman's arm. It *was* a woman's—sixty-odd years old at death, worn thin at the knee, marked by an old poorly-set fracture that had left a ridge like a healed river delta. Guild paperwork said only: *Donor 4471, purchased lawful, deliver finished to the Hall of Instruments by week's end.* The guild bought bones the way fishwives bought herring, in lots, by weight, no questions. The dead did not come with names attached. Names cost extra, and the guild never paid for what it could take for free.

Ulyana set her finest graver to the shaft and began the fluting work, the long shallow channels that would let the finished bone sing when strung in the Hall's great wind-chimes. And the warmth bloomed into voice.

Not in her ears. Never in her ears. In her hands, in the small bones of her wrists, in the marrow-deep place where she supposed a soul would keep its desk if a soul kept one. The dead spoke through their own substance; they had nothing else left to speak with. This woman's voice was dry and unhurried, the voice of someone who had spent decades deciding what her last words should be and was pleased, at last, to say them.

*Tell them the tide takes back what it lends,* the bone murmured as the graver whispered along the first channel. *Tell my sister I paid the ferryman double and he took it, he took it all, tell her the debt is closed—*

Ulyana carved and said nothing.

This was the rule. She had written it for herself at fifteen, inked it inside her own mind where no ledger clerk could find it: *What the dead give me, dies with me.* She was a vessel, and a vessel's virtue was in holding. The guild paid her to carve, and the carving let the dead finish their speaking—it emptied them, gently, the way you empty a jug—and once emptied, a bone became only bone, clean and quiet and fit for instruments. That was the theory the masters taught. What they did not teach, what Ulyana had learned alone at her bench over eleven hundred nights, was that the dead did not want to be emptied. They wanted to be *carried.* Every one of them pressed their final secret into her hands like a mother pressing an infant to a stranger's breast: *take it, take it, take it where I cannot go.*

And so she carried them. All of them. A locksmith's confession of the night he'd let a murderer through his client's door. A midwife's grief for seventeen children she had failed and one she had stolen and raised as her own. A tax collector who had loved a man he could never name aloud while alive and so named him, over and over, forty years too late, into the wrist-bone Ulyana was hollowing. She carried a harbor's worth of last words. She was, she sometimes thought in the small hours, the most crowded person in Grayharbor—a city of sixty thousand souls, and none of them as inhabited as she was.

*Vessia,* said the femur.

Ulyana's graver slipped. The blade skated a hairline across the channel, and she went very still, the way she had learned to go still when a master walked behind her bench.

*Vessia,* the woman said again, and the second time was different from the first—not a word but a key turning in a lock, not sound but weight. The lamp flame leaned sideways though no draft moved. Out in the dark workshop, the half-finished chimes hanging from the drying rack stirred without wind and gave off one low note, like a bell remembering a funeral.

The dead woman was weeping now. Ulyana felt it in the bone, a dampness that wasn't dampness, sorrow wicking up through the shaft the way oil wicks through a wick. *Vessia. Vessia. Tell someone. Please. There is no one left to tell.*

"I don't know that name," Ulyana whispered to the lamplight. It was true, and it was also, somehow, a lie—the word sat strangely on her tongue, familiar the way a stranger's face is familiar, the way you can be certain you'll see it again somewhere you aren't looking.

The dead woman only repeated herself, softer now, the way the dying soften. *Vessia. Vessia.* As if repetition were rope, and she were throwing the end of it across some vast darkness, hoping someone on the far side would catch hold and pull.

*

At dawn, Master Hollis found her exactly where he expected to find her, bent over Donor 4471 with her sleeves rolled and her eyes red-rimmed.

"Finished?" He picked up the femur, turned it against the gray window light, ran a thumbnail down the channels. His fingers were soft as eels'; everything about Hollis was soft, his voice, his belly, his conscience. "Clean work. The fluting's a hair uneven at the third mark."

"The bone had a bad set in it. Old fracture."

"Hm." He weighed the bone in his hand, and Ulyana watched his face for the flicker she had learned to watch for—the moment a master felt the faint wrongness of a freshly-carved piece, the residue of speech. Most of them never noticed. The guild hired carvers for steadiness of hand, not for hearing. Only the very old masters, the ones who touched every shipment going into the vaults, knew what lived in the work, and they never spoke of it either. The guild had many silences, nested inside each other like boxes.

"You were here all night again," Hollis said. It wasn't concern. It was inventory. "You should take a wife or a husband, Okafor. Something to go home to."

"I have the work," Ulyana said, and smiled the small flat smile she kept for exactly this purpose, and saw Hollis's attention slide off her the way water slides off oiled cloth. That was her other craft, practiced as carefully as the carving: being unremarkable. Twenty years in the guildhouse and no one could say what she looked like, only that the work got done, the quiet girl, the bone-girl, always at her bench. She had made herself a hole in the shape of a person. It kept her safe. It kept the dead safe too, all those smuggled secrets riding under her skin through guarded halls, invisible because no one ever bothered to look at a vessel.

She signed the delivery slip. *Carver: U. Okafor.* Her whole name existed nowhere else in the world. Not on a lease, not on a registry, not in a church book. Her parents were two names on two graves outside the city, and her grandmother's name—

Her grandmother's name was on something, somewhere. Grandmother Mirela had been guild too, before her; higher than carver. Ulyana had inherited from her only the gift, the bench tools, and a locked cedar chest she had never had the courage to open.

*Vessia,* said the emptied bone, one last time, as Hollis carried it toward the Hall of Instruments. Faint as breath on a winter window. The woman was nearly gone now, poured out into the channels, ready to become music. But the name stayed behind in Ulyana's hands, warm as the bone had been, refusing to cool.

*

She meant to let it go. She had let ten hundred secrets go into silence; she could let one more.

But that evening, closing up the workshop, she passed the map room—the long gallery where the guild kept its charts of every coast, every trade road, every kingdom and claim—and on impulse she had never once obeyed in twenty years, she went in. Lamplight fell on the great atlas chained open on its stand, the master copy, updated quarterly by cartographers who came and went like weather.

She turned pages. Northreach. The Ashen Coast. The Salt Kingdoms, the Inland Reaches, the Nine Cities of the Littoral. Countries whose names she knew, countries whose names she didn't, countries so old the maps showed only their ruins and their ghosts.

There was a gap. She noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth—by the ache of the absence, the strange smoothness where something should be. Between the Ashen Coast and the Salt Kingdoms the coastline simply stopped being detailed, softened into blank parchment, annotated in a clerk's faded hand: *unclaimed marshland, no trade value, unsurveyed.*

No country. No name. Just a hole shaped like a nation, sitting precisely where the dead woman's grief had pointed.

*Vessia,* Ulyana said silently, testing it against the blank space, and the atlas page seemed almost to shiver, as if the parchment itself were trying to remember and couldn't.

Behind her, out in the corridor, boots. Two pairs, heavy, moving fast, and between them a third sound that stopped her heart: dragging fabric, wet breathing, and a man's voice broken into pieces saying, over and over, *"the granddaughter—the map—find her the granddaughter—"*

Ulyana snuffed the lamp and stepped into the dark of the map room's deepest aisle, her pulse loud enough to drown the sea.
