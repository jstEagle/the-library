## 3. What the Guild Buys

The vaults beneath the guildhouse were older than the guildhouse itself.

Ulyana had known this the way one knows a scar's history—by touch, by rumor, by the way the stairs descended past any sensible depth and kept going, each step worn into a shallow bowl by feet that had climbed them for centuries before Grayharbor had a name. The walls sweated cold. The lamps burned a greenish flame that gave light without warmth, fed on something the guild bought in barrels and never spoke of, and they threw her shadow long and wrong against the stone as she went down.

In her arms she carried an inventory ledger, blank except for the first page, where Master Corvel's clerk had written in his tidy hand: *Autumn stock-taking. Carver Vasquez to verify provenance of bone goods, levels three through seven.*

It was a plausible assignment. That was what made it dangerous. Ulyana Okafor was good at bone; everyone knew it. She was quiet, punctual, incurious—or so the guild believed—and no one questioned sending her into the deep shelves to count what could not be counted anywhere else. For six years she had built that reputation brick by brick: never asking, never lingering, never letting her eyes rest too long on anything. Invisibility was a craft like carving. You did it with small strokes, patiently, until the world forgot you had edges at all.

But the dead woman's femur still sat wrapped in linen on her workbench upstairs, and twice now—in the dark between sleep and waking—she had heard the name again. *Vessia.* Spoken not with fear but with something worse. With longing.

And this morning, when she had unrolled the courier's map to check that it still hid well inside its hollowed bone, her eye had caught on a notation in the margin. Small. Almost apologetic. A word she hadn't been able to read before, because she hadn't known to look for it.

*Vessia.*

The same name the dead woman carried in her marrow.

So now she walked the third level of the vaults with a lamp in one hand and a lie in the other, and told herself she was only doing her job.

* * *

Level three held the ordinary treasures: reliquaries, singing skulls from drowned choirs, a cradle carved entirely from whale-ear bones that hummed lullabies in a language no living mother knew. She counted them. She wrote numbers. Her pen moved steadily and her mind moved elsewhere, down and down, toward the lower shelves where the lamps burned fewer and the air tasted of iron and old snow.

Level five was where things changed.

Here the tags were different. Every object in the upper vaults wore a tag of cream vellum, inked with provenance and price. But level five's tags were grey. Stiff. Made of some hide she didn't want to identify. And the writing on them was done in a hand she recognized—not Corvel's clerk, not Corvel himself, but a hand she had grown up watching across a kitchen table, guiding hers over copybook letters.

Her grandmother's hand.

Ulyana stood very still in the green lamplight and made herself breathe.

The first tagged object was small: a bird, taxidermied or perhaps only sleeping, its feathers the blue of dusk on water. The tag read:

*Songbird of Vessia. Sings the national anthem. Do not wind.*

She looked around the empty aisle, absurdly, as if someone might explain. Then she took out her ledger and wrote nothing, and moved to the next shelf.

A locket, silver, holding a curl of hair that was still growing. *Vessian make. Hair continues to grow forty years after cutting. Owner unknown.*

A jar of soil that whispered. *Earth from the Vessian royal orchard. Contents audible within three paces.*

A child's shoe. A wedding ring fused to a second ring that matched no finger. A flag folded so many times it had become a stone, heavy as sin, warm to the touch.

Every tag bore the same country. Every tag bore her grandmother's handwriting—the same slanted capitals, the same habit of pressing hard enough to emboss the vellum, the same little hook on the letter *g* that Ulyana had spent her childhood imitating badly.

*Vessia.* She said the word inside her own skull, careful as a thief. It fit in her mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for.

There was no Vessia. There had never been a Vessia. She had gone through every atlas in the guild library once, years ago, in one of her rare indulgences of curiosity, and found nothing between the Karst States and the Sea of Glass but open water and a note about treacherous shoals. No country. No anthem. No royal orchard to steal soil from.

And yet here sat its belongings, dozens of them, hundreds, shelved in the dark like preserves in a pantry, each one still alive, still whispering, still growing, still singing—kept by merchants who dealt, she understood now, not in impossible things but in *orphaned* ones. Things whose homes had been deleted. Things that belonged to a place that existed nowhere except in these basement rooms, where the guild kept what it had stolen along with the place itself.

Her hands were shaking. She set the lamp down on a shelf of jars—all labeled VESSIA, all faintly murmuring, a whole countryside's worth of earth packed in glass—and pressed her palms flat against the cold stone until the shaking stopped.

Think, she told herself. Think like a carver. Find the grain. Follow it.

Forty years ago. The map was forty years old, or its style was; the courier had called her *the granddaughter of the one who wrote us out*, and the tags below level five were written in that same grandmother's hand, and somewhere above her head the guild went about its respectable business of buying and selling wonders while underneath it all lay a country's worth of evidence that wonder could be amputated. That a whole nation could be cut away like necrotic bone, and the wound closed so cleanly that even the patient forgot it had ever had a limb there.

The dead woman's voice rose in her memory, patient and repeating: *Say it. Say it again.*

Ulyana picked up the lamp and went down to level seven, because stopping now would be worse than knowing.

* * *

> **From the private ledger of Master Corvel, entry dated the 14th of Emberfall:**
>
> *The courier problem resolves itself, as problems do, given sufficient gravity. He reached the yard and no further. The map, however, has not been recovered. I do not like unfinished entries.*
>
> *Query to myself: who saw him fall? Answer: the carver. Vasquez. She held him while he died, which is either misfortune or opportunity, depending on what he managed to say with his last breath. Couriers say very little at the end. They are trained to carry, not to speak.*
>
> *Still. I have read her file. Six years of exemplary silence. Granddaughter of Maren Okafor, which is a fact I hold close the way one holds a knife—one does not display it, but one is aware of the edge.*
>
> *Note for tomorrow: authorize the autumn inventory. Let her count the lower shelves herself. If the girl carries questions, better she should meet them standing among the answers, where the answers are surrounded by our locks. Curiosity handled properly becomes loyalty. Handled improperly it becomes inheritance, and we have already had one Okafor decide what the guild should keep and what it should forget. We will not survive two.*
>
> *Addendum: the fire at the spire dims. Reports put it smaller than a coin. Smaller than a fingernail. Belief starves slowly, then all at once. When it goes out, the door closes forever, and everything below level five becomes merely junk.*
>
> *I find I am not ready for these things to be junk. I find, examining my own heart as honestly as I can, that I want them to remain stolen forever. This is the most honest entry I have ever written, and I shall burn it by morning.*

* * *

On level seven the tags stopped having words Ulyana could read comfortably.

Not because the script was foreign—it was her grandmother's throughout—but because the objects themselves resisted being catalogued. A mirror that showed only empty streets. A bell that rang with the sound of a market day: haggling, laughter, a cart wheel, gulls. A single tooth, human, molar, tagged simply *Last proof. Handle as scripture.*

And in the deepest alcove, behind a door of black wood with no handle, a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Like breathing, if breathing could be homesick.

Ulyana stood before the door a long time. Her breath fogged in the cold. Somewhere above her, Grayharbor went about its morning—fish-carts and gulls and the honest clatter of a port town—and none of it, none of it, knew what was stacked beneath it like ballast in a ship's hold: the weight of a country, keeping the guild's fortunes stable.

*They didn't buy these,* she thought. *You don't buy things from a place that doesn't exist. You take them first, and then you take the place, and then you sell the pieces back to collectors who will never know what they're holding.*

Thieves of existence. That was the phrase her mind offered, and it was terrible enough that she almost laughed at it, alone in the dark, because it sounded like something from one of her own cheap novels—except that novels ended, and this was a stairwell, and the stairwell went up to a world built on the theft.

She thought of her grandmother's hands. How they had taught her the first spiral cut. How they had smelled of tallow and camphor. How those same hands had written *do not wind* beside a songbird that still remembered an anthem no atlas admitted to.

Grief, she discovered, could curdle. It sat in her chest like milk left out, sweet turning sharp.

The dead woman's femur. The courier's map. The tags. Three threads of the same grain, running true, converging somewhere below level seven behind a black door that breathed.

*I'm going to need help,* she thought, and the thought frightened her more than the door did, because help meant another person, and another person meant a name spoken aloud, and she had spent six years learning never to speak aloud.

She turned back up the stairs. On level five she paused, just once, beside the songbird. Its cage was covered with cloth, as though the guild couldn't bear to look at it and couldn't bear to throw it away. Beneath the cloth came the smallest sound—a few notes, over and over, worn thin as river stones. An anthem sung to nobody, for forty years, in a basement.

Ulyana lifted the corner of the cloth.

The bird opened its eyes. They were bright and ancient and unbearably patient, and they fixed on her face as if it had been expected a very long time.

"Vessia," Ulyana whispered—the first time she had ever broken her rule, the first time the word had crossed her lips into open air—and every jar of soil on the shelf went silent at once, listening, and the bird began, very softly, to sing the whole song through.
