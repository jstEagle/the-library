## 4. Grandmother's Hand

The dead woman had a name now, and it was not Vessia.

Ulyana learned this on the third night after the stranger died in her arms, when she finally made herself open the chest at the foot of her grandmother's bed. She had been avoiding it for two years—since the funeral, since she had carried the key home in her pocket like a stone she could not bring herself to throw into the harbor. The chest smelled of camphor and dried lavender and, beneath both, the faint mineral tang of bone dust that never quite leaves the hands of a carver, no matter how many decades pass.

Her grandmother's name had been Serafina Okafor, and in the guildhouse of Grayharbor they spoke it with something close to reverence. *Master Carver Emerita.* Forty years of service. Ledgers balanced to the half-penny. A seat on the council until her lungs gave out. Ulyana had grown up in the shadow of that reputation, learning early that the only way to be noticed was to be flawless, and the only way to be safe was to be invisible. She had chosen invisibility. Her name appeared on no guild ledger but the wage rolls, signed in a hand deliberately altered, her gift mentioned to no one.

Now she knelt on the cold floorboards of the little house above the chandlery, lifted the lid, and found the ledgers stacked where anyone might have found them—as if her grandmother had wanted them read. Or as if she had simply never imagined anyone would come looking. Both possibilities frightened Ulyana in different ways.

There were eleven of them. Not guild-issue; the bindings were plain calf, unmarked, the kind sold in any stationer's shop to tradesmen who wanted their accounts kept private. She took the earliest one to the table, turned up the lamp, and opened it.

The handwriting stopped her breath.

She knew that hand. Every carver in the guild knew it, though few had seen it outside the archive: the sharp ascenders, the peculiar way the letter *r* hooked backward like a claw, the numerals slanted as if leaning into a wind that blew only leftward. It was the hand that wrote the founding charters displayed behind glass in the vault antechamber. It was the hand, she realized with a slow cold spreading through her chest, that she had spent twenty years studying because Master Corvel kept one original decree locked in the deep archive and let no one copy it—and Ulyana, whose memory was her fortune, had copied it into herself.

That decree was dated forty-one years ago. It bore no country's name, only a seal she had never been able to place and a phrase she had never forgotten: *rendered null, rendered nowhere.*

And here was the same hand, decades earlier, writing ordinary entries. Purchases. Sales. And then, less ordinary ones.

*Received from the Vessian delegation: one mirror that shows the viewer as they will be, not as they are. Price negotiated in names.*

*The Spire fire gutters again. Believers dwindle. Corvel proposes we hasten matters. I have drafted the instrument. It wants only signatures.*

*Signed today. The country is out of all documents by morning. The map remains—burned, per protocol. One copy retained against future need. I keep it. Someone must, or the door starves entirely, and then what we took becomes worthless even to us.*

Ulyana set the ledger down because her hands were shaking too hard to read.

Through the wall, the chandlery clock struck two. Somewhere below, a dog began barking at nothing and then thought better of it.

She turned pages with the care owed to something venomous. The entries grew terser as the years advanced, the handwriting hardening from a young woman's flourish into the cramped, economical script Ulyana remembered from shopping lists and birthday letters. In the margin of one page, beside an entry recording the disposition of *seventeen Vessian bells, which ring themselves when their country is spoken aloud*, her grandmother had written a single line in smaller characters:

*I did not know they would forget how to grieve.*

Below it, in different ink, added later: *They do not grieve. That is the mercy of it. That is the horror of it. Both are true and I can no longer tell which I mean.*

* * *

The bones started talking that same night.

Not the bones in her workshop—she had locked that door, had walked past it three times with her jaw set, refusing. But there were always bones within reach in a carver's house: the polished vertebra strung as a curtain-weight, the little carved bird on the mantel, the needle-case of hollowed ulna by the mending basket. She picked up the needle-case at dusk to thread a torn sleeve, and the voice came through like water through a cracked jar.

*Vessia. Vessia.*

The dead woman from the femur—the one whose secret had started all of this—was no longer content to whisper in the pieces Ulyana carved. She had moved into every piece of dead material in the house. The word came urgent and doubled, the way you say a thing twice when you fear you will not be allowed to say it again.

"I hear you," Ulyana said to the empty room, and felt absurd, and said it again anyway. "I hear you."

*Vessia. You carry us. Name us. Name us before—*

The voice frayed. It came back thinner.

She understood then what was happening, in the way she understood bone—by grain and weight and the resistance it offered. The dead woman was spending herself. Every repetition cost something. Whatever force had held her in the femur was thinning, stretching across the objects Ulyana touched, desperate to be in more places, to make more noise, to reach further before whatever silence had swallowed Vessia swallowed her too.

By midnight Ulyana had gathered every carved and worked bone in the house onto the kitchen table—a macabre congregation under the lamp—and the word came from all of them at once, overlapping, a choir reduced to one ragged note.

*Vessia. Vessia. Vessia.*

"Why me?" she asked them. "There are other carvers. There are older hands."

But she knew. The courier's blood was still under her fingernails in imagination, his words still printed on the inside of her eyelids. *The granddaughter of the one who wrote us out.* The dead had not come to her by accident. They had followed the debt.

She looked down at the congregation of small white things on her table—at the bird she had carved at nine years old, sitting in this same kitchen while her grandmother sharpened tools behind her and told her, *always carve with the grain of what the person was, never against it*—and grief arrived, curdling as it came.

Because she had loved her. That was the unbearable architecture of it. The betrayal was not that Serafina Okafor had been a monster; monsters could be dismissed. The betrayal was that she had been kind. She had braided Ulyana's hair. She had taught her to read the grain of femurs and the grain of people. She had kept one copy of the burned map *against future need*, and then, apparently, lost it or hidden it or passed its keeping to someone else—and forty years of erasure had compounded like interest, and a courier had bled out in a guildyard trying to return it.

Ulyana pressed her palms flat against the table, against the hum of the dead, and made herself say it aloud, because the dead deserved honesty from someone in this family:

"She knew what she was doing."

The bones went quiet—not silenced, but listening.

"And she did it anyway," Ulyana said. "And then she wrote down that she was sorry, and kept the receipt, and called it mercy."

The word *Vessia* rose again, softer now. Almost gentle. As if the dead woman understood that rage was also a form of company.

Ulyana sat until the lamp burned low, the ledger open before her, the last entry visible. It was dated eight days before her grandmother's death, the handwriting gone shaky, the backward-clawed *r*s barely legible:

*The girl has my hands. She hides it, as I taught her. God forgive me, I hid it well enough that she may be the only one left who can undo it—if she ever learns what she is carrying. I dare not tell her. Love is a coward's word for what I feel. If she reads this, Ulyana: the map survives. Follow the debt.*

The lamp guttered. In the dark, from the table, from the mantel, from the curtain-weight turning slowly on its cord though there was no draft, the dead woman said:

*He knows you know. The master with the soft voice. He is coming to your door. Run before the third bell, granddaughter-of-Serafina. Run, and take the eleventh ledger—it is not accounts. It is names.*

Ulyana's blood went cold. She pulled the eleventh ledger from the chest and weighed it in her hands—heavier than the others, its spine reinforced, its pages thick. She opened it to the first leaf and found not figures but a list, column after column, in her grandmother's careful hand: names, thousands of them, each with a birth-date and a death-date and, beside most, a small annotation. *Bell-ringer. Bread-singer. Keeper of the eastern gate. She who told stories at the ford.*

A census of the erased. A country in a single book.

Below, the chandlery clock began to strike. One bell. Two.

Ulyana was already moving, stuffing the ledger inside her coat, grabbing the half-carved femur with the map sealed in its hollow core, blowing out the lamp as the third bell rang and boots sounded on the cobbles outside—too many boots, moving with the unhurried precision of people who already knew the doors were watched.

The dead woman's voice followed her to the window, thin as a wire:

*Vessia. Say it once more before you go. It is easier to run toward a name than away from a grave.*
