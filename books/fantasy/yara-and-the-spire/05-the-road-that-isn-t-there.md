## 5. The Road That Isn't There

Grayharbor let go of me in pieces.

First the lamplighters, snuffing their flames one by one as I slipped past them, my satchel heavy against my hip with a dead woman's bones and a folded map that weighed more than either. Then the fish-wives at the quay, already singing their gutting-songs to the grey dawn, and I kept my head down among them because grief is the best disguise a fleeing woman can wear. Last of all, the guildhouse itself, its bone-door white in the early light, watching me go with the blind patience of something that has outlived everyone who ever knocked.

I did not look back. Looking back is a kind of naming, and names are how the guild finds things.

The map was worse than any ledger I had ever carved. It was drawn on skin—sheepskin, I told myself, though I knew better—and the roads on it shifted when I wasn't looking, the way water shifts under ice. One road ran east out of Grayharbor, forked at a crooked willow, and vanished into a blank space where the cartographer had written nothing at all. Just an empty stretch of parchment, pale as an unwritten page, and beneath it, in a hand that trembled: *She will know the words.*

I did not know the words. But my grandmother's house stood on the way east, and I had emptied it of everything except what mattered, and there were songs in her effects—songs written in the same hand as the decree that unmade a country. Songs she should never have been able to write, unless some part of her had refused to forget.

*Vessia,* said the femur in my satchel, soft as breath. *Vessia.* The dead woman had been saying it for three days now, patient as tide.

"I hear you," I whispered to no one. "I'm going."

* * *

The marshlands began where the hard road ended, and they were exactly as the map promised: nothing. Not wilderness, not waste—nothing, a stretch of country my eyes slid off like rain off oiled leather. My feet found tussocks and hummocks to stand on, but between them lay a grey absence that made my teeth ache, the same sensation as reading a ledger entry where a name has been scraped away. The guild had done its work thoroughly here. Even the fog seemed embarrassed to be present.

I unfolded the map at the crooked willow and pressed my grandmother's songbook flat beside it. The first song was a walking song, six lines long, the meter of it suited to boots in mud:

*Marry the water, marry the reed,*
*carry your name like a seed—*

I sang it badly. My voice cracked on the second line, unused to carrying anything but silence, and for a moment nothing happened at all.

Then the ground shivered.

It began under my left foot—a firmness where there had been yielding muck, a straightness where there had been tangle. A plank. Then another, and another, walking themselves out ahead of me across the nothing, a corduroy road of old black timber rising from the marsh as if it had been lying in wait forty years for someone foolish enough to sing. Reeds bent aside. Water pooled along the edges in ditches that had dug themselves. And above it all hung a smell that had no business existing: woodsmoke and rye bread, the smell of a hearth somewhere, though no hearth could exist in a country that didn't.

I sat down on the first plank and shook until my teeth chattered. Not fear—or not only fear. It was the loneliness breaking. For thirty years I had carried other people's truths in silence, a vessel with no wine of her own, and now here was proof that truth spoken aloud could build a road under a person's feet. Memory made literal. Every word of the song laid another board.

*Faster,* urged the femur. *Sing faster. He is behind you.*

I looked back. Far off, at the edge of the nothing, a figure stood where the hard road ended. Too far to see a face; close enough to see that it was not moving, which was worse. Trackers do not hurry. They know their quarry walks into a country that does not exist, and they simply wait for it to stop existing around her.

I sang the walking song again, and again, and the road grew.

* * *

By afternoon the planks gave way to dry ground—real ground, or the memory of it—which held the ghost-shapes of wheel ruts. The songbook was running low on songs I could pronounce, and each one built less than the last, as though the country were growing tired of being remembered in fragments. I had just finished a lullaby about a moon made of cheese (Vessians, apparently, believed the moon was cheese, and who was I to argue) when I heard whistling.

Not the tracker. This whistling was tuneless, cheerful, and getting closer, and it belonged to a young man poling a flat-bottomed skiff through what I had thought was solid marsh-grass. He was lean and sun-browned, with a smuggler's easy slouch and eyes the colour of canal-water, and he took one look at me—one look at the road I had sung into being—and stopped whistling mid-note.

"Well," he said. "That's new."

"Is it." I did not loosen my grip on the satchel.

"Roads out here?" He poled closer, studying the planks as though they might bite. "There's no road out here. There's never been a road out here. My family's worked these waters four generations and—" He stopped. His face did something complicated. "Four generations," he repeated, quieter. "And every year on midwinter night my gran burns a candle in the window and cries, and when I ask her why, she says she doesn't know. She says her mother did it, and her mother before, and none of them ever knew."

The femur in my satchel had gone very still, the way the dead go still when they are listening hard.

"Tam," he said, offering the name the way smugglers offer everything—like it might be counterfeit. "Tam Veshok."

The name went through me like a hook through cloth. *Veshok.* Vessian. The ending was wrong, softened, docked like a ship's name in a foreign port—but the root of it was plain as bone.

"You're Vessian," I said.

"There's no such thing," he said automatically, the way a man recites something drilled into him. Then he heard himself, and frowned. "I mean—there's no such place. Is what they say. What everyone says." He looked at my road again, at the wheel-ruts in the memory-ground beyond it, at the smell of woodsmoke that clung to the air. "What everyone says," he said a third time, and his voice cracked on it, and I understood that I was watching a man discover a hole in himself he had lived inside his whole life without noticing.

"Your family mourns," I said, "because your family remembers. Not up here—" I touched my temple. "In the blood. In the hands. The guild erased this country from every ledger, every atlas, every mouth. But erasure has to be maintained, Tam. It leaks. It leaks out in candles lit for no reason and songs nobody knows the words to and—" I pulled the songbook from my satchel and held it out. "And handwriting that shouldn't exist."

He didn't take it. He was looking past me, down the road, at the figure that had appeared at its far end—closer now, maybe half a mile, still unhurried, wearing the grey travelling coat of a guild tracker and leading, absurdly, a donkey.

"He's been following you since Grayharbor," Tam said.

"Since before Grayharbor, I think."

"Then get in the boat."

"And go where?"

Tam's jaw set. Somewhere behind his eyes, a candle was burning in a window, and he was beginning to remember why. "My gran always said, when we asked about the crying, that we came from *inland*. Inland." He gestured at the whole grey impossible nothing. "Everyone says the marsh ends in nothing. Nobody ever asks why the nothing is exactly the shape of a country."

I climbed into the skiff. It seemed suddenly very important not to be standing on exposed ground when the tracker arrived, and besides—the map's blank places ended at a village marked in that trembling hand: *Tams-hollow, where the forgetting is thickest.*

"Your family came from a village," I said, as he poled us away from my road, into channels that no chart admitted to. "I think I know which one."

"Don't tell me its name." His voice was strange. "I don't—I want to see it first. Before I know whether to grieve properly."

*Vessia,* whispered the femur, urgent now, almost pleading. *Vessia. Vessia.*

"Hold that thought," I told the bone, and to Tam I said nothing at all, because some truths need to be walked into rather than handed over.

Behind us, faint across the water, came a sound that raised every hair on my body: the tracker, standing at the end of my road, had begun to sing. He had a good voice, low and true, and he was singing the walking song perfectly, every word of it—and where his boots rested, the planks were turning grey, and crumbling, and going back into the nothing.

The guild had learned the words.
