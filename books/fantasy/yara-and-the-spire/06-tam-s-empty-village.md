## 6. Tam's Empty Village

The road ended where the song began.

Ulyana felt it under her boots first—a seam in the world, like a bone knitted badly, the place where the marsh gave way to something older and more deliberate. Tam had been humming for an hour without knowing it, some tuneless thread of melody his mother had left him the way other mothers left lockets, and where the melody touched the ground, the ground remembered. A lane unspooled ahead of them, hedged with elder and hawthorn, its ruts worn deep by cartwheels that had not rolled in forty years.

"Is it always like this?" Tam asked, voice low, as if the lane might startle.

"Only here," Ulyana said. "Memory made literal." She kept her hand on the half-carved bone inside her coat, the map folded within it like a seed in a pod. "Everywhere else, forgetting is just absence. Here, it's architecture. Someone built this road out of remembering, and when no one remembers anymore, it falls down."

The dead had been growing louder since dawn. Not words yet—words came later, when she carved—but pressure, the way a full moon presses on a tide. They knew they were close. Whatever was left of Vessia's dead had been drifting toward them all morning like iron filings toward a lodestone, and by the time the village appeared between the hills, Ulyana could hardly hear Tam's footsteps over the hum of them.

It stood in a valley cupped like a palm: stone houses with slate roofs mostly whole, chimneys still standing against a sky gone the color of pewter, fields gone wild with rye that no one had sown but which grew anyway, stubborn as gossip. A well. A church—or what had been a church, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against its neighbor. Laundry lines rusted empty between houses. Doors stood open on rooms with tables set for meals forty years cold.

"Vesska," Tam whispered.

Ulyana turned. He had stopped at the edge of the last field, and his face was doing something complicated—grief and recognition fighting over the same ground.

"My grandmother's name," he said. "I never knew why she said it. She'd stand at the window in Grayharbor and say *Vesska* like a prayer, like a wound, and when I asked, she'd look at me like I'd struck her and say, *nothing, nothing, forget I said it.*" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "This is her village."

"Then you're the first of your family to walk this street in forty years," Ulyana said gently.

"No," Tam said, and his voice went strange. "Look."

* * *

Tam saw the figures the way you see heat-shimmer over summer stone—there and not-there, a suggestion of people at windows, shapes in doorways, someone bending over the wild rye as if harvesting a crop that had rotted decades ago. His grandmother's village, alive with ghosts or memories or something between. He had grown up mourning without knowing what he mourned; his family kept a black cloth folded in a drawer and told him it was for "the old sorrow," and here was the old sorrow standing in a doorway waving at no one.

He looked at Ulyana and saw her stop walking.

She went white—not frightened-white but emptied-white, the color of a page before ink. Her hand found the bone at her coat and gripped it like a railing over a long drop.

"They're here," she said. "All of them. Tam—"

And then the dead of Vessia came out of their houses.

They did not walk so much as arrive, one moment absent and the next present around her, crowding close the way the grieving crowd a coffin. Tam stumbled back from the pressure of them though he could not have said what he stepped away from. Ulyana stood in the middle of the lane with her eyes open and her face streaming tears she didn't seem to notice, and the air around her was full of voices only she could hear.

"*Name us,*" she repeated, or seemed to—the words coming through her like water through a broken dam. "*Name us. We were here. We were the Ferencs, we were the Orlai, we were the millers of the low field and the weavers of the high street—we had names, we had names, we had—*"

Her voice broke. She pressed both hands over her ears, which helped nothing; the voices weren't in her ears.

"I can't," she gasped. "There are hundreds of you. Thousands. I can't hold—I don't know your names, I never knew—"

"*She knew,*" the dead said, all together, and the word landed on Ulyana like a blow. "*The one who wrote us out. She knew every name. She wrote them all down before she unwrote them.*"

Tam caught Ulyana as her knees buckled. She was shaking. "They're not angry at me," she said, dazed. "That's the worst part. They're not even angry. They just want—" She swallowed. "They want to be real. That's all anyone wants, Tam. To have happened."

He held her up in the empty lane of his grandmother's village while the dead pressed close around them both, and he understood suddenly why his family kept the black cloth, why they flinched from the old sorrow. Forgetting wasn't peace. Forgetting was this: a town full of ghosts begging a stranger to say their names because no one living would.

"We'll go to the spire," he said. "We'll say all of it."

"Yes," Ulyana said. Then her eyes went past his shoulder and widened. "Tam—down—"

* * *

The tracker came out of the hedgerow like a blade coming out of a sheath.

Tam had noticed nothing—no footstep, no snapped twig—and later he would understand that the man moved the way guild enforcers were trained to move, through the blind spots of ordinary attention. What Tam saw was a lean figure in gray oilcloth with a crossbow already raised, bolt seated, aimed at Ulyana's back.

He did not think. Thinking was for afterward. He threw himself sideways into her, and the bolt meant for her spine took him high in the shoulder instead, punching through meat and gristle with a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard.

The pain arrived a heartbeat later, vast and bright and total. He went down into the wild rye with Ulyana's scream somewhere above him and the sky wheeling overhead, and through the roaring in his ears he heard her voice change—heard it go flat and cold and precise, the voice of a woman who had spent her life among bones and knew exactly how bodies failed.

"The shoulder's grazed, not pierced—you'll keep," she was saying, tearing his shirt open, pressing down hard enough to make him yelp. "Hold still. Hold *still.*"

The tracker was reloading. Tam watched him work with the strange clarity of the badly hurt: quick hands, a second bolt from a quiver at his hip, the crossbow levering closed with a click that sounded obscenely small for the thing it did. The man's face was calm. That was the worst of it. He wasn't cruel. He was doing a job, the way Tam mended nets, the way Ulyana carved bones.

"Give me the map," the tracker said. His voice was mild. "Master Corvel would prefer you alive, carver. The smuggler is incidental. Walk away and he lives."

"He's bleeding out in a field," Ulyana said.

"People bleed out everywhere. It's not a policy concern."

Something shifted in the air then—something Tam couldn't see but felt along his skin like a change in weather. Ulyana rose slowly from beside him, one hand pressed to his wound, the other closing around the carved bone at her neck. And the dead of Vessia rose with her.

The tracker took a step back. He was guild-trained; he had been warned about the carver's gift, probably in dry ledger language, *subject exhibits auditory contact with remains.* But warnings are paper, and the dead were not. The lane filled with them, dense as smoke, and though the man could not see them, he could see Ulyana—saw her standing in the middle of forty years of erased grief with her chin lifted and a thousand nameless voices pouring through her—and whatever he saw there made his calm crack down the middle.

"This is theft," he said, and his voice shook slightly. "Everything in that country belongs to the Guild. It was paid for. It was *entered in the ledgers.*"

"It was stolen," Ulyana said. "You know it. Corvel knows it. Even the ledgers know it—that's why they're written in a hand nobody wants to read twice."

The crossbow wavered. In that wavering, the dead surged—not touching him, they couldn't touch anything—but pressing their attention on him like heat, like weight, and Tam saw the man's composure go the way ice goes off a pond in spring, all at once and catastrophically. He fired wild, bolt screaming off into the rye, and then he turned and ran, crashing through the hedgerow, gone.

Silence came back gradually, like water returning to a drained pool.

Ulyana knelt again in the trampled grain and put her hands back on Tam's shoulder, and now they were shaking, now that it was over, now that it was safe to shake.

"You idiot," she whispered. "You absolute—you jumped in front of a crossbow."

"You're holding the map," Tam managed. His own voice sounded far away. "Corvel said it himself. You're the key. Keys get carried." He tried a grin and mostly failed. "Besides. My grandmother walked this street. Somebody in my family ought to be allowed to bleed on it."

She laughed—a wet, broken sound—and pressed her forehead briefly to his uninjured shoulder, careful of everything.

* * *

They sheltered that night in his grandmother's house, the one with the blue shutters he recognized from dreams he'd always assumed were invented. Ulyana boiled marsh herbs and packed the wound and stitched it with thread from a sewing basket that still sat by the window where some woman had left it mid-mending forty years ago. Tam lay on a bed that smelled of dust and lavender and watched her work by candlelight, her hands steady, her face exhausted.

Around them, the dead waited. Quieter now. Patient. They knew where she was going.

"Tell me if you want to turn back," Ulyana said quietly, tying off the stitch. "Truly, Tam. Corvel will find us tomorrow—he won't send another tracker, he'll come himself, with better offers and worse threats. What waits at the spire is truth, and truth about Vessia means learning what your family lost and why they grieved and who took it from them. It would be easier to forget. Your grandmother chose easier. Half a country chose easier, and who can blame them?"

Tam looked at the ceiling of his inheritance, at the water-stain shaped like a river delta above the bed where three generations of his blood had slept before anyone decided they had never existed.

"When my grandmother died," he said, "we burned the black cloth with her. My father said, *finally, we can put it down.* And I remember thinking—even as a boy—that putting it down felt like dropping something that had been handed to us to carry." He met her eyes. "I'm done setting it down. If the price of having it back is carrying it, I'll carry."

Ulyana nodded once. She blew out the candle, and in the dark the house breathed around them, full of the patient dead, and far off across the wild fields, toward the spire, the ember of dragon-fire pulsed once—dimmer than the night before, hungry, almost out.

And on the road behind them, unhurried, a lantern light bobbed through the marsh: Master Corvel, coming to make his offer.
