## 7. Corvel's Offer

He came without horses, without torches, without the soft crunch of boots on frost that Ulyana had learned to listen for across three nights of flight. He came instead the way fog comes off Grayharbor in autumn—simply present, one moment absent and the next standing at the edge of Tam's fire as though he had been invited.

Master Corvel was not what she had expected. She had imagined the guild's second chair as a man built of iron and appetite, all hard edges and visible hunger. Instead he was slight, silvering, with kind creases around his eyes and the unremarkable face of a favorite uncle. He wore traveling clothes of good gray wool and carried no weapon she could see. In his gloved hands he held a small lacquered box, and he warmed them at their fire as if he had walked a long way and was grateful for it.

Tam came up out of sleep with a knife already moving, and Ulyana caught his wrist—not because she trusted Corvel, but because she saw, in the way the firelight bent around the old man, that a blade would find nothing to bite.

"Sit, boy," Corvel said mildly. "I've crossed four hundred miles to talk. It would be rude to make me do it standing."

The dead pressed close around Ulyana, closer than they had since the village. She could feel them like a change in pressure before a storm, dozens of Vessian shoulders crowding behind her own, and the dead woman from the femur—the first voice, the name-speaker—was loudest of all: *Careful. Careful. He is gentle the way rot is gentle.*

"You're the tracker," Tam said. He had not sat. The knife stayed in his fist, low against his thigh.

"I'm the guild's negotiator. The tracker is still back at the ford, fishing your arrows out of his shoulder and composing a very dull report." Corvel extended his hands toward the fire again, and Ulyana noticed that they did not tremble with cold. Nothing about him trembled with anything. "Ulyana Okafor. Carver, third bench, Grayharbor chapter. Eleven years on the ledgers and never once entered in them yourself—you've been careful. I respect that. Your grandmother respected it too."

"Don't," Ulyana said.

"Say her name?" He tilted his head. "Miriam Okafor. First Chair of the Guild of Impossible Things. Author—" here his voice took on the cadence of a man reading from a familiar page— "of the Concord of Silence, forty years ago this winter. Yes. I know you know. I know what you found in her effects. I know where you've been and what you carry, because there is very little, child, that this guild does not know."

*He lies by telling truths,* the dead woman murmured. *It is his favorite instrument.*

Ulyana made herself look at the fire rather than at him. The flames burned low and gold, and beyond them the marsh stretched black and whispering, full of roads that existed only so long as she sang the fragments Tam's grandmother had taught her before forgetting why they mattered. Memory made literal. She had felt it under her feet every step of the way here: the world agreeing, grudgingly, to remember.

"You killed a courier," she said. "Two weeks ago. He died in my arms with my name in his mouth and your map in his blood."

"We did not kill him. The erasure did—or rather, the part of it we maintain did. Men who carry pieces of Vessia begin to come apart when they stray too far from the machinery that holds the silence together. He knew the risk. So did the six couriers before him." Corvel said it with genuine regret, and that was the worst thing yet; she believed him. "Which brings me to why I'm here, and why I came alone, unarmed, to sit at a smuggler's fire like an honest man."

He opened the lacquered box. Inside, on faded velvet, lay a seal ring of dark gold and a single sheet of heavy paper bearing the guild's sigil—a door with no handle—and beneath it, in ink gone brown with age, her grandmother's signature.

* * *

"Your grandmother's seat," Corvel said. "Second Chair. Vacant these eleven years because Miriam stipulated it be held for her heir, should one ever claim it. You are the heir. The papers only want your name."

The marsh wind moved through the reeds, and somewhere in it Ulyana could hear the faintest thread of a Vessian lullaby, rising and falling, holding a road open in the dark.

"Return the map," Corvel went on, unhurried, a man laying out goods at market. "That is all. Not your memories—we couldn't take those even if we wished, and we don't. Keep every word the dead have ever told you; carve them into bone until your hands give out. Take your seat at Grayharbor or at the capital chapter, whichever pleases you. The council keeps a residence in each. There is a salary that would embarrass a duke. There is safety—the kind you cannot buy, the kind that means no tracker ever follows you again, no courier ever dies in your arms." His kind eyes did not waver. "And there is this: the guild will never touch Vessia's memory again. That I am authorized to swear. The country stays forgotten—but its people, living and dead, will be left in peace to mourn something they can't name. Which, if you think honestly about it, is what they're doing now."

*He offers you her chair,* said the dead woman, and her voice had gone very quiet, very tired. *She was offered mine.*

"And if I refuse?"

Corvel closed the box. He looked, for a moment, genuinely sorry.

"Then in about a year the last courier routes degrade, the containment frays at the edges, and the guild does what it has done twice before and will do again as many times as necessary. It is not cruelty, Ulyana. Please believe that. It is arithmetic." He set the box on a flat stone between them, deliberately, within her reach. "But before you answer, you deserve to understand *why*. Most initiates never learn it. Your grandmother earned the truth; I think you have too."

He leaned toward the fire, and his shadow stretched long behind him, longer than any shadow had a right to be.

"The guild does not collect impossible things for profit, whatever the apprentices' rumors say. We collect them because impossible things are dangerous in aggregate. A lamp that burns without oil is a curiosity. A door that opens onto yesterday is a catastrophe. And Vessia—" he paused, and for the first time the uncle's warmth left his face entirely, leaving something older and wearier underneath— "Vessia was a country whose impossible things were not objects. They were habits. Practices. A whole national craft of remembering things out of erasure. Do you understand what I'm telling you? In Vessia, forgetting was not permanent. Anywhere. Their bone-singers could restore a name scrubbed from every record. Their memory-roads could reopen a path dissolved by decree. Their spire-fire burned on belief itself."

"Then your hoard—" Ulyana started.

"Is a prison," Corvel agreed. "Every Vessian artifact we seized is locked in vaults not because it shines but because, together, they could unmake erasure anywhere. Anywhere, child. Consider what that means. Every secret this guild has buried in four hundred years—every kingdom quietly deleted, every crime unwritten, every inconvenient person removed from the world's ledger—could be dragged back into the light by a handful of singing bones. Wars restarted. Graves reopened. Atrocities remembered by people who had been mercifully spared the knowing." He shook his head slowly. "Your grandmother understood before any of us. She wrote the Concord of Silence herself. She stood at the spire and helped starve the fire. She did it believing she was saving the world from its own memory."

*She did it believing,* the dead woman repeated, and the words went through Ulyana like a needle finding the seam in a wound.

"She regretted it," Ulyana said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flat and far away. "At the end. I found her ledgers. The later ones. She kept writing Vessia's name over and over in the margins until someone made her stop."

"Yes," Corvel said simply. "Guilt is a long illness. It killed her slower than any knife would have." He rose, brushing marsh-grass from his knees, and gestured at the box. "The offer stands until moonset. Take the chair, take the peace, and let the dead keep their mourning. Refuse, and I will do what I came prepared to do, which I promise you neither of us will enjoy." He turned to go, then paused, and looked back at her with something almost like tenderness. "You have her hands, you know. I watched you carve, years ago, through the workshop window. Same economy. Same patience. The guild has missed her."

* * *

He was gone between one heartbeat and the next, taking his impossible shadow with him, and the fire cracked and settled as though embarrassed by the interruption.

Tam crouched by the stone, staring at the box. "You're not seriously—"

"No." But Ulyana knelt anyway, and picked up the paper, and read her grandmother's signature again in the firelight. *Miriam Okafor*, in the hand she knew better than her own, the hand that had taught her to hold a graver, to read the grain of femur and rib, to listen. The hand that had written a country out of existence, and had then spent its last years scratching that country's name into margins like a penance no one would absolve.

*Temptation,* the dead woman said, *is only clarity that hasn't finished arriving.*

And it arrived, all at once, the way ice arrives on still water. Ulyana saw it whole: the chair, the salary, the safe and gilded silence, the decades of beautiful carvings while somewhere a fire starved and a nation mourned nothing and everything—and herself, sixty years old, first-benched and honored, writing decrees in a beloved careful hand, telling herself each one was arithmetic. Telling herself she was saving the world. Becoming, precisely and completely, her grandmother—with only the guilt arriving late, on schedule, to scratch names in margins.

Her grandmother had been offered mercy in exchange for memory, once. Ulyana had seen the ledger entry herself.

She fed the paper to the fire. Then the ring. The velvet caught and flared greenish, and the lacquered box sat empty on the stone, and Tam began to laugh—a short, disbelieving sound, half fear.

"Moonset," Ulyana said, standing, pulling the map from inside her coat where it had ridden against her heart since Grayharbor. "He gave us until moonset. So we climb tonight."

Above the black line of the marsh, higher than any hill had a right to stand in that flat country, the Spire of Vessia caught the last thin light—and at its summit, small and fierce and nearly out, something burned.
