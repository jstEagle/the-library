## 8. The Spire

The Spire of Vessia did not rise from the land so much as it was remembered into it.

Ulyana had seen it first as a smudge on the horizon, a gray thread stitched upright against the morning, and she had thought—absurdly—of a quill. Of handwriting. Of the long vertical stroke her grandmother's pen made when it signed something final.

By afternoon it filled half the sky.

It was not stone, exactly, though it wore the gray of granite and had the cold patience of mountains. When Ulyana laid her palm against its base, she felt a faint pulse, like touching the throat of something sleeping. The surface gave slightly under her hand—not soft, but *willing*, the way bone is willing, the way all living structure is willing to bear weight if you ask it honestly.

*We are here.* The dead were louder now than they had ever been, no longer whispers through carved marrow but a tide against her ribs. *We have always been here. The guild only taught the living to look away.*

Tam stood beside her, one hand pressed to the bandage at his side where the tracker's knife had found him in the village. The wound had slowed him, but he hadn't complained once, and Ulyana had learned in three days of marshland that Tam's silences were not empty ones. They were full of his grandmother's lullabies that he could hum but never translate. Full of a grief he'd carried his whole life without knowing its name.

Now he knew it. She had given him that much, at least—the truth of what they mourned. She sometimes wondered if it had been a kindness.

"It's warm," Tam said. His voice came out strange. "The spire. Why is it warm?"

"Because something inside it is still burning." Ulyana shouldered her pack, feeling the map shift within the hollowed femur where it rode against her spine. "And something else is starving."

She began to climb.

* * *

There were no stairs.

That was the first thing Tam understood about the Spire: it offered no stairs, no ladder, no path cut by human hands, because no human hands had built it. Instead there were holds and ledges that appeared as he reached for them, as if the spire were listening for intention and answering it. He tested each one with his weight before trusting it, and each held, warm beneath his palms like the flank of some enormous resting animal.

His side screamed with every reach. He let it scream. Pain was simple. Pain was a thing his body understood without needing anyone to remember it into existence.

Ulyana climbed above him, sure-footed in a way that surprised him until he remembered what her hands did for a living. Of course she trusted surfaces. She had spent her life being trusted with them.

Halfway up, the wind changed.

It didn't blow anymore so much as it *spoke*—a pressure against his ears, a murmur just below the threshold of words. Tam pressed himself against the spire's warm flank and listened, and heard, unmistakably, singing. A woman's voice. A melody he knew.

"That's—" His throat closed. "That's my grandmother's song. The one I hum to the goats. She used to sing it when she braided my hair."

"The dead kept it," Ulyana called down, her face pale and shining with effort. "When the living forgot, the dead kept everything. Hold on to the rail to your left."

"There's no rail."

"There is now."

There was. It hadn't been there a heartbeat before—a lip of gray stone, smooth as a banister—and Tam gripped it and laughed, a ragged sound torn out of him by altitude and terror and something dangerously close to joy. The world could be remembered back into shape. He had spent thirty years thinking memory was only an ache. It was a material. It was *load-bearing.*

They climbed through cloud. The cloud was wrong—too thin to be weather, too bright, threaded with shapes that might have been faces if he didn't look at them directly. Voices moved in it the way fish move under ice.

"How far?" he asked.

"To the summit? Another hour." Ulyana paused on a ledge, breathing hard, and Tam saw that her eyes were wet. "But there's something before the summit. The dead keep showing me. A door."

"A door in the middle of a tower?"

"In the heart of it." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, leaving a smear of chalk-dust and sweat. "Tam, the fire at the top isn't just a fire. It's the last dragon-fire in the world, and it doesn't burn wood or oil. It burns belief. Every person who remembered Vessia fed it a little, even without knowing—that's why your grandmother's songs mattered, why your village stayed standing when the maps went blank. But the rememberers are dying out. The fire is down to an ember. And when it goes out—"

She stopped.

"When it goes out," Tam said slowly, "Vessia doesn't come back."

"Vessia stops waiting to come back." Ulyana's voice cracked on it. "Everything the dead have held for forty years. Every name. Every house. Gone past even forgetting. Erasure becomes permanent."

Above them, dimmed by distance and cloud, the light of the summit pulsed once—weak, guttering, red-gold as a coal breathed on too gently.

"Then we shouldn't stand here talking," Tam said.

* * *

The brazier at the top of the world was a bowl of black iron wide as a wagon wheel, and the flame inside it was small.

That was what undid Ulyana—not the height, not the wind keening off the empty country below, but the smallness. All her life she had imagined dragon-fire as the stuff of ballads: a furnace, a flood, a wall of gold. This was a candle-flame crouching in an iron bowl, trembling, barely taller than her hand. It cast shadows that flickered like something exhausted trying to stay awake.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was dying.

She knelt at the rim of the brazier—carefully; the iron hummed against her knuckles—and the warmth that came off it was not heat exactly but *recognition*. It looked at her. She felt herself seen all the way through, every silence she had ever kept laid open like a ledger.

"I know," she whispered to it. "I know what I owe."

The dead rose with her out of the stairwell of the air.

All the way up the spire they had gathered behind her and around her, patient as sediment, and now they came onto the summit platform in their hundreds—in their thousands—filling the windless ring of stone around the brazier with a crowd that made no sound and displaced no grit and yet bent the light coming off the flame, so that its glow broke over them like water over stones. Ulyana saw them clearly for the first time in full numbers: fishermen with salt still drying in their beards, women in embroidered vests gone gray with age, children holding hands in lines, an old man carrying a fiddle worn smooth at the neck. Vessians. All of them. Forty years of a nation, standing patiently at the top of its own memory.

*The door,* they said, and every voice was one voice. *See the door.*

It stood at the center of the platform, where no door should be: a tall rectangle of darkness set into nothing, framed in carved bone-white stone. Ulyana approached it the way she approached a fresh femur on her bench—with reverence, with the knowledge that what was inside it deserved gentleness.

The carvings along the frame were Vessian script. Her fingers found them and read without understanding how she read: *Only the whole truth opens this. Half-truths are locks. Silence is a lock. Speak everything, or speak nothing.*

"The whole truth," she said aloud, and the flame in the brazier flared—an inch, maybe two, a brief gold gasp—and settled again, lower than before.

Behind her, Tam sucked in a breath. "It heard you."

"It hears everything said truly near it." Ulyana ran her thumb along the doorframe's edge. Cold. Absolute. Patient as her grandmother's ledgers. "That's the trap, Tam. That's why the guild left it alone all these years instead of breaking it. You can't lie your way through. You can't bargain. And a partial truth—" she remembered Corvel's smooth voice, *return the map, take the seat, live rich and safe* —"a partial truth probably welds it shut forever."

"What counts as whole?"

"Everything." The word came out of her flat and heavy. "What was done to Vessia. Who did it. Why." She turned to look at him, and the dying firelight made her shadow enormous against the clouds below. "My grandmother wrote the decree. And I—I carried the name *Vessia* in my hands for weeks before I ever said it out loud. I heard a dead woman beg me twice and I told myself it wasn't my place. My whole life I've known things and kept quiet because quiet was safe. That's part of the truth too. If the door wants everything, then it gets my shame along with her crime."

Tam limped to the brazier and stood looking down at the ember of the last dragon-fire, his face gilded and haggard. For a long moment he said nothing, and the wind off the empty country stirred the hem of his coat.

"My grandmother forgot on purpose," he said finally. "After the exile. She told my mother once—it's in the family stories, we just never knew what it meant—that she *chose* to put it down, because remembering hurt too much and the guild's men promised forgetting would be painless." His jaw worked. "She thought she was protecting us. So if we're telling the whole truth, tell that too. Tell that the erasure worked because it was *merciful*, and mercy is how it got in the door."

Ulyana felt the dead press closer at that, a thousand breaths drawn at once, and the flame sank.

Lower.

Low enough that the iron bowl threw back more shadow than light. Low enough that Ulyana could see, with terrible clarity, the shape of what remained: an ember no bigger than a robin's egg, pulsing slow, each pulse fainter than the last.

*I'm running out of time,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because the door demanded precision. *We're running out of time. The fire is running out of us.*

She turned to the dark rectangle and put both hands flat against its cold frame, the way she had once, as a girl, put her hands against her grandmother's study door and listened to the scratch of the pen that was writing a country out of the world.

Soon, she told the door. Soon I'll have the words.

From below—far below, thin with distance but climbing, borne up the spire's warm flank like smoke up a chimney—came a sound that froze the blood in her wrists.

Footsteps on remembered stone. Unhurried. Familiar.

And then a voice, courteous as a ledger entry, rising out of the stairwell of the air:

"Don't start without me, Miss Okafor. I've come to hear the whole truth myself—and to offer you one last chance to tell a different story."

Master Corvel stepped onto the summit, and behind him, knife already drawn, the tracker smiled at Tam.

The ember in the brazier guttered.
