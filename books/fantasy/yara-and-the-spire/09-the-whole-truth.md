## 9. The Whole Truth

The ember at the summit of the Spire was the color of a dying man's last word.

Ulyana felt it before she saw it—a warmth against her face like the memory of warmth, thin as paper. The brazier stood at the heart of the spire's crown, black iron worked into the shape of coiled wings, and inside it the dragon-fire had shrunk to something smaller than a candle. Forty years of forgetting, and this was all that remained: a coal the size of a walnut, breathing.

*It burns on belief,* the dead woman had told her, over and over, through every bone Ulyana had touched since Grayharbor. *It starves on silence.*

The wind off the Vessian plain smelled of wild rye and rain that hadn't fallen yet. Below, the stair wound down through the spire's hollow body, and along that stair, faint as lamplight through fog, came footsteps. Heavy ones. Unhurried ones.

Corvel.

"He's here," Tam said. He had propped himself against the doorframe with his knife drawn, his left arm bound in strips of his own shirt where the tracker's blade had found him in the village. The blood had soaked through already. He looked pale as bone-meal, but his voice held. "How long do you need?"

"I don't know." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the door.

The door itself was nothing—no wood, no iron, no stone. It stood at the center of the summit chamber, a rectangle of absence in the air, tall enough for grief and wide enough for a nation. Its edges shimmered when she breathed near it, the way water shivers before ice. It would not open for keys. It would not open for force. The dead had shown her what it wanted, pressing their cold hands over hers until she understood:

It opened only for the whole truth. Every piece of it. Spoken aloud, by someone who owed it.

The footsteps reached the top of the stair.

"Go," Tam said. "Hold the door. I'll hold him."

"You can barely stand."

"I can stand long enough." He smiled, and it was a smuggler's smile, crooked and unbothered, the one he'd worn on the marsh road when the ground rose out of the water under their feet because she'd sung a fragment of a song half-remembered from her grandmother's humming. "My family forgot why we mourned, Ulyana. I'm not forgetting again. Not tonight."

She kissed his forehead, tasting salt and ash, and turned to face the door.

Behind her, boots on stone. Corvel's voice, warm and reasonable as ever, drifted across the chamber like smoke finding a crack under a door.

"Ulyana Okafor. You've made a long climb for nothing."

* * *

Corvel stepped into the light of the ember, and up close he looked exactly as he always had—the same gray coat, the same ledger-bound calm, the same patient eyes of a man who believed that everything in the world could be entered in a column and balanced. Only now she saw what forty years of looking away had kept her from seeing: there was no one behind those eyes. Just arithmetic.

"Master Corvel." She did not turn around. "You're late. The dead have been waiting decades."

"The dead," he said mildly, "are an inventory problem." She heard Tam move, heard the scrape of a knife leaving a sheath, heard Corvel's soft sigh of a man inconvenienced by weather. "Girl. Step aside. You're wounded; I don't want to worsen your evening."

"My evening's already ruined," Tam said, "you should see the other fellow," and then steel rang against steel, and Ulyana forced herself not to look.

The door waited. Cold air moved through it, or something like air, carrying a sound like ten thousand people inhaling at once.

She put her hands against its edges. They passed through nothing, and the nothing was cold as riverbed clay, and somewhere beneath her palms she felt a pulse—not a heartbeat, slower, vaster, like the tide deciding whether to come in.

And the fire behind her guttered lower.

*Speak,* said every bone she carried. The femur at her breastbone, the small carved knucklebones in her pockets, the map folded inside the half-finished piece she'd never deliver. *Speak, or we go out like a lamp.*

"I don't know how," she whispered.

*You are a carver,* said the dead woman whose name she finally knew—Mirela, Mirela Vess, who had died with 'Vessia' twice on her lips. *You have spent your whole life listening. Now you are the bone. Let yourself be read.*

Ulyana closed her eyes.

"Vessia," she said.

The ember flared, once, hopeful—and dimmed again. Behind her, Tam cried out; she heard him hit the wall, heard him rise anyway.

"Not enough," Corvel said, breathing hard now. He had a knife in Tam's shoulder, she realized without looking; she could hear it in the wet hitch of his breath. "A name is not a truth, girl. My grandmother's council knew a hundred ways to say a lie. Do you think the door can't tell?"

"It isn't finished," she said. "Neither am I."

She thought of the ledger of small debts. Of ten thousand bones carved in a cellar smelling of tallow and linseed oil, and every secret she'd swallowed like a stone, building herself a body made entirely of other people's truths while keeping none of her own. She thought of her rule—*never repeat what the dead say*—and understood, finally, what the rule had really been. Not respect. Cowardice dressed in ceremony. A vessel that refuses to pour is just a locked room.

"All right," she said softly. "All right. Everything."

* * *

"There was a country called Vessia," Ulyana said, and the words came out of her like water from a broken dam, too fast, too much, thirty years of held breath. "It lay between the Serevan marshes and the gray coast, and it had eleven rivers and one spire, and its people sang their roads into being because they believed the land listened. It grew rye that ripened blue in autumn. It kept no prisons, because its worst punishment was a song unsung—your name left out of the evening rounds, so that you walked through your own town a stranger."

The ember brightened. The door's edges trembled.

"And forty years ago," she went on, and her voice cracked on it, "the Guild of Impossible Things decided that Vessia was too dangerous to be allowed to exist. Because its impossible things were honest things. Because a country where memory shapes the world cannot keep secrets, and the guild's entire fortune is secrets. So they came here with a decree written by my grandmother."

Tam made a sound—not pain. Grief.

"Her name was Halyna Okafor," Ulyana said, saying it aloud for the first time in her life, feeling the name leave her like a tooth pulled. "She was born in this country, in a village called Zorya, and she watched her own mother sing the field paths straight after floods. And she betrayed it. She wrote the erasure decree in her own hand—I have seen the hand, I know every loop and ligature of it, it taught me mine. She wrote Vessia out of the atlases, out of the ledgers, out of the mouths of everyone who had ever spoken it, and she took payment in impossible things: a lantern that burns regret, a bell rung only at funerals that haven't happened yet, a seed of the first tree. She helped steal a country's soul and sell it by the pound."

The wind stopped. The whole summit held still, listening.

"And I—" Ulyana's throat closed. The dead crowded close, patient, terrible, waiting for the part she had never wanted to reach. "I knew. Not all of it. But I heard Mirela die whispering a name that appeared in no book, and instead of asking why, I hid the map in a bone and told myself I was only being careful. I walked through the guild's vault and saw her lantern and her bell tagged like dry goods, and I went back to my bench and kept carving. For weeks. I chose the guild's quiet over the dead's truth because the quiet was warm and the truth was cold, and I have been choosing it my whole life. Silence upon silence upon silence. I am the granddaughter of the woman who wrote you out, and I let you stay written out because speaking would have cost me everything I had."

Her voice broke completely. She let it.

"That's the whole truth," she whispered. "There's nothing left. Take it."

*Not yet,* said the dead, gentle as snowfall. *Name us.*

So she did.

"Mirela Vess, who died at Grayharbor with her country's name twice on her lips. Yanko Petrov, ferryman of the River Sereva, who drowned carrying children to the marshes when the forgetting came. The twins Danya and Sol Koval, who shared one grave because neither would let go of the other's hand. Old Baba Ruta of Zorya, who remembered longest and was loneliest for it. Stefan the shepherd, Ilse the dyer, Marek who could whistle thunder out of a clear sky, Anna who mended nets and quarrels, Tomas, Petra, little Lilia who was four—" Her voice rose, cracked, kept going, name after name after name, the ones the bones had given her and the ones the map had shown her and the ones that simply arrived now, welling up out of her like blood from under a bandage, hundreds of them, thousands, the door drinking each one and trembling harder, the ember roaring up behind her until the summit chamber glowed gold as harvest—

"—and Halyna Okafor," Ulyana finished, "who was yours, and betrayed you, and whose debt I pay."

The door opened.

* * *

It did not swing. It simply ceased refusing, the way fog ceases at sunrise, and through it poured a light that was not light but remembering—the smell of blue rye, the sound of eleven rivers, the warmth of ten thousand voices singing names back into the evening rounds. The dragon-fire leapt in its brazier, one final flare, magnificent, vast, wings of flame unfurling up the spire's crown like the country drawing one enormous breath—and then, with a sound like a sigh at the end of a very long day, it went out.

Forever. And that was all right. It had never been meant to burn forever. It had been meant to burn until someone came home.

The dead went through. Ulyana felt them pass—through her, past her, around her like leaves in a wind that smelled of rye bread and woodsmoke—each one brushing her cheek in farewell, each one lighter than sorrow and heavier than gratitude. Mirela last of all, pausing at the threshold.

*Thank you, carver,* she said. *You kept us safe in bone until bone was no longer needed.*

Then she was gone, and the silence in Ulyana's chest where the voices had lived was the emptiest, fullest silence she had ever known.

Behind her, a knife clattered on stone. She turned.

Tam sat slumped against the wall, white as milk, his hand pressed to his shoulder—and Corvel stood over him, unarmed, unmoving, staring at the open door with his ledgers' logic crumbling visibly behind his eyes. Because the hoard was gone. She could feel it: every stolen thing in every vault from Grayharbor to the capital lifting, drifting, flying home through the door like iron filings to a magnet—the lantern of regret going back to the shrine that mourned it, the bell, the seed, forty years of theft unwinding in a single golden hour.

"The reckoning," Ulyana said quietly, "is written in your own books, Master Corvel. You always told me every debt gets collected."

He looked at her then, and for the first time since she'd known him, he had nothing to say.

Through the open door, beyond the fading light, she could see it: a country under morning, roads lying straight and true across wild rye fields, and far off, small figures coming out of houses—living figures, blinking, remembering—walking toward the spire.

Tam groaned. "Tell me," he managed, "one of those houses has a doctor."

Ulyana laughed—a wet, cracked, astonishing sound, her first true laugh in years—and took his good arm over her shoulder.

"Hold on," she said. "We're going home. Both of us."
