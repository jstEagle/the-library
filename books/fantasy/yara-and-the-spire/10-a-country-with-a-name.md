## 10. A Country With a Name

**Ulyana**

The dead did not leave all at once.

They went the way a fog lifts off the marsh at morning—thinning first at the edges, holding longest in the hollows—and for three days after the dragon-fire died, Ulyana woke in the gray light to find them still gathered at the foot of the Spire like pilgrims who had forgotten they were free to go. Some wept. Some laughed, that startled bark of people remembering laughter. Most simply walked, hand in hand with no one, out along the road that now existed because enough of them remembered walking it.

And one by one, they said their names to her as they passed, so she would not have to carry them anymore.

*Mira Osten-Vahl, dyer of wool.* *Sef Andric, who never learned to swim and drowned anyway, twice.* *Yelena, who wrote you out, and is sorry, and is gone.*

Her grandmother had been the last to leave. She had stood before Ulyana at the spire door with her hands folded the way she'd folded them over every ledger she'd ever balanced, and she had not asked forgiveness, which Ulyana understood, finally, as its own kind of gift. She had only said: *The ink was mine. The choice was mine. What you do with the truth of me is yours.* Then she had walked into the country whose name she helped erase, and the grass had closed behind her, and Ulyana had felt the weight of forty years of stolen silence lift off her chest like a hand withdrawing.

She had wept then. She was not ashamed of it. Grief, it turned out, could be clean when you knew what it was for.

Now she sat on the steps of a Vessian inn—the *Sable Ewe*, according to the sign, which had faded back into legibility overnight, letters surfacing through the paint like drowned things rising—carving a bone in the morning sun.

It was a deer bone. Tam had found it in the forest, shed and weathered and utterly ordinary, and had brought it to her with the careful expression of someone offering a gift they weren't sure would be welcome. She had held it all night by the fire, listening.

Nothing.

No whisper. No last secret pressed into the grain. No dying woman's name spoken twice. Just bone—its plain animal history of marrow and season, its dumb and honest matter. It was the quietest thing she had ever held, and her hands shook, and she could not stop smiling.

"You're doing it wrong," Tam said, dropping onto the step beside her. "You're supposed to look tortured. All my life I've watched carvers work, and every single one of them looked like they were being punished by the material."

"I'm not working for the Guild of Impossible Things anymore."

"No," Tam agreed. "You're working for an innkeeper who's paying you in stew." They leaned over to squint at the emerging pattern—her grandmother's knotwork, but opened up, unwound from its closed loops into something that kept going past the edge of the bone, unfinished on purpose. "What is it?"

"A ledger," Ulyana said.

Tam looked at her.

"Debts," she said, "and how they're paid."

* * *

**Tam**

Tam had spent thirty years mourning without knowing what for, and the strangest part of having it back—not just the country but the *why*, the shape of the wound—was how small the grief turned out to be once it had somewhere to live. Their mother had sung half a lullaby all their childhood, trailing off at the same line every time, and now Tam knew the rest of it, and sang it, badly, while they walked the boundary of what had been their family's field and was again. The wheat had gone wild in the forgetting—thinned, tangled with vetch—but under it the soil remembered, and the first thing they planted was the old grain, the blue-tinged Vessian barley that no seed catalog in the world had listed for four decades because no world had contained the place it came from.

The seed had come from the guild's vaults.

That was the part Tam still couldn't sit with quietly, some mornings. The hoard hadn't burned when the fire died. It had *scattered*—that was the only word anyone had for it. Ulyana said the impossible things wanted to go home, that erasure was a kind of theft and theft left a debt, and when the flame that burned on belief went out, every debt in the guild's keeping had been called in at once. The ledgers in Grayharbor had emptied themselves, ink crawling off the pages like insects. Objects had vanished from their cases mid-inventory, witnesses swore to it, and in the weeks that followed, reports drifted in from everywhere: a mirror returned to a village that had never been mapped; a singing stone back in a river no chart named; seeds arriving on doorsteps in paper packets with handwriting on them that made people cry without knowing why.

*Vessia* flickered onto maps the same way. Cartographers woke screaming about ink that moved against their hands. Atlases reprinted themselves in locked shops. Tam bought one in a market town two weeks after the fire—a proper atlas, expensive—and there it was between Corvane and the Sea of Glass: *Vessia*, in small confident type, with the Sable Ewe marked and the Spire marked and their own village named, *Ostrevell*, which they had repeated aloud forty times on the walk home like a prayer answering itself.

Master Corvel did not scatter. That was the other thing everyone talked about, in the low voice reserved for disasters one has survived.

He had come to the Spire expecting to bargain and found the door already open and the fire already dead, and he had stood in the great cold room with his ledger under his arm like a shield, and the ledger had opened itself, and every entry he had ever written in his own hand had risen off the page and wound around him—Ulyana described it later, having seen it through the closing door, though Tam noticed she never described it without stopping once to breathe. *Bound by his own accounts,* she said. The guild's oldest law, the one carved over the Bone-Door in Grayharbor: *what is entered must be answered for*. Corvel had written himself into existence as keeper of the hoard, arbiter of what was remembered, and when there was nothing left to keep, the law required him to account for himself.

They said the guild council convened in full for the first time in sixty years to hear those accounts read aloud—all of them, every erased place, every stolen miracle—and that when it was done, the enforcers had escorted him down into the vaults that were no longer vaults, just empty rooms, and sealed him in with nothing but blank paper and a pen, until every debt was written out in full.

Tam didn't wish him cruelty. But they slept better knowing he was spending his days writing the names of places he'd helped unname, over and over, until his hand learned them by heart.

* * *

**Ulyana**

The new ledger arrived in autumn, by courier—a girl of maybe sixteen, breathless, with a packet wrapped in oilcloth and a letter bearing a seal Ulyana didn't recognize: a spire and an open door.

*To the Carver of Ostrevell,* the letter began, *and any others who hold the memory of restored places:*

*The Guild of Impossible Things has voted, narrowly and after much shouting, to remake itself. We can no longer be keepers of what exists, since we have proven poor stewards of memory. We propose instead to become recorders of restoration—collectors not of objects but of accounts: how things were lost, how they were returned, what it cost. We cannot undo what we did. We can only enter it honestly.*

*We ask you, as the one who ended us, to help begin us again. Enclosed find a ledger, blank. The first entry is yours by right.*

*Respectfully, uneasily,*
*The Provisional Council*

Ulyana unwrapped the ledger and sat with it a long time. It was well-made—of course it was, the guild had always been meticulous—and the paper smelled of nothing, held nothing, waited.

She thought of her grandmother's ledgers in that elegant slanting hand, columns of small debts, each line a place going dark. She thought of her own rule, kept for twenty years: *never repeat what the dead say, never put your name on anything.* She had believed invisibility was safety. It had only been a smaller kind of erasure, self-administered.

She took out her carving knife, and on the first page, in a hand that was neither her grandmother's nor the careful null script of the guild, she wrote:

*Entry the first. Vessia, erased forty years by this guild with the labor of Yelena Okafor, my grandmother, and preserved against all their work by the dead themselves, who never forgot. Returned to the world by the death of a fire and the truth of a coward's granddaughter. Cost: everything I was. Worth:* 

She paused. Outside, Tam was teaching the neighbor's children the lullaby, getting it wrong, laughing at themselves. The barley was coming up blue-green in rows. Somewhere beyond the hills, couriers were carrying maps to cities where people would look at a shape they'd never seen and feel, unaccountably, homesick for it.

*Worth more than I know how to enter,* she finished, and signed it: *Ulyana Okafor, carver, granddaughter, citizen of Vessia.*

Then she picked up the deer bone—finished now, the knotwork running off its edge into nothing, open-ended, alive—and hung it above the inn door where travelers would pass beneath it, and she listened one more time to its beautiful, ordinary silence.

That night, a stranger knocked. A woman, mud to the knees, eyes red-rimmed, holding a scrap of parchment like it might bite. "They say you speak for the dead," she said. "There's another country. Smaller than this one. Nobody's ever heard of it—not even the new maps. But my mother used to hum its songs, and last week, when the guild's hoard scattered—" she held out the parchment, and on it, in ink that still seemed to be deciding whether to exist, was a name Ulyana had never read and somehow already knew.

"Come in," Ulyana said, and reached for her ledger.
