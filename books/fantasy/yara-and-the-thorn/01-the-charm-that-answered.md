## 1. The Charm That Answered

The bell above the shop door had a crack in it, so when Maren Oostvogel came in, she announced herself twice: once in tin, and once in grief.

"Lena, love," said the fishwife, and that was all she managed for a while. She stood in the doorway of the little bone-carver's shop on Saltpanner's Row with her shawl still dripping river fog and her hands wrapped around each other like they'd forgotten what they were for.

Yara—Lena, she corrected herself, twelve years of practice making the correction almost painless—set down her knife and the half-finished button of ox rib she'd been working. "Sit you down, Maren. I'll put the kettle on."

"I don't want tea." Maren sat anyway, because her knees had decided the matter without consulting her. "I want my Pieter back."

The words landed in the room the way a dropped plate lands: loud, then terribly quiet. Yara kept her hands busy, sweeping bone dust into a jar, because idle hands in Vell invited questions, and questions were the one commodity more dangerous than magic these days.

"He was a good man," Yara said. It wasn't much. Nothing ever was.

"Three weeks drowned, and they only fished him up Tuesday." Maren's voice had gone flat, the voice of someone who has cried herself empty and found there was more underneath after all. "The shrine priests won't say rites anymore—you know that, everyone knows that, the Inquisition shut the last of them down in spring. There's nobody to pray for him. Nobody to *ask*." Her red eyes came up, and Yara saw the shape of the request forming before it was spoken, the way you see weather building over water. "But you carve charms, don't you? For mourning. Everyone says your charms are... kind. That holding one is like being held."

*Everyone says.* Yara felt the familiar cold slide along her spine. What everyone said about Lena Reyes's charms was carefully managed rumor—the sort she'd spent a decade cultivating. Harmless. Sentimental. A woman who carved pretty bones and told sad widows comforting lies.

"That's all they are," Yara said gently. "Comfort. Bone remembers nothing, Maren. But it can hold a shape, and a shape can hold a thought, and a thought can hold you upright on the worst morning of your life. That's not magic. That's just craft, and craft is legal."

"Then make me one." Maren's jaw set with the stubbornness of a woman who hauled nets before dawn. "I don't care if it's craft or kindness or nothing at all. Make me something to hold."

So Yara did what she always did. She took a piece of whale rib from the oldest crate—old bone, dense and pale as winter milk—and she set Maren talking about Pieter while she worked. This was the real trick of it, the thing no warden would ever think to outlaw: she listened. She listened hard, and she let the listening move her hands.

Pieter, it turned out, had whistled while he mended nets, badly, constantly, songs that didn't exist. He'd called gulls by name, invented names, insulted them cheerfully. He'd brought Maren river pearls he found in fish stomachs and told her each one was worth a fortune, and they both knew they weren't, and he did it anyway, every market day for thirty years.

Yara's knife moved. A curl of whistle-notes along one edge. Two small gulls, beak to beak, where the charm would rest against the thumb. A pearl, left unpolished, rough as the day it came out of a fish's belly. The old carvers of her childhood would have called this work *listening to the bone*, and they would have meant something by it that Yara could never, ever let anyone in Vell suspect she understood.

"There," she said, an hour later, blowing the last dust from the grooves. "For holding. Not for anything else."

Maren paid in coins that smelled of brine and pressed the charm to her lips like a kiss, and Yara watched her go out into the gray street standing straighter, and thought—as she thought most days—that this was enough. This had to be enough. A false name, a warm shop, small mercies carved in bone. Twelve years since the purge had burned the god-tenders' villages and scattered their daughters; twelve years since a girl named anything but Lena had buried her true self under a city's noise. The gods were gone. The magic was gone. Only the comfort remained, and comfort hurt no one.

She should have known better. She had been raised by people whose whole faith was built on the truth that nothing holy ever really dies. It only sleeps.

* * *

That night the rain came in off the harbor, and Yara sat up late in the workroom above Bram's forge, finishing commissions by lamplight. Below her floorboards, the tinker's snores rattled the kettle hooks in time—a man who could sleep through a siege and once reportedly had. Odile's candle had gone out hours ago; the runaway scribe kept scholar's hours when she could and sinner's hours when she couldn't.

Yara was carving a birth-charm for the cooper's wife when she heard it.

At first she took it for the rain. Rain on this roof made a hundred sounds—hiss, tick, the gargle of the misaligned gutter that Bram swore he'd fix since before two winters past. But underneath the rain there was a rhythm that rain doesn't have. Words have rhythm.

"—cold, it's the cold that gets in, tell her the cold isn't punishment, it's only the cold—"

Yara's knife stopped.

The sound was coming from the crate of whale rib. From the offcuts. From the piece she'd been working.

She sat very still. Downstairs, Bram snored on, blessedly oblivious. The lamp guttered. And from the pale curve of bone under her hands, thin as a whisper through a wall, the voice came again, and this time she recognized the cadence if not the words—an old man's mutter, patient and foggy, the way the very old talk about weather and grievances as if they were the same subject.

"No," Yara said aloud. Her voice sounded absurd in the empty room. "No, you're dead. All of you are dead. It's been twelve years. I've *listened*. There's been nothing to hear for twelve years."

Because that was the other thing, the secret under the secret: Lena Reyes of Saltpanner's Row was not entirely a fiction. The girl she had been before could hear the dead—not speak with them, not summon them, just *hear*, the way some people hear a note too high for other ears. The god-tenders had called it the low gift, the humble gift, the gift fit for gravediggers and midwives. After the purge, Yara had fled to Vell half-deaf with grief, and the silence that met her there had seemed like mercy. She had built a life in it. She had told herself the quiet meant she was safe.

Now the quiet was leaking.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed distantly, as if reporting on someone else's hands. She picked up the whale-rib offcut—the one with the half-carved birth-spiral, harmless, ordinary, bought legally from a rendering yard—and held it to her ear like a shell.

"—tell the carver, she'll know, the daughter of Merewen will know, the tide's turning and the gates are thin and *someone has to carry word*—"

Yara dropped it. Bone clattered across the table and rolled off onto the floorboards, spinning, and she stood up so fast her chair went over backward, and for one long moment she just breathed, hand pressed to her mouth, staring at the little spiral of carved bone lying in the lamplight like the most ordinary thing in the world.

*The daughter of Merewen.*

Her mother's name. A name she had not heard spoken aloud in twelve years, not by anyone living, because no one alive knew it. It rose in her now with its whole weight attached—the smell of the shrine-house, wet stone and tallow smoke; the sound of her mother humming over the washing basin; the fire, later, seen from a hillside, too far away to be believed.

"It's waking," she whispered to the empty room, and the truth of it settled into her bones worse than any cold. "It's not gone. It's waking up."

She thought of Maren Oostvogel walking home through the rain with a mourning charm against her lips. Thought of every charm she'd sold these twelve years—hundreds of them, thousands, comfort and craft and nothing else, she'd sworn it. Had they all been nothing else? Or had she been scattering seeds of a forbidden thing across the whole of Vell, seed by seed by seed, without knowing the ground had thawed?

Somewhere out in the rain, a bell began to ring. Not the cracked bell of her own door—something farther off, deeper, the great bronze tongue of the Cathedral of the Covenant tolling the hour. Except it was not the hour. It was half past none o'clock, the dead middle of nothing, and the cathedral bells did not ring at night unless wardens were mustering, and wardens mustered at night for exactly one reason.

Yara righted her chair. She picked up the offcut of whale rib, turned it over twice, and did not hold it to her ear again, though every nerve in her wanted to.

Instead she pinched out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening—to the rain, to Bram's snoring, to the fading bell, and beneath it all, faint as breath in an empty house, the long slow sound of something vast beginning, after twelve silent years, to stir.

Tomorrow, she would have to find out what the dead wanted her to carry.

Tonight, all she could do was stand in the dark and be afraid.
