## 2. Wardens at the Door

The knock came on the third morning after the charm answered, which Yara considered a personal failing of the universe's sense of timing.

She was elbow-deep in a bowl of tallow when it came — three raps, measured, unhurried, the knock of someone who had never once been kept waiting by accident. She knew every kind of knock Vell had to offer. Customers knocked twice and cheerful. Debt collectors knocked four times and apologetic, as though the apology were legally required. Bram knocked with his elbow because his hands were always full.

This knock belonged to a person who expected the door to open itself.

"Yara." Odile appeared in the stairwell, ink-stained fingers curled around the banister, her braid escaping its ribbon in six directions. "There are men in grey at the door. Grey *cloaks*. And the woman in front has a pin shaped like an eye."

Yara's stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn, like a fish deciding whether to die.

"The eye of the Concord," she said, keeping her voice light. "How flattering. Nobody's sent the Inquisition for me before. I always assumed I wasn't important enough."

"You carve bones."

"I carve *charms*. There's a difference, and it's the difference between me being here and me not being here."

Odile looked at her for a long moment — that look she had, the one that made Yara feel like a manuscript with a page torn out. Odile had been a scribe in the Chantry archives before she'd run from them, and she read people the way she read everything: closely, hungrily, noting the margins.

"Well," Odile said at last. "Bram's answering the door. He's doing the thing with his shoulders."

"What thing?"

"The thing where he becomes enormous and harmless at the same time. It's quite remarkable. Like a barn that loves you."

* * *

Yara came down the stairs slowly, drying her hands on her apron, arranging her face into the pleasant blankness of Lena Reyes, bone-carver, unremarkable citizen, woman whose greatest crime was overcharging for luck-knots.

Inquisitor Sarel Voss stood in the middle of the workshop like a blade laid across a table.

She was smaller than Yara had imagined — Inquisitors were supposed to be imagined large — with iron-grey hair cropped short and a face that might once have been kind and had since been repurposed. Her grey cloak bore no dust despite the road. Behind her waited two wardens, helmets under their arms, wearing the expressions of men who would rather be anywhere, including a fire.

"Mistress Reyes." Voss's voice was dry paper. "Forgive the intrusion."

"No intrusion," boomed Bram, who was doing the shoulders thing magnificently. He filled half the room without moving. His beard alone could have sheltered a family of sparrows. "Always happy to receive the law. Tea? We've got tea. It's not good tea, but there's plenty of it."

"That won't be necessary." Voss produced a slate and chalk with the smoothness of long habit. "I'm conducting inquiries on behalf of the Concord. There have been... irregularities. In this quarter particularly. Reports of prayers answered."

"Prayers," said Yara.

"Answered ones." Voss's eyes were the color of shallow water over stone. "A fishwife on Salt Row claims her drowned husband spoke to her through a charm. Gave her a message. Knew things only he could know." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "You sell charms, I believe."

"I sell *carvings*," Yara said. "Little bones and knots and bits of pretty work. People hang them up and hope, same as they've always done. Hoping isn't outlawed. Yet."

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth. It might have been approval. It might have been the memory of a smile, filed away somewhere in the archives of her face.

"And do your carvings speak?"

"Only mine do," said Bram warmly. "She's got a wicked tongue on her. Talks all day. You'd think the house had two mistresses and both of them her."

One of the wardens made a sound that was possibly a swallowed laugh. Voss did not look at him, but the temperature of the room dropped several degrees anyway, and he became very interested in the ceiling.

Yara laughed too — a real laugh, God forgive her, bubbling up out of genuine nerves — and hated herself for how easy it felt. That was the poison of it. Bram and Odile were lying for her without knowing they were lying. They thought they were protecting a neighbor. They thought this was nothing, a bit of official fuss, a gray afternoon. They didn't know that every joke Bram cracked was a brick he was laying into a wall between the Inquisition and a woman who didn't exist.

Twelve years, and she'd never told them. Twelve years of Sunday dinners and mended boots and Odile reading aloud while Yara carved, and they knew everything about her except the single fact that would get them all hanged.

*You'd risk the rope for a stranger,* she thought, watching Bram beam at the Inquisitor like a lighthouse pretending to be a shed, *and you don't even know you're doing it.*

"The fishwife's charm," Voss said. "Bone, I'm told. Carved with a mourning knot." She turned her shallow-water gaze back to Yara. "That's rather your signature style, Mistress Reyes."

"It's everyone's style," said Odile, appearing from the stairwell with a ledger hugged to her chest like a shield. "Mourning knots are in the old pattern books — I copied them myself, before—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "They're common. Anyone could have carved it."

"*Anyone,*" Voss repeated softly.

"Half the city carves them," Odile went on, chin lifting, and there it was — the runaway scribe standing up to the Inquisition with nothing but a ledger and pure spite, bless her reckless heart. "There's a man on Cooper's Lane does them. Two women near the tannery. If you're going to arrest everyone with a knife and a sheep's knucklebone, Inquisitor, you'll need a bigger prison."

Silence. The warden studied the ceiling harder, as though it owed him money.

Voss wrote something on her slate. Then she looked at Yara again, and held the look one beat longer than courtesy required — long enough for Yara to feel it like a fingertip pressed to a bruise.

"I'll want a list," Voss said. "Of everyone who bought a mourning knot from this shop in the past season."

"I keep records," Odile said instantly.

"Odile," Yara said, mild as milk, "the records burned. Remember? When the kettle—"

"The kettle incident," Odile agreed, with the flawless timing of a woman who had spent years forging documents and could improvise a catastrophe without blinking. "Terrible shame. Paper and water, you know."

"Kettle incidents," murmured Voss, writing again. "How often these little households suffer them."

* * *

They left eventually — but only after Voss had walked the workshop twice, touching nothing, missing nothing. She paused at Yara's workbench. She looked at the half-carved luck-knot, at the knives, at the bowl of tallow, at the small brass scale Yara used for weighing charms against coin.

At the shelf where the finished pieces hung, each on its cord, swaying faintly in the draft.

Yara watched her look. Watched her stop, very slightly, at the fourth hook from the left — empty now, emptied three days ago, sold to a fishwife with salt-cracked hands and a grief too big for her chest.

Voss said nothing about it. She turned, thanked them for their time in a voice like a receipt, and left. The wardens followed. The door closed.

Nobody moved for a full ten seconds.

Then Bram let out a breath that rattled the windowpanes and sat down on his anvil-stool, which groaned in protest. "Well," he said. "I've been nervous around tax men, drunk men, and my late mother, but that woman makes my spine feel like a wet rope."

"She knows," Odile said quietly.

"She suspects," said Yara.

"There's a difference?"

"A very wide one, usually. Not always." Yara untied her apron with hands that wanted to shake and refused to allow it. "She'll go talk to the other carvers. She'll come back. People like her always come back."

"Then we'd best make sure there's nothing to find," Bram said, practical as ever, already reaching for the poker. "I'll bank the forge. Odile, hide anything with writing on it that shouldn't exist — and I mean *anything*, love, including those letters from your cousin, which are frankly seditious even by family standards."

"They're *affectionate* and *seditious*, thank you."

Yara stood in the middle of her warm, absurd, conspiring household and felt guilt settle over her like a coat made of lead.

They didn't ask why the Inquisitor had come. That was the thing. They didn't ask *why her, why this shop, why the questions landed here*. Bram banked the fire; Odile buried her papers; and between them they simply, wordlessly, assumed the trouble was theirs to share, because that was what this house did with trouble. It had never occurred to either of them that the trouble might be a person. Might be *her*.

"Yara?" Bram paused at the hearth, firelight catching the grey in his beard. "Whatever it is — if there's something you're carrying that's heavier than it looks — you know the rule in this house."

"Everyone carries," Yara said. "Nobody asks the weight."

"Nobody asks the weight," he agreed, "but everybody helps lift."

He went back to his coals. Odile muttered over her hidden ledgers. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor, and somewhere down Salt Row a fishwife was telling anyone who'd listen what her dead husband had said through a piece of sheep's bone — and somewhere beyond that, walking away down the cobbles without hurry, a woman in an undusted grey cloak carried a slate with four hooks' worth of conclusions chalked upon it, waiting only for the last one.

Yara sat down at her bench. She picked up her finest knife. And she looked, for a long moment, at the river-road door, and the drowned shrine it led to, and the voices in the bone that had started all of this — one of which, three nights ago, in the space between sleep and waking, had called her by a name she hadn't heard in twelve years.

Not *Lena*.

Her mother's voice, patient as tide, saying: *Yara. They are coming. Come to us first.*
