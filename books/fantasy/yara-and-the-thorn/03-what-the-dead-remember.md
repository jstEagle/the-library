## 3. What the Dead Remember

The bone would not stop whispering.

Yara had put it in the drawer beneath her workbench—wrapped in wool, weighed down with two iron washers, tucked behind the good carving knives—and still it murmured, soft as a moth against glass, all through the long night while she lay upstairs pretending sleep. By dawn she had named the sound nonsense. Bone settling. The house breathing. Her own mind, worn thin by three days of dread, inventing voices the way a lonely dog invents footsteps on the stairs.

By noon she had stopped lying to herself. She was, after all, a professional.

She opened the drawer and the whisper swelled like a held breath released. *Come,* it said. Not in words, exactly—the dead rarely bothered with words—but in the shape of a feeling, cold and directional, the way you can feel which window is open in winter without looking.

"You," Yara told the charm, "were supposed to be decorative."

The fishwife's mourning piece sat in her palm, small and pale as a knuckle, carved with the wave-pattern she'd done ten thousand times. Harmless. She had made sure it was harmless. She had sanded away every edge where meaning could catch, left no grooves for a soul to grip, and still old Maren's husband had spoken through it at the market stall three days ago, clear as church bells: *Tell my girl the blue coat fits her now.*

Twelve years of careful silence, and one charm had cracked it like river ice.

Yara wrapped the bone back in its wool, pulled her hood up, and did the single most stupid thing available to a woman whose continued survival depended on being forgettable.

She followed the voice.

* * *

It led her down. That was the first strange thing. Voices of the dead usually drifted up—chimney-smoke logic, her mother had always said, souls rise and so does their chatter. This one sank, threading downhill through Vell's oldest streets, past the tanneries where the smell could strip paint, down the switchback road to the harbor, and then past the harbor entirely, along the black shingle where nobody built because the sea took anything it was offered and said thank you by taking more.

The tide was out. It had gone out further than out, actually—it had simply *left*, the way a guest leaves a party early, without excuses. The seabed lay exposed for half a mile, glistening, littered with things the water had kept secret for generations: an anchor chain grown into coral, a boot, a bell the size of a cooking pot furred green with weed. And there, in a hollow of rock that must have been dry land when Vell was young, stood a shrine.

Drowned shrines were supposed to be a story. Odile had a whole lecture about them—the god-tenders' way-stations, little chapels raised at crossroads and shorelines, each one a resting spot on the old pilgrim roads. The Inquisition had spent two centuries breaking their stones and salting their foundations. Yara herself remembered only firelight and running, and she had worked very hard for twelve years at not remembering more.

This one had survived by drowning. The sea had covered it, and the Inquisition, whatever else could be said of them, did not swim.

Yara picked her way down the shingle, boots sinking in wet sand, telling herself the entire time that this was reconnaissance, prudence, due diligence—a carver needed to know what was waking before it woke all the way. The lie lasted until she reached the shrine's threshold and felt the air change, thick and sweet, like stepping into a room where incense had burned for a hundred years.

The shrine was small enough to hold six people standing. Its roof had half-fallen; barnacles crusted the lintel; a statue in the alcove wore so much weed it looked like a green ghost of itself. But the basin at its center—carved from a single shell-pink stone, big as a washing tub—still held seawater, and the seawater was glowing.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. Just faintly luminous, the way moonlight is luminous, and rising from the basin came the murmur she'd been following, multiplied now into a chorus, dozens of voices overlapping like wind through reeds.

*Oh no,* Yara thought. *No, no. I'm retired.*

"Hello," said a voice that was not dead.

Yara spun, hand going to the little knife in her sleeve—the carving knife, harmless, she told wardens it was harmless too—and found a girl sitting on the shrine's broken step. Or what looked like a girl. She had the general outline of one: young, dark-haired, barefoot. But her skin had the same faint shine as the water in the basin, and where her eyes should have been there was the flat silver light of the horizon at dusk.

"You're not dead," Yara said, which was not the opening line of any of the conversations she'd rehearsed in her life, but then none of those rehearsals had involved this.

"And you're not Lena Reyes." The girl smiled. It was a kind smile, which somehow made it worse. "You're the bone-speaker. We've been calling you for three days."

"We?"

"The dead." The girl gestured at the basin, at the chorus of murmurs, at the drowned world beyond it. "And me, I suppose, though I'm not dead either. Exactly. I'm the Ferryman's daughter. You may have heard of me." A pause. "You may not have. Father handled the rivers; I handled the crossings people make on foot. Small work. Forgs and footbridges. There used to be a shrine to me in every marsh town between here and the mountains."

Yara's mouth was very dry. "There's a chapel to you in Lowmarsh. My—" Her voice caught. "Someone painted your sign over its door when I was small. Two reeds crossed."

The girl's face lit, genuinely, childishly lit, and for a second she looked less like a god's daughter and more like someone's kid hearing their name read off a school list. "It's still there? Is the paint fresh?"

"It was old when I was six."

"Oh." The light dimmed a little. "Well. Old paint is better than none."

Yara crouched slowly, keeping her knees soft, the way you might near a strange dog—or near a stranger who thought you were a dog. "Why am I here? Truly. Because if this is about Maren's charm, I can explain—"

"It isn't about your charm. Your charm is a keyhole; I'm just using it to talk through." The Ferryman's daughter drew her knees up, and the water in the basin stirred, though there was no wind. "Bone-speaker, the covenant is failing."

"I don't know what that means."

"No. Why would you? They burned the teaching." The girl's silver eyes fixed on her, and Yara felt the look go through her like a hook through cloth. "Long ago, before your grandmother's grandmother, the gods and mortals made a bargain. Our realm would touch yours—gently, at the shrines, through prayers and small favors—and in exchange we would keep our hunger leashed. The covenant binds us to behave. It has been fraying for a century. Now it is tearing. Can't you hear it?" She tilted her head. "The prayers are getting through again. All of them. From everyone. Do you understand what that means? Every desperate wish in Vell, answered by whatever god happens to be nearest, with whatever power happens to be loose. No leash. No manners. No mercy trained into us."

The chorus in the basin rose, and this time Yara heard it properly—not a comforting murmur but a crowd, a market crowd, a crowd at a fire—

*—tell her the blue coat—*

*—who will tend the orchard now—*

*—it hurts, why does it still hurt—*

*—lena—*

Yara went still as a snared bird.

It had been twenty years since she'd heard that voice say that name. It had been twenty years since anyone alive could say it. It came from deep in the chorus, worn soft, the way a stair is worn by feet: *Lena*—no, not Lena, the other word under it, the true one buried beneath, and it rose toward her like a bubble through dark water, and the girl watched her with terrible gentleness and said nothing, and then the bubble broke the surface—

"—run *south*, little thorn, when it comes, run south—"

And then it was gone, swallowed back into the crowd, and Yara was on her hands and knees on wet stone without any memory of falling, and her face was wet, and it wasn't seawater.

"That one has been waiting longest," the Ferryman's daughter said quietly. "She gave up her place in the queue so she could stay near the surface. Stubborn woman. I let her."

"My mother died in the purge." Yara's voice came out scraped raw, barely hers. "I was six. Nobody—there was nobody left to tell me anything."

"There is somebody trying very hard to tell you now." The girl leaned forward. Up close her shine was unbearable, like staring at the sun through fog, and yet Yara couldn't look away. "So here is my errand, bone-speaker, and I'm sorry—I have been sorry for three days—that it falls to you. Word must reach the Thorn. Someone must learn why the covenant fails and carry that knowing to the axis-stone before the tearing finishes. The other gods are waking confused and hungry; they cannot carry it themselves. The mortal priests are ash. Only a speaker can hold a message this heavy. Only bone remembers."

"I'm not a speaker. I'm a *charlatan*. I sell lucky horses and safe voyages to grandmothers. I haven't done true work since I was—" She stopped. Since she was six. Since the night everything true about her had been folded up small and hidden in a false name and carried out of a burning village.

"You've done true work every day for twelve years," the girl said. "You just called it something safer."

The tide was turning. Yara could hear it far off, the sea remembering itself, coming back to reclaim its shrine and its god and its secrets. She should run. She should go home, melt the mourning charms, burn the wool, move three cities east and invent a third name.

Instead she heard herself ask, "What do you mean, *why* it fails? Covenants fail because they're neglected. Because people stopped believing."

"They stopped believing," the girl agreed. "But the covenant doesn't run on belief. It runs on a promise, sworn at the Thorn by someone who belonged to both realms. And that promise, bone-speaker, was broken on purpose. By someone who wanted the door between worlds open." Her silver eyes were very grave. "We do not know who. The dead remember pieces. That is why you must come here, and listen, and gather what the drowned know—and then decide, with open eyes, whether you'll carry it."

"Decide? I thought I'd already been chosen."

"You haven't been chosen. You've been *called*. Choosing comes after." The girl almost laughed. "Father always said mortals confuse the two. Being summoned is cheap. Saying yes is the expensive part."

Water slid across the shingle outside, cool and reaching, licking at the shrine's threshold like a cat testing a doorway. The glow in the basin began to fade, and the chorus with it, sinking back beneath the ordinary sounds of the world—except for one voice, the stubborn one, holding on at the surface, saying her buried name over and over, softer each time, like a lamp turned down instead of out.

"When the tide goes out again," the Ferryman's daughter said, rising, her edges already dissolving into shine, "come back. Bring anyone you trust. You'll need hands that aren't afraid, and a memory for old roads." She paused at the threshold, half-gone, and her smile turned wry. "And bone-speaker—do hurry. My father's river changed course last week. Rivers only do that when someone upstream is pulling."

Then she was water-light and gone, and Yara knelt alone in the drowned shrine with the tide creeping up the stones, and somewhere above her, back in the city she'd have to walk home through, a tinker and a scribe were waiting with supper and lies about her name, and one of them, she suddenly understood with a cold drop of certainty, knew far more about god-tenders than he had ever admitted.

Bram had a tattoo he called an old scar. Bram flinched at the word *shrine*.

Bram had sworn something once, and Yara was going to make him tell her what.
