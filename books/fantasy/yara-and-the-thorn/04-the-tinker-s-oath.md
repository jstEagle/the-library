## 4. The Tinker's Oath

Yara knew something was wrong the moment Bram set down his supper spoon without finishing his soup. In twelve years she had never once seen him abandon a meal, not during the fever winter, not when Odile burned the bread so badly it could have shingled a roof.

He sat across from her at the long table, hands flat on the wood like a man about to be audited. The fire in the hearth had gone low. Odile was out — gone to the archive with a forged pass and a face of pure innocence, which fooled no one, least of all the archivists who adored her.

"The charm," Bram said. "The one for Marisol the fishwife. It worked."

It wasn't a question. Word traveled through Vell faster than spilled oil, and by now half the harbor district had heard that the widow's husband spoke through a carved fish-bone for three whole nights before going quiet.

"It worked," Yara admitted. She kept her voice level. *Lena Reyes sells harmless charms*, the lie said, and she tried to lay it over her words like a cloth over a cage.

Bram nodded slowly. He turned his spoon over, studied it as if it might contain advice. Then he rolled up his left sleeve.

Yara had seen that arm ten thousand times — scarred from forge-sparks, freckled, thick as a ham. She had never looked closely at the inside of the wrist, where the light rarely reached. Now he held it up to the lamp, and she saw it: an old brand, deliberately blurred by years and probably by some acid or salt, but still legible if you knew what you were looking at. She knew. Everyone raised outside the cities knew.

A god-tender's mark. The open hand, palm outward. *I carry what the gods leave behind.*

"Bram," she breathed.

"I know what you're going to say," he said hurriedly. "You're going to say I should have told you. And I'm going to say that I was waiting for *you* to tell *me*, and we were both waiting so long we nearly got ourselves killed." He rubbed the faded brand with his thumb, the way a man touches an old ache before rain. "I served at the shrine of Merenth, down in the southern valleys. Eleven years old when they took me in. Orphan of the marsh fever. They fed me, taught me letters, taught me which bones sing and which ones only rattle."

Yara's heart was doing something complicated in her chest. "That was before the purge."

"Four years before. I was fifteen when the wardens came up the valley road with their firepots." His jaw tightened. "There were thirty-one tenders at Merenth. I counted them after. I was the only one who ran, because the high tender — a woman named Sefa, small as a sparrow, tough as bootleather — she grabbed me by the collar and shoved me into the root cellar and told me to *live*, boy, live loudly enough for all of us."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs Odile's cat — a creature of no discernible origin named Turnip — thumped down onto the landing and began washing himself with offensive thoroughness, as if he too required an alibi.

"I swore an oath at fourteen," Bram went on quietly. "Both hands on the shrine-stone. *What is holy I will keep; what is broken I will mend; what is hunted I will hide.*" He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only an old bruise being pressed. "And then I spent thirty years hiding nothing holier than smuggled tin and my own name. I didn't mend anything, Lena. I opened a tinker shop. I fixed kettles. I let them all go quiet."

"You survived," Yara said.

"That's what Sefa told me to call it." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly kind. "But here's the thing I've been chewing on since Marisol started telling the whole wharf about her talking charm. The oath I swore — it wasn't to the tenders. It was *through* them. To whatever was listening. And if the gods are waking up again—" he spread his big hands "—then maybe oaths don't die just because the people who witnessed them do."

Yara sat very still. Twelve years of careful silence, twelve years of *Lena Reyes, bone-carver, harmless as bread* — and underneath it all, this broad, gentle man had been carrying a shard of the same broken world she'd fled. They had shared a roof, shared meals, shared the bill for Turnip's veterinary disasters, and neither of them had ever once said the true thing aloud.

"Why now?" she asked softly. "Why tell me tonight?"

"Because tomorrow the Inquisitor's wardens knock on doors alphabetically, and ours starts with H." He almost smiled. "And because you looked at Marisol's charm the way I used to look at the shrine-stone. Like you were homesick for a place that doesn't exist anymore." He leaned forward. "Whatever you're carrying, girl — you don't have to carry it alone in my house. That's not how this household works. We hide things together or not at all."

Yara reached across the table and took his hand — the branded one — and held it the way you hold something you're afraid will apologize itself into nothing.

"My name isn't Lena," she heard herself say, and stopped, astonished, at the edge of everything else.

Bram squeezed her fingers. "I figured it wasn't *Lena*. No offense, but nobody born with your hands is named Lena." He stood, fetched the good bottle from behind the flour barrel, and poured two cups of plum brandy with the solemnity of a liturgy. "Tell me the rest when you're ready. Tonight we drink to oaths we broke and get to swear better ones."

They drank. Upstairs, Turnip sneezed with tremendous dignity.

* * *

Odile came home past midnight smelling of ink and panic, and found them both still awake, which surprised her enough that she set down her satchel very carefully, the way one does when entering a room where confessions are clearly in progress.

"Am I early for something?" she asked.

"You're right on time," Bram said. "We're telling the truth. Apparently it's catching."

Odile looked between them — at Yara, whose face must have shown more than she intended, and at Bram, whose sleeve was still rolled up. Something in her shoulders changed. Yara watched it happen: the scribe's perpetual alertness, the coiled readiness to deflect with a joke or a footnote, softening into decision.

"Oh," Odile said. "Well. If we're doing this." She pulled out a chair, sat, folded her hands like a student presenting a thesis, and then ruined the effect entirely by taking Bram's brandy cup and draining it.

She was nineteen. Yara sometimes forgot that, because Odile quoted archival law like a woman of fifty and lied to wardens like a woman of ninety. But sitting there in the lamplight with empty cheeks and chapped fingers stained blue-black, she looked every hour of her age and braver than most people manage in a lifetime.

"You both know I ran away from the Scriptorium of Anselm," she began. "Everyone assumes it's because I hated it. I loved it. That was the problem." Her words came fast now, a dam cracking. "Third year, I was assigned to the burning room. Every scriptorium has one — books condemned under the Edict of Silence, waiting for the monthly pyre. My job was to carry them. That's all. Carry, stack, watch them burn, log the titles afterward for the record." She swallowed. "Nobody checks whether a scribe logs titles accurately. Nobody checks whether she spends six months copying every forbidden rite she carries, in her own invented shorthand, into pages sewn inside her coat lining."

Yara felt the floor tilt gently beneath her certainties. "Odile—"

"Forty-one texts," Odile said. Her chin was up, but her voice wobbled on the number. "Prayers. Rites. A partial map of the pilgrim roads to — to somewhere east, somewhere the old tenders called the Thorn. I copied them because someone had to, because if everyone who remembered is dead and everything written is ash, then it never happened, do you understand? The gods don't just die. They get *erased*." She wiped her eyes with an angry knuckle. "I've been sleeping in your spare room for three years telling you I was a runaway clerk who misfiled things. I am a criminal against the Crown, technically. I could hang for the lining of my coat."

The silence that followed was not a heavy silence. Yara marveled at that. It should have crushed them — three liars around one table, each confession pulling threads loose from the others'. Instead it felt like a house finally admitting how many rooms it had.

"Well," Bram said at last, into his cup, "between the three of us we've got a deserter of the purge, a bone-witch with a false name, and a book-thief with contraband sewn into her coat. Anybody here actually innocent?"

"I am," said Odile. "Turnip is innocent. Everything else is negotiable."

Yara laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than she could measure — and the sound startled even her. It loosened something. When she spoke again, her voice came out steadier than she expected.

"The charm worked because the magic is coming back. Not trickling — *waking*. The dead spoke through Marisol's charm, and they've spoken since, and I followed them today to a drowned shrine by the weir, and—" she stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real meant the next step, and the next step meant leaving. "Something is failing. Something old. And I think I'm involved, and I think the two of you are now also involved, purely by proximity to me, and I am so sorry."

"Don't be sorry," Bram and Odile said together, and then glared at each other for the overlap, which broke the tension beautifully.

It was Odile who leaned forward, eyes bright with the particular fervor Yara had learned to fear and love. "Say the word *involved* again. Slowly. You followed voices in the bone to a shrine. Shrines sit on roads. Roads lead places." She was already reaching for her satchel, already drawing out a bundle of coat-lining pages tied with waxed thread. "I have forty-one forbidden texts and a map nobody alive has seen, and I have been *dying* for a reason—"

A fist struck the front door below. Once, twice — the flat, official cadence of knuckles trained to knock.

All three of them froze. From the landing above came a single indignant chirp from Turnip, who alone among the household owed nothing to anyone.

Bram rose without a sound, rolled his sleeve down over the faded hand, and blew out the lamp.
