## 5. Ashes of the God-Tenders

Odile had copied out the rite for safe travel eleven times in her life, and every one of those times she had been certain it would be the death of her. She had simply never imagined the death would come so quickly, or that it would involve Bram's mule.

"Her name is Buttercup," Bram said, "and she is a lady."

"She bit me."

"She's shy."

"She took a piece of my sleeve and I believe she was aiming higher." Odile held up her forearm as evidence. The wool was frayed into something resembling lace. "In the scriptorium we called this *marginalia*. When a text bites back."

Yara laughed — a short, startled sound, as if laughter were a charm she'd forgotten how to work. It was the first time Odile had heard it in three days, since the night they'd fled Vell through the tannery district with wardens' lanterns swinging behind them like slow yellow moons. Since then Yara had been all economy: pack the bone tools, count the coins, watch the road. A woman made of lists. Odile recognized the condition. She'd been a woman made of footnotes once herself.

The pilgrim road climbed away from Vell in long switchbacks, and it was, Odile thought, the saddest library she had ever walked through.

Shrines stood along it every half-mile or so — the old way-stations where travelers had once paused to leave an offering, whisper a name, ask a small mercy of a small god. Or they had stood. Most of what remained now were stumps: niches chiseled hollow, statues toppled face-down into the weeds, their hands broken off first, always the hands first. Someone had been thorough. Someone had wanted no god to be able to hold anything ever again.

"The Purge reached this far?" Bram asked quietly. He walked beside the mule, one hand on her neck, which was either affection or damage control.

"Further," Odile said. "This is the Meridian Road. It ran from Vell to the Thorn itself, back when pilgrims still went. My masters at the Athenaeum called the desecrations 'the Long Correction.'" She touched the broken shoulder of a fallen statue as she passed, a gesture so automatic she didn't notice she'd done it until Yara's eyes flicked to her hand. "They believed they were fixing something."

"And you believed them?"

"I believed whatever kept me fed." Odile said it lightly, and then wished she hadn't, because it came out lighter than the truth deserved. She tried again. "I was fourteen when they took me in. An orphan with good handwriting. Do you know what a gift that is, in a city full of people who can read but can't write? I copied edicts. Then I copied inventories of confiscated things. Then, because my hand was small and neat and I asked no questions, I copied the rites themselves — the ones they seized from the god-tenders. Not to destroy them. To *keep* them. The Inquisition liked having originals. Proof, they said. Trophies, I think. And I sat in a warm room with ink on my fingers and copied prayers by the yard, and told myself I wasn't praying."

"And were you?" Yara asked.

Odile looked down the road, at the next ruined shrine waiting for them like a sentence awaiting its verb.

"Every single time," she said.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a waystation inn, its roof open to the stars like a skull opened to the sky. Bram got a fire going with the competence of a man who had built ten thousand of them, and Buttercup was tethered at a respectful distance from Odile, by mutual treaty.

It was Yara who broke the silence, turning a piece of raw bone over in her fingers — not carving it, just holding it, the way another person might hold a rosary.

"You knew the road would have shrines on it," Yara said. "Before we left. You told us to take the Meridian instead of the lowland track, and when I asked why, you said, 'Because the lowland track has checkpoints.' Which was true. But it wasn't the whole truth, was it."

Odile warmed her hands at the fire and considered lying. It was a professional skill; she'd had excellent instruction.

"The whole truth," she said, "is that the shrines are a map. Not a metaphor — an actual map. The old god-tenders built them in sequence. Each shrine marked a day's walk from the last, and each was dedicated to a different aspect of the covenant. If you knew the order, the road itself became a text. A liturgy you could walk." She pulled her satchel into her lap and drew out a battered notebook, its cover stained with ink of at least four vintages. "I copied the sequence years ago. Shrine of the Threshold. Shrine of the Ledger. Shrine of the Unbinding. They lead to the Thorn the way verses lead to a refrain."

Bram leaned forward, firelight catching the gray in his beard. "You've been carrying that book since Vell?"

"Since before Vell. Since the Athenaeum burned." Odile heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for cataloguing losses. "I was twenty-two. They said the god-tenders did it themselves, out of spite. I believed that too, for about a year. Then I found a marginal note in my own handwriting on a page I'd copied the week before the fire, and I realized I'd been the last person to touch some of those texts while they were still legal. Somebody very high up decided the copies were more dangerous than the originals. So they burned the archive and left me alive, because a copyist who doesn't know what she carries is safer than any locked room."

She closed the notebook. Her hands were steady. She was rather proud of that.

"So you've been running longer than I have," Yara said softly.

"I've been *hiding* longer than you have. Running implies direction." Odile managed a smile. "You gave me one, actually. Three days ago, when you knocked on our door at midnight with wardens' bells ringing and said 'the Thorn,' and Bram started packing before you finished the sentence. I've never seen him pack that fast. He once took forty minutes deciding between two identical hammers."

"They were not identical," Bram said with dignity. "One had better balance."

* * *

The second day brought them to the Shrine of the Threshold, the first station of the walking liturgy, and it was worse than the others, because someone hadn't finished the job.

The niche was gouged empty, the guardian statue dragged down and defaced — but beneath the rubble, half-buried, a foundation stone remained, and on it the old dedication was still legible: *ALL WHO PASS WERE ONCE ARRIVING.* Below it, newer scratches. Wardens' tallies, Odile guessed, counting shrines destroyed. And below *those*, almost hidden in the shadow of the stone, something else: a small chalk mark, fresh, no more than a season old. A circle with a line through it. The sign of the god-tenders.

Yara crouched over it for a long time.

"That's a tenders' mark," she said at last. Her voice had gone somewhere Odile couldn't follow — down, and inward. "My mother drew that sign. On doorposts. On my wrist, once, in lampblack, when I was small enough to still think it was a game." She traced the air above the chalk without touching it. "Someone came through here recently. Someone who still knows the signs."

"Refugees, maybe," Bram offered gently. "Or survivors keeping the old stations, the way you'd keep a grave."

"Or a guide," Odile said, and immediately regretted it, because three heads turned toward her with the particular hunger of people who want good news. She sighed and opened her notebook. "If the shrines are a liturgy, then someone who walks it regularly — marking the stations, keeping the signs fresh — isn't just remembering. They're *practicing*. And if there are practicing god-tenders between here and the Thorn..." She tapped the chalk mark. "Then we may not be walking toward a ruin alone. We may be walking toward whoever lit the match Yara felt in that charm."

Yara stood slowly. In the afternoon light her face did something complicated — hope and dread sharing the same small space, like two people squeezing through a narrow door.

"My mother's voice was in the bone," she said. It was the most she had ever given them of herself, and she gave it looking at the chalk mark rather than at any of them. "At the drowned shrine. Among all the dead, hers. She said—" Yara stopped. Started again. "She said *the road remembers you.* I thought it was just grief talking. Grief says a lot of things."

"And now?" Bram asked.

"Now I think the road might actually remember me. And I don't know whether that's a welcome or a warning." She shouldered her pack. "We make the next station by dark. Odile — how far to the Shrine of the Ledger?"

Odile consulted her notebook, and found her own handwriting suddenly strange to her, the way a word repeated too many times goes strange. Twelve years of copying other people's holy words, and only now, walking the road they described, did the words stand up and become places.

"A day and a half," she said. "Assuming Buttercup cooperates."

Buttercup, hearing her name, bit the fence post.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep, Odile sat up by the banked fire and copied — not from her stolen texts, but from the day itself. The tally-scratches. The chalk sign. Yara's face when she spoke of her mother. She wrote quickly, in her smallest hand, and when she finished she sat looking at the page and understood, with the mild horror of a scholar arriving late at an obvious conclusion, that she was no longer making copies.

She was writing an original.

Somewhere down the dark road, a nightjar called three times, paused, and called again — and Odile froze, because that was not a nightjar's rhythm. That was a signal. Three and two. The old tenders' call, the one she had copied a hundred times in the margins of confiscated rites without ever once expecting to hear it answered from a hillside.

From the darkness beyond the dead shrine, someone whistled back.
