## 6. The Inquisitor's Mercy

Sarel Voss had been an Inquisitor of the Third Circle for nine years, and in that time she had learned that most people were liars in the same way that most rivers were wet — thoroughly, inevitably, and without malice. They lied about taxes. They lied about their neighbors' goats. They lied about whether they had seen a woman with bone-carved charms pass through their village, and they lied so badly that Voss had long ago stopped taking offense at it and begun to find it almost restful.

She was not resting now.

She stood at the edge of the ferry crossing on the River Senn, three days out of Vell, watching the mist come off the water like something being poured, and she did not feel restful at all. She felt the way she had felt at eleven years old, standing in the temple doorway while the god-tenders burned, holding her little brother's hand too tightly and telling him everything would be all right, which was the first great lie of her life and, she suspected now, not the last.

"Ma'am?" said Warden Pellik, her junior, a broad young man whose face was built for honesty and who therefore made a poor warden. "The ferryman says there's four travelers wants crossing before dawn. Won't give names."

"Of course he won't," Voss said.

"Should I—"

"No." She held up one finger. "We wait. We watch. If it's them, we take them gently. If it's pilgrims, we apologize for our faces."

Pellik looked relieved. He always looked relieved when she said *gently*, which told her something about what his previous postings must have been like, and she chose not to ask.

The truth — and Voss kept her truths in a locked drawer inside herself, organized and dusted weekly — the truth was that she was no longer certain what she would do if it *was* them.

* * *

Three nights ago, in a rented room above a chandlery in the town of Marrow's Bend, Inquisitor Sarel Voss had done something forbidden by every statute she enforced.

She had prayed.

Not officially. Not kneeling, not aloud, nothing that could be entered into evidence. She had simply lain awake staring at the ceiling beams and thought, with the raw and unguarded desperation of a woman three weeks past her brother's death-day, *Tam. If you can hear me. Tam.*

And Tam had answered.

Not in any way a court would accept. No voice from the rafters, no ghost seated on the foot of the bed offering testimony. Just this: the smell of tallow and river mud, which had been the smell of their childhood; the sensation of a small cold hand slipping into hers, exactly the weight of a boy's hand, eleven years old forever; and then, faint as a word spoken through a wall, a sound she had spent twenty years trying to forget and one night remembering whole —

Her own name. The childhood one. The one nobody living knew.

*Sarel.*

She had sat up in bed with her heart going like a drum corps and her eyes streaming and said, to the empty room, "This is impossible."

The room had smelled like tallow and river mud for another minute, and then it hadn't.

Voss was not a stupid woman. Stupid women did not reach the Third Circle. She understood perfectly well what had happened, because it was the very thing she'd been sent upcountry to investigate: answered prayers. Reported in fishing villages, in market towns, in a leper hospice outside Corvane where a dying woman had allegedly sung a hymn nobody had taught her. The covenant was failing, or waking, or both, and the dead were leaning against the door of the world the way children lean against a kitchen door when supper's late.

And here was the problem, the problem she had not put into any report: the statutes called answered prayers *heresy*. But no statute in existence, however old, however thorough, could make her believe that hearing her brother's voice had been anything other than the single kindest thing the universe had ever done for her.

Duty said: report yourself. Duty said: you are compromised, hand the investigation to Pellik, go home, sit in a small room until the wonder wears off.

Wonder said nothing at all. Wonder just sat there in the dark of her, warm as a small cold hand, and waited to see what she would do.

* * *

The ferry came across in the grey half-light, low in the water, and Voss counted shapes in the bow. Four. One big man standing at the prow like a fencepost that had opinions. One slight figure hunched over what was unmistakably, even at forty paces, a satchel full of books. Two women near the stern, one tall and watchful, one smaller, wrapped in a fishwife's shawl, doing a remarkably bad job of looking like someone who had ever gutted a fish in her life.

"There," Pellik breathed. "That's the carver. The face matches the description from Vell."

It did. Even at distance, even in mist, Voss recognized the face she'd studied on the warrant: sharp-boned, dark-eyed, wearing the expression of a woman performing the role of *innocent traveler* with the total commitment of someone who had been doing it for years.

Yara. Or Lena Reyes, or whatever the name under the name was. The bone-carver whose mourning charm had worked. The woman at the center of the storm.

Voss raised her hand, and Pellik stepped forward onto the shingle, and she heard herself say — clearly, in her official voice, the voice that carried across water:

"Inquisitor's office! The ferry is stopped by order of the Crown! All passengers will disembark!"

Everything happened very fast after that, and also, strangely, very slowly, the way things do when part of your mind has already left the room.

The big man moved in front of the two women like a wall deciding to be a wall. The scribe clutched her satchel. And Yara — Lena — the carver — stepped forward instead of back, which was wrong, fugitives ran, and looked directly at Voss across the water with an expression that was not guilt and not fear but recognition, terrible and calm, as if she could see the locked drawer in Voss and had read its contents.

"You've heard him too," the woman said. Her voice carried impossibly well over the water, or perhaps the water carried it, which should have frightened Voss more than it did. "Haven't you. That's why you came yourself. That's why you keep saying *gently.*"

Pellik glanced at her. "Ma'am? Said what?"

"The dead are answering prayers," the woman called. "You know it. Your office knows it, that's why they sent you. And your law says I'm a criminal for carrying word of it. So here is my question, Inquisitor, and I'll only ask once." She spread her hands, empty, palms up, a bone-carver's scarred hands. "Your brother. When he spoke to you. Was that a crime?"

The world went very quiet.

Mist moved on the river. Somewhere upstream a bird complained. Pellik was saying, "Ma'am, I don't — what brother? Ma'am, is she mad?" and his voice seemed to come from a long way off, because Sarel Voss was standing in a rented room above a chandlery smelling tallow and river mud, and a small cold hand was in hers, and a voice through a wall was saying her true name, and every statute she had sworn to uphold was asking her to call that moment filth, corruption, heresy —

"I have a duty," she said. Her voice cracked on *duty,* which was mortifying, and she pressed on anyway, because a woman who had lied her whole career about small things could at least stop lying about a large one. "I have a duty and I am going to fail it. That is not an accusation I welcome. Get back in the boat."

"Ma'am—" Pellik started.

"Pellik." She did not look at him. "There are four of them and two of us and the river is wide and my heart is not in it, and I will not write in my report that it was. Get back in the boat, and when we return to Vell, you will tell the Second Circle that I lost the fugitives at the Senn crossing in fog, which will be nearly true, and you will never speak to me again of brothers. Am I understood?"

He understood. He was honest-faced and slow to anger and he had perhaps suspected for a week that something in his superior was breaking, and to his eternal credit he simply touched his cap and said, "Fog it is, ma'am."

* * *

The ferry pushed off. Voss stood on the shingle and watched it shrink into the mist, and just before it went, she committed the carver's face to memory with an Inquisitor's discipline — the set of the jaw, the scar through the left eyebrow, the eyes that had seen her drawer and opened it without force.

Because letting them go was not the same as believing them. Letting them go was a debt, and Sarel Voss paid her debts. Whatever the woman was walking toward — the old road, the shrines, whatever lay at the end of the pilgrim way — Voss intended to be there. Not to arrest her. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. But to see. To stand at whatever altar this was and learn whether the universe's kindness to grieving sisters extended to everyone, or only to those willing to walk into the dark and ask.

"You're crying, ma'am," Pellik observed, tactfully, staring straight ahead at the fog.

"It's the mist," said Voss.

"Fierce mist this morning."

"Notorious, this river."

They stood together in the notorious mist like two soldiers after a battle nobody had won, and far away across the water, so faint it might have been the current, might have been memory, might have been a boy's laugh, something whistled three notes and then two.

Voss did not pray back. She was still, formally, an officer of the law, and there were limits.

But she listened. She listened all the way down.

* * *

On the far bank, wringing out her shawl, Odile said, "She let us go. Why did she let us go? Bram, why did she let us go? Fugitives don't get *let go*. That's not how fugitive-ing works."

"She's coming after us," Yara said quietly. She was watching the mist where the ferry had been, and her face wore an expression Bram had never seen on it in twelve years of friendship — hope and dread in equal measure, braided tight. "Not to arrest us. I don't think she knows what for yet. But she'll find us."

"How can you possibly know that?"

"Because," Yara said, turning toward the hills where the old pilgrim road climbed into the grey, "the dead told me she would. And for the first time in twelve years—" she laughed, a short, unsteady sound, "—I actually believed them."

Above them, on the ridge, the road waited, and somewhere along it, past the desecrated shrines, past the drowned chapel, the Thorn was pulling at the world like a wound pulls at a bandage. Four travelers began to climb.
