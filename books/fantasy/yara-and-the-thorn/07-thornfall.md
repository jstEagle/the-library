## 7. Thornfall

The road stopped pretending to be a road three days out from the Thorn.

What had been pilgrim-way—cracked flagstones, then gravel, then a footpath worn stubborn into the heather—simply gave up, the way old men give up arguments, with a shrug and a scatter. Beyond it the land rose in slow gray folds toward a horizon that was wrong in a way Yara couldn't name until she could: there were no birds. Not circling, not singing, not one small brown job hopping insolently near their boots hoping for crumbs. Odile had noticed it too; she kept glancing up with the expression of a scholar whose footnote had walked off the page.

"No birds," Bram said, because Bram said things, in the tone of a man remarking on weather or a neighbor's fence. "You'd think even the end of the world would keep sparrows."

"Maybe the sparrows know something we don't," Odile said.

"Everything knows something we don't. That's the trouble with everything."

Yara smiled at them the way you smile at a fire in a cold room—with gratitude and a low, constant fear of losing it. Three days ago, on the ash-strewn pilgrim road, she had told them pieces of herself: Yara, not Lena. God-tender's daughter. The purge she'd run from at fourteen with her mother's bone knife and nothing else. They hadn't flinched. Bram had simply nodded and cooked dinner as if feeding a fugitive god-daughter were an ordinary Tuesday. Odile had asked four hundred questions, which was somehow better than no questions at all.

They were walking toward the broken place where heaven had once been nailed to earth, and her found family was arguing about sparrows.

She loved them so much it frightened her.

* * *

They saw the Thorn at dusk on the third day, and Yara understood why no birds came.

It stood in the center of a vast bowl of stone—a spire, or what had been a spire, taller than any tower in Vell, but shattered. That was the only word. It looked as if something impossibly vast had gripped the axis-stone of the world and snapped it, and the fragments still hung where they'd broken, drifting a handspan apart from each other, defying every law Odile had ever copied down. Chunks of white stone the size of houses floated in a slow spiral around the central stump, turning so gradually you could watch them only by waiting. Between the shards hung threads of light, silver-white and taut as harp strings, and when the wind moved through them they made a sound.

Yara heard it before anyone else. Of course she did.

It was not wind-chime music. It was voices. Hundreds of them, thousands, all speaking at once in a murmur like rain on a roof—the dead, the dead, the dead of both realms caught in the failing seams between worlds—and beneath them, deeper, a groan like a ship's hull taking water. The covenant wasn't just breaking. It was *straining*, the way bone strains before it splits, and she could hear the exact note at which it would go.

She put a hand over her ear and made a sound she did not intend to make.

Bram was beside her instantly. "Bad?"

"It sings," she said. "The Thorn sings. All of it. Every shard." Her voice came out thin. "Bram, I can hear how it breaks. There's a note—I can hear the note it breaks on."

He looked at her for a long moment, this broad, patient man with sawdust permanently in his beard, and then he said the thing that undid her a little: "Then we'd best get you close enough to fix it, hadn't we." Not a question. Not awe. Just the calm of a tinker who had spent forty years fixing things other people declared ruined.

Odile, meanwhile, had her nose in the last intact page of her notes, muttering. "*When the axis fell, the god-tenders sealed the ways... the rite requires a soul of the threshold...*" She looked up, and Yara watched the color leave her face by degrees, like a tide going out. "Yara. The Rite of the First Binding. I copied a fragment of it years ago in the Vault of the Ninth Shrine, and I thought—I thought it was metaphor. Ritual language. 'A soul belonging wholly to neither heaven nor earth, who speaks its own true name before gods and mortals alike.'" Her voice dropped. "It isn't metaphor. It's a specification. Whoever reforges the covenant has to stand *in* the seam. In the between-place. And they have to mean their name."

The wind through the hanging shards moaned. Somewhere among the thousand voices, Yara heard—or imagined she heard—a tone she had spent twelve years not listening for. Her mother's voice, saying her name. Not *Lena*. The other one. The buried one.

"I know," Yara said quietly. "I've known since the drowned shrine. I hoped I was wrong." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I'm never wrong about the dead. That's the whole cursed problem."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen pilgrim-shelter, its roof long since caved, its shrine-niche empty except for bird droppings—which struck Yara as darkly funny, given the absence of birds, as if even abandonment left evidence. Bram built a small fire with the competence of a man who believed no problem existed that couldn't be improved by tea. Odile sat wrapped in two blankets, reading her forbidden fragment by firelight for perhaps the tenth time, lips moving.

Yara couldn't sleep. She climbed out onto the rocks above the camp and looked at the Thorn under the stars, and listened.

Up close, the voices resolved. A woman praying for her son's fever to break—in Vell? Here? Time ran strangely near the seam. An old man thanking someone, over and over, for a harvest. Children's voices, wordless, the way children pray. And threaded through them all, closer now, unmistakable: her mother. Not words yet. Just presence, the particular warmth-in-the-chest that Yara had known since infancy, the feeling of being *known*.

"You could have told us sooner," Odile said behind her. Not accusing. Just noting, the way she noted everything.

"I told you everything important twelve years late. I'm sorry. I was—" Yara searched for the word. "Practicing. Being Lena Reyes. Twelve years of practice, and do you know what I've realized, sitting here?" She hugged her knees. "I was very good at her. Kind. Careful. Sold charms that were only wood and good intentions. Made friends with a tinker who fixes things and a scribe who steals books." She glanced sideways. "And every single habit I built to hide—the listening, the patience, the carving truth into bone where no warden would look for it—it turns out those were exactly the skills the seam wants. Odile, I spent twelve years becoming the person who could do this. My whole false life was rehearsal. I don't know whether that's holy or obscene."

Odile considered this with the gravity she gave to difficult manuscripts. "My grandmother used to say God hides the tool inside the scabbard," she offered. "Then she'd hit me with a spoon. I don't know why I'm telling you this."

Yara laughed despite everything, and below them Bram's voice drifted up: "Are you two conspiring? There's tea. Conspiring is better with tea."

* * *

In the morning they descended into the bowl of stone, and the world thinned.

Yara felt it first as a lightness in her ears, the sensation of a door opening onto a larger room. Then the air itself changed texture—thinner, yes, but also more *occupied*, as though every breath passed through a crowd. The floating shards grew overhead as they approached until they blotted out half the sky, each one carved with reliefs worn smooth: processions of figures, human and otherwise, hands joined in a chain that wound around the entire spire. Gods and mortals holding hands. Someone had thought that worth carving into the axis of creation, and Yara thought: *they were right to.*

At the base of the central stump, the seam itself showed—a line of silver light running around the stone like a hairline crack in porcelain, humming that breaking note, and through it she could see. Not with eyes. But she could see: green fields that were not quite fields, a shore where a ferry waited with no boatman, the pale suggestion of towers. Heaven, leaking.

"The rite," Odile read aloud, voice shaking, "requires the speaker to stand within the seam and speak the true name before gods and mortals. The name must be offered freely. The binding takes—" she paused— "takes *from the speaker that which joins the realms.* Whatever gift of theirs crosses between."

Hearing of the dead. Yara had known it since the shrine; hearing it aloud still landed like a stone in her chest. Twelve years of hiding the gift, hating it, depending on it—and now she would have to hand it over forever, the way you pay a toll with your own hand.

"That's tomorrow's problem," Bram said firmly, which was his way of saying *I see you're grieving; I'll stand next to you while you do it.*

But tomorrow refused to wait politely. Because it was then that Yara heard the footsteps on the northern slope—boots, many, in disciplined rhythm—and turned to see, descending into the bowl of the broken world with the dawn behind them, a column of wardens in gray.

And at their head, riding, one hand pressed flat against her sternum as though holding something in, rode Inquisitor Sarel Voss.

Beside Yara, Odile swore in six languages, four of them dead ones. Bram reached for his hammer. And far above them, the hanging shards of the Thorn sang the breaking note a little louder—as if the seam itself knew that everything it required had finally arrived in one place: a liminal soul, a true name, and a witness in gray coming down the hill with wonder and duty at war on her face.

Voss's eyes found Yara across the distance. She did not draw her weapon.

She drew a breath, and called out across the silence of the birdless morning:

"You. Bone-carver. Before you do whatever it is you've come to do—you should know I heard my brother's voice last night. And I want to know *why* it was his voice that answered, and not God's."
