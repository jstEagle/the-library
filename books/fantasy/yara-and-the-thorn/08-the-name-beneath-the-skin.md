## 8. The Name Beneath the Skin

The Thorn did not let them sleep so much as it let them rest, which was a different thing entirely. Its light came up through the cracked ground like memory through an old scar — pale gold, pulseless — and it filled their little camp beneath the leaning stones with the color of late afternoon that never ended.

Yara sat between Bram's snoring bulk and Odile's nest of borrowed books and could not close her eyes. The bone charm she still carried — the fishwife's mourning piece, the first one that had answered — lay warm in her palm, warmer than her own blood, and it hummed against her skin like a cat that wanted something.

*Tomorrow,* it seemed to say. *Tomorrow you will climb down into the broken place, and tomorrow you will need to be someone.*

And she did not know who.

She said it out loud before she could stop herself. "I don't know what name to use."

Bram's snore caught, stuttered, resumed. Odile, who slept the way cats do — lightly, and with one ear open — rolled over and blinked at her through the golden half-dark. "What?"

"The rite." Yara turned the charm over in her fingers. "It wants my true name. Spoken before gods and mortals alike. And I've been sitting here trying to remember the last time I said it, and I can't. I don't think I've said it in twelve years. I'm not sure it still fits."

Odile sat up properly now, hair like a crow that had lost an argument with the wind. Across the fire-pit, Bram's breathing had gone suspiciously even, in the way of a large man pretending very hard to be asleep.

"You could just tell us," Odile said quietly. "Practice on us. We're not gods, but we're reasonably mortal."

Yara looked at her. At Bram, whose back rose and fell with theatrical innocence. At the light of the Thorn pooling around them all like something patient.

Twelve years of careful silence. Twelve years of Lena Reyes, bone-carver, harmless, forgettable, safe. She had built that woman plank by plank the way Bram built a cabinet, and she had loved her, in a way, the way you love a house that keeps the rain off.

But a house is not a self.

"Alright," Yara heard herself say, and her voice came out strange — lighter, unburdened, frightened. "But you should know it isn't a small thing. Once I start, I won't be able to stop at the comfortable parts. And there aren't many comfortable parts."

* * *

She began, as her mother had begun every story, with the river.

"I was born on the water," she said. "Not in Vell. Upriver, where the God-Tenders kept their sanctuary — the Whitewash, they called it, because the buildings were lime-white and the novices were always scrubbing them. You've heard of the god-tenders?"

"Everyone's heard rumors of the god-tenders," Odile whispered. "Half the forbidden rites I copied had their fingerprints on them. I thought the purge had—"

"Killed them all? It did. Eventually. But I was seven when it started, and for seven years before that I was the happiest child who ever lived." Yara smiled despite herself, and the smile hurt her face with its unfamiliarity. "My mother was the Keeper of Voices. That's not a grand thing — it means she kept the records, the names of the dead that families wanted remembered. But she had the hearing too. All of us did, to some degree. The children would play games with the old bones of the shrine-yard, listening for echoes. I was good at it. Frighteningly good, my mother said, with the particular pride of a mother who is also slightly worried.

"My name—" and here her voice did break, just a little, and she let it, because that was the whole point, wasn't it, "—my name was Yara Whitlow. Born Yara of the Whitlow line, Keepers of Voices, six generations back on my mother's side and further than that on the river itself."

The name hung in the golden air. It sounded absurdly loud. Bram had stopped pretending to sleep; he sat up slowly, the way you'd rise in a chapel.

"Yara," Odile repeated softly. Testing it.

"That's me. Or it was. Then the wardens came at midwinter." Yara folded her hands around the charm until its warmth bit. "You have to understand how fast it happened. One week the shrines were open and the dead were chatty and ordinary as weather, and the next there were fires on the far bank and my mother was sewing coin purses into our hems and teaching me a new name. Lena, she said. Plain name. Forgettable. She put me on a grain barge going downstream to Vell with a carved token in my pocket and instructions: *never answer when they call your true name, never listen to the bone where anyone can see you, survive.*"

"And she?" Bram asked, very gently.

"She stayed." Yara had told no one this. Not the whole of it. Even saying it now felt like pulling a splinter out backward, slow and clean and terrible. "Someone had to hold the records so the dead wouldn't be forgotten — that was her whole faith, that no voice should be lost. She stayed with the archives. I got on the barge because I was seven and she told me to, and I have spent twelve years being angry about both halves of that sentence. Angry that she sent me away. Angrier that she didn't come."

The Thorn's light pulsed once, faintly, as if listening.

"The purge reached Vell within the year. Wardens going door to door with lists. So Lena Reyes learned to carve charms that were only wood and bone and hope, nothing in them, no listening at all. And it worked. Twelve years it worked." She laughed, short and bitter. "Except it didn't work, did it? It just waited. Because the magic wasn't gone — it was sleeping, same as the gods, and when the covenant started cracking, everything woke up at once. Including me."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere below them, deep in the shattered ground, the Thorn groaned like a ship in a swell.

Odile was crying without any fuss about it — tears simply running down her face while her expression stayed scholarly, taking notes on her own grief. "Your mother," she said. "In the shrine. The drowned one. When the voices came through the bone — you heard her, didn't you? That's why you went white."

"One of the voices," Yara admitted. "I couldn't be sure. The dead don't speak in order, they speak all at once, like a crowd behind a wall. But there was a cadence I knew." Her throat closed. "If I do this rite tomorrow, that's what I give up. The wall comes down forever. I'll never hear her again — or confirm it was her, or say goodbye, or anything. Silence, permanent, in exchange for both worlds staying stitched together."

"Well," said Bram.

They both looked at him. He was holding his mug of cold tea with two enormous hands, and his face — usually so easy, so ready to laugh at his own jokes — was grave as a gravestone.

"You're not going to like this," he said, "but it seems to be a night for truths, so here's mine, since yours apparently has to come out anyway. I wasn't always a tinker, girl. I was a tender. Third order, low rank, mended shrine-stones along the pilgrim roads. When the purge came, I didn't stay and I didn't fight. I melted my badge down and sold the metal and told myself the gods were done with me anyway." His jaw worked. "Swore an oath at nineteen that I broke at twenty-two. Forty years I've been carrying that. So when I say you don't walk into that broken stone alone tomorrow, I'm not being sentimental. I'm finishing an oath. Late. Badly. But finishing it."

"Bram—"

"No, hear me." He set the mug down with the care of a man handling something holy. "You spent twelve years keeping us safe by lying to us, and we let you, because it was easier. Well, the lie's paid for. From here on you're Yara Whitlow of the Whitlow line, and Yara Whitlow doesn't go alone."

Odile wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, the way she corrected a misprinted page. "I copied forbidden rites to eat, did I ever mention that? Three winters in the Scriptorium cellars, forging indulgences and transcribing things that got two of my friends burned. I know the liturgy of the reforging, Yara. Not well. But I know it. Which means I know that the rite requires witnesses. Mortal witnesses. 'Before gods and mortals alike' — it's not poetry, it's a requirement. Two mortals, standing witness." She lifted her chin. "Congratulations. You're legally obligated to let us come."

Yara stared at them. Something was happening in her chest that she had no craft for, no carving technique, no twelve years of practice. It was enormous and simple and it was going to make her cry in front of people, which Lena Reyes had sworn never to do.

"You realize," she managed, "that I lied to you for over a decade. About my name. My family. Everything."

"You realize," Odile countered, "that we liked you better than we liked the truth, and neither of us ever once checked your references."

A sound escaped Yara that was half laugh and half sob, and then it was all sob for a while, and Bram's arm came around her shoulders like a beam settling into place, and Odile pressed close on her other side, and the three of them sat in the endless gold light of the broken axis of heaven and earth while Yara Whitlow wept out twelve years of Lena Reyes like poison from a wound.

When it was over she felt scraped hollow and strangely new. Lighter. As if the false name had been armor after all — heavy, dented, but hers — and she had finally set it down.

"Say it again," Odile murmured into her shoulder. "Your name. I want to hear it when it sounds happy."

Yara took a breath. The charm in her hand had gone quiet, contented, like the cat that got what it wanted.

"Yara Whitlow," she said, and it fit.

* * *

She woke before dawn, if dawn still meant anything under the Thorn's deathless glow, and found Bram already awake, packing the gear with the grim efficiency of a man who had once marched pilgrim roads for a living. Odile stood at the lip of the descent — the great fissure where the axis-stone had shattered downward into light — reading aloud from a page she must have copied in another life, her lips moving fast.

Below, in the broken heart of the world, something was climbing up to meet them.

Yara saw it clearly in the space of one heartbeat: a figure made of river-light and old bone, ascending the shattered steps with the unhurried grace of someone who has all eternity and intends to use it. Behind it, fainter, a second figure — small, familiar, wearing her mother's posture.

The dead were coming to witness too.

"Yara," Odile called, without turning around. "You'd better get down here. I think your audience is arriving early."
