## 9. The Reforging

The Thorn did not look like the end of the world. It looked like a tree that had given up pretending.

It stood in a bowl of gray grass at the crown of a hill no map had ever agreed to name—a great black trunk, split down the middle from crown to root, the two halves leaning apart like an old couple who had run out of things to say. Through the split Yara could see the sky on the other side of the sky: not stars, but something brighter and stranger, the pale gold of a realm where the light came from everywhere at once.

The covenant was supposed to hold those two worlds apart just enough. It was fraying like a cheap hem.

"Charming spot," Bram said, setting down the pack he'd carried for nine days without complaint, though he had complained about everything else—the mud, the weather, Odile's singing, the particular way Yara chewed. "Very dramatic. Good acoustics."

"You would notice the acoustics," Odile said. She was already unwrapping her notes, her hands steady even though her eyes were enormous.

Yara didn't answer them. She couldn't. The dead were *loud* here.

They pressed against her from all sides—thousands of voices, the god-tenders who had died in the purge twelve years ago, the pilgrims who had walked this road before the shrines were broken, older dead still, names worn smooth as river stones. For twelve years she had kept them at arm's length, a murmur behind glass. Here there was no glass. They knew why she'd come. They were waiting.

And beneath all their voices, one she had been hearing faintly since the drowned shrine, growing clearer with every mile: her mother's. Not words yet. Just warmth, the way a hand near your cheek is warm before it touches.

"Lena." Bram's voice, gentler than his usual rasp. He only used that name when he wanted her attention; he'd known it wasn't hers for three days now, and had decided, in his tinker's practical way, that names were tools and he'd use whichever worked. "What do we do?"

"We wait," she said. "She's coming."

Odile looked up sharply. "Who?"

But the answer was already cresting the hill's far side.

* * *

Inquisitor Sarel Voss came alone.

That was the first thing Yara noticed, and the second was worse: Voss looked *awful*. Her wardens' coat was unbuttoned. Her braid had half-collapsed somewhere between Vell and here, and she hadn't fixed it, which for Sarel Voss was roughly equivalent to arriving naked. She walked like a woman who had made a decision several times and kept unmaking it with every step.

Her hand rested on her iron rod. It did not grip it.

"Halt," she said, when she was twenty paces away. Her voice cracked on the word and she tried again. "*Halt.* In the name of the Concord."

"The Concord hasn't existed in twelve years," Odile said, stepping slightly in front of Yara, which was absurd, because Odile weighed less than a sack of flour. "You know that. You've heard it."

"I've heard—" Voss stopped. Yara saw her jaw work. "I heard my brother's voice on the night of the frost moon. Tammas. Drowned when I was nineteen. He said my name. He said *Sarrie, the door is open, someone has to close it.*" She stared at Yara. "Three days later I find a bone-carver's mark on a desecrated shrine, and every warden in my company dreaming of golden light. So you will tell me what you are doing at the axis of the world, and you will tell me now."

"She's going to fix it," Bram said.

"She's going to *break* herself fixing it," Odile said, glaring at Yara, because they'd had this argument six times on the road and apparently intended to have it a seventh in front of the Inquisitor.

"Someone has to close it," Yara said quietly, and watched Voss flinch at her brother's own words thrown back. "The covenant was forged by people who belonged fully to neither realm—god-tenders' children, born on the threshold. My mother was one of the last. I'm what's left of her line." She took a breath. "My name isn't Lena Reyes. It's Yara Hartwell-of-the-Thorn, daughter of Maren, daughter of the god-tenders of Vell. And the covenant needs a true name spoken here, at the split, or both realms come apart like wet paper."

Silence. The wind moved through the split trunk and came out sounding like distant singing.

"And the cost?" Voss asked. She was too intelligent not to ask. "Covenants bind with something. What does this one take?"

Yara held her gaze. "Everything I am on the other side of the door."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. Bram said he'd carry her back to Vell over his shoulder if he had to, and meant it, and cried while saying it, which took most of the force out of the threat but none of the sincerity. Odile laid out alternatives from her forbidden copies—substitutions, proxies, rites involving seven virgins and a white goat that nobody could locate on short notice.

Yara let them talk. She sat against the Thorn's cold bark and listened to the dead.

Her mother's voice was closer now. Close enough to have shape.

*Maren*, Yara thought toward it, not daring more. *Was it worth it? Your life, for the last binding?*

The answer wasn't words. It was a kitchen memory: bread dough under small hands, laughter, the specific feeling of being *wanted somewhere*. Yes, it said. A thousand times, yes. Now stop stalling, little thorn.

Yara laughed, wetly, and stood up.

"Enough," she said. "Bram. Odile. Come here."

They came. Of course they came. That was the whole miracle of them.

"You can't follow me through," she said. "But you can stand at the threshold and be the rope I hold onto. That's not nothing. That might be everything. When I speak my name, I'll need something mortal to come back *to*. So look at me. Both of you. Remember me exactly as I am right now, so that whatever comes out the other side has something accurate to return to."

Bram wiped his nose with the back of his wrist. "You're going to be insufferable about this forever, aren't you. 'Saved the world, don't forget to thank me.'"

"Devastatingly insufferable."

"Good," he said thickly. "That's how I'll know it's really you."

"Inquisitor," Yara called.

Voss stood ten paces off, rod lowered, face unreadable. "Carver."

"When this is done, magic returns. The Concord's laws die with it. There will be trials, reckonings. God-tenders' survivors coming out of hiding." She paused. "There aren't many of us left. I'd take it as a kindness if you stood between us and anyone who thinks the purge should be finished."

Voss considered for a long moment. Then she did something Yara had never seen her do: she set the iron rod down in the grass, deliberately, and stepped away from it.

"My brother said someone has to close the door," she said. "He didn't say anything about arresting the person holding it shut. Go, Yara Hartwell-of-the-Thorn." She said the name carefully, like handling something consecrated. "I'll watch the road."

* * *

The split in the trunk was barely wide enough for a body. Yara turned sideways and slid in, and the moment her shoulders crossed the bark, the world changed key.

Inside the Thorn there was no up. No down. Only the seam between realms, gold light on one side of her, gray morning on the other, and her body the hinge between. The dead were everywhere—not pressing now, but *gathered*, arranged in rings around her like a congregation, and at the center of the innermost ring stood a woman with bread-flour still ghosting her forearms after twelve years in the ground.

"Mama."

*Little thorn.* Her mother's voice, full and warm and unbearably present. *You grew.*

"You're about to watch me un-grow a little."

*A little. Yes.* Grief moved across the dead face, and pride underneath it, vast as weather. *Listen. The rite is simple, which is why it's hard. Speak your name—all of it, the buried parts—and then offer what makes you the door: your hearing. Every voice you've ever carried, every whisper in the bone. Give it to the covenant. It will become the mortar. The realms will seal against it, sealed by a bridge that chose to stop being a bridge and become a wall.*

"Will it hurt?"

*Yes,* her mother said honestly. *But not for long. Nothing lasts long, except love, and you've made an unreasonable amount of that out there. I counted. I'm very proud. Also tell Bram his stew needs salt, I've been listening to him cook for years and it's been agony.*

Yara laughed, and the laugh broke, and her mother's presence wrapped around her one last time like arms.

Then Yara turned to face the seam, planted her feet—one foot in gold, one foot in gray—and opened herself completely for the first time in twelve years.

Every voice she had ever hushed came flooding in. The fishwife's husband. The drowned shrine's minor goddess. Ten thousand dead of Vell. The old gods stirring in their realm, curious, hopeful. The whole choir.

She breathed in, and spoke.

"Yara Hartwell-of-the-Thorn. Daughter of Maren. Daughter of the god-tenders. Bone-carver, liar, survivor. I am the door, and I am the lock, and this is my name."

The seam shuddered. Light poured through the crack in the world, demanding binding, demanding payment.

"And this is my offering," Yara said, and reached into her own chest—not flesh, but deeper, into the place where twelve years of whispers lived—and *pulled*.

It was like being unstitched from herself. Every voice tore free at once, and each one took a thread of her with it. She gave them the fishwife's grief-song. She gave them her mother's laugh. She gave them the murmurs she'd sold as harmless charms, the secrets strangers had paid copper to have carved, the voice of the Ferryman's daughter, the whole vast tender orchestra of the dead that had been her secret companion, her curse, her identity, since she was a child hiding under a burning shrine.

The covenant drank it like rain on cracked earth.

The seam began to close. Gold met gray along her body's line and fused, and the sound was enormous and total and then—

Silence.

Not quiet. Silence. For the first time since childhood, Yara's skull held nothing but her own thoughts, echoing in rooms suddenly, terribly empty. She screamed, maybe. She couldn't have said.

Then hands. Mortal hands, rough tinker's hands and ink-stained scribe's hands, dragging her backward through the narrowing gap, out onto honest grass under an honest gray sky, and the split in the Thorn sealed above her with a sound like a struck bell, and there was nothing on the other side of the world's walls at all.

Nothing. Just wind. Just her family's terrified faces. Just her own heartbeat, loud and solitary and hers.

"It's done," she rasped. "It's done. It's so *quiet*."

Bram was crying again. Odile was laughing and crying simultaneously, which she managed with scholarly composure. Voss stood at the hill's edge with her rod still lying in the grass, looking at the sealed Thorn like a woman watching a wound close that she'd been born believing would never heal.

Yara lay in the gray grass, deaf to eternity, alive, and started—to her own considerable surprise—to laugh.

"Salt," she said. "In the stew. Apparently everyone's been thinking it for years."

* * *

They came down off the hill at dusk, four figures on the old pilgrim road, and ahead of them, impossibly, the lights of Vell—more lights than usual, lanterns lit early, doors open. Word traveled strangely these days. Something had shifted in the air of the world and everyone felt it, though no one yet knew its name.

A name, Yara thought. Someone ought to give it one. Someone ought to carve it somewhere permanent, where it wouldn't fray.

She touched her signet knife in her pocket—her carving tool, her oldest friend—and found, for the first time in twelve years, that she knew exactly what she wanted to sign beneath it.

Below them, on the road into the city, a crowd had gathered at the gate. And at its front, unmistakably, stood a figure in a wardens' coat with the collar torn off, raising a hand—not in warning.

In welcome.
