## 10. A Quiet House in Vell

The first frost of the season came to Vell on a morning when Yara was carving a charm for a woman who wanted nothing more than a blessing on her hens.

It was, she reflected as the knife moved through the riverbone, a perfectly ordinary request. A hen-blessing. No voices in it, no whispers from beyond the veil of the world, no dead mothers murmuring through the marrow. Just bone and knife and a nervous woman named Marta who kept eleven chickens and worried about foxes.

Yara finished the last curl of the design — a simple knotwork hen, wings folded, safe in its little circle — and held it up to the window light.

"Good?" she asked.

Bram looked up from the workbench across the shop, where he'd been repairing a kettle with more enthusiasm than skill. He squinted. "Looks like a duck."

"It's a hen."

"All your birds look like ducks."

"That's called style, Bram."

"That's called a duck." He went back to his kettle, grinning into it.

Yara laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in a room where anyone could hear her — and tied the ribbon through the charm's bore-hole. Twelve years ago, she would have hidden this piece under three layers of harmless trinkets and sold it with a wink and a lie. Now there was a sign in her window that read *Yara Hartwell-Adjunct, Charms & Carvings* — Odile had insisted on the "adjunct," claiming it lent scholarly credibility, though Yara suspected she just liked the word — and customers came in openly asking for blessings, wards, mourning-pieces, love-knots, and occasionally, still, things that looked like ducks.

Magic was legal. The shrines were reopening one by one, their desecrated stones righted by volunteer crews and their altars scrubbed of soot. Last month the little shrine to the Ferryman's daughter at the Salt Gate had been rededicated, and Yara had gone, and stood before the statue of a girl holding a lantern over dark water, and found that her eyes stung even though the silence in her skull stayed perfectly, permanently quiet.

That was the trade. She had made it at the Thorn with her true name in her mouth and the whole broken world listening, and she had never once regretted it, except sometimes at night, in the old way, when grief came knocking and there was no voice left to answer it.

She had learned to answer it herself instead. It turned out that was enough.

* * *

Odile came up the stairs just past midmorning, arms full of paper, ink-stained to the elbow, hair escaping its braid in every direction like she'd lost an argument with a windstorm.

"The Rite of First Frost," she announced, dropping everything on Yara's counter with a thump that rattled the hen-charms. "Recovered. Mostly. The third verse is fragmentary — half of it burned, half of it water-damaged, and one line apparently eaten by a mouse, which I have decided to take personally."

"What does the mouse-eaten line say?"

"'And the [illegible] shall keep the [illegible] against the coming of the long [something].'"

"So we're trusting the frost to... something."

"We're trusting the frost to *keep* something," Odile said with dignity, "against the coming of the long something. Theologically sound. Practically useless. I've written to the archivists at Candlemere about it." She paused, then added, quieter: "There's a copy going to the shrine at Salt Gate too. For her."

For her. The Ferryman's daughter, who had begged a bone-carver in a drowned shrine to carry word that the covenant was failing, and who had gotten far more than she asked for. Yara touched the fresh copy of the rite, feeling the texture of the paper. Somewhere in those verses, restored now to the living world, was a prayer the girl's father had once spoken over her. Yara couldn't hear it anymore. But other people could, and would, every year at first frost, forever.

"I'll carve a lantern charm to go with it," Yara said. "For the offering shelf."

"You're going to make yourself cry carving a lantern charm."

"I'm going to make *you* cry watching me carve a lantern charm. There's a difference."

"There is not," said Odile, who was already wiping her eyes, "a difference."

* * *

The afternoon brought customers in ones and twos, the way good afternoons did.

A young man buying a warding knot for his sister's door, because she'd taken a post at the reopened shrine in Harrow Lane and their mother worried. An old soldier who ordered a mourning-piece for a friend lost thirty years ago at the border wars, and who sat in the shop's one chair while Yara carved it, talking the whole time, because he'd waited three decades to say the man's name aloud and apparently had a great deal saved up. Yara carved and listened and did not hear any dead in the bone, only the living man's voice, rough and steady, saying *Tomas liked rain, did I tell you? Tomas liked rain more than anything,* and thought: this is what I have instead. This. The living, telling me their dead.

It was not nothing. She was beginning to suspect it might even be everything.

Near closing, Marta returned for her hen charm, paid in eggs — six of them, warm from under the actual hens — and lingered at the door.

"They say the Inquisitor herself comes to the shrines now," Marta said, in the tone people used for gossip they hoped wasn't gossip. "Voss. That they see her at Salt Gate some mornings, just standing. Praying, maybe."

"She prays," Yara said.

Marta nodded, satisfied, and went home to her hens.

Yara stood a moment at the window, watching the street lamps come on. Sarel Voss, once the most feared warden in Vell, now a familiar figure at the shrines — she came to Salt Gate often, always alone, always at dawn, and she had never once come to the shop, though twice Yara had seen her pause outside it, look up at the sign, and walk on. Once, leaving a lantern charm at the shrine's offering shelf, Yara had found another offering already there: a small carved boat, crude but careful, unmistakably whittled by someone who was very good at hunting and very bad at art.

Yara had left it exactly where it was. Some prayers weren't hers to answer. But she'd started leaving a fresh lantern charm beside the boat each week, and each week the boat was still there, and neither of them ever spoke of it, and somehow that was its own kind of covenant.

* * *

Evening settled in the way it did above Bram's forge: warmth rising through the floorboards, the smell of bread from the kitchen, the clank-and-hiss of Bram putting off dinner in favor of "one more thing" at the anvil.

Yara found him there, sleeves rolled, arguing with a hinge.

"Dinner's ready," she said.

"This hinge disagrees."

"The hinge is winning?"

"The hinge has been winning for an hour." He set it down anyway and wiped his hands, and for a moment they stood together in the forge-light, two people who had fled a city side by side and come back to find it — and themselves — remade. His oath to the god-tenders, broken and grieved and finally forgiven; his hands, which had once tended altars, now mending kettles and hinges and, on festival days, helping Yara hang charms over the doors of neighbors who remembered when such things got you burned.

"Bram," she said. "Thank you. For the shop. For all of it."

He waved this off, embarrassed, the way he always did. "You pay rent."

"I pay rent *late.*"

"Consistently late. It's practically a tradition." He bumped her shoulder with his. "Go eat. Odile gets frightening when the bread goes cold."

At the bottom of the stairs he stopped, and without turning around, said: "The sign painter asked if you wanted your full name on the shopfront next time it's repainted. Hartwell-Adjunct and all."

Yara considered it. Her name — her true name, the one spoken before gods and mortals at the broken axis of the world, the one that had sealed heaven shut and opened her own life wide — was public record now, inscribed in the restored rites, known to scholars and shrine-keepers and one former Inquisitor who never spoke it aloud but clearly carried it somewhere behind her ribs.

But on a shopfront in Vell, among hens and hinges and warm bread, she found she preferred the short version. The daily one. The one her family used.

"Lena's fine," she said, and smiled at her own surprise. "Lena's mine now too."

Bram grinned and went down to dinner.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and Odile had fallen asleep over her manuscripts for the third night running — Yara draped a blanket over her and closed the mouse-defiled folio with reverence — Yara climbed to her own small room under the eaves and lit the lamp.

On the desk lay the day's last piece: a lantern charm, unfinished, meant for Salt Gate in the morning. She picked up her knife and worked by lamplight, the blade moving through the bone in slow, sure strokes. Around the lantern she carved a ring of small figures — a tinker with broad shoulders, a scribe with wild hair, a woman with a knife, a stern figure standing watch at a distance — and beneath them all, at the base where the maker's mark belonged, she cut the sign she had begun using months ago and would use for the rest of her life:

A small bird. Wings folded. Safe in its circle.

It looked, if she was honest, a little like a duck.

She held it to the light, and the silence in her was vast and clean and entirely her own, and somewhere below, the forge banked itself for the night, and the frost began, gently, to fall on a city where the gods could hear again — and where, in a quiet house above a warm forge, a woman signed her work with her own hand, and slept without a single ghost, and dreamed, for once, of nothing but tomorrow.
