## 1. The Smuggler's Tongue

The mule was offended, and Gideon Pemberton could not for the life of him work out why.

"It's the same bridge," he told her. "You crossed it in spring. You crossed it in autumn. It has not grown teeth since."

The mule — Hesper, though her papers called her something duller — planted all four hooves at the edge of the crossing and gave him a look of profound, ancient grievance. Below her, the river ran white and loud through the gorge, and the plank bridge swayed on its ropes like a thing deciding whether to be brave.

"You're being dramatic," Gideon said.

*You're being loud,* said Hesper.

He glanced up the trail, the way he always did, before answering. Old habit. Old, necessary habit. The wind came down off the Thornfell peaks carrying nothing but cold and pine resin, and no one was near enough to see a broad-shouldered man in a drover's coat standing at a rope bridge arguing with a mule about structural engineering. Still, he lowered his voice anyway. He always lowered his voice.

"The ropes are sound. I checked them at dawn."

*Ropes are sound,* Hesper agreed, with the air of someone conceding a point entirely unrelated to the argument. *Ropes are not the problem.*

"Then what is?"

She turned her long face toward the far side of the gorge, ears swiveling, nostrils working. Gideon waited. This was the part that people who imagined beast-speaking as a party trick never understood: the animals were almost never wrong, but they were also almost never clear. They spoke in impressions and instincts, in smells and old memories, and it took years to translate the language into anything a man could act on.

Hesper sent him an image: the far bank, the boulder field beyond it, and threaded through the stones — a scent. Warm. Dog-like, but wrong. Musk and iron and something older.

Wolf.

"Not hunting us," Gideon murmured. "Just watching."

*Watching,* Hesper agreed, satisfied that he had finally caught up. She stepped onto the bridge with the dignity of a queen boarding a barge, and Gideon followed, keeping his hand light on the lead rope, feeling the planks flex over the roaring water.

That was the trouble with his gift, when he bothered to think about it, which he tried not to more than once or twice a season: it had never once made his life easier. It only made it longer. Every animal between Harrowgate and the high passes knew things — where the snow bridges were rotten, which passes held crown patrols, which travelers carried sickness or knives or honest coin — and every animal expected him to listen. A man who could hear a storm coming from three valleys away did not get the luxury of pretending he hadn't heard.

Behind him, eight mules and two shaggy mountain ponies picked their way across the bridge one at a time, laden with casks of brandy that had never seen a customs house and bolts of southern wool that had never seen a tax stamp. Contraband, in the polite way of the trade. Gideon had been guiding such loads through the Thornfell high routes since he was fourteen, since Corvin Ashgrave had found a skinny orphan boy crouched in a stable yard talking a terrified carthorse out of kicking down a door, and had looked at him the way other men looked at gold veins in bare rock.

*"You'll do," Corvin had said. "Keep your mouth shut around folk, mind. Around everything else, talk all you like."*

Keep your mouth shut. That had been the whole of his education in survival, and Corvin had compressed it into five words because Corvin believed most wisdom fit in five words if you were too stupid to need ten.

Gideon had kept his mouth shut for twenty-two years. He was very good at it. He was fluent, in fact, in silence — fluent in the particular stillness a man learns when the only conversation he truly cares about is one that could hang him.

Because that was the arithmetic of it, plain and simple. The old laws had winked at cunning-folk and hedge-witches, had tolerated a man who whispered to horses so long as the whispering stayed useful and stayed hidden. But the new king had new men, and the new men had spent two years drafting a piece of parchment that everyone in the lowlands called the Veil Edict, and rumor said it would be read aloud in every market square by month's end. Rumor said what it contained, too. Gideon didn't let himself believe rumors. He just noticed, quietly, that he'd started listening to them all the way through instead of walking away halfway.

The last mule crossed. Gideon took up the lead and put the crossing behind them, climbing into the thin sunlight above the gorge, and that was when the ravens began.

*Man. Man with soft hands. Man who answers.*

They came off the crag in ones and twos, black against the blue, folding down to land on the boulders beside the trail like a delegation arriving slightly ahead of schedule. There were six of them, and Gideon knew four by name and all six by reputation, because ravens kept better ledgers than any guild clerk alive.

"Morning," he said.

*Morning, morning, morning,* they agreed, hopping along beside the mules with their heads tipped sideways, eyes like wet black coins. *Riders. Riders in the green pass. Iron men, iron smell, dogs on rope.*

Gideon's stomach performed a slow, practiced drop. "How many?"

*Many-many. More than paws.* One raven — the eldest, missing an eye from some ancient disagreement with a hawk — hopped closer. *Green pass is shut. Grey pass is open. Grey pass smells of nothing. Nothing is good.*

"Grey pass adds two days."

Ravens did not shrug, exactly. They did something with their feathers that amounted to the same thing. *Two days is better than rope. Rope is bad. We saw rope in the green pass. Rope and fire and shouting.*

Fire and shouting. Gideon turned the words over the way he'd turn a coin to check for shaving. Crown patrols didn't burn things in the high passes without reason, and the only reasons up here walked on four legs, or worse, walked on two and spoke to them.

"Thank you," he said quietly. "Watch the grey pass for me?"

*We watch. We watch. Fat scraps for watching?*

"There's hard sausage in my pack and you know there's hard sausage in my pack."

The ravens considered this acceptable payment, and rose off the rocks in a ragged black scatter, wheeling west toward the grey pass, and Gideon stood in the trail with his hand resting on Hesper's warm neck and felt the loneliness arrive right on schedule, the way it did every time the birds left. That was the cruel trick of his gift, the part nobody warned you about. It wasn't dangerous because someone might catch you using it. It was dangerous because using it worked. Because every conversation ended with him more certain of the world and more alone in it — the only man in Thornfell who got his news from birds, his warnings from mules, his company from creatures who could never once ask him how he slept.

"Grey pass," he told Hesper.

*Long way,* she complained.

*Long life,* he answered, and she had no reply for that, which among mules was as close to agreement as the world ever came.

* * *

They made the grey pass by late afternoon, the trail narrowing until the loaded mules walked in single file along a shelf of stone with a drop on the left that went down and down into blue shadow. Gideon liked this country best when he permitted himself to admit it. The high fells of Thornfell, all scree and granite and sudden grass, the wind combing through in long silver strokes. Nobody came up here but smugglers and shepherds and the occasional fool of a poet. Nobody wrote laws up here. Up here, the world was still mostly run by weather.

He was half a day's walk from the drop point, camped in a hollow below a leaning shelf of rock with the mules picketed and a small fire going — small, always small; smoke was a signature — when the wolf came out of the dusk.

Gideon knew she was there before he saw her. Hesper's head came up, and one of the ponies blew softly through her nostrils, and the night arranged itself differently, the way it does when something large and careful enters it. Then she simply appeared at the edge of the firelight: a grey female, lean from winter, ribs showing under fur gone rough at the haunch, one ear tattered from some old fight.

"Evening," Gideon said.

*Small fire,* the wolf observed. *Small fire means frightened man.*

"Fires are expensive this year."

*Man who lies to wolves is a fool,* she said, ambling closer with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once needed to hurry. *Man who lies gently is a friend. You lie gently.*

"Thank you. I think."

She settled at the edge of the light, folding her legs beneath her, and Gideon threw her the heel of his sausage without pretending to himself that he hadn't been planning to. She caught it, swallowed it whole, and watched him with yellow eyes while the fire popped and spat.

*You taste of worry,* she said.

"I'm told worry improves with age."

*The iron men came through the green pass three nights past,* the wolf went on, in the flat way wolves state things, no cruelty in it, no comfort either. *They took the shepherd on the middle fell. The one who whistled to the foxes. They put him on a horse with his hands tied, and the foxes followed the horses for two days, and then there was road, and then there was nothing for the foxes to follow.*

Gideon sat very still. The fire cracked. Somewhere above, loose stone ticked down a slope.

The shepherd on the middle fell. He'd met the man twice — a quiet fellow, weathered as a fence post, whose foxes brought him gloves people lost and eggs people dropped. Whistling to foxes wasn't even speaking, properly. Not the way Gideon spoke. It was less. And they'd taken him anyway.

"They're coming up here," Gideon said. "Into the high routes."

*The mountains have always hidden your kind,* the wolf said. *Now the mountains are being counted. Stone by stone.* She yawned, enormously, showing him the whole architecture of her opinion about men with irons and ropes. *I don't care what you are, meat-giver. But you should care. You smell of the speaking. Everything in these hills knows it. The birds follow you like priests. The mules argue with you like lawyers.*

"That obvious?"

*To us,* she said. *And soon, to them.* She rose, stretched, and melted back toward the dark treeline, pausing once to look over her shoulder. *Go the long ways, meat-giver. Go in the rain, when scent dies. And stop talking aloud where the echoes carry.*

Then she was gone, and Gideon sat alone with his small fire and his heart going like a fist on a door.

It was at precisely that moment — because the universe had a sense of humor, and it was aimed at him — that he heard boots on the trail below the hollow.

Not one pair. Several. And the jingle of harness, and the dull knock of metal against metal that meant iron men, and a dog's low, interrogative sniffing.

Gideon did the arithmetic in the space of one breath. Eight mules of contraband, a fire, a hollow with exactly one exit, and a crown patrol coming up the switchback with a hound. His hand went to the fire, smothering it under the flat stone he kept for the purpose, and he was moving before the embers finished dying, murmuring to the mules — *quiet, stand quiet, breathe slow, we are stones, we have always been stones* — and the mules, bless their stubborn hearts, went still and silent as the hillside itself.

Which was when the hound crested the trail and looked straight at him, and Gideon, exhausted, rattled, thinking about shepherds on the middle fell and ropes and fires, did the single most natural thing in his life.

He spoke back.

*Nothing here,* he told the hound, calm as still water, pushing the thought gentle and wide, the way you'd lay a blanket over a restless child. *No men here. Only stones and wind. Go home, good dog. Go home.*

The hound stopped. Its tail moved once, uncertainly. Behind it, voices — *"What's he got?" "Scent's gone strange, sarge."* — and the hound sat down on the trail, blinking, torn between its nose and the warm blanket of suggestion settling over its mind.

And then, from the darkness of the treeline above the hollow, a wolf howled — long, rising, unmistakably deliberate — and the hound's training drowned Gideon's suggestion in an instant. It was on its feet, hackles up, answering instinct screaming *wolf, wolf, enemy*, and the patrol's attention went where the dog went.

"Wolf!" someone shouted. "There's your scent, sarge — big one, too. Loose on the fells!"

Boots pounded away up the trail after noise and drama and a grey shape that led them singing away over the ridgeline, and Gideon stood in the dark among his motionless mules with his heart somewhere around his boot heels, waiting until the last echo of the chase faded before he let out a breath that shook on the way out.

The wolf reappeared perhaps an hour later, trotting into the hollow with her tongue lolling, insufferably pleased with herself.

*They are chasing my cousin's echo now,* she reported. *He is enjoying it. He says the short one throws stones badly.*

Gideon slid down against the picketed mule and laughed — a shaky, disbelieving laugh that came out closer to something else entirely, and that he covered, as he covered everything, with his hand over his mouth.

"Why?" he asked her, when he could speak. "Why help me?"

The wolf sat and regarded him for a long moment, head tilted, firelight-less dark pressing in around them both.

*Because you are the only man in these mountains who asks questions in our tongue,* she said. *And because the counting has begun, meat-giver, and when the counting finishes, the list will not stop at shepherds.* She stood, shaking herself. *The foxes from the middle fell came to me yesterday. They had nothing left to follow but a story. So now I am following it too.*

"What story?"

*That the roads are closing,* the wolf said. *All of them. Even the long ones. Even yours.* And she padded to the edge of the dark, and paused, and added, almost as an afterthought, in the tone wolves use for the things they mean most:

*A storm comes behind me. In it, something small. Something that speaks. It is running, meat-giver, and it is running toward your door.*

He opened his mouth to ask what she meant — *what thing, what door, whose door* — but the night had already swallowed her, and the wind came down off the peaks carrying the first fat drops of rain, and above the hollow the clouds rolled in heavy and black over Thornfell, gathering like a debt come due.
