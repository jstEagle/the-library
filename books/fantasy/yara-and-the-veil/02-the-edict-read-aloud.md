## 2. The Edict Read Aloud

The rain had followed Gideon down out of the passes for two days, thinning from a howl to a drizzle by the time he reached Harrowgate, as if even the weather lost its temper in the lowlands and settled instead for sulking.

He didn't mind. Rain meant mud, and mud meant no footprints worth reading, and the mule—Bramble, though her previous owner had called her Gertrude and she still considered that an insult of the highest order—walked better in the wet, her hooves soft on the road.

*You're slowing down,* he told her, quietly, under the sound of the drizzle. *Left hind. Don't argue with me.*

*I am not slowing down,* Bramble said, which was what she always said, and then she shortened her stride on the left hind exactly enough to prove she'd been hiding it. *There's a farrier in this town who owes me better oats than last time.*

"There is," Gideon murmured, and only realized he'd spoken aloud when a woman passing under her umbrella gave him a look—not suspicious, not yet, just the mild look one gives a man who talks to himself. He touched his cap and kept walking. That was the trick of it. You learned to shape every word into something a passerby could file away as *eccentric* rather than *criminal*. Eccentric got you invited to dinners where you were a curiosity. Criminal, these days, got you other kinds of invitations entirely.

Harrowgate looked the same as it had in spring. That was the thing that sat wrong in his chest before he could even name it. Same crooked lanes, same slate roofs dripping, same smell of coal smoke and wet wool and the fish carts down by the water. Same old men outside the Wheelwright's Rest arguing about the same three topics in rotation: the price of grain, the state of the bridge, and whether Aldous Pike's new wife had actually run off with a tinker or merely with the idea of one.

Gideon had been coming to Harrowgate since he was seventeen years old, when Corvin first put him on a road and said, *You've got a face nobody remembers and a voice everything does. Use both.* He'd drunk in the Rest a hundred times. He'd helped deliver calves for Widow Marsh. He'd once talked a suicidal cart horse back off a bridge in the middle of market day without anyone noticing, which remained, in his own private accounting, the finest piece of work he'd ever done, and no one would ever know to thank him for it.

Home wasn't the word for Harrowgate. But it was adjacent to home. It was the town where home came to sell its wool.

He left Bramble at the farrier's—there was a brief negotiation involving oats, an apology owed for the name Gertrude, and a promise regarding her left hind—and walked into the square with his collar up and his business, ostensibly, being a delivery of mountain tobacco to Tam Hask's shop near the church.

That was when he heard the bell.

Not the church bell. The crier's hand bell, small and bright and wrong, cutting through the market murmur like a knife through bread. Gideon stopped in the middle of the lane. A woman behind him muttered at his elbows. He didn't move.

The crier climbed the steps of the market cross, shook his bell until the whole square had turned toward him, and unrolled a paper with the particular ceremony of a man who has been told the news is important and intends to be important along with it.

"By order of His Majesty's Crown," he called, "and by decree of the Lord Protector's Office of Purity and Order, hear now the Veil Edict, proclaimed in every town and parish of the realm!"

Gideon's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, the way it did when a mountain path gave way half a step beneath your boot.

*"Let it be known,"* the crier read, *"that among the subjects of the Crown there dwell persons of corrupted gift, who hold converse with the beasts of field and forest. Such commerce is unnatural. Such tongues are unclean. Where such speech exists, disorder follows, as night follows day."*

The square went quiet in stages. First the fish sellers stopped calling their prices. Then the children stopped chasing each other around the well. Then even the argument outside the Wheelwright's Rest wound down, three voices trailing off mid-sentence, and Aldous Pike's mouth stayed open on some unfinished opinion about tinkers.

Gideon stood very still. He had known this was coming—he'd heard whispers in the high country for a month, carried up the passes by smugglers and sheep traders, and once, more usefully, by a raven who had watched a proclamation in Kingsreach and repeated fragments of it with a mimic's cruel precision. Knowing a thing was coming, he had learned, prepared him almost not at all for its arrival.

*"All such persons,"* the crier continued, *"shall surrender themselves to the lawful authorities within forty days of this proclamation, that they may receive the Crown's mercy: the holy curing of their affliction, administered without cost to the subject or his family."*

*Curing.* The word landed in Gideon's ear and sat there, foreign as a coin from a country that didn't exist. He thought of a wolf in the high passes, three nights ago, telling him about her pups in a language older than any edict. Unclean tongue. Corrupted gift. He tried to fit those words over the memory and found they wouldn't lie flat; they sat on top of it like a lid on a pot too full.

Around him, Harrowgate began to react. Not all at once. Not dramatically. Harrowgate was too old and too tired for drama. It reacted the way a body reacts to cold water—one small shock at a time.

*"Those who do not surrender themselves,"* the crier went on, *"shall be declared hunted. Any subject providing information regarding the identity or whereabouts of a beast-speaker shall render loyal service to the Crown and shall be entitled to the reward posted herein. Any subject harboring such a person—"*

He paused. Gideon watched the man's eyes move down the page, and saw, strangely, a flicker of reluctance there, quickly mastered.

*"—shall be held complicit, and shall share the fate of the one they shelter."*

Silence, then. Real silence, except the rain on slate and somewhere a gull complaining about the interruption to commerce.

Then a voice from the crowd—a woman's, steady, the kind of voice used for asking after neighbors' health: "What does the curing involve?"

The crier looked up from his paper. "Madam, I merely proclaim it."

"Yes, but you've read it. What does it involve?"

"I believe it involves prayer," the crier said, in the tone of a man describing a procedure he has elected not to think about. "And certain... measures."

"What measures?"

"The Office of Purity has specialists," the crier said, and rolled the paper up with finality, as if rolling the questions up with it. "Inquiries beyond the scope of proclamation may be directed to the district office. In Kingsreach." He paused, then added, because he was human underneath the bell and the paper, and quietly: "It's a long way to send inquiries, madam."

"It is," the widow Marsh agreed, from beside Gideon, and he startled, because he hadn't heard her come up. She was seventy-three and moved like a rumor through her own town. Her eyes were fixed on the crier, but her voice, when it came again, was pitched for Gideon alone. "You've gone white as a herring, lad."

"Tobacco delivery," Gideon said. "Long road."

"Mm," said Widow Marsh, who had watched him talk to her cows for eleven years and had never once mentioned it, and whose silence on the subject he was suddenly, desperately reevaluating.

* * *

He made the tobacco delivery in a fog. Tam Hask counted the bales, paid him in coin that clinked wrong somehow—too much, Gideon thought distantly, or maybe just differently—and asked if he'd be staying the night at the Rest.

"No," Gideon heard himself say. "Road's calling."

"Roads don't call," Tam said, with the ghost of a smile that didn't reach anywhere important. "Men just listen for them."

Outside the shop, the square had resumed its business, but it was a different business now. Gideon stood under the eaves and watched, and what he watched was not the market but the looking.

That was the thing about the Edict that no proclamation had managed to convey. It wasn't the words on the paper. It was the way people's heads moved afterward. For thirty years, Gideon had survived in a country full of neighbors by being unremarkable, and the whole architecture of that survival rested on a simple truth: nobody looks carefully at anybody. People see each other in summaries. *The smith. The widow. The boy who talks to himself.*

But a reward changes the arithmetic. A reward turns summaries into ledgers. And so Gideon stood in the rain and watched Aldous Pike look at the miller's son—look, actually *look*, a long slow inventory of a young man everyone in town knew fed pigeons with an unusual degree of conversation—and watched Pike catch himself doing it, and glance away, ashamed. And watched him look again anyway.

He watched two women by the well fall silent mid-gossip and study each other instead, each with the same careful new question in her face: *what don't I know about you?*

He watched a shepherd named Coll Dunny, whom Gideon had known for fifteen years, whose dogs Gideon had quietly trained through the man's own hands without his ever quite realizing why his animals turned out so obedient—watched Coll stand in the middle of the square with his crook in his fist and an expression like a man doing sums that keep coming out wrong.

And Gideon understood, standing there getting steadily wetter, that the Edict didn't need to hunt anyone. Not really. Its genius was simpler and crueller than hounds and ropes. It had just taught an entire town to look at itself sideways. Every friendship in Harrowgate had overnight acquired a second, hidden ledger entry. Every kindness was now potentially a confession waiting to be priced.

*Any subject harboring such a person shall share the fate of the one they shelter.*

Forty days. And a reward. Gideon thought about the number of people who knew approximately what he was. Widow Marsh. Tam, maybe. Old Fennick at the farrier's, certainly—the man had heard him negotiate with Bramble for a decade and charged him accordingly, which was to say, fairly, which in the farriering trade was its own kind of confession of knowledge.

Good people. All of them good people. And every single one of them now worth a reward for betraying him, and facing ruin for not.

"That's the clever part," he murmured to himself, and then flinched at his own voice, aloud, in public, in a town where talking to yourself had just become a symptom.

"You're doing it again," said a voice near his knee.

A cat sat under the eaves beside him, a big grey torn tomcat with one ear crumpled from some ancient disagreement, whom Gideon had known for years under the name His Excellency, bestowed by the cook at the Rest and ratified by general usage. His Excellency was washing one paw with the air of a creature profoundly unbothered by empires and edicts alike.

*You're doing it again,* he repeated. *Talking in the flat speech in the open square. Honestly, Gideon, for a clever man you have the operational security of a barnyard hen.*

"Noted," Gideon said under his breath, lips barely moving. "Anything moving?"

*Everything moves. It's a market day. If you mean anything interesting—riders came through two days ago. Grey cloaks. Measured horses. They asked questions at the toll house and wrote things down. They're asking about names.*

Gideon felt the rain go cold on the back of his neck, colder than rain ought to go. "What kind of names?"

*The kind that get written down,* said His Excellency, and returned to his paw, having delivered, in his view, sufficient intelligence for one morning.

Grey cloaks. Measured horses. Questions and lists. Gideon had spent his whole life learning to read sign—tracks in snow, bent grass, the temper of ravens—and here was sign written across his own town square in invisible ink: *the hunt has already started, and the list already has columns, and you are not hearing about this on the first page.*

He should leave today. Tonight at the latest. Back up into the high passes, where the Edict was a rumor and the crown's men moved like tourists and every stone knew his name in the good way.

But the passes were also where Corvin ran his routes, and Corvin would have heard the proclamation too, and Gideon had known the man for thirty years and loved him for twenty-five of them, and trusted him completely right up until the part of his brain that did arithmetic for a living pointed out that trust and arithmetic had never once been introduced.

*Go up and talk to your mountains,* His Excellency advised, reading his stillness the way cats read everything. *You're no good to anyone standing in the rain composing your will in public.*

"I'm composing nothing," Gideon said. "I'm deciding which direction is least likely to kill me."

*Ah,* said the cat. *So, the usual decision. My professional recommendation is indoors, near a fire, but nobody ever takes it.*

* * *

He meant to leave by sundown. He got as far as collecting Bramble from Fennick's stable, settling her account, and loading the last of his gear.

Fennick walked him out, wiping his hands on his leather apron, and stopped at the gate. He was a squat man built like his own anvil, sixty years old, and he had never in Gideon's memory said anything kind without disguising it as complaint.

"Your mule's sound now," he said. "Left hind was just a stone bruise. I fixed it. Didn't charge extra, on account of you being the only customer I've ever had who thanks the animal by name and means it." He looked past Gideon at the wet street, emptying toward evening, at the notice that had already been nailed to the post beside the toll house, ink still running slightly in the drizzle. Forty days. A reward figure printed neatly at the bottom, the way you'd price a ewe.

"Fennick," Gideon said carefully.

"Don't," said Fennick. "Whatever you were going to say—don't say it out loud in my yard, in my town, on the first day of a thing like this." He shifted his weight. He was looking at the middle distance, at the notice, at forty days. "I'll tell you what I told the crier, when he asked me after the meeting if I had any inquiries. I told him I keep animals for a living and I've never met one yet that needed curing of anything except worms." He finally looked at Gideon, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and afraid, all three at once, which Gideon thought might be the most honest expression he'd ever seen on a human face. "Forty days is a long time, boy. Roads go north through the passes. Some roads don't come back. Take the one that doesn't."

Gideon's throat had gone tight. "Fennick—"

"And that's all I'll say, and if anyone asks, I said your mule bites." Fennick turned and stomped back toward his forge, and paused at the door, and added, gruffly, to the wall: "She doesn't bite. You know she doesn't bite. But they don't."

Gideon stood in the yard a moment longer, rain dripping off his hood, and understood that he had just received, from a man risking his family to give it, the kindest sentence ever constructed out of complaints about a mule.

He led Bramble out through the west gate as the light failed. The high road to the passes ran north, and he took it, because where else was there—but he hadn't gone half a mile before he stopped, because the ravens were talking.

They'd been following him since the toll house, three of them, riding the wind above the road, and their conversation had the particular crispness of birds with news.

*The grey riders,* one said. *The ones who write. They've gone up-country.*

*Which road?* another demanded.

*The Thorn road. Toward the high villages.*

Toward the high villages. Toward the scattered homesteads above the tree line where families kept to themselves and raised quiet children who grew up knowing things about foxes that no one taught them.

Gideon stood in the darkening road with Bramble's reins loose in his hand, and the arithmetic he was so good at—the smuggler's arithmetic, distances and loads and likelihoods—turned over and produced an answer he didn't want.

Somewhere up in those hills, in some smoke-dark cabin with rain on the roof, there was a child—or there might be, there could be—who talked to animals the way he did. Who'd been born with the unclean tongue, the corrupted gift, the thing that had made his own childhood a long exercise in hiding. Who had forty days, minus two, before someone with a list came up the track.

He could go north to Corvin, to the routes, to safety and business as usual. Or he could go up.

*Well?* said Bramble, stamping once against the cold. *Are we standing in roads all night, or do you finally have a destination?*

Gideon looked up at the black shoulder of the mountains, where the storm was building, and heard, very faintly on the wind, something that might have been thunder and might not.

"We're going up," he said.

And far off, in the high country, through the rain and the rising wind, something answered—a thin, high cry that was not a bird and not a wolf, and that carried, impossibly, across five miles of darkening valley, a note of a child's voice in it, calling for help in the only language that had ever been safe to speak.

Gideon's blood went cold, and then, immediately after, went decided.

"Bramble," he said. "Run."
