## 3. A Child at the Door

The storm came down off the high passes like something with a grudge.

Gideon heard it coming an hour before it arrived — not in the wind, though the wind was loud enough, but in the birds. The jays went silent all at once, the way they did when the mountain told them to get under cover, and old Barley, his mule, pressed herself against the barn wall and informed him, with the flat certainty of her kind, that the sky intended murder.

*I know,* Gideon said back, in the wordless way that wasn't words at all. *Get inside. I'll see to the shutters.*

She flicked one ear at him, which from Barley was a whole paragraph of commentary, and let herself into the barn. She'd been doing that more lately — talking to him freely, when for years she'd kept her opinions to herself unless asked. Animals were like that. They knew when a man was frightened, even if he never said so, and Gideon had been frightened since the day the crier came to Harrowgate.

He worked the shutters while the first fat drops hit the roof like thrown gravel. The cabin sat alone above the treeline where no road went — deliberately so. A man who could speak to beasts learned early to live where witnesses were few and four-legged. He had wood stacked deep, water drawn, smoke cured meat hanging in the cold cellar. He could outlast any storm.

What he couldn't outlast, he'd begun to suspect, was the Edict.

* * *

The cat noticed the girl before he did.

Her name was Ember, though Gideon hadn't named her; she'd simply arrived one winter, thin as a whip, and appointed herself mistress of the hearth through sheer force of will. She was lying on the warm stones by the fire that evening, storm rattling the windows, when her head came up. Not lazily, the way cats wake. All at once, ears forward, tail still.

Gideon looked up from his mending. "What is it?"

Ember didn't answer him. Cats rarely bothered. Instead she flowed toward the door — low, belly nearly brushing the floorboards, in the way she moved when something interested rather than threatened her — and sat before it, staring at the bottom gap where the wind whistled through.

Then Gideon heard it too. A scratching. Small, rhythmic, patient. Not the scrabble of a pine marten after the meat stores. Something weaker. Something that scratched three times, stopped as though gathering courage, then scratched again.

He should have opened the window instead and looked out with a loaded crossbow in hand. That was the sensible thing, the safe thing, the thing a hunted man did when anything knocked during a storm. Crown patrols didn't ride in weather like this, but desperate people did desperate things, and there were men in Harrowgate who'd sell their mothers for the bounty on a beast-speaker's head.

He lit the lamp anyway and opened the door.

The wind took it and tried to tear it off its hinges, and what the lamplight found huddled against his doorframe was not a wolf, not a soldier, not anything he'd braced for. It was a child. A girl of maybe nine or ten, soaked to the skin, lips gone blue, wearing a coat that had been made for someone twice her size and patched by someone who loved her. Her eyes — huge, dark, fever-bright — fixed on him for half a heartbeat, and then slid past him entirely.

To Ember.

"You're warm," the girl said. Not to Gideon. To the cat. "You're so warm. Please."

And Ember — who had ignored barons' children, merchants, and Gideon himself for the better part of a decade — stepped forward and pressed her head against the girl's dripping hand.

Gideon stood very still in the doorway with the storm screaming at his back, and felt the ground tilt under a life he'd thought was settled.

Because he'd heard it. Plainly. Not the tone and intent that any sensitive soul might catch in an animal's posture, not the guesswork of a lonely man projecting meaning onto a cat. Words. The girl had spoken *to* the cat, in that same wordless way that wasn't words, and the cat had understood, and the cat had answered — a thread of warmth and welcome that Gideon felt pass between them like heat off a forge.

A child. A beast-speaker. At his door.

*They're hunting us now,* he thought. *All of us.*

"Inside," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he meant it. "Before you drown standing up."

* * *

He got her dry the way you get anything done in a storm: quickly and without ceremony. Stripped the sodden coat, wrapped her in his oldest blanket, set her by the fire with broth he heated because his hands needed something to do besides shake. She drank it in small careful sips, watching him over the rim of the bowl with those dark eyes, and Ember curled in her lap purring like a millwheel, which was frankly insulting. Eight years of sardines and Gideon got a tail-flick on a good day.

"What's your name?" he asked.

She considered him for a long moment. Weighing him, he realized. The way Barley weighed a new pack-saddle.

"Wren," she said finally.

"Just Wren?"

"Wren," she agreed, as if that settled it, and went back to the broth.

He didn't push. He knew about names. His own had been given by a smuggler kingpin who'd found a boy alone in the high passes talking to goats, and Corvin Pemberton had never once asked what Gideon's mother had called him before the fever took everyone else. Names were things you kept small, in their world. Things you kept hidden.

"How far did you walk?" he asked instead.

"A long time." A pause. "Since the fire."

Something cold slid down Gideon's spine that had nothing to do with the draft under the door. "There was a fire?"

Wren's hands went still around the bowl. Ember butted her chin, and the girl's fingers resumed their slow stroking, automatic, the two of them comforting each other without either seeming to notice.

"They came at night," she said, in the flat reciting voice children use for things too large to hold any other way. "The ones in grey. They said Mama talked to animals, which was true, she talked to the chickens all the time, but everybody talks to chickens, only she really talked to them." Her voice caught and steadied itself with visible effort. "They took her away in a cart. And then the house burned."

"Was it an accident?"

"I don't know." She looked up at him, and there was nothing childish in her face at all. "I ran. Ember would've run with me but she lives here, I think. I saw her in my head on the road, all warm. So I came here."

Gideon set down his own untouched bowl. "You saw her. In your head. A cat you'd never met, miles away."

"She's loud," Wren said, as if this explained everything, and perhaps it did. "Cats are mostly quiet but she shouts. Like a kettle."

Despite everything — despite the fear sitting in his chest like swallowed river-stone — Gideon felt his mouth twitch. "She does shout. Yes."

"The man behind me doesn't shout. He whispers." Wren's eyes went to the shuttered window, to the black beyond it. "Hounds. He has hounds that listen. One of them heard me talk to a fox, three days ago, and it howled, and then the riders came. I've been running since. They're close. Maybe a day. Maybe less."

The fire popped. Rain hammered the roof like a fist demanding entry.

Gideon rose and checked the shutters he'd already checked, latched the latch he'd already latched, and stood a moment with his hand flat against the cold oak, listening to the storm and hearing underneath it, imagined or not, the far-off music of hounds.

This was the arithmetic, then. The sum he'd been avoiding since the crier read the Edict in Harrowgate square. A child asleep under his roof, a child whose mind rang like a bell to every creature within ten miles. If the hunters followed her here — and they would, because hounds that listened for voices would find her eventually no matter how far she ran — they would find not one beast-speaker but two. And worse than two: a grown man, known in every lowland market from Harrowgate to the river, harboring the very thing the crown had sworn to cure or kill.

Taking her in was a death sentence. Not maybe, not possibly. A certainty, deferred. He'd spent forty years keeping his head down and his gift secret and his heart locked away behind a door even he barely opened, and all of it — the caution, the silence, the long gray years of being fluent in a language that could hang him — all of it ended the moment he'd opened that door instead of the window.

He could still send her out. Dawn was hours off; the storm would cover her; she might survive. Might. A starving child, alone, with trained killers a day behind her and winter coming down the passes like a debt collector.

Ember looked up at him from Wren's lap. In the private language of cats, she conveyed an opinion with perfect clarity: *Well?*

"Oh, hush," Gideon muttered.

"What?" Wren asked, blinking sleepily.

"Nothing. The cat's giving me orders."

"She does that." Wren smiled — the first one, small and cracked and real — and then her eyes closed and she was asleep between one breath and the next, the way only children and soldiers can sleep, dropping straight through exhaustion into somewhere deep and safe. Or trusting. Which was either the same thing, at her age, or the saddest difference in the world.

* * *

He sat up all night. Partly watchfulness, partly honesty with himself: he couldn't have slept beside that small breathing shape if the mountain itself had ordered him to.

Toward the small hours the storm began to spend itself, the wind dropping from scream to mutter, and in the quiet Gideon heard Barley stir in the barn and send him a sleepy thread of inquiry — *all well?* — and he sent back *all well*, and marveled, as he did most nights, at how much comfort could travel along so thin a wire.

Then, nearer than Barley, fainter than breath: another thread. Small, flickering, worn down to almost nothing by hunger and terror and a hundred miles of road. But unmistakably shaped like a question, and unmistakably aimed at him.

*Are you like me?* the sleeping girl asked the darkness, without waking. The way you ask a thing you've been afraid to ask awake. *Is there anyone else like me?*

Gideon closed his eyes.

*Yes,* he answered, knowing full well she might not remember it come morning, and not caring. *Yes, little wren. There's one other. He's old, and he's scared, and he's right here.*

Outside, the last rain fell soft on the roof. Somewhere down the valley, distant and patient, a hound gave voice once — a single long note, carried on the wind like a promise.

Gideon looked at the sleeping child, at the cat curled tight against her heartbeat, and understood that the choice had already been made. It had been made the moment he opened the door wide instead of narrow. Everything left was just deciding what kind of man died for it.

He went to the window, eased the shutter a finger's width, and watched the eastern sky where the storm was breaking apart.

Two days' hard ride south, if Maren Ostwick was still alive, if the disgraced old scholar still kept her books in that crooked house below the ridge — there might be answers. There might be a plan. There was certainly a ledger, if rumor spoke true, of every gifted soul the crown hadn't yet found.

And the ravens — the ravens had been saying a strange word all week, over and over, passed bird to bird down the mountains like contraband. He hadn't paid it mind. Ravens exaggerated.

But the word, he remembered now, turning it over in the dark: *Veil.*
