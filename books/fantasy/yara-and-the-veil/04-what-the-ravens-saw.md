## 4. What the Ravens Saw

Wren woke to the smell of porridge and the sound of a cat arguing.

Not hissing, exactly. Arguing. A low, conversational grumble from somewhere near the hearth, and then — and this was the strange part, the part that made her open her eyes before she'd decided she was brave enough — a voice answering it. A man's voice, rough and quiet, the way people talk when they think no one's listening.

"No, you can't have the bacon end. She needs feeding more than you do."

A pause. A chirp of protest.

"Because I said so, that's why. You're a cat. Cats don't get opinions about bacon."

Another pause, longer, distinctly offended.

"Fine. *Half* the bacon end. But you're a menace and I want it noted."

Wren lay still under the wool blanket and listened to this with her heart going like a rabbit's. Because she knew that language. Not the words — anyone could say *bacon* — but the shape underneath them, the tilt of meaning that wasn't meant for human ears at all. He was talking to the cat the way her grandmother used to talk to the bees. The way Mama had talked to the old plow horse, Bessie, right up until the soldiers came.

The cat was a huge gray tom with one torn ear, sprawled on the hearthstones like he owned the deed to the cabin. When Wren shifted, he turned his head and looked at her with yellow eyes that seemed, frankly, smug.

"You're awake," Gideon said. He was at the stove, broad-shouldered and careful-moving, the kind of man who seemed to apologize to furniture just by walking past it. "Good. You slept two days. I was starting to take it personally."

"Two days?" Her voice came out as a croak.

"Storm kept you down, and hunger did the rest." He set a bowl on the table — porridge with honey stirred through it, actual honey — and pulled out the chair across from her without sitting in it. Like he was giving her the whole room to herself. "Eat slow. Your stomach will lie to you about how much room it has."

She ate. It was the best thing she'd ever tasted, which she knew wasn't true, because Mama's barley stew had been better, but grief does funny things to porridge.

The cat jumped up onto the bench beside her, close enough that his warmth pressed against her arm. He looked at her expectantly.

"He wants you to know," Gideon said, with the air of a long-suffering translator, "that he found you first. That you were brought here under *his* authority, and that any gratitude you feel should be directed accordingly."

Wren looked at the cat. The cat looked at her.

"*Bracken*," she said softly. "*Big ears. Fat belly. Old soldier.*"

The cat's purr started up like a small landslide.

Gideon had gone very still by the stove. "You can hear him too."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it. She took another spoonful of porridge instead, and watched him decide what to do with the fact of her. She'd seen adults make that face before. In her village there had been eleven of the gifted, counting her mama and her gran, and every one of them wore that same look around strangers: hope and fear wrestling behind the eyes, each one waiting for the other to lose.

"You're safe here," Gideon said finally. "As safe as anywhere is. Eat your porridge."

Outside, the storm had passed, and sunlight came through the window in pale bars. Somewhere up on the ridge, a raven called — three notes, descending, the sound that had followed her all the way down the mountain.

It took another day before she told him anything, and even then it came out sideways, the way truth does when it's been sitting in a wound.

They were splitting firewood in the yard, or rather Gideon was splitting it and Wren was carrying it, having insisted with the ferocity of the truly proud that she earn her keep. Bracken supervised from atop the woodpile, offering commentary neither of them asked for.

"They came at dawn," Wren said, to the woodpile more than to Gideon. "Inquisitor Vess's riders. Grey cloaks. Nobody ran, because where would you run to? It's Thornfell."

Gideon's axe paused mid-swing.

"There was a list," she went on. "There's always a list. They read names off it in the square, and if your name was on it, you got to stand in front of everybody while they put the iron collar on you and read the Edict again. Then they took you to the tithe barn." She swallowed. "Mama was third name. Gran was fourth. Old Tamsin the beekeeper was fifth. There were nine names. Nine of us in the whole village, and they knew every single one."

"How did they know?"

"Somebody told." Wren shrugged, and hated how small the shrug made her feel. "That's the thing everyone argues about now. Who told. Because the list was *right*. Down to little Josper who could only talk to chickens, and he was seven."

The axe came down. The log split clean.

"What happened in the tithe barn?" Gideon asked, very quietly.

"You know what happened."

"I need to hear you say it. If I'm going to help you — and I am, that's already decided, Bracken chose you and I've learned not to argue with him — then I need to know what's following you."

She set down the armload of wood. Her hands were shaking, so she watched them instead of looking at him.

"The cure," she said. "That's what they call it. They sit you in a chair and there's a man with a tuning fork and a bell, and they ring the bell and strike the fork and ask you questions, over and over, until the voices go away. Except sometimes it doesn't work right away, so they do it again, and again, and the ones it doesn't work for—" Her breath caught. "Tamsin fought. She was sixty-three and she fought them like a mountain lion, and they tied her to the barn door and burned it. With her in it. And they made us all watch, and Vess stood there in her grey cloak and said it was mercy. She said the fire was *mercy*, because a broken gift is worse than none."

The yard was very quiet. Even Bracken had stopped talking.

"And you ran," Gideon said.

"Mama told me to run." The words came out fast now, tumbling, the dam breaking after four days of holding. "She grabbed me and shoved me out the back and told me to go north and find the ravens, because the ravens would tell me things, and she said—" Wren's voice cracked clean through. "She said *find the Veil*. She said her own mama told her stories about it, a door out of Thornfell, a way past the mountains that the crown doesn't know, that nobody knows, that the erased people went through a generation ago. Everyone says it's a story. Everyone says there's nothing outside Thornfell. But Mama said stories are how the truth hides when it's being hunted, and she told me to find it, and I don't even know if it's *real*, I just—"

She was crying now, hard, the ugly crying of a child who has been strong far past her ration. Gideon crouched down to her level, laid the axe aside, and didn't touch her — just stayed there, solid as a fencepost in a gale, until the worst of it passed.

"A door out of Thornfell," he repeated slowly. "The Veil."

"That's what it's called. In the stories. A seam in the world, past the high passes, where Thornfell stops being Thornfell." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "Gran said the crown erased the country on purpose. Drew new maps that stop at the mountains. Said there are people out there who never surrendered, never got listed, never got cured. Living outside the law like it never touched them."

Gideon sat back on his heels. And Wren, who had spent her short life learning to read the gifted and the ungifted alike, watched something move across his face that she couldn't name at first. It took her a moment to recognize it, because she'd only ever seen it aimed at other people.

Hope. Terrified, disbelieving, furious hope.

Then his head tilted, slightly, the way it did when he was listening to something she couldn't hear — except this time it wasn't the wind.

"Wait," he said. "Say that part again. About the ravens."

"The ravens? Mama said—"

"No, before. You've been here four days. Have you noticed the ravens?"

Wren blinked. Now that he mentioned it — there had been ravens. Two of them, big glossy ones, who had appeared on the ridge above the cabin the morning after the storm and had not, in her observation, appeared to do anything whatsoever except sit there looking important.

"They've been following me since the village," she said. "I thought they were— well. Omens."

"Ravens aren't omens. Ravens are gossips with wings." Gideon stood, and there was a new energy in him, restless, coiling. "There's a pair that works the high passes. I pay them in suet and secrets. Three days ago they started acting strange — flying a pattern, coming back agitated, doing their *come-see-come-see* call every evening at dusk. I thought it was weather. Or wolves." He turned to her. "What color were the riders' cloaks?"

"Grey."

"How many?"

"I saw eight. There might have been more."

"When did they come to your village?"

"Nineteen days ago. Maybe twenty."

Gideon closed his eyes, and Wren watched his lips move faintly, counting backward. Then he whistled — two sharp notes — and both ravens dropped off the ridge immediately, gliding down into the yard like they'd been waiting all week for an invitation.

They landed on the fence rail, folded their wings, and regarded the humans with the air of officials arriving to deliver bad news they had personally composed.

"All right," Gideon said to them. "Show me."

And the ravens *talked*. Not in words, exactly — raven-speech was a tumble of clicks and croaks and wing-signals, more felt than heard — but Wren caught pieces of it, the way you catch pieces of a conversation through a wall. *Grey men. Horses. Fire-smell. Roads. Roads. Roads.* One of them hopped twice and spread its wings wide, wide, wider — *many* — and then pointed its beak south, toward the lowlands, and made a sound like a bell struck once, hard.

Wren went cold all over. "That's the tuning fork," she whispered. "That's the sound the cure makes."

"I know," Gideon said grimly. "I've heard it from the ridgeline twice this season. I thought it was patrols doing drills." He paced a few steps, raven eyes tracking him. "How long," he asked them, "have the grey men been on the roads?"

The answer came back in a rush of wingbeats and croaks, and whatever it was, it drained the color out of his face.

"Wren. How many villages like yours are in the southern valleys?"

"I don't — a dozen? More?"

"They've been riding circuit for six weeks." His voice was flat and controlled, the way a man sounds when he's holding a lot of weight very still. "Six weeks of lists and barns and bells, and my ravens have been trying to tell me since the first frost, and I thought they wanted more suet." He laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "I speak beast, and I still manage to not listen."

One of the ravens — the bigger one, with a scarred beak — hopped closer along the rail and fixed Gideon with one black eye. Then it said something short and deliberate, three syllables, and Wren felt the meaning land in her chest like a stone dropping into a well, clear all the way down.

*You're next.*

"They know about you," she breathed.

"They've known about me for years. I've just been careful enough to be worth ignoring." Gideon looked south, toward the lowlands, toward Harrowgate where he clearly went to sell whatever a man like him sold. His jaw worked. "That's over now. Taking you in — that was the last careful thing I'll ever do."

He was quiet a long moment. The wind moved through the pines. Bracken came down off the woodpile and leaned against Wren's leg, heavy and warm, and she scratched his torn ear because it steadied her hands.

"The Veil," Gideon said at last, half to himself. "A door out of Thornfell. Twenty years I've run these mountains and I've never heard so much as a campfire rumor of it. But the erased country, the dead villages, the places maps pretend don't exist—" He shook his head slowly. "Old Maren Ostwick used to mutter about the blank spaces. Said the blank spaces were the most honest parts of any map. She'd know. Or she'd know who'd know."

"Who's Maren Ostwick?"

"Someone who owes me a debt she doesn't know about yet." He turned and looked at Wren properly then, and she saw that the fear hadn't left his face — it had just been promoted, moved to the back office, given a desk behind the resolve. "Here's what happens now. We rest tonight. Tomorrow we start asking questions I swore I'd never ask out loud. And when Vess's riders come up this road — because they will come, the ravens say soon — we won't be here."

Wren hugged Bracken's neck and buried her face in his fur, and let herself feel it, just for a moment: the thing her mama had sent her down the mountain to find, arriving not as a door or a light or a miracle, but as a rumor in a stranger's yard, dressed in feathers and firewood smoke.

Hope, wearing borrowed clothes.

It was enough. It would have to be.

Above the fence rail, the scarred raven ruffed its feathers, turned its beak north toward the deep high passes, and spoke one final word into the cold afternoon air.

*Come.*

Gideon looked at it for a long time. Then he went inside to pack.
