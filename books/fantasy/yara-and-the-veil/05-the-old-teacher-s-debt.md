## 5. The Old Teacher's Debt

The rain had followed them out of Harrowgate like a debt collector, patient and certain it would be paid.

Gideon led them off the shepherd's track an hour past dusk, down a slope so overgrown with gorse that Wren asked, twice, whether he was lost. He wasn't lost. You couldn't be lost on a road you'd walked at fourteen with a sack of stolen candles over your shoulder and Maren Ostwick's voice snapping at your heels — *mind the third bush, boy, the third bush, it bites* — though he kept this to himself. The girl was exhausted and frightened and had eaten half a loaf of his bread in the manner of someone who expected bread to be taken away again, and there was no comfort in telling her that her guide's whole map of safety had been drawn by a disgraced scholar who talked to herself more than to anyone else.

"She lives out here alone?" Wren asked, hunching into the oilcloth he'd wrapped her in.

"She lives out here on purpose," Gideon said. "There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty. "Alone is what happens to you. On purpose is what you choose instead."

Wren chewed on that the way she'd chewed on the bread, as if expecting it to be confiscated. Then she said, "I like her already," which made him laugh for the first time in four days, a rusty sound that startled a nightjar out of the gorse. The nightjar wheeled off cursing in the liquid tongue of its kind — *dark-soon-dark-soon-dark* — and Gideon answered without thinking, one soft syllable that meant *I know*, and felt Wren's eyes on his back, wide and wondering, all the rest of the way down.

Maren's cottage sat in a fold of the hills where three streams met, low-roofed and turf-covered, so thoroughly surrendered to the landscape that from twenty paces it looked like nothing at all. That had always been the point. The last time Gideon had stood before that door he'd been nineteen and furious, slamming out of it after an argument he could no longer even remember the shape of, and he had told himself for fifteen years afterward that there'd been time to go back. There is always time to go back until suddenly there isn't.

He raised his hand to knock, hesitated, and then didn't knock at all, because the door opened first.

Maren Ostwick stood in the gap holding a candle that did very little for her, since her glare supplied plenty of light of its own. She was smaller than he remembered — or perhaps he had simply grown, or perhaps the world had shrunk everyone except the ones who refused — but her eyes were the same: quick, gray, and calibrated to find the exact weight of any nonsense brought to her door.

"You," she said.

"Hello, Maren."

"Fourteen years." She looked him up and down, taking inventory the way she took inventory of everything: mud on his boots, storm-wet cloak, a child half-hidden behind his leg, and — Gideon saw her notice it — the cat, Bramble, riding inside his satchel with only her head out, wearing the smug expression cats reserve for having won an argument nobody else knew was happening. "Fourteen years, and you arrive in a storm, at night, with a fugitive child and an outlawed animal, looking like something the mountain coughed up and immediately regretted."

"That's roughly the size of it."

"I taught you better manners than that."

"No," Gideon said gently. "You tried. Everyone else gave up sooner."

Something moved at the corner of Maren's mouth — not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, haunting premises she no longer owned. She stood aside. "Well. Don't stand in the rain being clever at me. Get in before you flood my floor."

* * *

Inside, the cottage was exactly as Gideon remembered it and also completely rearranged, which was very Maren. Same low beams, same iron stove ticking away in the corner, same smell of ink and dried yarrow and woodsmoke. But the shelves had migrated, the table had turned to face the wall, and every window was shuttered and curtained twice, which the Maren of fifteen years ago would have called paranoia and the Maren of tonight apparently called Tuesday.

She fed Wren first — barley soup, thick enough to stand a spoon in, with the command "eat, don't talk, talking comes after eating" delivered in the tone of a woman who had raised no children of her own and therefore believed they could actually be governed this way. To Gideon's quiet astonishment, Wren obeyed, and visibly improved under the treatment, color coming back into her face between spoonfuls.

Bramble installed herself on the hearthstone and began a conversation with the fire, which consisted mostly of the cat making small chirring noises and the fire popping back, both parties deeply satisfied. Maren watched this for a long moment over Wren's head.

"How long," she said quietly, "since Harrowgate read the Edict?"

"Nine days."

"And how long since you decided to come to me?"

Gideon looked into his cup. "About eight."

"Mm." She stirred the pot she didn't need to stir. "And what happened in the ninth day, I wonder, that finally overcame your famous pride."

"It wasn't pride," he said, and then, because lying to Maren Ostwick had never once worked and wasn't going to start now: "It was fear. She scratched at my door half-dead, and I took her in, and somewhere between that moment and the next morning I understood that I'd been waiting fifteen years for an excuse to stop hiding. Which is a stupid thing to understand about yourself."

"It's not stupid." Maren set down the ladle. "It's late, is what it is. But late isn't stupid."

Wren, finished eating, had begun to droop where she sat. Maren jerked her chin toward the box bed in the corner — "in there, girl, sleep comes before questions too" — and Wren went, pulling off her boots and burrowing into the blankets with the efficiency of the truly worn-out, and within minutes her breathing slowed into the deep rhythm of a child who has decided, provisionally and against all evidence, that this room is safe.

Then Maren took the candle to the far wall, pressed on a beam that Gideon had leaned against a thousand times in his youth and never once thought about, and a section of the shelving swung inward with a groan of well-oiled hinges.

"Well," Gideon said. "That's new."

"Fourteen years new." Maren lifted the candle high. "Come and see what disgrace looks like when it keeps records."

* * *

The room beyond was barely a room — more a cellar carved upward, dry-lined and lamp-lit, and it was full of paper.

Not scattered paper, not hoarded paper, not the comfortable chaos of a scholar's study. This was paper organized with the ferocious discipline of a woman who had decided that if the crown intended to erase people, somebody would keep count. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, stuffed with bound volumes, loose folios, rolled maps. And on the great table in the center lay the master work: a ledger the size of a paving stone, open, its margins crowded, its pages dense with Maren's small fierce handwriting.

Gideon came closer, and the words stopped being words and became names.

*Halla Voss, miller's daughter, Alderbrook — hears horses. Alive, last confirmed spring.*
*Toma Rill, shepherd, Greyfell — birds, all kinds. Alive. Moved twice.*
*The Fenwick twins — crows. Alive, whereabouts unknown.*
*Sorrel Ash, formerly of Nethermarch —* and here the handwriting faltered, crossed itself out, began again — *status uncertain.*

Page after page after page. Hundreds of names. Some entries were short and some ran on for lines, little biographies compressed to their bones — *shy, stutters unless speaking to goats; mother doesn't know; good hands with animals, they trust her faster than people do* — and some entries were struck through entirely, a single clean line through each name, and beside those, in the margin, always the same notation in letters gone deliberately small and hard:

*Cured.*

Gideon touched one of those struck-through names with one finger. It was colder than touching a grave marker, somehow, because a stone at least admits what it is.

"How many?" he asked. His voice came out wrong.

"Eight hundred and forty-one living, that I know of, as of last month." Maren's voice was flat, which he recognized now as the sound of her grief — she never wept, she itemized. "Two hundred and six struck through. And I don't know how many I've missed. Villages burn quietly these days. The criers are careful about which towns get the reading and which just get soldiers." She closed the ledger with both hands, as if shutting a coffin lid. "You asked me once why I was disgraced. Do you remember? You were seventeen, and you asked it the way boys ask everything, meaning *what's the story* rather than *what did it cost.*"

"I remember asking. I don't remember listening."

"No," Maren said. "You didn't." She lowered herself onto the bench, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet. "I was court scholar, Gideon. Archivist to the Office of Borders. It was me — my survey, my maps — that they used to draw the line around Thornfell and declare it unmade. Erased from law. I signed the document. My hand drew the border that made all of this possible." She laid her palm flat on the ledger. "So when the first rumors came of what 'curing' meant, I couldn't claim innocence. I owed these people. All of them. Every name in this book is a payment against a debt I can never finish paying."

The lamp flame bent and steadied. From beyond the wall came the faint sound of Wren turning in her sleep, and the cat's low answering purr through the stones, two small voices keeping each other company in the dark.

"The Veil," Gideon said.

Maren's head came up sharply. "Where did you hear that word?"

"The girl. Rumor reached her village before the soldiers did. A door out of Thornfell. People say it's a story."

"People say lots of things about things they're afraid of." Maren rose and went to the shelves, running her fingers along the spines until she found what she wanted — a slim volume, its leather cracked, its title burned away. "Here's what I can tell you, and understand that I tell it as a scholar, which means I'll tell you what I know and what I merely believe and I will not blur the two, because blurring those is how countries end up erasing themselves. What I *know*: the old records, the true ones, the pre-crown ones I salvaged before they burned my archive — they describe Thornfell as enclosed. Not bordered. *Enclosed.* Like a jar with a lid. The mountains don't pass into other lands; other lands simply aren't there. The smugglers' roads — yours — run through passes that shouldn't connect, distances that don't add up. You've felt it. Don't pretend you haven't."

Gideon thought of the long nights in the high passes, the way the stars sometimes seemed arranged for a different sky, the way a two-day walk could take four hours if you let the road choose its own pace. He'd blamed exhaustion. He'd blamed brandy. He'd blamed anything except the truth, because the truth had no use for a man who needed to move contraband on schedule.

"I've felt it," he admitted.

"What I *believe*," Maren said, "is that the lid was put on deliberately, a generation ago, by people who wanted Thornfell to forget itself. And that lids — real ones, the kind made of more than law — have a seam. The records call it the Veil, and they say it opens. Rarely. Never cheaply." She set the book down between them, open to a page of diagrams that hurt to look at, geometry that kept trying to become something else when you weren't watching it directly. "And they do not say what it costs, because whoever wrote this section wrote it in cipher, and I have spent eleven years failing to break it."

"But you believe you know where it is."

"I believe the drowned road leads toward it. Your drowned road." She fixed him with the gray eyes. "Which is why you came, isn't it? Not for shelter. For the road."

"For the road," Gideon agreed. "For the guide. For—" he gestured helplessly at the ledger, at eight hundred and forty-one living names, "—for whatever this is. Because I've got a child asleep in your bed who talks to cats, and a crown patrol that watched me talk to a wolf, and every talent I have spent thirty years burying is suddenly the only thing I've got worth using. And I'm tired, Maren. I am so tired of being fluent in a language that gets people killed."

For a long moment Maren said nothing. Then she laughed — short, hard, surprised out of her. "Thirty years. I've been at it forty, and I never learned to say it that plainly. Maybe that's why my students always liked you better."

"Nobody liked me better."

"They liked that you broke things and apologized for it. Very charming. Infuriating." She closed the cipher book and stood straighter, and Gideon watched her assemble herself into decision the way she'd once assembled lectures, piece by piece, no wasted motion. "Very well. I'll guide you. Me, personally, not just directions — I want to see this thing with my own eyes before I write one word about it in any record. I'm too old to freeze on scree slopes and too stubborn to admit it, so we leave at dawn and you'll carry my pack and complain about nothing."

"Done."

"And one condition more." Her voice changed, and the change was worse than shouting. She put her hand flat on the ledger again. "This comes with us. Or rather — *you* come with it. Swear it, Gideon. Whatever happens on that road — hunger, ambush, the crown, worse — this book survives. Eight hundred and forty-one people who are alive because somebody bothered to write down that they exist. If the Veil opens and we all walk through and Thornfell stays erased behind us, this book goes through too, so that somewhere, someday, somebody knows they were here. That they counted." Her hand was trembling slightly now, and she looked at it with distaste, as at a faulty instrument. "I failed the ones who are gone. I will not fail the ones still breathing. Swear it on something that will actually hold you — not your honor, boy, I've seen your honor, it naps through the winter. Swear on the voices."

The words landed harder than she could know. Gideon stood in the lamplight of a hidden room full of rescued names and thought of every beast that had ever spoken to him — the mule with opinions, the ravens trading gossip, the wolf whose warning call had saved him from the patrol, the cat currently upstairs guarding a sleeping girl — all of them, all his life, the only congregation he'd ever had.

"I swear," he said. "On every voice I've ever heard. The ledger survives."

Maren studied him for a long moment, checking the join, testing whether the oath bore weight. Then she nodded once and blew out the lamp.

"Good. Sleep, then. Dawn comes early and I pack heavy." At the doorway she paused, her back to him, and said, quieter: "You did right, coming here. I want that recorded too. Fourteen years I've wondered if teaching you was a waste of a good candle. Turns out it wasn't. Turns out you were just... late."

* * *

He woke before dawn to the smell of Maren already cooking and the sound of Wren chattering at Bramble in the corner, and it took him a sleepy, golden moment to understand what else he was hearing — the low murmur of Maren's voice from the table, not talking to herself after all, but reading aloud. Names. Working down the ledger column by column in the dark before departure, the way another woman might pray.

Then she stopped. Mid-name. The silence that followed had a texture to it, and Gideon sat up fast.

"Maren?"

"Riders," she said. Her face, when she turned, was gray. "Three hours ago, maybe four, on the north ridgeline. Toma Rill sent a kestrel — it arrived just before you did yesterday, I hadn't marked the entry yet." Her finger rested on the page, on a name freshly inked beneath the kestrel's report, and Gideon leaned over and read it, and the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the dawn.

*Inquisitor Vess. Southbound. Asking after a girl.*

Behind him, Wren had gone very still, and the cat had stopped purring.
