## 6. Down the Drowned Road

The road had drowned the year Gideon turned nineteen, when the sluice gates up at Corvin's dam failed in a spring melt and took half the valley's old trade route with them. Everyone else called it a tragedy. Gideon had called it a gift, because a drowned road was a secret road, and a secret road was the only kind worth knowing.

Now he stood at its edge in the grey hour before dawn with a child on his back and a scholar wheezing behind him, and he thought: *well, old friend. Let's see if you remember me either.*

The water lay over the path like smoked glass, hip-deep at its shallowest, and it ran cold enough to stop a heart that wasn't paying attention. On either side the pines came down to the floodline, black and dripping, close as conspirators.

"We can't be expected," Maren Ostwick said from behind him, "to walk through *that*."

"You can't," Gideon agreed. "I can."

"And the girl?"

"The girl rides." He reached back and patted Wren's knee where her legs locked around his ribs. She hadn't let go of his collar since they'd left the cabin, not once, not even to sleep. "She's lighter than she looks. Barely more than a sack of turnips."

"I'm *not*," said Wren, into the back of his neck.

"She's not," Maren conceded.

Gideon waded in.

The cold climbed him like something with intentions — ankles, knees, waist — and Wren made a small sound of outrage against his shoulder when it found her boots. He counted his steps by feel beneath the water: here the paving stones still held, here the culvert had collapsed and the current dragged left, here — third stone past the leaning birch — the road dipped into an old pothole deep as his chest. "Tuck your feet up," he told Wren, and felt her obey, and stepped high through the deep place with his teeth clenched and his breath coming out in white flags.

Behind him, Maren muttered something in Old Low Thornish that would have gotten her excommunicated twice over in her scholarly days.

"There's a ledge," Gideon said, when his feet found stone again. "Halfway along. Dry, or near enough. We'll rest there till the light's better for traveling."

"And after the ledge?"

"After the ledge we come out at Miller's Folly, and from there—" He stopped himself. From there everything was guesswork. From there they were three people with one map in their heads and an Inquisitor somewhere behind them with dogs and warrants and, if the ravens were right, no particular hurry, because Vess struck him as a woman who understood that fear did most of the chasing for you.

"From there," Maren prompted.

"From there, north," he said, as if it were a plan.

* * *

The ledge ran under an overhang where the flood hadn't reached, a shelf of dry gravel wide enough for two to sit if they were fond of each other, which by now — four days of flight, shared bread, shared secrets — they nearly were. Gideon got a fire going no bigger than two cupped hands, the kind that gave off heat and almost no light. Wren fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, in the way of children who have learned that sleep is a thing you take while it's offered.

Maren watched him feed a twig to the flames.

"You're listening for something," she said.

"Hm?"

"You've been cocking your head like that since the water. Like a heron. You've been doing it since Harrowgate, honestly, but worse today."

Gideon weighed how much truth the moment could carry. Four days ago this woman had opened a hidden ledger and shown him forty names written in her careful hand, living names, hunted names, and sworn him to their protection. She knew what he was. But knowing what a man is and watching him do it are different meats.

"Otters," he said finally.

Maren blinked. "Otters."

"They moved into the drowned road after the flood. Good territory — fish, cover, no trappers, because nobody knows the road's down here but me." He hesitated. "I asked them to scout ahead. Yesterday evening, before we came down. They'll report when they catch up."

"You *asked* them."

"I asked them."

"What do you ask, exactly? What are the words?"

Gideon felt his mouth pull sideways. Of all the questions she might have asked — is it safe, does it hurt, what happens if you're caught — Maren Ostwick, disgraced scholar, wanted grammar. "There aren't words. Not like speech. It's more..." He groped for it. "You know how you can tell what someone means before they finish a sentence? Tone, hands, eyes? It's all that, but louder. With otters it's mostly pictures and appetite. Fish, danger, *where*, *how many*. They don't really do tenses."

"So your scouts can tell you what's ahead of them right now, but not what passed an hour ago."

"That's about the shape of it."

Maren nodded slowly, filing it away somewhere behind her eyes where she kept everything, and Gideon thought — not for the first time — that being looked at by her was like being read by a very patient librarian. Then her gaze went soft and strange.

"What did they say? When you asked them?"

He looked into the little fire. The truth was embarrassing. It was always embarrassing; that was the thing nobody warned you about, having a gift instead of a talent. "They said yes," he said. "They always say yes. Otters don't see much reason to refuse anybody."

* * *

The report came an hour past dawn, and it came in terror.

Gideon felt it before he heard anything — a cold thread pulled through the water, upstream, urgent. Then two slick heads broke the surface of the flooded road thirty feet off, dove, surfaced again closer, and chattered at him in a cascade of images so fast and jumbled he had to shut his eyes to sort them.

Water. Shadow moving through water. Many legs. Teeth-smell. A red coat like a wound. *Coming, coming, coming.*

He was on his feet with Wren in his arms before he'd decided to stand.

"Gideon?"

"Dogs." His voice came out flat and quick, smuggler's voice, the one that wasted nothing. "Six of them maybe, maybe eight, in the water behind us with riders. Otters say less than an hour back." He kicked dirt over the fire. "They're not following our trail. They're just walking the road, same as us. Sooner or later—"

"Sooner," Maren said grimly, already shouldering her pack. "We're in a ditch with one exit apiece, boy. Where do we go?"

Where indeed. The drowned road ran straight as an honest man's opinion for three more miles, then climbed out of the flood at Miller's Folly onto open ground — open ground with no cover and a watchman's view in every direction, ground where eight hounds would have them like gulls on a dropped pie.

Unless.

Gideon closed his eyes and reached sideways, downstream, into the flooded lanes and culverts where the otters played. *Danger comes,* he pushed at them, at all of them, every slick brown mind within reach. *Big danger, loud danger. Can you make noise? Can you splash and slap and raise every bird on the water between here and the bend? Make it sound like ten men marching.*

Otters, as established, rarely refused anyone. And mischief — mischief was practically their mother tongue.

"Now," he said aloud, and led them on, fast, Wren on his back again and half awake, mumbling questions he didn't have time to answer.

A quarter mile on, the world behind them exploded. Wings shrieked up off the flooded oaks in a black clatter, ducks burst from the reeds in panic-packs, and underneath it all came a sound Gideon had never heard otters make at scale: slapping, splashing, a rolling drumbeat of water-noise, deliberately enormous. Somewhere back along the road a horse screamed and a man shouted, and the whole engineered catastrophe folded in on itself as the riders fought animals they couldn't see and birds they couldn't quiet.

Wren, peering over Gideon's shoulder, began to laugh — a rusty, astonished laugh, the first he'd heard from her since the night at his door. "What *is* that?"

"That," said Gideon, grinning despite everything, grinning so hard his face hurt, "is the Harrowgate Symphony. Sold out nightly. Come on — while the orchestra's playing, we exit through the side door."

* * *

The side door was a smuggler's climb he'd cut himself at twenty-two: a goat track up the gorge wall behind a curtain of hanging moss, invisible unless you knew to look for the blaze mark shaped like a fish on a birch trunk. It was brutal going. Twice he had to pass Wren down to Maren and haul himself up barehanded. By the time they crested the ridge into the thin sunlight above the gorge, all three of them were shaking, and none of them were laughing anymore, because from the height they could see down the drowned road — and see the riders.

Seven of them, strung out along the floodline, and even at this distance the coats were unmistakable. Crown red. Inquisition grey. And out ahead of the horses, working the bank in long casting loops, the hounds.

One of the hounds stopped.

It was too far to see details, but Gideon saw it stop — saw the whole animal go still the way water goes still before it freezes — and then it turned its great head and looked directly up the gorge wall at the exact patch of moss and rock where the three of them crouched, and it *howled*.

Not a trail-howl. Not the belling chase-cry of a dog that has scent. This was shorter, higher, jagged at the edges, and Gideon felt it land in his chest like a hand.

Because he knew that voice.

"Bramble," he breathed.

"What?" Maren's head snapped around. "What did you say?"

"He knows me." The words came out of Gideon like something being extracted. "That hound — I know that hound. Bramble. He belonged to a gamekeeper up past Hollow Bridge, old man Hedley, and when the keeper died the crown kennels took the litter, and I — I used to talk to him over the fence. Years of it. He was a pup. I taught him to sit for a strip of dried eel." He could hear his own voice getting thinner and hated it. "He's not pointing at our scent. He's saying hello."

Below them, faint across the distance, the hound howled again. And there — he couldn't mistake it, wouldn't mistake it — buried in the cry was the rising interrogative note that Bramble had used his whole life for one purpose only. *Are you there? Is it you? Are you coming?*

"Oh," said Maren softly. All the horror in the world fit inside that single syllable.

"He doesn't understand," Gideon said. His hands had gone numb on the rock. "He can't understand. Dogs don't lie and they don't grasp betrayal, there's no room in them for it. All he knows is my voice came out of the water yesterday asking for help, and now my voice is up on this hill, and he wants—" His throat closed. "He wants to know why I won't come and scratch his ears."

Wren slid her small cold hand into his.

"If they follow Bramble up here," Maren said, and there was no softness in her now, only the ledger-keeper's arithmetic, "we're dead. If we run, he tracks us anyway, better than any rider could. Gideon." She gripped his sleeve. "Can you tell him to stop? To sit? To forget he ever knew you?"

"I can try."

"And will it work?"

Gideon looked down at the gorge, at the small brown shape at the foot of the cliff with its head thrown back, singing its cracked-hearted question into the rocks, while behind it the grey coats turned their horses toward the noise.

"No," he said. "But I'm going to do it anyway."

He filled his lungs and sent his voice down the cliff face, low and shaped, the way he'd spoken to wolves and mules and every creature since childhood — not words, never words, but the thing underneath words. *Go home. Sit. Stay. Good dog, best dog. Go home.*

The howling stopped.

For three full heartbeats the gorge held its breath, and Gideon let himself believe — one bright, foolish second — that it had worked, that obedience and love were the same thing after all.

Then Bramble sat down on the riverbank, pointed his nose at the sky exactly as Gideon had taught him a decade ago, and howled the signal every crown handler paid gold to hear: *found, found, found.*

"Well," said Maren, already running. "North, then. Run *now*."

And Gideon ran, Wren clinging to his back, Bramble's voice rising behind them through the gorge like a bell tolling for a wedding he'd been invited to and couldn't attend — and far off beyond the northern ridges, half a day's hard travel, an old ruined watchtower waited with whoever, or whatever, was hiding inside it.
