## 7. The Company of Strays

Maren Ostwick had spent forty years believing she was too old to be surprised, and the world had spent those same forty years taking a personal interest in proving her wrong.

The ruined watchtower rose out of the pines like a broken tooth — three stories of old imperial stone, one wall collapsed into a slope of tumbled blocks, ivy doing its patient work on the rest. Maren had known it existed because Maren knew most things that were written down, and even the erased left records if you knew where to dig. But what she had not known, could not have known from any ledger or crumbling survey, was that it was occupied.

"Stop," Gideon said quietly, and stopped, and Wren bumped into his back like a small warm door swinging shut.

The girl had been walking in his shadow for three days now, close enough to count his bootlaces. Maren had watched it happen with an ache she did not permit herself to name. Children attached themselves to safety wherever they found it. It did not mean the safety would last. Nothing lasted; that was rather the point of her ledger.

"There's smoke," Wren said. "Smell it? And horses." She paused, head tilting the way she did when she was listening to something no one else could hear. "Two horses. They're arguing about whose fault the rain was."

Maren adjusted the strap of her satchel and said, dryly, to no one in particular: "Wonderful. A fortress full of gossiping livestock."

Then a crow dropped out of the pine canopy like a thrown stone and landed on the trail not six feet ahead of them, and looked at them — not at their faces but at their *eyes*, one after another, cataloguing them — and croaked a single syllable that made Wren flinch and Gideon go very still.

"It says there are four," Wren translated softly. "Four people inside. It says—" Her brow furrowed. "It says one of them is angry all the time and it's very tired of her."

"That would be Sorrel," said a voice from the tower's doorway.

A man stepped out into the grey light. He was perhaps thirty, built like a man who had spent his life around horses — bowlegged, weather-browned, hands loose and easy at his sides. He wore no uniform, but Maren noted the way he stood, weight balanced, one hand near where a cavalry saber would hang. A deserter carried his training with him like a scar.

"I heard you coming half a mile out," he said. "Your mule complains."

"My mule is honest," Maren replied. "A rarity these days."

His eyes moved over the three of them — the scholar with her satchel, the big smuggler with his careful stillness, the child. When they reached Wren they softened, then hardened again immediately, as though softness were a luxury he'd been billed for once and refused to pay for twice.

"You're running from Vess," he said. Not a question.

"We're running toward something better than standing still," Maren said. "Which happens to be away from Vess."

"And which are you?" His gaze settled on Gideon. "Speaker?"

The silence stretched a moment. Maren watched Gideon weigh it — she had learned to watch him do this, the invisible scales behind his eyes. Every day on the drowned road he had measured truth against danger, and every day so far truth had won by a narrow margin. She found herself holding her breath anyway.

"Yes," Gideon said.

*Beasts,* the word he didn't say hung there anyway. *All of them.*

The horse-man exhaled through his nose. "Well," he said. "Come in, then. I'm Tam. And I suppose we'd better introduce you before Sorrel comes out shooting."

* * *

The tower's ground floor had been roofed with salvaged slate and pine boughs, and a fire burned in a hearth that had probably warmed imperial soldiers two hundred years dead. Two bedrolls on the east side, two on the west, a border as legible as any map Maren had ever drawn. The twins sat on the eastern side — a boy and a girl, seventeen maybe, with identical sharp chins and hair the color of wet crows' feathers, which Maren suspected was not a coincidence so much as a lifestyle. Crows perched along every gap in the ruined walls above them like a jury of disapproving relations.

On the western side sat a woman alone.

She was perhaps fifty, grey-streaked and hard-mouthed, mending a coat with the violent efficiency of someone who considered thread a personal enemy. She did not look up when they entered. She did not look up when Tam made introductions. She looked up only when Wren, wandering curiously, drifted too near the western bedroll — and even then it was only her eyes, rising flat and cold as a pond in January.

"Keep your brats on your side," she said.

"She's not my—"

"She's nobody's, that's how it starts," the woman said, and bent back to her coat.

"Sorrel," Tam said, in the tone of a man who had said the word many times and expected nothing from it.

Maren chose the eastern side of the fire without ceremony and began unpacking her satchel with the deliberate clatter of a woman announcing an occupation. "Maren Ostwick. Formerly of the Royal Academy, formerly of several other places that no longer admit knowing me. I keep records." She drew out her ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on her knee. "Names. Of people like us. Where they were, where they're going, whether they're alive. It has become, in recent months, rather the most important book in Thornfell."

That got reactions. The boy twin leaned forward. The girl twin went pale. Tam's jaw tightened. Sorrel's needle paused for exactly one heartbeat, then resumed.

"A list," the boy said. "Of every gifted person alive."

"Corrected daily," Maren said. "I'm pleased to report it's shorter than the crown believes and longer than I can bear."

"Where do you keep it when you sleep?" the girl asked, and her voice was thin, and Maren understood at once that this child had already imagined soldiers finding such a book.

"With me. Always." Maren met her eyes. "You may hold me to that, young lady. What are your names?"

"Ilsa," said the girl.

"Brann," said the boy, and then, because twins apparently could not bear to let a sentence end singly: "We talk to crows."

"They mostly complain," Ilsa added.

"They've a lot to complain about," Brann said. "People being the general topic."

Gideon had taken a place near the door — always the door, Maren had noticed; the man sat like a cat facing the exit — and it was there, half in shadow, that Sorrel finally favored him with her attention.

"And you," she said. "Tam says beast-speaker. That true?"

"It's true."

"All beasts?"

"So far as I've found."

Sorrel set down her mending. She unfolded from her bedroll with a creak of joints and crossed the room, unhurried, until she stood over him. Up close Maren could see the grief in her — it was not worn on the surface like Tam's wariness or the twins' fear. It was structural. Load-bearing grief. Take it out and the whole woman would come down.

"Wolves?" Sorrel said.

"Yes."

"Cattle?"

"Yes."

"Snakes?"

"...Yes, though they're poor conversationalists."

Something flickered across Sorrel's face — contempt, or envy, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Must be nice," she said softly. "Being good at it."

And she went back to her side of the fire and took up her needle, and the conversation, such as it was, was over.

* * *

They stayed the night, because the rain returned and even fugitives must occasionally yield to weather, and Maren lay awake on her bedroll listening to a household assemble itself badly around her.

It was the horses that started it. Tam had two, picketed outside, and sometime past midnight Maren heard Gideon rise, quiet as falling snow, and slip out. She counted to two hundred before following, because she was old and curious and had never once in her life managed to mind her own business.

In the lean-to shelter by the tower wall, Gideon stood between the horses with his palm flat against a bay mare's neck, murmuring. Tam was already there, arms folded, watching with an expression Maren needed a moment to read. It was hunger. Pure, embarrassed hunger — the look of a man watching someone speak fluently a language he'd only ever stammered in.

"What's she saying?" Tam asked.

"She's saying the black one kicked her at the last ford and she wants it entered into the record." Gideon's mouth curved. "I told her I know a scholar who keeps records."

Tam laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening for the first time in years. Then it died. "I was a courier," he said, to the horse as much as to Gideon. "Third Lancers. When the Edict came down, my captain called us all together and said anyone with the taint should step forward for curing, and it would be kept quiet, and we'd be treated fair." He scratched the mare's jaw. "I believed him for about four seconds. Long enough to step forward."

"He didn't keep it quiet."

"They rode me out of camp at dawn. Said I should be grateful they didn't ride me further." He shrugged, a soldier's shrug, designed to carry nothing. "So. Beast-speaker who talks to everything, and horse-speaker who got caught. That's the company, is it? You and the scholar and the crows and—" he nodded back toward the tower, toward the west side of the fire, "—and her."

"You know her story?"

"Bits. She doesn't tell it. What I've pieced together from Brann's crows—" Tam lowered his voice, "—her brother. Ten years ago. They were crossing the Whitefell river in flood, and the horses spooked, and she was talking to them, trying to calm them, and her brother went under trying to hold a line. She says her gift killed him. Says the animals listened to *her* instead of saving *him*."

"And what do the crows say actually happened?"

Tam was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the shelter's tin roof. "Crows say she dove in after him four times. Four. Before the cold took her legs out from under her." He shook his head. "But grief doesn't keep ledgers, does it. It just keeps the worst page."

Maren thought of her own ledger, bound in oiled cloth, heavy with names. She thought: *all of us keeping accounts, and none of us keeping them honestly.*

Inside, the argument had already started without them.

* * *

"—can't stay here, that's the whole point of the crows' report," Brann was saying when Maren and the others came in dripping. He gestured upward at the birds silhouetted in the wall-gaps. "Vess's riders turned south at Harrowgate, but south leads here eventually. Everything leads here eventually. It's a crossroads with a roof."

"So we move," Ilsa said.

"Move where? That's the question, isn't it." Tam crouched by the fire, feeding it a stick. "Because there's a rumor going around the roads, and I'll wager these three didn't walk out of nowhere for their health. Go on, scholar. Tell them."

Every eye came to Maren. She sighed internally, hung her wet cloak by the fire, and sat with the dignity of a woman who had given difficult lectures before.

"The Veil," she said. "Very well. Yes. There are stories — consistent ones, from sources I consider reliable, which is to say dead scholars and dishonest smugglers — of a doorway in the far north of Thornfell. Old. Older than the kingdom that erased this country. The erasure itself may be connected to it, though the record grows... speculative there. The stories say it leads out. Out of Thornfell. Somewhere the Edict cannot follow."

Silence. Even Sorrel's needle had stopped.

"Out," Ilsa repeated, tasting the word.

"It's a story," Sorrel said.

"Most true things start as stories," Maren said. "I've read the originals."

"You've read *stories.*" Sorrel jabbed her needle toward the satchel. "And you're dragging a child north on the strength of them. Through winter passes. With Inquisitors on the roads. That's not hope, that's negligence with better handwriting."

"Sorrel," Tam said warningly.

"No, let me finish, since nobody else will say it." She stood, and now she addressed herself directly to Gideon, who had taken a seat near the fire with Wren asleep against his shoulder — when had that happened? Maren hadn't seen the girl migrate; children moved like cats, only when unobserved. "Here's what I know about you, beast-speaker. You come up the drowned road with a scholar and a little girl, clean-fed, well-shod, boots worth more than my coat. Nobody walks out of Harrowgate that tidy after the Edict. So either you were already running before it came down — which makes you a criminal first and a refugee second — or somebody helped you, and I'd like to know who, and what they got."

"The drowned road is mine," Gideon said evenly. "I ran cargo through Thornfell for fifteen years. Corvin Aldane's routes. If that makes me a criminal first, I'll wear it."

"*Corvin.*" Sorrel laughed, and it was an awful sound, all edges. "Corvin's man. Wonderful. And the girl? Whose is she?"

"She's hers," Gideon said, and something in his voice changed — dropped lower, quieter, the way water goes quiet before it goes deep. "Her village burned. Vess burned it. She walked out of the ashes and knocked on my door, and now she's mine to look after as much as anyone's anybody's. Which is to say: I've decided it, and that's the whole of my authority."

"Decisions get people killed," Sorrel said.

"Not deciding gets them killed slower," Gideon said. "I've watched it."

For a long moment the only sounds were the fire and the rain and, high above, the soft shifting of crow feathers. Maren realized she was gripping her ledger through its oilcloth and made herself stop.

It was Wren who broke it — of course it was; the child had the unerring instinct of the young for waking at precisely the worst moment. She lifted her head from Gideon's shoulder, blinking, and looked around the hostile circle of faces with the frank appraisal of someone shopping for a family.

"The crows like him," she announced.

"Wren—" Ilsa began.

"They do. They've been saying so since you came in. They say he smells like the mountain." She rubbed her eyes. "Also there's a dog problem. The crows saw dogs today. Big ones, on the south road, moving fast, with men in grey."

The temperature of the room changed instantly. Tam was on his feet. "Grey? Grey cloaks?"

"Inquisition grey," Brann said quietly. All around the walls, the crows had gone silent, heads cocked. "How far, Ilse?"

His sister's eyes had gone distant, listening to voices on the wind. Her face, when it emptied of color, looked terribly like her brother's.

"Half a day," she said. "Maybe less. They're following the flooded road."

Maren looked at Gideon. Gideon looked at Maren, and she saw it land in him — the knowledge arriving like a stone down a well — *the flooded road is mine. Only mine. Someone told.*

"Pack everything," Gideon said, already rising, easing Wren gently off his shoulder and onto her feet. "We leave in ten minutes. North."

"And if we don't want to go north with you?" Sorrel said.

But she was already rolling her bedroll as she said it, and everyone in the tower, including Maren — especially Maren — pretended not to notice.
