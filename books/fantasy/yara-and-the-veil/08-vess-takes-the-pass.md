## 8. Vess Takes the Pass

The tent smelled of wet canvas and lamp oil, and Inquisitor Vess found both preferable to the smell of the prisoner.

He was a small man, the smuggler, with the particular sanded-down quality of people who had spent their lives being rained on in high places. His hands were lashed to the table in front of him — not cruelly, she had been specific about that — and he sat as though the ropes were a formality he had decided not to take personally. There was a mug of tea near his elbow. Untouched. That was either defiance or good sense; army tea could strip rust.

"You're comfortable," she said.

"Cozy," he agreed. His voice was gravel over gravel. "You want to untie me so I can hold the cup? My hands are getting ideas."

"They'll survive." She sat across from him, laid her ledger flat, and dipped her pen. "Name."

"Pass."

"That isn't a name."

"It's what my mother called me when I was slow coming home. Close enough."

Vess did not smile. It was important, in her experience, never to smile in the first hour. Smiling was currency, and she did not spend it early. She wrote *refuses* in her neat, upright hand and let the silence do its work. Silence worked on most men the way water worked on most stone. You only needed to be more patient than the mountain.

This one lasted eleven minutes, which she noted with some respect.

"All right," he said finally, when she turned the page for no reason at all. "What do you want to know?"

"Everything you know about the routes through Thornfell. Who runs them. Where they cross the high passes this season."

"And if I tell you?"

"Then your trial will be brief, and your sentence will be transportation instead of the rope. I keep my word, Mr.—"

"Dole." He said it like an apology. "Pike Dole. And you won't like what I know, Inquisitor, because what I know is that the roads are closed. Every pass out of the mountains has gone quiet since the Edict. Nobody runs goods anymore because goods talk now." He leaned forward as far as the ropes allowed, and something in his face shifted from calculation to something rawer. "You know what I mean. Beasts. The mules stopped answering. The ravens stopped carrying word. A man can't trust his own pack animals to keep his secrets, and a smuggler without secrets is just a man carrying heavy things uphill for nothing."

"The beasts don't talk," Vess said. It came out flat and automatic, a coin worn smooth from handling. "There are people who claim they do, and those people are sick, and there is a cure."

Pike Dole looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, bitter, entirely without humor. "Cure. Right. Is that what they're calling it down in Harrowgate?"

She let that pass. "Who else uses the routes?"

"Everyone who ever learned them from Corvin."

"Corvin Ash." She wrote the name. She had written the name before.

"The very same. King of nothing, ruler of all the mud between Thornfell and the lowlands." Dole hesitated, and she watched the hesitation happen, watched him weigh something behind his eyes. Men always hesitated before they sold a friend. It was practically a ritual. "If it's beast-speakers you're hunting, you're wasting your time on Corvin. He's clean. It's his boy you want."

Something in the room changed temperature. Vess kept her pen moving. "His boy."

"Not blood. Took him in young, raised him on the passes. Quiet lad. Strange lad." Dole's voice dropped, and here it came — the fear, finally, leaking through the cracks in the gravel. "I saw him once. Up by the Widow's Tooth, three winters back. Fog thick enough to spread on bread, and we were lost, proper lost, and Corvin's boy walks out into it and *talks*. To a wolf. Big gray devil standing in the mist like it had an appointment. And it *listened* to him. Then it went, and ten minutes later the fog lifted and there was the trail, plain as scripture." Dole swallowed. "Wolves don't listen, Inquisitor."

"No," Vess said quietly. "They don't."

"Gideon," Dole said. "Gideon Pemberton. That's your speaker. If half what I've heard is true, every animal on that mountain knows his voice. And if he's taken up with the ones you're chasing—" He shook his head slowly. "Then God help you. Because that boy knows Thornfell better than the crown does. Better than the maps do. There isn't a goat track up there he hasn't walked, and there isn't a creature on it that wouldn't die for him."

Vess finished writing. She blotted the page with care, folded her hands on top of the ledger, and looked at Pike Dole for a moment longer than she needed to.

"Thank you," she said. "You'll have your transportation. Someone will bring you that tea now, if you like it. I'd advise against it personally."

"Your mercy overwhelms me," Dole said.

Outside the tent, the camp was striking itself in efficient order — wagons groaning, horses stamping, soldiers moving with the brisk purpose of people who wanted very much to be somewhere warmer. Vess stood in the gray morning light and watched her own breath drift, and for a moment she did not move at all.

*Gideon Pemberton.*

She knew the name. Of course she knew it. It had appeared twice in Maren Ostwick's confiscated papers, years ago, in a list of students — the disgraced scholar's little academy of the afflicted, scattered to the winds when the University stripped her title. *P., G., aptitude exceptional, control fragile.* Vess had underlined it once, in red, in another life, and then filed it away because there had been larger fish and smaller fires.

Now the fish had a name spoken aloud by a frightened man in a wet tent, and the name came with a wolf standing in fog like it had an appointment.

She walked to her own tent — larger than the prisoner's, spartan inside, one cot, one desk, one locked chest — and sent her adjutant away with orders she didn't need to think about. Break camp. The high passes. The old smuggler roads toward the spine of Thornfell. They would move within the hour.

Then she unlocked the chest.

She did not open it often. Once a year, perhaps. Twice, in bad years. Inside, beneath a fold of oilcloth, was a bundle of letters tied with string gone soft with age, and a single portrait miniature done by some provincial painter with more patience than talent. The face in the miniature was young. Round-cheeked. There was a smudge of paint where the artist had attempted dimples and given up.

*Lira.*

Her sister had been eight when the voices started, and Vess had been fourteen, and neither of them had known what it was. That was the thing no one understood — the thing the pamphlets and the proclamations never managed to capture. It hadn't looked like sickness. It hadn't looked like anything at all. Lira had simply started sitting in the henhouse for hours, laughing, coming in with straw in her hair and stories about a rooster named Bartholomew who was, she insisted, a tremendous gossip. Their father had thought it a phase. Their mother had prayed.

And Vess — Vess, who was fourteen and correct and terrified of everything she could not explain — Vess had told the parish rector.

That was all. That was the whole of it. She had told a grown man in a black coat what her sister did in the henhouse, because she had believed, truly believed, that the grown man in the black coat would help. Would cure. Would fix whatever small broken thing had gotten into her sister the way you got a splinter out of a thumb.

The men who came wore the same coats the Inquisition wore now. They took Lira away in a wagon on a Tuesday. She remembered because it was raining, and Lira had waved from the wagon window the entire way down the lane, waving and waving, certain — even then, even knowing nothing — certain that her big sister would fix it.

Lira came back four years later. That was the part the pamphlets didn't mention either: they brought them back. Cured. The cure worked, in the sense that the voices stopped. It also took, in roughly equal measure, nearly everything else. Her sister had come home tall and quiet and smooth as river stone, and she had looked at their mother without recognition for two full days, and she had never once — not once in twenty years since — laughed the way she had laughed in the henhouse. She lived now in a charity house in the capital, approved of by everyone, wanting nothing, troubling no one. Vess visited her every year on her birthday and brought honey cakes, and Lira ate them politely and thanked her in a voice like a well-behaved echo, and every year Vess left the house feeling as though she had visited a grave where the occupant had been required to keep breathing.

*The cure works,* she told herself, the way she told herself every time. *It works. She is safe. She is well. She suffers nothing.*

It was true. It was all true, and it was also a lie, and Vess had spent twenty years learning to hold both truths in her hands at once without letting them see each other.

Because here was the arithmetic that kept her armored: if the cure was a lie, then what she had done to Lira was not medicine. And if what she had done was not medicine, then it was simply the first entry in a very long ledger, and the Inquisition was not a hospital but a machine for making sisters into strangers, and every speaker she dragged out of the mountains in chains was somebody's Lira, waving from a wagon window in the rain.

She could not afford that arithmetic. No one doing her work could afford it. So she did what armored people do: she made the enemy monstrous instead. Beast-speakers trafficked with powers beyond the Veil. They consorted with wolves. They were, at best, gravely ill, and at worst gateways for things the crown had spent generations burying, and the Edict was mercy, hard mercy, the kind that loved the sinner enough to empty them out.

Most days the armor held.

Today, sitting on her cot with a miniature of a dimpled girl in her hands, it cracked. Just slightly. Along the old seam.

*A wolf standing in the fog like it had an appointment.* What must that be like — to speak, and be answered? To call, and be *known*? She tried to imagine Lira in the henhouse, really tried, and found to her horror that the memory had edges now, official edges, contaminated by reports and testimony and twenty years of telling herself a story about sickness. She could no longer remember whether Lira had seemed happy. She could only remember that she had seemed wrong. Had she actually seemed wrong? Or had Vess merely needed her to be?

"How long does a crack take to close?" she asked the empty tent, and the tent, having no gift for conversation, declined to answer.

She put the miniature back. Locked the chest. Stood, and rolled her shoulders back, and felt the armor settle — plate by plate, certainty by certainty, the familiar weight that let her walk upright through a world that would otherwise break her heart daily.

By the time she stepped out again, her adjutant was waiting with the news that the column was ready, and also, unprompted, with the observation that the scouts had found wolf sign along the eastern ridge, unusually fresh, unusually bold.

"Wolves don't come this close to armed camps," the adjutant said. "Never seen anything like it. Almost like they're—"

"Watching," Vess finished.

She mounted her gray mare, and the mare tossed her head and sidestepped in a way she never did, ears flat, eyes rolling white toward the treeline, and Vess felt a cold finger trace her spine. The horse was afraid of something in the trees. Something that was, presumably, merely a wolf.

Merely a wolf, unless the stories were true, and the stories were never true, and the woman who had reported her own sister to the black coats did not get to start believing in talking animals now, on a gray morning, with a fugitive child and a ledger-keeper and a smuggler's adopted son somewhere ahead of her in the high passes, running toward a door that did not exist.

"Inquisitor?" the adjutant said. "Your orders?"

Vess gathered the reins. Somewhere on the ridge above them, faint and unbothered, a wolf began to howl, and the mare shuddered beneath her, and far off in the trees a second voice joined the first, and then a third.

"We take the pass," Vess said. "All of us. Now. Before the weather turns or the wildlife holds parliament." She nudged the mare forward, and added, more softly, to no one: "And find me Gideon Pemberton."

The wolves, she could have sworn, stopped howling all at once — as if they had heard.

* * *

They climbed for the rest of the day, and the mountain gave them fog by afternoon, thick as spilled milk, and somewhere in that white blindness a scout came stumbling back into camp white-faced, reporting that their lead mule had refused the trail — planted all four hooves, would not budge, kept turning her head toward the fog as though listening to someone.

Vess looked at the trembling animal, then up at the wall of cloud swallowing the pass ahead.

"Well," she said quietly, to the mule, to the mountain, to the girl with dimples she had put in a wagon twenty years ago. "Then he's close."

She drew her cloak tight and walked into the fog.
