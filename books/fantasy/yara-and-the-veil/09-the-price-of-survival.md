## 9. The Price of Survival

Sorrel counted her sins the way other people counted coins — often, and always coming up short.

She was counting them now, in fact, as the company picked its way up the scree slope below Mount Cael's grey shoulder: one brother, dead; one crown patrol, escaped by a margin so thin she could still feel it whistling past her ears; and one stolen wheel of hard cheese, which she did not regret even slightly. The cheese was in her pack. Some debts you paid. Some you ate.

"You're smiling," Ilsa said, falling into step beside her. Brann trailed a few paces back with his arm raised, fingers twitching, receiving the morning reports from the crows the way other people received letters.

"I'm grimacing."

"It looks like smiling. On you it's hard to tell."

"That's a mercy, girl. Learn to appreciate it."

Ilsa grinned and dropped back to her brother, and Sorrel returned to her counting, because counting kept her from looking up the slope, where Gideon Pemberton walked point with that long, unhurried stride of his, as if the whole crown of Thornfell weren't trying to kill them and he were merely out for a constitutional. Maren trudged behind him, muttering elevations. Tam led the horses, talking to them under his breath in that soft way of his that made Sorrel's skin crawl and her chest ache in equal measure. And Wren —

Wren was climbing rocks like a squirrel with a grudge, darting ahead, doubling back, narrating everything to the mountain in a whisper. *Look at this lichen. Look at this beetle. Hello, beetle.*

Sorrel had decided, somewhere around the third day of this pilgrimage, that she disliked the child enormously, in the specific way one dislikes a thing that reminds you of what you used to be before you learned better. The girl spoke to everything. Beetles. Firewood. Once, memorably and at length, to a puddle.

It would end badly. Gifts like theirs always ended badly. Sorrel had a brother's grave to prove it.

"Loose stone!" someone shouted, and then the world went sideways.

* * *

Later, Sorrel would try to reconstruct exactly how the ambush began, and she never quite managed it. There was a crack like the mountain clearing its throat, and then the entire face of the scree field decided, all at once, to become a river. She threw herself flat against the slope with her arms wrapped around her pack — the cheese, at least, she could save — and rode the slide until it dumped her behind a shelf of granite the size of a chapel.

The noise was tremendous. Stone on stone, the shriek of it, the deep drumroll underneath. When it finally ground itself out, the silence rang worse than the noise.

Sorrel spat grit and took inventory by shouting names. "Gideon!"

"Here." From upslope, calm as Sunday.

"Maren?"

"Alive! My notes are alive!"

"Tam? Twins? Wren?"

A chorus of here, here, two heres, and one bright "the beetle's fine!"

And then a sound none of them made. A groan. Human, male, and deeply unhappy, drifting up from the tumbled rocks downslope.

Sorrel went cold in a way the wind couldn't account for.

They found him wedged between two boulders like a coin in a crack: a man of maybe fifty, dressed in the mud-colored wool of the high-country poor, one leg pinned badly wrong beneath a slab he'd evidently been too slow to dodge. He hadn't been following them. He'd simply been *there*, some solitary highlander crossing the slope at precisely the wrong hour, and the mountain had swatted him the way mountains do — without malice, without preference, without caring whose life got ruined.

His face was grey as the stones around him. His lips were already going blue at the edges.

"Well," he said, when five heads appeared over the lip of his rocky trap. His voice was thin but wry. "You'll be the crown's rescue expedition, then."

Nobody laughed except Wren, who laughed too loudly, the way children do when they're frightened.

* * *

They gathered ten yards off, in the lee of the granite shelf, and this was where the argument happened. Sorrel didn't start it. She only finished it, the way she finished most things.

"We can't move him," Gideon said. He'd gone down and looked at the leg; he came back up with his jaw set carefully neutral, which told Sorrel everything. "Not without a sledge and six men. The pin will have to be levered, and even then—"

"Then we don't," Sorrel said.

Gideon looked at her.

"He needs a surgeon, a sledge, and a miracle," she said, keeping her voice flat and reasonable, the voice of arithmetic rather than cruelty. "We have half a wheel of cheese. Every hour we stand here, Vess's riders close another mile. The supplies we're carrying are the difference between this company reaching the Veil or starving in a pass with a name nobody remembers. That man is dying whether we stay or go. Staying just means he doesn't die alone." She shrugged. "Some people think that matters. It doesn't."

"Sorrel," Tam said quietly.

"Don't *Sorrel* me. You've all been thinking it. I'm merely the one rude enough to say it." She looked around the circle — at the twins, who wouldn't meet her eyes; at Maren, whose scholarly face was doing something complicated; at little Wren, who was looking at Sorrel with those enormous dark eyes that saw far too much for a nine-year-old. "You want to know why I say it out loud? Because somebody has to carry it. That's all kindness is, most days — finding someone willing to be hated for being right."

The wind moved through the scree, ticking stone against stone.

"You're wrong about one thing," Sorrel added, softer than she meant to. "It isn't that I don't care. It's that I cared once, and my caring bought nothing but a second grave. Weigh that against your cheese and see which tips the scale."

For a moment, she thought she'd won. She almost wanted to.

Then Gideon Pemberton crouched down, picked up a flattish rock, and turned it over in his hands like a man deciding whether to skip it across a pond.

"When I was seventeen," he said, "Corvin left a man on a mountain. Not much older than you, Tam. Broke his ankle on the Drowned Road. Corvin said the same things you just said, Sorrel, and he said them better, because he'd had more practice lying to himself." He set the rock down. "The man begged. Not for rescue — he knew better. He begged for his boots. Said he'd need them where he was going. Corvin took them." Gideon's voice stayed level, but something under it had gone very quiet and very hard. "I've spent twenty years walking these roads wondering if every ghost I pass is wearing them."

"That's a story about a smuggler," Sorrel said. "We're not smugglers."

"No. We're people deciding what we'll be when we get wherever we're going." He stood. "Maren, how's your knowledge of levers?"

"Gideon—"

"Levers, Maren."

The old scholar sighed the sigh of a woman who had buried her principles years ago and found them resurrecting at inconvenient moments. "Adequate," she said. "Brann, ask your crows if there's a shepherd's track above us. Tam, get the rope. And somebody comfort that man — he can hear us, you know."

"He shouldn't," Sorrel said.

"No," Gideon agreed. "But he does anyway. Same as the rest of us."

He didn't look at her when he said it. That was the worst part. He didn't look at her at all.

* * *

It took four hours.

Four hours of wedging branches under the slab, of Maren directing geometry like a siege engineer, of Tam and Gideon hauling until the tendons in their necks stood out like bridge cables, while the man — his name was Aldous, he was a herb-gatherer, he had a sister in a valley whose name he repeated like a prayer — talked steadily to Wren to keep from screaming. Sorrel worked the lever with the rest of them because she refused, absolutely refused, to be the person who argued and then stood aside. If they were going to be fools, they would be fools together, and she would hate herself efficiently later, in private, where hating yourself belonged.

When the slab finally tipped free, the sound Aldous made wasn't words. They bound the leg as well as anyone could — Maren splinted it with tent poles and an amount of swearing that would have embarrassed a drover — and rigged a litter from the rope and oiled canvas, and by then the light was going amber and long, and Vess was another five miles closer, and Sorrel carried the front handles of the litter because her shoulders were strongest and because carrying something heavy was easier than carrying what she was thinking.

They made camp in a hollow below the ridgeline, a cup of stone with a single scrub pine leaning over it like a guilty conspirator. No fire. Cold rations. Aldous drifted in and out, fever-bright, and Wren appointed herself his nurse with the absolute authority of a nine-year-old who has decided something, feeding him snow-water drop by drop and telling him about the beetle.

Sorrel took first watch because she couldn't imagine sleeping.

That was where Gideon found her, two hours into the dark, sitting with her back to the pine and her knife across her knees. He lowered himself onto the rock beside her without asking, the way comfortable people do, and for a while neither of them said anything. Below, the valley was a floor of black velvet pricked with one distant light. A farmhouse, probably. Somebody's ordinary life.

"You hate me right now," Gideon said eventually.

"Fiercely."

"Good. Hate's honest. It's the polite ones you have to watch." He passed her a strip of dried venison. She took it because refusing would have been a conversation. "You argued well, you know. Better than Corvin did, if it helps."

"It doesn't."

"No. I didn't think it would." He chewed for a while. "Can I tell you the part of that story I left out?"

"You'll tell it whether I say yes."

"The man with the boots. I went back for him. Two days later, alone, against Corvin's orders." The stars were out now, hard and sharp and cold. "He was dead. But I took him off that mountain and buried him with his boots off, because I couldn't stand the thought of him needing them where there was nowhere left to walk. And I've never once regretted it, even though it changed nothing. Even though nobody knew." He glanced over. "Until now, I suppose. Twenty years late."

Sorrel stared at the single light in the valley until it blurred.

"My brother's name was Edden," she said, because the night was dark and the venison was salty and because some stones, once turned over, insist on showing their underside. "My gift killed him. That's the story everyone knows. The gift slipped, spooked a bull, and Edden stepped in front of me." She paused. "The gift didn't slip. I called the bull. I was fourteen and furious at Edden for something so small I've forgotten it, and I called the bull, and I've been paying for it for thirty years. So when I say leave the man on the mountain, understand — I'm not cruel. I'm *owed*. The world owes me a pass for what I did, and I collect it in advance, everywhere I go."

Gideon was quiet for a long moment.

"That's not a debt, Sorrel. That's a prison. And you built it yourself, which is either the saddest or bravest thing I've heard all year." He stood, brushing off his breeches. "Your watch. Scream if anything approaches. Or don't — Brann's crows will probably tell us first."

She let him get three steps toward his bedroll.

"Pemberton." Her voice came out rougher than gravel. "If it happens again — if we're cornered someday and it's supplies or a stranger — I'll argue to leave them. Every time. I need you to know that."

"I know," Gideon said. "And I'll overrule you every time, and you'll help me anyway, and one day you'll notice that's just what family does." He settled down beside the snoring shape of Maren. "Sleep when your watch is done."

Sorrel sat with the knife across her knees and did not sleep, and did not look at the valley light again, and thought about boots.

* * *

Two mornings later, the mountain tried to kill them properly.

It happened fast, the way the bad things always did: Wren ranging ahead on the descent, light-footed and invincible, finding a shortcut through a tumble of boulders — and the shortcut shifting. A slab the size of a cottage door, balanced for a thousand years on nothing but luck, chose that exact moment to remember gravity.

Sorrel saw it start. Saw Wren freeze beneath it, a small dark figure with her face tipped up, too stunned even to scream.

And then a man on a ruined leg was moving.

She never understood, afterward, how Aldous crossed that distance. Fourteen feet, maybe fifteen, on a splinted leg, on scree, with no warning and no hesitation — he simply *arrived*, shoulder-first, and hit the girl like a falling tree, and the both of them went skidding out from under the slab an instant before it came down where Wren had been standing. The impact shook the ground. Dust rolled over them in a slow brown wave.

When it cleared, Wren was sitting in the wreckage of a shrub, white-faced, entirely whole. Aldous lay beside her with fresh blood on his temple and a grin splitting his dusty beard.

"Told you," he said vaguely, to no one in particular. "Herb-gatherers fall down for a living. We're very good at it."

They dug him out and repacked the splint, and Wren refused to leave his side for the rest of the day, holding his hand over the rough ground as though she were towing him. Twice Sorrel caught the girl whispering to him — not to his ear, Sorrel noticed. To his *hurt leg*. And twice she saw the leg's answering twitch, the pain easing by some fraction no physician could measure.

Well. Thought Sorrel. There it is. The girl's gift runs to mending what hurts. Give it time. Give the crown time. They'll find a way to hang her for that too.

That evening, Aldous, propped against his pack with Wren guarding him like a knight of very small stature, cleared his throat and addressed the whole fireless circle.

"I owe you all a debt," he said, "and highlanders pay debts in the only coin we carry. Information." His eyes went around the group, and something in them had changed since the scree slope — some wariness burned away, replaced by a decision. "You're heading for the Veil. Don't trouble denying it; a one-legged man hears everything from the back of a litter, and you lot argue like a married council. So hear me." He leaned forward, and the firelight they weren't allowed to have seemed to gather in his eyes anyway.

"There's a bridge," Aldous said, "three days south of here, over the Hollowmere gorge. Old, beautiful, and rotten to its bones — the locals call it Hollow Bridge and won't cross after dark. And as of two days ago—" he tapped his own ear, "—word traveling down the shepherd tracks says there's soldiers camped at the far end of it. Waiting. With a lady in grey leading them, and a man in a smuggler's coat pointing the way."

The silence that followed had weight enough to bend the pine branches.

Sorrel felt it land in her chest like a swallowed stone. She looked across the hollow at Gideon, and watched the color leave his face — not fear, exactly. Something worse. Recognition.

"A smuggler's coat," Gideon said quietly.

"Yes," said Aldous.

"Not just any smuggler's coat." Gideon's voice was very steady, the way a rope is steady right before it parts. "There's only one man in Thornfell who knows these roads well enough to sell them." He stared into the dark beyond the hollow, at nothing, at the past. "Corvin."
