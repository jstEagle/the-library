## 10. Where the Map Ends

The map ended the way old men do — quietly, and with more dignity than anyone expected.

Maren Ostwick had carried that map for thirty years, rolled tight in oilcloth against her spine like a second spine of its own. She unrolled it now across a flat stone at the top of Greywater Pass, weighting the corners with four pebbles and one very indignant beetle, which Brann relocated with an apology.

"Here," she said, tapping a blank space just past the inked edge of the mountains. The parchment simply stopped. No *here be dragons*, no decorative flourish. The cartographer had drawn his border, signed his name in a neat clerk's hand, and then — nothing. As if the world beyond were a page he'd declined to write.

"That's it?" Tam asked. He was still favoring his left leg from the scree slope, though he'd sooner bite through his own tongue than say so twice.

"That's it," Maren said. "By order of the Crown Survey, Thornfell ceases to exist at this line. Not conquered. Not annexed. *Erased.* A generation ago they decided these valleys were empty, and everyone living in them became a rumor." She looked up, and there was something almost young in her face, something Gideon had never seen there in all the years since she'd first caught him talking to a mule and instead of reporting him had said, *Well. You're doing it wrong.* "Which means, children, that everything past this line is legally impossible."

"Good," said Sorrel, who had been legally impossible her whole life and had the scars to show for it. "I've always wanted to live somewhere that doesn't exist."

Wren laughed — the first real laugh out of her since Brann fell, since the gorge, since the crows had come back without his voice and only his last words folded carefully inside their wings. It startled all of us. I think it startled Wren most of all, the way a sneeze startles you when you've been holding your breath for days. Ilsa reached over and took her sister-less hand, and nobody made anything of it, which is the kindest thing a company of strays can do for each other.

I looked down the far side of the pass, where the land dropped into a green valley stitched with smoke from chimney fires. Real smoke. Cooking smoke. Somewhere below, someone was baking bread, and my stomach informed me with great authority that we would be investigating that whether the law permitted it or not.

"The ravens say there's a village," I said. "Call it Hollowmere, though they don't hold much with names themselves. They say the people there don't ask questions." I paused. "They also say there's a dog the size of a yearling calf who takes a professional interest in strangers, so maybe walk behind me."

* * *

We came down into Hollowmere as the light went long and gold, and it was true what the ravens said about the dog. His name, we learned later, was Barnaby, and he was indeed enormous, and he did indeed take a professional interest — by which I mean he trotted out to meet us, sniffed every single member of our party with the thoroughness of a customs official, and then sat down directly in front of Wren and waited, tail sweeping the road, until she scratched his ears.

"He says we smell like the mountain," Wren reported, "and like worry, but not like lies."

"A discerning animal," said Maren approvingly.

"He says most people smell like lies a little," Wren added. "He says it's mostly the ones who smile too fast."

Sorrel shot me a look. I pretended not to receive it. There are conversations you can have with your eyebrows, and that one would have taken all night.

The village itself was nothing the Crown would ever have counted: crooked houses with turf roofs going green, geese with strong opinions, a smithy whose forge-smell reached us before its sound did. But it was the details that undid me. A carved wolf's head over a doorpost — not a warning, the way such carvings were in Harrowgate, but a welcome, worn smooth by hands touching it on the way in. A girl of perhaps eight sitting on a fence, having what appeared to be a serious negotiation with three chickens, and losing gracefully. An old man who tipped his hat to Ilsa and Brann's crows as they passed overhead, as one greets respectable neighbors.

Nobody stared. That was the thing. In Harrowgate, after the Edict was read, people had learned to stare — to look at each other sideways, weighing, wondering, wondering who might be wondering about them. Here, a woman hanging washing glanced at our ragged, road-worn company, took in the horse-speaker walking stiff-legged beside a limping deserter, the scholar with contraband maps in her pack, the twins with crow feathers in their hair, the bitter woman, the beast-tongued man, and the child who talked to dogs, and simply said:

"You'll want the inn, then. Marta does a stew on seventh-days. Tonight's a seventh-day, if you're asking."

"We're asking," said Tam, with feeling.

* * *

The inn was called the Erased Lamb, which Sorrel declared the funniest name she'd heard in her life and refused to explain why, laughing until she had to sit down, which frightened all of us more than any patrol ever had. The innkeeper, Marta, was broad as a barn door and moved like weather. She took one look at us, set out nine bowls without counting heads (she counted right; I checked), and said the rooms upstairs were cold but honest.

I sat with my back to the wall, because some habits outlive the danger that bred them, and watched the room fill.

Musicians arrived by degrees, the way musicians do — first a fiddler, then a man with a drum he'd clearly made himself from a cheese press, then a woman with a wooden flute who played the way rain falls, easy and everywhere. And the music did something to our company that I have thought about many times since. Tam, who hadn't slept properly since the gorge, closed his eyes and let the fiddle carry him somewhere kinder than memory. Ilsa danced with a farmer's son and laughed at her own feet. Even Maren — stern Maren, ledger-keeping Maren — tapped one finger against her bowl in time, and when I caught her at it she glared at me with such ferocity that I knew she'd been caught exactly right.

Wren danced too. With Barnaby, who proved to have two left front paws and no shame whatsoever.

And Sorrel — Sorrel stood at the edge of it all with her arms crossed, wearing her bitterness like a coat two sizes too small, until the flute-woman drifted near and changed the tune to something older, slower, and Sorrel's crossed arms loosened by one notch, and that was enough. That was a beginning. You don't ask a wounded creature to run; you let it stand near the fire and decide the fire isn't lying.

I stepped outside for air, because joy can be heavier than grief when you're carrying both.

The night was huge and clean, stars in reckless abundance, no city glow to shame them. The valley breathed around me — woodsmoke, cut hay, the mineral smell of the river — and beneath it all, faint as a whispered conversation in another room, the voices of beasts. Goats complaining philosophically. A barn owl announcing its shift. Somewhere a fox telling jokes to no one, because foxes do.

For twenty-nine years those voices had been my secret and my sentence. Fluent in a language that could kill me. In Harrowgate I'd learned to keep my mouth shut around animals in public, to speak only in looks and gestures, to be a man alone in a crowd of creatures who knew me. Loneliest fluency in the world.

But here — here a boy was leaning on a fence talking earnestly to a donkey, and the donkey was answering, and neither of them was whispering, and nobody was burning anybody.

"Gideon." Maren had followed me out, moving quietly for a woman her age. She stood beside me at the fence and didn't say anything for a while, which is one of the things I love about her, insofar as I permit myself to love things, which lately has been more than is safe.

"They never left," I said finally. "The erased. Everyone said Thornfell was emptied out, and they just — stayed. Kept their villages, kept their names off every list, kept living."

"Crowns erase things on paper," Maren said. "Paper doesn't reach very deep into a valley." She pulled the map from her oilcloth, held it up against the actual living dark of the actual living hills, and snorted. "Thirty years I've studied this line. Do you know what I never once considered?" She turned the map over. The back was blank. "That the truth might be printed here. On the side nobody reads."

I laughed. It felt strange in my chest, like a door opening in a house I'd boarded shut.

"Wren's sleeping by the fire," Maren said, quieter now. "Barnaby wouldn't leave her, so Marta gave them both the good blanket. She asked about you before she went under. Asked if you'd stay." She didn't look at me. Scholars rarely look at you when they're handing you something fragile. "I told her I didn't know. I find I dislike not knowing."

"I don't know either," I admitted. Beyond the Veil was a story, a rumor dressed in hope. This valley was real bread and real music and a boy on a fence being understood by a donkey. Part of me wanted to stop here, stop running, plant myself in impossible soil and grow. "But there are others Maren. Your ledger has names. Names still hunted. If there's a way out of Thornfell—"

"Then it isn't only ours to take." She nodded slowly. "No. It isn't." She rolled the map away and squeezed my shoulder once, hard, which from Maren was an embrace with honors. "Get some sleep, smuggler. Tomorrow we ask the locals about doors."

* * *

I did sleep, eventually, in a room that smelled of lavender and mouse (the mouse introduced herself around midnight; we reached an accommodation regarding the floor vs. the rafters). And in the morning Hollowmere gave us its strangest gift, which was an ordinary day.

Tam helped mend a fence and got into a lengthy dispute with the farmer's horses about post placement, which the horses won. Brann — quiet Brann, hollow-eyed Brann, who had spoken perhaps forty words since the gorge — sat on a woodpile for two hours while the village crows came one by one to sit near him, not asking anything, just being near, the way you sit with the bereaved. Ilsa cried once, briefly, seeing it, and then went and made bread, aggressively, as though kneading dough could knead her heart back into shape. Perhaps it can. The loaves were magnificent.

And Wren — Wren ran through the village like she owned it, dog at her heels, showing every child she met how to listen to chickens, and the children listened, and the chickens held forth at tremendous length, and no soldier came, and no bell rang, and for one whole morning the world was the shape it should have been all along.

I stood in the square and watched her and felt something crack gently in me, the way ice cracks before it becomes water. Because I knew — the way you know weather coming, the way you know a bridge that groans — that this was borrowed. Happiness bought on credit. Somewhere south of us, Vess was riding with her hounds and her certainty, and Corvin was spending my name like coin, and Maren's duplicated ledger pages were sitting in whatever strongbox or sleeve they'd been copied into, patient as seeds.

But knowing a thing is borrowed doesn't stop you loving it. That's the trap of belonging. It gets into you precisely so it can hurt you later, and you let it, every time, because the alternative is to be Harrowgate — alive, lawful, and looking at your neighbors sideways forever.

That evening Marta fed us again, and the fiddler played again, and Maren sat with the village headman, a white-haired woman named Odile who spoke the way maps don't, and asked careful questions about the high cliffs to the north, and Odile answered even more carefully, and twice their eyes flicked to me — the beast-speaker — as though whatever door they were discussing had opinions about who came knocking.

I was refilling bowls when I noticed the ravens.

Our ravens — Ilsa and Brann's scouts, six of them, roosting on the inn's ridgebeam — had gone silent. Not resting-silent. *Listening*-silent, heads cocked all to the same quarter, southwest, the way we'd come.

I carried my bowl outside and stood in the yard and asked, softly, in the old tongue, *What do you see?*

And the eldest raven — the scarred one, the one who'd been tracking Vess's riders since Harrowgate — flew down and landed on the fence rail and looked at me with one flat dark eye, and said the thing I will hear in my sleep for a long time after.

*Riders,* it said. *Many. And they are not searching anymore.*

*They are following.*

Behind me, through the bright window, the fiddle was still playing.
