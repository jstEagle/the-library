## 11. The Ledger Betrayed

Tam had spent six years in the king's cavalry learning exactly how soldiers moved when they were following orders, and four months out of the saddle learning how they moved when they were following *him* — which was to say, badly, and with a great deal of complaining about the mud. So when he stood at the ruined watchtower's broken parapet at dawn and saw the dust plume rising from the eastern road, he knew before his heart finished dropping into his boots that this was not a patrol.

Patrols wandered. Patrols argued about whose turn it was to walk point. Patrols stopped to argue with farmers and steal eggs. This column came on like a needle finding the center of a map — straight, unhurried, certain.

"Brann," Tam said quietly. "Ilsa."

The twins were already awake. They were always already awake; crows kept terrible hours and worse gossip, and their speakers absorbed the habit. Ilsa appeared at his elbow without seeming to cross any of the space between, a thin girl with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had seen too much through too many borrowed wings.

"How many?" she asked.

"Forty. Maybe fifty." Tam's mouth was dry. "Cavalry outriders, foot behind. They're coming for us."

"They can't be," Brann said, joining them, still buttoning his coat wrong. "Nobody knows—"

"Somebody knows."

The word hung there between the three of them like something dead on a hook. Tam watched it swing. He thought of the fire last night, everyone laughing, Wren asleep against Gideon's shoulder, Sorrel almost smiling — almost, the way a cat almost purrs, in a way you could never prove. He thought of Maren Ostwick up past midnight with her papers, as always, her little lamp burning like a saint's candle.

He thought: *somebody knows.*

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the ridge, the camp had dissolved into its component anxieties. Horses were being saddled by people who owned horses and led by people who didn't. Sorrel had taken the high ground with her crossbow and an expression suggesting she'd been right about everything all along and would be mentioning it shortly. Gideon stood in the middle of it all with Wren clinging to his sleeve, talking low and fast to the mule — because of course he was, even now, even with soldiers coming, the man could not walk ten paces without negotiating with something on four legs.

"—yes, I know the road is wet, she knows the road is wet, we *all* know the road is wet—" Gideon paused, listened to whatever the mule said, and pinched the bridge of his nose. "Fine. FINE. You lead. But if you take us down the goat track again I'm letting Sorrel carry you next time."

"He's bargaining with a mule while the crown descends upon us," Sorrel called down from the rocks. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"Why not both?" Gideon called back, and there was a scattered, ragged laugh — the kind of laugh people make when they're frightened and grateful to anyone who gives them permission.

Tam didn't laugh. He was watching Maren.

The old scholar stood at the tower door with her ledger clutched to her chest — the ledger, the great book of names, every gifted soul still breathing in Thornfell, written in her small precise hand. She'd carried it across half the country like a relic. She was looking at the dust plume with an expression Tam couldn't read, and that frightened him more than the soldiers did, because Tam had spent years reading officers' faces for the order that would get him killed, and he could read anybody.

Almost anybody.

"Maren," he said. "We need to move. Now. The drowned road, like Gideon said — the otters will scout the crossings."

"Yes," she said. "Yes, of course." But she didn't move. Her thumb was moving along the ledger's spine, back and forth, back and forth, like a woman worrying a rosary.

And then Tam saw it.

The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth against the rain — Maren was nothing if not careful — but the wrapping had slipped, just enough, and Tam could see the edge of the cover. The leather was dark where it should have been pale. Warped. The whole book had the swollen, buckled look of something that had been wet and dried badly.

They hadn't crossed water in nine days.

"Maren," Tam said again, and his voice came out strange. "When did your book get soaked?"

* * *

She lied to him first. He gave her that much credit later, when everything was ash between them — she lied to his face, quickly, competently, the way a scholar lies, with citations. "Rain in the pass, before the watchtower. Paper remembers water, young man, no matter how carefully one dries it."

But Tam had grown up in a house of printers. He knew paper the way Gideon knew beasts. And paper that has been soaked dries cockled, wavy, every page rippled like wind over a wheat field — and the pages he'd glimpsed over Maren's shoulder these past weeks had been flat. Smooth. Fresh.

Somebody had copied the ledger. Somebody had made a new book, and the old one had gotten wet doing it, or been made wet deliberately, and Maren Ostwick — proud, brilliant, impossible Maren — was standing in front of him holding the evidence and lying about rain.

"Ilsa," Tam said, not taking his eyes off the old woman. "How many crows does it take to count fifty soldiers?"

"One," said Ilsa, "if the crow is me."

"And how long since anyone new joined this company?"

Silence. The good kind, then the bad kind. Brann frowned. Sorrel came down off her rocks with the crossbow held casually, which meant not casually at all. Gideon stopped mid-negotiation with the mule and turned around slowly.

"What are you asking, Tam?" Gideon said. His voice was gentle. That was the worst part. Even now, even suspecting what Tam suspected, the man sounded like someone asking after a sick horse.

"I'm asking," Tam said, "why fifty crown soldiers know exactly where a camp of eight fugitives is sleeping, in a ruin that isn't on any map, off a road that officially doesn't exist." He turned to Maren. "And I'm asking why your ledger smells like fresh ink."

* * *

It went badly, the way such things go. Not with shouting — worse, with quiet.

Sorrel wanted to tie her up. "Search her pack," Sorrel said, flat and cold, and Tam hated how reasonable it sounded. "Search everything. We've got a child here. We've got people who've bled on this road. If she sold us—"

"I have not *sold* anyone," Maren snapped, and for a moment the old fire was there, the imperious scholar who'd once made a university department resign en masse. "I have spent thirty years keeping the only honest census this country has ever had. Every name in that book is a life I have guarded with—"

"With what?" Sorrel stepped closer. "With copies?"

The word landed like a dropped glass.

Maren went white. It was extraordinary to watch, Tam thought distantly — a woman so controlled, so armored in erudition, simply cracking down the middle. Her hands tightened on the ledger until the knuckles went the color of tallow.

Gideon moved then. Not toward Maren — toward Wren, drawing the girl gently behind him, and Tam understood that Gideon had done the arithmetic too, and that whatever else happened, the child would not watch this.

"Maren," Gideon said quietly. "Tell us."

"You don't understand what they did." Her voice shook. "You weren't there. When they took my position, my name, my *library* — do you know what an Inquisitor does with a scholar? They don't burn scholars, boy. Burning is a mercy for peasants. They *read* you. Vess sat in my study for three days and turned my life into an inventory, and at the end of it she put a document in front of me and said, quite pleasantly, 'Continue your work, madam. Send me duplicates. Or I will send for the people in your book, starting with the ones you love most.'"

The wind moved through the ruined tower. Somewhere below, the mule stamped impatiently, because mules do not care for dramatic pauses.

"So I wrote them," Maren said. "Every month. Duplicates. Pages and pages of names, in my own hand, delivered to a drop in Harrowgate, and every month I told myself I was buying time — that the information was stale by the time it arrived, that I was protecting far more than I was betraying, that a ledger in motion saves lives a ledger in hiding cannot—"

"You told yourself stories," Sorrel said.

"I told myself *the truth as I could bear it*," Maren flared, and then, horribly, her voice broke. "There are two hundred and eleven names in that book. Two hundred and eleven people alive *because* I knew where they were, because I sent warnings, because I moved them before the soldiers came. Yes. Some of those soldiers came because of me too. Do you think I don't do that sum every night? Do you think I sleep?"

"Do you?" Tam asked softly. "Because the soldiers outside aren't lost, Maren. They're not searching. They're coming *here*, to a ruin that isn't on maps, and the only person who knew this place was on your route notes."

Maren looked at him. And Tam watched the last wall go.

"The route notes," she whispered. "Oh. Oh, God. I copied the route notes too. Last week. They demanded the itinerary — they said if I withheld it they'd assume the worst and act accordingly, and I thought — I thought the schedule would change, I thought we'd move faster than the post, I thought—"

"You thought," Sorrel said, "and people die. Same as always, scholar."

"That's enough." Gideon's voice cut across the clearing, quiet and absolute, the voice he used on spooked animals. "Sorrel. Enough."

"She sold us."

"She survived," Gideon said. "Those aren't the same thing, though I know it feels like it." He turned to Maren, and Tam saw something complicated move across his face — anger, yes, but underneath it, recognition. "Did you tell them about Wren? By name?"

Maren's silence was answer enough.

Gideon closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he was still gentle, which took a strength Tam wasn't sure he possessed. "Then we ride. All of us. Now. And Maren walks in the middle where I can see her, and when this is over — if we're alive, if we reach the Veil — then we decide what she owes. Not before. Nobody decides anything with fifty soldiers on the road. Agreed?"

Nobody agreed. But nobody objected either, which in that company amounted to a treaty.

* * *

They went down off the tor in a strung-out line, horses and fear and one deeply offended mule leading the way down a slope that thought it was a cliff. Tam rode rear guard, which was where he'd always been most comfortable — watching the thing coming for you instead of the backs of the people you'd failed.

Ilsa dropped back beside him. Above them, three crows wheeled, reporting.

"Two miles," she said. "Maybe less. Brann says the outriders split — they're trying to flank the drowned road."

"They know the road."

"They know the road," she agreed. Then, quieter: "Tam. Was it really her? The old woman?"

"It was really her."

"But she didn't *want*—"

"No," Tam said. "She didn't want. That's the thing nobody tells you about betrayal. It hardly ever looks like the stories. It looks like a tired person making the best bargain available and calling it survival." He glanced at her. "Your brother would've said something cleverer."

"Brann says clever things. I say true ones." Ilsa's jaw was set. "If Vess gets that ledger — the real one, the full one —"

"Then two hundred and eleven names stop being people and start being targets." Tam checked the girth strap out of pure nerves. "So the ledger doesn't get captured. Whatever happens to us."

Isla looked at him sharply. "Whatever happens to *us*?"

"Priorities, twin," Brann called from ahead, having heard everything, because crows hear everything and so do their people. "He's learned them from the army."

"I unlearned them from the army," Tam said. "Took me four months and a very judgmental mare."

Despite everything — despite the dust plume and the cracked trust and the old woman riding silent in the middle of the line with her ruined book hugged to her chest like a wounded bird — Ilsa smiled. Small, but real. That was the company of strays for you. Broken crockery, glued together, somehow holding water.

* * *

They made the drowned road by late morning, the flooded smugglers' route winding through country so wet and green it seemed to apologize for Thornfell's stony north. The otters met them at the second crossing — Gideon whistled, low and odd, and three sleek heads broke the water like punctuation marks. He crouched at the bank for a long moment, listening, and when he stood his face had gone careful.

"Soldiers at the fifth crossing," he reported. "Ahead of us, not behind. They were told where we'd be." He looked back down the line, and his gaze rested on Maren for half a heartbeat — not accusing, just weighing, the way you weigh a bridge before crossing it. "The good news is they don't know about the ford at the willow stand. The otters say the water's chest-high but honest."

"Honest water," muttered Sorrel. "At least somebody in this company doesn't lie."

"Sorrel," Tam started, but Gideon caught his eye and shook his head. Later. Everything was later, now. Trust had shattered in the camp like a dropped jug, and all of them were walking barefoot through the pieces, pretending not to bleed.

They forded at the willows, cold to the ribs, Wren riding the mule across with the solemn dignity of a queen reviewing troops. On the far bank, soaked and shivering, Maren tried to hand Gideon the ledger.

"Take it," she said. "Please. I am apparently the worst possible guardian of it. I have been the worst possible guardian of it for two years, and pride kept me from saying so, and pride may yet kill every one of you."

Gideon looked at the book for a long moment. Then he closed Maren's fingers back around it.

"You keep it," he said. "You keep it, and you remember every name in it, and you spend the rest of your life earning the right to have written them. That's heavier than carrying it, Maren. I wouldn't rob you of the weight."

Tam, wringing out his sleeves, thought: *that's the difference between a soldier and whatever Gideon is.* A soldier would have taken the book. A soldier would have been right to.

Maren made a sound that might have been thanks or grief, and tucked the ledger away, and the company moved on into the green wet country with fifty soldiers somewhere behind them and the shape of a rumor — the Veil, the door, the way out — pulling them forward like a fish on a line.

It was Brann's crow that found the thing, an hour later. The bird came down shrieking from the ridgeline, agitated, circling, and Ilsa went pale as she listened.

"There's a rider ahead of us," she said. "Alone. Sitting his horse at the top of the gorge road like he's waiting for a coach." She swallowed. "Brann says he's dressed fine. Too fine for a soldier. And he's smiling."

Tam felt the cold come up out of the river water and settle into his bones, because he'd heard Gideon talk about exactly one man with that particular mixture of hatred and grief in his voice.

"Gideon," Tam called up the line. "Someone's waiting for us on the gorge road. Someone who dresses well."

Gideon stopped. Turned. And Tam watched the color leave the smuggler's face like water out of a breached hull.

"Corvin," Gideon said, to no one, to the river, to the ghost of every kindness that had ever been a transaction. "Of course it's Corvin."
