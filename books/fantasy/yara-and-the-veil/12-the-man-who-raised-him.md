## 12. The Man Who Raised Him

The river knew him first.

Gideon felt it as a change in the water's voice — the low chatter of the gorge current shifting, the way a room goes quiet when someone unwelcome walks in. The otters had gone still on the far bank, all six of them lined up like small brown witnesses, and otters only did that when something was wrong or something was interesting, and lately those had become the same thing.

"He's alone," Gideon said.

"You're certain?" Maren asked, easing Wren back behind a boulder with one hand while the other stayed white-knuckled on her walking stick. For a disgraced scholar of seventy-odd years, she could move like a woman half that when she wanted to, and she wanted to now.

"The river says so." He crouched at the water's edge, let his fingers trail the current. It was an old habit, half comfort and half listening. "One horse. No soldiers. No dogs." He paused. "He wouldn't bring dogs. He knows I'd hear them coming a mile off, and he wants me to hear *him*."

"Why would anyone want that?" Tam muttered from the rocks above.

"Because Corvin Ashgrave has never once in his life done anything he didn't want witnessed." Gideon stood, wiped his hand on his coat, and found that his hand wasn't entirely steady. "He wants me to know it's him. That's the whole point of dressing well for a mountain road."

Wren peered around Maren's skirts — she'd taken to standing behind Maren rather than Gideon, which stung less than it might have, since it was sensible. "Is he the one who sold you?"

Gideon looked down at her. She'd been asking blunter and blunter questions all week, the way children do once they've decided the adults have stopped protecting them from answers. "He's the one who taught me which stars to steer by," he said. "Whether he sold anything is what we're about to find out."

"And if he did?"

"Then I'll owe him a debt of honesty," Gideon said. "I'll tell him exactly what I think of him."

Sorrel, sharpening her knife against a stone with slow, deliberate strokes, made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Do try not to enjoy it too much."

* * *

The gorge road ran along the river's north bank, a shelf of packed earth barely wide enough for two horses abreast, cut into the cliff face where the water had spent ten thousand years arguing with stone and mostly winning. Halfway along, the shelf widened into a gravel flat the smugglers called the Parlor, because it was where you sat down with whoever was waiting for you, and some conversations ended in trade and others in blood, and the Parlor didn't judge.

Corvin Ashgrave sat in the Parlor on a folding camp chair that had no business existing that far into the wilds, eating dried apricots from a lacquered box.

He hadn't changed. That was the first cruelty of it. Gideon had spent eleven days imagining him older, softer, diminished by whatever price the crown had paid him, and instead there he was: broad through the shoulders, silver at the temples in the way that men pay barbers to achieve, boots polished to a shine that mocked the entire concept of wilderness. His coat was deep green wool, crown-cut, with the discreet grey piping of a licensed trader. He looked like a man who had never once slept in rain he hadn't chosen.

"Gideon." Corvin said his name the way he'd said it a thousand times over thirty years — warm, unhurried, faintly amused, as if Gideon were a slightly late delivery. "You look terrible. You always did take badly to being hunted."

"You've seen my hunting before, then," Gideon said. "From which side?"

"Ah." Corvin set down the apricot box and folded his hands, and Gideon hated how familiar the gesture was, hated that he knew what came next, because Corvin folding his hands meant Corvin was about to be reasonable, and Corvin being reasonable had talked more people onto the high passes in winter than greed ever had. "Sit down, boy."

"I'm forty-one years old."

"Sit down anyway. You'll want to be sitting."

Gideon didn't sit. Behind him, at the mouth of the Parlor, the others had come up the road in a loose line — Maren with her stick planted like a general's standard, Tam and Sorrel flanking, the twins somewhere up on the rocks where crow-speakers always put themselves, and Wren pressed close to Maren. Gideon heard the girl's heart without meaning to; he'd been hearing hearts more and more lately, though hearts weren't beasts and never answered him. Fear made everything loud.

"You sold the drowned road," Gideon said. "Hollow Bridge, the otter crossings, the Parlor itself. Vess knew exactly where we'd come out of the fens. Her hounds picked up our trail within hours of the flooded route. There's no way she knew unless someone drew her the map."

"There is no version of this conversation," Corvin said mildly, "where you don't already know the answer. So yes. I sold the road."

The words landed the way a dropped stone lands in still water — rings going outward, everyone on the gravel flat watching them spread. Sorrel's knife stopped moving. Tam made a low sound in his throat.

Gideon had rehearsed rage on the walk down. He'd built it carefully, log by log, the way you build a fire in wet weather. But standing here, what rose instead was older and stupider: the memory of being nine years old and hungry, and a well-dressed man crouching down to his eye level on just such a road, saying *you've got a way with animals, boy? Good. So have I, and mine is legal. Let's compare notes.*

"You taught me the passes," Gideon said. "You taught me which ravens take bribes and which take offense. You carried me through the fever winter of '89 on your own saddle. And you sold me to the Inquisition for — what? What does amnesty even buy these days?"

"Not being burned," Corvin said simply. "Which is more than your friend Maren's ledger-buyers got, if you're pricing things honestly." He glanced past Gideon at the company and inclined his head, courteous as a merchant at market. "Maren Ostwick. Still keeping lists of people you get killed?"

"Shorter than yours," Maren said crisply.

"Mm." Corvin turned back to Gideon, and for a moment — just a moment — something moved behind the polish. Not guilt. Gideon knew guilt when he saw it; he saw it in mirrors. Something smaller and sadder. Nostalgia, maybe, which in a man like Corvin was just arithmetic performed on the past. "I'm going to offer you something, and I need you to listen to all of it before you start talking to the fish about me."

"The fish already have opinions."

"Of course they do. You raised them to be judgmental." Corvin rose from the camp chair, unhurried, and walked to the gorge edge where the river ran white and loud thirty feet below. He gestured at the country beyond — the grey shoulders of the Thornfell range, the dark stitchwork of pine, the whole vast erased nothing that the maps called a blank and the crown called Crown Forest Reserve Number Four, as if naming it that would make it forget it had ever had a name. "Look at it, Gideon. Look at what we spent our lives in. A country that doesn't exist. Every year colder, every year thinner, every year fewer of us. The Edict didn't create the hunting, it just made it official. Do you know what happens to a man who runs routes for a living when the crown closes the roads?"

"He retires," Gideon said. "Buys a tavern. Becomes insufferable."

"He drowns," Corvin said. "Slowly or quickly, but he drowns. I chose slowly. Amnesty, a license, a house in Harrowgate with a garden. All I had to give them was ink. Roads on paper. Names on paper." His eyes came back to Gideon's, and there it was at last, the transactional heart of him, laid open without shame because shame had never once entered into it. "Your name was on the second page. Wren's was on the third."

* * *

Later, Gideon would try to remember whether he'd moved toward Corvin or whether Corvin had moved toward him, and he would not be able to say. What he remembered was Tam's hand on his shoulder, firm as a fence rail, and Sorrel saying quietly, "He's not worth the river's time," which was the closest thing to tenderness she'd offered anyone in weeks.

"She's a child," Gideon said. His voice came out low and even, which surprised him. "Nine years old. She scratched at my door half-dead in a storm. Whatever ledger you're keeping, whatever column she belongs in — there's no number that covers her."

"No," Corvin agreed. "There isn't. Which brings me to my offer."

"Don't," Maren said, from behind. "Boy, don't listen. Men like this make offers the way spiders make silk."

"I'll listen," Gideon said, because he owed the man who'd carried him through the fever winter at least that much. "For old roads' sake. Then we're square."

Corvin smiled, and it was the smile Gideon had followed up a hundred mountains, and it cost him nothing, and that was the whole tragedy in miniature. "Vess rides three hours behind me with sixty men and a witch-finder. I told her I'd speak to you first, because I told her you were reasonable, and because — " a flicker, quickly smoothed, "— because I preferred to be the one holding this conversation. Here is the offer. Amnesty for all of them. Full papers. The scholar keeps her freedom and her library habits. The deserter gets his discharge backdated and honorable. The twins, the sharp one, the stranger they pulled off the scree slope — pardoned, resettled, wages from the crown's beast corps if any of them are fool enough to want work. Everyone walks out of this gorge alive, inside the law, by sundown tomorrow."

The Parlor went very quiet. Gideon heard the river, and the wind in the pines, and Tam breathing beside him, and understood that Corvin had chosen his bait well, because every soul on this gravel had spent a month wanting exactly that, and here it was, gift-wrapped in green wool.

"And the catch," Gideon said.

"One item stays off the list." Corvin's voice didn't change at all, and that was the obscenity of it — the same tone he used for quoting tolls. "The girl. Wren comes back with me to Vess. The Inquisitor has a particular interest; the child spoke to her captors' horses the whole wagon ride, apparently, which impressed even the witch-finder. Vess wants her cured personally." A beat. "You can't save everyone, Gideon. I learned that lesson at twenty and it's kept me alive twenty-five years past my expected date. Take the deal for the rest. They'll curse you for a season and thank you for a lifetime. It's the best mathematics you will ever be offered."

Gideon looked at him for a long moment. Thirty years of memories rearranged themselves, not vanishing — that was the terrible part — but re-sorting, the way a ledger re-sorts when you learn the columns were mislabeled. Every kindness filed under *love* quietly migrated to the column marked *investment*. The fever winter. The stars. Even, God help him, the nine-year-old boy carried on a saddle: cargo, maintained.

"You know what the cure is," Gideon said. "You've seen them after. Empty houses with the doors painted shut."

"I've seen survivors," Corvin said.

"That's not the same thing."

"It's the only thing on offer in this country. It's the only thing that's ever been on offer." And then — briefly, terribly — Corvin Ashgrave looked tired. Just for a breath, the polish thinned and Gideon saw the man underneath, sixty years old, standing on a ledge above a drowned road in boots that had never touched honest mud, and looking, for once, like what he was: an orphan from the pass country who had decided at some point long ago that the world was a market and he would rather be a seller than goods. "I carried you out of the snow once," Corvin said quietly. "Let me carry you out of this. It's the same act. It was always the same act. Leave the girl."

Behind Gideon, he heard Wren speak for the first time. Her voice was small and steady, the voice of a child who had already lost one village and priced her own odds accordingly.

"He's not going to leave me," she said. "But thank you for the arithmetic."

And despite everything — despite the gorge and the witch-finder three hours out and the thirty years of misfiled kindness — Gideon laughed. One short, cracked bark of it, startled out of him. Wren looked up at him, puzzled, and he shook his head. *Later*, he mouthed. *I'll explain later.*

"No," Gideon said.

Corvin sighed, and reached into his coat.

Everything on the gravel flat moved at once — Tam's hand to his belt, Sorrel's knife coming up, Maren shouting something — but what Corvin drew out was not a weapon. It was a whistle. Bone, old, yellowed, carved in the shape of a leaping hound, and Gideon felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world, because he knew that whistle. He'd heard it on night roads all his childhood. It was the calling-whistle for the pass hounds, the big black ones, the ones Corvin had bred himself, back when breeding a beast meant loving it, back before everything was ink.

"You said no dogs," Gideon said.

"I said *you'd* hear them coming," Corvin said. "That was true when I arrived." He put the whistle to his lips.

The sound that came out was thin and sweet and wrong, a note bent out of its true shape, and the gorge answered it. From downstream, from the treeline, from the high rocks — belling. Deep-throated, many-voiced, closing fast, and beneath Gideon's feet the river rose up in a chorus of alarm: *they are coming, they are coming, they do not smell right, they smell of nothing at all.*

Cured hounds. Emptied. Dogs whose voices had been taken and replaced with obedience, running a scent-line straight down the gorge road toward sixty men and a witch-finder, with a kingpin's whistle to guide them.

"You were never buying my forgiveness," Gideon said. "You were holding me here until the leash caught up."

"I was offering you a future," Corvin said, folding his hands again. "It's not my fault you've developed a taste for the present."

"Down!" Maren's voice cracked like a whip. "Everyone down — the cut-path, the otter run, GO —"

And then it was all motion: the company breaking for the narrow scramble on the north wall where the flood-scour had cut a stair into the rock, Wren scooped under Gideon's arm like a bundle of sticks, Tam hauling Maren, Sorrel covering the rear with her knife held low. Behind them, unhurried, Corvin folded his camp chair. Above them, on the rocks, the crows went up in a black ragged sheet, screaming the alarm in a language the hounds couldn't hear anymore but the mountains could.

Gideon reached the foot of the cut-path, looked back once.

Corvin stood alone in the Parlor in his polished boots, watching him go, and lifted one hand — not a threat, not quite a wave. A farewell between business partners whose accounts had finally failed to balance.

"Three hours," Gideon said to his people, to the wind, to anyone listening. "Maybe less. The cut-path is climbable but the gorge crossing isn't — the bridge at the narrows is the only way over, and Vess will know that too."

"Then we'd better hope," Maren said grimly, pulling herself up the first handhold, "that the bridge is still standing when we get there."

From far downstream, getting closer with every breath, the hounds sang their hollow song.
