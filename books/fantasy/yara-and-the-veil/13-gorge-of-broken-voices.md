## 13. Gorge of Broken Voices

The crows told us about the gorge before we ever saw it.

They came in at dawn, three of them, landing on the broken parapet of the watchtower with the particular hunched insolence of birds who believe — correctly, as it turned out — that they are the smartest things present. I felt their words arrive the way you feel rain coming: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron.

*Stone split. River loud. Man-who-smells-of-knife says narrow way.*

"Thank you," I said aloud, because Brann always said you should thank them, they remember slights longer than kindnesses, and a crow holding a grudge will tell every crow from here to the coast that you are rude.

Brann was already awake, of course. My brother slept like a cat in a sunbeam and woke like one too — all at once, mid-stretch, hair going eleven directions. He shuffled over to stand beside me at the wall, shoulder warm against mine, and together we watched the three crows preen and wait for praise.

"Narrow way," he repeated. "Narrow is bad."

"Narrow is fast," I said. "Fast is what we want. Vess's riders were two valleys back last night."

"That's what worries me." Gideon had come up behind us without making a sound, which was a thing he did, a smuggler's habit worn into his bones. He looked tired in the particular gray way he looked tired every morning since Harrowgate, like a man carrying water in his hands and refusing to set it down. "The twins' birds say the crossing at Hollow Bridge is watched?"

"The bridge is watched," I confirmed. "But there's another way across. The crows have seen it. An old smuggler's line, upstream. The gorge narrows to a stone spine — a natural causeway over the water."

"How narrow?" Maren asked, joining us with her ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest the way other people carry infants.

I asked the crows. They conferred among themselves with a sound like dry leaves arguing.

*Wide enough for sheep,* they reported finally, with the air of creatures who considered this generous.

"Wide enough for sheep," I translated.

"Sorrel's not a sheep," Tam muttered from below, where he was failing to convince his mare that breakfast existed. The mare was speaking back to him in the flat equine equivalent of *no*, and honestly I sided with the horse. Sorrel had been walking like a drawn blade for three days.

"She'll manage," Gideon said, and then, quieter, so only those of us on the wall heard it: "Everyone manages. That's the whole trick."

Later — much later, when the wind came down the gorge and I heard nothing but river where my brother's voice should have been — I would think about how casually he said it. How we all nodded. How none of us knew yet that *managing* has a price, and the road keeps accounts.

* * *

We reached the gorge by late afternoon, and it was worse than the crows had let on, because crows measure with wings and we measured with feet.

The water ran a hundred feet below, white and furious, so loud that ordinary speech died in your throat. You didn't talk at the gorge. You shouted, or you didn't bother. The stone spine itself was exactly as advertised: a rib of gray rock maybe four feet wide at its broadest, slick with spray, running forty yards across the gap like the backbone of something enormous that had lain down across the river to die.

"It's beautiful," Wren breathed. She'd been riding on Tam's shoulders for the last mile, claiming her legs were tired, which everyone knew meant she wanted the view. Even now she leaned out over the drop with the fearlessness of children and goats, and Gideon's hand shot out and gathered her collar without even looking, the way you catch a falling cup.

"It's beautiful from *here*," Gideon said. "From the middle it's just rock and regret. We cross single file. Hands free. No talking once you're on the spine — the wind comes up that channel sideways and it will lie to your feet."

"Ilsa goes first," Brann said.

I looked at him. "Why me?"

"Because if the crows need to tell you anything, they'll tell you first, and you can pass it back down the line. And because—" he grinned, that grin that had gotten us fed through two famines and out of one jail cell, "—you're steadier than me and everyone knows it."

It was true and he knew it was true and he said it anyway, easily, the way he gave away everything: compliments, food, the last dry sock. My brother hoarded nothing except secrets about his own fears, and I was the only one he ever shared those with, usually at night, usually in whispers, usually ending with both of us laughing too hard to be scared anymore.

So I went first.

The spine was everything Gideon promised and less. The wind came up the gorge in gusts that shoved like drunk uncles; the spray made the stone treacherous as ice in patches; the roar below swallowed every thought until all that was left was *next step, next step, next step.* Halfway across I stopped listening to my feet and listened instead to the crows, who had flown out over the chasm and were wheeling above us, calling directions against the wind.

*Left side dry. Left side dry. Small-brother coming.*

I made it. I stepped off onto solid ground on the far side and my knees did an undignified wobble and Tam, waiting there, gripped my arm and said something I couldn't hear over the river but understood anyway, because his face was doing the thing faces do after a crossing like that.

Then it was Brann's turn.

He went light, the way he did everything. Ilsa first, then Wren, then Maren, then Tam with the mare led loose behind (she crossed like a queen, glaring), then Sorrel, who crossed like a woman settling a debt with the stone personally. Gideon went last, because Gideon always went last, because Gideon had decided somewhere back on the drowned road that his job was to be between us and whatever came, forever, and none of us had the heart or the argument to dissuade him.

Brann was nearly across. Ten yards. Eight.

I was watching him, grinning, already composing the joke I'd make — something about him walking like a duck, something to take the fear out of it retroactively — when the wind came.

Not a gust. A *shove*. The whole gorge inhaled and exhaled at once, a great gray fist of air funneling up the channel, and I saw it hit my brother like a hand.

He didn't scream. That's the part I keep returning to, the part I turn over at night like a stone looking for what's underneath. He didn't scream. His arms went out, his body tipped, and in the half-second before the rock took him he looked straight across the gap at me — found me instantly, the way he always found me in crowds — and his mouth moved.

Then the spine shrugged him off, and the river took him, and the world went very simple and very quiet except for the water, which never stops, which doesn't care, which roared on with my brother in its teeth like it was nothing, like he was nothing.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to go back onto the spine.

Gideon tells me later that he caught me two steps out, that I fought him, that it took Tam as well, that I said words he declines to repeat. I don't remember any of it. What I remember is the far bank, and the spray, and the empty place on the rock where my brother had been, and the crows.

The crows.

They went down. All three of them, folding off the wind like dropped stones, diving toward the white water while the rest of us stood frozen on either side of the gorge. And this is the thing about crows, the thing people forget because crows are rude and thieving and laugh at funerals: they do not abandon their people. Brann had fed them from his hand since the watchtower. Brann had learned their names — actual names, Fess and Cobb and the old scarred female he called the Duchess — and they had accepted them, and they dove into the throat of that gorge after him as though love were a thing with wings, which I suppose it is.

Sorrel got a fire going. I don't know how long we waited. Hours. The light went from gold to copper to nothing. Somewhere in there Wren fell asleep against Maren's side, exhausted past grief into the blunt mercy of childhood, and no one woke her, and I hated her a little for it and loved her more.

And then, near midnight, with the fire burned to red coals and my hands so cold I'd stopped feeling them, the Duchess came back.

She landed on the rock in front of me, wet to the skin, feathers dark and dripping, and she looked at me with one bright eye, and she opened her beak, and she gave me my brother's last words.

That's what nobody understands about the gift, before they have to learn it the hard way. It isn't a trick. It isn't a convenience. It's a door, and sometimes what comes through it is a mercy, and sometimes what comes through it is the worst thing that will ever happen to you, dressed in feathers, delivered faithfully, because the bird promised and birds keep their promises.

His words came in fragments, the way drowning men speak, carried up from the water's edge where the crows had found what there was to find. But they were his. His cadence, his warmth, his terrible timing.

*Tell Ilsa—*

A pause. The wind. The river underneath everything, still roaring.

*Tell Ilsa it wasn't scary. Tell Ilsa the water was cold but the flying—*

Another fragment, further down the river, from Fess, breathless:

*—the crows came. He saw the crows come. He was laughing.*

And then the Duchess, last of all, the final piece, the one she had held back until she was sure I could bear it, though she couldn't have known, birds don't know these things, and yet:

*He said: don't let her go quiet. He said: she does that. When it hurts. Don't let her go quiet.*

I sat by the fire and I repeated his words to them, all of them, out loud, in order, because Brann deserved a proper delivery and because saying them made them real and real was the only way through. Gideon cried. I had never seen him cry and I will never tell anyone I saw it, but his shoulders shook and he didn't try to hide it, and Wren woke up and understood immediately, the way children understand death instantly and totally, and she crawled into my lap despite being far too big for it and stayed there.

Tam broke the mare's lead rope rather than ask her to carry anything that night. Sorrel sat apart with her arms around her knees and said, to the fire or to no one: "I argued to leave a stranger on the scree. I argued for the supplies. And the river took the best of us instead." Her voice cracked down the middle. "There's no fairness in it. There's no ledger that balances."

Maren, of all people, said nothing at all. She held her ledger against her chest and stared into the coals, and I remember thinking, distantly, through the wool of my own grief: *she knows something about prices, that one. She's been counting them for years.*

* * *

In the morning we buried what the river returned, which was little, which was almost nothing, which was somehow worse — a grave too small, filled with stones from the gorge because it was all we had of him. Tam cut a marker. Gideon stood at the head of it and said, in his low rough voice, "He crossed every hard road laughing. I only knew him nine days. They were the best nine days this company has had, and I include the day we ate well."

Nobody laughed. But Wren said, wetly, "He'd have laughed at that," and that was almost the same thing.

I did not cry at the grave. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, since I'm the only witness left who'd tell it true. I did not cry, because crying is noise and I had gone quiet, the way I go quiet, the way he knew I'd go quiet, the way he'd sent word from the middle of a river specifically to stop me from doing it.

*Don't let her go quiet.*

So instead I climbed the rise above the grave where the crows were waiting — all three, damp and solemn, which for crows is unprecedented — and I spoke to them in front of everyone, my voice raw as torn cloth.

"Fess. Cobb. Duchess. You went down for him. You brought his words home when no one else in this world could. From this day, whatever we have, you have a share of. Whatever we know, you know first. And when you die — not soon, mind, I forbid it — you'll be buried under the best tree I can find."

The Duchess tilted her head. Then she hopped closer and did the most shocking thing a crow has ever done in my hearing: she pressed her forehead, briefly, against my knuckle.

*Small-brother's sister,* she said, and it sounded like a name being given. *We remember.*

* * *

We walked that day because stopping felt like drowning and moving felt like almost-drowning, which is at least a different sensation. The company moved like a body with something missing. Twice I started to turn and whisper a joke to the space beside my left shoulder before remembering. Twice the crows filled the silence instead, bringing news of the road ahead, and each time their voices arrived I felt it like a hand on a wound.

Toward evening, Gideon fell into step beside me. He didn't offer comfort, which I was grateful for; Gideon had a smuggler's sense for cargo, and he seemed to understand that my grief was packed fragile and labeled accordingly.

"The birds say anything about the country ahead?" he asked finally. Carefully. Giving me somewhere safe to put my voice.

I asked. The crows had been ranging wide since morning, scouting our flanks out of habit and loyalty both, and what they brought back made them excited in the way of birds who have seen something worth gossiping about.

"There's a valley ahead," I said. "High up. And there's—" I paused, because the report was strange even by crow standards. "There's a bear. An old one, huge, gray-muzzled. It's been walking the same direction we're traveling. The crows say it walks like it's *going somewhere*. Like it knows the road."

Gideon stopped walking.

"Maren," he called forward. "How far to the cave the carvings mentioned? The ones from the erased villages?"

Maren turned, and I saw her face do something complicated — surprise, yes, but underneath it something older and more frightened, a look like a woman hearing a debt called due. She glanced up at the crows wheeling overhead, then at the gray bulk of mountain rising ahead of us.

"Closer than I'd like," she said quietly. "And if a bear is showing you the way, children, then the mountain has opinions about us. The old stories say that when Thornfell's beasts choose to guide someone..." She hesitated.

"What?" Wren asked.

"When they guide you," Maren said, "it's because the Veil is near. And the Veil, my dears, always asks for something."

Above us, the crows wheeled once against the darkening sky — three shadows where there should still have been a fourth — and flew on toward the high valley, and the bear's tracks in the old snow led the way.
