## 14. What the Mountain Remembers

Maren Ostwick had spent forty years arguing that knowledge was a kindness. She had written three treatises on the subject, all of them burned or confiscated or — in one memorable case — used by a fishmonger in Dell's Crossing to wrap herring, which she chose to take as commentary on their market value rather than their content.

Knowledge was a kindness, she had always maintained, because ignorance was a wound that festered. The man who did not know the bridge was out did not drown any drier than the man who did.

She was beginning, on the ninth day out from the drowned road, to reconsider the entire thesis.

The company walked ahead of her in a straggling line along the high shoulder of the mountain: Tam up front with his coat collar turned against the wind, talking under his breath to nothing in particular — though Maren knew from experience that somewhere within a mile there would be horses listening and taking notes. The twins ranged the slopes like a pair of ill-omened sheepdogs, Ilsa whistling, Brann throwing pebbles at crows he claimed were insubordinate. Sorrel brought up the rear with her hand never far from her knife, watching the backtrail the way other people watched a pot about to boil over. And Wren — Wren walked beside Gideon, as she always did now, chattering at him about everything and nothing, and Gideon wore the particular expression of a man who has been thoroughly captured by something small and fierce and has decided not to struggle.

Maren followed, leaning on her staff, keeping her ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart, and tried not to think about what she'd seen in the firelight two nights past, when she'd thumbed through the old fragments one more time and found a line of Old Thornic she'd translated wrong thirty years ago.

*One life, freely given.*

She had spent two nights telling herself it was a mistranslation after all. A scribal error. A metaphor. Scholars were very good at metaphors when the literal reading would ruin their sleep.

That was when the bear arrived.

* * *

It came out of the treeline without ceremony, the way weather does. One moment the slope ahead was empty brown grass and wind-bent pine; the next there was a bear standing in the path, and every human thought on the mountainside stopped at once.

It was old. That was the first thing Maren noticed, scholar before survivor. Its muzzle was gone silver-grey, its pelt scarred along one flank in long pale seams, its ears ragged as old parchment. It stood taller than Tam and looked at them not with the dull caution of a beast surprised but with the weary patience of a landlord arriving to find squatters.

"Nobody move," Gideon said quietly. "Nobody run."

"I wasn't going to run," Brann said, in the voice of a boy who had absolutely been calculating distances. "I was going to walk away briskly."

"Its ears are forward," Gideon murmured. He took one slow step off the trail. "It's not hunting. It's—"

He stopped. His face did something complicated.

"Gideon?" Wren whispered.

"It wants us to follow it."

"That," said Sorrel flatly, "is exactly what a bear that wants to eat us would want us to think."

Gideon didn't answer right away. He stood with his head slightly tilted, listening to a voice none of the rest of them could hear, and Maren watched him and felt the familiar ache of it — the loneliness of translation, being the only one in the room who knew what was being said.

"It's asking for me specifically," he said at last, and there was wonder in it, and something underneath the wonder that frightened him. "By name. Not my smell. My *name*. It says—" He faltered, started again. "It says the mountain remembers me. It says the mountain remembers *all of us*, and it's tired of waiting."

The bear turned and began to walk, unhurried, along the trail they had been following anyway. Then it paused and looked back over one grey shoulder, and the look was so unmistakably *well?* that even Sorrel made an involuntary noise of indignation.

"I don't trust it," she announced.

"You don't have to trust it," Gideon said. "You have to outrun it, and you can't. Neither can I. Follow the bear."

"That is the worst advice I've ever heard spoken aloud."

"He's right, though," Ilsa said. Her crows had come down from the crags and were sitting in a row on a dead pine, unusually silent, unusually attentive, all their black heads turned toward the bear like a congregation. "The crows are bowing. Crows don't bow."

"They're crows," said Brann. "Bowing is beneath them."

"They're bowing," Ilsa repeated, and started walking.

Maren went last, because someone had to keep an eye on the whole foolish procession, and because — though she would have denied it under torture — she wanted to be near the bear. Near it, and afraid of it, in the way she had been afraid her whole life of exactly the things she most wanted to understand.

Old Thornic scholars had a word for such creatures. *Veth-mar.* Old-walker. Memory-that-breathes. The texts spoke of them the way sailors speak of the sea: with reverence, and with instructions never, ever to turn your back.

* * *

The bear led them up a fold in the mountain that no map Maren had ever memorized admitted existed — and she had memorized maps the way other women memorized hymns. The trail narrowed, switched back twice, and finally slipped between two slabs of granite into a hollow where the wind suddenly died, as if the mountain had put a finger to its lips.

The cave mouth was half-hidden by a curtain of ivy gone rust-red with autumn. Above it, worn nearly smooth, there were carvings.

Maren's breath left her in a rush, and her knees — forty years older than anyone else's in this company — nearly followed.

"Circles," she breathed. "Look. Look at the lintel stone. That's the triple circle of Thornfell, pre-Edict, pre-crown — and beneath it, the seam symbol. The door." Her fingers hovered an inch from the stone, close enough to feel the cold coming off it. "I've seen this mark once before, on a coin fragment in the Harrowgate museum cellar, and the curator told me I was imagining the pattern." She laughed, a short, disbelieving sound. "Forty years they called me mad, and here it is, carved six inches deep in living rock, and the rock agrees with me."

"The rock," said Sorrel, "is also full of a bear."

But the others had gathered round now, even Tam, even the twins, and Wren had her palm flat against the stone the way children touch things — wholly, without apology.

"There are pictures inside," Wren said. "Lots of people. All walking the same way."

The bear rumbled once, low, and stepped aside from the cave mouth with the air of a doorman who has done this before and does not require gratitude.

They lit no torches at first; they didn't need to. The cave's throat bent, and beyond the bend there was light — a pale, sourceless glow off the walls themselves, some property of the strange white stone, so that the carvings seemed to float up out of the dark at them like fish rising in a still pond.

Maren had read descriptions of this place. She had assumed the descriptions were embellished. Scholars of her acquaintance, herself included, embellished constantly; it was practically the job.

The reality stopped her mouth.

The walls were covered — floor to ceiling, wall to wall, a hundred feet of it — in carvings so fine they might have been done with needles. Processions of people. Beasts of every kind walking among them, bears and wolves and crows and horses and small quick weasel-shapes, rendered with such affection that you could see individual whiskers. And everywhere, recurring like a heartbeat, the seam: a vertical line of light in a cliff face, drawn with rays around it, and figures passing through.

"The Veil," Tam said hoarsely. "It's real. Sweet saints, it's actually real. Maren — tell me I'm reading this right. Tell me this says what I think it says."

Maren moved along the wall slowly, reading, translating aloud in a half-voice, the habit of a lifetime of lecturing to empty halls.

"This panel is a departure. See, the country behind them — fields, towers, that's Thornfell as it was. And the people here are grieving, see the raised arms, but the ones going through the door—" She paused. Adjusted her spectacles, which needed no adjusting. "The ones going through are singing. Or marching. Hard to say with ancient sculptors; everyone either sings or grieves, nobody ever just walks."

"And the beasts?" Gideon asked quietly. He was standing very still before the largest panel, and Maren saw that his hand was pressed flat against the carving of a wolf, fingertips in the grooves meant for fur. "Why are the beasts on both sides?"

"I don't know yet. Give me time. There's an inscription above the central figure, it's badly eroded—"

She worked at it while the others spread through the gallery behind her, their voices bouncing softly — Ilsa and Brann arguing about whether a certain crow was carrying a key or a worm (Brann held, with passion, that no self-respecting crow carries keys), Wren narrating each animal to Gideon, Tam walking the procession with his hat off, respectful as a man in church.

Maren translated the inscription alone.

It took her ten minutes. It was Old Thornic of the late period, formal register, the kind used for oaths and law. She worked methodically, word by word, and the words came together in her mind like stones into a cairn, each one heavier than the last:

*The door opens to the freely given. One life, given whole, of the giver's own will. So it was paid in the first crossing, so must it ever be. The beasts know the price. The beasts remember.*

She read it four times.

Then she stood very still in the pale light, an old woman with cold hands and forty years of burned treatises behind her, and listened to the laughter of children echo off the walls of the place that proved her life's work true, and understood that she was holding in her two hands the single most important sentence in the history of her country, and that if she spoke it aloud, one of the people laughing behind her would die.

Not might. Would. Because she already knew these people — knew them the way you know people you've kept alive in a ledger, page by page, name by name. Gideon would offer first; she had watched him for nine days, and generosity came off that man like heat off a stove. Then Tam, out of soldier's duty. Then Sorrel, out of something darker that Maren recognized and pitied. They would argue. Wren would try to volunteer and be forbidden, and carry that wound forever. And then someone would actually do it, because that was what this band of strays did — they kept choosing each other at cost, over and over, like fools, like saints.

And the price was real. The stone said so. The stone had said so for centuries, patiently, to whoever came.

*The beasts know the price. The beasts remember.*

Her eyes went to the largest panel again, to Gideon's wolf. And then she saw what she had missed, scholar's blindness, looking without seeing: the panels didn't end at the door. There was a final scene, smaller, tucked in a corner like an afterthought — the door open, the fugitives through, and the beasts standing between the door and a fallen figure. Beasts massed at the threshold like a wave. Carved deep, re-carved, touched by generations of hands until the stone shone: the animals refusing. Standing in the doorway. Breaking the frame itself, the seam-symbol scored through, deliberately, violently — and beyond it, on the far wall, a new carving, younger, rougher: people walking free, and beasts beside them, and no payment shown anywhere.

Someone had added to this wall within living memory. Perhaps within years.

The price was demanded. And once — perhaps more than once — the price had been refused, and refused successfully, and the record of that refusal was scratched into the stone like a shout.

Or the carver had lied. Hopeful hands could carve lies as easily as fearful ones. She had no way to know. That was the thing about knowledge that no treatise had ever prepared her for: it came in pieces, and the pieces did not fit, and the gap between them was exactly wide enough to drive a decision through.

"Maren?" Gideon's voice, behind her. "What does it say? You've gone white."

She turned.

They were all looking at her now. Eight faces in pale light — Tam steady, the twins curious, Sorrel suspicious as ever, Wren bright-eyed, Gideon worried for her, worrying about *her* while he himself walked toward a door with his name on it. Nine days she had watched them feed each other from short rations, mend each other's boots, stand each other's watches. Nine days of found family, assembling itself out of wreckage with the stubbornness of weeds through pavement.

Knowledge is a kindness, she had written. But there are two kinds of knowing: the kind that prepares, and the kind that kills hope early. If she told them now, tonight, on this mountain, days from the Veil, the price would sit in every silence between them. Wren would lie awake doing arithmetic. Gideon would begin arranging himself for sacrifice the way he arranged everything else — quietly, without asking permission.

If she waited — if she told them at the door, or told them gently, or told them only Gideon — she bought them the road. Days of unburdened walking. The chance to arrive as people still capable of joy, whatever waited at the end.

It was a lie of omission. It was also, possibly, mercy. She had spent her whole career unable to distinguish between those two, and here, finally, was the exam.

"It says," Maren said, and her voice was admirably steady, "that the Veil is real. That it opened before, in the first age, and the beasts walked beside our ancestors through it. That the beasts remember us — that they have kept the memory of this place for a thousand years, waiting for people worth leading here." She gestured at the great procession, the singing figures, the wolves with their needle-carved fur. "Every rumor you've ever heard is true. We are not running toward a story anymore, my dears. We are running toward a fact."

A cheer went up — Tam's fist in the air, Wren whooping, even Sorrel permitting herself one sharp exhale that was almost a laugh. The crows outside, hearing it through the stone, set up a racket of their own.

Only Gideon kept looking at her. Only Gideon glanced, briefly, at the corner panel where the beasts broke the door, then back at her face, with the faint crease between his brows that meant a man was filing something away to ask later, privately, when no one else could hear.

Maren met his eyes and did not flinch, and hated herself a little, and held the ledger of names against her chest, and thought: *forgive me. I will tell you the truth when truth can save more than it kills. Until then, let them sing.*

The bear was waiting at the cave mouth when they emerged into the late gold light. It looked at Maren longest of all — a long, level, silver-muzzled regard, as if it too could read, and had read the inscription over her shoulder, and was curious what she would do about it.

Then it turned downhill, toward the south and the last passes, and walked ahead of them into the dusk like a guide who knew exactly where the road ended — and what stood waiting there.

Behind them, far to the north, Maren later learned, a rider in Inquisition grey paused on a ridge, looked down at a ruined watchtower and the cold ashes of a camp that wasn't there anymore, and smiled.

The road to the Veil ran through a crossing called Hollow Bridge.

They had four days' start, and no idea whatsoever what was coming.
