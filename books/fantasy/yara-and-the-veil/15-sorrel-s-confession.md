## 15. Sorrel's Confession

The watch was mine from moonrise until the small hours, which everyone in the company knew meant it was the worst watch, and everyone knew I had claimed it on purpose, and everyone was too polite — or too frightened of me, which I preferred — to say so.

We were camped in a fold of hills below the old shepherd's road, a shallow bowl of grass and wind-bent pines with one good approach from the east. The horses dozed in their picket line, Tam having murmured something to the gray mare before turning in that made her stand like a sentry all night, ears swiveling at nothing. Wren slept curled against Maren's ledger-satchel as if it were a large, important dog. Brann and Ilsa snored in unison, which should not have been possible for twins who could not agree on anything else.

And Gideon Pemberton sat up by the fire's last coals, whittling a stick into something that had started as a spoon and was rapidly becoming a weapon.

I settled across the fire from him with my knees drawn up and my knife out — not to threaten anyone, just because a knife is comforting company on a cold watch — and said, "You're holding it wrong."

"It's a spoon."

"It was a spoon an hour ago."

He turned it in his fingers, considered. "It's developed opinions."

"Everything you touch develops opinions," I said. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it?"

He laughed, quiet, so as not to wake the sleepers. And then he did the thing he always did when conversation got near anything true: he looked past me, toward the treeline, where I knew — we all knew — he was half-listening to whatever the night birds and the field mice and God knew what else were saying about us.

"Anything?" I asked, flat.

"A fox two ridges over complaining about her cubs," he said. "A barn owl who thinks we're rude for camping in her hunting ground." He paused. "And a hedgehog under Tam's bedroll who would like to register a complaint."

"There's no such thing."

"There is now. He moved in this afternoon. Tam named him General."

I almost smiled. I felt it coming, like weather, and shut it down at the border. This was the trouble with Gideon Pemberton: he was warm the way a hearth is warm, and you could stand next to him for months before you noticed your frost melting, and by then it was too late to pretend you hadn't.

That was why I'd taken the night watch. Not for the company's sake.

For mine.

* * *

The fire sank. Gideon fed it one of our precious split logs, and the sparks went up like a handful of thrown stars, and somewhere in the dark an owl asked its question again and again, and he answered it — not aloud, but I saw his lips move, saw his head tilt, and I hated him a little for how easy it all looked, the way some people are easy with a gift they never asked for while other people choke on theirs.

"You can ask," he said suddenly, without looking at me.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever it is you've been circling since the scree slope. You look at me like a woman composing a letter she'll never send." He set the not-spoon down. "I'm a patient man, Sorrel, but I've read the same page of you forty times now."

"I didn't know I was a book."

"Everyone's a book," he said. "Most people just don't bother reading each other."

I turned my knife over in my lap. The blade caught the firelight and threw a thin orange line along the pine needles.

"My brother's name was Alden," I said.

He went very still. Not dramatically — Gideon was incapable of drama; he had the emotional range of a well-made chair — but stillness came off him like cold off stone, and he listened, which was the whole reason I had waited for a night watch. Because Gideon Pemberton listened the way other men prayed.

"He was older than me by two years. Bigger, louder, braver, stupider — the usual arrangement. We grew up in the hill country east of Harrowgate, back when nobody had heard of the Veil Edict, back when a gift was just a strange thing your family pretended wasn't there at supper." I found myself smiling at the memory, which was dangerous, so I kept talking fast. "Mine showed early. Birds, mostly. Crows especially. I could tell them things and they'd do them — simple things, fetch this, follow that. Alden thought it was the finest thing he'd ever seen. Used to make me order crows to steal pies off windowsills and call it training."

Gideon's mouth curved. "Practical."

"Criminal," I said. "But yes. Practical."

* * *

Here is the story I told the company, the story that has followed me down every mile of this wretched road, told around fires by people who thought I couldn't hear them, softened with every retelling the way a stone softens in a river:

Sorrel's gift killed her brother. She called the crows and the crows brought the patrol, or spooked the horses, or caused the accident — versions vary — and ever since, poor bitter Sorrel has carried the weight of a death her magic caused. It explained her. It excused her sharp tongue and her flint heart. It made her tragic, and tragedy is a kind of armor, if you're lucky enough to be granted one.

Here is the truth, which I have never said aloud to any living soul, and which sat in my chest now like a swallowed coal:

My gift didn't kill Alden.

I let him die.

* * *

It happened in autumn, three years ago. We were running lantern-oil over the border passes — not smugglers exactly, more like people who noticed that oil cost four times as much on one side of a mountain as the other and drew the obvious conclusion. Alden drove the cart. I walked ahead with the crows as outriders, and the crows saw the crown patrol before we did: six riders, moving to cut the pass behind a fallen tree, patient as spiders.

There was a game trail branching off to the north. Narrow, rotten with wet leaves, hugging a slope that dropped sixty feet into a boulder-field and a river that ate travelers twice a year. I knew it. Alden knew it. We had used it twice before, and both times Alden had said the same thing afterward: *never again, Sorrie, that path is a coffin with scenery.*

I stood at the fork with the crows thick on the branches above me, screaming warnings I already knew, and I calculated.

If we doubled back, we lost the cart, the oil, everything — a season's wages, and Alden's debts besides, and he owed men who did not accept apologies. If we took the game trail, we might get through. Might. With a loaded cart, in the dark, on leaves.

Sixty feet. Rocks. River.

I looked at the trail. I looked at Alden's face, turned back toward me, trusting, asking with his eyebrows whether I'd found a way out.

And I thought — and I have relived this thought ten thousand times, and it has never once changed, and I could recite it in my sleep, and sometimes I think I do — I thought: *if we lose the cart, we're finished anyway.*

Not: *he might die.* Not even: *we might die.* Just the ledger. Just the arithmetic. A season's wages weighed against my brother's life, and I put my thumb on the scale without telling him what side of the trail I was sending him down. I gave him the hand signal for *clear*, the one that meant *go, all's well.*

He believed me because I had never once lied to him.

The cart went over a quarter mile down the slope, where the leaves lay thickest over the rot. I heard it happen. I will spare you what I heard, because I have to carry it and you don't, but I heard all of it, every second of it, standing at the top of that slope with my hands over my ears doing nothing, absolutely nothing, because going down after him would only have meant dying too, and I had already done my sums.

The crows stayed with him. All night. I could hear them, and I made myself listen to every word, and that — that, I think, is the nearest I've come to penance, though it isn't nearly enough, because penance should cost something and listening was merely unbearable.

When the patrol found me the next morning, walking down the high road alone with my face arranged into grief, I told them the crows had panicked the horses. I built the story right there, out loud, with tears, and it was so much easier than the truth, and it worked so well, and I have been living inside it ever since, the way you live inside a coat that fits badly but keeps off the rain.

"So," I said to Gideon, twenty-odd years later and a hundred miles from that mountain, my voice coming out steady, which I had not expected and did not deserve, "you see the difficulty. Everyone in this company believes my gift killed my brother. It's kinder that way. And when the Inquisition catches us — when, not if — and they offer us the cure, I'll take it. I want you to understand that, because you keep looking at me like I'm someone worth saving, and I need one person in this world to know that I'll take the cure gladly. Whatever they carve out of me, whatever emptiness they leave — it won't be a violation. It'll be a sentence finally served."

* * *

The fire cracked. Somewhere off in the dark, the owl asked again, and Gideon did not answer it, which I noted distantly, the way you notice rain starting.

I had expected many responses. Horror. Disgust. Perhaps that particular gentle pity that is worse than disgust. Perhaps he would simply stand, and walk away, and take the first watch himself, and after that things between us would be correct and cold forever, which was fine, which was what I deserved, which was—

"No," Gideon said.

I blinked. "No, what?"

"No, I don't agree." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his face in the ember-light was not horrified and not pitying. It was stubborn. Gideon's whole face was, at bottom, stubborn; it was the load-bearing feature of the man. "You've carried that story so long you've forgotten it's a story. You made it up on a mountainside to explain something that didn't need explaining."

"Didn't need—"

"Sorrel." He said my name the way he spoke to frightened animals, low and level, and I resented it and let him do it. "You were twenty-two years old, in the dark, with a patrol on your heels and debts on your brother's back, and you made a judgment about a trail. That's all. That's the entire crime. A judgment. The same judgment a hundred smugglers have made on a hundred nights and lived to be grandfathers about."

"The trail killed him."

"No," Gideon said. "The trail didn't kill him, and you didn't kill him, and here's the part you won't want to hear, so I'll say it slowly." He held my eyes. "You didn't even choose wrong. You chose the only road that offered any chance at all, and you gave him the sign because the sign was true — it *was* clear. It was clear, and the mountain lied, and the mountain has been lying to you ever since in your own voice."

"You weren't there."

"No. But I've spent my life listening to things that can't lie," he said, "and I know what lying sounds like. It sounds exactly like that. Exactly like a woman on night watch rehearsing a punishment for herself because a punishment is easier than a grief."

My jaw ached. I realized I was gripping my knife hard enough to hurt my hand, and I made myself loosen my fingers, one by one.

"You don't understand," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, traitorous thing. "I did the sums. I stood there and I *weighed him*. What kind of person weighs their own brother against a cartload of oil?"

"A frightened one," said Gideon. "Which is the only kind there is."

"And the cure—"

"Would not be a punishment," he said, "because there is no crime. And I'll tell you what it would actually be, since you seem determined to romanticize it." His voice roughened, and for the first time all night he looked away from me, out at the dark trees, at whatever was listening out there. "I've seen cured ones. Before the Edict made it fashionable. A drover outside Harrowgate, years back — they took the voices out of him and left the rest, and he sat by his own fire like furniture. His dogs didn't know him anymore. That's what their cure is, Sorrel. Not a sentence served. An amputation performed on a healthy limb by butchers who've confused silence with virtue."

"They'd empty me out," I said. "Maybe that's the mercy."

"It is not," he said, with sudden heat, "the mercy. And you don't get to decide it is, because I forbid it."

"You *forbid*—" A laugh tore out of me, ragged, entirely unplanned. "Who in God's name put you in charge of my soul?"

"Nobody," Gideon said placidly. "Your soul clearly manages its own affairs badly. Someone had to intervene."

I stared at him. He picked up the not-spoon and resumed whittling, chips falling into the embers, as if he had not just spent the last five minutes prying the floorboards up off the cellar where I kept everything.

"The forgiveness is yours," he said, to the spoon rather than to me. "Whenever you want it. It's not going anywhere. But I won't agree that you deserve the alternative, and I won't stand quietly by while you volunteer for it, and if the day comes when the Inquisition offers you that bargain, you will find me inconveniently in the way." He glanced up. "Fair warning."

"That's not how forgiveness works," I managed. "You can't just — hold it in reserve. Like grain."

"Why not? It keeps well."

"Gideon—"

"Also," he said, "your watch. There's a fox coming up the eastern rise, and she's very interested in our food sack, and I could tell her to go away but I suspect you'd rather handle it yourself."

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand — quickly, roughly, hoping he'd pretend not to notice, and he pretended, because he was a decent man, damn him, decent in the specific irritating way of people who refuse to let you be worse than you are. I took the food sack and hung it higher in the tree, and the fox watched me from the bracken with bright professional eyes, and I told her — aloud, uselessly, wordlessly, whatever it is I do — that this camp was closed, and she sighed like a merchant and melted away.

When I came back to the fire, Gideon had fallen asleep sitting up, the not-spoon in his hand, his head tipped against a pine trunk, breathing slow. The owl asked its question. The embers breathed. Wren murmured in her sleep and rolled toward the warmth.

Forgiveness offered, sitting there in the coals like a banked flame. Not accepted. Not yet. Maybe not for a long time. But I sat down across from him and kept the watch, and for the first time in three years I did not spend the dark hours doing sums.

Near dawn, Ilsa's crows came down out of the pines in a ragged, frantic black spill, screaming a single message over and over, and Ilsa sat bolt upright with her eyes wide, and the words the birds brought her were these:

Riders. Many riders. Moving on Hollow Bridge.
