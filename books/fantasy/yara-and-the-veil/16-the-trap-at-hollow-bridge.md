## 16. The Trap at Hollow Bridge

The bridge had no right to be called a bridge. It was a hunched spine of old stone spanning the Hollow — a gorge where the river had given up on being loud and settled for being cold — and it was the last crossing before the high country where the Veil waited. Maren said it had stood since before the erasure. The moss on it looked older than the crown itself.

Gideon did not like it.

Not because of the stone, which had carried smugglers and their mules for three generations and would carry them a while yet. Not because of the drop, though the drop was enough to make a sensible man walk wide. He didn't like it because it was quiet. Too quiet. The Hollow was usually full of talk — dippers working the shallows, an old heron who complained about everything, water saying its one long word over and over. This morning the river spoke in a whisper, and the birds were gone from the alders, and every beast-sense he owned stood up straight with its ears back.

"They know," he said softly to Maren, at the head of the line.

"Of course they know," Maren murmured, adjusting the ledger wrapped against her chest like a swaddled infant. "Vess has tracked us across half of Thornfell. The question is whether she knows *here*."

"The ravens say riders took the north road at dusk." That was Ilsa, low, with Brann nodding beside her. A crow sat on his shoulder doing its statue impression, which meant it wasn't relaxed at all. Crows only went still when they were listening hard.

"North road's a feint," said Tam. He kept touching his horse's neck the way a man touches a talisman. "Or bait. She wants us at a crossing. There's only one."

Gideon crouched and put two fingers in the wet gravel at the bridgefoot. Hooves. Many. But days old, layered over each other, coming and going — not passing through. Stopping. Turning. A camp that had been patient.

He rose slowly. His mouth had gone dry, which was absurd; he'd crossed a hundred bridges with the crown's shadow on his heels. But this time he had more than contraband to lose. He had a girl with storm-grey eyes who was currently arguing with Sorrel about whether jerky counted as breakfast if you ate it before dawn. (It did not, Sorrel maintained, on strictly moral grounds.)

"Wren," he called, keeping his voice easy. "Walk with me."

She came, chewing, triumphant, having evidently won the jerky argument by simply eating it. She fell into step beside him at the edge of the trees and studied the bridge with the frank, unafraid stare she gave everything, as if the world were a door she intended to open eventually.

"You're doing the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The one where your jaw goes all stone. You do it when you're scared but don't want the horses to know." She glanced at the pack animals. "The horses already know."

Of course they did. He exhaled through his nose. "Wren. If anything happens at this crossing—"

"Nothing's going to happen."

"—if anything happens, you go to Maren. You stay behind her. You don't argue, you don't bargain, you don't try to be brave. Brave is for afterward. First comes alive."

Wren considered this with great seriousness, the way she considered everything, then said, "You always come for me, though. Right? At Harrowgate you came. In the flood you came."

"Every time."

"Then it doesn't matter what happens." She said it simply, as a fact of the world, like rain moving east. And then, because she was eleven and mercies embarrassed her, she added, "Your bootlace is undone," and ran off to pester Brann about whether crows could count past seven. (They could. They just refused to demonstrate on demand.)

Gideon knelt and tied the lace. His hands were steady. It was the rest of him that felt like the bridge looked: old, load-bearing, and quietly afraid of the weight.

* * *

They crossed in ones and twos, as tradition demanded — never all the cargo on one plank — and it was nearly done, Tam and the horses across, Maren mid-span with Wren trotting behind, when the alders on the far bank stopped pretending.

They came out of the treeline in a long unhurried line, grey cloaks beaded with morning fog, crossbows already levelled, and at their center rode a woman on a black gelding with white stockings, sitting straight-backed and still as a saint on a cathedral wall.

Inquisitor Vess.

"There now," she said, and her voice carried across the water without effort, the way voices do in gorges when the air itself conspires to deliver them. "No one needs to be a story today. Lay down what you're carrying and step away from it, and most of you will walk out of this gorge. I give you my word on the Edict."

"Somebody's word," Sorrel said bitterly, at Gideon's shoulder. He hadn't heard her cross back. Her hand was on her knife, and her knuckles were white around the grip, and Gideon remembered what she'd told him on night watch — *the cure feels like punishment I've earned* — and understood that for her this moment had teeth on both sides.

Maren had stopped dead in the middle of the bridge. Wren stood close against her side. Too far to reach. Ten yards of slick ancient stone between them, and forty crossbows drinking in the space.

"Slowly," Vess called. "All of you. The ledger too, Mistress Ostwick. Yes, we know about the ledger."

Gideon's blood went cold in a new way. The ledger was hidden under Maren's cloak, wrapped in oilcloth, and no soldier should have known its shape.

And then a man stepped out from among the soldiers, and the cold became something else entirely.

Corvin.

He wore a new coat. That was the detail that lodged in Gideon like a splinter — a fine coat, oiled wool, crown green, and Corvin filled it out the same as ever, broad and easy, with his grey stubble and his broken-knuckled hands and the half-smile that had greeted Gideon every time Gideon had come down out of the passes with a run completed and coin owed. Twenty years of that smile. Campfires. Lessons in reading weather and reading men. *Trust the road, lad, and the mule before the man.* Corvin, who had taught him that the first rule of smuggling was that everyone has a price, and the second rule was to hope you never learn your own.

"Hello, Gideon," Corvin said. Warm as a hearth. He turned, unhurried, and pointed one thick finger across the river, and the finger found Gideon in the trees as precisely as a compass finds north.

"That's him. That's the beast-talker I told you of. Raised him myself, near enough." He said it loudly, for the soldiers' benefit, a man delivering proof of honest work. Then, quieter, across the water, almost fond: "Sorry, lad. Amnesty's amnesty. You understand better than most — everything's got a price. I just finally learned mine."

Everything in Gideon went very simple and very hot. Later he would not remember crossing the open ground at the bridgefoot, but he must have, because suddenly he was standing at the south end of the span with forty crossbow bolts trained on his chest and his voice tearing loose.

"You *pointed* at me." His own voice sounded strange to him, scraped raw. "Twenty years, Corvin. You taught me which stars don't lie."

"Aye, and you were the best I ever had." Corvin's jaw worked. For half a heartbeat something old moved behind his eyes — grief, maybe, or the ghost of it. "That's why it had to be you worth so much."

"He's lying," Wren said suddenly, from the middle of the bridge. Her voice rang thin and fierce over the water. "He's not sorry at all. I can hear it in — " 

"Hold her," Vess said.

Two soldiers moved onto the span. Maren threw an arm out, and Tam shouted something, and a bolt thudded into the planking a foot from his boot in answer, and then it was over the way bad things are over — quickly, efficiently, without any drama the heart can use. A soldier took Wren's shoulder. She bit him, God bless her, and another caught her arms, and they walked her off the bridge between them, small and furious, her heels dragging twin lines through the frost.

Something broke loose in Gideon's chest, some animal thing that had been leashed all his life by prudence, by secrecy, by the Edict's long cold shadow. He took one step onto the bridge.

A dozen bolts shifted to follow him.

"Stop." Vess raised one gloved hand, and the line held. She rode forward to the bridge's far end, close enough now that Gideon could see her properly for the first time: a narrow face built for disapproval, eyes the flat brown of a frozen pond, and — unexpectedly — a small scar through one eyebrow, old and badly stitched, that made her look briefly, horribly human.

"Gideon Pemberton," she said, reading the name off him like a warrant. "Guides, smuggler, beast-speaker of considerable talent. Do you know how I know 'considerable'?" She didn't wait. "Because my hounds have followed your voice for two hundred miles, and they are *fond* of you. That is a rare thing, Mr. Pemberton. Beasts do not grow fond of liars or cowards." She swung down from the gelding, unhurried. "So here is the arrangement. You will surrender — truly surrender, no tricks, no whispered pleas to your feathered spies — and the child walks free. Unharmed. I am not in the business of curing children by force. That is a slander, and I'll thank you not to believe it."

Behind her, held between soldiers, Wren shook her head, small and sharp. *She's lying,* the shake said. And Gideon, who had spent his life hearing truth from creatures that never spoke a word of human language, believed the girl over the Inquisitor without a flicker of doubt.

But believing didn't move the crossbows.

His knife came out of his belt slowly. He'd carried it eleven years; Corvin had given it to him at fourteen, hilt worn smooth, saying *a blade's for opening crates, lad, nothing else.* He crouched — the soldiers tensed — and laid it flat on the old stones of the bridgefoot, and stood back from it with his empty hands open.

"She goes free first," he said. "Then I'm yours. Not before. I've been lied to once this morning already; I'm not hungry for a second helping."

Vess studied him for a long moment. Something passed behind those pond-brown eyes — recognition, maybe, of a kind she didn't welcome. A man trading himself for a child. She knew that arithmetic. Somewhere, Gideon thought, remembering Wren's whispers from her weeks in the Inquisition's camps, somewhere there was a sister.

"Release the child," Vess said.

The soldiers holding Wren hesitated — and then let go, and Wren stumbled forward two steps and stopped, staring at Gideon, her whole body a question.

"No," she said aloud. "No, you *come with me*, that was the *deal*, you always come—"

"I know." He kept his voice steady, though it cost him something structural. "Go to Maren. Remember what I said. Alive first. Brave after."

"You're being an idiot," Wren informed him, tears cutting clean tracks down her furious face, "and I'm going to be so angry at you *forever*, and I'm going to tell the bears where you *sleep*—" But she was walking backward, toward the trees, toward Maren, letting herself be pulled, because underneath all the fire she was the sharpest child he'd ever met and she understood exactly what the bargain was and exactly who was paying it.

At the treeline she turned once. Looked at him the way she'd looked at the world since the night she scratched at his door: like a door she intended to open.

Then the trees took her, and Maren's arm around her shoulders, and she was gone.

* * *

They bound his wrists in front of him, which was either carelessness or courtesy — Vess's order, he suspected, so he could gesture while he talked, because they wanted him talking. Interrogation, transport, the capital. He catalogued it all with the numb diligence of a man taking inventory of a wrecked ship.

Sorrel spat at Corvin's boots as they led Gideon past. Corvin didn't flinch. That was somehow worse than if he had.

"Was any of it real?" Gideon asked him quietly. "The fires. The lessons. Or was I just stock?"

Corvin's mouth opened, closed, opened again. Whatever came out, Gideon never heard it, because Vess's voice cut across the gorge like a blade finding the gap in armor:

"Mount up. We ride before—"

She stopped.

Everyone stopped, because the gorge itself changed. It began at the far end of the Hollow, a sound first — low, vast, plural, like wind trying to remember how to be a voice — and then the ravens came. Hundreds of them, pouring down the gorge in a river of black wings, wheeling over the soldiers in perfect silence, and beneath the silence, felt rather than heard, something that made the horses scream and fight their bits and the soldiers' torch-steadied hands go white.

Gideon stood among them with his hands bound and knew exactly what it was, and felt his heart crack open with something that was not fear at all.

They had heard. The ravens, the wolves, the otters, the old heron, every creature whose language he had spoken in secret all his life — word had gone down the mountain faster than any rider, and they had come.

For him.

Vess wrenched her gelding's head around, shouting orders into the storm of wings, and above the chaos, clear as a bell through the roar, Gideon heard Wren's voice from the trees, whooping like a girl who has just been handed the whole sky:

"*I TOLD YOU THEY'D COME!*"
