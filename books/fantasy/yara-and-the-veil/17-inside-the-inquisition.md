## 17. Inside the Inquisition

The cage was too small for a girl, a goat, and a full night's worth of crying, but the crown had never been famous for generous architecture.

Wren had been in it for two days. Or three. It was hard to keep count when the days were all gray and the guards changed shifts at hours that seemed chosen specifically to ruin her sleep. She'd tried marking the posts with her thumbnail, but the wood was green pine, soft as bread, and she'd only succeeded in carving what looked like a lopsided fish, which the goat had then attempted to eat.

"You're no help at all," she told him.

The goat — a bony white creature with the eyes of an old man who has heard every joke twice — blinked at her and chewed thoughtfully on the fish's tail.

She was in the Inquisitor's camp. That fact sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone whenever she let herself think about it directly, so mostly she didn't. Mostly she thought about other things: Gideon's hands giving her soup; Maren's spectacles fogging when she laughed, which was rare but magnificent when it happened; the way Sorrel said *child* like an insult but always stood between Wren and whatever was coming.

Then the stone would roll over in her stomach and she would think about it anyway, and her eyes would sting, and the goat would press his warm stinking flank against her side as if that were comfort, which — annoyingly — it was.

*They took me so he wouldn't have to watch,* she reminded herself for the hundredth time. *He gave up his knife. He'll come.*

She believed it the way you believe in spring in a hard winter: not because you can see any evidence, but because the alternative is unbearable.

* * *

On the third day — or possibly the fourth; the fish-carving was getting unreliable — they moved her cage to a new spot in the camp, near the horse lines.

Wren discovered this within ten minutes, because the horses started talking about her.

Not out loud, exactly. Not in words the way people did. Horse-speech wasn't words so much as pictures and feelings strung together, a language made of weather. The gray mare nearest her cage sent out a ripple of curiosity — *small creature, new smell, why chained?* — and the bay beside her answered with the equine equivalent of a shrug, and the old chestnut gelding at the end sent something that was unmistakably gossip: *the tall cold one rides this one now, careful, she kicks.*

Wren pressed her face to the cage slats and nearly wept with relief.

"Hello," she whispered. "Hello, hello. Oh, I have missed talking to anything that isn't a goat."

The goat, insulted, bit her sleeve.

The horses' heads came up. They stared at her the way horses do — ears forward, all attention, like doors opening. Then the gray mare walked over, slow and deliberate, and stood close enough that Wren could see her breath fog the air between them.

*You speak,* the mare said. Her mind-voice smelled like hay and rain, if smells could be voices. *Small one speaks true speech.*

"I do." Wren kept her voice low. The nearest guard was twenty feet away, picking at his bootlaces, bored enough to be dangerous. "Please don't be loud about it. Loud is how people I love ended up dead."

The horses went quiet. Even the chestnut stopped chewing. Something passed down the line from horse to horse, a feeling Wren knew well — the careful stillness of creatures deciding whether to trust.

Then the bay gelding stepped closer, and asked the question horses always asked first, before anything else, because it was the question that mattered:

*Are you kind?*

"I'm trying to be," Wren said honestly. "I got a cat to trust me once. And a goat." She glared at the goat. "Mostly."

A ripple through the line that could only be described as laughter. Horses found goats funny; this appeared to be universal.

And just like that, Wren had friends. Real ones, with hooves. Over the next hour — while the guard dozed against a wagon wheel and dreamed of whatever guards dream of (his bootlace, probably) — the horses told her everything about the camp. Where the tents were. How many soldiers, and which ones were cruel (*the short one with the red beard, kick him if you ever get the chance, we would ourselves if we weren't professional*) and which were merely tired. Where Vess's tent stood, apart from all the others, black as a closed fist.

It was the chestnut, the old gossip, who told her about the sister.

He'd belonged to the Inquisitor for years, longer than any of the others. He carried her on long roads and stood patient while she interrogated prisoners, and he knew things about her that no soldier in camp knew, because horses see everything and are told nothing worth hiding.

*There is another,* the chestnut said, when Wren asked what the tall cold one loved. *She does not ride anymore. She lives in the last wagon, the one with no windows.*

"A prisoner?"

*No.* A pause, the picture forming slowly in the old horse's mind. *No chains. No locks. But she does not leave. She sits. Someone brings her food and she forgets to eat it until reminded. Her eyes—*

The chestnut struggled. Horse-language was built for weather and wolves and the location of grass, not for this.

*Her eyes are like a house where everyone left in the middle of supper. The Inquisitor goes to her every night. Every night, small one. She brushes the woman's hair. She talks to her — real words, soft words, words I am not supposed to understand. She says:* *I did the right thing. You know I did the right thing.* *And the woman looks at the wall and does not answer, because there is no one inside to answer.*

Wren sat very still in her cage, and the stone in her stomach turned into something else entirely.

"She reported her," Wren said quietly. "Her own sister. To be cured. And the cure—" She remembered the village. She remembered Mistress Annel, who used to laugh like a kettle, standing in the ashes saying *yes, yes* to questions no one had asked. "The cure hollowed her out."

*Yes,* said the chestnut. *And every night the tall cold one asks a dead house if she did right. And every night the house says nothing. And every morning she puts on her armor and hunts your kind harder, as if hunting will make it true.*

* * *

That evening, they brought Wren before the Inquisitor.

She had expected a tent full of instruments. What she got was worse: a tidy table, a lamp, two cups of tea, and Vess herself sitting across from them with the weary patience of a woman doing paperwork.

"Sit," said Vess.

Wren stayed standing. It was the only rebellion available and she took it.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"Because you took me," Wren said. "I didn't fill out any forms."

Something flickered at the corner of Vess's mouth — gone before it could declare itself a smile. "Your friend Gideon Pemberton surrendered his weapon rather than see you harmed. Sentimental. Foolish. Useful." She folded her hands. "He'll come for you. That is the entire point of you, child. You are bait, and the trap is already set, and there is nothing you can do about any of it. So let us not pretend this is a negotiation. This is simply me being honest with you, which is more courtesy than most get."

"That's very generous," Wren said. "Would you like me to cry? I can cry. I'm good at it. There's a technique."

Vess studied her. Up close, without her armor of distance, the Inquisitor looked tired in a way that went all the way down, like a well. "You're not afraid of me."

"Oh, I'm terrified," Wren said. "But my grandmother used to say fear is like a goat. If you feed it, it follows you everywhere and eats your things." She paused. "I realize that metaphor may be more vivid than I intended. There's a goat in my cage."

For a moment — half a heartbeat — Vess looked genuinely lost, as if no one had spoken to her like a person in years, and she had forgotten what the procedure was. Then the armor came back up, plate by plate.

"The Edict exists to protect people like you," she said. "The gift burns out young speakers. It isolates them. It makes them liars by necessity. The cure—"

"Have you seen it?" Wren asked.

"—saves them from that fate."

"Have you *seen* it?"

Silence. The lamp hissed.

"Because I have," Wren said. "My whole village. Mistress Annel, who taught me letters. They cured her, and afterward she smiled at nothing all day and couldn't remember my name, but she could remember that she used to have a name. Do you know what that's like? Being a memory of yourself?" Wren's voice shook and she let it. "You should. You look at her every night."

Vess went very still.

"Yes," Wren said softly. "I know about the wagon with no windows. Horses talk, Inquisitor. They see everything and nobody thinks to hide from them, because everyone thinks they're furniture. Your own gelding told me. The old chestnut. He says you brush her hair and ask her if you did the right thing." Wren took a step closer, heart hammering, guard or no guard. "And she doesn't answer. Because there's no one home to answer. You cured her into an empty room and now you knock on the door every single night hoping somebody's moved back in."

"Enough." The word cracked like a whip, and the tent flap stirred, and the guard outside shifted — but Vess raised one finger and held the world still. Her hands, Wren noticed, were trembling. Just slightly. Just at the edges.

"You know nothing," Vess said, low and dangerous.

"I know she forgets to eat unless someone reminds her," Wren said, "and you remind her, because even an empty room deserves supper. I know you say *I did the right thing* like a prayer to a god who died. And I know—" Wren's voice broke, and she pushed through it. "I know that if you take me to be cured, someday a little girl is going to stand in front of the thing I become and beg it to remember her, and it won't, and you'll have made that happen. To both of us. Again."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the chestnut stamped, once.

"You want to save children from the gift," Wren said. "Fine. I believe you. I think that part is even true. But you've seen what the cure costs, and you send people into it anyway, because stopping now would mean admitting—" she faltered, then finished, gentle as she could, "—admitting you didn't save your sister. You emptied her. Those aren't the same thing, and you know it, deep down, in the place you only visit at night, with a hairbrush in your hand."

Vess rose. Slowly. She crossed the space between them and stood looking down, and Wren made herself stay, feet rooted, chin up, though every instinct screamed to run.

"If you speak of my sister again," Vess said quietly, "I will have your tongue out before you can finish the sentence."

"Then you'll have proven my point," Wren whispered, "because you can't argue with it."

For a long moment neither of them breathed. The lamp flame leaned sideways in some draft Wren couldn't feel, and in its light Vess's face was suddenly, terribly human — a woman holding a door shut against something that had been knocking for years.

Then it closed. The armor settled. She sat back down, picked up her tea, and said, in a perfectly level voice:

"Take the child back to her cage."

* * *

But she didn't sleep that night.

Wren knew, because the horses told her. The chestnut stood his whole shift watching the black tent, and what he felt coming off it was not the cold iron certainty of an Inquisitor. It was a storm trying to decide which way to break.

*Toward dawn,* he reported to the others, and to Wren through the fence slats, *she came out and walked to the windowless wagon. Stayed a long time. When she came away she was carrying the hairbrush, and she stood in the middle of the camp like a woman who has forgotten what her own hands are for.*

"And then?" Wren whispered.

*Then she looked east,* the chestnut said, *toward the mountains. Toward wherever your friends are going. And she said one word. She said:*

*"...Enough?"*

The old horse wasn't sure. Neither was Wren. Enough meaning *enough of this hunt.* Enough meaning *how much more must I pay.* Enough meaning *God, stop asking me if I did the right thing.*

Or perhaps just a woman saying goodbye to a question she'd carried so long it had grown into her spine.

Wren lay back against the goat — who complained, and then forgave her — and stared through the cage slats at the stars.

*I planted it,* she thought. *A seed the size of a doubt. Now it either grows or it dies, and I'll probably never know which.*

She was almost asleep when she heard it: hoofbeats at the edge of camp, muffled, wrong — unshod hooves, moving soft, moving together, a whole herd moving like a held breath. The camp horses lifted their heads as one, and the feeling that rolled down the lines was so startling that Wren sat bolt upright, and the goat cried out in protest, and somewhere far off a wolf began to sing.

The horses knew the travelers on the wind.

*It's him,* the gray mare breathed, ears forward toward the dark mountains. *The speaker your kind fights for. He comes — and small one, he is not alone. He has brought the mountain with him.*

Wren gripped the cage slats and grinned into the darkness, heart pounding like a drum.

Gideon was coming — and he hadn't come to negotiate.
