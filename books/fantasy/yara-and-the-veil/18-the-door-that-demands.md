## 18. The Door That Demands

Maren Ostwick had spent forty years among books, and she had learned this: the truth always arrives at the worst possible hour, wearing its worst possible clothes.

It arrived now, on a morning of thin grey mist, as the company stood at the foot of a cliff that was not on any map she had ever copied, and looked up at a seam of light.

She had known for eleven days. She had known since the bear.

The bear had led them through the high cave — ancient Sorrel-of-the-mountains, white-muzzled and patient, moving at the pace of a creature who had all the time in the world — and Maren had stood in the flicker of Gideon's lantern before the carvings, and she had read them, because reading was the one thing she had never been able to stop doing, not even when it broke her.

*The door opens to a life freely given.*

She had read it twice. Three times. Then she had turned back to the others, who were laughing softly in the lantern light, Brann's ghost somehow present in the way Ilsa touched the carvings with both hands, and she had said: "Old prophecies. Mostly weather."

Mostly weather. She had lied to seven people and a cat, and the cat had looked at her with an expression that suggested it knew, because cats always knew, and said nothing, because cats were merciful that way.

Now there was no more lying to be done, because they had arrived.

The seam of light ran vertically up the cliff face, perhaps the height of three men, no wider than a hand. It did not behave like light. It did not cast shadows; it *gathered* them. The mist around it bent inward slightly, the way water bends toward a drain, and when Wren stepped closer, the seam brightened — just barely, just perceptibly — like a candle acknowledging a breath.

"Well," said Tam. "That's not nothing."

"It's very much not nothing," said Ilsa.

Maren's heart was doing something arrhythmic and undignified. She set down her pack, which weighed more than it had eleven days ago, because guilt, she had discovered, has weight, and it accumulates in canvas.

"Gideon," she said. "Wren. Everyone. Sit down, please. There is something I must tell you, and I would rather tell it standing on my own feet than have it knocked out of me later."

Gideon turned. He had that look he got sometimes, the one of a man who has spent his life listening to things that don't speak in words and has therefore become very good at hearing what people mean instead of what they say. "Maren," he said carefully. "How long have you known?"

"Eleven days," she said. "Since the cave." She drew a breath that felt like swallowing gravel. "I am sorry. I told you it was mostly weather. It is not mostly weather."

* * *

They sat on the cold stone, all six of them — Gideon, Wren, Tam, Ilsa, Sorrel, and Maren herself, plus the cat, Bristle, who sat apart from the group in the manner of one attending a funeral out of obligation rather than grief — and Maren told them what the carvings said. All of it. The old tongue, the older script beneath it, the record of the last opening, four hundred years past, when the erased founders of Thornfell had sealed themselves out of the crown's reach.

"The Veil opens," she said, "for one life freely given. Not taken. Not bargained. Given. The carvings are explicit on the distinction — the scholars who wrote them argued about it for generations, which is how you know it mattered. A life surrendered willingly. And then the door stays open, for all who wish to pass."

Silence. The kind of silence that has mass.

Then Sorrel laughed.

It was not a kind laugh, but it wasn't a cruel one either. It was the laugh of a woman who had been waiting for the bill to arrive. "Of course," Sorrel said. "Of course it costs. Everything on this road has cost. Why should the exit be free?"

"Because—" Wren began.

"I'm volunteering," Sorrel said.

The word landed like a dropped plate.

"Don't look at me like that," Sorrel went on, and her voice was steady in the way that ice is steady — smooth, load-bearing, and thin. "You all know what I did. You know why I'm here. I let my brother die on a mountainside because staying meant dying too, and I chose myself, and I have chosen myself every day since, and the crown's cure would have been justice but this—" she gestured at the seam of light, at the gathered shadows bending toward it— "this is better than justice. This is arithmetic. One life, freely given, against everyone else's. I've been in debt my whole life. Let me finally pay something down."

"No," said Gideon.

"Sorrel—" said Tam.

"I wasn't asking permission." But her voice cracked on *permission*, just slightly, and Maren saw it, because Maren had spent forty years watching students pretend not to feel things while feeling them enormously.

"I'm volunteering too," said Tam.

And then, horribly, it became a competition.

"I'm volunteering," said Ilsa.

"You're eighteen," said Tam.

"So are you!"

"Yes, but I'm a deserter, so technically I'm worth less—"

"That is the stupidest thing anyone has ever said in my hearing," said Ilsa, "and I grew up with Brann."

At the name, she stopped. Her jaw worked. Maren watched grief move through the girl like weather through a valley — fast, total, gone — and leave behind the thing it always left.

"He'd volunteer," Ilsa said quietly. "If he were here. So I'll do it twice. Once for each of us."

"Wren is not volunteering," said Gideon, who had been silent until now, "because Wren is twelve, and if anyone suggests otherwise I will carry them bodily down this mountain."

"I wasn't going to," said Wren, with the dignity of the deeply offended. Then, after a moment: "I was thinking about it."

"Wren."

"I *stopped* thinking about it! When you made the face!"

Maren put her head briefly in her hands. Forty years of scholarship. Two published treatises on the ethics of translation. And here she sat, on a frozen mountain, moderating a bidding war over who got to die.

"Stop," she said, and her voice came out in the register she had used on lecture halls full of young men who talked over her, the one that could frost glass. "All of you. Stop. Please."

They stopped. Even the wind seemed to pause out of professional courtesy.

"You misunderstand the carving," she said. "Or rather — I misread it to you, because I was a coward, and I am correcting that now. Listen to the whole line. *The door opens to a life freely given.* Not offered. Not volunteered. Not auctioned off by the noblest bidder. *Given.* The old word is *dan-veil* — a gift, in the true sense. A gift cannot be taken from you. A gift cannot be seized by someone determined to be heroic. And—" her voice caught, and she steadied it— "a gift given to escape guilt is not freely given. It is purchased. The carvings distinguish. The founders were very clear, because the first time the Veil opened, a woman tried to buy passage with a condemned prisoner, and the door stayed shut, and she wrote the lesson into the stone so no one would ever mistake payment for love again."

Sorrel stared at her. "So what? We sit here until someone feels sufficiently pure?"

"We sit here," Maren said gently, "until we understand that the door may not want what any of us is trying to give it."

* * *

They made camp below the cliff, because no one could think of anything else to do, and because grief and revelation are both improved by soup.

Tam cooked. Tam always cooked when the world went wrong — Maren had noticed it weeks ago, the way the big deserter retreated into chopping onions as though onions were a problem he could actually solve. Tonight he added the last of the dried plums to the pot without asking, which was Tam's version of a eulogy.

Bristle sat on Maren's knee. This was unprecedented. In eleven days of travel the cat had sat on no one's knee, preferring the strategic independence of whichever backpack was warmest, and now here she was, kneading Maren's thigh with the focused intensity of a creature delivering a message.

"You know," Maren murmured to her, "most scholars wait for their colleagues to review their work."

Bristle purred, unhelpfully.

Across the fire, Wren was talking to the horses — the two exhausted, borrowed, half-starved creatures they'd acquired at the erased villages — and Maren watched Gideon watch her. There was a particular quality to it. Pride and dread braided together, the exact expression Maren imagined she'd have worn watching a brilliant student walk into a country that wanted her dead.

Gideon caught Maren looking. He crossed the fire and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, which from Gideon was practically an embrace.

"You hid it for eleven days," he said. Not accusing. Just placing it on the table between them, where he placed everything.

"I did."

"Why?"

Maren considered lying again, and found she had run out. "Because hope is heavy," she said. "And you were all carrying enough. Because I told myself I'd find another answer in the carvings if I read them enough times, which is the scholar's disease — the belief that the text will bend if you love it hard enough." She smiled, and it hurt. "My husband used to say I loved books more than people. He was wrong. I love books *instead of* people, because books can't die for me. And now the book says someone dies for the book, and I find I mind very much."

Gideon was quiet a while. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the seam of light hummed — not audibly, but in the teeth, in the fillings, in the bones behind the eyes.

"Maren," he said at last. "When we get through — if we get through — the ledger. Your ledger of names. What happens to it?"

She blinked. Of all the questions. "It comes with us. Every name. Every person still alive behind us. Why do you ask?"

"Because if that door wants a gift," Gideon said, "I intend to make sure it understands exactly what it's being offered. Not a guilty woman. Not a grieving girl. Someone who knows precisely what he's trading away." He looked at his own hands, the hands that had spoken to wolves and mules and ravens for forty years. "Someone who's done the arithmetic already."

"Gideon—"

"Don't argue with me, Maren. You'll lose. I've been arguing with mules my whole life; I know how it ends."

"That is not the flex you believe it to be," said Maren, "given that mules win most arguments."

He almost smiled. "Get some sleep, old woman."

"Flatterer," said Maren, and did not sleep.

* * *

She woke near dawn to voices, and lay still in her bedroll, and listened, because listening was the oldest trick she had.

They were at the cliff. All five of them, wrapped in blankets against the cold, standing before the seam of light, and they were talking — not arguing, talking — in the low, overlapping way of people who have been having the same conversation for hours.

"—not saying I get to decide," Sorrel was saying. "I'm saying my case is strongest. Guilt is the opposite of freely given, fine, fine, the old woman made her point. But I've also got the least to live *for*. That's not guilt, that's inventory."

"You have a great deal to live for," said Tam. "You just haven't unpacked it yet."

"Oh, listen to him. Three weeks of knowing me and he's unpacking me."

"You'd do the same for me."

"I would not. I'd push you off the scree slope myself—"

"And *that*, ladies and gentlemen, is love," said Ilsa.

Maren rose silently and came to stand a little apart, and what she saw stopped her breath.

The seam of light was brighter.

Not much. A candle's worth brighter. But it moved — rippled — the way a curtain moves when someone stands behind it, considering whether to come out. And Wren stood closest, hand raised but not touching, and the light leaned toward her hand the way flowers lean toward sun, and Maren felt an old scholar's terror rise in her throat, the terror of a text behaving in ways the text does not.

"It likes us," Wren said wonderingly. "Is that strange? It feels like it likes us."

"It's hungry," said Sorrel flatly.

"No." Gideon had gone very still. He was looking at the seam with an expression Maren had seen once before, on the drowned road, when a hound had recognized his voice — the look of a man hearing something in a language only he speaks. "No, she's right. It's not hunger. It's..." He shook his head slowly. "It's lonely."

The word fell into the cold air and stayed there.

"Four hundred years," Maren whispered, understanding arriving all at once, unwelcome as always. "Four hundred years shut. The founders gave a life to open it, and then it closed behind them, and it has been shut ever since, waiting, and the carvings say *freely given* because—" she pressed her hand to her mouth. "Oh. Oh, the fools. The poor, magnificent fools. It doesn't want a death. It never wanted a death. It wants what the death *meant*. It wants to be opened by someone who chooses the others over himself. Any of us would satisfy the letter. But it's been waiting four hundred years for the spirit."

They all looked at her.

"So anyone," said Tam slowly.

"Anyone who means it," said Maren. "Truly means it. Which means—" and her voice failed her, because the truth was assembled now and it was terrible in its simplicity— "which means several of you might open it. And I cannot tell which. And neither, I suspect, can it."

Silence again. But a different silence. The kind that comes before an avalanche, or a decision.

Sorrel took a step forward.

Tam stepped forward at the same time.

So did Ilsa.

So — hand slipping out of Wren's grasp, quiet as snowfall — did Gideon.

Four volunteers. Four gifts, laid on the stone of the morning like offerings, none willing to withdraw, all watching each other with the fierce, furious tenderness of people who have buried too many friends on this road and refuse to bury more.

And the seam of light flared — bright, brighter, blinding — and began, with a sound like a held breath released after four hundred years, to widen.

"No," Maren breathed. "No, no — you mustn't rush it, you mustn't *all*—"

But the door was opening, and it did not seem inclined to choose politely, and Gideon Pemberton was walking toward the light with his arms loose at his sides and an expression on his face that Maren Ostwick, who had read every tragedy ever written, recognized instantly as the expression of a man who had already settled the matter in private and intended to cheat his way to the front of the queue.
