## 19. Gideon's Bargain

The Inquisitor's camp sat in the crook of the valley like a knife left in bread — casual, deliberate, and meant to be found.

Gideon had watched it for two hours from the spine of the ridge above Hollow Bridge, flat on his belly in the heather while Wren slept an arm's length away, her breath whistling faintly through a nose still scabbed from the bridge. Below, torchlight made orange pools between the tents. He counted sentries, counted horses, counted the small barred wagon at the center of it all with its single guard and its smell of cold iron.

He counted, and then he did arithmetic that made him sick.

*They have her,* said the raven on his shoulder, whose name was Sock because he'd found her as a fledgling tangled in one. *They have the girl. They are not hurting her yet.*

"Not yet," Gideon murmured. "Yet is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

*You taste of iron,* Sock observed. *Like blood. Like a nail held too long.*

"That's about right."

Behind him, down the ridge, the others waited at the tree line with Maren's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle. Sorrel with her bow strung. Tam whispering to his mare, who kept turning her ears toward the camp and stamping. Ilsa alone now, since the gorge, standing a little apart from everyone the way a house stands apart after the fire.

And Maren, who would argue. Who *should* argue. That was why Gideon hadn't told her. It was the first plan he'd ever made without the old teacher's voice in his ear saying *and what does it cost, Gideon, and who pays it*, and the silence of that voice felt like stepping off a map.

The answer to both questions was simple enough. It cost everything. And he paid it.

He'd been readying himself for this since the cave of carvings, if he were honest — since the moment he'd seen Maren's face over those old scratched figures and understood she was hiding something. He'd gotten the truth out of her two nights later, gently, the way you coax a splinter: the Veil opened for one life freely given. Not taken. Given. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole cruelty of it. You couldn't sacrifice a stranger. You couldn't be pushed. You had to walk up to the seam of light in the cliff face and mean it.

Well. Gideon Pemberton had spent forty-one years meaning things quietly. He could manage one more.

He kissed Wren's forehead while she slept — she'd have bitten him for it awake, which was how he knew she'd be fine eventually — and told the wolf dozing at her feet to stay, and got no argument from the wolf because wolves understand choices better than people do. Then he went down the mountain through the dark, alone but for Sock, who refused point-blank to leave and whom he hadn't the heart or the authority to make.

The sentry challenge came at the camp's edge: "*Stand and be seen!*"

"I'm seen," Gideon said, hands wide. "I'm the most visible man in Thornfell. Ask your ravens."

The sentry did not ask his ravens, having none, which Gideon considered a professional failing. But word traveled up the chain quickly, and within ten minutes he was marched past cook fires and nervous soldiers into the largest tent, where Inquisitor Vess sat at a folding table with maps weighted by stones and her sister's empty eyes, he'd been told, reflected somewhere behind them all.

She looked older than rumor made her. Thinner. A woman built out of discipline the way other people were built out of bone.

"Gideon Pemberton," she said. "Corvin sold me your roads. He didn't sell me your nerve. Walk into my camp alone — why?"

"Because I want a trade."

Her eyebrow moved perhaps a quarter inch. For Vess, a carnival.

"The girl," Gideon said. "Wren. You release her — released, mind, not exchanged on some bridge with crossbows, actually let go, unharmed, free to walk wherever she likes — and I give you every beast-speaker in the company. Names, routes, the location of the ledger, my testimony, whatever you want. Me included. Kneeling in irons by dawn."

"And why," Vess said slowly, "would the famous smuggler hand me his own neck?"

"Because the cure doesn't work."

The tent went very quiet. Somewhere outside, a horse shifted.

"I've seen what comes out of your curing houses," Gideon pressed on, and heard his own voice go low and steady, which surprised him; he'd expected shaking. "My teacher showed me her ledger. She's tracked survivors. What walks out of those houses wears the person's face and nothing else. You know it. I think—" he looked at her, really looked, "—I think you've known it for years, and it's the reason you hunt so hard. Because if you catch them all fast enough, before anyone talks, you never have to be wrong out loud."

Something crossed Vess's face like weather over water. It was gone before it landed.

"Sentiment," she said. "The trade is irregular. But acceptable." She drew a folded paper toward herself. "The girl goes free. The rest are yours to deliver." Her pen paused. "You're doing this for a child you met in a storm."

"Yes."

"Six weeks ago."

"Yes."

Vess set down the pen and studied him the way Maren studied old texts — looking for the forgery, the later addition, the lie stitched into the binding. "My sister would have done something like this," she said, almost to herself. "Before."

"Is she—"

"You've bought your trade, Mr. Pemberton. Don't spend it on conversation." She signed the paper. "Release at first light. You will remain in camp until the fugitives are taken or until they reach the mountain — whichever comes first. If they escape entirely, the deal holds anyway. I keep my word." A beat. "It's the only thing left that I do keep."

Gideon nodded. At the tent flap he stopped, because there was one more thing and it burned going out.

"They'll get there before you can take them," he said. "The pass is three days. The mountain is one, if you know the way, and they don't. So here's my advice, free of charge: don't follow us up. Whatever you're looking for at the top, it isn't worth it."

"And what's at the top?"

"Hope," said Gideon, "with a very steep price tag."

Sock laughed from the dark outside — a raven's laugh is the ugliest sound in nature — and Vess's eyes flicked to the tent flap and then away, deliberately, as though deciding not to see the bird was a form of mercy.

* * *

Maren hit him with her stick.

Not hard. Maren Ostwick had once disarmed a bandit with a candlestick and a footnote, and when she chose to strike, things stayed struck. This was the other kind — the open-palmed swat of a woman who has been afraid.

"You *fool*. You magnificent, mule-headed—" swat "—you traded yourself for the girl and told *no one*?"

"Told you now."

"After signing the paper!"

"How did you—" Gideon glanced at Sock, perched smugly on the watchtower rubble. "You flew ahead."

*I contain multitudes,* said Sock.

The camp of the strays had broken apart around the news the way ice breaks — along lines nobody had noticed. Tam was furious in the loud way, pacing and telling his mare about it, the mare nodding along like a co-conspirator. Sorrel was furious in the quiet way, arms crossed, jaw tight, which from Sorrel was practically weeping. Ilsa hadn't spoken much since Brann fell, but she spoke now.

"He did what any of us would've done," she said. "That's the whole problem with this company. Any of us would do it, and we keep pretending we wouldn't."

"We should vote," Tam began.

"There's no vote," Gideon said. "It's done. Ink's dry. And even if it weren't — someone has to pay the door. You all read the carvings. One life, freely given." He looked around the fire, at each of them in turn, and made his voice gentle, because this next part mattered more than the trade, more than the Veil, maybe more than anything he'd ever say. "But it doesn't have to be one of you. It was always going to be me. From the cave, from before the cave. I've been walking toward this door since I was nine years old talking to my mother's hens. Everything else was just the long road to it."

"That's nonsense," said Maren, wiping her eyes with a sleeve already soaked. "People aren't roads."

"This one is," said Gideon, and smiled, and got half a wet laugh out of her despite everything, which he counted as a victory.

Sorrel spoke last. She came and stood close, so the others couldn't hear, and her voice had gravel in it.

"I let my brother die," she said, "and I've been paying for it ever since, and tomorrow you get to pay for strangers and be called a hero for it. That's not fair."

"No," Gideon agreed. "It isn't."

"I hate you a little."

"I'd worry if you didn't."

She punched his shoulder — Sorrel's version of a blessing — and went to check her bowstrings, and Gideon sat down between Wren and the fire and let the warmth soak into him and thought, absurdly, of his mother's hens. Henrietta had been the best conversationalist. Terrible gossip. He hoped wherever hens went, they remembered him fondly.

Wren leaned against his ribs, small and sharp-elbowed and alive, freed at dawn under Vess's seal and furious about being freed at all. "When I'm big," she announced, "I'm going to come back and rescue you."

"From where? The Veil takes you through."

"From *anywhere*. Doors go both ways if you kick them enough."

Gideon laughed until his chest hurt, which he suspected was not entirely laughter's fault.

*Sleep,* urged the wolf, pressing warm against his other side. *The mountain climbs early.*

*Sleep,* agreed the fire, popping. *I'll hold the dark off. It's the least I can do.*

Forty-one years of voices, he thought, drifting. Tomorrow they'd go quiet, and the world would become a large dumb beautiful animal he could only watch. He tried to imagine silence where there had always been speech, and failed, and decided that was probably merciful.

* * *

They climbed at gray light, the whole ragged company plus one condemned man, and Vess kept her word — no riders came behind, at least none that the crows could find, though Ilsa kept scanning the sky with Brann's absence written across her shoulders.

The mountain did not want them comfortable. It threw wind at them sideways, then sleet, then a silence so total that their own footsteps sounded like trespass. Twice Gideon asked the stone for safe footing and twice it grumbled but obliged, in the way of an old man asked to move his chair.

By afternoon they reached it.

He had imagined a gate. A door with hinges, maybe, something with a lintel you could knock on politely. What was carved into the cliff face instead was a *seam* — a vertical thread of light thinner than a hand's width, running up the rock higher than any rope would reach, humming a note Gideon felt in his back teeth. Around it, ancient carvings spiraled outward: beasts of every kind, wolves and bears and crows and salmon and one enormous shapeless thing that might have been the mountain itself, all flowing toward the line of light like rivers to a sea. And beneath, worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands, a single sentence in the old tongue.

Maren translated it aloud, though everyone already knew. "*One life, freely given, opens the way.*"

"Well," said Gideon, before anyone could start. "You know."

He hugged Tam first, because Tam cried easily and it was kindest to get it over with. He hugged Ilsa, who held on a long time and whispered "Brann would've liked you" into his coat, which undid him worse than Tam's tears. He hugged Sorrel last and longest, and said into her hair, "You're forgiven. You've been forgiven since before you asked. Spend it on somebody."

Then Maren — who gripped his face in both hands and said, "Forty years I hid from the crown, and the bravest thing I ever saw was a smuggler's son walking uphill. Write that on my stone." Then Wren.

Wren did not hug him. Wren stood back with her chin up and her fists balled and said, "This is the stupidest plan anyone has ever had, and I've met Corvin," and then she hugged him anyway, ferociously, around the middle, and Gideon held her and thought: *yes. Yes, this. This is what it's for.*

He turned to the seam.

The light hummed. The carvings seemed to shift as he approached, though he told himself it was wind-shadow. He put his palm against the cold rock beside the thread of brightness and gathered everything in himself — the fear, the grief, the forty-one years of speaking softly in a world that killed soft speakers — and he arranged it into an offering.

"I give it," he said. "Freely. Open the door."

The seam flickered.

And then the mountain answered — but not the way he expected.

It began with the birds. A single crow dropped out of the gray sky and landed on the carving of a crow, and then another, and another, until the ledge above the seam was black with them and the sound they made was not cawing but something structured, deliberate, *rhythmic*. Then the wolves came over the ridge — six of them, eight, a dozen, moving without hostility, flanking the company like honor guards. A bear, vast and gray-muzzled, the same ancient bear Maren had sworn led them to the cave of carvings, planted itself before the seam and did not move. Otters appeared impossibly from a frozen stream, chittering. A red stag stepped from the pines with his whole herd behind him. Horses — wild ones, no brand on them, though Tam's mare broke from the company to stand among them as though she'd been waiting all her life for permission.

"What," said Tam, in a very small voice, "is happening."

Gideon knew. His gift translated it before his mind caught up, pouring the meaning into him in a flood, and he stood in the middle of the gathering with tears freezing on his face as the beasts of Thornfell spoke — all of them, together, in the old shared grammar that predates speech:

*No.*

"I have to," he said aloud, absurdly, arguing with a bear in front of twenty people. "It's the price. One life, freely given—"

*Given,* rumbled the bear, and the word arrived in Gideon's head with the weight of continents, *was never meant to mean yours alone. Every beast you ever saved carries a debt. Every beast you ever fed, guided, sheltered, named. You have spent your whole life paying forward a currency you didn't know you were minting. The door asks for one life. We bring thousands.*

And Gideon saw it then — truly saw it — the web he'd never known he'd woven. The mule from his first run, whose grandchildren were here. The wolf he'd freed from a snare twenty years ago, whose pack stood before him now. Ravens whose ravens' grandparents had carried messages for him. Every kindness traded onward and downward and through the generations like coin, compounding, coming due all at once on a mountainside in the country that wasn't on any map.

"You can't pay my debt for me," he whispered.

*We are not paying your debt,* said the wolves, and the crows, and the stag, in terrible harmonious chorus. *We are refusing the price. The door was built by the old people with the beasts' consent — consent to hold a boundary, not to starve on love. It has forgotten itself. Remind it.*

The bear rose onto her hind legs, taller than the carvings, and pressed one vast paw flat against the seam of light. The wolves pressed their shoulders to the rock. The crows descended like a black river pouring into the crack. The stag lowered his antlers, and the herd behind him, and Tam's mare screamed defiance, and the whole living weight of Thornfell — every claw and hoof and wing, every creature Gideon had ever spoken with and ten thousand he never had — leaned against the door that demanded death

and the seam

cracked

open.

Light poured out, gold and green and the color of the inside of spring, and the humming became singing, and where there had been a thread there was now a doorway wide enough to drive a cart through, standing open on the face of the cliff with no frame and no hinge and no toll.

"Go," Gideon breathed, and then shouted it, laughing and sobbing at once. "GO! All of you! GO!"

They went — Maren with her ledger clutched to her chest, Tam and the horses, Ilsa walking through with her hand raised in farewell to the crows wheeling overhead, Sorrel pausing at the threshold to look back once with an expression Gideon would carry the rest of his life. Wren stopped dead in the light, turned around, and pointed at him.

"You," she commanded, "are coming too. Nobody gets left. Those are the rules now. I just made them."

"Wren—"

"MOVE, old man!"

Gideon Pemberton took one step toward the shining door — and felt something in him *shift*, some vast attention turning its gaze upon him, weighing, measuring, and reaching, gently, terribly, for the one thing he had carried longest—

The voices began to fade.

"No," Gideon whispered, staggering, as the wolves' speech thinned into ordinary sound, as the bear's continent-weighted words dissolved into a growl, as the crows' chorus collapsed into mere cawing. "No — wait — I didn't finish paying—"

*You don't have to,* said one last voice, warm and familiar — Sock, from his shoulder, her words already going round and distant, like footsteps walking away down a long hall. *The door takes its toll regardless. It's taking mine too, little nail. But it lets us all through. Listen — listen — the last thing I'll ever tell you—*

The world went silent.

Not silent — *ordinary*. Wind. Rock. A bear breathing. Birds making bird noises at nothing.

The doorway stood open. His friends were through. Wren was screaming his name from the light.

Gideon ran.
