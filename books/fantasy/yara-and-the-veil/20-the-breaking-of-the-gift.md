## 20. The Breaking of the Gift

The light did not burn.

That was the first thing Gideon understood about the Veil, standing before it with his hands open and his heart already spent: everyone who had ever written about it — Maren's ledgers, the carvings in the bear's cave, the frightened whispers passed between smugglers over a hundred years — had all been wrong about the same thing. They called it a seam of fire, a blade of heaven, a wound in the world. But it was none of those. It was simply bright, and warm, and patient, the way morning is patient with a man who doesn't want to get out of bed.

It waited for him.

Behind him, the beasts of Thornfell filled the hollow like a congregation that would not be dismissed. Wolves on the ridgeline, still as carvings. The old bear from the cave, planted in the snow like a boulder that had decided to grow fur. Crows in their hundreds along the dead branches, and beneath them hawks, foxes, a red stag with antlers like winter branches, and — closest of all, because she had always been closest — Yara, the wild mare, her breath steaming, her dark eyes fixed on him with an expression he had never needed words to read.

*Don't,* she was saying. She had been saying it since dawn. *Don't you dare.*

"I'm not asking your permission," Gideon told her quietly, and heard Wren make a small broken sound behind him.

He turned to face them one last time. Wren, red-eyed, gripping Sorrel's hand hard enough to whiten both their knuckles. Tam with his jaw set like a man holding a door shut against a flood. Ilsa, hollowed by grief, Brann's last words still living somewhere behind her eyes. Old Maren, weeping without shame, clutching her ledger to her chest as though paper could argue with fate. Sorrel, whose face had finally, in these last hours, unclenched into something almost gentle.

"You'll tell them," Gideon said. "On the other side. You'll tell them what Thornfell was. All of it. The good roads and the bad ones."

"Gideon—" Maren started.

"No." He smiled, and was surprised to find the smile came easy. He'd expected terror. What he felt instead was something like the last mile of a long contraband run, when the pass opens and the valley shows itself and every aching step suddenly seems worth the price. "I've been running cargo my whole life, Maren. This is just the last delivery."

He stepped toward the light.

The beasts did not move. That was the strange part. He had braced himself for a wall of bodies, for wolves' teeth and the mare's screaming, but they only watched him, and their watching was worse than any barrier — dozens of minds pressing against his all at once, saying his name in a hundred tongues that were not tongues. *Gideon-of-the-roads. Gideon-who-listens. Ours.*

"Yours," he agreed aloud, and walked into the brightness—

—and stopped.

Because the Veil spoke.

Not in words. It had no words; it was older than words. It spoke the way an avalanche speaks, the way a river freezing speaks: as fact, as inevitability, as weight. And what it said was this:

*One life, freely given. Accepted.*

And then, gentler, almost tender, the way a butcher strokes the steer:

*Come.*

Gideon stood in the light and offered everything he had. Not his body — the Veil didn't want meat, he understood that now, it wanted the giving itself, the moment when a soul opens its hand and lets go. So he opened his hand. He thought of his mother's kitchen, of Corvin teaching him star-paths with a stolen bottle of wine between them, of Maren's chalk-dusted lectures, of Wren scratching at his door in the storm. He thought of every beast he had ever loved, and he gave them all up freely, gladly, into the light.

The Veil began to take.

He felt it the way you feel a tooth being drawn — not pain exactly, but absence arriving ahead of itself, a socket opening where something lived. His gift went first at the edges. The ravens went quiet — not silent, for the birds still croaked and argued overhead, but *quiet*, in the way that mattered. Their meaning, which had poured into him for thirty years as naturally as rain off a roof, thinned to mere sound. Noise without sense.

*No,* said something in him, panicking. *No, wait—*

Too late. The fox-minds winked out like lanterns in a wind. The wolves' long gray thoughts, the patience of the bear, the hawk's cold arithmetic of the sky — gone, gone, gone, each silence a small death inside him. He gasped. He had not known until that moment how crowded his own skull had always been, how many voices had kept him company in the loneliest years of his life. He was losing them all, and the strangest grief of it was realizing that he had never once been alone, not truly, not since he was a boy — and now he would be.

Only one voice remained, fighting upstream against the current, ragged and furious and unmistakable.

*NO.*

Yara.

She came out of the gathered beasts like a hammer coming off an anvil. The whole congregation broke with her — the wolves pouring down the ridge, the crows rising in a black storm, the bear surging forward with a roar that shook ice from the cliff — and the Veil, which had waited a thousand years for a willing sacrifice, flared white with what Gideon could only describe, even afterward, as *alarm*.

*One life,* the mountain said. *Freely given—*

*Given back,* answered a thousand minds at once, in the voiceless voice they all shared, and Gideon, drowning in the last of his gift, understood what was happening and laughed out loud, because it was so perfectly, absurdly simple.

The Veil wanted one life given. It had never said the life had to stay taken. And here were all the creatures he had ever helped, ever saved, ever guided through passes and fed through winters and spoken to on long gray nights when no human would listen — and they had pooled their claim on him like merchants combining coin. *Ours,* they said, all of them together. *You cannot have what belongs to us.*

The light shuddered. Cracked — not like breaking glass, but like river ice in spring, like something that had held too long finally remembering it was allowed to move. The seam in the cliff widened, groaned, and tore open from crown to foot with a sound like a choir clearing its throat.

And the Veil, having lost its bargain, took its toll anyway.

Gideon felt it go — the last thread, the deepest channel, the well itself. Not painfully. Almost kindly. Like a hand lifting a heavy pack from shoulders that had carried it so long they'd forgotten its weight. One breath he was fluent in the oldest language in the world.

The next breath, he was only a man.

* * *

He came out on the far side on his knees, in grass — actual green grass, knee-high, humming with bees — under a sky the color of a robin's egg. Behind him the Veil hung open, a doorway of soft gold light in the cliff, no longer a seam but a gate, swinging wide as if it had only ever wanted permission to do so.

The fugitives came through in a rush, half-carrying each other, staring around at the meadow, the distant terraced fields, the smoke of hearths rising from a valley where people were already running toward them — people in rough homespun, faces brown and open, descendants of the erased, come to see what the mountain had finally given back.

But Gideon knelt in the grass and listened, and heard nothing.

A lark sang overhead. Just a lark. Notes, pretty and meaningless, going nowhere, arriving nowhere. Somewhere near his knee a bee droned its one dull syllable. In the treeline a bird scolded another bird, and there was no translation, none, and there never would be again.

"It worked," Wren whispered beside him. She was crying and laughing at once. "You're alive, it worked, it *worked*—"

"Yes," Gideon said. His voice sounded strange to him. Thin. Alone in his own head for the first time since boyhood. "Yes, it did."

Wren looked at his face and stopped laughing. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing." He tried the smile again; this time it took some work. "Nothing's wrong. Everything's right. I just—" He gestured helplessly at the singing lark, the droning bee, the whole chattering world. "I can't hear them anymore, little one. Any of them. The toll got paid after all. Just... differently than anyone planned."

Wren stared at him. Then, with the terrible directness of children, she asked: "Was it worth it?"

And Gideon, who had spent the night ready to die, who had felt thirty years of companionship sluiced out of him like water from a breached hull, considered the question honestly. He looked at the open gate. At the valley full of the living. At Wren, upright, breathing, free. At the mare.

Because Yara had come through too. She stood a few paces off in the tall grass, soaked in golden light, watching him with those dark, bottomless eyes — and she was still watching him. Still *there*. Choosing him. Not because he could speak to her. Now, perhaps, precisely because he couldn't, and had come anyway, and had given everything, and had stayed.

"Yeah," Gideon said, and found he meant it. "Yeah, it was worth it."

* * *

They might have had that whole golden hour to themselves, if the Veil hadn't crackled behind them.

Gideon turned, and his stomach dropped through the sweet grass and the warm earth and kept falling.

Inquisitor Vess stood in the gateway.

She was alone — her soldiers strung out behind her down the far slope, too far, arriving in ones and twos with their crossbows half-raised and no orders to loose. She must have ridden through the night, driven harder than any of them had guessed. Snow still clung to her cloak. Her face was gray with exhaustion, and her hand rested on her sword, and her eyes swept the meadow — the fugitives, the villagers running down from the terraces, the beasts scattered through the grass like a dream she couldn't wake from — and Gideon saw the exact moment she understood that it was over. That the door was open, and would not close, and could not be guarded, and that everything she had burned and broken and betrayed had slipped through her fingers into a country she would never map.

Her gaze found Gideon. Stopped.

"You," she said. Her voice was ash. "I had your name before Hollow Bridge. I had your routes, your friends, your girl. I had *everything*." Her hand tightened on the sword. "Why am I standing here with nothing?"

"Because you were hunting the wrong animal," Gideon said. He rose slowly, knees aching, and put himself — deliberately, obviously — between her and Wren. "You've been chasing speakers your whole life, Inquisitor. You never once asked what the beasts themselves wanted."

"They're animals."

"So were we," said Ilsa quietly, from beside him. "That's what the Edict said. That's what made it legal."

Vess's eyes moved across them — Tam, Sorrel, Maren clutching her ledger, the twins — and then they snagged, as Gideon had feared and prayed they would, on the crowd coming down from the terraces. On the faces. Ordinary faces, tired faces, laughing and crying faces. And among them, walking slow, wrapped in a shawl despite the warm air, a woman with silver hair and eyes like windows with nobody home.

Vess made a sound Gideon would remember for the rest of his life. Small. Unarmored.

"Sera," she said.

The woman didn't respond. She walked past her sister the way you walk past furniture, smiling vaguely at the sky, and paused once to watch a bee work a clover flower with an expression of mild, empty wonder — the wonder of someone seeing everything and inhabiting nothing. The cure. The crown's great mercy, administered twenty years ago to a girl who talked to cats. Kept afterward in a comfortable room in the Inquisition's own house, Gideon realized, like a trophy. Like proof the treatment worked.

Like a wound wearing armor, looking at its own reflection.

"She's happy," Vess said. It came out like a question. Then again, firmer, the way you drive a stake into ground that keeps shifting: "*She's safe.* The cure saved her. From all of this. From—" Her hand swept at the open gate, the beasts, the free faces. "Whatever this is."

Wren stepped out from behind Gideon before he could stop her.

"She talks to horses," Wren said. "Your sister. Even cured. Even emptied out. When nobody's looking, she goes to the stable and she hums to them, and they come to her, and for a minute — just a minute — there's somebody home behind her eyes. I heard your horses say so." The child lifted her chin. "You didn't save her, lady. You just locked the door on a person and called the lock a cure. And you've been guarding that lock ever since so you'd never have to open it and see."

The meadow went very quiet. Even the villagers stilled.

Vess looked at her sister. Sera smiled at the bee. And the Inquisitor — who had burned villages, who had bought roads and broken men, who had hunted a child across half a frozen country — closed her eyes, and Gideon watched twenty years of certainty die on her face not with a battle cry but with the small, terrible sound of a key being set down.

When she opened her eyes, they were wet, and her voice was level, and she was more frightening and more human than Gideon had ever seen her.

"Go," she said. "All of you. Before I remember what I'm supposed to do."

Nobody moved for a heartbeat. Then Maren stepped forward, and — Gideon would swear this to his grave — pressed her ledger into Vess's hands. "Every gifted soul still living," the old scholar said gruffly. "Names, gifts, kin. Some of them are cured like your sister. Some of them are hiding in cellars praying for a door. If you're done burning, start reading."

Vess stared at the book as if it might bite. Behind her, her soldiers reached the crest of the hill, crossbow bolts glinting, calling her name — *Inquisitor! Your orders, Inquisitor!* — and she raised one hand without turning, and they halted.

"Go," she said again, softer. "The door won't wait forever."

It would, actually — the gate stood open and easy as a tavern door — but nobody corrected her. The fugitives gathered themselves, and the villagers came among them with bread and blankets and questions, and the procession started down toward the valley while Vess stood alone in the gateway with a ledger in her arms and her empty-eyed sister's hand finding hers, at last, by accident or instinct, fingers curling into fingers.

Gideon was the last to leave the meadow. At the edge of the grass he stopped and looked back once. Yara stood beside him — beside him, of her own will, no voices between them now, nothing but choice — and together they watched the Inquisitor of Thornfell sink slowly to her knees in the clover, holding her sister, as the golden light of the open Veil fell over them both like a blessing neither of them had earned and both of them, maybe, needed.

Then Gideon turned downhill, toward the smoke and the music and the strangers with kind faces, into a country with no name and no maps and no law but what they built themselves.

He couldn't hear the birds anymore. But Wren slipped her small hand into his, and pointed to where swallows were stitching the evening sky above the valley, and told him, word by word, exactly what they were saying.

There was a lot of it. Apparently they had opinions about the newcomers.

Gideon laughed — really laughed, for the first time in longer than he could measure — and walked on down into the light.
