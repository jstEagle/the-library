## 21. A Country With No Name

The first thing Sorrel noticed about the other side of the Veil was the quiet — not silence, exactly, but a different quality of sound, like the world had been holding its breath for a generation and had finally remembered how to exhale.

She stood on a hillside of grass so green it looked painted, and she waited for the grief to arrive. It always did. Grief was the most punctual companion she'd ever had; it had walked beside her since the day her brother died in the snow, and it had kept pace through every mile of Thornfell, through the gorge where Brann fell, through the seam of light that should have taken Gideon Pemberton and didn't.

But this morning the grief came late, and when it came it came gently, the way an old dog comes to sit by a fire it knows it's welcome at.

"Are you going to stand there all day?" Maren asked, hauling herself up the slope beside her. The old scholar moved like a woman twice her age and thought like one half of it, and she had not stopped touching things since they'd crossed — the grass, the stones, the bark of trees that grew in shapes no Thornfell tree grew.

"Maybe," Sorrel said.

"It's very good standing."

"I know what I'm doing."

"You're cataloguing," Maren said, with the small smile she reserved for catching people out. "You've been doing it since we came through. Counting exits. Noting sightlines. Old habits."

Sorrel didn't deny it. She had counted three ways down off the hillside, two stands of timber thick enough to hide in, and one narrow valley to the east where smoke rose from cookfires — too many fires for a camp, too few for a town. A settlement. People.

People who were supposed not to exist.

Below them, spread along the river valley, the others were still emerging from the pass in ones and twos, blinking like creatures born underground. Tam stood with his hand on a boulder, weeping without any apparent awareness he was doing it. Ilsa sat alone on a flat stone, her face turned toward the sky, listening — though there were no crows here yet, or if there were, they hadn't found her. Wren hadn't let go of Gideon's sleeve since the light released him.

And Gideon himself — Gideon stood in the middle of the meadow with his head tilted, the way he'd tilted it his whole life, listening for voices that would never come again. Every few moments his expression flickered, hope and loss trading places so quickly it hurt to watch.

"He'll be all right," Maren said quietly, following her gaze.

"No, he won't," Sorrel said. "Not for a long time. Maybe never entirely." She paused. "But he'll be better than all right. That's worse, somehow."

Maren looked at her. "You've changed your mind about him."

"I haven't changed my mind about anything. I just—" Sorrel exhaled. "I ran out of reasons, Maren. He gave me a reason on that scree slope, and then another at Hollow Bridge, and then he went and offered his life at the door like it was nothing, like it was arithmetic, and I—" Her voice caught on something sharp. "I spent eleven years telling myself people only save you when it costs them nothing. He keeps proving me wrong, and I'm tired of being wrong."

Maren patted her arm once, briskly, the way she corrected a misfiled page. "Then stop arguing with the evidence. You were always a terrible liar anyway. Your face gives everything away."

"My face has kept me alive."

"Your face is going to get you hugged," Maren said, "if you don't learn to control it," and she went down the hill toward the others, leaving Sorrel glaring at her back and, despite herself, almost smiling.

* * *

They smelled the bread before they saw the village.

It came drifting up the valley on the morning wind — woodsmoke and yeast and something with honey in it — and the whole ragged company stopped at once, like a herd catching a scent. Wren's stomach growled loud enough that Tam laughed for the first time since the gorge, a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh.

"They know we're here," Gideon said. He wasn't listening anymore — Sorrel could see him resisting the habit, jaw tight — but he was watching the treeline the way he watched everything, patient as stone. "Have been since dawn. Someone's been standing under those pines for an hour deciding whether to come out."

As if summoned, they came out. A dozen of them, maybe more, stepping from between the trees with the unhurried confidence of people on their own ground: farmers mostly, by their hands and clothes, and hunters, and one old woman leaning on a staff who walked at the front like a drawn blade walking.

None of them carried weapons openly. All of them carried weapons visibly.

The old woman stopped ten paces away and looked them over — the mud, the bandages, Ilsa's empty hands, Wren pressed against Gideon's side, Maren clutching her ledger to her chest like a shield.

"You came through the door," the old woman said. Not a question.

"Yes," Maren answered.

"How many?"

"Nineteen living. Two dead on the road behind us." Maren's voice didn't waver, but Sorrel saw her knuckles whiten on the ledger. "Brann Ostwick— no relation— and a man called Denny Fell, who saved a child's life and never learned her name."

The old woman closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, something had shifted in her face — recognition, or its ghost. "Ostwick," she repeated. "There were Ostwicks. Before. In the old country." She looked at Maren more closely. "You have the look of the lowland schools. Scholar?"

"Disgraced scholar."

"Aren't we all." The old woman turned and raised her voice, calling back toward the trees in a language Sorrel didn't know — old Thornfellish, she realized, the tongue that had been scrubbed from records and schoolrooms, surviving here the way a seed survives winter. "Come," the woman said, switching back. "You'll eat first. Questions after. That's the law here."

"There's no law here," Tam said. "That's the whole point of the place."

The old woman smiled, and it transformed her face into someone's grandmother, someone's memory. "Boy, there is nothing *but* law here. We wrote our own. First law: nobody eats alone. Second law: nobody grieves alone. Third law—" her eyes passed over each of them, lingering a moment on Gideon, on Wren, on Ilsa's silent shoulders, "—nobody is a thing here. Only people. Come and eat."

* * *

The village had no name, which the villagers considered a point of pride.

"We had one," a broad-shouldered man told Sorrel over the third bowl of stew, while around them the fugitives were being fed, bandaged, and fussed over by strangers with the terrifying efficiency of people who had done this before. "Before the erasure. My great-grandmother's generation burned the maps and the records and took the name with them. Let the crown erase a country that doesn't exist. Can't kill what you can't find on paper."

"And the crown never came looking?"

"Oh, they came. Twice, in my grandfather's time. Soldiers rode up the valley and found farmers and sheep and nothing worth taxing, because everything worth having was hidden in cellars and written in no ledger." He grinned. "We keep our own ledgers now. But we burn them every year at midwinter and start fresh. A country that forgets itself on purpose can't be betrayed by its own memory."

Sorrel thought of Maren's ledger — the real one, the copy that had betrayed them, the pages Corvin had sold. She thought of Brann's name, which existed now nowhere except in nineteen heads and one sister's heart, and decided that was the safest archive in either country.

"You're staring at my bread," the man observed.

"I'm staring at your honey," Sorrel corrected. "Bread's just the delivery system."

He laughed and pushed the honey pot across the table, and Sorrel ate until the hollow ache in her middle — the one that wasn't hunger, that had never been hunger — went quiet for a while.

* * *

She found Gideon at dusk, sitting on the fence of a paddock behind the last house, watching the village horses.

They were good horses, shaggy and sure-footed, and they knew he was there. They always knew. Even now — especially now — they drifted toward him along the fence line, ears forward, breathing at him, waiting for something. Waiting for the voice.

He didn't speak to them. He couldn't. Sorrel watched him raise one hand slowly, and the nearest mare — a gray with a scarred muzzle — pressed her nose into his palm and stayed there, and Gideon Pemberton bowed his head over that touch like a man receiving communion, and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say.

Sorrel sat down on the fence beside him. The mare regarded her with one dark eye and decided she was acceptable.

"I owe you words," Sorrel said.

Gideon glanced at her. "You don't."

"I do, and you're going to stand there and take them, because taking things gracefully is apparently the only skill you have." She kept her eyes on the horses. It was easier. "On the scree slope, I told you to leave the stranger to die. You overruled me. I hated you for it. I told myself you were a sentimental fool who'd get us all killed for a conscience, and when Denny pulled Wren out of the ravine two days later, I still hated you, because being wrong felt worse than being cruel."

The mare snuffled against his hand. Gideon waited. He was good at waiting. It was, Sorrel suspected, most of what his gift had ever actually been — the patience to let another creature finish its sentence.

"At Hollow Bridge," she went on, "when Vess had the knife at Wren's throat, you gave up your blade without a blink. And at the Veil—" Her throat closed. She made herself go through it. "At the Veil you traded yourself like a sack of flour. Quietly. Without asking anyone's permission. Including mine."

"You'd have said no."

"Damn right I'd have said no. That's not the point." She finally turned and looked at him — at the tired, lined, stubborn face of a man who had spent his whole life fluent in a language the world wanted dead, and had given up the language itself rather than let a child pay for it. "I spent years believing my gift made me a killer. That it killed Bran. And then I told you the truth of it on night watch — that I let him die, that I chose myself — and you wouldn't give me the punishment I asked for. You just... forgave me. Like handing me bread. Like it cost you nothing."

"It didn't cost me nothing," Gideon said quietly. "It cost me watching you carry it another day. That's a price."

Sorrel laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her. "You're infuriating."

"So I'm told. Frequently. By mules."

And there it was — the joke he shouldn't have been able to make yet, the wound worn lightly, and Sorrel understood suddenly that he would survive this. Not whole. Nobody came through a door like that whole. But alive in the way that mattered.

"I accept it," she said. "The forgiveness. I've been chewing on it for two hundred miles and it doesn't get smaller, so I'm done chewing." She stuck out her hand, rough palm up, the way deals were sealed among smugglers and soldiers and everyone else the law had no use for. "Sorrel Vane accepts forgiveness from Gideon Pemberton, witnessed by one horse and whatever gods bother with this valley."

Gideon took her hand. His grip was warm and callused and steady. "Witnessed," he agreed solemnly. "The horse will keep the record."

"She'd better. I hear she's trustworthy."

The gray mare blew air through her lips, equine and dismissive, and both of them laughed, and somewhere in the laughing Sorrel felt something shift its weight and settle — not leave, grief never left, but lie down at last, curling up at her feet instead of dragging at her heels.

* * *

The birds started at dawn.

Sorrel woke in the loft of a house whose owners had simply appeared at supper and said *you'll sleep here*, and lay still beneath the wool blanket, listening. Song thrushes, blackbirds, something with a voice like water dropping — a chorus such as Thornfell's poisoned valleys hadn't hosted in decades. Here, past the Veil, the world sang like it used to before anyone taught it fear.

She heard Wren's bare feet hit the floorboards below, quick and eager, and heard the girl slip out the door into the grey-gold light, and Sorrel knew — the way you know a storm is coming, the way you know bread is done — what was about to happen.

She got up and leaned out the window.

Wren stood in the dooryard with her face tipped up to the branches, and the birds had gathered for her — dozens of them, lining the roof beam and the fence rail and the low wall, singing straight down at her the way they used to sing at Gideon. And Wren was answering. Chirps and whistles, fluid and joyful, a conversation in full flow, the child translating the morning for anyone who cared to listen.

Gideon came around the corner of the house with an armful of split firewood, and stopped.

Sorrel watched him watch it. Watched the flinch move through him — the reflexive tilt of the head, the reaching for voices that weren't there anymore, the sudden terrible clarity of a man hearing music he once played. The firewood trembled in his arms. He set it down very carefully, piece by piece, as if his hands needed a job or they'd shake apart.

Wren saw him. She went quiet mid-phrase, and the birds went quiet with her, and for one long moment the dooryard held its breath.

Then the girl crossed the yard, took his wood-stained hand in both of hers, and tugged him into the middle of the dooryard, into the center of all that song.

"Dance," Sorrel heard her say. "You don't need words for dancing."

"I don't dance."

"Everyone dances. Badly. That's what makes it dancing." And Wren spun him, this big scarred smuggler with his gift gone silent, spun him right there in the trampled grass while the thrushes started up again overhead, and Gideon laughed — actually laughed, helpless and rusty — and let himself be led.

Wren laced her fingers through his and kept them there. And when the blackbird on the fence delivered some long rippling phrase, she squeezed his hand twice — *that means good morning, that means the worms are plentiful, that means stay* — translating with her grip what she couldn't translate any other way, giving him the conversation secondhand, through her skin.

Gideon closed his eyes. Squeezed back.

From the window, Sorrel watched the birds sing and the man listen with his whole body, and understood that the Veil had taken his gift but not his fluency — that you could lose the language and keep the love, and the love might be the larger portion after all.

Behind her, Maren's voice, dry as autumn: "If you cry on that windowsill, the owners will think the house leaks."

"Shut up, Maren."

"Gladly. Pass the honey."

* * *

They stayed six days. Then seven. Nobody announced a decision to stay; it simply stopped occurring to anyone to leave.

Tam took over the far paddocks and could be found at all hours talking to the village horses — aloud, unhidden, grinning like a man let out of prison, because here he was. Ilsa walked the ridgelines every morning at first light, and on the fourth day the local crows found her — a different dialect, Maren speculated, a different country's grammar — and came home with her at dusk in a ragged black ribbon, and Ilsa smiled for the first time since the gorge, and the whole village pretended not to see because some griefs deserve privacy even in their healing.

Maren started a new ledger. One page. She showed it to Sorrel on the fifth evening: a single sheet listing names — theirs, the villagers', the dead — and at the bottom, in her precise scholar's hand: *To be burned at midwinter. To be remembered anyway.*

"That's contradictory," Sorrel said.

"That's people," said Maren.

On the seventh evening, Sorrel climbed the hill above the village where Gideon sat most nights, watching the pass through the mountains — watching the place where the Veil's light had sealed itself shut behind them like a healed scar.

"The others are worried about you," she said, sitting down beside him.

"I know."

"You keep looking at that pass like it owes you money."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Below them the village lamps were coming alight one by one, and the smell of baking bread rose again, insistent and ordinary, the smell of a world that intended to continue.

"I keep thinking," Gideon said slowly, "about what it cost. Brann. Denny. My—" his hand flexed, the old gesture, reaching for voices. "And whether a door like that stays shut. Whether it *should*."

"Why shouldn't it?"

"Because there are more of us back there." He said it plainly, the way he said everything, no drama in it at all. "More children scratching at doors in storms. More women like Maren keeping lists. More men like Tam hiding in cavalry lines. The Veil opened for nineteen of us. What about the nine hundredth?"

Sorrel looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at the pass, at the faint place in the cliffs where the evening light seemed to pool oddly, to gather, to *wait*.

"You're going back, aren't you," she said. "Or you're going to make the door come to you. One of the two."

Gideon didn't answer. But the gray mare, grazing loose on the hillside below, lifted her head and looked at the same spot in the cliffs — looked at it, and then looked at Gideon, and blew out a long deliberate breath, the way a creature does when it has understood something and is waiting, patiently, for the rest of them to catch up.

Sorrel felt the hair rise on her arms.

"Well," she said. "I suppose somebody had better tell Maren to sharpen her pen."

Far off in the gathering dark, where the light pooled in the rock, something shimmered — once, softly, like a held breath beginning to release.
