## 22. Yara and the Veil

The children of the valley called the lesson "Gideon's walking school," which he supposed was fair, because that was exactly what it was. No chalk. No slate. No roof. Just a line of small bodies trudging up the sheep path behind him while he pointed out the things that would keep them alive.

"That," he said, indicating a scree slope glittering in the morning light like a spilled purse of coins, "is a liar."

"It's rocks," said Pip, who was seven and had appointed herself his chief skeptic sometime around midwinter.

"It's rocks that *lie*. You look at it and it says, *come across me, I'm quick and easy.* You believe it, and it slides you down to the river in about four seconds. The mountain tells the truth all day long, but you have to learn which parts of it are gossip."

Behind him, a dozen children laughed, and Wren — thirteen now, taller than she had any right to be, with a braid Sorrel had bullied into respectability — snorted in a way that meant she'd heard this speech before and approved of it anyway.

He taught them the way Maren had taught him, before the ledger and the Veil and everything after. Read the wind off the grass before you trust the sky. A mule that plants its feet is not stubborn, it is *right*, and you'd best find out why. Water that goes quiet has found a hole to fall into. He taught them the smell of a storm two valleys off, the difference between a hawk's shadow and a crow's, the way a goat's ears telegraph its opinion of you long before it does anything regrettable.

What he did not teach them — what he *could* not teach them, ever again — was the rest of it. The listening underneath the listening. The language he had spoken since before he had words for it, the one that had cost him everything and given him everything and was, as of one bright terrible morning at the seam of the world, gone.

He missed it the way a man misses a limb. Not constantly. But every morning, in the half-second before he woke fully, he reached for it.

* * *

The valley had no name yet. That was the first thing he'd had to get used to. Beyond the Veil, the erased descendants of old Thornfell had built their houses along a river that ran the wrong way according to every map Gideon had ever memorized, and they had looked at the fugitives' question — *what do we call this place?* — with the mild bafflement of people who had never needed one. "It's here," an old woman named Dagny had said, shrugging. "Call it that if you like."

So some of them did, at first. *Here.* It didn't stick. Nothing stuck, and eventually the not-sticking became the name of the thing itself: a country that refused to be named, the way a wild thing refuses a collar. Maren, who had spent a lifetime believing that everything true could be written down, had come around to the idea with the slow wonder of a woman watching snow fall for the first time. "A country that exists only by consent," she'd murmured, ink-stained fingers hovering over a blank page. "I've spent forty years in the wrong profession."

The ledger, she had burned. All of it, every name of every gifted survivor, in a copper bowl on her doorstep while the whole settlement watched. "Names are how they hunt us," she said. "From now on, we find each other the old way. We ask."

The children in his walking school were a mixture — some born beyond the Veil, some carried through in the exodus of the past seasons, a few orphans of the Inquisition's last bad year. None of them had the gift. That was the strange arithmetic of the new country: the Veil had taken Gideon's voice to the beasts as its toll, but it seemed to have taken everyone's, or perhaps the gift simply didn't cross, like a seed that won't grow in foreign soil. The children of the erased could talk to animals the way anyone talks to animals — hopefully, and mostly being ignored.

They didn't seem to mind. They had never known anything else.

Gideon minded. Quietly. On good days.

* * *

The mare came in autumn.

He had known her before, in the old country — a bay with a white sock and an opinion of humans that could curdle milk. She had run with the hill herds above Thornfell, and once, years ago, he had talked her out of a bog with nothing but patience and a very specific insult about her ancestry that she had apparently found persuasive. She had screamed at him then, a sound like a door tearing off its hinges, and he had laughed until his ribs hurt, and that had been the whole of their acquaintance. Two minutes and a bog.

He did not expect her to remember. He certainly did not expect her to *care*.

But there she was, on the third morning of the walking school, standing at the treeline above the sheep path with the wind combing her mane, watching the children the way a hawk watches a field. The children noticed her at once, because children notice everything and pretend not to.

"Gideon," whispered Pip, in the tone of a girl reporting a miracle or a crime. "There's a horse."

"I see her."

"Is she yours?"

"No one is hers," he said. "Least of all me."

But she followed them that day. Kept a respectful distance on the climb, drifted closer on the descent, and when a child named Oskar slipped on wet stone and went down hard on his knee, the mare was there before Gideon was — standing over the boy, head lowered, breathing that great warm hay-scented breath against his tear-streaked face until he stopped crying out of sheer astonishment.

By midwinter she was simply part of the school. She walked at Gideon's left side, a hand's breadth from his shoulder, never touching, never leading, never following — *beside*, which was a word he had come to understand meant more than all the others. The children named her Yara, over his objection that naming a wild mare was like naming the weather. The name stuck anyway. Names, he was learning, were not always how they hunted you. Sometimes a name was just a hand held out.

He tried, in those first months, everything. He talked to her the way he had talked to ten thousand beasts — the low tones, the honest posture, the vocabulary of the body that had once been a second mother tongue. And she watched him with one dark, intelligent eye, and she understood *him*, he was almost certain of that — understood the man, the meaning, the shape of his attention — but the old channel between them was closed, and they both knew it, and she stayed anyway.

That was the part that undid him, on certain evenings, when the school was dismissed and the light went long and gold across the valley. He would sit on the fence rail of Dagny's paddock — she had a paddock now, though no fence in the new country had ever successfully contained her; it was more of a suggestion, a place she occasionally consented to be — and Yara would stand next to him, and he would talk to her about nothing. The weather. Sorrel's terrible bread. The way Wren was growing out of her boots again.

And she would stand there and listen, voicelessly, the way he now lived, and it was — he searched for the word for a long time, and finally borrowed one from Maren, who had used it about the burned ledger — *sufficient*.

Not what he'd had. Not what he'd lost. But sufficient, the way bread is sufficient, the way a roof is sufficient. The way a friend's silence, at the right moment, is worth more than speech.

* * *

Sorrel found him there one evening in early spring, when the snow was going rotten in the fields and the river had found its loud voice again. She climbed the paddock rail with the economy of movement she'd never lost, even with the limp the scree slope had given her, and sat, and for a while they watched Yara crop the wet grass.

"Tam's back from the pass," she said.

Gideon's heart did the thing it always did at that sentence — the lurch, the old reflex, the body remembering that news from the pass had once only ever meant one of two things, and both of them were bad.

"He say anything?"

"He said a lot of things. He always does. But the short version is this: there's a family camped at the Veil. Five of them, maybe six. Been there two weeks." She paused, and he could feel her choosing the words with the care she'd learned to give things, late and at great cost. "They came from our side, Gideon. From *here*. They're trying to go *back*."

He was quiet for a moment. The river talked to itself in the shallows. "The Veil doesn't open."

"The Veil opened." Sorrel's voice was flat, but he'd sat enough night watches with her to hear the thing underneath the flatness. "Tam watched it happen. The seam just — parted. Like it had been waiting for someone to knock. They walked through, all of them, and nothing was asked. No price. No toll." She turned and looked at him then, and her eyes in the failing light were the same eyes that had once argued with him on a freezing slope about whether a stranger was worth a crate of medicine. "It opens both ways now. It's been opening both ways since the day it broke for you."

He looked at his hands. They were a working man's hands, scarred from rope and stone, and they had held a knife at a bridge and given it up, and they had pressed against a seam of light while every creature he had ever saved came out of the dark to stand between him and a bargain. He had thought, in the months after, that the Veil had simply been broken — that the beasts' refusal had smashed the lock along with the door, and what remained was just a hole in the world.

But a hole doesn't choose whom to admit. A hole doesn't wait. And it had waited, all these months, closed, until a family stood before it and *asked*.

"It's not broken," he said slowly. "It's changed. It stopped demanding."

"Or it got what it wanted and doesn't need to anymore." Sorrel shrugged. "Maren's got four theories. She's writing them all down, which is very funny, given the ledger. Wren's got a fifth theory that involves the mare, but Wren's theories involve the mare in everything lately."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "What's the family going to do? Come back here, if it opens?"

"They're deciding. That's the thing, Gideon." Sorrel picked a bit of bark off the rail and flicked it away. "Nobody has to decide anymore. That's what Tam kept saying, all the way down the pass, grinning like an idiot. The door goes both ways and it doesn't take anything from anyone. Which means—" She stopped, and he watched her wrestle with it, this woman who had once believed the world was a ledger of debts, every kindness a loan, every mercy an accounting error. "Which means nobody's trapped. Not on either side. The ones who stayed. The ones who were *cured*." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the word. "The ones who were left behind."

He thought of the erased villages, the ones that had never fled, that had lived under the crown's nose for a generation by keeping their heads down and their gifts hidden. He thought of children like Wren, still being born on the far side of the mountains, scratching at strangers' doors in storms. He thought of Vess — and he did think of her, more often than he'd expected to, the Inquisitor with the wound wearing armor, who had stood at the edge of the light with her sister's emptiness reflected in her face and had, in the end, let them go. He wondered, sometimes, whether she was still hunting. He wondered what she would do with a door that asked for nothing.

"Someone should go tell them," he said. "The villages on the far side. That it's open. That there's a place."

"Tam's already volunteering. He'd volunteer to fetch the moon if someone suggested it." Sorrel slid off the rail, and her hand landed on his shoulder — brief, hard, the whole of her forgiveness still folded inside it, accepted at last but never once mentioned, because some things between them were better left as actions. "Maren wants you to lead it. She says you're the only one who knows both sides of the mountains anymore. She said — and I quote, because it made me laugh — 'Pemberton has spent his whole life being a door for other people. He may as well be a good one.'"

He watched her walk back toward the lights of the settlement, her uneven stride steady as a drumbeat, and he sat a long while after she was gone.

* * *

Yara came to stand beside him when the stars were out. She did that now, in the evenings — came without being called, which was the whole miracle of it, the whole impossible daily resurrection of it. A wild mare, choosing him. Every day, choosing him again.

"I'd have to cross it," he told her. "The Veil. To carry the word. And I don't know what it would take from me this time. It already took the only thing I had left to give." He reached up and touched her neck, the coarse warmth of her, the muscle shifting under his palm. "I'm not sure there's anything left to take."

She turned her head and regarded him with one eye that held the whole dark hillside in it, and she did not answer, because she couldn't, and he understood her perfectly anyway. It was, he realized, the great joke of his life — the gift gone, and him more fluent now than he had ever been. He had spent thirty years hearing what animals said. It had taken losing that to learn what they *meant*.

And what she meant, standing hipshot in the starlight with her breath steaming, was the same thing she meant every evening: *I will be here when you go. I will be here when you come back. Choose, and I will match you.*

Below in the valley, a lamp moved in Wren's window — up late again, reading Maren's new book, the one about a country with no name. Somewhere Sorrel was burning the bread for tomorrow, on principle. Tam was probably already packing.

Gideon looked east, toward the pass, toward the seam in the world that no longer demanded anything — that waited, patient as a friend, for whoever had the courage to knock.

"All right," he said to the mare, to the mountain, to the open door at the edge of everything. "In the morning, then."

Yara blew out a long breath, and turned her head toward the pass, and stood beside him a while longer in the dark — two voices, both quiet now, both going anyway.
