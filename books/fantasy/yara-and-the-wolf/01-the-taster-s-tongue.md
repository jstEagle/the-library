## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The arsenic was in the stew again.

Paloma Almeida tasted it the way other men heard their own names — instantly, without effort, and with a small private weariness. It sat at the back of the flavor like a guest who had arrived early and hoped not to be noticed: a faint metallic sweetness beneath the lamb and the dark beer and the prunes, so delicate that a courtier chewing with his mouth open and his mind on the queen's neckline would never find it. But Paloma had been finding it for eleven years, and it was never late, never shy, never entirely absent from a Thursday supper.

He swallowed.

That was the first rule of the trade, the one Vess had beaten into him before he'd grown his first beard: *you do not spit.* Spitting was a statement. Spitting was an accusation with a wet end to it. A taster who spat had already chosen a side, named a suspect, and made himself a piece on the board — and pieces got moved, and pieces got sacrificed. A taster who swallowed was furniture. Furniture survived.

So he swallowed the arsenic, and the lamb, and the prunes, and he let the poison settle into him the way rain settles into a stone that has been rained on for a very long time. Somewhere in his blood, the eleventh year of small doses met the tenth and the ninth, and his body did what his body had been trained to do, which was nothing at all.

"Well?" said Master Corvane, the under-steward, a man shaped like a wine cask and about as curious.

"Excellent," Paloma said. "The prunes are doing something interesting with the lamb."

Corvane grunted, satisfied, and waved the serving line forward. The king's supper went out into the hall in a procession of silver and steam, and Paloma stepped back against the wall with his hands folded, becoming the thing he had spent a decade perfecting: a man no one looked at twice.

It was craft. People thought invisibility was a quality you were born with, like a plain face, but it wasn't. It was a discipline, and Paloma was its monk. He had learned to stand where the candlelight ended rather than where it began. He had learned to hold his cup at the angle that said *I am drinking, do not address me.* He had learned that if you blinked a half-second slower than other men, they found you unsettling, and if you blinked a half-second faster, they found you stupid, and either way they stopped talking in front of you — which was, of course, the entire point. He knew the weight of every poison in the royal pharmacopoeia by the shape of its silence in a spoon. He knew which of the king's twelve cousins wanted the king dead, and in what order they had tried, and how much each failure had cost them, because he had tasted every attempt and said nothing, and would say nothing, until the day the guild told him otherwise.

The guild. He kept its ledgers. But that was the night's work, and the night's work lived in a different room of his life, the way a man's shadow lives in a different country than his body.

In the hall, King Aldous the Third ate his Thursday stew and did not die, and would not die, because the arsenic in it was not meant to kill him. That was the part that had taken Paloma three years to understand and would take most people a lifetime: the poison was *calibrated*. A slow arsenic, dosed to erode a man's nerve and shorten his temper and make him sign, in his worsening years, the sort of decrees that a certain faction of the court found convenient. The king was not being murdered. The king was being *edited*. And the editor had never once considered that the man with the tasting spoon had read the draft.

Paloma catalogued it, as he catalogued everything. *Thursday stew, arsenic, slow, same hand as the last nine Thursdays, same source — the brown spice jar, third shelf, the one the under-cook Marla refills from a parcel she believes comes from her sister in the country and actually comes from a man with a scarred knuckle who visits the kitchen gate on the second and fourth Friday of each month.* He filed it in the vast silent archive of his skull, where it joined the nightshade in the king's wine on feast days (meant for the king's brother, who drank first from the shared cup, and who had been dying by inches for two years to no one's official knowledge), and the hemlock trace in the honey (a test, once, to see if the taster was awake — he had been, and had said *delicious*, and had passed), and four hundred other entries that would never be spoken aloud in this life or any other.

That was the loneliness of it. He had never fully let himself think of it as loneliness before — loneliness was a complaint, and complaints were visible, and visibility was death. But tonight, standing in the warm dark of the serving passage while the hall roared with music and the king laughed at something his favorite cousin said, Paloma felt the shape of it plainly, the way you feel a stone in your shoe only when you finally stop walking.

He knew everything. He could say nothing. And the two facts together meant that he belonged to no one.

A taster cannot be a friend. A friend might be asked, over wine, *what do you think of the chancellor?* and would have to lie, and lying badly is fatal, and lying well is worse, because it works, and you do it again, and one morning you are a man made entirely of other people's false impressions, and there is no one left who has ever met you. A taster cannot love, not properly, because love is a kind of telling, and telling is the one thing his trade had burned out of him like moisture from salt. A taster cannot even be honest with himself after a while. He tastes the poison, he swallows the poison, he files the poison, and somewhere in the filing the man does the filing becomes a ledger too — columns of facts, no commentary, no one to read him.

He was twenty-nine years old and the only creature in the palace who knew his true name was a woman who paid him.

"Almeida." Corvane's voice, at his elbow, and Paloma came back into his body the way a swimmer comes up through dark water — smoothly, without splashing, so no one sees he had ever been under. "His Majesty wants the honey wine at the third course instead of the second. Note it."

"Note it," Paloma agreed, and Corvane moved off, already forgetting him, which was the highest compliment the palace paid.

* * *

The third course came, and with it the honey wine, and with the honey wine a surprise.

Not in the wine. Paloma tasted it first in the air — a wrongness in the kitchen's smell, a sweetness where there should have been fat and smoke. Then in the small bowl of sugared plums that traveled with the wine, set before the king's brother, Lord Edvard, who had grown too ill of late to attend feasts and had come tonight against his physician's advice, pale and sweating and grateful to be among people.

Foxglove. Not in the wine — in the plums. Dosed for a man whose heart was already weakened by two years of slow nightshade, a mercy-killing dressed as a dessert. And the hand that had arranged it was, by the placement of the bowl and the timing of the course change, the same hand that had requested the wine be moved to the third course.

Corvane had requested it. Corvane, who never requested anything, who was a cask, who was furniture, who had been furniture for thirty years — furniture did not rearrange the order of a king's courses.

Paloma stood in the serving passage with the bowl of plums in his hands and did the arithmetic he was built for. If Edvard died tonight, the suspicion would fall on the kitchen, on Marla, on the sugar, on the taster. A dead prince's poisoner is always found, and the finding is always convenient, and the convenient place for a taster to be found guilty is exactly where this bowl would put him. Corvane was not editing the king anymore. Corvane was *spending* him — spending the brother, spending Marla, spending the taster, buying himself a clear road to the under-stewardship of the treasury or whatever it was casks dreamed of.

He had perhaps four minutes before the plums reached the king's brother's hand.

He could swallow it. That was the rule. That was the whole architecture of his life: swallow, file, survive. He could watch Lord Edvard eat a sugared plum and fold gently over the table, and tomorrow he would taste the inquiry's questions and say *I noticed nothing*, and he would survive, because he always survived, because surviving was the only thing he had ever been allowed to be good at.

He looked at the bowl. Small plums, glazed with sugar, dusted with gold leaf because it was a feast. Somewhere in the city below the palace, in a room he had not thought about in years, there had once been a girl who stole sugared plums off the windowsill and ate them all before he could have a single one, and laughed at him with juice on her chin.

Yara.

He had not let himself think the name in a long time. It lived in the ledger of him, in the sealed section, in the entry he never opened: *sold, years ago, debt of the father, buyer unknown.* He had built his whole silence on top of that entry like a man builds a house on a grave.

He did not know, standing there with four minutes on the clock, why the name came to him now. He only knew that the girl with the juice on her chin would have looked at the plums, and looked at him, and said, *well? Are you going to let them?*

Paloma Almeida, who had swallowed eleven years of poison without a word, did not spit.

He did something smaller and more terrible. He turned the bowl in his hands as he carried it through the passage — a quarter-turn, the kind of adjustment a tired servant makes without thinking — and at the doorway to the hall he stumbled, gently, gracefully, the way a man stumbles when his boots are new, and the plums went down among the rushes at the threshold, and he knelt and gathered them with murmured apologies while Lord Edvard, three tables away, complained amiably about the delay.

"Clumsy," said Corvane behind him, softly, and there was something in the softness that Paloma had never heard there before. Something that was listening.

"Clumsy," Paloma agreed, and smiled the small stupid smile of a man who is nothing but a pair of hands, and felt the foxglove sugar dissolve on his fingertips, sweet, sweet, and deadly, and filed it.

* * *

The guild kept its offices above a chandler's shop in the Tallow District, and Paloma climbed to it that night with the foxglove still ghosting his fingertips and the day's entries stacked in his head like unsent letters. He let himself in with the iron key that did not exist on any inventory, and lit the single lamp, and sat down before the Ledger of Impossible Things.

It was his job, the ledger. Entries in trade: years of life, borrowed luck, forgotten names, favors owed between people who did not officially exist. His hand was considered the steadiest in the guild, his discretion absolute. Vess said he wrote numbers the way other men prayed.

He opened to the night's page and began to write. *Thursday. Stew, arsenic, slow, unchanged. Wine, moved, purpose unclear. Plums, foxglove, intercepted, cost: one stumble, one suspicion, one Corvane listening.* He wrote the palace's business in the guild's cipher, and the guild's business in his own head, and between the two of them he kept the only honest accounts in the kingdom.

Near the bottom of the page, in the section reserved for standing debts and old sales, the ink shifted.

He stared at it. He had been writing that page for six years. The ink did not shift. Ink was the one honest thing in his life.

But it had shifted — a small dark movement in the entry at the very bottom, the old one, the sealed one, the one written in a hand that was not his and that he had never been able to stop himself from reading a hundred times:

*One soul. Girl, aged nine. Sold against debt of the father. Buyer: —*

And then the buyer's name, which had been a smooth blank smudge for as long as Paloma had kept the book, moved. The smudge stirred like something waking under a blanket. And beneath it, in letters that had not been there a moment before, faint as breath on cold glass, a word began to form.

Paloma did not read it. He closed the ledger with a sound like a held breath released, and sat in the lamplight with his heart going hard, and told himself the rules: *never read the entries aloud, never read them twice, never —*

The lamp guttered though there was no draft.

And from the dark of the stairwell below, in the empty chandler's shop at midnight, came the sound of the front door opening, unhurried, and slow footsteps on the stairs, and — impossibly, absurdly, terribly — the soft click of claws.
