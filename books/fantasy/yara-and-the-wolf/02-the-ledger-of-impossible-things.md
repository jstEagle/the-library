## 2. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The footsteps stopped three stairs below the landing.

Paloma did not move. He had learned long ago that stillness was the first armor of an invisible man — before silence, before the flat unremarkable face, before the trick of positioning yourself in a room where sightlines naturally slid past you. Stillness first. Everything else after.

In the lamplight the ledger sat closed under his hands, warm as a sleeping animal.

The claws clicked once more. Then, unhurried, they receded — down the remaining stairs, across the chandler's floor, and out through the front door, which opened and shut with the same patient courtesy with which it had opened.

Paloma let out a breath he'd been rationing for two minutes.

"You left the door unbolted," said Vess, from directly behind him.

He did not jump. He was proud of that. His heart performed a full revolution without his permission, but his shoulders stayed level as he turned, and Vess stood at the top of the stairwell with a covered dish in one hand and her stick in the other, small and round and dry as a wasp's nest, watching him with eyes like wet river stones.

"The door was bolted," Paloma said.

"Mm," said Vess. "And now it isn't. Put the ledger down, boy. You've been reading."

"I've been *keeping*," he said. "There's a difference. Keeping is what you pay me for."

Vess came forward and set the covered dish on the table — bread, cheese, a pear cut into careful slices, because Vess believed fear could be managed with food the way other people believed it could be managed with prayer. She lifted the cloth, nudged the plate toward him, and then, without asking, laid her hand flat on the ledger's cover.

Her hand was small and brown-spotted and steady. It had been steady for seventy years, most of them spent in this trade.

"Which page?" she asked.

"None," said Paloma.

"Paloma."

"The winter entries. Traded years. Borrowed luck." He reached for the pear because it gave his hands somewhere honest to be. "A soul sold under a name I almost recognized. That's all."

Vess took her hand off the book the way a woman lifts her palm off a hot stove — not fast, nothing so undignified as fast, but with a total finality, as though she would never touch it again tonight.

"Almost recognized," she repeated.

"It's nothing." He said it the way he said everything: evenly, quietly, pitched to be forgotten the moment it left his mouth. That was his gift, the court called it. The taster's tongue, the taster's face. A man who notices everything and says nothing, and whose saying-nothing is itself a kind of noticing. "Half the names in those books are almost something. That's what makes them impossible things. If they were plainly anything, they'd just be crimes."

Vess sat down across from him. She did this slowly, joints announcing themselves, and folded her hands on the table between the plate and the ledger, so that her fingers lay exactly on the border between the food and the thing he must not ask about.

"When I was your age," she said, "I read an entry aloud."

Paloma went very still again. This time it wasn't craft.

"It was nothing dramatic," Vess continued. "That's the trap of it. You think the danger comes dressed in thunder. No. It was a borrowed-luck entry. A fisherman up the coast who bought seven years of another man's fair weather, and the entry was written in the old shorthand — the true shorthand, not the cipher we show the masters — and reading true shorthand aloud isn't reading, boy. It's *calling*. Every word in those books is a name wearing a coat. Say the coat's name out loud and the thing inside hears you."

"What happened?" Paloma asked.

"For eleven days, everywhere I walked, it rained," Vess said. "Only where I walked. A column of rain, moving through the city, following me from market to market like a dog that loves you. People started to notice. Do you understand? *I* started to be noticed. And in our trade, noticed is dead." She turned the ledger a quarter-turn on the table, aligning its spine with the table edge, a small compulsive neatness. "On the twelfth day I paid the rain back out of my own account and it stopped, and no one ever knew, and I never read aloud again. Not even in my sleep. Especially not in sleep. I sleep with wax in my ears some nights, if the work has been heavy. You should too."

"I don't dream the entries," Paloma said.

"No," Vess agreed. "You dream the *court*. The king's supper. The arsenic going down slow in the stew while you smile and swallow your own portion and say nothing. That's worse, if anything. The ledgers only hold impossible things. The court holds profitable ones." She pushed the plate another inch toward him. "Eat. Then go home. The entries will keep until tomorrow, and tomorrow I'll do the winter pages myself."

"That's not necessary—"

"It's not a kindness," Vess said. "It's arithmetic. You've been keeping the books for four years and you're too good at it. Good clerks get curious. Curious clerks read twice. Read twice, and the second reading isn't reading anymore — it's *memorizing*, and a memorized entry doesn't stay in your head like ink, boy. It stays like a seed."

* * *

He ate the pear because refusing Vess's food was a language she heard as insult. They talked of nothing — the price of candlewax, the chandler's cough, whether the new king's master of coin was a fool or merely played one. Safe subjects, surface subjects, the two of them skating across the evening like it was frozen water.

But underneath, Paloma kept returning, the tongue returns to a chipped tooth, to the page he had closed.

*One soul, sold entire, by the bearer's own kin.*

Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain.

Seller's mark: a debt-symbol he knew — every clerk in the guild knew it — because it was the mark used for debts of blood, the oldest kind, the kind the guild rarely touched and never spoke above a whisper. And the name of the sold —

Not Yara. It hadn't been Yara. He had checked, letter by letter, the way he checked the king's soup, methodically, tasting each syllable for poison.

It had been *Ysara*.

Close enough to make his mouth go dry. Far enough to let him breathe.

Names drifted in the guild's books the way silt drifts in a river. Sold souls were renamed routinely — a soul with a clean name fetched a better price, the same way a stolen horse does. *Ysara* could have been Yara renamed. It could equally have been a hundred other girls in a hundred other years, because grief makes pattern-seekers of us all, and Paloma had spent his whole life training himself to see patterns other people were blind to, which meant he also saw the false ones, glittering, everywhere, like coins at the bottom of a well.

Except.

Except that the sale date was the autumn their father died. Except that the seller's mark was pressed deep into the vellum, hard enough to tear, the way a man presses when his hand is shaking. Except that the buyer was the Court Beneath the Mountain, and everyone knew the mountain court bought *children* — that was the whisper, that was the story nursemaids told, the court below liked young souls because young souls burned longer.

Yara would have been nine.

"You've gone somewhere," Vess said.

"Nowhere," said Paloma. "Tired."

Vess looked at him for a long moment. In the lamplight her wet-stone eyes did the thing they always did, which was give back exactly nothing, reflect nothing, promise nothing. Forty years of the trade had polished her into a mirror that showed you only yourself, and whatever you brought to look with.

"Go home," she said gently. "Sleep. And Paloma—" She waited until he looked up. "Whatever you almost recognized, be glad you only almost did. Recognition is how the books catch you. First you recognize a name. Then you recognize a debt. Then one night you find yourself recognizing the whole ledger, every entry, top to bottom, and you understand that you were never keeping it at all. It was keeping *you*. There are clerks in the ground who learned that too late."

* * *

He walked home through the sleeping city the way he always did, hugging the wall-side of the street, moving in the seams between lamplight pools. Invisible by craft. It had been years since invisibility had felt like anything at all; it was simply the texture of his life, the way water is the texture of a fish's. But tonight the seams between the lights felt less like cover and more like a wound — tonight, for the first time in years, he caught himself wanting to be seen. Wanting to grab a stranger by the collar and say: *my sister's name is in a book, or a name like my sister's is in a book, and I don't know which is worse, and there is no one in this city I am allowed to tell.*

He said none of it. He walked home. That was the whole art of Paloma Almeida: the wanting and the walking home, coiled together, year after year.

His rooms were over a dyer's shop and smelled permanently of mordant and wool. He lit no lamp — habit; light invited eyes — and sat on his cot in the dark and made himself run the discipline, the nightly inventory, the taster's catechism:

*What did I taste today.* Arsenic, slow-acting, in the king's venison stew. Filed, not reported — reporting meant becoming visible, and visible men died quickly in that court; better to salt the king's next meals with the antidote regimen disguised as seasoning, better to nudge the kitchen boys toward a different supplier, quiet corrections, invisible mercy. The king would live and never know he'd been dying. That was the job. That was always the job.

*What did I hear.* The whispers about the mountain. The kitchen girl's god. The ledger's ink.

*What did I almost say.* Everything.

He slept badly, and dreamed — as Vess had predicted — not of the ledger but of the court: long tables, a hundred faces, himself moving among them with the tray, and every face turning to look at him as he passed, which had never once happened in his life, and in the dream he understood with perfect clarity that this was the most terrifying thing that had ever happened to him.

He woke before dawn with the taste of venison in his mouth, though he hadn't eaten meat in a week.

* * *

At midday he tasted the king's breakfast — nothing, today; plain eggs, honest bread, boredom — and carried his report to the under-steward with his face set to its usual blankness. At dusk he went to the chandler's shop.

Vess was already at the table, and the table was wrong.

She had the winter ledger open, but not to the traded-years section. She had it to the flyleaf — the blank page at the very front, the page where nothing was ever written — and she was standing over it with her stick gripped in both hands like a spear, and her face was the color of tallow.

On the flyleaf, in fresh wet ink, in handwriting Paloma knew better than his own—

Because it *was* his own.

—was written a single line:

*Who is Yara?*

Neither of them moved for a long time. Somewhere below, out in the midnight street, a dog began to bark, and then stopped all at once, mid-note, as if a hand had been laid gently over its mouth.

"I told you," Vess said softly, without turning around. "Never read them aloud. Never read them twice."

"I didn't," Paloma said. His voice came out strange, flattened, the voice he used in the throne room. "Vess. I swear it on my own name, I never read it twice."

"I know," said Vess. "That's what frightens me."

She turned, then, and looked at him, and for the first time in the four years he had served her, Vess's wet-stone eyes reflected something back: fear, his own fear, worn by someone else.

"The ink shifted on its own last night," she said. "Before you ever opened the book. Whatever is looking for that name, boy — it isn't looking through you."

The lamp guttered. And far beneath the floor, deeper than any cellar the chandler's shop had ever owned, something patient began — very faintly, very kindly — to knock.
