## 3. Prayers Answered

The girl's name was Odile, and she prayed the way people stole — quickly, quietly, with one eye on the door.

Paloma noticed her because noticing was the only thing he'd ever been good at. It was two hours before dawn in the castle kitchens, that dead hour when even the mice grew bored, and he had come down for a cup of the scalded milk the dairymaids left behind after skimming. He drank it standing in the shadow of the great hearth, where the embers still breathed orange, and he watched Odile kneel beside the flour barrels with her hands folded so tight her knuckles went white.

She thought she was alone. Nobody is ever alone in a castle. There is always a taster, or a spy, or a man who keeps ledgers for things that shouldn't fit in ledgers, standing just outside the light.

Her lips moved. No sound came out at first, and then a whisper escaped her, thin as steam:

"*Old one under the hill. Grandmother's grandmother called you and you came. We have nothing left. My mother's cough won't stop. The winter took the goat and the rent took the rest. Old one, I have no coin and no name worth selling. But if you're there — if any of the old ones are still there — I'll give you the first cake from every bake. The first and best. Forever. Please.*"

She said *please* twice. Paloma had heard a thousand prayers in his years at court — the chaplain's polished ones, the king's drunken ones, Lady Sarei's ambitious ones whispered into gilt-framed mirrors — and none of them had ever said please twice.

He took his milk and left without a sound. That was the craft of him. He filed the prayer away the way he filed the taste of bitter almonds: not his business, not yet, maybe never. A kitchen girl praying to a god nobody had worshipped since before the mountain got its name was sad, but sadness was cheap in this castle. It didn't require action.

He would think about that, later. How easy it had been to walk away the first time.

* * *

Three days later, the abundance began.

It started small, which was how all impossible things started — another lesson the guild had taught him, though the guild didn't know it had taught him anything. The kitchen ovens, temperamental for a decade, began to hold their heat perfectly. Flour that had weevils in it on Monday came out of the same sacks clean as snow on Wednesday. The cream rose thick enough to stand a spoon in, and the spoon stood.

Then it stopped being small.

Odile pulled four hundred perfect eggs from a delivery that was supposed to be two hundred rotten ones. The fishmonger opened his cart expecting stink and found silver-bright trout still flipping, fresh as if caught that morning, though no boat had gone out in a week of storms. A barrel labeled *turnips* yielded honey, real honey, pale and smelling of some flower nobody could name — a flower that didn't grow anywhere in the valley, the herbalist swore, her voice shaking.

And there were cakes. Every morning, on the windowsill above Odile's station, a small brown cake appeared, still warm. Simple fare — flour, fat, a little salt — but the smell of it made grown cooks stop what they were doing and stare at nothing.

"It's her tithe," said Marta, the oldest under-cook, in the hushed voice women used for births and deaths. "First and best of every bake. Someone's collecting."

"Someone's stealing," said the steward's man, but he didn't investigate. That was the strange part. Men like him investigated everything; it was how they justified their wages. He looked at the windowsill cakes and found somewhere else to be.

By the end of the week, the whole castle was eating better than it had in years, and nobody could say why, and everybody had a theory. The court does not tolerate an unexplained blessing. An unexplained blessing might be aimed at someone else.

* * *

"You can taste it, can't you."

Vess said it over supper in her rooms above the guildhouse, a cramped space that smelled of ink and lamp oil and the pickled herring she refused to give up. She was seventy if she was a day, sharp as a paper edge, and she had taught Paloma more about survival than his mother ever had — mostly by example, occasionally by throwing things.

"Taste what?" He knew. He wanted to hear her say it.

"The food. Don't lie to me, boy, lying is my trade." She pushed her plate toward him. "Bread. Tell me what's wrong with it."

He tasted it. Nothing was wrong with it. That was what was wrong with it. "There's no... residue. No chalk, no alum, no bean-flour stretch. It's just bread. Honest bread. Nobody in this city has baked honest bread in twenty years."

"And the honey?"

He'd tasted that too, at the feast two nights past, and it had frightened him in a way poison never did. Poisons spoke a language he knew — *this one kills slowly, this one blinds, this one was made by a man who hates another man.* The honey spoke no language at all. It tasted like the memory of summer, like a place he'd never been, like being six years old and safe. It tasted like something that wanted him to trust it.

"No poison," he said. "Something else. Something older."

Vess sat back and looked at him for a long moment, and he saw something in her face he had never seen there: fear. Vess did not do fear. Vess had once stared down a guild master who'd threatened to erase her son from the ledgers, and the guild master had apologized.

"The mountain," she said quietly. "They're saying it down in the trade-halls. The old power's stirring. Prayers going up unanswered for centuries suddenly getting answered, and not kindly, not cleanly — answered like a debt collector knocking." She turned her cup in her hands. "Your wolf-mountain. The one your sister—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than he intended. He softened it with silence, which was his other trick.

Vess let it go, because she always did, and they ate honest bread in unhappy quiet.

But later, walking home through the wet streets, he let himself think about Yara, which he tried not to do more than once a season, on the grounds that grief kept poorly and spoiled fast. She had been gone nineteen years. Taken — sold? lost? — during the famine year, when their father went up the mountain with empty hands and came back with grain and no daughter and a story that changed every time he told it. *The priests took her. The mountain took her. She wandered off.* Their father drank himself into the river within five years, and Paloma had gone to the capital with nothing but his strangely accurate tongue, and had built a life on tasting other people's attempts at murder while saying nothing about his own.

Nineteen years. And now the mountain was answering prayers again.

Coincidence, said the careful part of him, the invisible part. Mountains don't keep schedules.

Tell that to the honey, said the rest of him.

* * *

The court noticed, of course. The court noticed everything eventually, usually too late and always loudly.

Chaplain Ferren preached a thunderous sermon against "rustic superstition," naming no gods in particular, which everyone understood to mean all the gods the church had buried. The king's chamberlain ordered the kitchen inspected three times and found nothing but excellence, which infuriated him more than rot would have. Lady Sarei, who collected rumors the way other ladies collected fans, held a salon where the fashionable position was elegant skepticism, and half the guests went home and burned a pinch of salt at their thresholds anyway.

And beneath all the noise, quieter, the servants talked. Not about whether — servants had never doubted the old ones; doubt was a luxury of rank — but about *where*. Under the mountain, they said. Under *that* mountain. The one the castle was practically leaning against. The one whose roots the lowest cellars almost touched.

"They say the court down there kept the old ones asleep," Marta told the scullery boys, in the tone of a woman who wanted very much to be overheard. "Bound them. Fed on them, some tell it. And now the bindings are going soft."

"Bindings don't go soft," said a scullery boy.

"Everything goes soft," said Marta. "Ask any toothless cook."

Paloma stood at his station during the state dinner that night, tasting each course ahead of the king, and listened to the lords and ladies speculate over the miraculous menu. A marquis proposed that a rival house was smuggling in fine food to curry favor. A countess proposed witchcraft, and named three women who had wronged her, in case anyone was taking notes. The king himself laughed and said the gods were dead and good riddance, and everyone laughed with him, the way everyone laughs with kings.

Paloma tasted the seventh course — quail in that impossible honey — and felt it wrap around his tongue like a hand. *Trust me,* it said. *Remember being loved.*

He swallowed it, because swallowing was his job, and for the first time in nine years of service he was afraid the job wouldn't save him. You can spit out arsenic in time. You cannot spit out a memory of safety.

That night he dreamed of a door in the side of the mountain, and behind the door, something enormous turning over in its sleep, and the turning sounded like pages.

* * *

He should not have gone to the guildhouse. He knew the rules; he'd copied them onto the flyleaf of his own hand often enough. *Enter late. Leave early. Record, don't read. And never — never — speak an entry aloud.* But the dreams had shaken something loose, and the honey was still humming under his skin, and the streets were empty and rain-slicked, and the guildhouse window on Guilder Lane had its familiar crack of candlelight, and Paloma had spent his whole life walking past doors. Just once, he wanted to open one.

The night-warden let him in with a nod. Tasters had odd hours; the guild asked no questions it could bill for instead.

The archive room smelled of dust and tallow and, underneath, faintly, of that same unnamed flower. Paloma stopped in the doorway. Sniffed. Told himself he imagined it.

The ledgers lived in iron cabinets, one drawer per category: Years Sold, Luck Borrowed, Names Forgotten, Debts Unpayable. His own work lay open on the central desk — the current volume, half-entered, waiting for his neat hand. He lit a second lamp, sat, dipped his pen.

And froze.

The ink was moving.

Not flowing. Moving. The entries on the open page — routine stuff, a decade of labor traded by a miller for his son's schooling, three years of borrowed luck owed by a card-sharp — were shifting, the letters rearranging themselves with the slow patience of ants. He watched the word *owed* become the word *owned*. Watched a date rewrite itself backward by a century. Watched, at the bottom margin, a line of text surface through the paper like a body rising through dark water, letter by letter:

*and the first and best of every bake*

He knew that phrase. He had heard it whispered by firelight three days before the abundance began.

His hand shook so badly he set the pen down. The guild's ledgers recorded bargains; that was all they did; they were paper and calf-skin and ink, and ink did not *anticipate*. Ink did not record prayers made in empty kitchens to gods the church had drowned. Unless—

Unless the ledgers had always been listening to something else. Unless the guild itself, all its ledgers and categories and iron drawers, was merely a copy — a clerk's transcription of a far older book, written somewhere far below, in something darker than ink.

The line at the bottom of the page faded. The entries settled. The word *owned* stayed owned.

Paloma sat in the lamplight for a long time, listening to the rain and the sound of his own heart, doing the arithmetic he had avoided for nineteen years. If the ledgers were listening downward — if the guild's whole trade ran, like a millwheel, on currents coming from under the mountain — then every bargain he had ever entered was downstream of whatever slept down there. Including the entry he had never dared pull. Including the file marked with a surname he shared.

The old power wasn't just stirring. It was reading its mail.

He closed the volume, snuffed the lamps, and walked home through the rain with his collar up and his mind racing. Tomorrow, he told himself, he would ask Vess, carefully, sideways, about the deep archives. About the oldest volumes. About whether the guild kept records going back to the famine year.

Tomorrow, he would find out what his father had really bought with grain.

Behind him, up the dark slope of the mountain, something answered a prayer no one else had heard — and in the guildhouse window, faint through the rain, the candle flame bent sideways, all at once, as if something below had exhaled.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in his narrow room above the chandler's shop with the lamp burning low, and he let himself remember Yara properly, which hurt exactly as much as it always did.

Eight years old and already faster than him on the hill paths. Already better at whistling. She used to make him trade tastes — *you try mine, I'll try yours* — berries and bark and once, memorably, a beetle, both of them laughing until they sickened. She'd had no idea, then, that her brother's tongue was a rare thing, a thing men would someday pay to borrow. To her it was just a game they played, the two of them against the dullness of the world.

*You try mine,* she'd say. *I'll try yours. Then neither of us is alone in knowing.*

He wondered — for the ten-thousandth time, in the ten-thousandth phrasing — whether she had known what was happening on the mountain road that day. Whether she had gone willingly, trusting their father's smooth story, or whether she had screamed, and the trees had simply declined to carry the sound. Nineteen years was long enough for a scream to fade. It was not long enough for the wondering to.

Outside, the rain stopped. In the sudden quiet, Paloma heard, very distinctly, from the direction of the mountain: a bell.

One note. Deep enough to feel in his teeth, in his sternum, in the scar tissue of every poison he had ever swallowed. Not a church bell. Church bells broke the air; this one seemed to come from inside the stone itself, rolling up through the foundations of the city, setting the water in his washbasin trembling in rings.

The court's dogs did not bark. That was the worst part. Every dog in the capital, bred and trained to announce anything unusual, sat perfectly silent, ears flat, as if acknowledging a superior.

The bell rang once and stopped. Somewhere beneath the city, Paloma was quite sure, something had just opened its eyes. And somewhere in the guild's iron cabinets, in a drawer marked *Debts Unpayable*, an entry nineteen years old had just begun — he could feel it the way he felt arsenic, a slow cold bloom at the root of his tongue — to change.

In the morning, he told himself. Tomorrow he would pull the oldest volumes. Tonight he sat with the lamp and listened to the mountain breathe.

But when he finally drifted toward sleep near dawn, the last thing he heard was not the bell, and not the rain, and not the breathing stone. It was a voice — patient, amused, with a wolf's low gravel in it — speaking from nowhere, from everywhere, from directly behind his left ear:

*"You try mine," it said. "I'll try yours."*
