## 4. The Wolf at the Well

The well behind the east kitchens had gone dry three years ago. Everyone knew it. The court's water came up through the new aqueduct now, silver-piped and king-blessed, and the old stone well sat in its crook of ivy like a tooth left in an empty jaw. Servants used the shadow of it for assignations and stolen wine. The guards never bothered to patrol it.

Which was why Paloma went there when he could not bear to be watched even by people who never looked at him.

It was past midnight on the third night after the ink moved in the ledger. He had not slept. He had tried, and what sleep gave him instead was the kitchen girl's prayer repeated over and over in a voice that was almost his sister's, and the smell of honey that had no source, and the memory of black letters crawling across a page like insects deciding to be words again. So he rose, dressed in the grey clothes that made him furniture, and walked out into the cold with his hands in his sleeves and his face arranged into nothing.

The moon was a paring of rind over the mountain. The mountain itself stood against the stars the way it always did — vast, patient, closer than geography should allow — and Paloma caught himself watching it the way a man watches a dog he suspects of dreaming about him.

He was halfway across the courtyard when he realized the dark beside the well was occupied.

His first instinct was craft: keep walking, alter nothing, become less. His feet were already adjusting their rhythm, his shoulders already rounding into the posture of a servant hauling himself to some late chore, when the dark spoke.

"Paloma-of-the-nine-names."

He stopped. Not because he chose to. Because his body had heard something older than choice in that voice, the way a rabbit's body hears a hawk before the rabbit does.

The voice was low and rough and unhurried, gravel turned over in deep water, and it had said a name that had not been spoken aloud in nineteen years. Not *Paloma Almeida*, taster to the crown. Not *Master Ledgerman*. *Paloma-of-the-nine-names* — the game Yara invented when they were small, because their mother died when he was four and he kept trying to collect new mothers the way other children collected river stones, and Yara declared that any boy so hungry for names must have nine of them at least, and they spent one whole summer inventing them under the plum tree.

No living person knew that name.

Paloma did not run. Running made you visible; that was the first law of survival at court, learned before he learned his letters. But every craft he owned deserted him at once, and he found himself simply standing in the open courtyard, exposed as a man on a stage, looking at the darkness beside the dry well.

The darkness looked back.

It came forward by degrees, resolving not out of shadow but out of scale — he had taken it for a heap of sacking or a fallen trellis, and then the heap breathed, and then the heap was a wolf the size of a hay wagon standing in the moonlight with its head level with Paloma's chest. Its coat was the grey of storm-light on slate, of ash before the wind takes it, of all the colors winter keeps in reserve. It did not crouch. It did not bare teeth. It regarded him with yellow eyes that were wrong in a way his mind circled and could not land on, until it landed:

They were patient eyes. A man's patience, set in a wolf's face. The patience of something that had waited so long that waiting had stopped being a strategy and become a nature.

"There you are," the wolf said gently. "I've been trying to find the right night."

Paloma's mouth was dry in the specific way it got when a poison was working — but there was no poison. There was only the air, and the cold, and the enormous fact of a speaking wolf, and beneath all of it a terror so clean it felt almost like clarity. "This isn't happening," he said. His voice came out thin. "I'm asleep. This is the dream I've been having for three nights."

"If you were asleep," the wolf said, "you'd have somewhere to sit down." It lowered its great head toward the dry well, almost politely. "Come stand where I can see your face properly. You're very good at being seen badly. I nearly lost you twice between the kitchen door and here."

Every instinct screamed at him to go back inside, to bar his door, to file this away in the category of things observed and never named. That was how he had survived: everything observed, nothing named, all dangers swallowed in silence. And yet his feet carried him forward, three steps, five, until the wolf's breath stirred his hair — it smelled of cold stone and juniper and, absurdly, of the plum tree from the summer of the nine names — and he stood close enough to see that the moonlight went strange around the animal, bending slightly inward, as if the wolf were heavier than the world expected.

"You know who I am," Paloma said.

"I know who everyone is." The wolf's ears tilted. "That is not a boast. It is a consequence. I have been under this mountain longer than your court has been above it, and I hear everything that grows, everything that prays, everything that is sold." The last word landed with a weight that made Paloma's stomach drop. "And lately I hear something else. I hear a ledger rewriting itself. I hear a god waking in the walls of the world. And I hear a man who tastes poison for cowards asking the dark, night after night, whether his sister's soul is among the things that were taken from him."

The courtyard tilted. Or Paloma did. He put a hand against the cold lip of the dry well to steady himself, and the stone bit his palm, and the pain was ordinary and real and somehow the worst thing about the whole encounter, because it meant he was awake.

"I never asked the dark anything," he said.

"No," the wolf agreed. "You asked it everything. You just asked it silently. Silence carries, little taster. Better than words do."

*Little taster.* No one had called him that. No one had called him *little* anything since he was small enough to fit under Yara's arm when they slept. His throat closed. He became aware that his careful silence — the silence he had built plank by plank over nineteen years, the silence that fed him and housed him and kept him invisible and alive — had developed its first crack, and through the crack something was pouring that he could not swallow.

"My sister is dead," he said.

"Your sister is *held*," said the wolf. "There is a difference, and it is the difference that runs under this whole kingdom like water under ice."

Paloma gripped the well's edge. Below them, in its empty dark, the dry well smelled faintly of wet stone — though it had been dry for three years — and of something sweeter underneath, honey and plum blossom, impossible in the dead of winter. "Say what you came to say," he managed. "Or don't. If this is a poison, I'll taste it either way. That's my trade. But don't lead me around the garden first. I do the leading, usually. I know how it's done."

Something moved through the wolf's face — the wolf equivalent of a raised eyebrow, of a smile held back on principle. "A merchant to the marrow," it said. "Good. Then let us speak terms. I will tell you plainly, since you ask plainly." The yellow eyes fixed on him, and the patience in them shifted, and Paloma saw — briefly, terribly — the thing underneath the patience, the thing that had been waiting, and it was vaster and hungrier and sadder than anything wearing a shape should be.

"Nineteen years ago," the wolf said, "a debt came due in a poor house outside this city. A father owed money to men who collect in ways that are not written anywhere your court would recognize. He could not pay. So he paid in the only currency those collectors accept from men who have nothing." The wolf's voice did not soften; it went quieter, which was worse. "He sold them a soul. Two children in that house, and the collectors let him choose, and he chose the one whose name brought a better price."

The world went white at the edges. Paloma heard his own breathing, ragged, too loud. "Yara," he whispered. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. Saying it aloud broke something; saying it aloud was the crack running wall to wall.

"The entry exists," the wolf said. "In a ledger older than yours, kept deeper than yours. Buyer of record: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Seller of record—" the wolf pronounced each word like a coin placed on a table, "—Tomas Almeida. Your father. Paid in full, witnessed and sealed, the year of the Red Frost."

Paloma's knees went. He sat down hard on the well's rim without meaning to, without any of his craft, a man simply falling onto stone in the dark. Nineteen years. Nineteen years of believing she had died of the winter fever like half the street, believing it because it was easier than believing anything else, and all that time she had not gone anywhere — she had been *taken*, priced, invoiced, filed. And his father — his silent, broken father, who drank himself into the grave two years later and wept once, unforgettably, at Yara's empty grave-marker, and Paloma had thought the tears were grief.

They had been arithmetic. They had been a man counting what he'd spent.

"Why," Paloma said. It wasn't a question. It came out of him like blood from a wound. "Why are you telling me this. What do you want. Nothing under the sky tells the truth for free. I work for a guild that trades in souls, wolf. I know the price of truth better than anyone alive. So name it."

The wolf was quiet for a long moment. In that moment it seemed to grow older; the moonlight bent harder around it, and far above, on the black shoulder of the mountain, something answered — a single deep note, felt in the stones rather than heard, like a bell rung in the roots of the world.

"What I want," the wolf said at last, "is a road opened. What I offer is this: I will take you down to where your sister's soul is kept. I will show you the ledger of record. I will help you bring her out. And in exchange—" the yellow eyes did not blink, and wolves, Paloma knew, blink; whatever this was, it was making an effort to blink and failing, "—in exchange, when the time comes, you will carry a truth into your bright poisoned court above. A truth about a man you love. And you will speak it aloud, in daylight, where it can do its work. You will become visible, Paloma-of-the-nine-names. Visible and unarmed. That is my price."

"Not a price," Paloma said hoarsely. "A wound."

"Yes," the wolf agreed, simply. "Truths usually are. That's why your kind sells them so cheaply and buys them back so dearly."

Paloma looked down into the dry well. The sweetness rose out of it, plum and honey, and for a moment — he would swear this later, to himself, in the privacy of his own skull — he saw far down in the dark a small light, warm-colored, wavering, the way a candle wavers when someone walks past it. The way a flame wavers when someone leans close.

"Yara," he said again, to the light.

The light leaned toward him.

He stood up. His legs held, mostly. Somewhere behind his ribs, the silence he had lived inside for nineteen years lay with a long fracture through it, and he understood with terrible clarity that it would never hold sound the same way again. Whatever happened next — whether the wolf lied, whether this was some poisoner's elaborate trap, whether he lost Vess and the guild and the invisibility that had kept him breathing — he was going to find out what was at the bottom of that light.

"All right," he said.

The wolf tilted its head.

"Three nights from now," Paloma said. "I have obligations I cannot vanish from without vanishing permanently. The court sups on Thursday, and the stew will be laced again, and if the taster disappears before the tasting they will look for reasons, and reasons are the one thing I can't afford." He heard his own voice steadying, finding its old flat ledgerkeeper calm, and marveled distantly that it still worked with the crack running through it. "Three nights. Here. After the second bell."

The wolf regarded him for a long moment with those patient, patient eyes.

"You're not afraid," it observed.

"I'm terrified," Paloma said. "I'm just practiced at it. There's a difference, and it turns out I know more about differences than I thought."

The great grey shape stepped backward into the shadow of the ivy, and the shadow accepted it, and the last thing to remain visible was the eyes — yellow, patient, and for one flicker unmistakably amused.

"Thursday, then, little taster," said the dark. "Bring nothing. Tell no one. And Paloma — eat nothing anyone gives you between now and then. Not everything in that court poisons the body."

Then the courtyard was empty, and the well was dry, and the sweet smell faded like a hand withdrawn.

Paloma stood alone under the paring of moon with his heart slamming and his silence broken, and did the only thing he knew how to do with unbearable knowledge.

He went inside, and began, silently, to count the cost.

* * *

He did not sleep, but near dawn he drifted, and dreamed of the plum tree, and woke at first light with his decision already made and cold in his chest like swallowed iron. There was one thing left to do before Thursday, one thing he had avoided since the ink crawled: the oldest volume, the deep shelf, the red-bound ledger no ledgerman touched without the masters' leave.

He told himself he was checking the wolf's story. He knew, as he pulled on his grey clothes, that if the entry was there, he would not survive knowing it and staying silent — not anymore, not with the crack already through the wall.

He lit the lamp in his narrow room, unlocked the false back of his bookshelf, and drew out the oldest volume the guild possessed. Its leather was warm. That was the thing he could never explain to anyone, the thing that had kept him from opening it for years: the leather was warm, like something sleeping.

He turned to the year of the Red Frost.

The entry was waiting for him. It had been waiting for nineteen years, and it had rearranged itself since the last time he'd glimpsed these pages — the ink no longer sitting on the parchment but *rising* off it, faintly luminous, legible in the dark:

*Soul, one (1). Female child, age seven. Name retained by seller. Sold by Tomas Almeida, debtor, street of the Dyers. Purchased in settlement of debt, witnessed and sealed. Buyer: THE COURT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN. Condition on delivery: alive. Intended use: burning.*

And beneath it, in fresh ink, wet enough to shine, in a hand that had not written any other line in the volume:

*Thursday,* it said. *After the second bell.*
