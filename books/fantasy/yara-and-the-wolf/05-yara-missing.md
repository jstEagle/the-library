## 5. Yara, Missing

The guild kept its oldest volumes in a room that did not officially exist.

You reached it by taking the archive stairs past the third landing and stepping through the wall where the plaster bulged like a healed bruise. The trick was not to hesitate. Hesitation made the wall solid. Belief — or something close enough to belief that the difference stopped mattering — made it give. I had carried ledgers through that wall for nine years without once thinking about what the room beyond might hold, because thinking about things was not my trade. Swallowing them was.

Tonight I thought about everything.

I had told myself I was only going down for the winter tallies. Vess was at the masters' conclave until dawn, which meant I had six hours in which no one would ask me what I was reading. Six hours is a long time when you have trained your whole life to be unremarkable. It is an eternity when you finally want to be seen — even if only by a book.

The old volumes lived on shelves of black wood that drank the candlelight rather than reflecting it. They were bound in leather gone soft as skin, stamped with sigils from before the kingdom had a name, and they were arranged by nothing I could determine except perhaps grief. I pulled one at random: *Ledger of Borrowed Years, Third Compilation*. Another: *Register of Luck Detained in Transit*. A third whose title had been scraped off so thoroughly the cover was smooth as river stone.

Somewhere in these shelves was a name I had spent twenty years not thinking about.

Yara.

I had not said her name aloud since I was eleven years old. That was not sentiment; it was craft. In the court, names were handles, and handles got pulled. But names also worked the other way. Say a name enough times in enough rooms and someone eventually comes looking for the person attached to it. So I had folded Yara up small and put her away the way I folded away every dangerous thing: carefully, silently, completely.

Now the wolf had unfolded her in front of me with four words at the well. *I know who sold her.*

And here was the thing that had dragged me out of bed and through a wall at midnight: the wolf had not said *who bought* her. Everyone knew souls could be bought. Souls were stock-in-trade. The question that had been chewing through me like slow arsenic was the other word. *Sold.* Which meant there had been a seller. Which meant someone had stood at a table like the ones I kept accounts for and traded my sister the way traders traded rain in a drought.

I wanted it to be a stranger. God help me, I wanted it to be anyone but who I feared it was.

* * *

We were poor the way river stones are poor — smooth, worn, and used to it. Father was a ferrer's apprentice with ruined hands and a talent for debts, and Mother had been dead long enough that she existed mostly as a smell of rosemary and an argument we didn't have. There were three of us then. Me, Yara, and the hunger that came in every winter like a relative.

Yara was the loud one. Twelve years old and already the kind of girl who talked to strangers, argued with priests, and sang on the causeway where singing was forbidden. I was the quiet one even then. She used to say the gods split our family's voice in half and gave her all of it.

"Then you'll go deaf," I told her, "and I'll finally get some sleep."

"You'd miss me," she said.

"I'd sleep."

She laughed and pushed me into the canal, and I let her think she'd won, because letting Yara win was the cheapest way to hear her laugh, and her laugh was the only luxury our house owned.

That autumn the debt came due. I remember it as weather more than event — Father's face going grey, men at the door, the smell of tallow candles burning down toward nothing. I remember being sent to fetch water and coming back to a kitchen where everyone was very still, and Father was holding a piece of paper the way you hold something already broken.

"What is it?" Yara asked, because she asked everything.

"Nothing that concerns children," Father said.

But he wouldn't look at her. And she noticed. She was twelve and she noticed, and she looked at me across the table with those quick dark eyes, and whatever she saw in my face must have frightened her, because Yara — Yara who was never quiet — went silent too.

Three weeks later she was gone. No body, no search, no mourning. The neighbors said she'd run off to the coast with a tinker's son. Father said it once, twice, until it wore a groove in him and he could say it in his sleep. I said nothing at all. I was eleven, and I had already learned that silence was the only armor that fit.

What I remember last, though — what I have never once managed to fold away — is this: the morning after she vanished, Father paid off the debt. Every copper. He set the coins on the table in neat stacks like a man who had been saving for years, and he looked at them, and he wept.

Not with relief.

* * *

My hands were steady. Nine years of holding poison on my tongue had taught my hands never to shake, and I hated them for it now.

I searched by year. The guild's compilations ran deep — deeper than the masters admitted to clients. Most people thought the guild traded in favors and luck and the odd decade of life. Most people slept better that way. But there were older volumes, from the founding era of the trade, when the guild had been less a business than a priesthood with arithmetic, and in those volumes the entries stopped being euphemisms. They wrote *soul* plainly. They wrote prices in currencies that had no coins.

I found the compilation for the year I was seven. Set it on the reading stand. Opened it.

The pages smelled of cold iron and church incense, which is a combination no honest book should manage. The entries were written in the old clerk's hand, dense and unhurried, each one a small catastrophe rendered in beautiful penmanship. *One year of sight, sold by the bearer, purchased by —* redacted. *Sixty nights of true sleep, pawned against —* redacted. The redactions moved slightly when I wasn't looking directly at them. Old magic. Expensive magic.

I went through the volume page by page, and I told myself I would know the entry when I found it, and I was wrong, because when I found it I did not know anything. My mind simply refused, for three full breaths, to read the letters in order.

**Entry 447. One soul entire, female child, aged twelve years, sound and unbonded. Sold by the bearer, Tomás Almeida, against debt of forty crowns owed to the Ferrers' Compact. Purchased by: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Consideration rendered: full satisfaction of debt, plus one boon unnamed, redeemable by the bearer or his heirs.**

Plus one boon unnamed.

Plus one boon unnamed.

I read it again. And again. And the arithmetic assembled itself in my chest with the terrible inevitability of a poison taking hold. Forty crowns of debt — and Father had paid it in coin the morning after Yara vanished. Because the consideration had come through. Because the buyer had delivered. Because my sister's soul had been worth exactly one unnamed favor, and my father had spent it.

He spent it on me.

It arrived without evidence, the way the worst truths do — whole, finished, needing no proof. The year after Yara disappeared, Father's fortunes turned. Small mercies stacking into larger ones. The ferrer he apprenticed under died and left him the shop. A creditor forgave a loan on his deathbed, inexplicably. When the fever came through the quarter it took the neighbors on both sides and passed our door like a polite guest. And when the Guild of Impossible Things came recruiting — as they did once a generation, looking for children with the right kind of blood, the right kind of tongue — they looked at me, the invisible boy, the boy nobody ever remembered to punish, and they took me in and trained me and fed me and made me untouchable.

I had always assumed I was lucky.

I had always assumed I was good.

I stood in a room that did not exist, holding a book older than the kingdom, and understood that I was neither. I was purchased goods. My invisibility was not a talent I had honed; it was a product. Somebody's soul had been the raw material, and my sister's soul had been the price, and the guild — the institution I had served faithfully for nine years, keeping its books, tasting its enemies' poisons, swallowing its secrets like I swallowed everything else — the guild had held the receipt the entire time.

Vess's voice came back to me from a hundred evenings in this archive: *Never read the entries aloud, Paloma. Names wake up.*

I hadn't read it aloud. But I had read it. And Entry 447 woke up anyway, somewhere behind my ribs, and began to scream.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about fury: it tastes like nothing.

I am qualified to make that claim. I have tasted hemlock and nightshade, arsenic slow and fast, the distilled grudges of six noble houses. Fury, by comparison, was flavorless — just heat without character, occupying my mouth like an intruder with no manners. I sat down on the archive floor because standing had become complicated, and I put the ledger on my knees, and I was furious in every direction at once.

At Father, who had died five years ago leaving me nothing but his hands and his silences, and who could therefore never explain, never apologize, never tell me whether he had chosen me over her or simply chosen survival and called it fate. At the guild, which had taken me in knowing — *knowing*, the entry proved it, the guild brokered the sale, the guild's ink was on the page — and had set me to work cataloging other people's stolen children while mine gathered dust two shelves away. At the court above, which ate souls like sweetmeats. At the Court Beneath the Mountain, wherever and whatever that was, that had priced my sister at forty crowns plus one.

And underneath all of it, quieter and worse: at myself. Twenty years of not asking. Twenty years of professional silence turned inward, aimed at the one question that mattered. I had built an entire life on the discipline of not noticing, and the discipline had worked so well that I had never once noticed the shape of my own wound.

Yara had known, though. Standing in that kitchen with the paper on the table, she had known. She had looked at me across the table, twelve years old, and she had seen it coming, and she had gone quiet — for me, probably, even then. Even at the end, she gave me her voice's half of the family so I could keep mine.

The candle had burned down to a stub. Somewhere above me, the guild was waking; I could hear the first apprentices moving through the upper rooms, and in an hour Vess would return from the conclave and look at my face and know everything, because Vess always knew everything, and I was suddenly no longer certain whether that was protection or surveillance.

I copied the entry. All of it, including the redactions, including the guild's countersignature — a mark I recognized, a stylized knot that appeared on the seal of every ledger I had ever kept. Then I closed the volume and put it back precisely where it had stood, spine aligned to the half-inch, because whoever I became next, I was still, for a few hours more, a man whose craft was leaving no trace.

Except the world had changed while I knelt there among the black shelves. When I pressed my hand to the bruised plaster to leave, the wall hesitated. Not me — the wall. As if the room had weighed what I carried out of it and found it heavier than what I'd brought in.

In the stairwell, the cold hit me, and with it a thought that stopped my foot on the second landing:

The buyer's name. *The Court Beneath the Mountain.* I had heard it before — not whispered, not rumored, but spoken. Spoken patiently, in a man's voice, at a well, by something wearing a wolf. *Your sister's soul feeds a candle in a court below the mountain,* it had said. It had known. Of course it had known. It had sent me digging for the shovel.

The wolf had offered me truth at a terrible price, and I had spent the night trying to find the truth for free instead, because that was my nature: taste everything, commit to nothing. But there are poisons you cannot take in sips. This was one. The truth had entered me whole and it was already working.

I climbed the rest of the stairs with Entry 447 folded against my chest, and I made my decision the way I made all my decisions now — quickly, silently, and with a taste of iron I could not spit out.

At dawn, I would go back to the well, and this time I would listen to the wolf.
