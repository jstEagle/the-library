## 6. The Price of Truth

I have worn this shape for four hundred years, and in all that time I have learned one thing about men: they do not fear the wolf at the door. They fear the wolf who knows their name.

Paloma Almeida came back to the well at moonset, three nights after I first spoke to him, exactly as I knew he would. He came the way his kind always comes to me — sideways, along the hedge line, with his shoulders folded in as if he could make himself smaller by an act of will. A taster's walk. A servant's walk. The walk of a man who has spent his whole life practicing the art of not being seen.

I let him find me. That is a courtesy I extend to no one else.

He stopped at the edge of the well's stone lip, close enough to see my eyes in the dark and far enough to pretend he hadn't come looking. His hands were empty. Of course they were empty — a man like him carries nothing that can be taken from him. But his pulse was a drumbeat I could taste on the air, quick and salted, and underneath it something older than fear.

Hope. Hope smells like green wood before it burns.

"You came back," I said.

"I came for water." He held up a bucket as if to prove it, a small tin thing that caught the moonlight.

"Your kitchen master sent you to the well at moonset? When the night soil men are still abroad and the court's own dogs are restless?" I let my mouth open, just slightly. Not a threat. An invitation to notice that I was choosing not to be one. "You lie badly for a professional."

That stung him. I watched it land, watched his jaw tighten. Good. A man who cannot be flattered must be goaded.

"I don't know what you are," he said.

"True. Continue."

"What do you want from me?"

* * *

I will tell you what I wanted, since he asked so plainly and since no one has asked me anything plainly in four centuries. I wanted the mountain opened. I wanted the road below unsealed and a soul-candle lifted from its stand before the wax burned down to nothing. I wanted the jailers of my brothers and sisters made to answer for the ledgers of flesh they keep beneath the world.

And I wanted, I confess — because gods are not better than men, only older and more honest about their appetites — I wanted a pair of hands. Small ones. Invisible ones. Hands that had spent twenty years learning how to move through a court without disturbing the air.

The guild thinks its ledgers make them powerful. The king thinks his poisoners make him safe. Both of them are wrong in the same way: they believe that power is what you take. Power is what you are given, freely, by someone who trusts you. It is the only kind that cannot be confiscated.

So I gave Paloma Almeida a gift on the third night, and I made it look like a burden.

"Sit down," I told him.

"I'd rather stand."

"Stand, then. You'll want the ground under you when I'm finished." I settled my haunches against the well-stone and looked up at him, letting him see the whole length of my patience. "Do you know why your king fears old things, taster? Not because we are strong. Strong things can be killed. He fears us because we remember. Memory is the one poison no immunity protects against."

His eyes flicked — just once, just briefly — toward the satchel under his arm. The ledger satchel. He carried it everywhere now; I had watched the habit take root over the past weeks, the way a man carries a wound he hasn't decided to show a physician.

"You know what's in the deep volumes," he said. Not a question anymore. He had found her entry; I had felt the finding, the way you feel a splinter leave your own hand when someone else pulls it free. "You know who sold her."

"I know everyone who sold her. There were four hands on that bargain, taster. Your father's, which signed. Vess's, which witnessed and brokered. The subterranean court's, which bought. And one more."

The wind moved through the hedges. Paloma did not move at all, which is how I knew I had finally reached the place where he lived.

"The fourth hand," he said carefully, "was the buyer's clerk."

"The fourth hand took payment." I rose, and I saw his whole body recalibrate around my height, my shadow, the size of the thing wearing a wolf's shape and speaking with a man's grammar. "Your sister's soul was not purchased with coin. Coin is for horses. Souls are purchased with protection. With advancement. With a blind eye turned at the right moment. Someone in your bright court above grew fat on Yara Almeida's name, and grew comfortable, and grew careless, and every year since has dined on the interest."

"Tell me who."

"Ah." I sat back down. Deliberately. Making him feel the distance between kneeling and standing, between asking and receiving. "There it is. The question. Do you hear yourself, taster? *Tell me who.* Three days ago you would have bitten off your own tongue before asking a stranger for anything. Three days ago you were a man who filed poison reports in silence and called it survival."

"Tell me who," he said again, and his voice did not shake, and I thought: *there it is. There is the thing they buried under all that careful nothing.*

* * *

Here is the truth about bargains, which no priest will tell you and no ledger records: the price is never the point. The price is the door. What matters is what a man becomes while walking through it.

Lord Cassel. I will say the name here, in the dark, where no one but the well and the moon and a bound god can hear it. Lord Aldric Cassel, keeper of the privy purse, master of the king's household, the man who found a half-starved ledger boy with an unusual tongue and set him to work tasting poison because it amused him to own something rare. The man who taught Paloma Almeida that invisibility was safety. Who fed him, clothed him, shielded him from the guild's harsher masters, and never once mentioned — not once in twenty years — that the boy's own sister had been the coin that purchased such generous patronage.

Cassel did not sign the sale himself. He is too clever for signatures. But it was his purse that paid the debt, his steward who carried the message below, his protection that the guild accepted in exchange for a girl's soul and a brother's blindness. And every year since, on midwinter night, Cassel lights a candle in his private chapel that does not burn wax. It burns her.

I have watched him do it. I have watched him bow his head over it, pious as any churchman, grateful as a man saying grace over a meal. He believes himself a good patron. He believes the girl was already lost, that the debt was real, that he merely paid it in the only currency that would serve. Men always believe this. It is the special genius of the corrupt: they can commit any sin provided they narrate it as necessity.

And now you understand my bargain, or the shape of it. But understanding the shape is not the same as feeling the teeth.

"Lord Cassel," I said aloud.

Paloma went white. Not the theatrical white of shock — the true white, the drain-everything-from-the-face white of a body deciding all at once that it would rather be somewhere else. His hand found the well's stone lip and gripped it.

"No," he said.

"He lights a candle for her every midwinter. In his private chapel. East wall, third alcove, behind the screen with the peacock inlay. You have walked past it a hundred times."

"*No.*"

"He calls it devotion. The court calls it piety. The guild calls it a maintenance fee. Only the girl knows what it is, and she is not permitted to know anything anymore."

"Stop." His voice cracked on it. "You're lying. You're — Cassel pulled me out of the scullery. Cassel kept Vess from selling *me* when my tongue came in. He—" Paloma pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes, and for a moment, just a moment, he was not a court creature at all. He was a boy in a kitchen doorway watching his sister be explained away. "Why would you tell me this?"

"Because it's true. Because truth is the only currency I hold, bound as I am." I came closer, slow, giving him every chance to flee. He didn't. I had known he wouldn't. "And because I am offering you a trade, taster, and I want you to understand precisely what you would be buying."

"Which is?"

"A road. The old road under the mountain, sealed these four hundred years by the jailers' court. I can open it. I cannot walk it — my binding ends at the threshold — but I can unseal it, and I can teach you to read the light down there, and I can put a word in your mouth that will get you past the first gate alive. In exchange—"

"In exchange I destroy Cassel."

The words came out flat and hard, and I confess they pleased me more than they should have. Old gods are permitted small cruelties of enjoyment. It is one of the few entertainments left to us.

"Not destroy," I said. "*Name.* You will carry a truth into the court above and speak it where it must be spoken, at the moment it must be spoken, and the truth will do the rest. Truths are like poisons, taster. You of all people should appreciate this. The dose makes the venom. The timing makes the kill. I am not asking you to stab your patron. I am asking you to administer a fact."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Yara's candle burns down. Four years of wax remain, perhaps five. When it is gone, there is nothing left to steal back. No rescue. No reckoning. She will not even qualify as dead, because she was never permitted to fully live." I let the silence sit between us, heavy as snowpack. "Refuse, and go back to tasting the king's supper and filing your silent reports and being invisible until invisibility is all that remains of you. That is also a life. Many have chosen it. Most stop dreaming within a decade."

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. The well dripped. Somewhere across the court grounds a dog began to sing to the moon and thought better of it halfway through.

I watched him the way I have watched men decide things for four hundred years, and I will tell you what I saw, because it matters. I saw temptation arrive dressed as duty. I saw him reach for it. I saw him tell himself, in the space of six heartbeats, that destroying Cassel was justice and not vengeance, that speaking truth was principle and not betrayal, that he would be doing the court a service rather than serving something older and hungrier than the court could imagine.

This is how corruption works. Not as a fall. As a persuasion.

"Do you swear," he said finally, "that what you've told me about Cassel is true? All of it?"

"I am bound in wolf-shape by powers older than your guild's founding charter. Within this shape, I cannot speak a false word. It is the single mercy of my condition." I bared my teeth, which is a wolf's smile, which is not the same as yours. "Everything I have told you tonight is true. What I have *not* told you — that is another matter, and you will not think to ask until it is too late to matter."

"That's not reassuring."

"It is not meant to be. It is meant to be honest. You will find, if you live long enough, that honesty is far more frightening than reassurance." I stood again, and this time I did not sit back down. "Decide, taster. The road opens at the dark of the moon. Six nights. If you are not at the mountain's root by then, the seal renews for another century, and your sister's wax burns alone in the dark without anyone to grieve her properly."

"If I do this," Paloma said slowly, "if I carry your truth into the court — Cassel falls. Everything he's built. Everyone he's sheltered. Half the quiet arrangements that keep that mad king fed and calm. It won't just be him. It'll be an earthquake."

"Yes."

"And you don't care."

"I care a great deal," I said, "about very different things than you do. This is what it means to deal with a god, taster. We are not men with longer lifespans. We do not weigh lives the way you weigh them. We weigh *debts*. And the debt against your court is four hundred years in the accumulating, and it is overdue, and I have been waiting a very long time for someone with your particular talents to come and stand at my well."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he looks at the king's supper, cataloging, testing, searching for the arsenic in the stew. And I let him look. I hid nothing, because there is a particular kind of seduction in being seen clearly by something dangerous, and I needed him to feel it. I needed him to understand that power offered freely is more intoxicating than power seized, because it leaves no wound on the pride. Only a mark on the soul.

"All right," he said quietly.

"Say it plainly."

"All right. I'll carry your truth. I'll open your road. Just — give me the word. Give me the moment. Tell me how to do it so that Yara comes first. Before justice. Before the reckoning. Before whatever you actually want, wolf, because I know there's something underneath all this that you haven't said." His chin came up, and there it was again, that buried spine straightening for the first time in twenty years. "Yara comes first."

"Yara comes first," I agreed.

It was even true. It simply wasn't the whole truth, and I had already told him what I thought of the difference.

He picked up his ridiculous tin bucket, drew his water with steady hands, and walked back along the hedge line with his shoulders a little less folded than before. I watched him go. I marked him, the way you mark a dose, the way you mark a debt.

Six nights. And in the court above, in a chapel with peacock screens, a candle that was not a candle guttered softly on its stand, and a man knelt before it believing himself its benefactor.

Let him kneel. Let them all kneel, soon enough.

I turned my face toward the mountain, and I began, patiently, to remember how to open doors.
