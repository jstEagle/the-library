## 7. Poison at the Feast

There were two hundred and eleven dishes at the king's midwinter feast, and I tasted every one of them.

Not personally — no tongue could hold that many poisons before sundown, not even mine. I had six tasters under me that night, each sworn to silence, each carrying a silver spoon and a small vial of antidote they'd never be quick enough to use. They worked the outer tables. The high table was mine alone, as it had been for nine years.

I stood behind the king's right shoulder in my grey livery, grey as the wall, grey as the shadow a candle doesn't bother to cast. That is the whole art of my trade: to be furniture that eats. A taster who is noticed is a taster who is poisoned next, or bought, or buried under the flagstones of some cellar with his throat opened and his pockets turned out. Nine years I had been nobody. It had kept me alive through three succession crises, two wars, and one very memorable night when the Duke of Marrow decided the king's wine merchant knew too much about the duke's debts.

The stew arrived at the ninth course. Venison, dark beer, dried plums, bread thickeners, marrow bones. I took my spoonful and let it sit on my tongue the way you'd let a stranger sit in your house while you counted the knives.

Salt. Smoke. Plum. And underneath, faint as a whisper through a wall —

Arsenic. Not fresh arsenic, which bites like a green apple and announces itself within the hour. This was old arsenic, aged in wine lees until it lost its voice, dosed small enough that the king would sicken slowly over weeks, blame his heart, and die in bed surrounded by grieving courtiers who would each have an alibi stitched from gold.

I swallowed it. Of course I swallowed it. My body was a ledger of everything I'd ever taken, and this entry was small. I set the spoon down, touched the rim of the king's bowl — our signal for *delayed*, for *not tonight* — and a page whisked the dish away on the pretense that the plums had burned.

And that should have been all. That should have been the whole of my evening: one quiet murder postponed, one more secret folded into the grey coat of a man nobody saw.

But then the guild's envoys came up from their rooms below, and everything changed.

* * *

They were five. Vess had chosen them herself, or so she'd told me when she pressed the invitation into my hands three days before — an actual paper invitation, sealed with the guild's wax, which was against a dozen rules. Guild envoys did not attend royal feasts. Guild envoys existed in the space between what the crown admitted and what it paid for. But the midwinter treaty negotiations had brought the mountain lords down from the north, and the mountain lords had brought with them certain debts, and Vess said the masters wanted eyes in the hall.

"Stand where you always stand," she'd said. "Taste what you always taste. And Paloma — if anything feels wrong, you come find me. You don't act. You *find me*."

She had gripped my wrist harder than she needed to. Vess was seventy years old and her grip could still crack walnuts, but this hadn't been strength. It had been fear.

Now I understood why.

The envoys sat at the fourth table, the one reserved for honored merchants — a fiction everyone enjoyed pretending was true. Their leader was a narrow woman called Mistress Sable, though her real name was in the ledgers under something older and less flattering. She ate carefully, the way guild people eat, testing everything twice, because guild people know better than anyone how food can be made to lie.

The dish that went out to them was quail, roasted, glazed with honey and bitter orange. It came from the same tray as the king's. Same kitchen, same birds, same glaze.

Same glaze — but not the same spoon.

I watched the under-cook carry it out. He was new; I'd marked him weeks ago because he was too calm for a kitchen boy, and because he held his hands the way a fencer holds them. He set the quail before Mistress Sable, bowed exactly the correct depth, and walked back toward the kitchens without once looking at the table he'd just served.

That was the mistake. Every servant looks back at a noble table. It's instinct. It's fear. A boy who doesn't look has already decided what he'll see.

I moved. I don't remember deciding to move. My feet carried me along the wall, past the musicians, past the salt cellars big as cradles, and I took up a position behind the fourth table where the light didn't reach, and when a serving girl came by with the wine I lifted the cup meant for Mistress Sable as if I were refreshing it, and I tasted.

Honey. Orange. And hemlock, cut with something I didn't know — something that made the hemlock patient, made it wait, made it look like apoplexy instead of murder.

Three kinds of poison in one hall. One for a king, slow and deniable. One for the guild's envoys, fast and disguised. And whatever third thing was moving in the dark beneath both of them, because no one poisons a king and five guild envoys on the same night by accident. Someone wanted the treaty dead, or wanted the guild blamed, or wanted the whole hall to drown in suspicion until the mountain lords packed up their debts and went home.

Mistress Sable reached for her cup.

* * *

Here is what nine years of invisibility teaches you about the moment you stop being invisible: it does not feel like stepping into light. It feels like falling.

Every eye in a great hall is a weight. Two hundred nobles, forty guards, a king whose attention is the most dangerous substance in any room — heavier than lead, more corrosive than anything on my tongue. I had spent my whole adult life ensuring that weight never found me. I had built myself out of grey cloth and lowered eyes and a walk designed to occupy no space at all. When people described me afterward — the few times anyone bothered — they said things like *the taster? Thin fellow. Quiet. Might have been there, couldn't say.*

If I spoke now, I would never be that man again. There is no returning to invisibility. A name spoken in a hall full of enemies is a coin spent forever.

And if I stayed silent, five people would die at a table ten feet away from me, and the guild would bleed for it, and Vess — Vess who had taught me to count the knives — would learn that her eyes in the hall had watched her colleagues die and said nothing.

I thought of the ledger. I thought of the entry I wasn't supposed to have read, the soul sold under a name I almost recognized, and the way the ink had shifted on its own two nights ago, like something turning over in its sleep. I thought of the wolf at the well, its patient man's eyes, its voice like stones rolling downhill: *You will have to become visible before you can become true.*

I had told myself it was a dream. Standing there with hemlock on my tongue, I stopped telling myself that.

Mistress Sable lifted the cup.

"No."

My voice. Out loud. In the hall. It came out rusty, a door unopened for years, and it was nowhere near loud enough — but the girl beside me flinched, and the flinch rippled, and the ripple reached the high table, and the king's chancellor turned his head like a weathervane finding wind.

"No," I said again, louder, and stepped out of the wall's shadow into the light, into the weight, into the terrible open country of being seen. "Do not drink that."

The hall did not go silent all at once. Silence in a room that size moves like a tide, table by table, conversation dying mid-word, laughter choking off, until the only sound left was the fire and the far-off clink of the kitchens that didn't yet know the world had stopped. Two hundred faces turned to me. I felt them the way I imagined the mountain lords felt weather.

A guard started forward. The king raised one finger, and the guard stopped as if he'd struck glass.

"The taster," the king said. His voice was mild. Mildness from that mouth was worse than shouting. "Paloma, isn't it. You've stood behind my shoulder for what — nine years? Ten? And in all that time I don't believe you've ever spoken a word I could hear." He smiled without warmth. "So this must be worth hearing."

"It is, Majesty." My mouth was dry in a way no antidote fixed. "There is hemlock in the wine at this table. Cut with valerian root to slow it — it would present as apoplexy. The same hand prepared the king's venison stew with aged arsenic, dosed for a death over several weeks." I heard my own voice steadying as it went on, the craftsman's cadence taking over, cataloguing, precise. "Two poisons, two targets, one kitchen. Whoever did this wanted the treaty drowned in blood and blame. I cannot say whose hand held the spoon. I can say the boy who served this table did not look back at it, and no frightened servant fails to look back."

The silence after that had texture. It had edges. Somewhere in it, very softly, a chair scraped — the under-cook's escort of guards had already found him, or someone had gone to find him, and either way the machinery of consequence was turning.

Then Mistress Sable did the worst thing possible. She looked at me.

Not the blank stare of a stranger. The look of a guild mistress reading a page she thought had been torn out, and finding it intact, and wondering who had hidden it and why. Her eyes went from my face to my hands — taster's hands, scarred faintly at the knuckle where we keep our testing needle — and something in them closed like a ledger snapping shut.

"You have a good tongue," she said pleasantly, to the hall, to the king. "The guild thanks the crown for its vigilance." And under the pleasantness, pitched only for me, only for a taster's ears across four feet of marble floor: *"Vess's boy. Oh, you foolish, visible creature. Do you know what you've just cost us?"*

I did not answer. I bowed — to her, to the king, to the whole unbearable weight of being perceived — and retreated one step, and another, back toward the wall that would never again take me in.

The king was saying something about investigations. The chancellor was saying something about the mountain lords' comfort. The under-cook was being marched through the far doors, and whether he was guilty or a purchased scapegoat, he wore the face of a man who understood that the gray machinery of the court had selected him, and that selection was a sentence regardless of verdict.

And through all of it, at the edge of the dais, standing among the courtiers like a grey wolf among fat sheep, was Lord Cassel.

My protector. The man who had placed me in the taster's chair nine years ago, whose seal had put grey livery on my back, whose patronage was the roof over every quiet year I'd ever had. He was watching me with an expression I had never seen on him before, and it took me a long moment to name it, because I had never had occasion to see a man look at a shield he'd trusted and realize the shield had grown eyes.

He raised two fingers, barely, off the armrest of his chair. The smallest gesture in the world. An order older than language: *after. We will speak after.*

Behind him, beyond the high windows, beyond the frozen gardens and the frozen city, the mountain stood black against the stars. I could not have said what made me look at it. But as my eyes found that dark silhouette, the taste in my mouth changed — the hemlock fading, the arsenic fading, and underneath them something else entirely, cold and wild and patient, like snowmelt finding a crack in stone.

Something beneath the mountain had watched me speak. Something beneath the mountain was pleased.

Lord Cassel beckoned, and I went to him, and the hall's eyes followed me the whole way like knives I could never again stop counting.
