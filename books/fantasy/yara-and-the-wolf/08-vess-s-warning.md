## 8. Vess's Warning

Vess had buried three ledgers in her life, and she remembered where every one of them lay.

The first went into the ground behind her mother's house in the fen country, wrapped in oilcloth, when she was nineteen and the guild's collectors came through burning what couldn't be carried. The second went into a river. The third — the third she had never buried at all, because it wasn't paper, and you could not drown what lived in a person's chest.

She was thinking about buried things the night Paloma came to her door with Yara's page in his fist.

She knew before he knocked. She always knew. Thirty years of keeping the Guild of Impossible Things' accounts had given her an ear for footsteps that carried weight in them, and his had been getting heavier for six days — since the wolf at the well, since the ink began to move on its own, since whatever else he hadn't told her and thought he'd hidden. He hid things beautifully. That was the tragedy of him. He'd been trained to hide things so well that he believed he succeeded.

"Vess." His voice through the door. "Open. Please."

*Please.* In eleven years he had never once said please to her. She set down her pen, capped the ink, and pulled the bolt.

He filled her doorway the way a blade fills a sheath — narrowly, precisely, all edge. His face was the taster's face, the flat gray calm they beat into every apprentice who survives their first slow arsenic, but his hand shook, and in it was a single sheet of guild vellum, old stock, the kind with the watermark of the mountain that they'd stopped using twenty years ago because too many pages of it came back stained.

"I want to know," he said, "who brokered this."

Behind him, the corridor of the counting-house was empty. Night shift. The other keepers would be at supper or asleep or doing whatever it is keepers do when they are not pretending to sleep. Vess stood aside and let him in, and she watched him take in her room — the low lamp, the two cups already set out, the kettle still warm — and she watched him understand that she had been waiting for this hour the way one waits for a wound to finish opening.

"You set out two cups," he said.

"I did."

"That's an answer, isn't it."

"It's an answer," Vess said, "sit down."

* * *

She had been younger than Paloma was now, the year the guild nearly died.

That was the part nobody wrote down, the part that lived only in the oldest keepers' mouths and in the margins of ledgers kept in cipher: that the Guild of Impossible Things had come within a season of becoming one more impossible thing itself, vanished, unmourned, its debts called in by creditors who did not accept apologies. There had been a war then — there is always a war — and wars are bad seasons for a trade in souls and favors and forgotten names, because war floods the market with both supply and demand until the price of everything human drops to nothing and the whole ledger rots from the inside.

The court above had stopped paying its debts. That was the beginning. Not malice — arithmetic. The crown owed the guild for nine borrowed years of a general's luck, three favors of succession, and the forgetting of a name that had needed very much forgetting, and the crown's new treasurer had looked at the accounts and decided that the guild's contracts were unenforceable, because who would dare collect? Who would stand in the marble gallery of the palace and say aloud, *the king owes a debt to the trade of impossible things*? Collection required visibility, and visibility was death, and so the court simply — didn't pay. And a guild that cannot collect is not a guild. It is a list of victims.

The masters had met through that entire winter behind doors Vess was not yet important enough to enter, and had come out each time grayer. Keepers resigned. Two drowned themselves in the record-vault, which the guild recorded, in its own fashion, as *assets returned*. And in the spring, a delegation had gone down under the mountain — to the Court Beneath, which even the guild dealt with rarely, reluctantly, at arm's length — to ask for a loan.

The Court Beneath did not lend money. It lent time. It lent certainty. And it took payment in the only currency that never rots.

"The guild survived," Vess told him. Her voice stayed level. She had kept ledgers through poison trials and purges; her voice could stay level through anything, and she hated it for that. "The loan was honored, the debts above were collected by means I will not describe to you over tea, and the guild stands today because of what we sold that spring. Four entries. Four. Out of forty years of entries, four, and every master signed, and every master swore, and I—"

She stopped. Paloma sat across from her with the page flat on his knee, and he had not touched his tea, and he was watching her with those eyes that cataloged everything and confessed nothing, and she realized she was no longer telling him the history. She was circling it. She had been circling it for eleven years.

"You were junior keeper," he said quietly. "The year of the loan. You've told me that much."

"I was junior keeper."

"Junior keepers don't negotiate. Junior keepers carry the satchel and hold the pen."

"No," Vess agreed. "They don't negotiate."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Outside, somewhere across the rooftops, the watch called the second hour, and the sound seemed to pass over the little room like water over a stone.

"The buyer listed here," Paloma said, and turned the page toward her, and his finger — steady now, steadier than hers felt — came down on the notation beneath *Court Beneath the Mountain*: *brokered, V.A.* "That's your mark. That's your hand. You taught me that hand. I mixed my ink to match that hand for three years before they let me near the real ledgers."

"Yes," Vess said.

"Say it plainly."

"I brokered the sale of your sister's soul." She made herself say it at speaking volume, in her own kitchen, to his face, because he deserved plainness if he deserved nothing else. "I drafted the contract. I held the pen. I took the commission the guild paid me, which fed me for a year, and I have taken out that page in my mind every night for twenty-two years and read it aloud to myself in defiance of the first rule of this trade, and it has never once gotten easier, and now you know, and I would give every remaining year in my body to unknow that you know."

Paloma did not move. That was the worst of it. She had braced for fury — she had rehearsed against fury, against grief, against the cold quiet violence of which she knew he was capable — but he simply sat, a man tasting something, working out what was in it. That was how he met the world. Everything arrived in his mouth eventually, all the poisons of other people, and he named them and swallowed them and filed them away without accusation.

"What was the debt," he said at last. "Our father's debt. What had he done."

"He bought back your mother's grave-rights," Vess said. "From the parish that seized them. On credit, from men who break fingers. And when they came to collect, the sum had — grown. Debts do. And he had two children and no trade, and he went looking for someone who buys impossible things, and the guild pointed him down the mountain, and the mountain's court named its price, and—" she spread her hands, a keeper's gesture, *the entry writes itself* — "and there was a boy of five who had just been brought to the palace kitchens because his tongue could taste nightshade in honey, and the court above was paying, quietly, generously, for such tongues. The sale of your sister covered his debt and yours. Yours being the placement fee. They charged him, Paloma. To place you where you'd be safe, they charged him, and the only coin left in the house was her."

"And you let him believe," Paloma said, and now there was something moving under the flat gray, a pressure like water under ice, "that selling her soul to be a candle under a mountain was a kindness. Because it kept me alive."

"He believed it himself by the end. That's not the same as my letting him. Though I let him, yes. I was very good at letting people believe things. It's most of the trade." She poured his tea. Her hand did not shake, and she noted, distantly, that she wished it would. "And I will tell you the rest of it, since you've earned plainness: I argued for your sister specifically. There were other entries on the table that spring. A debtor in the city with three daughters and no use for any of them. A condemned man's ghost, freely offered. I argued for Yara Almeida, age eight, because the court below wanted a young soul, an unbent one, and a satisfied buyer pays interest, and the guild needed interest that year the way a drowning man needs air. I chose her. Not your father. Me. Whatever he carries in whatever hole he's rotting in, I chose her, and I have been the guild's best keeper ever since, because guilt makes magnificent bookkeepers."

Silence. The lamp burned. Paloma lifted the tea at last, and — she saw it clearly, and knew he did not bother to hide it from her — tasted it first, out of habit older than thought, and set it down untasted anyway, because tonight of all nights he would not swallow anything she gave him without knowing what was in it.

"Why tell me the choice was yours," he said. "You could have hidden behind Father forever. You've had twenty-two years to hide."

"Because of what happens next," Vess said. "Because you have a wolf's words in your ear and moving ink in the vault and a page in your hand, and you are going to do something, and I need you to hear me before you do it."

* * *

"Let them lie," she said.

He looked up.

"The dead bargains. Let them lie." She leaned forward, and thirty years of craft went into keeping her voice gentle, because gentleness was the last instrument she had left. "I know how it looks from where you sit. You've spent your life invisible, swallowing danger in silence, and now you've learned the silence was purchased — that your invisibility was bought with your sister's light. Every entry in that ledger of yours is screaming at you. I know. I've heard them scream for twenty-two years; I keep them, Paloma, I know exactly what they sound like. But listen to me as the only person in this building who loves you and has also read the contract."

"The contract says she serves," Paloma said. "It doesn't say she dies."

"A soul-candle serves until the wick gives out. Eight years old, twenty-two years burned. Most give out at forty years. Some sooner, if the court below draws harder, and the court below has been drawing harder lately — you've seen the ink move, don't lie to me, everyone in the vault has seen the ink move. So you have perhaps fifteen years, maybe ten, in which she is still enough herself that what you bring back will be her and not an echo wearing her shape. If you go down there. If you can go down there. Which you cannot."

"The wolf—"

"The wolf." Vess closed her eyes. Opened them. "Do you know what the Court Beneath actually is? Not the story we put in the apprentices' lessons — jailers of the old gods, wardens of the deep peace. The truth. The truth is that the Court Beneath is the lock, and the lock was built by frightened people, and a lock only stays shut while everyone whose business depends on it keeps polishing it. The guild polishes it. We have always polished it. Our whole trade runs through that lock — souls in, time out, favors flowing — and the day the old gods wake, the day that court answers for itself, every contract the guild has ever written becomes ash and every master becomes a thief standing in the dock. Including me. Including, if you make yourself visible, including you."

"So this is about protecting the guild."

"This is about protecting *you*," Vess said, and her voice cracked on it, one hairline fracture after decades of perfect level, and she hated herself for the crack and let it stand, because it was the truest thing she owned. "Yes, the guild, fine, the guild is my life's sin and I'll answer for it. But you — Paloma. You are a taster. Your weapon is a tongue and your armor is being nobody, and you propose to walk into a court of immortal jailers carrying a truth that will burn Lord Cassel, who is the only shield you have ever had, armed with nothing but the wolf's promises. Old gods are not kind. Free ones least of all. It needs you the way I needed your father — it needs a small mortal hand to carry the pen — and when the entry is written, it will not care what it cost you. I have brokered with powers, my love. I know the shape of their mercy. It is the shape of whatever's left over once they're paid."

"Cassel," Paloma said slowly, "is the man who signs the poisoners' budgets."

"Cassel is the man who kept the poisoners off you for eleven years, whatever else he is, which is more than the wolf has ever done." She reached across the table — slowly, the way you reach toward a bird — and laid her hand on the page in his lap, over the watermark mountain, over her own young signature. "Let the dead bargains lie. Let her rest. Grieve her properly, at last, instead of this — this open vein you've been living with. And when the reckoning comes, and it is coming, I feel it in the ink same as you do, let it fall on the people who made the bargains. On me. I am the entry, Paloma. Punish the entry. Don't become one."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he covered her hand with his — briefly, warmly, terribly — and lifted her hand away from the page as if setting down something fragile.

"You begged my father cowardice too," he said. "Didn't you. That spring. You sat him down in some kitchen like this one and you loved him exactly this much and you told him to let it lie."

Vess had no answer. Twenty-two years of answers, and none.

Paloma rose. He folded Yara's page along its old creases, careful, precise, a man packing the one tool he trusted, and tucked it inside his coat against his heart, where a taster learns to keep the things that can kill him.

"I'm not angry at you," he said at the door, and that was somehow the cruelest sentence he'd spoken all night. "You were junior keeper holding the pen for monsters. I understand monsters better every day. But you taught me the first rule of the trade myself, Vess, my first week in the vault: *an entry is not settled until it is read aloud.*"

"That rule is for debts," she whispered. "Not people."

"Everything in the ledger is people," said Paloma Almeida, and went out into the dark.

Vess sat alone a long while with two cups of untouched tea going cold. Then she unlocked her strongbox, took out the thin black volume she had kept for twenty-two years against every rule she enforced, turned to the page marked with her own name, and read her entry aloud to the empty room — the way she did every night, the penance no one had assigned her.

When she finished, the ink was moving.

Her own name, her own confession, letter by letter, rearranging itself on the page — and beneath it, in a fresh, wet, waiting line, a new heading had appeared where no heading should be:

**COLLECTIONS OUTSTANDING: ONE (1) — BROKER, TO BE CALLED UPON WHEN THE WOLF REACHES THE DOOR.**

Vess snuffed the lamp and sat in the dark, and did not pray, because she knew better than anyone alive exactly which old things answered.
