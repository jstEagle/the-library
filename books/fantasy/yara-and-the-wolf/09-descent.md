## 9. Descent

The wolf did not take him through the gates.

That was the first thing Paloma understood about the road below: it had never been meant for the living of the city above. The wolf led him past the granaries, past the tannery reek, past the last crooked house where the city gave up pretending to be a city, and stopped at a shelf of black rock that looked like nothing at all — a fold in the hillside, a shadow with an edge.

"Here," the wolf said. Its voice was the voice he'd heard at the well: patient, old, worn smooth like a river stone. "You'll want to breathe before you go in."

"I've been breathing all night," Paloma said. His breath came out white, though the morning was not cold enough for it.

"Not down there." The wolf turned its head, and its eyes — a man's eyes in a wolf's face, gray and unhurried and terribly sad — held his. "Down there, you'll be breathing other people's air. Other people's years. Keep your own close. It's the only coin you can spend that they won't count."

Paloma had spent his whole life keeping things close. Poison under his tongue, names behind his teeth, his sister's absence folded flat and small where no one could see it. He knew how to keep. What he did not know was how to stop.

"I'm ready," he said, which was a lie, and the wolf's ears flicked as if it tasted the lie and found it familiar.

"Nobody is," the wolf said. "That's what makes it a sacrifice instead of a stroll."

The rock opened. Not swung, not crumbled — opened, the way a hand opens, deliberately, with intention behind it, and the dark inside was not the dark of a cellar or a cave but something older, something that felt *attended*.

Paloma stepped in, and the mountain closed over him like water.

* * *

For the first hour there was nothing but stone and the sound of their passage — the soft fall of the wolf's paws, the harder fall of his boots, his own breath loud as a bellows in his ears. The road descended in long spirals, cut smooth, cut *deliberately*, and here and there he passed carvings so worn they were only suggestions: figures with raised arms, figures kneeling, figures whose faces had been rubbed away by centuries of hands or weather or shame.

"Who built this?" he asked, because silence had always been his profession and he was tired of practicing it.

"Prayer built it," the wolf said. "Every step of it. They used to come up from below, in the old days. Or the faithful used to come down. It's hard to remember which direction worship traveled when both ends of the road had gods on them."

"And now?"

"Now there are jailers at the bottom who have forgotten they were ever anything else."

The words sat in Paloma's chest like a swallowed coal.

Then the road changed, and he stopped thinking in words at all.

It began as a glimmer far below — a pale thread winding down through the dark like a river seen from a cliff at night. He thought at first it was torchlight, some deep station of the road where travelers kept lamps burning. But as they descended the light resolved, and Paloma's taster's tongue went dry, because the light was not light the way fire is light. It moved the way voices move. It rose in little columns and eddies and bursts, it clustered and thinned and flared, and each pulse of it carried — he could not say how he knew, but he knew, the way he knew arsenic from hemlock by one touch on the tongue — each pulse of it carried a *want*.

"Prayers," the wolf said. "You're seeing what prayer looks like when it has nowhere to go. Up there, you speak into the air and the air forgets you. Down here, the mountain keeps everything. Every word ever said to a god beneath this rock. It doesn't rot. It doesn't fade. It just — waits."

The light was everywhere now. It drifted along the walls in slow rivers. It pooled in the hollows of the worn carvings until the kneeling figures seemed to glow from inside, supplicants lit by their own asking. And when Paloma walked through it — because there was no way not to walk through it; the road ran through the heart of the shining — it touched him.

He heard a woman's voice, cracked and small: *let my son live past winter, please, please, I will give anything—*

A man's voice, steady with desperation: *I have been faithful. I have been faithful. Why won't you look at me—*

A child's voice, so simple it went through him like a blade: *come back, come back, come back—*

They weren't echoes. Echoes repeat. These were *present*. Each prayer was still being prayed, still burning with the wanting of whoever had spoken it, years ago, decades ago, centuries ago, and the mountain had gathered every one and kept them the way Paloma kept poisons in his memory: catalogued, patient, unspent.

"How many?" he whispered. His voice came out wrong, scraped thin.

"All of them," the wolf said quietly. "This is what your court sits on top of. A thousand years of people begging, and above them a handful of men selling the answers."

Paloma stood in the drifting light and let it move through him, and he thought of the ledger. Of course he thought of the ledger. He had spent six years writing in it — traded years, borrowed luck, favors called in across distances that should have made calling impossible. He had told himself it was bookkeeping. He had told himself someone had to keep the accounts clean.

Now he watched a dead woman's plea for her son drift past his shoulder like snow, still warm, still *hoping*, and understood that the ledger was not bookkeeping. It was a harvest record.

"Keep walking," the wolf said gently. "Worse is below."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. Comfort is how they catch you."

* * *

The chains began at the third turning.

At first he took them for stalactites — pale strands hanging from the ceiling of a vast chamber the road skirted, hundreds of them, thousands, catching the prayer-light and throwing it back in broken colors. Then the road curved closer, and Paloma saw that the strands were braided. Each one was woven out of smaller threads, and each smaller thread was made of — he leaned toward one, careful not to touch — each thread was made of words.

Debts. He could read them. That was the horror of it; his guild-trained eye could not *not* read them. One strand, thick as his wrist: *Owen Marsh, three years of his life, sold by Owen Marsh to the Guild of Impossible Things, price: freedom from debt, witness: Vess.* Another, thinner, frayed near the bottom: *a name, forgotten by everyone who loved it, held in trust.* Another, so fine it was nearly invisible, and yet it burned brighter than any of them, and Paloma could not make himself read what was written on it because he already knew, in the marrow of him, whose it was.

"They hang here," the wolf said. "Every bargain the guild ever struck, every promise the court ever broke, every soul-note signed in good faith or bad. The mountain weighs them. Debts have weight, taster. You of all people should know — you carry enough of them."

"I carry *other people's* secrets," Paloma said. "Not my debts."

"No?" The wolf looked back at him, and the prayer-light moved in its gray eyes. "Then why does your stride shorten every time we pass one?"

Paloma didn't answer. But he counted, because counting was what he did, what he was — and by the time the chamber ended he had passed four hundred strands, five hundred, more, and somewhere past seven hundred he had stopped being able to pretend that none of them were his doing. He had entered the sales into the ledger himself. Neat figures. Clean lines. *Buyer, seller, goods, price.* He had written *one soul* in the same ink he wrote *two barrels of luck* and never once let his pen shake.

His pen had shaken now. His hands shook now, walking through a cavern hung with the physical weight of everything he'd helped keep tidy.

One strand hung low across the path, and he had to duck under it, and in ducking his face passed within a finger's width of the braided words, and he heard it — not read it, *heard* it, the way you hear a whisper in a quiet room:

*Yara Almeida. Age eleven. Sold by Aldous Almeida to discharge a debt of thirty crowns to the Guild of Impossible Things. Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Term: perpetual.*

Eleven. She had been eleven. He had been fourteen, already apprenticed to Vess, already learning to hold poison on his tongue without swallowing, and while he practiced his invisibility his father had walked into the guild hall and sold his sister the way a man sells a mule, and Paloma had *entered it in the ledger*, had written *goods: one soul* in his neat apprentice hand, had dusted the page afterward so the ink would dry clean—

"Stop," the wolf said.

Paloma realized he had stopped breathing. He made himself start again, dragging air in through his nose, using the discipline of it — in for four, hold for four, the way Vess had taught him for holding poison — until his hands were only trembling instead of shaking.

"She's at the bottom," he said. Not a question.

"She is."

"And the ones who bought her."

"Are waiting for you. They've known you were coming since the well. Perhaps longer. Time runs strangely down here, and jailers are patient the way wolves are patient." The wolf's mouth curved, which in a wolf's face was not entirely a smile. "Present company excepted."

"What do they want with me?"

"The same thing everyone wants, taster. Something you have that they can't take cleanly. They want the wolf's true name. And they think you love your sister enough to sell it."

Paloma looked down the long spiral of the road, where the prayer-light thickened into a glow like a city seen from above at festival time, and the chains hung thicker there, older, heavier, and somewhere among all that light was a candle with his sister's soul burning on it.

"Then they've mispriced me," he said.

"Mm," the wolf said. "We'll see. Love makes liars of us all eventually. Come. The last stretch is the worst — the prayers get newer."

Newer. Paloma hadn't thought of that. The prayers near the top were centuries old, worn smooth, almost gentle in their antiquity. But as they descended, the light grew sharper, hotter, the wants rawer, less resigned — and then he began to recognize things. Not voices. Wants. *Let the king's feast go well. Let the envoys arrive safely. Let no one notice.* Court prayers. Guild prayers. Prayers he had heard spoken aloud in antechambers, muttered by frightened clerks and ambitious lords, prayers that had gone up and been forgotten by everyone except the mountain, which forgot nothing.

And threaded through them, bright as a dropped needle, small and fierce and repeated ten thousand times over six years, from a kitchen, from a chapel, from a narrow bed in a corner of the servants' wing:

*Yara. Yara Almeida. If anyone is listening — I'm still here. I'm still me. My name is Yara.*

She had prayed. All this time, she had prayed, and the prayers had come down here and piled up around her like snow against a door, and she was still praying, and no one had ever once answered.

Paloma knelt in the middle of the road, in the middle of the shining, and pressed his palm flat to the stone, and for the first time since he was a boy he spoke a prayer of his own.

*I'm coming,* he said, to whatever was listening, to the mountain itself if nothing else. *Hold on. I'm coming, and I'm bringing the bill.*

The wolf waited, silent, its gray eyes full of something that might have been pity and might have been warning, and below them the light of ten thousand unanswered prayers burned on, leading down like a lit fuse.

Far beneath his feet — deeper than the road, deeper than the glow — something vast shifted in its sleep, and the chains overhead all rang together, softly, like a thousand small bells, like a court clearing its throat.

"They know you're here now," the wolf said. "Walk fast, taster. And when we reach the doors — whatever you see, however she looks — do not stare. Down there, looking too long is the same as touching."

Paloma rose. He settled his shoulders the way he settled himself at the king's table, invisible, essential, poison-ready.

"Lead on," he said, and followed the wolf down into the light of everything his world had stolen.
