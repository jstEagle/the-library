## 10. The Court Beneath

The road ended where roads are supposed to end, and the court began.

Paloma had imagined a gate. He had imagined guards, doors, thresholds — some clean line he could cross and know he'd crossed. Instead the passage simply opened, the way a mouth opens, and the air changed against his skin like the air of a house where someone has just died. Warm. Attended. Full of things breathing that were not wind.

He made himself walk through anyway.

*Don't stop. Stopping is what guilty men do.*

The wolf's last instruction still sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: *You will be invisible there, because you have practiced being invisible all your life. But listen to me, taster. There is one thing in that court you must not look at. You'll know it when you see it, because it will be the only light in the whole place worth looking at. Do not look long. The court notices longing the way your king notices poison.*

*How do I find her without looking?*

*That,* the wolf had said, with something like pity in its patient eyes, *is the cruelty of it.*

So Paloma did what he did best. He became furniture.

It was easier than it should have been, down here. The court beneath the mountain was full of servants, and every one of them moved the way he moved — smooth, unremarkable, eyes down, hands quiet. They were not human, most of them, or they had stopped being human so long ago that the difference was academic. Their skin had the grey sheen of candle wax. Some of them had too many joints in their fingers. None of them spoke unless spoken to, and when they spoke, their voices came out flat as ledger lines.

Paloma fell into step among them carrying nothing, which was itself a kind of disguise: a servant between errands is invisible everywhere, above or below. He kept his pace even. He kept his shoulders rounded. He let his face do the thing he'd spent twenty years training it to do, which was nothing at all.

And he looked, without looking.

The court was vast in a way that refused to be measured. Corridors branched and doubled back; ceilings rose into darkness that no torch reached, because there were no torches. The light came from candles — thousands of them, tens of thousands, standing on iron racks along the walls in ranks that vanished around corners and up stairways and into halls whose purpose Paloma did not want to guess. Each flame burned in its own color. Each one flickered with a rhythm that no draft explained.

He walked past a blue-white flame shaped like a teardrop and felt, for half a heartbeat, an old man's cough. He passed a green one that guttered the way a gambler flinches, and heard dice rolling somewhere far away. A red one, steady and stubborn, smelled of bread.

They were souls. All of them. Every candle in the endless racks was somebody, burning.

His stomach, trained to hold arsenic and hemlock without complaint, turned over anyway.

This was the trade. This was what the ledgers recorded in neat columns, what Vess balanced with her careful hand, what the guild called *fulfillment of contract*. Not years. Not favors. This. Somebody's mother, standing on a rack in the dark, giving light to a court that had never known the sun.

*Keep walking,* he told himself. *Grief is visible. Grief gets you noticed.*

He catalogued instead. It was the only tool he had, so he used it the way he always did: coldly, completely. Four great halls. The central one largest, its ceiling lost in black, its floor polished dark stone that reflected the candle-racks so perfectly it looked as if another court burned upside down beneath this one. At the far end of that hall stood a dais, and on the dais stood thrones — nine of them, arranged in a crescent — and only three were occupied. The occupants did not move. He did not let his eyes rest on them longer than a blink. Even from across the hall he could feel them noticing the way a scale notices weight.

The jailers. The wolf had called them that. Gods who had once been gods' keepers, and had learned, over the ages, that keeping was a kind of owning, and owning a kind of eating.

A servant brushed past him, close enough that its waxy fingers grazed his sleeve. It didn't look at him. Nothing looked at him. Down here, among creatures who could taste intention the way he tasted poison, being empty was armor. Twenty years of swallowing danger in silence had made him fluent in silence, and silence, it turned out, was the native language of this place.

He hated how good he was at it.

* * *

He found the third hall by following the arithmetic.

The other halls held thousands of candles each — common souls, sold in lots, their flames small and uniform. But a corridor off the eastern gallery led to a smaller chamber, and the smaller chamber held perhaps forty stands, and each stand held a flame that burned larger, brighter, stranger than the rest. Premium stock. Names that had fetched prices.

He counted the racks the way he'd counted courses at the king's table, eyes sliding, never fixing. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. Thirty-three.

On the thirty-fourth stand, a candle burned gold.

Not gold like wealth. Gold like wheat at harvest, like honey held to the sun, like the exact color of a girl's dress on the last summer day before everything went wrong. The flame moved wrong — not flickering but *dancing*, quick and bright and stubborn, the way Yara had danced in the kitchen when she was seven and their mother still laughed at things.

Every nerve in Paloma's body pulled toward it.

He kept walking. Three steps past. Four. His heart slammed so hard he was sure it echoed off the stone, sure the whole court could hear it, sure that any moment a waxen hand would close on his shoulder and a flat voice would say, *this one looks.*

*She's real,* he thought, and the thought nearly buckled him. *She's real, she's burning, she's right there.*

He had prepared himself for grief. What he hadn't prepared for was tenderness — sudden, physical, unbearable, the way you feel a wound you'd forgotten you had. She was *here*. Nine years of not knowing whether she was dead or worse, nine years of a hollow shape in his chest he'd learned to walk around, and the answer had been: a candle. A golden candle on an iron rack, lighting a thief's hallway.

He turned back along the gallery, as servants do, taking the long way to nowhere, passing her again. One breath of a glance this time. The flame leaned, very slightly, toward him.

Toward *him*.

He felt it like a hook behind his ribs. The flame knew. However much of Yara was left in that light — however dissolved, however dimmed by years of service — something in it recognized the way he walked, the set of his shoulders, the brother-shaped absence beside it.

*Don't,* he begged silently. *Please. Don't lean. Don't reach. If you reach for me they'll see, and then we're both—*

The flame steadied itself. Slowly. Deliberately. As if somewhere inside the gold, his sister had understood, and chosen, out of the same stubbornness that had kept her dancing when the world went bad, to protect him.

Paloma walked on with his face empty and his heart coming apart.

* * *

The problem with invisibility, he thought later — pacing his assigned corner of the eastern gallery, waiting for the change of watch that would thin the court's attention — is that you pay for it in the same coin you earn it. To be unseen you must be nothing. And nothing cannot pick up a candle.

He began, then, the slow work of mapping.

The gallery changed watch at intervals he measured by counting his own pulse — the jailers kept no bells, and time down here moved like syrup. When the waxen servants rotated, there was a span, four hundred heartbeats maybe, when the premium chamber stood with only one attendant, and the attendant dozed. Not slept — these things likely didn't sleep — but its attention sank, the way a guard's does at the dead hour. Four hundred heartbeats. He'd crossed a feast hall under sharper eyes than that.

But the warding flame — that was the problem. Between him and the racks, at the chamber's entrance, burned a fire unlike the others: white, tall, utterly still, giving no heat. He could feel it tasting everything that passed, the way his own tongue tasted the king's stew. It read intentions. He knew craft like that; he'd served men who owned lesser versions of it. You could not lie to a flame like that with your face. You might, possibly, lie to it with your blood.

He put that thought away carefully, the way he'd filed the arsenic in the stew — not solved, but catalogued, held, ready.

For now, he watched. For now, he learned the rhythms of the court: the hour when two of the nine thrones emptied, the procession of servants carrying fresh wax — fresh *wax*, and he made himself not think about what souls burned down to — the strange hush that fell whenever one of the seated jailers turned its head, which was rare, and terrible, and once, memorably, directed at nothing near him at all, thank every forgotten god.

And in the middle of the watching, in the dead center of his careful invisibility, Yara's flame did something new.

It had begun, over the hours, to burn brighter when he was near. Subtly. Only a shade. Anyone else would have missed it. But he had spent his life reading the almost-nothing: the grain of arsenic in wine, the tremor in a steward's hand. He read his sister the way he read a dish, and she was saying, in the only language she had left, *I know you're here.*

He should have moved farther away. That was the discipline. That was the craft.

Instead, on his next circuit, he paused — one heartbeat, no more — beside her stand. And he did the bravest, most foolish thing he had done since he entered the mountain.

He straightened his little finger, where it hung at his side, and tapped it twice against the iron rack. The old signal. Kitchen code, from before. *I'm here. Hold on.*

The flame did not move. The flame was perfectly, beautifully still — because she understood too, because she had always been the quicker of them, because she knew that stillness was the loudest answer.

But as he turned away, Paloma felt something warm run down his face, and realized he was crying, silently, the way he did everything, and that his tears were the first honest thing the court beneath the mountain had seen from him.

He wiped them before the warding flame could taste the salt.

* * *

The jailer noticed him in the ninth hour. Or rather — it noticed the space where feeling had been.

He was back in the main hall, resuming his servant's drift, when the nearest of the seated figures rose. It was tall the way a pillar is tall, grey the way ash is grey, and its face was a suggestion of a face, worn away like a coin rubbed between fingers for centuries. It crossed the floor toward the eastern gallery, and every servant in its path flattened itself against the walls, and Paloma flattened too, heart hammering his old four-count rhythm, eyes down, empty, empty, empty.

The jailer stopped beside him.

He felt it reading him. It wasn't like the warding flame's tasting; it was slower, deeper, a thumbing through of pages. He threw everything he had into blankness — the blankness of the ledger before ink, the blankness of a face at a poisoning, the blankness he'd worn so long it had grown into his bones. *Nothing here. A servant. Furniture. Nobody's brother.*

The jailer's ruined face inclined toward him. From somewhere inside it came a sound like a ledger closing.

"You," it said, in a voice like dust settling, "smell of the upper air."

Paloma bowed. Servants bow. "Many pass through, lord. I carry trays."

"Do you." The grey face hovered closer. "Trays. And before trays?"

"Poison, lord." The truth — the one coin he could spend safely. "Above. I am a taster."

Something moved across the worn features. It might have been amusement. It might have been appetite.

"A taster," the jailer said. "How useful. We have a flame above stairs who has grown careless, and our own cup requires testing, of a sort. You will serve."

It turned away, already losing interest in him the way great powers lose interest in small things, and Paloma bowed again to its back and let his knees nearly give once it had passed.

*Serve.* He had wanted access. He had wanted exactly this — a reason to move freely, a function no one questions. The court had handed it to him because it saw nothing in him worth fearing.

Invisibility as skill. Invisibility as wound. And now, perhaps, invisibility as weapon.

He raised his eyes, just once, down the length of the eastern gallery, to where a golden flame burned among forty others — brighter now, he was almost certain, though he would never swear to it.

*I'm coming for you,* he thought, with everything he could not say aloud. *Hold still. Burn slow.*

Behind him, in the deep of the hall, the warding flame at the chamber door turned its white face very slightly — as if something in the salt of his tears, somewhere in its long memory, had begun to taste familiar.
