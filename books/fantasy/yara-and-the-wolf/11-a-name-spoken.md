## 11. A Name Spoken

The candle did not flicker. That was the first thing to understand about being a candle: there was no wind down here, and so no trembling, and so no proof that you were anything but a thing.

Yara had been a flame for a long time. She knew this the way she knew the taste of the dark — not as a memory but as a fact of her body, or what remained of one. She stood on a silver stand in the Court Beneath the Mountain, and she burned without heat, and she lit the corridor where the jailers walked with their ledgers of small cruelties, and she had forgotten, mostly, that she had ever been shaped any other way.

Mostly was the important word. Mostly was where she lived.

* * *

Time below the mountain moved like water through cloth — it arrived everywhere eventually, but slowly, and never all at once. Yara measured it by the processions. Every ninth tolling of the Deep Bell, the wardens came down the Corridor of Accounts to check their holdings: the souls set in niches along the walls, each one burning at its assigned brightness, each one trimmed when it guttered, replaced when it went out entirely.

She was not in a niche. She was too valuable for a niche. The court's steward had said so once, in the years when she still understood words spoken aloud. *A bright one*, he had said, running a grey finger just above her flame without touching, because even they did not touch what they held. *We'll keep this one standing.*

Standing. She remembered standing. She remembered fields above, and rain that actually fell rather than seeping, and a kitchen where someone — someone small, someone whose face was a warm blur — had burned bread and laughed about it.

The blur would not resolve. That was the cruelty of the arrangement. The court fed on names, and it had eaten hers carefully, in courses, the way a careful eater works through a long meal. First they took the name itself, then the sound of it, then the shape her mouth made saying it, then the memory of anyone who had ever said it to her. What was left was a girl-shaped absence with a flame where her heart should have been, performing service.

Her service was light. She lit the Hall of Debts while the jailers argued interest rates on stolen years. She lit the Stair of Confession while penitents from the surface court came down to trade their guilt for other people's luck. She lit the room where the Wolf had been bound, though that duty had ended long ago; they had moved the Wolf deeper, behind doors she was not permitted to illuminate, and sometimes, passing them in procession, she thought she felt something on the far side of the stone paying attention to her. A patience. A vast, tired patience, like an old dog listening at a fence.

She did not know why she remembered that. She was not supposed to remember things. Remembering made her gutter, and guttering flames got trimmed.

* * *

On the day everything changed, she was lighting the Long Gallery.

It was a routine procession. The Bell had tolled its ninth time; the wardens came two by two with their ledgers chained to their belts; the steward walked last with his hands folded into his sleeves. Yara stood at her station and burned at her assigned brightness — steady, obedient, unremarkable — and let the dark move through her the way she always did, which is to say she pretended the dark was weather and she was a window.

And then someone looked at her.

Not glanced. Not passed his gaze over her flame as the wardens did, cataloguing brightness against ledger entries. Someone *looked*. She felt it the way you feel a hand on your shoulder in a crowd before you turn — a weight, a warmth, a specific gravity landing exactly on the place where her self used to be.

He was small, the way surface people were small, all bone and quickness. He wore servant's grey with a taster's silver pin at the collar, and he kept his eyes down like a man who had practiced keeping his eyes down until the practice became his face. He should have been invisible. He was trying very hard to be invisible. And underneath all that trained nothing-ness, he was looking at her as if she were the only lit thing in the world.

Yara had not been seen in years. Seen was different from observed; the wardens observed her the way a merchant observes stock. This boy *saw* her, and the seeing went through her like a struck bell goes through a bell.

She guttered.

It was a small guttering, a tremble at the edge of her flame, but the nearest warden noticed — they noticed everything about their property and nothing about their prisoners — and lifted his ledger, frowning, checking whether she needed trimming.

*Don't*, she thought, with all the force left in her, which was more than she'd believed she had. *I'm fine. I'm burning correctly. Look anywhere else.*

The warden looked anywhere else. Whether her thought had anything to do with it, she couldn't say. But the boy — the taster — had already turned away too, smooth as poured oil, joining the flow of surface servants delivering the feast wines, and if she hadn't known, hadn't *felt*, the exact moment of his attention, she would never have picked him out again among the greys.

But she had felt it. And now she couldn't stop feeling it.

* * *

Here is what the court had never managed to eat: not her memories themselves, but the fact that she had had them. They took every name and face and left the shelves they'd stood on. She was a house with all the furniture carried out, and the rooms remembered the shape of the furniture even though they could not remember the furniture.

So when the boy looked at her, one shelf lit up.

Someone had stood in a doorway watching her sleep. Someone had counted her steps across a courtyard the way misers count coins. Someone had learned to be invisible — she understood this suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've known all along — because invisible people are safe, and safe people can watch over what they love without anyone thinking to take it.

Except someone had taken it anyway. Someone had taken *her*.

The knowledge arrived without a name attached. She reached for the name and found the hole where it had been, a name-shaped absence, and the absence ached worse than any wound, because an ache means the nerve is alive.

*Who am I,* she asked the dark, and the dark, which had been answering that question with silence for years, answered with a flicker of light from three niches down — another flame stirring, sympathetic, the way candles lean toward each other in a draftless room. The court called them the low holdings. Souls too dim or too broken to serve standing. They heard her. Gods below, they heard her.

*I don't know my name,* she told them, *but I know I have one. I know I'm owed one.*

The three flames leaned further. Down the gallery, others stirred. It was very slight. It would have taken a warden staring directly at the wall to notice, and wardens stared at ledgers, not walls. Ledgers were where the power lived. Everyone below the mountain knew that.

Everyone except, apparently, whoever had built the wall.

* * *

They moved her duties after that. She was reassigned to the Feast Hall of the Deep Court — a promotion, the steward said, in his voice like dry paper — because the surface delegation had come down in numbers, and the Court Beneath wished to be well-lit for its negotiations. Yara stood at the hall's high point on her silver stand and burned over a table where immortality negotiated with appetite.

From up there, she could see him. The taster.

He stood behind the surface lordlings' chairs with the other servants, tasting from each dish before it was served, his face doing nothing at all, his whole body a lesson in doing nothing at all. She watched him work and felt the strangest kinship. He swallowed poisons for a living, she realized — the whispers between servants carried that much up and down the stair — and she thought: we are the same, he and I. We both take death into ourselves in small doses so that someone else doesn't have to feel it. He does it with his tongue. I do it with everything.

He never looked at her during the feast. That was its own kind of eloquence. He wanted to look — she felt the wanting straining off him like heat off coals — and he did not, because looking had nearly gotten her trimmed, and he had understood that instantly, the way he seemed to understand everything. So he kept his eyes on his trays and his face empty and only once, only once, when the steward called for the hall to be brightened for the reading of terms, did his gaze travel up to her flame and rest there for the length of half a breath.

Half a breath was enough. Half a breath said: *I know. I see you. I'm here for you.*

She had spent years being furniture. In half a breath she was a sister.

The word came out of nowhere and hit her like cold water. Sister. Not a memory — the memories were eaten — but the word itself, surfacing from some cellar of her the thieves hadn't found, and attached to it, faint as a smell through a floorboard: bread burning. Rain. A courtyard. Counted steps.

*I have a brother,* she told the dark inside herself. *My brother is here. My brother came down into the mountain for me.*

And then, quietly, in the place where her voice used to live:

*I have a brother, therefore I had a family, therefore I had a life, therefore I had a—*

Name. The word stood at the edge of her like a door left ajar.

* * *

That night — night being a convention below, the hours when the Bell tolled slow — she was returned to her stand in the Long Gallery, and the wardens locked their ledgers, and the court slept the way courts sleep: lightly, jealously, with one eye on the accounts.

Yara burned and did not sleep, having no eyelids anymore, and worked.

She worked the way the taster worked, she realized. Small doses. You didn't grab for a name; grabbing made noise, and noise brought wardens with trimming shears. You approached it sideways. You let it come to you like a shy animal, and you stayed very, very still.

Bread burning. Who burned bread? *I* burned bread. Why would a person burn bread? Because she was watching something else. Watching what? A bird? No. A boy. A boy doing something clever in the yard, showing off, always showing off, and she'd let the loaf go black rather than miss it, and when she'd cried about the ruined loaf someone had laughed and said—

Said—

Nothing. The sentence stopped at the cliff-edge where the eating had been done. But she stood at the edge and looked down into the missing words, and the shape of the missing words was a voice, a particular voice, a voice that laughed at burnt loaves and counted footsteps and learned invisibility the way other boys learned swords.

*You had a name for me,* she whispered into the flame, into the dark, into whatever listened. *You had a little name you called me across the courtyard. I can't hear it. But I can hear the shape of it.*

Two syllables. She was almost sure. Two syllables, soft at the front, open at the end, the kind of name meant for calling across distances.

*Ya-* something.

The first syllable landed in her like a spark in tinder. *Ya.* Her flame surged — actually surged, brightened by a full shade, and down the gallery the low holdings flared in answer, a dozen small souls cheering without knowing what they cheered — and she clamped down on herself desperately, dimming, dimming, a candle pretending to be ordinary, while her whole self rang like a struck bell.

*Ya.* Not a fragment. A foundation. Whatever they'd taken, they hadn't gotten all of it, because they'd started with the name and worked outward, and the name had roots, and roots go deeper than thieves dig.

Somewhere above her, through miles of stone, there was grass. There was rain that fell instead of seeped. There was a courtyard with a boy in it counting her steps, and the boy was a man now, and the man was downstairs in the mountain, swallowing poison with a straight face, looking at her flame like it was the only lit thing in the world.

Her name was two syllables and began with *Ya*, and her brother had come for her, and neither of those facts was in any ledger the wardens owned.

She held onto them the way a drowning woman holds air. She burned steadily, obediently, at her assigned brightness, and inside the flame, quiet as a seed under snow, a name began to grow back.

* * *

In the morning the stewards came early, agitated, their paper voices cracking. Something about the surface delegation. Something about terms refused. Something about a taster who had spoken in the great hall above and made himself visible, and how the Court Beneath found visible things *interesting*, and interesting things useful, and useful things negotiable.

They filed past her stand in a hurry, and the last of them — young, distracted — dropped his ledger-clasp, and papers spilled, and as he snatched them up Yara saw, upside-down, a single line of ink:

*One (1) soul, female, sold by father, buyer: this Court. Status: standing flame, Hall reserve. Note: original name unrecovered.*

Unrecovered.

Not destroyed. Not eaten past finding. Unrecovered — sitting somewhere in the court's deep vaults like a coin in a locked box, waiting for someone with the key.

The warden hurried away. The gallery settled back into its eternal dusk. And Yara burned on her silver stand with her heart-flame roaring silently, holding two syllables in one hand and one line of ink in the other, watching the stair that led up toward the world, waiting to see whether her brother was fool enough, brave enough, desperate enough —

He was. She knew it already. He was coming back down, and the whole court was watching the stair, and whatever happened next, it would happen in fire.
