## 12. The Bargain Refused

The candle that was his sister burned at the center of the hall, and the court beneath the mountain wanted to talk business.

That was the thing about courts — above or below, they were all the same animal. Silk over teeth. Courtesy laid down like a tablecloth so no one could see the stains underneath. The jailers of the old gods had waited centuries in their vaulted dark, and when a living man finally walked their road carrying a stolen truth in his mouth like a hot coal, what did they do?

They offered him wine.

The cup was obsidian, thin as an eggshell, and whatever it held moved with its own slow weather. A servant-thing poured it — I never learned if it had been born to the shape or pressed into it — and set the cup before me without meeting my eyes. Even here, I recognized the gesture. It was my own craft, inverted. Above, they tested the king's food on a man trained to be nobody. Here, they offered poison openly, because down here everyone was somebody, and everybody's price was known.

I did not drink.

"You don't trust us." The voice came from the throne — if you could call three slabs of black stone arranged like a jaw a throne. The one who sat upon them had been beautiful once, in the way old coins are beautiful: worn features, worn authority, something precious rubbed nearly away. "Wise. But then, you don't trust anyone, do you, taster? That is your whole profession. Your whole *life*."

"I trust arithmetic," I said. "You want something from me. That makes this a negotiation, not hospitality."

A ripple went through the assembled court — amusement, or the memory of it. There were perhaps forty of them visible, and more in the shadows beyond the light of the soul-candles, ranks upon ranks of them, the way a cellar holds more bottles than a table ever shows. They wore shapes borrowed from the world above: lords' faces, ladies' faces, some wearing the forms of animals the way a man wears a coat he hasn't grown into yet.

"Arithmetic," the seated one repeated, tasting the word. "Then let us do sums."

It rose. When it moved, the candlelight bent toward it, hungry, and I made myself look at anything else — at Yara most of all, because not looking would have been worse.

She stood on her stand at the hall's heart, where she had stood for eleven years. She was not flesh. She was flame — a tall, steady burn the color of late afternoon through honey, and she gave her light to everything around her without ever being diminished, which is either the nature of souls or the cruelest joke ever played on them. The court's lamps fed on her. Their whole dark city read by her. And she did not scream, because whatever remained of my sister inside that brightness had been sanded down until screaming wasn't in her anymore.

But her light had flickered. Twice since I entered the hall. Twice, when my eyes lingered.

*I must not look too long.* The wolf's warning, given at the well, given again at the gate. *They will feel attention the way skin feels a fingertip.*

So I looked, and looked away, and kept my face doing nothing at all, which is the finest work I know.

"The sum," the jailer said, descending from its jaw-throne, unhurried, its footsteps making no sound but leaving small frost-flowers on the stone that bloomed and died behind it. "Eleven years ago, a desperate man sold a girl's soul to settle a debt he could not pay. The Guild of Impossible Things brokered the sale. The Court Beneath received the goods. All correct. All lawful, by the laws we keep down here, which are older and less forgiving than yours." It stopped ten feet from me, close enough that I could see there was no moisture in its eyes at all. "And now a brother comes down a dead road to buy her back. Tell me the sum you expected to pay."

"I didn't come to buy," I said. "I came to take."

More ripple. Someone laughed — a high, glassy sound.

"Take," the jailer echoed. "With what army? With what power? You are a kitchen instrument. A tongue on legs. You have walked into the one place in creation where being invisible buys you nothing, because down here, little taster, *everyone sees everything*. We watched you cross the road. We watched you flinch at the debts hanging in the air. We watched you look at the candle—" and here its voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse than cruelty, "—and we watched her flicker. Do you know what happens to a soul-candle that gutters? It goes out. It does not die. Dying implies it was alive to begin with. It simply ceases, the way a word ceases when the last mouth saying it closes."

Every swallowed poison I owned rose up my throat at once, and I swallowed them back down, the way I'd practiced ten thousand times. Arsenic in the stew. Nightshade in the wine. The slow lead of grief. I let them settle into their shelves.

"Then say your price," I said. "Before I lose interest."

"*Interest.*" It smiled. The smile had too many years in it. "Very well. Here is our ledger, taster, balanced against yours."

It gestured, and the air between us filled with writing. Not ink — light, drawn out of Yara's own burning, which meant every word cost her something, and the court knew it, and did it anyway. Words hung in the dark:

*A soul for a name.*

"We will release her," the jailer said. "Fully. Not a guttering wick sent up the mountain to learn how to be human again — we have seen how that ends; the ones you people ransom always arrive home as strangers wearing their own faces. No. We will restore her. Whole. Every year of herself that burned in our service, returned. She will walk out of this mountain remembering her mother's voice, her own name, the taste of the bread she ate the morning before her father signed her away. She will be *Yara*, entire."

My heart did something stupid and enormous in my chest. Eleven years of wanting, handed to me across a frost-bloomed floor like a dish I was meant to taste.

"And in exchange," I said, because my craft demanded I hear the whole poison before I named it, "you want—"

"The wolf's true name."

There it was. I had known it was coming since the moment the servant-thing poured wine I wouldn't drink. In any negotiation worth having, both parties already know the shape of the trade before a single word is spoken. Everything else is theater.

"Its true name," the jailer continued, "spoken freely, by you, into our keeping. The name it bore before binding. The name that is the knot of it. Speak it, and the wolf-shape comes off like a coat, and what is underneath belongs to whoever holds the name."

"And if I refuse?"

It spread its hands. Around the hall, forty faces spread theirs, in perfect unison, and the shadowed hundreds beyond the light spread hands I couldn't see, and I felt the movement in the dark the way you feel a draft under a door.

"Then the candle stands," the jailer said. "For another eleven years. For another hundred. Souls keep beautifully down here, taster. That is rather the point of us."

* * *

I asked for time to consider, because asking for time is free, and they granted it, because granting things costs them nothing and makes them look merciful.

They put me in a room that was almost a guest chamber and almost a cell, which I suspect is the only kind of room this court has ever built. A bed of stone dressed in furs that breathed faintly, as if recently alive. A window that showed not the mountain's rock but a slow grey nothing, like the inside of a closed eye. On the table, the obsidian cup, refilled. Always refilled. The court's patience, served by the glassful.

I sat on the bed and did the arithmetic I'd promised.

The trade was clean. That was what frightened me. A soul restored for a name surrendered — even, even, almost kind. If I had been a simpler man, I'd have spoken the wolf's name before the furs finished warming under me. I loved my sister. I had crossed a dead road full of hanging debts to reach her. What was a god's name against a girl's whole self?

But I had spent twenty years learning the difference between a poison that kills and a poison that *changes* you, and this offer stank of the second kind.

Because the court hadn't asked for the wolf itself. It hadn't asked for the wolf bound, delivered, caged. It had asked for the *name* — the knot, the unbinding word. They wanted the key, not the prisoner.

And a jailer who asks for a key is planning to open something.

I thought about what the wolf had told me on the road, in its voice like stones settling in a riverbed: *Before your guild, before your king's grandfather's grandfather, we were many. Then we were few. Then we were shaped. Something had to hold us in those shapes, taster, and holding is a hunger all its own.*

The jailers had held the old gods for an age. Bound them, leashed them, worn them into wolf-shapes and river-shapes and other patient prisons. And now the old gods were stirring — the kitchen girl's answered prayer, the ink crawling on its own in the ledgers, the whole rotten world's rules going soft at the edges. The jailers felt it. Of course they did. They lived on top of it.

And what does any institution do when the thing it imprisons begins to wake?

It doesn't tighten the chains first. Chains are crude, and waking gods laugh at chains. It gets the *key*. It takes custody of the unbinding word, so that when the day comes — and the day was coming, I could feel it in my teeth like weather — the jailers alone would decide what woke, and when, and in what shape, and owing whom.

Or worse. Far worse, and I turned it over twice before I let myself believe it:

Maybe the jailers weren't afraid of the gods waking.

Maybe they were tired of *guarding* them.

An unbound old god is a power beyond anything this world has seen in an age. And a court that holds its true name holds its leash even after the wolf-shape burns off — a leash on something that could crack the mountain, unmake kings, rewrite the arithmetic of souls entirely. The jailers had spent centuries as wardens in the dark, powerful in the way gaolers are powerful: immensely, and nowhere, and over no one who mattered.

Hand them the wolf's name, and they would unbind it themselves. Not to free it. To *own* it. To walk up out of the mountain wearing an old god like a crown, and every court above — my king, my poisoners, my guild with its ledgers of impossible things — would become a footnote by morning.

They weren't offering to sell me my sister.

They were offering to hire me as the man who opened the door.

And the terrible, shameful part — the part I have never said aloud to anyone — is that for the length of one long breath, I considered it. Yara whole. Yara laughing. Yara remembering bread and our mother's voice. Bought with a word, paid by someone else's ruin, the way everything in my life had always been bought: invisibly, at another's expense. My father had done exactly this arithmetic eleven years ago. *One girl against one debt.* He had chosen himself, and called it necessity.

I lay back on the furs and stared at the grey nothing-window and understood, at last, why the wolf had chosen me. Not because I was clever. Because I was the one man in two worlds who knew precisely how much a person costs, because I had spent my whole life being the currency.

* * *

They came for my answer at what might have been dawn, though dawn means nothing under a mountain. The jailer itself, with two attendants whose faces had been left deliberately unfinished, like sketches. We returned to the hall. Yara still burned. Her light found me and steadied, just slightly, and I kept my gaze moving, kept my face empty, and grieved in advance for the thing I was about to do to us both.

"Well?" the jailer said.

"The trade is refused."

The silence afterward had texture. Weight. Forty faces going very still, and the dark beyond the light going stiller.

"You would leave your sister burning," the jailer said, "for a wolf."

"I would leave her burning," I said, "rather than hand a leash for a god to the people who made a candle of a child and call it keeping accounts." My voice stayed level. Twenty years of practice, holding level while the world went wrong on my tongue. "You don't want the wolf freed and you don't want it bound. You want it *yours*. And when you wear it up the mountain, everyone I've ever known becomes fuel. She'd understand that. She'd be the first to understand it. That's what makes refusing bearable — barely — and it's also why I'll never forgive you for making the offer at all."

Something crossed the jailer's face that was not anger. Anger would have been fine; anger I could work with. What crossed it was *interest*, the way a merchant looks at a stall he'd underestimated.

"No one refuses this court," it said softly.

"Someone just did."

"Then you should know the consequences, taster, since you love arithmetic." It began to walk toward me again, frost blooming and dying under each step. "Refusal has a price schedule. First: the girl stays. That you knew. Second: *you* stay — or rather, you leave here owing us, and we collect on debts the way mountains collect rain. Slowly, everywhere, forever. Third—" and it stopped close enough that I could count the ages in its ruined beauty, "—you will tell us one true thing before you go. Not the wolf's name. Merely... a courtesy. The name of the friend above who armed you. Who taught you the road. Who whispered in your ear that the guild's masters could be made to burn. Give us *that*, and we will let the refusal pass unpriced. Consider it the court's mercy."

And I saw it then, the whole shape of the trap, laid flat and gleaming. They didn't need the wolf's name from me. Any leash requires a door, and doors can be found by other hands, other tongues, other broken men. What they needed now — what they had needed all along — was the road *upward*. A foothold in my world. A name they could leverage, corrupt, own.

They wanted Cassel. Lord Cassel, who had kept me fed and hidden and alive for fifteen years, whose protection was the only roof my dangerous life had ever had. The wolf had promised me that delivering one truth about Cassel would destroy him — that was the price *it* had set, the coin I'd already agreed to spend, and God forgive me, I had agreed.

Now the court below wanted the same man, for the same destruction, as the fee for my own survival.

Two powers, one sacrifice, and me standing in the middle holding the knife I'd already promised to someone else.

"I need no mercy," I heard myself say, and my voice sounded far away, like a prayer from the wrong side of a wall. "And I give you nothing."

The jailer studied me for a long moment. Then it smiled, and this time the smile was honest, which was the worst thing it had shown me yet.

"Brave," it said. "Futile, but brave. Go home to your mountain, taster. Keep your silence. It has always fit you so well." It turned back toward its jaw of thrones, then paused. "Oh — and one kindness, freely given, since you refused ours. The wolf that sent you? Ask it, when next you speak, what happened to the last soul who carried its truths. Ask it what the road cost *him*. Gods make bargains, little man. They do not make friends."

The attendants walked me out. Past the ranks of watching faces, past the shadowed hundreds, past the stand where my sister burned.

And as I passed — as my eyes swept across her the way a man sweeps his eyes past a stranger's window — her flame leaned. Just slightly. Just the width of a breath, the way a candle leans toward an open door.

The whole court saw it.

The whole court went silent.

And somewhere behind me, soft as frost forming, the jailer began to laugh.
