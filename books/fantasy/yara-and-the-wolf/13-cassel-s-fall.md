## 13. Cassel's Fall

There is a way to say a thing that makes it sound like nothing.

I learned it before I learned to read. My father taught it to me over the supper table in a house that smelled of tallow and fear: *if you must speak, speak so small that no one hears you coming.* A truth delivered gently can gut a man as cleanly as one shouted from the rooftops. The difference is only who gets blamed for the bleeding.

I had spent eleven years saying nothing at all. Now I had to say everything, and say it small.

* * *

Lord Cassel kept his apartments above the west colonnade, where the morning light came through glass tinted amber by some alchemist long dead. He liked to take his breakfast there alone — or rather, he liked to be seen taking his breakfast there alone, which is a different appetite entirely. Power wants witnesses. That was the first lesson of the court, and Cassel had taught it to me without ever meaning to, back when he pulled me out of the poisoners' kennels and set me at the king's table with a silver spoon and a taster's tongue.

He had been kind to me. I want to write that down plainly, because everything else I am about to write will try to bury it.

Kind, in the way a man is kind to a knife he keeps sharp. He fed me, housed me, taught me which lords could be tasted and which must never be looked at too long. When the Guild of Impossible Things sent its collectors and I had no coin, it was Cassel's seal that quieted them. He never asked what I owed them. He never asked about the ledgers I kept by night, though a man as careful as Cassel notices everything, and I have always known he noticed me.

That was the shape of my protection: not love, but use. And I had told myself for eleven years that use was close enough.

The wolf had given me three days to decide. *Carry the truth into the light,* it had said at the well, patient as winter, *and the road below opens. But understand what you carry. Truths are not gifts. They are debts with teeth.*

Now the debt was due, and I stood in the amber light of Cassel's antechamber with the words folded up inside me like a blade wrapped in linen.

* * *

He received me standing, which was itself a message. Lords sit when they mean to grant something. They stand when they mean to take.

"Paloma." He said my name the way he always did, tasting it slightly, as if checking it for spoilage. "You look ill."

"I've been keeping late hours." True. The ledgers did not keep themselves, and lately the ink had been moving on its own, entries rewriting themselves in a hand none of the guild's scribes would claim. "My lord, I need access to the undercroft archives. The sealed shelves. The ones that require your countersignature."

A pause. Long enough for me to count his breaths, an old habit — four in, seven held, five out. A man who holds his breath that long is a man accustomed to swallowing things.

"The sealed shelves are closed even to me," he said. "By royal writ."

"They were closed by royal writ," I said. "Before the mountain began to sing."

I watched him decide whether to pretend he hadn't heard the rumors. He decided against it; pretending had stopped working in this court two weeks ago, the night the kitchen girl's prayer was answered with a harvest that should not exist. Since then everyone pretended less and feared more, and fear made men honest in the specific way that made them dangerous.

"You've heard it too," Cassel said quietly. "The singing."

"Everyone has heard it. Only some of us know why."

And there it was — the hook, baited with exactly enough truth. This was the craft of it, the terrible craft: give a powerful man a mystery and he will open any door to solve it. Give him a mystery that touches his own guilt and he will open every door.

Cassel crossed to the window. Below, the colonnade stretched toward the great hall where preparations for the autumn feast were already underway — scaffolding, garlands, the kitchen carts going back and forth like beetles. Somewhere in that hall, in three days' time, poison meant for the guild's envoys would sit in a cup of mulled wine, and I would either drink it silently as I had drunk everything else, or I would speak.

But that came later. First, the door.

"My lord," I said, "there is an entry in a ledger older than the guild. It concerns a soul sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain. It concerns a debt that the crown has been paying interest on for twenty years. And it concerns you."

His shoulders did not move. His hands did not tighten. But his breath changed — six held now instead of seven — and I thought: *there. There is the wound. I have found it without looking, the way I find arsenic in a stew, by the smallest wrongness in the whole.*

"You are my taster," Cassel said. "Not my confessor."

"No," I agreed. "But I am the only man in this court whose silence has kept you alive, and we both know it. Every meal. Every cup. Eleven years. If I stop tasting tomorrow, how long do you think you last? A week? Less? Lord Vane's people have been seasoning your wine since spring. You knew. You let me stand between you and it anyway, because letting me is cheaper than making peace."

I had never spoken to him this way. No one spoke to Cassel this way. I felt the transgression in my own body like a fever — my hands steady, trained steady, while everything underneath them shook.

"What is it you want, Paloma?"

"The countersignature. And an hour alone with the sealed shelves."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tonight I taste your supper as always," I said. "And the night after. And the night after that. Until one evening I don't."

It was blackmail. Let me be exact about that, because the wolf's truth deserved a precise vessel, and precision was all I had left. I blackmailed the only man who had ever stood between me and the guild's collectors, using the only skill I had, and I did it with a calm I did not feel, because the alternative was Yara's candle burning forever in a dark I could not reach.

Cassel looked at me for a long time. Then he laughed — short, genuine, ugly with surprise.

"There you are," he said. "Eleven years, and there you finally are. I wondered what was under all that quiet." He went to his desk, drew out a ring of keys, and pressed one into my palm. His fingers were warm. I hated that they were warm. "One hour. And Paloma — whatever you find down there, remember who taught you to look."

* * *

The sealed shelves gave me what I needed, but not what I wanted. That is the nature of archives; they deal in confirmation, not comfort.

The entry was in a hand I recognized — Vess's early script, before her letters grew cautious. *Sold: one soul, female child, age nine. Seller: Almeida, R., against debt of forty crowns. Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Brokered under seal. Interest accruing to the crown's account.* And beneath it, in different ink, added years later: *Guarantor: C.*

Cassel had guaranteed the sale. Not committed it — my father had done that, may whatever gods remain hold him accountable — but guaranteed it. Put his name beneath it. Made himself the court's surety, the collateral, the man who would answer if the bargain failed. For twenty years his name had been nailed into the mountain along with my sister, and the old god bound in wolf-shape had shown me exactly what that meant: when the freed gods rose to call their debts, the guarantors would be called first.

That was the truth I carried. Not scandal. Arithmetic. Cassel's name, written in a ledger, attached to a stolen child, and the mountain beginning to sing because the payment was coming due.

I could have burned the page. I want that recorded too — that I stood in the archive with the page in my hands and the brazier three steps away, and I considered it, seriously, the way a starving man considers the last loaf. Burn it, and the wolf's road stays shut, and Yara stays a candle, and Cassel stays my shield, and I stay invisible until the day the invisible die.

I put the page back on its shelf instead. Some choices you make with your stomach, not your head. Mine turned over once and settled, cold and certain, on the side of my sister.

* * *

I chose the feast. Of course I chose the feast. A truth about a lord lands hardest where the whole court is watching, and the whole court would be watching the autumn feast — the king under his canopy, the guild's envoys in their grey traveling cloaks, the long tables bent under food I would taste dish by dish by dish.

The poison came in the fourth course, in the venison, slow arsenic laced with something sweeter that masked the bite of it. Meant for the envoys. I drank the sample, catalogued it, filed it away without accusation — eleven years of habit, smooth as water — and then, when the taster's silence had done its work and the hall had relaxed into eating, I stood up.

I had planned the moment for hours and it still felt like falling.

"Your Majesty." My voice came out smaller than I intended, so I planted my feet and made it larger. "Before the wine is poured, there is a matter the crown should hear. Not from the guild. Not from the mountain. From me."

The hall went quiet the way a forest goes quiet — gradually, then completely. Three hundred faces turning. The king's fingers stilled on his goblet. At the far table, Vess sat very still, and I saw her understand before anyone else did, saw her mouth form the shape of *no, don't,* and I looked away from her because looking at her would have undone me.

"A soul was sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain twenty years ago," I said. "Sold by a father against a debt of forty crowns. Guaranteed — signed for, promised, collateralized — by Lord Cassel, whose name stands in the ledger beside a nine-year-old girl's to this day. The mountain sings because the debt is called. And when the old gods rise to collect, they will begin with the guarantors."

Silence. The particular silence of a room full of people doing arithmetic of their own — checking their own names against their own ledgers, wondering what was written under their seals while they slept.

Cassel did not stand. That was the worst of it. He stayed seated at the high table with his hands flat on the cloth, and he looked at me across the length of the hall with an expression I will taste for the rest of my life, and it was not rage. It was recognition. As if he had been waiting eleven years for the quiet man to open his mouth, and had simply hoped, foolishly, kindly, that the day would never come.

"Is this true?" the king asked him.

Cassel rose then, slowly, smoothing his coat. "Majesty, the entry exists. The guarantee was made under the previous administration, under duress, to prevent—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Even now, even falling, he was calculating which lie would bear weight. "Yes," he said finally. "It's true. And I would make the same signature again, because the alternative was the guild's collapse and a war with the mountain. Some debts keep the peace."

"Some debts," said a voice from the shadows of the colonnade, "keep children."

The guild's senior envoy stepped forward, grey cloak, grey face, carrying a ledger of her own. She did not look at me. She did not need to; my part was played, and instruments are not thanked. What followed took less than an hour: the reading of the entry aloud — which Vess had warned me never to do, and which I now understood she had warned me about for reasons that went deeper than guild law — the king's writ of seizure, the stripping of Cassel's offices, the guards coming for him between one course and the next.

They took him past my seat. He slowed. Just slightly. Just enough.

"You tasted for me every day for eleven years," he said, "and this is the one thing you never warned me about. Your own teeth." Then the guards had him, and the doors closed, and the feast resumed around the hole he left in it, because feasts always resume. That is the court's deepest law: the meal continues.

I sat back down. The wine was poured. I drank my sample of it and found nothing, and for the first time in my life the nothing tasted worse than poison.

* * *

That night the well behind the kitchens sang.

Not with water-sound. With the mountain's note, rising through the stones, and standing on its rim was the wolf, grey as ash, eyes patient as ever.

"The road is open," it said. "You kept your word. I kept mine. Do you feel it?"

I did feel it — a loosening in the air, a thinning, as if the world had been a held breath and someone had finally let part of it out. Beneath my feet, deep down, something vast shifted its weight.

"But hear the rest of the price," the wolf said, and its voice gentled in a way that frightened me more than menace would have. "You have lost your shield. By tomorrow's dusk, Cassel's enemies will remember that you were his creature, and the guild's masters will remember that you broke the seal of the trade before the whole court. You have made yourself visible, little taster. Visible men need somewhere to be. The road below is the only place left where your name isn't already written in someone's ledger."

"And if I go down," I said, "do I come back up?"

The wolf looked at me for a long moment, and for the first time since the well, its patience cracked — just a hairline, just enough to show the old, old sorrow underneath.

"That," it said, "depends on what you find. And on what you're willing to trade for it. Come. The mountain is singing your sister's name, and it won't sing it forever."

I took one last look at the court above — the lit windows, the guards hurrying, the whole glittering machine grinding on without me, exactly as it had ground on before me. Somewhere in it, Vess was burning papers; I was sure of it. Somewhere in it, men were deciding whether I was a hero or a traitor, and both answers ended the same way.

I turned my back on all of it and followed the wolf down into the dark, carrying nothing but my tongue, my guilt, and a single drop of immunity eleven years in the swallowing.

The stone swallowed the light behind us step by step, like a throat.
