## 14. Ledger-Burning

The summons came at the fourth bell of the morning, which was wrong twice over. The Guild of Impossible Things did not summon its bookkeeper in daylight, and it did not send a boy to do it — but there he stood at Vess's door, wet-eared and nervous, wearing the livery of the Counting House as if he'd stolen it from a line of washing.

"Mistress Vess. The masters request your presence."

"Request," Vess said. She looked at him a long moment, the way she'd taught Paloma to look at a dish: not at what it showed, but at what it carried underneath. The boy's hands were steady. His eyes were sorry. That told her everything the word *request* had tried to soften.

She put on her grey shawl, the one with no pockets, because they would search her, and she wanted them to find nothing and believe that meant something. Then she followed the boy down through the kitchen ward, past the fishmongers shouting down the price of yesterday's herring, into the narrow door between two shuttered shops that smelled always of tallow and old rain.

The stairs went down. They always went down. In eleven years of keeping the ledgers she had never once climbed a stair for the guild.

* * *

The Counting House proper was a long room lit by lamps that burned without oil, because the guild had bought their light decades ago from something that lived under a frozen lake, and the light did not flicker, did not warm, did not behave like light at all. It simply endured, pale and patient, the way debts endure.

Nine masters sat behind the great table. Vess counted them out of habit and found the number wrong: nine where there should be ten, Master Corvin's chair empty, and no one looking at it. So Corvin was dead, or fled, or worse — retired, in the guild's sense of the word, which meant his name had been read backward out of the rolls until nothing remained of him but a debt nobody could collect.

At the table's center sat First Master Ondrel, a small neat man with small neat hands, the kind of man who could weigh a soul on his tongue and tell you its market rate to the copper. He had been handsome once, in the way a knife is handsome.

"Sister Vess," he said. "Sit."

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit for this."

She stayed standing. It was petty, and it was all she had.

Ondrel opened a ledger before him — not one of hers; she kept the true books, the ones written in ink mixed with ash and patience. This was a working copy, a merchant's summary, and on the page it displayed lay an entry she knew by heart before he even turned it toward her.

**Almeida, P. Taster. Debts: one protection, purchased 11 years prior, paid in service. One immunity, accrued by installment. Status:** —

The status column was blank. She had left it blank herself, three nights ago, when Paloma had stood in this room and spoken aloud at a feast he should have died before speaking at, and the masters had asked her, coolly, whether her boy understood what treason against the trade incurred.

"He understands," she had said.

She wondered, now, if she had lied. She hoped she had. Paloma had spent his whole life learning exactly what things cost. It would be fitting if he'd chosen the one bill he couldn't pay on purpose.

"Your protégé," Ondrel said, "descended into the mountain. Our people watched him do it. He walked the old road, entered the Court Beneath, and stole from it." He paused, delicately, the way a man pauses over a phrase he finds distasteful. "He stole a soul."

"The court below sold souls first," Vess said. "We brokered them. I don't recall treason being the word for it."

"We brokered," Ondrel agreed, mild as milk. "That is the difference. A broker stands between. Your boy has picked a side. Worse — he has picked a side *visibly*. The court above knows a guild taster spoke poison-truth at the king's own feast and lived. The court below knows a guild man lifted a candle from its stand and walked away with it. Both courts are asking the same question, Sister Vess, and I will let you guess what it is."

*Whose man is he?*

She said nothing. Saying nothing was a craft too, and she had practiced it longer than Paloma had.

"The masters have voted," Ondrel went on. "It wasn't close. The trade survives by being impossible to find and expensive to forgive. He has made us findable and cheap. The sentence is execution, by the guild's method — his page read backward, his protections revoked, his immunity returned to the pool, and his body left for whichever court reaches it first. Poetic, really. He poisoned himself into safety over eleven years, drop by drop. We will take those drops back in a single evening."

"And my vote?" Vess asked. "I notice nobody counted it."

"Your vote is why you're here." Ondrel folded his small hands. "You will perform the revocation yourself. It must be the hand that wrote the entries, or the ink resists. And afterward, Sister Vess, you will grieve, and then you will return to your desk, because the ledgers do not keep themselves, and sentiment—" his mouth thinned, "—sentiment is a debt we stopped carrying years ago."

Vess looked at the empty tenth chair. She thought: *Corvin voted no.* She thought: *and there sits his absence, tidy as arithmetic.*

"When?" she asked.

"Now," said Ondrel. "The books are open. Bring the page."

* * *

They did not blindfold her going down to the Vault of Names, which told her they no longer feared what she might do with what she knew. That was its own kind of insult. Two masters flanked her — Halloway and the one called only Mother Sedge, who was not anyone's mother and had outlived four First Masters by the simple method of agreeing with whoever held the key. Behind them came the boy in borrowed livery again, carrying the iron box, because even here, even now, the guild made children carry the weight.

The Vault was small. People imagined grandeur — they always did — but names need very little room. There was a table, a lamp of lake-light, and shelves of ledgers rising into a dark the lamp never reached. The air tasted of iron filings and church. Every hair on Vess's arms stood up, the way it does before lightning, except that underground there was no lightning coming. Just the slow pressure of ten thousand bargains, all of them still insisting they were fair.

She found the volume by touch. Third shelf, black spine, the year of the great fever — the year the guild had nearly broken, the year a desperate father had come up the mountain road with two children and a debt and been allowed to keep only one.

She set the book on the table. She did not open it.

"You're hesitating," Mother Sedge observed.

"I'm remembering," Vess said. "There's a difference, though I wouldn't expect the trade to know it anymore."

She opened the book.

The pages breathed. They always did that — a soft exhalation, as if the paper had been waiting. And there it was, in her own handwriting, the handwriting that had recorded forty years of impossible commerce: **Almeida, P.**

One protection, purchased. One immunity, accrued. And beneath it, in the margin, where bookkeepers write the things that aren't numbers: a small notation she'd made eleven years ago and never repeated aloud. Not a fact. A hope. *May this cover him better than we covered her.*

She had failed at that, hadn't she. The covering. All these years she'd told herself the arrangement was mercy — that Yara Almeida's soul, sold south, had at least purchased her brother a life inside the walls of power, fed, trained, invisible, safe. She had helped broker that sale. She had been young then and terrified and the guild had been three bad quarters from ruin, and the Court Beneath the Mountain paid magnificently, and the father had signed without reading, and someone had to hold the pen steady while he did.

Someone had to hold the pen. That was the sentence she had built her whole life around. Someone has to hold the pen, and better it be someone who feels sick about it.

"Read it backward," Ondrel said, from the doorway. He had not come down into the vault itself. First Masters never did; proximity to uncollected debts aged the skin. "Word by word. You know the rite."

She knew the rite. Every keeper did, though most performed it only on dead men's pages, closing accounts, tidying the world's arithmetic. Read the entry from end to beginning, unspelling what the spelling did. The words didn't merely record the protections — recording was binding, in the guild's grammar, had been since the first keeper traded her own name for the alphabet that made this all possible. Unsay the entry and the unsaying took.

His protection would peel away first. Every favor owed him, every quiet arrangement, every guard who'd been paid to look through him — gone, all at once, everywhere. He would become visible to every eye in both courts simultaneously, a man-shaped debt suddenly called due.

Then the immunity. Eleven years of swallowed arsenic and nightshade and patience, drawn back out of his blood in a single night. He would be ordinary by morning. Ordinary, and naked, and hunted, and standing in the middle of a war between gods.

Unless he was already dead. Unless the court below had taken him, and this was all ceremony, burning a page for a man who had no further use for pages.

She almost preferred that. It would have hurt less cleanly.

"Sister Vess," Mother Sedge said, and there was — Vess noted it with the last part of her mind still taking inventory — something almost gentle in it. "It goes easier if you don't think of him while you do it."

"That," said Vess, "is precisely what I intend not to do."

And she struck a match.

The guild's method was fire and speech together — the flame to carry the words upward, the way prayers were supposed to travel before the guild taught everyone better uses for them. She touched the match to the corner of the page and watched the fire take hold, patient, orange, ordinary. Of everything in the vault, only the fire behaved like fire.

Then she began to read, backward.

"*—service in payment, protection one—*" The words came out wrong in reverse, syllables folding against each other, and the vault heard them anyway. She felt the first knot loosen somewhere far above, felt it in her teeth. Somewhere in the palace a guard captain would blink and wonder why he'd ever bothered forgetting to see a taster's face.

"*—installment by, immunity one—*"

This one fought her. Eleven years of accrual makes a dense thing; the words resisted reversal like a nail pulled against the grain. She read louder. The flame climbed the page, eating the margin first — eating her little note, *may this cover him*, curling it brown, then black, then into nothing.

Her eyes were stinging. Smoke, she told herself. Lamp smoke. The lake-light didn't smoke, but she told herself that anyway, because telling yourself things was half of surviving this trade and she had survived it longer than most.

"*—Almeida, P.—*"

The name came out of her mouth and the fire took the page whole, a single bright swallow, and Vess felt the account close with a sensation like a door shutting in a house she used to live in. The ledger in front of her was blank where he had been. Not empty — blank, smooth, as if no one named Paloma Almeida had ever cost the guild anything or been owed anything in return. As if a boy had never learned to taste poison by practicing on her kitchen scraps. As if she had never taught him that noticing everything and saying nothing were the same skill, and that mastery of it was a kind of dying done slowly, in installments, like his immunity.

The ash drifted down onto the table. She put out the last ember with two fingers, and did not flinch, and hated every master standing behind her with a completeness that surprised her, because she had thought she'd rationed out her hatred years ago and here it came, whole, unfaded, like something the guild had never managed to buy.

"It is done," Mother Sedge said. "You did well, sister."

"I didn't do well," Vess said. "I did what I was told. Kindly never confuse the two around me again."

She turned to go. Her legs carried her, which seemed generous of them.

At the vault door, Ondrel's voice caught her, light as dust.

"One more matter, Sister Vess. The girl's page. Yara Almeida, sold to the court below. With the brother's account closed, the family ledger is unbalanced. The masters feel it should be tidied. If the boy lives and comes back, he comes back owing us nothing and owning nothing — including any claim on his sister's contract. You understand? Whatever he stole down there, whatever he thinks he redeemed — as of tonight, the trade holds no record that she was ever ours to sell. Which means no record that anyone owes her justice."

Vess stopped on the threshold. The stairs went up ahead of her, and it occurred to her, with something adjacent to laughter, that these were the first stairs she'd climbed for the guild in eleven years, and she was climbing them away from it.

"Understood," she said.

But she was lying, and she was good at it, and neither of them could taste the difference. Because she had done one thing in the vault that none of them had watched closely enough to catch. When she struck the match — before the rite, before the reading, in the half-second when every eye in the room was on the book and not on the keeper's hands — she had torn a strip from the bottom of his page. Four words, her own handwriting, folded small and tucked into her sleeve, where it had been resting against her pulse all this time like a second heart:

*Yara Almeida. Sold.*

Not evidence. Not leverage. Just proof. Proof that a girl had existed, and been priced, and been worth more than the number.

The masters believed they had just erased Paloma Almeida. They believed erasure was safety, that unwritten meant unprotected, that a man with no debts had nothing anchoring him to the world.

Vess climbed the stairs, holding her stolen scrap of paper, and thought of her boy walking up out of the mountain with a soul-candle in his hands, invisible to every ledger now, unprotected by anything but his own stubborn tongue — and she realized, with a cold settling joy, what she had actually done.

She hadn't erased him.

She'd freed him.
