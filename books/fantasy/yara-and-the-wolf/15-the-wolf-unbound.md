## 15. The Wolf Unbound

I have been a wolf for so long that I had forgotten I was ever anything else.

That is the first mercy of a shape: you forget. The mountain gave me fur and hunger and four feet of silence, and for nine hundred years I padded the borderlands between the court above and the court beneath, and I told myself that forgetting was freedom. It was not. It was only a smaller cage with softer walls.

I knew the boy would come down before he knew it himself. Tasters always come. They spend their whole lives swallowing other people's deaths, and eventually they want to know what their own would taste like.

He came down the old road with my truth in his mouth like a hot coal, and he did exactly what I asked. He carried it up. He fed it to Lord Cassel's enemies and watched his protector burn, and when he came back to me his hands were shaking and his eyes were dry, which is worse, and he said, *You never told me it would feel like this.*

I said, *I told you the price. I never said the coin would be clean.*

That was three days ago. Three days, in which he walked into the Court Beneath and stood before the jailers in their robes of ledger-leather and refused them. Refused them. Nine hundred years I have watched men stand in that hall, and every one of them — every priest, every guild-master, every king's man who came seeking power or pardon or a dead wife's name — every one of them flinched when the jailers opened the book.

Paloma Almeida did not flinch. He said no. He said it quietly, the way he does everything, and then he turned his back on the throne of chains and walked toward the candle-stands where the stolen souls burn like tallow, looking for one particular flame.

The jailers let him walk. That is how I know what comes next.

They do not punish refusal. They simply change the terms until refusal becomes impossible. I have seen it done to mountains. I have seen it done to rivers. And now I feel their instruments turning toward me, the great compasses of binding swinging out of the deep vaults, and I understand that the boy has cost me my last disguise. As long as my name stayed buried under the trade, as long as the ledgers kept me filed between *borrowed luck* and *forgotten years*, the court could pretend I was property. Property is safe. Property has a price.

But he looked at me and called me by nothing at all — called me *wolf*, just *wolf* — and in doing so he made me a question instead of an entry. The court hates questions.

So tonight they will try to bind me forever, and I must decide, in the last hours of being a small grey thing that can fit under a bridge, whether to let them.

* * *

You should understand what binding means, since the word gets used loosely above ground, the way people say *drowned* about a spilled cup.

A god cannot be killed. We tried killing each other once, in the years before the mountain, and the world cracked in eleven places and we stopped. So the clever ones — and there were clever ones, before cleverness became a trade — built the alternative. Binding is not death. Binding is *edit*. They take everything you were and fold it down and down until what remains fits in a shape, and then they sew the shape shut with oaths and names, and they leave one seam open so that something of you stays aware enough to suffer. A bound god watches. That is the punishment. Not pain — pain ends, pain is honest. Watching. Watching the world use your silence as a floor to build on.

For nine centuries I have watched the Guild of Impossible Things grow fat on the interest of stolen souls. I have watched the court above season its politics with arsenic and call it governance. I have watched a girl named Yara Almeida burn on a stand in the Hall of Ledgers, and I watched her brother taste poison in the dark and say nothing, because saying nothing was the only prayer anyone had taught him.

I chose him the way water chooses a crack. Not because he was good. Because he was *precise*. Because a man who can taste one grain of arsenic in a king's stew is a man who can taste a lie in a god's mouth — and gods lie, boy, remember that, whatever happens tonight, remember that I lied to you about Cassel, I let you believe the truth would set you free when I knew it would only make room.

Here is the thing I have never said aloud, in all these years of patient eyes:

I am not gentle. I was never gentle. Gentleness is a discipline, like your tasting, and I have practiced it for nine hundred years the way you practice swallowing. But the court is coming with its compasses, and when they bind a god they take away the discipline along with everything else, they seal it in the same tomb as the rage, and what walks out of that vault wearing my shape will be all hunger and no manners, forever, leashed to whoever holds the chain.

They mean to make me a weapon. They have wanted to since the day the girl's father signed his daughter away and I first put my nose to that paper and smelled the old magic underneath the ink.

And the boy is going to try to save me.

I can smell him coming. Fear and lamp-oil and the ghost of arsenic he'll never wash out of his skin. He is somewhere in the lower galleries with a stolen candle already cupped against his chest — yes, I know, little taster, I have known since you crossed the third gate, the flame leans toward you like a plant toward sun, souls remember kindness — and he is going to see the compasses, and he is going to do something brave and stupid and exact, because that is the only kind of thing he knows how to do.

So I had better reach him first.

* * *

I find him at the mouth of the Hall of Ledgers, pressed into an alcove behind a curtain of hanging debts — the court files its unpaid vows here, strips of vellum that sway without wind — and he has the candle shielded inside his coat, and his heart is going like a bird in a box.

He sees me and something in his face breaks open. Not relief. Relief is simple. What crosses his face is the look of a man who has just realized that the plan he made in the dark does not survive the light.

"They're coming for you," he whispers. "I saw them carrying the — the rings. Great iron circles, covered in writing. Four of them. And chains that didn't clink."

"Chains that don't clink are the worst kind," I say. "Clinking means iron. Silent means oath."

"I can get you out." His voice drops into the register he uses for lying to kings. "There's a service road behind the hall, the one the ash-carriers use. If we go now, before they finish the rite—"

"No."

He stops. He is very good at stopping; it is most of his craft. "No?"

"No." I sit, because what I am about to say deserves a body at rest. In this shape I am barely bigger than a hound, grey as winter, and when I look up at him my eyes are a man's, and he has never quite gotten used to that, and I count on it now. "Listen to me, Paloma. You have spent your whole life being careful with dangerous things. Poisons, ledgers, truths. So be careful with this one. You cannot save me. Saving implies keeping what is saved. There is nothing left of me worth keeping except the discipline, and the discipline dies in the binding. Do you understand? Whatever they seal in those rings tonight will not be the thing that taught you the road. It will be my shape stuffed with my appetite and pointed at the world."

His jaw sets. I have watched him do this across banquet tables while swallowing things that would kill a horse. "Then what do you want me to do? Stand here?"

"I want you to free me."

The word lands on him like cold water. I watch it run down through him, past the plans, past the guilt, all the way to the place where he keeps the things he won't look at directly.

"Free," he says. "Not save."

"Not save. Save is what you do to something small. Free is what you do to something old, and you do it standing well back." I step closer, close enough that he can see the seams in me if he looks — the faint stitching of oath-light where the binding holds my true size folded down into this borrowed body. "Paloma. I need to tell you what you would be letting out, because I will not trick you into this. Trickery is the court's craft, not mine, and besides — you'd taste the lie. You always do.

"When they cut me loose, everything folded comes open at once. Nine hundred years of watching. Every debt I've memorized. Every name in every ledger, including the ones written in the margins where even Vess doesn't read. And the rage. Especially the rage. A free old god is a storm with a memory, and storms do not check with the boy who opened the door before they level the village. I may stay gentle. I have had long practice. Or I may look at the court above — the king who seasons his rivals, the masters who file children under *assets* — and I may decide the world needs editing, and I will be *able*, Paloma, I will finally be able, and no one — not you, not Yara, not all the prayers in the mountain — will have the leash."

The candle shifts under his coat. He puts his hand over his chest as if steadying it. Her flame, his sister, burning patient against his heartbeat.

"And if I don't," he says slowly, working it through the way he works a strange flavor, holding it on the tongue, "if I leave you to the rings — what walks out of that vault points at the world too. Just on a shorter chain."

"Now you're tasting it," I say.

He crouches down until we are eye to eye, the taster and the wolf, in the corridor of hanging debts, and for a moment neither of us is anything but afraid. I want him to know that. Gods are permitted one honesty per century, and I am spending mine here: I am afraid. Not of the rings. Of myself. Of the door swinging wide after nine hundred years of discipline, and finding out what was underneath the discipline all along.

"Why ask me?" he says at last. "You could bite through any chain they bring. You could run."

"The chains aren't for catching me. They're for closing the seam." I nod at the faint stitch of light along my flank. "Once the rings seat, the fold becomes permanent. No road, no bargain, no god — nothing opens it. Ever. And the thing wearing my shape serves the holder of the chain until the world ends." I hold his gaze. "There is exactly one moment when the binding can go either way, and it is the moment before the first ring seats. One breath, boy. In that breath someone must speak the counter-word, and the counter-word is not a name and it is not an oath. It is a choice, spoken plainly, by someone the binding recognizes as having claim on me."

"What claim do I have on you?"

"You looked at me and saw a wolf instead of an entry." I smile, insofar as a wolf can. "In the court's grammar, that makes us kin. Kinship is the oldest claim there is. Older than trade. Older than law. It is the one thing the ledgers never figured out how to file."

He is quiet for a long time. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the court is singing its evening lies, and the sound comes down the mountain like oil poured on water.

"If I free you," he says, "and you turn, and you hurt her —"

"Then you will have learned what the court learned nine hundred years ago," I say, "and you will spend the rest of your life the way I spent mine. Watching. Trying to hold a storm with your hands. I cannot promise you gentleness. Anyone who promises you gentleness is selling something. I can only promise you this: I have watched you swallow poison in silence for six years to keep a court running that did not deserve your tongue. And I have decided — and deciding is more than a bound thing should be able to do, which tells you how thin my chains have worn — I have decided that if I am ever free, I would like to be the kind of thing that deserves what you're about to risk."

"That's not a promise either."

"No," I agree. "It's a wager. Trust is not a promise, Paloma. A promise is a ledger entry. Trust is a leap into the dark with no entry made, no collateral held, no recourse. It is the one transaction the guild never mastered, which is why the guild will die." I lower my head until my forehead nearly touches his boot, an old gesture from before the mountain, from before shapes. "So. Will you leap?"

He doesn't answer with words. He does something better and worse: he takes the candle out of his coat.

Yara's flame stands steady in the draftless air, small and gold and unbearably alive, and in its light his face is stripped down to the bone of him — grief and fury and love, all the coins he's been hoarding, spent at once. He holds the flame between us.

"She comes first," he says. "Whatever happens with you, whatever you become — she comes first. Swear it, or refuse to swear it, but say it plain."

"I swear nothing," I say, and his face begins to close, and I finish quickly: "because a sworn thing can be unsworn by any clerk with the right ink. Instead I give you my nature. When I am free, the first thing I will smell is her — a soul that burned seven years without being consumed, which is impossible, which means the mountain itself has been protecting her, which means the mountain and I are not finished with each other. She comes first because she is the proof. She is the flaw in their arithmetic. Everything I do, I do starting from her."

Down the corridor, iron begins to sing. The rings. Four voices of metal, each one engraved with a ninth of my name, being carried toward the vault floor where the compasses wait. The jailers' footsteps make no sound at all. Oath-bearers never do.

Paloma stands. He tucks the candle back against his heart, and he looks down at me, and I watch him do the bravest thing I have ever witnessed, which is not the descent, not the refusal, not any of the loud courages. He simply decides. I see it happen. The tension goes out of his shoulders the way it does when he swallows a poison and commits to surviving it.

"How much time?" he asks.

"One ring seats within the hour. All four by dawn."

"Then tell me the counter-word."

"It isn't a word." I rise, shaking off nine hundred years of stillness, feeling the stitches strain, feeling underneath them something vast turn over in its sleep and begin, slowly, joyfully, terribly, to wake. "It's a sentence. And you have to mean every syllable, because the binding will taste it, and it has tasted nine hundred years of lies. Are you ready to learn what frees a god?"

Above us, far above, a bell tolls in the court of men, and the mountain shivers, and somewhere in the Hall of Ledgers a page turns itself.

"Tell me," says Paloma Almeida.
