## 16. Stealing a Soul

The flame did not flicker.

That was the first thing Paloma understood about it, standing in the shadow of the colonnade while the jailers changed their watch. Every other fire he had ever known breathed — leaned and swayed with the air around it, the way all living things lean. This one stood perfectly straight, a column of white no taller than a candle, seated in an iron cradle at the center of the round chamber, and it did not move at all.

It was the stillness of something held. Something bound.

Around it, ranked in concentric circles like a court around a throne, stood the soul-candles. Hundreds of them. Small flames of every color — blue, gold, a green like riverwater, one that burned a bruised violet — each pinned to its stand by a collar of black iron. They were souls. He had known it since the descent, felt it in his teeth the way he felt arsenic: not seeing, *knowing*. Each flame a person, thinned down to light and made useful.

And near the front of the third ring, burning a warm ordinary yellow, the color of kitchen lamplight, of home —

He did not look at her. Not directly. Not yet.

He counted instead. That was the craft. When the tongue found something terrible, you did not flinch; you cataloged. Two jailers on the door behind him, their skin the grey of deep places, their eyes like coins left long in water. One more circling the outer ring, slow as a minute hand. The warding flame's cradle bolted to the floor. No guards within arm's reach of it — they didn't think it needed any. Why would it? Nothing in this mountain could touch it. Nothing below, nothing above.

Nothing except a man who had spent twenty years drinking poison a drop at a time.

*You have an immunity in you worth more than any soul in the ledgers,* the wolf had said. *The court built its warding on the principle that poison cannot be turned against itself. But you are not poison, little taster. You are the antidote. Everything you ever swallowed and survived is still in you, layered, learned. Your body has been negotiating with death for two decades, and it has never once lost.*

*One drop,* Paloma had said.

*One drop is all a flame needs,* the wolf had said, *if the drop is honest.*

The circling jailer passed him. Paloma stood against the wall in the posture of furniture, hands loose, eyes lowered, breath shallow. Invisibility was not a cloak; it was a discipline. You made yourself a thing the eye slid past — a servant, a shadow, a stool. He had done it at a thousand feasts. He could do it here, where the stakes were not his life but his sister's whole remainder.

The jailer passed. Did not pause.

Paloma let out his breath by fractions.

* * *

He had prepared the way he prepared for a feast: by knowing every dish before it touched his tongue.

Three nights in the mountain road, while the old gods' prayers echoed past as ribbons of light, the wolf had taught him what the warding flame was. Not fire. A *fasting* — the old gods' own hunger, siphoned off and set upright, made to burn backward. It consumed prayer. It consumed soul-light. It fed the court and kept the gods asleep beneath everything.

"And how," Paloma had asked, "does one poison a hunger?"

"The way you poison anything," the wolf said, its grey eyes patient in the dark. "You give it something it wants. And you make the wanting fatal." It had looked at him a long moment. "Your immunity is not merely resistance, taster. It is a record. Every venom your body mastered is a lesson written into your blood. To a flame that feeds, you will taste of everything it has ever devoured — every soul it stripped thin, every prayer it swallowed. You will taste like a feast it already ate. And a hunger cannot refuse its own meal."

"And when it realizes I am not a feast?"

"By then," said the wolf, "it will be busy dying."

* * *

Now he moved.

Not toward her — not first. First toward the flame, because the flame was the lock and Yara was the prize, and a man who went for the prize before the lock deserved what courts did to thieves.

The chamber was quiet in the way of deep water. The jailers did not speak; perhaps they could not. Their footsteps made no sound on the stone, only a pressure he felt in his inner ear, like the moment before a storm breaks. He crossed between the rings of candles with the unhurried gait of a man who belonged, head bowed, carrying an empty tray he had taken from an alcove three corridors back. Servants carried trays. Nobody questioned a tray.

The white flame grew as he approached. Up close he could feel its heat — or not heat exactly, but *appetite*, a pull at his skin like standing too near an open mouth. It knew he was there the way a ledger knows ink. It tasted the air around him.

He stopped at the edge of the cradle's dais and knelt, as if setting down the tray, as if he were some functionary come to feed it. His heart was a drum in his ears, and he did a thing he had never once allowed himself at any feast: he listened to it without calming it. Fear was proof he was doing something that mattered.

*Now,* said the wolf's voice, not aloud but along his bones. *Little taster. The drop.*

Paloma opened a vein of intention rather than flesh — that was how it actually worked, though it felt like both. He gathered up what lived in him. Twenty years of it. The arsenic in the king's stew his third year, held on his tongue for the count of ten and swallowed on faith. The nightshade distilled in wine so fine he had praised its vintage even as his throat closed. The slow lead of bad pipes, the quick cruelty of cantarella, the wedding-gift poisons, the apology poisons, the eleven kinds of grief served in goblets by smiling men. Every swallow he had ever survived rose in him now, and his body — that patient, unthanked scholar — translated them all into a single word.

It gathered at his fingertip the way a tear gathers at a lash.

He reached out and let it fall into the white flame.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The flame stood straight and indifferent, and Paloma thought with a strange clarity: *I have killed us both, and Vess will never know, and Yara will burn alone forever—*

Then the flame *bent*.

It leaned toward him — the first movement it had ever made, the first breath it had drawn in centuries — and drank. Paloma watched it take his offering the way a starving man takes bread, watched it swell, whiten, climb half a foot in the air with a sound like a long-held note finally released. The jailers' heads came up. The pressure in the room changed.

The flame shuddered.

And then, from its center, it began — quietly, almost politely — to eat itself.

The appetite he had given it was a memory of everything it had consumed, and hunger could not distinguish memory from meat. It turned inward. It gulped its own white length like a serpent swallowing its tail, guttered, flared, guttered again, and with each contraction the collars on the surrounding stands loosened by a hair's width — Paloma heard it, a hundred tiny iron clicks, like rain beginning.

"NOW," the wolf's voice cracked through his bones like ice in a river.

The chamber broke open into motion. Jailers converging, coin-eyes fixed, the pressure of their steps now a sound, a rising drone like a struck bell that would not stop ringing. Paloma dropped the tray and ran — not away, but *in*, weaving between the rings of candles, yellow-yellow-blue-gold, reading colors as he passed the way he'd once read steam off a soup, until—

There.

A small flame the color of hearthlight. On its iron stand. On its collar a plate engraved not with letters but with a feeling, and the feeling was *a girl on a hill counting hawks, a girl who gave her bread crusts to crows, a girl who called him Pali and meant it kindly.*

Yara.

His hands, trained to hold a king's cup steady while poison shimmered in it, held steady now. He gripped the stand. The drone behind him was almost upon him. The warding flame gave one last convulsive roar and went out — and in the sudden dark-everywhere, the hundred collars sprang open all at once, and the chamber filled with light as freed flames began, wonder of wonders, to *rise*.

Jailers shouted in a language like rockfall.

Paloma lifted the candle from its stand. It weighed nothing and everything. The little flame bent toward his chest as if it recognized the warmth there, and he cupped his palm behind it, sheltering it from the wind of his own running, the way you shelter a match, the way you shelter—

"Pali."

It was not a voice. It was less than a voice — a flicker, a warmth against his thumb, a syllable shaped in fire. But it was hers. She remembered. Somewhere inside the thin burning brightness, his sister had heard his heartbeat and known it.

"I've got you," Paloma said, and his voice broke on it, twenty years of silence breaking with it. "I've got you, Yara. Hold on to me."

Then he ran.

Behind him the freed souls rose in a spiral column through the chamber's broken roof-eye, streaming upward toward the mountain road like sparks returning to a bonfire, and the jailers' bell-drone turned from anger into something older and much worse: alarm. The court Beneath had kept its prisoners orderly for a thousand years. It had rules for thieves, rules for rebels, rules for gods.

It had no rule for a hundred souls escaping at once, led by a servant with an empty tray.

Paloma ran into the dark of the corridor with his sister burning bright in his hands, following the pull of old prayers, and far ahead, at the end of all the tunnels, something vast and unbound shook the mountain to its roots — waking.
