## 17. The Long Way Up

**Paloma**

The road behind them was coming apart like a sentence someone had lost faith in.

Stones that had stood for a thousand years under the mountain were unstitching themselves, rising a hand's width off the ground and hanging there, trembling, before falling back in wrong orders. The prayers that lit the walls—the little flames of other people's desperate wishes—were guttering out one by one as the court below woke to what had been stolen from it. Each dead flame left a darker dark, and into each dark came the sound of pursuit: not footsteps yet, but the pressure of them, the way you feel a shout coming in the throat before it arrives.

Yara ran ahead of me. That was wrong. Everything about it was wrong. She should have been behind me where I could see her; she should have been small enough to carry. Instead she was tall now, taller than I remembered, and she moved like something that had been taught to move by candlelight—all flicker and lean—and when she glanced back at me her eyes did the thing I would never get used to, which was to glow very faintly at the edges, like paper held too near a flame.

"Left," she said.

"There's no left."

"There will be." And there was—a fissure in the wall, breathing cold air, wide enough for one person if that person hadn't eaten in a while. She went through it without slowing. I went through it because the alternative was staying in a corridor with whatever was learning how to walk behind us.

The fissure became a stair. The stair became a shaft. We climbed.

I am a taster. My whole craft is knowing what is about to happen half a second before it happens—in the stew, in the wine, in the smile of a man holding a goblet. For eleven years I have lived half a second ahead of everyone else, and alone. Now I was living half a second behind my own sister, following her up a dying road inside a mountain whose jailers wanted us both, and I didn't know a single thing about her except her name, which I had just spent everything to give back to her.

"Yara," I said. "Slow down."

"No."

"The steps are—"

"I know what the steps are." She did slow, though. Barely. "They're debts. Everything down here is debts. You can feel where the ledger touched the stone." She put her palm flat on the wall as we climbed, and where her hand passed, the rock went pale, like frost retreating from a window. "I know this mountain better than you do, Paloma. I've been its furniture for nine years."

That shut me up for a hundred steps. Then two hundred. The shaft narrowed. Somewhere below us, the pressure of pursuit found our scent, and I heard it change—it wasn't a feeling anymore, it was an actual sound, boots on stone, unhurried, because the court beneath the mountain had never once needed to hurry.

"They're not running," Yara said.

"No."

"Good." Her voice did something complicated. "I hate it when they run. It's worse."

* * *

**Yara**

Here is what it is like to be a candle:

You do not burn. That's the first thing people get wrong. You are what burns. The court puts your soul in a stand of black glass and they feed you light—you understand? They feed you. Light goes in, and what it eats on the way through is you. Your fourth summer. The smell of rain on the road to the fair. Which hand your mother favored. Whether you liked the sea. It takes the things that burn longest first, because those are worth the most, and it leaves the gristle: how to walk, how to speak, how to serve at table with your eyes lowered so no one looks at you too long, because if anyone looks at you too long, the court notices, and the court noticing is the only pain left to you.

My brother looked at me too long. In the candle hall, with the warding flame fat and orange above me and every warden in the court turning toward the sound of a dropped soul-lamp—he looked at me the way you look at water when you've been three days in a desert, and he didn't even try to hide it, and I felt my name come back into me like a swallowed coal.

Now we were climbing, and the name was still burning in me, and I hated him a little for it, because the name hurt. A name is a debt too. It's a debt you owe yourself.

He kept looking at me whenever he thought I wouldn't notice. I noticed. Noticing people notice you—that was the last skill the flame left me, and it is sharper than any knife.

At a landing where the stairs ended in rubble and we had to crawl, he said, "Your hair is longer," and then immediately looked like he wanted to eat the words.

"It isn't mine anymore," I said. "None of it grew for me. It grew for the flame. They keep the vessels tidy." I pulled myself over the rubble. "Why did you come?"

"What?"

"You heard me." I turned and looked at him properly, which was dangerous, and I did it anyway, because I had been safe for nine years and safety had made me nothing. "The court below doesn't lose things. The warding flame has stood since before there were kings up top. One taster with a drop of his own blood-poison does not stumble his way into the deepest room in the mountain by accident. Someone told you how. Someone paid the price. So why, Paloma? Why did you come?"

Behind him, far down the shaft, the torchlight of the pursuit rounded a bend. Unhurried. Patient as interest accruing.

He opened his mouth. I watched him do the thing I remembered—I remembered it suddenly, all at once, a whole scene returned to me like stolen property—my brother at nine years old, standing in the doorway while men carried our father's debt papers out of the house, opening his mouth to say something true, and then closing it, and folding himself small, and becoming invisible instead. He'd been doing it ever since. I could tell, even now. Even with ash in his hair and god-poison in his veins.

"Don't lie to me," I said. "I spent nine years being lied to by fire. I know all the flavors."

* * *

**Paloma**

Guilt, or love. That was the question, and I had been avoiding it since the well, maybe since the ledger, maybe since I was nine years old in a doorway.

Because here was the truth I knew and had never once said aloud, not to Vess, not to Cassel, not to God: I hadn't thought about Yara in six years. Not really. I had thought about a shape called Yara, a grief-shaped hole shaped like a little girl with river-colored eyes, and I had kept that shape polished and carried it everywhere like a merchant carries a relic, but I had not thought about my sister. Sisters grow. I had known that and declined to know it. It was easier to mourn a girl who stayed nine forever than to imagine a woman out there being used as a lamp, because if I imagined it truly—if I let myself think of her grown, aware, serving at some underground table with her eyes down—then I would have had to do something, and doing something meant being seen, and being seen meant dying, and I had built my entire self around the single commandment: survive, unnoticed, at any price.

The price had turned out to be her. I understood that now. My invisibility had not been free. Somebody pays for every impossible thing; that was the whole business of the guild; I wrote the entries myself. And the payment for eleven years of Paloma Almeida being nobody had been itemized long ago, in a ledger entry in my father's hand, and it read: *one soul, secondhand, female, aged nine.*

So was it guilt?

Yes. Obviously, it was guilt. Guilt with legs.

But.

The wolf had asked me at the well, in its patient man's voice, whether I wanted the truth or wanted to be innocent, and I had said the truth, and it had cost me Cassel, and I had learned something in the delivering of it: that guilt is a debt, and debts can be paid off and closed. But love isn't a debt. Love is a trade that never balances. You keep paying. There is no entry marked *settled.*

And somewhere between the warding flame going dark under my one drop of everything-I-had-swallowed and the moment I lifted my sister's soul off its stand—somewhere in that instant, holding a cylinder of black glass with a woman's whole self guttering inside it—I had stopped feeling guilty and started feeling terrified, and terror for another person is just love wearing work clothes.

"Torchlight's gaining," I said.

"You're stalling."

"I'm a coward. Stalling is my native language." I sat back against the rubble, deliberately, with hunters climbing toward us, because she deserved better than an answer given at a run. "Both. It was both. It started as guilt—I'll swear that to your face, Yara, because you asked for hard truth and hard truth is the only kind I've got left. For six years I didn't even look for you. I filed you away the way I file poison names. Known substance, sealed container, do not open. And then I found your entry in the ledger and I found out who signed it, and the guilt got so big I couldn't swallow it, and I have swallowed everything, Yara. Arsenic. Hemlock. The tears of a god. Nothing has ever been too big to go down quietly until you."

Her face didn't move. Down the shaft, the torchlight brightened.

"But guilt is a debt," I said, "and debts end. And this—" I gestured at her, at the climb, at the whole collapsing world. "This isn't ending. If we get out, if I carry you up every stair in this mountain, it won't be settled. I'll still be here the next time. And the next. That's the difference. Guilt would have gotten you off the stand and quit. This doesn't know how to quit. I don't have a word for it that isn't embarrassing."

"Love," Yara said.

"Love," I agreed, and my voice cracked on it like a boy's, and I let it crack, because invisibility was over. I'd spent it. All of it. There was no more hiding left in me anywhere.

She looked at me for a long moment. Her eyes weren't glowing anymore—or less, anyway, the flame-light fading out of them degree by degree as we climbed farther from her stand—and underneath the glow they were river-colored, exactly as I'd kept them, exactly wrong.

"That's the first honest thing anyone has said to me in nine years," she said. "It tastes terrible."

"I'm a taster. Terrible is how you know it's real."

Something happened to her mouth then. It took me a moment to recognize it, it was so strange on her face, worn and rusted from disuse.

My sister smiled.

Then the wall behind us burst open.

* * *

**Yara**

They came through the stone like the stone owed them money.

Wardens. Three of them, then five, gray-robed, faceless—not faceless as in hidden, faceless as in *unwritten*, their features rubbed smooth by centuries of taking other people's names as tax—and where they stepped, the rubble of the broken wall politely rearranged itself into footing for them, because even stone settles its accounts here.

Paloma was up and moving before the dust settled. Of course he was. Half a second ahead, always.

"Run," he said. "Don't fight. They can't be fought—they're made of other people's courage, it turns in their hands—"

"I know what they're made of," I snapped. "I served their wine."

We ran. Up. Always up. The shaft had ended and now there was a gallery, a vaulted throat of the old road, and the prayers in the walls were relighting—do you understand, they were *relighting*, one after another, little flames waking in the stone like a city lighting its lamps at dusk—as whatever the wolf was unbinding spread upward through the mountain. And every prayer that caught fire pushed the wardens back a step. I saw it. I saw a warden reach for my brother and touch, instead, the burning wall where some farmer four hundred years dead had prayed *let the lamb live through winter*, and the warden's hand came away smoking, and the warden screamed in a voice made of forty borrowed screams.

"The gods are waking," Paloma panted beside me. "The wolf said—when it's free—the old ones rise, and everything they touch—"

"I know what the wolf said." I did know. Some of it had leaked into me through the flame, in the years of service. Fragments of jailers' talk. The old gods bound under the mountain. The wolf that was neither old nor young, neither bound nor free, the hinge, the lock, the key that had grown tired of being a key. "I also know what wakes up hungry after nine hundred years, brother, and it isn't gratitude."

A warden got past the burning prayers. One. It came down the gallery toward us with that terrible unhurried gait, and Paloma moved to stand in front of me—stupid, brave, automatic—and I grabbed his arm.

"No. Let me."

"Yara—"

"I served their wine," I said again, and stepped forward, and did the thing I had practiced ten thousand times in the dark: I made myself nothing. Eyes down. Shoulders folded. A vessel, a lamp, furniture. Every trick the flame had beaten into me and every trick it thought it had burned away—and the warden slowed, confused, because its whole senses were tuned to souls, and mine was guttering low, half-eaten, hard to see. Hard to notice.

It passed within an arm's reach of me. Its blank face turned. Something in it strained toward the taste of me, the way a tongue finds blood in water.

And Paloma—my careful, invisible, half-a-second-ahead brother—did the least invisible thing I had ever seen anyone do. He picked up a piece of the shattered wall and hit the warden with it.

It shouldn't have worked. It especially shouldn't have worked because the stone he picked up was part of a prayer—the old masons had set them right in the rock—and the fragment in his hands was burning with somebody's plea, some grandmother's *keep him safe on the road*, and when it struck the warden, the prayer went into the warden instead of around it.

The warden came apart. Not violently. Worse. It came apart *gratefully*, unwritten features softening into—for one second—one real human face, old, tired, relieved, mouthing a name none of us would ever know. Then it was dust, and the dust was ordinary dust, and the gallery was quiet except for the relighting prayers and my brother's breath.

He stood there holding a rock like a man holding a snake that hadn't bitten him yet.

"You hit it," I said.

"It seemed rude not to."

"With a prayer."

"I've been swallowing poisons for eleven years," he said. "I've been due to throw something."

I laughed. It came out of me broken and rusty and it hurt my throat, and it was the first laugh that belonged to me in nine years, and I hated him for causing it and I would have followed him into the deep courts of hell for causing it, and in fact I was currently doing exactly that in reverse.

"Come on," I said. "There's a faster way up. The jailers' stair. But, Paloma—"

"Yes?"

"When we reach the mouth of the mountain, the wolf will be waiting. It always waits at thresholds. It's a creature of doors." I started walking, fast, because I could feel it already, faint as a draft under a door: the presence at the top of the world, grey and patient, and the particular quality of its patience, which had never once in nine hundred years been free. "And it hasn't told you the last line of the bargain. Old gods don't forget prices. They only defer them."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I was sold to pay one," I said, "and the seller wore a wolf's advice, and my brother's ledgers, and my father's hand. Debts travel down, Paloma. Ours is nearly paid." The stair to the surface rose ahead of us, and beyond it, thin as a knife-cut, daylight. "Its final bill is waiting at the top. And I don't think it accepts coin."
