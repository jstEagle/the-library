## 18. What the Gods Demand

The mountain spat us out at dawn.

I will remember that exit for the rest of my life, however long it turns out to be. Not the terror of it — though there was terror, plenty — but the light. After the Court Beneath, where every candle was a soul and every shadow had a ledger-entry, the sun coming over the eastern ridge was almost an insult. It fell on wet grass and ordinary stones and my sister's grey face, and it made no promises at all.

Yara walked beside me. Not *with* me, exactly. Beside me, the way a candle walks beside the hand that carries it. She had a body again — the wolf had seen to that much on the road up, breathing something into the shape of her that the subterranean court had never owned — but the body was new to her, or old to her in a way that hurt. She moved like a woman remembering how doors work. Twice she stopped and stared at her own hands as if they were letters in a language she'd spoken as a child.

"Keep moving," I said, for the third time.

"You keep saying that."

"It keeps being true."

She almost smiled. That was new too. In the court below she had not smiled; she had burned, steadily, patiently, the way the jailers had made her burn, and when I lifted her from the stand she weighed nothing and everything at once. Now, under open sky, there was a flicker at the corner of her mouth that I might have imagined. I decided not to imagine it. I decided to believe in it the way you believe in a rope bridge: with your whole weight.

The mountain's mouth was a seam of black stone behind us, already knitting itself closed. I could feel it sealing through my boots, a slow shudder in the ground, like a great throat deciding whether to swallow. Somewhere down there, the jailers were discovering their warding flame had gone out. Somewhere down there, a court built on debts was waking to the fact that its most valuable asset had been carried off by a poison-taster with soft hands and an ordinary face.

They would come. Not fast — courts are never fast, that's what makes them courts — but inevitably.

"We should be at the tree line," I said. "By nightfall. If we—"

"*No.*"

Not Yara. The voice came from everywhere, from the grass and the stones and the thin morning wind, and then it came from in front of us, because the wolf was there.

It had not walked up. It had simply arrived, the way weather arrives, the way a decision arrives once you've finally made it. It stood between us and the tree line, grey as the mountain's shoulder, enormous in the way that things which are mostly spirit are enormous — your eyes kept trying to find its edges and failing. Its eyes were the same patient man's eyes that had spoken to me at the well, a lifetime ago. Three weeks. Maybe four. Time had gone strange since the ink started moving in the ledgers.

"Old friend," it said to me.

"Don't," I said. "Don't call me that."

The wolf's ears tilted. "You freed me."

"I cheated a court of jailers to steal my sister's soul, and freeing you was a side effect." My voice came out flatter than I intended. Flat is a habit; flat has kept me alive for eleven years. "You want something. You didn't run across half a mountain to say hello."

Behind me, Yara went very still. Stillness, I would learn later, was her first language — she'd had years of practice at standing quietly while powerful people looked through her. She knew what a predator's patience looked like before I did.

The wolf sat. That somehow made it worse. A standing god is a spectacle; a sitting god is waiting for you to finish talking.

"A debt," it said. "There is always a debt. You know this better than anyone alive, Paloma Almeida. You spent your life reading the arithmetic of souls."

"I unmade the ledgers."

"You unmade the guild's ledgers." Its tail moved once, slowly, stirring the grass without touching it. "Debts older than ink do not live in books. They live in the thing owed. And I am owed."

"Name it," I said, and hated myself for how quickly I said it. Eleven years of swallowing other people's poisons, and apparently I would swallow anything if someone only asked politely enough.

"A soul," said the wolf, "for a soul. You took a soul out of the dark. Balance must be returned to the dark. That is the oldest law there is, older than the jailers, older than the guild, older than your king and his poisoned suppers. The court below kept us bound by keeping the scales tipped. You have tipped them."

Wind moved through the valley. Grass bent. My heart was doing something complicated and stupid.

"And whose soul," I said, "were you planning to take?"

The wolf looked at me. Then, with terrible gentleness, it looked past me.

I turned.

Yara was standing very straight, hands folded in front of her the way she used to fold them in the kitchen, before — during — all those years I thought she was dead or worse, all those years our father's debt bought my invisibility with her burning. Her chin was up. Of course her chin was up. We had the same stubborn jaw; I'd just spent my whole life pointing mine at the floor instead of the world.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard me out," she said.

"There's nothing to hear. I know exactly what you're about to say. You're going to say it was your choice to make and I stole it from you when I pulled you off that stand."

"It *was* my choice to make." Her voice cracked on the last word, and something in me cracked with it. "Paloma. I have been a candle. Do you understand what that means? I have had no hands for — I don't know how long. No mouth. Just burning, and the court drinking my light, and every year a little more of *Yara* going into the flame. And now I'm standing in the grass and I can feel the wind and I can't — I don't know how to be a person anymore. Maybe the law is right. Maybe the dark should get its balance. Maybe what's left of me belongs there, and what you're carrying down the mountain is just — the shape of me. An aftertaste."

"That's not—"

"Let me finish." She stepped forward, toward the wolf, and the wolf inclined its great head slightly, courteous as a banker. "I'm offering. Do you hear the difference? Nobody sold me this time. Nobody brokered me, nobody filed me in a ledger. *I* am choosing to go back. That's the one thing they never let me do down there. So let me do it."

For a moment — I am not proud of this — I wavered.

That's the truth of it, and I promised myself I'd stop lying in my own head somewhere around the eighth circle of the mountain road: for one long breath, some tired, poisoned, practical corner of me thought, *she's right, she's broken, maybe the flame was where she'd ended up anyway, maybe this is just finishing the transaction.*

Then I remembered her at nine years old, teaching me the names of herbs by taste, daring me to chew pennyroyal, laughing when I spat. I remembered her holding my hand under the table the day our father sold something he didn't tell us he was selling. I remembered her name — her actual name, the one she'd begun to forget down there, the one my looking at her had called back into her like a coal breathed upon.

Nobody gets to finish that transaction. Not the guild. Not the court. Not the law itself.

"No," I said again, and this time it came out the way it must sound when kings hear it. "You've got it wrong, Yara. You think the choice they never gave you was the choice to leave. It wasn't. The choice they never gave you was the chance to be refused. Everyone in your whole life took yes for an answer. Father took it. Vess took it. The court took it. Well—" and here I turned to the wolf, and I felt eleven years of careful invisibility gather behind my words like a held breath, "—not me. I am refusing you both. Refusing her offer and refusing your price. Find another soul."

The wolf's eyes narrowed. Not in anger. In interest. That was somehow far more frightening.

"There is no other soul," it said. "Balance is not a market, taster. You cannot haggle with the dark."

"Can't I? I've spent my life in trade. Everything haggles, if you understand what it actually wants." I stepped forward until I could have touched its fur, and did not touch it, because I am not entirely a fool. "You don't want a soul. Souls are currency; what you want is *balance*, and balance is a number, and numbers have more than one solution. Ask any bookkeeper."

"Careful," the wolf said softly.

"The guild taught me arithmetic. You taught me better. You told me yourself — the court below stayed in power by tipping the scales. Debts stacked on one side. So here's mine." And I reached, with hands that shook only slightly, into the center of myself, into that place behind the tongue where eleven years of immunity lived — every arsenic catalogued, every nightshade survived, every quiet supper where I swallowed a little of death so the king wouldn't swallow much, building in my blood a tolerance that was also, in every way that mattered, a *power*. The thing that kept me alive. The thing that kept me invisible. The thing that made me useful, and therefore protected, and therefore *Paloma Almeida, who survives*.

"I offer my immunity," I said. "Weigh it."

The valley went silent. Even the wind stopped to listen.

"A soul for a soul," the wolf said slowly, "is not a metaphor, taster. What you carry is not a soul."

"Isn't it?" My mouth was dry. Dry mouths are dangerous for a taster; we read the world through spit and fear. "Eleven years I've been pouring death into myself and letting it pour back out changed. Every poison I ever swallowed left something behind — call it what you want. Immunity. Tolerance. Call it an education. But I promise you, wolf, whatever lives in my blood is *me*. Distilled. Refined. The purest thing I own. It kept me alive when everyone around me died of the same soup. It made me invisible because invisible men are cheap and immune men are priceless. It is the single most valuable thing I possess, and I am offering it to you freely, unasked, unsold — the one transaction in my life nobody brokered. Doesn't the dark weigh *that*?"

The wolf regarded me for a very long time. Behind its eyes, something vast turned over — I felt it the way you feel weather change, pressure behind the teeth.

"You understand," it said at last, "what you are asking. Your tongue goes ordinary. Your blood forgets. The next poison will be your first, and it will kill you like anyone. And you were — you *are* — the most hunted man in two kingdoms. The guild wants your blood for treason against the trade. The court below wants it for theft. The king's poisoners want it for the feast. Without your gift, taster, you will not survive the season."

"Probably not," I agreed. "But she might. And anyway—" and here I did something I hadn't done in eleven years, something I'd nearly forgotten the mechanics of. I laughed. Short, rusty, real. "Anyway, I've been tasting poison for a king who deserves hanging. Better my last years belong to me than my whole life belong to them."

"Paloma." Yara's voice, small. "Paloma, don't—"

"You refused my refusal," I said to her. "Fair's fair."

The wolf rose. It came closer, close enough that I could see mountains moving in the depths of its eyes, close enough that its breath smelled of deep snow and old prayers. When it spoke, its voice had dropped into something formal, ancient, terrible — the voice of a thing reciting terms.

"Paloma Almeida. Do you offer this freely — your immunity, your protection, your invisibility, all that your swallowed deaths have purchased — with no coercion and no expectation of return?"

"Freely," I said.

"Do you understand the weight of what you offer?"

"Heavier than a soul," I said. "A soul is given once. This was earned."

Something that might have been a smile moved under all that grey fur.

"*Accepted,*" said the wolf, and the world exhaled.

It did not bite me. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger survives the burning of all ledgers: it did not bite. It only breathed out, one long slow breath, and the breath went through me like winter water, and I felt it happen — felt my own blood go quiet. All those catalogued poisons, all those survived suppers, that library of death I'd spent eleven years shelving inside myself — it simply left. Not painfully. Worse than painfully: *neutrally*, the way a fever breaks, the way a name leaves a ledger when the page burns. I swayed. Yara caught my arm. Her hands were solid and warm and human and shaking.

"Oh," I said, because eloquence had also, apparently, been part of the package. "Oh, that's — hm."

"What does it feel like?" she whispered.

"Ordinary," I said, and found I was crying a little, and let it happen, because invisible men don't cry and I was done being one. "It feels like being nobody. It's wonderful. It's awful. Don't ever do it."

The wolf stepped back. Between us the morning lay level and bright, and something in the ground beneath us felt — balanced. Truly balanced, for the first time since a desperate man signed his daughter's name into a book, decades ago.

"The scales are even," the wolf said. "The law is satisfied. What the gods demanded, the gods have received." It turned toward the ridgeline, and the rising sun caught its fur and made it briefly unbearable to look at. "Now, taster. Sister. Walk fast and walk low, because the balance is even but the world is not. The jailers' court is empty and furious. The guild's masters have ordered pages burned and names spoken. And the old gods — *we* — are rising, all of us, this morning, out of every crack and well and forgotten shrine in this kingdom. Which means every debt ever written is about to be read aloud."

It looked back at me once, those patient man's eyes holding mine.

"Run, Paloma Almeida. Because when the judgment comes to the capital — and it is coming today — the old gods will call every debtor to account." A pause. Wind again, warmer now, smelling of rain. "And your name is still written in one place you could not burn. The guild's masters kept a copy."

Behind us, far below in the mountain's sealed throat, something began — very faintly, very patiently — to laugh.
