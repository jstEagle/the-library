## 19. The Judgment of the Old Gods

*Vess*

Vess had kept the guild's ledgers for forty years, and she had never once heard them read aloud.

She understood, standing in the ruined square before the mountain's mouth, why the rule existed. The words were not words the way ink was ink. When the old god's voice lifted the first entry from the ash-stinking air — when it spoke *one year of a miller's daughter's laughter, sold in the spring of the bad harvest, price: two sacks of flour and a favor owed* — the year itself came back. Vess felt the miller's daughter laughing somewhere behind her ribs, felt the spring of the bad harvest like weather moving through her bones. Every entry was a small resurrection and a small accusation, and there were ten thousand entries, and the god was not stopping.

It stood at the mountain's mouth in no shape at all — a suggestion of antlers, a suggestion of floodwater, a suggestion of the space between heartbeats — and beside it, smaller and grey and still wearing its wolf-shape out of what looked like habit, sat the one they had called the Wolf. It had not spoken since the road collapsed behind it. It only watched, and its patient man's eyes moved across the crowd of the court like a reader moving across a familiar page.

The court had come because it had no choice. That was the first thing everyone understood and no one said. When the gods rose, the king's summoners had thrown every binding in the royal vault at the mountain, and the bindings had come back to them as rain, falling gently, uselessly, on the marble steps of the palace. So the king came, and his poisoners came, and the guild's masters came, and the whole glittering rotten apparatus of the city assembled itself on the plain below the mountain like a congregation that had forgotten which of them was being buried.

Vess stood with the masters, in her grey guild robes, and she kept her hands still, and she waited for her name.

She had known it would come. She had burned Paloma's page to save him, and the burning had left a hole in the ledger the size of a boy's whole life, and holes, she now understood, were the one thing the old gods could see more clearly than anything else.

The god read on. *A stolen name, returned to no one. A luck borrowed and never repaid, with interest compounding these sixty years. The soul of a midwife, taken as surety, held as collateral, spent as currency —* and with each entry, somewhere in the crowd, a person fell to their knees, or wept, or simply stopped moving, the way a clock stops. The returned things went home. The debts stayed where they were, hanging in the air over their debtors like the chains Vess had seen in the mountain road, except these chains were made of being *known*.

*Paloma*, she thought. *You absolute fool. You brave, impossible fool.*

The wolf's ears turned. It looked at her across the crowd, and its patient eyes were not unkind, and that was worse.

Then the god's voice changed key, and Vess's knees went soft.

*The Guild of Impossible Things. Come forward.*

* * *

*The First Master*

Corven Hale had built the guild from three frightened smugglers and a stolen ledger into the quiet engine of the kingdom, and he had done it by understanding one truth above all others: everything could be owned, and everything could be traded, and the only sin was being caught holding something you couldn't pay for.

He walked forward now with his hands open and his spine straight, because a man who looked guilty died, and a man who looked like a merchant might yet negotiate.

"Honored ones," he began. "The guild has served this kingdom's balance for a century. Every trade was consented to, every contract signed—"

*Signed*, the god said, and the word came down on him like a door. *Show us the signatures.*

Hale had prepared for this. He had brought the master ledger itself, the first one, the true one, because he had calculated — correctly, he still believed — that the gods would want the accounts settled and the guild was the only institution in the kingdom that knew where the accounts were. He opened the book with a flourish he had practiced.

The god did not take the book. The god took the *signatures*.

They rose off the pages like smoke given weight, ten thousand of them, and they hung in the air over the plain, and they were not signatures. They were people. A farmer's careful block letters that had been his whole education. A child's wobble. A hand shaking with fever, signing away a daughter because the alternative was a winter with no food and six other mouths. A hand signing away a *brother*, and Vess, watching from the ranks, saw Paloma flinch as if the ink had touched him.

Each signature, the god said, was a person consenting to a trade they could not possibly have understood, in a language they could not read, under pressure they could not survive. *We have read your law, Corven Hale. We have read it the way you read your ledgers. Would you like to hear what we found in it?*

"I would like to discuss terms," Hale said, and Vess closed her eyes, because she had warned him, she had *told* him, that the old gods did not negotiate, they *counted*.

*Terms*, the god said, almost gently. *Yes. Here are yours.*

And it began to read the guild's debts aloud — not the entries, the *debts*. Every year of a traded life, priced by the guild at what the market would bear and by the gods at what the life had actually been. The difference was a number so large it made a sound. The whole plain heard it. It was not a word. It was the sound a mountain would make if it wept, and when it finished, the master ledger in Hale's hands was simply ash, and Hale was simply a man, and the guild — Vess felt it go even as she stood inside it — the guild was a hundred years of arithmetic with the arithmetic removed.

*You kept the accounts*, the god said. *You will help close them. Every name you ever traded, you will carry to its owner, on foot, and you will say: I took this, and I am sorry, and there is no price. You will say it until you believe there is no price. When you believe it, you will be free.*

Hale's face did something Vess had never seen it do in thirty years. It broke.

* * *

*Paloma*

Paloma stood at the edge of everything, in the clothes he'd fled the mountain in, with his sister's hand cold in his, and watched the world he had spent his life being invisible inside get read aloud like a confession.

The king's poisoners went next.

He had expected the king to be judged first — the king, whose supper Paloma had tasted for eleven years, whose slow arsenic stew had been the first entry Paloma ever filed without accusation. But the gods, it turned out, did not care much for crowns. *A crown is a debt with a hat on*, the wolf had said once on the road down, and Paloma was beginning to understand that the wolf had been making jokes the entire time, terrible ones, at the end of the world.

The poisoners were called by name. All forty of them, from the Master of the King's Cellars to the girl who dusted the vials, and the god read each of their works back to them: the arsenic in the stew, the hemlock in the treaty wine, the thing they had put in Lord Cassel's cup — and Paloma's stomach turned, because Cassel's name came up in the poisoners' accounts the way a coin comes up in a beggar's teeth, passed and passed and finally spent.

*You made a trade of the body itself*, the god said. *You turned the daily bread of a kingdom into a weapon, and you called it service, and you were paid in titles. Here is your judgment: you will taste every poison you ever made. Not the dose that kills. The dose that teaches. You will know what your work did, from the inside, for as long as it took to be done to.*

Paloma heard the justice in it and felt sick anyway, because he had been one of them, in the way that mattered. He had swallowed the arsenic and filed it and said nothing. His silence had been a payment too. He had known, somewhere under the craft and the invisibility, that the ledgers he kept by night were written in the same ink as the ledgers he kept by day.

The god's attention moved across the crowd, and Paloma felt it coming the way you feel a wave coming, and he did not hide. He had spent eleven years learning to be unnoticeable, and he stood there in the open and let himself be noticed, and it was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the mountain, harder than the flame.

*The taster*, the god said.

The crowd turned. He felt eleven years of careful nothing fall off him all at once, and underneath it he was just a thin man with a scarred tongue and his sister's hand in his.

*You swallowed a kingdom's poisons and kept its secrets, and you kept your own counsel, and you sold nothing*, the god said. *And when the price of the truth was your own immunity, you paid it without haggling. We have watched men bargain with us for a crust. You gave away the one thing that kept you breathing, for a girl.*

"For my sister," Paloma said. His voice came out rough. He hadn't spoken to a god before; it felt like speaking into a well and hearing the whole sky answer.

*We know what she is to you. We read it in the road you walked. Hear your judgment, taster.* A pause, and the whole plain leaned in. *You have none.*

Someone in the crowd made a sound. Yara's hand tightened on his.

*You paid your debts on the road down*, the god said. *You refused the bargain that would have unbound us for their purposes. You carried a truth you did not want to carry and you did not flinch from what it cost. The old law is simple: what is owed is collected, and what is paid is paid. You are paid.* And then, in a tone that was almost — almost — the wolf's dry humor: *Your tongue is your own. We suggest you use it for better things than silence.*

Paloma bowed, because he didn't know what else to do with his body, and the god had already moved on, and that was its own strange mercy: to be seen fully, judged, and released, all in the space of a breath. Eleven years of invisibility, and it turned out being visible only hurt once.

* * *

*The Wolf*

The wolf had been bound for so long that freedom still felt like a coat that didn't fit.

It sat at its siblings' flank and watched the court of men come apart the way a bad wall comes apart — not all at once, but along the lines that had been weak the whole time. The subterranean court had been dragged up from beneath the mountain in the first hour, its jailers blinking in the daylight like drowned men, its living candles carried in a procession that made the whole plain weep. The god of the floodwater read the jailers' accounts personally, and it read them slowly, and it did not use the gentle voice it had used on the guild.

The jailers had been set to keep the old gods bound. That had been their purpose, and purposes were not crimes. But somewhere in the long dark they had discovered that a bound god's dreams could be harvested, that a soul-candle burned brighter if the soul remembered its name, that the machinery of imprisonment could be turned into an economy. They had built a court. They had built *accounts*. And now every entry was being read back to them in the voice of the thing they had jailed, and the wolf watched them understand, one by one, that there was no ledger in existence where their side of the column balanced.

*You were made to hold a door*, the floodwater said. *You made the door a market. Your judgment is the door. Hold it — from the inside, with the rest of us, until the last debt is paid.*

The jailers were drawn down into the mountain, and the mountain closed over them like water, and the wolf felt something in its chest that it had not felt in an age. Not triumph. Relief. The specific, exhausted relief of a debt coming due and being *payable*.

Then its siblings turned to it.

*Little brother*, said the floodwater. *You were bound in wolf-shape and set to walk among men, and we have read the terms of your binding, and we find them interesting.*

*Do you*, said the wolf.

*You were not set to guard the door*, the floodwater said. *You were set to find the one who could unmake the ledgers. The jailers bound you to their purpose. But the one who bound you is dead, and the purpose is ash, and you are — * the floodwater paused, and the wolf braced itself, because its siblings had never in all eternity paused out of courtesy — *you are what you choose.*

The wolf looked out across the plain. The court of men was dispersing in the way of things that have been judged — some weeping, some hollow, some already, horribly, calculating how to survive the new arithmetic. It found the taster and his sister at the edge of the crowd, two thin figures holding hands like children crossing a road. It found the old woman Vess, who had burned a page to save a boy and was now standing very straight under the weight of her own walking sentence. It found the mountain, quiet now, holding its door.

It had been a wolf for a thousand years. It had been patient for a thousand years. Patience, it was discovering, had been the binding's last trick: a bound thing tells itself it is choosing to wait.

*Little brother?* said the floodwater.

The wolf stood, and shook itself, and felt the wolf-shape come loose like a coat finally, finally taken off.

* * *

*Yara*

Yara had been a candle for nine years, and she was still learning what hands were for.

She held her brother's hand and watched a god the size of weather pronounce the last judgments, and the names came back to her the way feeling comes back to a sleeping limb — painfully, in floods. She remembered her name. She remembered his. She remembered a kitchen with flour on the table and a father who had, she now understood, sat across from her at that table knowing what he'd signed, and smiled at her anyway, and she was not ready to think about that yet. Maybe in the hills. The wolf — the thing that had been the wolf, the tall grey-eyed figure standing at the mountain's mouth now with its shape still deciding what to be — had said the hills were good for thinking.

The last entry of the day was not a judgment. The gods read it quietly, almost tenderly, and Yara felt every soul they named go home: the miller's daughter, the midwife, the child with the wobbling signature, all the traded and the taken, streaming up the mountain road in lights that no longer needed candlesticks.

Then the tall grey figure turned and looked at her and her brother, and its eyes were the patient man's eyes she had felt on her through nine years of dark, the one warmth in the jailers' cold, and it said:

"The ledgers are ash. But ash remembers the shape of what burned. Someone has to carry the names back — the ones that were traded, the ones that were lost, the ones nobody wrote down at all." It glanced at Paloma. "You two have experience with trades."

Paloma made a sound that was half laugh and half sob. "We don't have a guild anymore."

"No," the god-that-had-been-a-wolf said, and its smile was the first new thing in a thousand years. "You'll have to do it for free."

Yara looked at the hills, and at her brother's scarred hand in hers, and at the long road where the debts had hung like chains and now hung like nothing at all, and she thought: *I was a candle. I can be a lantern.*

"Where do we start?" she asked.

The grey figure turned toward the first name, and the mountain, behind it, opened its door just wide enough to let the light out.
