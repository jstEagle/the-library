## 20. Aftertaste

The first thing Paloma learned about being ordinary was that bread tasted like bread.

He sat in Vess's kitchen on the third morning after the mountain, holding a heel of yesterday's loaf, chewing slowly, waiting for something. A bitterness at the back of the tongue. A metallic thread. The faint sweet rot that meant nightshade, the almond ghost of cyanide, the slow mineral patience of arsenic working through a stew.

Nothing came. Just bread. Warm from the oven's edge, a little stale, honest as dirt.

"You keep doing that," Yara said.

She was at the table with a cup of barley tea she hadn't drunk, turning it in her hands the way she'd once turned everything—examining it, testing its weight, as if the world were still made of things that could be taken from you by standing too long in one place. Her hair was growing back in strange patches, silver where it had been brown, and she flinched sometimes at candlelight, though she was getting better. She was getting better the way ice gets better. Slowly, and all at once, and never quite the same shape as before.

"Doing what?"

"Tasting things like they're going to lie to you."

Paloma looked down at the bread. "Habit."

"It isn't a habit," Yara said. Not unkindly. She had spent years as a living candle in a court beneath the earth; she knew better than anyone alive what habits did to a person when the person was gone and the habit remained. "It's a profession. And professions don't die just because the guild does."

She was right, which was annoying, which was also familiar. He set the bread down and flexed his right hand under the table, the scarred one, the one that had held every poison in the kingdom and outlived them all because he'd traded his immunity for his sister's soul, and now held nothing but itself.

His tongue felt like a stranger's tongue. Like wearing someone else's boots.

* * *

Vess found him an hour later in the scullery, scrubbing a pot that didn't need scrubbing.

In the old days—the days that had ended eleven days ago, though they already felt like someone else's biography—Vess had moved through kitchens like a rumor moves through a court: everywhere at once, owned by no one, leaving traces of herself in other people's sentences. Now she stood in the doorway of her own kitchen with flour on her apron and her hands bare, no ledger, no rings, nothing to count, and she looked lost in a way she would have denied under torture.

"You're cleaning my pots," she said.

"They're dirty."

"I pay people for that."

"The guild paid people for that," Paloma said. "The guild is ash."

Vess came into the room and took the pot from him, or tried to. He didn't let go. For a moment they stood there, two middle-aged conspirators wrestling silently over a cooking vessel, and then both of them laughed at once and let it fall into the basin with a crash that startled them both.

"I burned them," Vess said quietly. "The ledgers. All of them. Every volume back to the founding." She said it like a confession, which it was, and an absolution, which it was trying to be. "Eleven days ago I fed four hundred years of the trade into the oven, page by page, and watched the names go up. Some of those names were debts. Some of them were the only proof certain people had ever existed."

"I know. I was there for the last of them."

"You were barely there for anything. You slept for two days. Your heart stopped twice while you slept, according to the physician I bribed not to tell me things I already knew." She touched his jaw, tilting his face toward the window light, examining him with the clinical tenderness of a woman who had trained poisoners and buried friends. "Your tongue is ruined, you understand that. Whatever the gods took when they took your immunity, they left the instrument behind. It works. It just doesn't *know* anything anymore. You'll taste wine and get wine."

"Terrifying."

"It is, actually." Vess released him and leaned against the basin, arms folded. "You spent thirty years being the most dangerous mouth in this kingdom, Paloma. Do you know how many people are still alive who remember that? The king's new taster will be dead within the month—old habits, old enemies—and when he is, someone will come looking for the man who survived every poison ever served to a crown. They'll want your tongue. Or your silence. Or your head on a wall."

"Let them come. I'll cook for them."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking." He picked up the pot again, because his hands wanted work. "I spent my whole life invisible, Vess. Invisible and safe and alone, tasting death in silence so that other people could live loudly. And it turns out—" He stopped. Started again. "It turns out the silence was the poison. All those years. I just couldn't taste it, because I'd built myself immune to everything except that."

Vess said nothing for a long time. In the courtyard beyond the window, Yara was teaching the kitchen girl's youngest to skip stones off the rain barrel, badly, laughing when she missed. Watching her sister-figure learn gravity.

"You gave up your protection for her," Vess said finally.

"I gave up my protection for the truth. She just happened to be carrying it."

"That's not what happened."

"No," Paloma admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

The dreams came at night, and in the dreams his tongue worked.

He dreamed of the king's supper table, the long candles, the stew steaming in its golden bowl, and he dipped the spoon and tasted arsenic, slow and patient, and woke before he could decide whether to say anything. He dreamed of the ledger hall, shelves of calfskin volumes stretching into dark, and ink crawling across pages by itself, writing entries for things that hadn't been traded yet. He dreamed of the wolf, except it wasn't a wolf anymore, it was a grey figure at the edge of firelight with a smile like the first new thing in a thousand years, and it never spoke in the dreams, only watched, patient as a debt.

On the sixth morning he woke from the supper dream with Yara sitting on the edge of his cot, watching him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of someone cataloguing symptoms.

"You cried out," she said.

"What did I say?"

"Nothing. That's what frightened me. You made the sound of a man swallowing."

Paloma sat up. His mouth tasted of copper and dream-arsenic, which meant nothing, which was the whole point of his new life: things tasting of themselves and meaning nothing at all. "I dreamed I was still employed."

"By whom?"

"Everyone. Anyone. Take your pick. The court, the guild, the mountain." He rubbed his face. "Thirty years of being needed by terrible institutions, little sister. It seems need leaves a residue."

Yara considered this. Then she got up, went to the kitchen, and came back with bread and honey and the last of the autumn apples, and put them on the cot beside him with the ceremony of a woman laying out an altar.

"Eat," she said. "Taste it wrong. Taste it like a man with no profession. Practice."

So he did. Bread, honey, apple. Ordinary flavors arriving one at a time, unaccompanied, asking nothing. Somewhere around the third bite he realized his hands had stopped shaking, and he couldn't say when.

* * *

On the ninth day, the visitor came.

Paloma heard the knock and knew before Vess answered it that it was trouble, not because his tongue told him anything—it told him nothing, faithfully, always—but because trouble knocked politely, and everyone else in Vess's orbit banged.

The man in the doorway wore merchant's clothes over courtier's shoulders, and he smiled with exactly the right number of teeth. Behind him, in the street, two others waited near a horse that was too good for the clothes its rider wore.

"Mistress Ashgrove," the man said. "And the famous taster. What luck. I bring compliments from persons who prefer to remain unnamed, and an inquiry."

"Inquiries cost money," Vess said pleasantly. "We're out of that business."

"Not an inquiry about trade." The man's eyes slid to Paloma. "An inquiry about services. There are persons in this city—unnamed, as I said—who have recently suffered certain losses. Debts erased. Contracts voided. Names returned to their owners without compensation. These persons feel a great absence where their advantages used to be, and they are told the man responsible is a former taster whose tongue, remarkably, still functions in the old way despite reports to the contrary."

"My tongue," Paloma said, "tastes soup."

"So we've heard. And yet." The man produced, from inside his coat, a small sealed flask, and set it on the doorstep stone between them like a challenge or an offering. "A simple test. Identify what's in this, and the unnamed persons will know the reports are false. Fail to identify it, and they'll know the reports are true, and leave you in the peace you've purchased. Either way, everyone learns something."

Vess's hand found Paloma's arm. *Don't,* said the hand.

Paloma looked at the flask. Green glass, wax seal, the kind sold in apothecary rows to men with more money than patience. He thought of thirty years of swallowed dangers. He thought of a god's voice saying *you'll have to do it for free.* He thought of bread tasting like bread.

He picked up the flask, broke the seal, and drank all of it.

The merchant-courtier's smile collapsed through several expressions in rapid succession, ending somewhere near horror.

"Bitter," Paloma said. "Herbal. Probably harmless. Definitely rude." He handed back the empty flask. "Tell your unnamed persons that the taster is dead. Tell them the man standing here cooks for his sister and scrubs pots for an old woman who pays him in criticism. And tell them—" he smiled, and it was the first smile in his life that had never been part of a job— "tell them the age of buying things that aren't yours to sell is over. Something very large and very old checked the books. It found them wanting. Next time, it may not be in a reading mood."

The man left. The horse left. The street stayed empty for a long moment, and then filled again with ordinary noise, carts and children and someone arguing about fish prices, the sound of a world that had briefly held its breath and decided to go on breathing.

Vess stared at him. "That flask could have contained anything."

"It contained cowardice," Paloma said. "I've tasted enough of that to recognize the vintage."

"You don't *taste* anything anymore."

"No," he agreed. "But I remember everything. Turns out that was the skill all along. The tongue was just the excuse."

* * *

That evening Yara cooked.

It was a disaster. She had been a soul-candle for seven years, serving a subterranean court that ate light instead of food, and her hands remembered nothing useful and her temper remembered everything. She burned the onions, oversalted the broth, and dropped an entire pan of root vegetables on the floor, and then stood in the wreckage breathing hard while Paloma wisely said nothing.

"I used to be able to do this," she said. "Before. I cooked for us. When Father was alive and before—" She stopped, the way she always stopped at that word.

"Before the sale," Paloma finished, because they had agreed, in the mountain, on hard honesty: no more protecting each other from the shape of what happened. "Before Father sold you."

"Before Father sold me." She picked up the pan. Set it down again. "I keep waiting to feel like a person. Everyone keeps telling me I am one. But some evenings I feel like a candle somebody lit and forgot to blow out, and I stand here burning at nothing, and I think: is this all? Is this what I was saved *for*, onions?"

"No," Paloma said. "Onions are just the beginning. Trust me. I spent thirty years thinking I was a tongue, and it turned out I was a brother. We're both mislabeled. It takes time to grow into the real name."

Yara laughed—a short, wet, real laugh, the kind that costs something—and threw a dishcloth at his head, and he caught it, and they cleaned up the vegetables together, and the burnt smell stayed in the kitchen all night like a guest who'd earned the right to stay.

Later, much later, when Yara had gone to bed and the coals had settled, Paloma stood alone in the dark kitchen and whispered the prayer he'd been carrying for nine days without saying aloud.

It wasn't addressed to anyone in particular. That was the fashion now, among people who had seen gods rise out of mountains and read debts aloud in voices like avalanches: you prayed to whoever was listening, and trusted the listening to sort itself out.

*I don't ask for the tongue back,* he said. *I don't ask for the safety, or the invisibility, or any of it. I just—let her mornings keep coming. Let the candles stay blown out. Let me be ordinary long enough to learn what ordinary is for.*

The coals shifted in the grate.

And something answered—not a voice, not a word, nothing so expensive as that. Just a warmth that moved through the kitchen like a hand passing over bread dough, gentle and brief and gone, and the smell of the burnt onions lifted out of the room entirely, replaced by the clean green scent of the first day of spring, months early, impossible, kind.

Paloma stood very still in the dark.

Then, carefully, like a man handling the most dangerous substance of his career, he began to smile.
