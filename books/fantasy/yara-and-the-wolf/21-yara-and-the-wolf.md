## 21. Yara and the Wolf

The first thing I learned about being whole again was that the wind has a temperature.

That sounds like nothing. I know it sounds like nothing. But for years—years I cannot count, because the court beneath kept no seasons and permitted no forgetting that wasn't theirs—the world had been one long lukewarm. The flame I served as a candle burned at a heat designed by jailers: warm enough to keep a soul pliant, cool enough to keep it from ever dreaming of warmth elsewhere. When Paloma lifted me off that stand, when he carried me up through the collapsing road with my light guttering in his cupped hands like something he was afraid to breathe on, I had assumed that everything after would feel like more of the same. More warmth, maybe. Better warmth.

I had not remembered cold. I had not remembered that wind could find the gap between your collar and your neck and slide in like a knife made of weather, and that you would *shiver*, and that shivering was the body's way of saying *you are here, you are still here.*

So on the first morning of my second life, I stood on Vess's kitchen step and let the wind cut me and I wept about it, which frightened the kitchen girl who had prayed to a forgotten god once and been answered with abundance. She brought me bread. I ate it slowly, because eating slowly was also new, and because I had spent so long being consumed that I wanted to be the one doing the consuming for a change, deliberately, at my own pace, tasting every crumb.

My name is Yara Almeida. I say it to myself each morning before I open my eyes. Some days the name comes easily, bright as a bell. Other days it arrives late, limping, and I lie there in the dark of Vess's spare room holding still until it finds me, because I have sworn an oath to myself that I will never again be somewhere without knowing what to call me.

Paloma understands this. He does not rush me. He is a man who spent his whole life noticing everything and saying nothing, and now that he says things, he says them carefully, the way he used to taste poison—one small sip, wait, watch, decide. He has lost his tongue's gift. He told me once, in his flat quiet way, that plain bread tastes like plain bread now and he still hasn't gotten over the wonder of it. He says the word *ordinary* the way other men say a lover's name.

We are learning to be people together, which is its own kind of difficult, because neither of us had much practice before.

* * *

The wolf came back in autumn, when the hills above the city turned the color of old brass.

I was gathering rowan berries—I had developed a passion for picking things, for the small violent satisfaction of *this belongs to me now, I am taking it*—when I heard the grass move behind me in a way grass does not move when it is only wind. I turned.

It sat at the edge of the clearing, enormous, grey as the underside of clouds, watching me with eyes that were not a wolf's eyes. They were patient eyes. Old eyes. Eyes like a man who has waited out centuries and expects to wait out several more.

"Hello," I said. My voice did not shake. This surprised me. Perhaps wholeness includes that: the steadiness of someone who has already been eaten and come back.

*You are not afraid,* it said. Its voice arrived the way its eyes did—not through my ears but directly into the remembering part of me, the part that had once been a soul-candle and knew the language of things without bodies.

"I'm afraid," I said honestly. "But not of you. Not exactly. It's more that—" I gestured with my berry basket at the hills, the sky, the entire outrageous fact of existence. "Everything is very large lately. You're included."

Something happened around its mouth that I chose to interpret as a smile.

*Your brother freed me,* it said. *And I promised him, in the manner of my kind, which means the promise binds me whether I like it or not: I owe your family a debt. Debts are the bones of the old world. I would see them paid.*

"We don't want debts," I said. "Debts are what put me under the mountain. My father sold me against one. Paloma unmade the ledgers. We're finished with accounts."

The wolf tilted its great head, and I felt the weight of its attention like sunlight through a magnifying glass.

*There is a difference,* it said, *between a debt and a debt. Your father's bargain was a chain dressed in ledger ink. Mine is simpler. Someone gave me back my own shape. What is owed for that cannot be paid in coin or silence or souls. It can only be paid forward.*

"Paid how?"

*You were a candle,* it said. *You know what it is to burn for someone else's convenience. There are others like you. Names the guild took. Years it borrowed and never returned. Luck siphoned from cradles. The ledgers burned, yes—but burning a record does not return the thing recorded. Somewhere under this city and beyond it, people are walking around hollow and don't know why their lives taste of ash. The court above is broken. The guild's masters are judged or fled or ash themselves. No one is left with the craft to find them.* Its eyes settled on me. *No one is left who knows what it is to be a stolen thing and then to be found.*

The wind moved through the rowan tree. Berries dropped. I remember that specifically, the soft red patter of them on the grass, because in that moment something clicked into place in me with an almost audible sound—the click of a lock that had been waiting all my new life for the right key.

"I'll need my brother," I said.

*I counted on it,* said the wolf. *Loyalty runs in your blood like a river. I only showed it the sea.*

* * *

Paloma took some convincing, which surprised the wolf and did not surprise me at all.

He listened to the whole proposal standing very still in Vess's kitchen with his arms crossed, wearing the face he used to wear tasting the king's supper—that careful blankness that meant a storm was happening behind it. When the wolf finished, he said, "No," and began washing dishes.

"You haven't heard the terms—" I started.

"There are always terms." He scrubbed a pot with more force than crockery deserves. "Terms are how our family lost you the first time. Someone offers something beautiful and buries the price in the fine print, and the fine print is where I've lived my entire life. No."

"It's not a trade," I said. "It's work. Honest work. Returning things."

He set the pot down. His hands were still. That was how I knew how much this mattered to him—Paloma's hands went still when he was most moved; they only shook over trivia.

"Returning things," he repeated softly.

"That's what I said."

"You know what I did for twenty years?" He didn't wait for me to answer. "I kept books of theft. Beautiful books. Entries for traded years, borrowed luck, souls sold under names almost recognizable. And I filed it all away without accusation, because filing without accusation was the only way to survive, and I told myself survival was enough. Then I burned those books, and I thought: that's the end of it. Ash. Finished." He looked out the window toward the hills, toward the wolf that was, presumably, pretending not to listen with its whole ancient attention. "But a record isn't the thing. Burning the map doesn't give back the country. Is that what you're telling me?"

"Yes," I said. "Exactly that."

He was quiet a long moment. Then he picked the pot back up and said, "Fine. But I make the ledgers this time, and I make them the way they should have been made from the start: entries only for things given back. A ledger of returns. Every name restored gets a line, and every line gets closed, and closed lines get torn out and burned, because a debt that's paid shouldn't keep existing on paper." He pointed the dripping ladle at the wolf through the window. "And nobody—god, human, or otherwise—tells me never to read the entries aloud. Ever again."

Through the glass, the wolf's shoulders did something that in a dog would have been a shrug and in a god was probably consent.

*Agreed,* it said into all our heads at once, and Vess, coming in from the yard with an armload of firewood, dropped half of it and said, "If we are starting a trade in impossible restorations out of my kitchen, someone owes me rent," which is how Vess joined, though she'd deny to her dying day that she joined anything.

* * *

Our first restoration was a cooper's son named Ansel Ferro, whose luck had been borrowed at birth and never returned—which is why, the wolf explained, he had survived two shipwrecks and a collapsing kiln and yet could never hold onto a copper, a friend, or a good night's sleep.

Finding him was craft-work, and it turned out we had craft. Paloma remembered the guild's methods from the inside: how stolen luck leaves a signature, a thinness in the air around the unlucky, like a room where furniture was removed and the floorboards show paler rectangles where it stood. He couldn't taste poison anymore, but it turned out his tongue hadn't gone entirely ordinary—it could taste *absence*, which is nearly the same gift inverted, and far more useful for our purposes.

Mine was stranger. I had been a flame. I had burned in the court beneath as a living candle, and something of that remained: I could feel taken things. Hollow people pulled at me faintly, the way a missing tooth pulls at the tongue. I would walk through a market and feel a tug toward a woman mending nets who had sold seven years of her life to pay a landlord, and a tug toward a boy who could no longer remember his grandmother's face because his name for her had been entered in a ledger and traded to a man who wanted to forget his own crimes.

The returning itself required both of us. Paloma would write the entry—the name, the thing taken, the taker when we could find them—and I would speak it aloud, because speaking aloud was forbidden in the old guild and therefore, we reasoned, exactly correct for the new one. And when the name was spoken, the wolf would do whatever wolves do at the bottom of old bargains. It would simply look at the place where the theft lived, the way you look hard at a knot, and the knot would come undone.

Ansel Ferro got his luck back on a Tuesday. He cried in our presence for an hour and then bought us dinner at the tavern he'd been thrown out of eleven times, where the landlady—who had once refused him credit—kept refilling his cup for free and calling him *love*, and he kept looking at her like she'd handed him the moon.

"That," Paloma said to me quietly, across the table, watching Ansel laugh at a joke for perhaps the first time in his adult life, "is better than plain bread."

"It's better than most things," I agreed.

* * *

We worked through winter. Word travels strangely when the work itself is strange—a fishwife tells a ferryman, a ferryman tells a priest, a priest tells a penitent, and soon people were finding their way up the hill path to Vess's kitchen with that specific hesitance of the hollow, asking for the brother and sister who *give things back.*

Some nights I dreamed of the court beneath. In the dreams the warding flame relit itself, and I stood on the stand again, and the jailers walked past, and I could not remember my name. On those nights I would get up, go to the window, and look at the hills until I saw the wolf—a grey shape moving along the ridgeline, free, unbound, answering to no one. It never came to the window. It never had to. Seeing it run was enough. *Neither pet nor prisoner,* I would think, and the thought would settle over me like a blanket. There was a free thing in the world. Therefore freedom existed. Therefore I was in it.

Paloma had harder nights. He never spoke of Cassel, his protector, whom he destroyed by delivering the truth the wolf had packaged for him—but I would find him at dawn sitting at the table with a closed line torn from the returns-ledger, ready for burning, and I learned not to ask whose guilt he was feeding to the fire that morning alongside the paper.

Guilt, I have decided, is just loyalty that got lost and is trying to find its way home. Our whole new trade was built on that idea, really: taking the love that had once curdled into silence, into complicity, into ledgers of theft, and turning it back toward its true purpose. Loyalty and truth finally pointing the same direction. It had taken a soul sold, a god unbound, and a court brought down to align them. We intended to keep them aligned one name at a time.

* * *

Spring came early that year, or perhaps the freed gods were generous with it. The hills went green overnight, aggressive green, showing-off green.

On the first warm evening, the wolf came down from the ridge and walked with us—Paloma on its left, me on its right, the three of us moving through the rowan grove like the strangest procession the valley had ever seen. It had grown no smaller. Freed gods don't ration themselves.

*A question,* it said presently. *Asked of you both, and answer honestly, because honesty is the only currency I respect since I stopped accepting souls.*

"Ask," said Paloma.

*When the last entry closes—when every stolen year is home and every hollow person is full—what will you be?*

We walked a while without answering. Below us the city lit its lamps one by one, and I thought about the woman who got her seven years back and immediately used one of them to plant an orchard, because that is what returned time is for: extravagant, useless, beautiful things.

"I'll be a man who cooks," Paloma said finally. "For my sister. Badly at first, then less badly. Ordinary food, tasted by no one but us."

*And you?* The great head turned to me.

I watched the hills going blue with dusk and thought about the girl I had been before the mountain—the one our father sold, the one who had a name and then a number and then a flame and then, impossibly, a name again. I thought about all the names in Paloma's ledger of returns, each one a person waking up whole, and about the ones we hadn't found yet, scattered past the city, past the hills, maybe past the mountains themselves—because the guild's trade, we were learning from the recovered fragments Vess kept smuggling home, had never been confined to one kingdom. There were other ledgers. Other courts. Other candles.

Somewhere out there, someone was standing in the dark not knowing what to call herself.

"I'll be busy," I said.

The wolf made a sound then that I felt in my ribs rather than my ears—a low rolling note like distant, friendly thunder—and I understood that I had answered correctly.

*Good,* it said. *Because I bring news, little candle, and you will want to sit down for it, though I know you hate that. There is a city across the salt water where the ledgers did not burn. And last night, on the wind, I caught a name being spoken there—a name in your voice, in your family's cadence, sold under it decades ago.* Its yellow eyes found mine, then Paloma's. *Someone with your blood is still burning.*
