## 22. One Dish at a Time

The kitchen was small enough that two people had to negotiate the space like dancers, and that, Paloma thought, was the whole point of it.

He'd chosen the house in the lower town for the kitchen first and the rooms second. It had a window over the basin that caught the morning light, a stove that drew properly if you coaxed the flue, and a door that locked — though neither of them bothered much with locks anymore. What was there to lock out? The guild's ledgers were ash. The guild's masters were scattered or answering questions before tribunals of old gods, which was a sentence he would not have believed himself capable of writing even in his own head a year ago. And the court above had a new king, or rather an old king stripped down to something almost honest by the judgment that had passed through the palace like a flood through a granary, taking the spoiled grain and leaving the rest.

Paloma did not think about the court much anymore. That was the strangest mercy of his new life: he had the luxury of not thinking.

"Yara," he said, "you're holding the onion like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money." She didn't look up from the cutting board. Her knife work was still clumsy — she'd only been a person again for eight months, and hands remembered being candles longer than they remembered anything else — but she was stubborn in the way of someone relearning the world on her own terms. "Everything owed me money. Eight years of wages, back interest, compensation for emotional suffering."

"You can't bill an onion."

"I can bill whoever put an onion in front of me." She hacked it in half. Her eyes watered. She refused to acknowledge this. "There. A massacre. Are you proud?"

"I'm proud," he said, and found that it was true, and that the truth of it sat in his chest without weight, the way good things apparently could when you let them.

* * *

He cooked the way he used to taste — with total attention, total presence, and now, for the first time, with pleasure.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, losing an immunity. He had traded it to the wolf at the mountain's mouth without hesitation, because the arithmetic had never been arithmetic at all: one tongue that could detect any poison ever brewed against one sister's soul. Any taster in the kingdom would have made the trade drunk. But he hadn't understood until the winter passed what else went with it. His tongue was ordinary now. Bread tasted like bread. Wine tasted like wine or, if it was bad, like vinegar — he couldn't tell arsenic from almond, nightshade from nutmeg, and every meal was therefore an act of faith.

He had expected to be afraid. He wasn't. Or rather, he was afraid the way everyone else was afraid, which turned out to be survivable. Everyone else had been doing it their whole lives — eating food they hadn't tested, trusting cooks they'd never met, drinking well water on faith. It was like discovering that the whole world had been walking on a tightrope all along, casually, without him, and that falling off was rare enough to be a story rather than an expectation.

His invisibility, too, was gone. Not magically — it had never been magic, only craft, only the discipline of being unremarkable — but the habit had died with the purpose. These days he shopped at the market and the fishwife knew his name and argued with him about mackerel. These days he laughed in public, at full volume, and people looked over, and it didn't feel like the moment before an execution. It felt like being one voice among many, which was apparently what belonging sounded like from the inside.

"Smell this," Yara said, thrusting a spoon at him.

He smelled it. Bay, stock, the sweetness of onion done slowly, a little too much salt because she still didn't trust her own hand. "More thyme," he said. "And it's your stew, so if you want it salty, let it be salty."

"It shouldn't be salty just because I want it to be. There are correct amounts of things."

"Says the woman who set fire to bread twice last month."

"The oven is treacherous." She added the thyme anyway, grudgingly, and stirred, and the smell that came up off the pot was so simple and so complete that Paloma had to turn away toward the window for a moment and pretend to check the light.

Eight months ago she had not known her own name. He had carried her soul-candle up out of the dark in his two hands, cupping the flame against the wind of the collapsing road, and for three days afterward she had been a person-shaped absence — she could walk, speak, follow instructions, but nothing stuck, no name held, no memory gathered moss. Vess had said souls were like soil after a flood; everything was still there, it just needed time to settle. So they had settled her. Slowly. One dish at a time, one name at a time, one memory returned to its shelf. The day she'd remembered their mother's lullaby she'd cried for six hours and then demanded dinner as though nothing happened, and Paloma had understood that she was going to be fine — not healed, maybe never entirely, but *fine*, in the way that meant the wound lived in the past tense instead of the present one.

* * *

In the afternoon he walked to the market, which he did most days now, partly for provisions and partly because he liked being a man with errands.

The town was still putting itself back together. The judgment of the old gods had not destroyed the court — that had surprised everyone, Paloma included. It had *audited* it. Debts read aloud in voices like landslide, ledgers balanced in public, the worst offenders given over to whatever the gods did with the truly guilty (nobody asked twice), and the merely corrupt made to repay, year by year, favor by favor, name by name. The king's new master of kitchens was a woman who had once been a scullery maid and who treated poisoners the way a good cook treated weevils: found, removed, prevented. Paloma had met her once. She'd shaken his hand and said, "You're the one who spoke at the feast," and he'd said, "I'm the one who used to be invisible," and she'd laughed and hired him as a consultant anyway, which he'd declined, politely, because he was done tasting for other people's safety forever.

But there was work that remained. There was always work that remained. That was the trade he and Yara were building in the ruins of the old one: returning what the guild stole. Names, mostly. Years, sometimes. Luck, when they could find whose it had been. The guild's ledgers were ash, burned by Vess's hand in the last loyal act of a long compromised life, but ash remembers its shape, and between Yara's half-remembered knowledge of the court below and Paloma's ten years of bookkeeping, they were reconstructing entries one at a time. Somewhere in the hills there was a shepherd walking around with thirty borrowed years of luck that belonged to a miller's son. Somewhere a noblewoman wore a name that had been bought off a drowned child. They found them. Slowly. One name at a time.

Vess herself kept the shop now — a small stationer's, of all things, selling paper and ink to people who would use them for nothing worse than letters. She and Paloma were not what they had been. Forgiveness, he'd learned, was not a door you walked through but a room you swept daily. But she came to Sunday dinners, and she always brought honey cakes, and she always ate first from every dish she brought, ostentatiously, meeting his eyes, and he always laughed, and it got a little easier each time.

At the greengrocer's he bought leeks, because Yara had developed an opinion about leeks, and opinions were the surest sign of a soul settling.

* * *

Evening. The stew went on the table with bread and butter and the last of the apple preserves, and they ate like ordinary people, which is to say greedily and while talking.

"—and then Marta says, *that's not how you salt a fish*, and I say, Marta, I was a candle for eight years, my standards are extremely low, and she laughs so hard she drops a whole cod—"

"You tell that story constantly."

"It gets better every time. Like wine." She pointed her spoon at him. "You tell the wolf story constantly, so don't start."

"I do not tell the wolf story constantly."

"You told the baker's daughter the wolf story. She's nine."

"She asked what was under the mountain. I gave an age-appropriate summary."

"You told a nine-year-old that the old god of the mountain has *patient eyes* and then couldn't explain what that meant. She had nightmares."

"That's theology," Paloma said. "It's supposed to be unsettling."

Yara snorted into her stew. Outside, the light was going amber over the rooftops, and somewhere down the street a dog was barking at absolutely nothing, and a cart rattled past, and the whole ordinary machinery of an ordinary evening ground happily along. Paloma ate his stew — his sister's stew, oversalted, overthymed, perfect — and felt the particular fullness that no amount of royal banquets had ever given him, because banquet-fullness was about abundance and this was about something else entirely. This was about the fact that every person at the table had chosen to be there.

Afterward she washed and he dried, because that was the division of labor and neither of them trusted the other with the opposite half. Through the window over the basin the first stars were coming out. Above them, faint behind the evening haze, the shoulder of the mountain stood dark against the sky.

"Do you ever miss it?" Yara asked suddenly. She was scrubbing the pot with more force than the pot required. "The tasting. Being able to know. You used to know everything — what was safe, what was poisoned, who wanted whom dead. Now you eat soup on faith like the rest of us."

Paloma considered the question honestly, because she deserved honest answers now; honesty was the household religion.

"I miss knowing," he admitted. "Sometimes. When the wine tastes odd and I can't tell if it's corked or cursed. When Vess brings honey cakes and some ancient reflex wants to test them before she eats hers." He set a plate in the rack. "But missing it is like missing a wall I used to live inside. The wall kept things out. It also kept me in. And I spent ten years pressed up against it, listening, learning everything and saying nothing, and I called that survival, and it was, but it wasn't living. This—" he gestured with the towel at the kitchen, the steam, the sister, the whole small lit room of it, "—this is living. Even the parts where the soup might kill me."

"The soup is not going to kill you."

"The soup is genuinely mediocre. Those are different problems."

She threw a dishrag at his head. He caught it, because some skills survive the loss of immunity, and they were both laughing when the knock came at the door.

* * *

It was the miller's son — the one whose stolen years they'd recovered in the autumn — come to bring them a sack of fresh-ground flour and stay for gossip, and after him it was just the two of them again, the dishes done, the lamp low, the house settling into its nighttime creaks.

Paloma banked the stove and stood a moment in the middle of the kitchen, doing nothing, being no one in particular, and found that he loved it.

Old habits die hard, though, and the oldest of his habits rose in him now the way it always did at the end of the day, quietly, in the space between waking and sleep. He leaned his forehead briefly against the cool stone of the windowsill — the way he'd once leaned against the palace walls, listening for what moved beneath — and whispered the prayer he'd been whispering since the mountain, the one he'd never dared pray aloud before the world broke open:

*Keep her. Keep this. Let it be enough.*

Nothing answered, of course. Nothing thundered, nothing shifted, no ink crawled across any page. He was a powerless man in a small kitchen, and the world ran on flour and weather and human stubbornness.

Except.

Except that the flue, which always needed coaxing, settled itself with a soft sigh, drawing clean. Except that the banked coals glowed steady and warm instead of guttering, holding their heat like a promise kept. Except that through the window, above the dark shoulder of the mountain, one star burned a little brighter than the others — steady, patient, watching — and Paloma felt, distinctly and without alarm, the sensation of being noticed by something vast and being found, for once in his life, entirely unobjectionable.

Something kind, then. Something that remembered a man who freed it and a girl who offered herself back, and who had chosen otherwise, and who now washed dishes in a small house in the lower town.

"Goodnight," Paloma said to the star, feeling foolish, meaning it completely.

The star did not answer. But the warmth held, all night long, and in the morning the bread rose higher than it had any right to, and Yara took one bite and frowned at him suspiciously and said, "What did you *do* to this?" — and Paloma Almeida, former taster, former ledger-keeper, former invisible man, current brother, current cook, current resident of the visible world, just smiled and passed her the butter.
