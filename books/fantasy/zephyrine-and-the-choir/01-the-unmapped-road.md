## 1. The Unmapped Road

The pass didn't exist. That was the whole point of it.

Cyrus Vane stood at the mouth of the Hollow Stair and listened to the mountain breathe. Thornfell did that at this hour—exhaled cold air up through its cracks and vents as the sun warmed the peaks above, a long slow sigh that smelled of wet slate and pine resin. Most travelers heard nothing. Cyrus had spent twenty-nine years learning to hear everything.

Behind her, the caravan waited: eleven mules, nine men, one nervous fence named Corvas who kept touching the ledger inside his coat like a talisman. Salt, iron nails, and thirty bolts of grey silk that had never been within a hundred miles of any loom the tax collectors knew about. Good cargo. Quiet cargo. The kind that wanted to stay invisible almost as much as she did.

"The map says there's no road here," Corvas said for the fourth time.

"Then throw away the map."

"I'm not going to throw away a perfectly good—"

"Corvas." She turned, letting him see her face in the grey pre-dawn light: sharp-boned, weathered, unremarkable except for the eyes, which were the flat patient grey of river stone and missed nothing. "Every map you've ever bought is a lie told on purpose. Surveyors draw what they're paid to draw. Cartographers leave out whatever their patrons want left out. That's not corruption, that's just ink doing what ink does." She tapped the rolled tube slung across his back. "What I sell isn't maps. It's the corrections."

"And the corrections cost how much?"

"More than you paid."

He swallowed. Good. A man who understood he was being robbed politely was far easier to lead through dangerous country than a man who thought he was getting a bargain.

Cyrus turned back to the Stair. From below it looked like nothing—a fault line in the cliff face, choked with scree and deadfall, too narrow to be a pass, too steep to be worth clearing. Officially, the Royal Survey of 1147 declared this entire section of Thornfell "geologically unstable, impassable, of no strategic or commercial interest." She'd read the original survey notes once, in an archive in Lowmark, and found the surveyor's marginal note: *No route here. Do not look again.* Someone had paid for those words. Someone always did.

But the mountain itself disagreed with the ink. The scree here was sorted—fine gravel on the east side where water sorted it, big blocks on the west where gravity dropped them—and water only sorts stone if water has been running a very long time along a path someone keeps clear. The deadfall had been cut, not fallen; the saw-marks were old but honest, hidden under moss by people who understood that the best disguise for a maintained road is a road that looks abandoned.

Her mother's people had cut this pass. Or her grandmother's. Nobody had written down who, because writing things down was how you got hanged, and Cyrus's family had been professionally unwritten for longer than anyone could prove. She kept the knowledge the way she kept everything: nowhere. In her feet, her hands, the back of her throat when she tasted certain airs. There was no document anywhere in the world with her name on it and a true address beside it. She had checked. Repeatedly. With ladders and lockpicks and, once, a very long night in the census house at Grebe's Ford.

That was the trade. You could know every secret path through the mountains, and no path at all back to belonging.

She started climbing.

* * *

The Hollow Stair earned its name two hours in, where the fault line narrowed and the route dropped into a slot canyon barely wider than her shoulders. Overhead the rock closed to a ragged seam of white sky. The mules came through in single file, breath steaming, hooves finding purchase Cyrus had tested herself twice last month—one of many trips she made before taking coin, because the difference between a smuggler and a corpse was usually just preparation.

"Hand on the rope," she called back. "Left foot high, trust the ledge, don't trust your eyes. Your eyes will tell you there's nothing under you. Your eyes are wrong."

One by one they came through, swearing softly, and one by one the ledge held them, because she had known it would. She had known it the way she knew the sound a slope makes before it slides—a low subterranean click, like a knuckle cracking—or the way morning mist behaves over a hollow full of old mine workings (it pools, it never pools over solid ground). Twenty-nine years of listening. No letters after her name, no guild card, no apprenticeship papers. Just the whole geography of Thornfell folded up somewhere behind her sternum.

At midday they stopped in a hanging valley that appeared on no chart, ringed by three peaks and floored with grass gone silver in the wind. There was a spring here, and a low stone shelter roofed with turf, older than any kingdom currently claiming these mountains. Somebody's grandmother had built it. Somebody's somebody.

Corvas sat against the wall, ate hard sausage, and studied her with the frank rudeness of a man doing arithmetic.

"No name," he said finally. "I asked around before I hired you. Vane, they said. Just Cyrus Vane. No family holding, no village, no debts, no guild. In my business that's either very good or very bad."

"It's neither. It's expensive."

"Everyone's from somewhere."

"Not everyone." She drank from the spring. Cold enough to ache in her teeth. "Some of us are from nowhere on purpose. Nowhere is a career."

"What happens when you're done? When you're too slow for the Stair, too old for the passes. Where does a woman from nowhere retire to?"

The question landed harder than it should have. She let the silence stretch until he looked uncomfortable, then said, "Ask me in thirty years," and went to check the mules' feet.

But she thought about it the rest of the afternoon, while the caravan wound down the far side of the pass along ledges she navigated by memory alone—the split boulder shaped like a tooth, the cairn with a third stone missing (she always put it back; someone else always knocked it off), the place where the trail kinked left for no visible reason because the visible reason was a sinkhole that had eaten a horse in 1102, according to no record whatsoever. She knew all of it. Every switchback, every seep, every cave with good air and every cave with bad. What she didn't know was a single door in the world that would open to her without payment. Her life was a series of transactions conducted flawlessly with people who would forget her face within a season. She made herself unforgettable for exactly as long as the job required, and then she erased herself again, stroke by careful stroke.

It worked. It had always worked. She told herself the small tight feeling under her ribs was just hunger, and shared out the last of the dried apricots.

* * *

They came down out of the high country at dusk, through a fold in the foothills that delivered them onto the cart-road south of Thornfell's Toe as neatly as a key turning a lock. Below, the valley lights were coming on—Lampwick Crossing, a customs town, fat and blind and glowing. On the official maps, the road they now stood on connected to Lampwick by a detour of six miles east. In truth it connected directly, and had for two centuries, and the toll station at the crossing watched a road that nobody used.

"We'll come in from the north side after full dark," Cyrus told Corvas. "The customs men rotate watches at moonrise. There'll be eleven minutes when the north post is empty because young Aldery sees his girl on those nights and thinks no one knows."

"How do you—"

"Because I'm the one who told her which nights his shift runs late." She shouldered her pack. "Eleven minutes. Move like you mean it and we're drinking in Lampwick by midnight with stamped papers for cargo that was never inspected."

Corvas laughed, delighted and unsettled at once, the way clients always were. This was the product, really—not the passes but this feeling, this vertigo of learning the world had seams. People paid her for the same sensation children get peeking behind a stage curtain. She took their coin and never told them the price she paid to keep the curtain knowledge: that a person who lives behind the stage stops appearing in any story the audience can see.

They moved at full dark. The watch rotation ran exactly as she'd promised—she'd have been more surprised if it hadn't—and eleven minutes proved generous. By the time the moon cleared the eastern ridge they were through, silk and salt and nails, papers stamped by a customs officer who waved them past with the bored hand of a man guarding the wrong road.

Corvas paid her in a lamp-lit room above the Soldered Kettle, counting coins onto the table with genuine reverence. "You're wasted on this," he said. "You could be surveyor general with that head."

"A surveyor general has a name in a ledger," Cyrus said, "and a house with her name on the door, and enemies who can read." She swept the coins into her pouch. "I prefer being weather. Weather doesn't get arrested."

He laughed. She didn't.

* * *

She stayed the night anyway—rare for her, but the descent had been hard and even mountains need recovering from—and took a room at the Kettle under the name Mistral, one of her rotating stock. In the morning she meant to walk north again, three days to her cache at Greywacke Saddle, and she was downstairs before dawn with tea, planning the route out of pure habit, when the bookbinder's shop across the lane caught her eye.

It hadn't been there before.

That was nonsense, of course. Shops didn't appear. But she knew Lampwick Crossing the way she knew everything in these mountains—which buildings leaned, which cellars flooded, which businesses changed hands—and she could swear on every false name she owned that this storefront had been a cobbler's last spring, shuttered, its sign blank. Now a painted board hung there: *Quill & Crease — Bindings, Repairs, Appraisals.* Lamplight inside, though the hour was early. A figure moving behind the glass, arranging books.

Cyrus stood in the street with her tea going cold and felt the particular prickle she trusted more than any map: the feeling of a route that shouldn't connect connecting. She had a rule about such feelings. The rule was *go toward it*, because in her experience the mountain only hid things from people unwilling to look.

She crossed the lane and pushed open the door. A bell rang, small and silver. Inside smelled of glue and leather dust and something fainter underneath—old paper, the specific dry sweetness of pages a century removed from the tree. Shelves rose floor to ceiling. And everywhere, everywhere, books: ledgers, almanacs, registers, journals bound in calfskin and buckram and cracked boards, thousands of records of thousands of lives.

Exactly the kind of place she had spent her life avoiding.

The figure behind the counter was a tall, spare person of indeterminate age with ink-stained fingers and an expression of mild professional welcome. "Early customer," they said. "Rare thing. Looking for anything particular?"

"Wrong direction," Cyrus said. "I'm looking to be unrecorded, not recorded."

"Mm. Then you've come to the wrong shop twice over." The binder smiled without warmth. "This is where records come to be born."

Against her better judgment—against every rule she had—Cyrus's eye snagged on the counter display: a single ledger, ancient, its spine re-sewn with modern thread, lying open under a brass reading lamp. Prophecy registries, the faded label said. Northern ranges. Centennial entries.

She should have walked out. She had built her whole existence out of walking out.

Instead she leaned over the counter, and the lamplight fell across the page, and there it was—tonight's date, written a hundred years ago in brown old ink, beside a line of prophecy in a hand like drifting smoke. She read the words once. Then again. The shop went very quiet, the way the Hollow Stair went quiet right before the rock decided something.

*On the night of the Choir's singing, the Undercourt shall claim the soul sold to it — and it shall be hers.*

And beneath that, in slightly different ink, a name she had never seen written anywhere, because she had never known it existed. Her name. Not Cyrus. Something older, stranger, hers the way a scar is hers—

"That item," the binder said gently, "is not for sale."

Cyrus Vane—who was perhaps not Cyrus Vane at all—looked up from the page into the lamplight, and her invisibility, the life's work of twenty-nine years, lay in pieces around her feet like a shattered pane of ice.

"Then tell me," she said, "who sold it."
