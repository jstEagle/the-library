## 2. A Name in Old Ink

The rain came down the mountain like it had somewhere to be, and Cyrus Vane let it chase her into Thornfell's lower town with the rest of the soaked and miserable.

She hated coming down. The town was a knot of official roads and official names, a place where every door was written down somewhere by someone who got paid to notice things. Up in the passes she was a ghost with excellent boots. Down here she was just another wet traveler, and wet travelers got remembered.

But the caravan master had paid her in coin and one favor, and the favor was this: three days in town before the next run north. Three days to resupply, to sell the sketches she'd made of the high passes—under a false name, through a false buyer, always—and to sleep in an actual bed at Marla's boarding house, where the stew was good and nobody asked questions because Marla had trained her not to answer them.

Three days. She could be invisible for three days. She'd been invisible for twenty-six years.

The bookbinder's shop sat on Tallow Lane, wedged between a chandler and a shop that sold nothing but locks, which Cyrus had always found honest of it. The sign read **QUILL & VELLUM**, though the quill had faded to a brown smear and the vellum had been re-lettered so many times it looked like a scar. She'd been in twice before, both times to offload maps that needed a professional eye before they went to buyers who paid in silver instead of suspicion.

The bell over the door announced her, which she disliked on principle.

Inside smelled of glue and leather and the particular dustiness of old paper—the smell of other people's lives pressed flat. The proprietor, a narrow man named Fenwick with ink-stained fingers and spectacles thick as bottle bottoms, looked up from his workbench and did the thing everyone did: looked *through* her. She'd spent years cultivating that. Plain coat, plain face, a way of standing that suggested she was furniture that had wandered indoors. It worked on Fenwick. He nodded once and went back to his binding.

Cyrus drifted along the shelves, ostensibly browsing. Really she was waiting out the rain, and really-really she was killing time until Marla's stew, and really-really-really—

Later, she would try to reconstruct why she stopped at that shelf. There was no reason. It was the reject shelf, the one where Fenwick put damaged stock: water-warbled ledgers, books with broken spines, estate lots nobody wanted. She'd walked past it both previous visits without a glance.

Today she glanced.

The ledger at the end of the row was fat as a Bible and bound in green leather gone black at the corners, its spine cracked in three places. Someone had tried to repair it once, badly—a strip of modern cloth glued across the worst break, already peeling. The title on the front had been stamped in gold that time had eaten down to ghosts:

**REGISTRAR OF THE THORNFELL PROPHECIES, VOL. III**

Cyrus knew about the prophecy ledgers the way everyone in the mountains knew about them—as a story, a warning, a thing grandmothers invoked when children wandered too near cave mouths. A century ago, some self-important clerk of the old Thornfell courts had gone around collecting predictions from hedge-witches and mad hermits and writing them all down in enormous books, cross-indexed by date, as if the future were a delivery schedule. Most of the volumes had burned in the courthouse fire thirty years back. The ones that survived were scattered in private collections or, apparently, rotting on a bookbinder's reject shelf in the lower town.

She should have kept walking. Every instinct she owned said keep walking, you don't touch indexed things, indexed things are how people get found.

Her hand touched it anyway.

The leather was cold. That was the first wrongness, though she didn't register it as such yet—just a small animal note of *this has been sitting near stone*, which made no sense in a shop above ground. She opened the cover. The flyleaf held an ownership inscription in a copperplate hand: *Property of the Office of Records, Thornfell Township. If found, return under penalty of law.*

Under penalty of law. She almost smiled. Half the roads she drew didn't exist under penalty of law either.

The ledger was organized exactly as the stories claimed: dates down the left margin, prophecies entered beside them in a dozen different hands, some careful, some scrawled, one that appeared to have been written by someone holding the pen in their teeth. Fenwick's price sticker said two coppers. Two coppers for a century of other people's doom.

She flipped pages, half-reading. *On the third of Frostmoon, the miller's second son shall drown in dry land.* *When the twin stars cross, a red winter.* The usual. Hedge-witch weather and livestock omens, the kind of vague menace that fit any year if you squinted. She turned faster, skimming dates, letting the century scroll backward under her thumb. Rain ticked against the window. Fenwick's paste brush whispered. Somewhere outside a cart groaned past.

Then her thumb stopped.

Not because she decided to stop. Her thumb stopped the way a foot stops when it finds a stair that isn't there.

The entry was dated tonight. Not the month, not the season—*the day*. The seventeenth of Thawmonth, in the current year, written down a hundred years ago by a hand that slanted hard to the right, pressing deep enough to emboss the next page. She could feel the grooves with her fingertip, the way you can feel a grave marker worn smooth.

Beside the date, in that same gouging hand:

*On this night the Choir beneath sings its hundredth year, and the court below will call in what is owed them. The soul sold at the midwife's fire comes due. Seek the girl called Vane, who is not Vane. Her true name is Zephyrine, and Zephyrine will walk down of her own will or be carried.*

The shop went very quiet. Or the world did. Rain still hit the glass; Fenwick still brushed his paste; but all of it moved behind a pane of something, far away, unimportant, while Cyrus stood in a puddle of her own making and read the line again. And again. And a fourth time, hunting for a loophole, some alternate reading, the way she hunted for a second exit in every room she entered.

*The girl called Vane, who is not Vane.*

Vane was the name she'd built herself, brick by brick, at fourteen, when she'd realized the name she'd grown up with was itself a coat she could take off. Before that she'd been Cy, short for nothing anyone would say aloud, raised above Marla's boarding house with no surname and no story and a landlady who went pale whenever anyone asked where the girl came from. Vane had been her first act of authorship. She'd chosen it because it sounded like nothing, meant nothing, pointed at nothing. Weathervane. Whatever way the wind blew.

And here was a dead man's pen saying: that name is a lie, and we know the real one.

*Zephyrine.*

She mouthed it without sound. It tasted foreign and familiar at once, like a word from a language she'd spoken as an infant. Something behind her sternum answered it—not memory, nothing so useful, but a resonance, a tuning fork struck against bone. Her body knew the shape of it even if her life didn't.

*Zephyrine will walk down of her own will or be carried.*

Down. Down where? Everyone in Thornfell knew what lived under the mountain, in the loose, don't-say-it-aloud way people know things: the Undercourt, the gentry below, the fair folk or whatever you wanted to call them, who traded in music and memory and took payment in ways no magistrate could adjudicate. Smugglers had rules about them. Cyrus had rules about them, and rule one was *don't*, and rule two was *don't*, and every rule after that was a variation.

*The soul sold at the midwife's fire.*

Midwife. The word snagged like a fishhook. Marla had been a midwife once—before the boarding house, before Cyrus. That was one of the few facts of her childhood Marla offered freely, usually as a deflection: *I caught half the babies in this valley, girl, I've no patience left for nonsense.* Cyrus had never thought anything of it. Why would she? Half the older women in Thornfell had caught babies.

A fire, though. She searched her memory for a fire and found only smoke—an image, not a memory. Smoke and a woman's voice singing low, and warmth, and being carried. She'd assumed it was a dream she'd never outgrown. Twenty-six years old and still dreaming of smoke.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with detached professional interest, the way she'd note a rockfall sign: *unstable slope, proceed with caution.* Except there was no proceeding around this. You couldn't route around your own name.

"Fenwick." Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "Where did this come from?"

He looked up, blinking. "Eh? Oh—that lot came off the Harrow estate, upvalley. Whole library sold for debts. That one's water-damaged, see the warping? Two coppers and it's yours."

"Who wrote in it? The registrar?"

"Dead before my grandfather was born, by all accounts." Fenwick shrugged, returning to his spine. "Folk say he went mad near the end. Started claiming the entries weren't predictions at all. Said they were—" he waved his brush vaguely, "—invoices. Said the mountain kept accounts."

Invoices. The word landed in her stomach and stayed there, cold as the ledger's cover.

"And the Harrow estate," she said. "Upvalley. Near which pass?"

"The one that isn't on your maps, I'd wager." He chuckled at his own joke, having no idea it wasn't one.

She bought the ledger. Two coppers, plus a third so Fenwick wouldn't remember her face any more clearly than he already did—she'd learned long ago that a small overpayment bought a fog of goodwill, and goodwill blurred memories better than any hood. She wrapped the book in oilcloth, tucked it inside her coat against her ribs, and stepped out into the rain, which had gentled to a fine grey drift.

*

She didn't go straight to Marla's.

That was the tell, and she knew it. For twenty-six years her pattern had been simple: trouble appears, Cyrus moves, Cyrus is three ridges away before the trouble finishes forming. It was the whole architecture of her life. Erase, evade, redraw. She had eleven safehouses in these mountains and could reach six of them blindfolded.

Instead she climbed to the roof of the chandlery next door—easy climb, hidden ledge, a spot she used for watching the street—and sat in the drizzle with her back against the chimney and the ledger open on her knees under an awning of oilcloth, and read it cover to cover.

Because running required knowing what she was running from, and she didn't know yet. All she had was a line of ink. So she did what she did with any unmapped territory: she surveyed it.

Most of the volume was dross. Weather, births, deaths, a suspicious number of prophecies about pigs. But threaded through it, maybe one entry in forty, was a different kind of entry—same gouging hand, same embossed certainty—and those entries were all about the court below. *The Choir beneath sings, and the mountain holds.* *What is given at the fire cannot be taken back at the door.* *Names are coin below; spend yours wisely.* One entry, dated sixty years past, read simply: *They do not forget. They do not forgive. They wait, and they count, and the counting always ends.*

And interspersed among them, worse: entries about souls. Sold, pledged, traded. Dates set a lifetime out, like harvests. She found three other names in the ledger besides hers—three other people, born decades ago, whose nights of reckoning had already passed. No record of what happened to them. The ledger didn't care about outcomes. It was, as Fenwick's mad registrar might have said, an invoice book, not a results book.

The light failed. Lamps came on along Tallow Lane, blurry gold coins in the wet dark. From her ledge she could see the upper town, and above it the mass of Thornfell itself, black against the last bruise of sky, its summit lost in cloud. Somewhere inside all that stone was a court, and a choir, and a debt a hundred years old with her name—her *real* name—written in its margin.

*Zephyrine will walk down of her own will or be carried.*

She thought about the smoke dream. She thought about Marla going pale. She thought about the fact that in twenty-six years, Marla had never once told her where she'd come from, and Cyrus had never pushed, because not-pushing was the deal, the unspoken contract between them: *I won't ask where I'm from if you won't make me leave.*

Every contract had terms. She'd learned that young. And every contract, sooner or later, came due.

The rain picked up again. Below her, a figure turned onto Tallow Lane carrying a lantern, and even at this distance Cyrus knew the walk—the rolling, deliberate gait of a woman who had delivered a thousand babies and suffered zero fools. Marla, home late from market, a basket on her arm, heading for the boarding house where supper would be waiting and the lies would be warm as the stove.

Cyrus closed the ledger. Held it against her chest. Felt its cold soak through her shirt.

Twenty-six years of being no one, and it had taken one line of old ink to undo it. Not soldiers, not magistrates, not any of the bounty men who'd chased her over the years. A dead clerk with a heavy hand and a taste for other people's doom.

She watched Marla's lantern bob closer, closer, and understood that the next hour would decide everything. Because there were two kinds of people in the world: the ones who ran from a name they didn't choose, and the ones who went and asked why.

She'd spent her whole life as the first kind.

*Zephyrine,* said the ledger, in her own bones. *Walk down of your own will or be carried.*

Marla's key scraped in the boarding house lock, three doors down.

Cyrus dropped from the ledge, silent as falling snow, and went to get answers—or failing that, to get the truth out of the one person alive who owed her it.
