## 3. What the Landlady Knows

The boarding house smelled the way it had smelled for thirty years: tallow smoke, boiled linen, and the ghost of the onion soup Marla made on cold nights because meat cost too much and love had to be served somehow.

Cyrus stood in the kitchen doorway and watched her landlady set down a lantern, hang a wet cloak on its peg, and only then notice she wasn't alone. Marla didn't startle. She was sixty and built like a fence post that had survived every storm by refusing to be interesting. But her hands went still over the cloak's buttons—still in a way Cyrus had seen exactly once before, the night a tax collector knocked asking after unregistered tenants.

"Cyrus." Marla hung the cloak. "You're dripping on my floor."

"You mopped it this morning," Cyrus said. "I checked before I went out."

"Then you know I mind." Marla turned to the stove. "There's soup."

"I don't want soup."

"You never want soup until you want it." The pot lid clinked. "Sit down."

Cyrus sat. That was the trap of Marla, always had been: she issued orders so ordinary that obedience felt like habit rather than surrender. Cyrus had spent two decades learning to read men with knives and magistrates with seals, and this woman in a darned wool shawl could still put her in a chair with three words.

Marla ladled soup into the chipped brown bowl—the chipped one, always the chipped one for Cyrus, since age six, because Marla said chips were character—and set it down. Then she sat across the table, folded her hands, and waited.

Cyrus slid the ledger across the wood.

She'd wrapped it in oilcloth against the rain, but she unwrapped it now with the care of someone handling a live adder, and turned it to the page she had memorized on the walk over. The ink was a century old and brown as dried blood. The script was formal, liturgical, the kind used for land grants and death warrants.

*On the night of the Choir's singing, the Undercourt claims what was sold.*

*Zephyrine.*

Her name. Not Vane, not any of the eleven names she'd worn through the Thornfell passes. A name she had never heard spoken aloud and yet recognized the way you recognize your own hand at the end of your arm.

"I need you to tell me what this is," Cyrus said.

Marla looked at the page for a long moment. Her face did something complicated and then did nothing at all, which was worse.

"It's a book," Marla said.

"Marla."

"It's an old book in a shop that should have burned down years ago, and you went poking through it, and now you're sitting in my kitchen at midnight with rain in your boots wanting me to explain a hundred years of somebody else's scribbling." Marla's voice stayed level, the voice she used for quoting rents. "I can't do that."

"You *can*." Cyrus kept her own voice low. That was important. Shouting meant losing, and she had already lost too much tonight. "Because there are exactly four people in this world who've known me longer than a season. Three of them are dead smugglers. You're the last one. So either the whole world conspired to write my true name in a prophecy ledger, or *you* knew. One of those requires more conspirators than I've ever owed money to."

Marla's thumb rubbed the table's grain. Around and around, following a knot.

"Your mother came to me in the spring of the year you were born," she said finally. "Or I came to her. Depending how you count. I was the midwife."

Cyrus blinked. Thirty years, and Marla had never once mentioned it. "You told me you found me on a doorstep."

"I told you what kept you fed and ignorant and alive." Marla's eyes came up, and they were flint. "Don't you dare look at me like that, girl. Ignorance is a garment. I dressed you in it the way I'd have dressed you in wool against a winter you can't imagine."

"Try me."

"No." Marla stood, went to the cupboard, took down the bottle she kept for boarders who paid late and griefs that paid never, and poured two fingers into two cups. She pushed one across the table. "Drink. Because if I'm going to say any of it out loud, you'll want your hands doing something, or you'll do something with them we'll both regret."

Cyrus drank. It tasted like kerosene and apology.

* * *

"The fire came first," Marla said. "Before you were born. Before the bargain."

She turned her cup in slow circles, watching the liquor climb the sides.

"There was a village up the Sorrow Reach, before the slide took the road in. Ashfall, it was called. You won't find it on any map."

"I would have found it," Cyrus said. "It's what I do."

"You'd have found where it *was*." Marla shook her head. "Not what it was. Ashfall kept old ways, girl. Old bargains. Every generation, the mountain beneath us took its due—a tithe of voices, they called it, though nobody alive remembered what that meant anymore. They sang at midwinter and told themselves it was just singing. And your mother—" Marla stopped. Started again. "Your mother sang like nothing I've heard before or since. When she sang, the fire sat up straighter in the grate. That's not a figure of speech. I saw it."

Cyrus thought of the ledger's careful liturgical hand. *What was sold.* She kept her mouth shut and let the woman talk, the same way she let marks talk when the mark still had things left to give away.

"Your mother came to me heavy with child. With *children*." Marla's voice caught on the word and smoothed itself over, quick as a card sharp hiding a tell—but not quite quick enough, and Cyrus felt the floor tilt under her chair. "Twins. Two heartbeats, clear as church bells, and I'd delivered enough to know. Your mother knew too. And she was terrified, because the tithe was coming due that winter, and there was a prophecy in Ashfall older than the chapel—a copy of it, anyway—that named the family that would pay it. Hers."

"The ledger."

"A cousin of it, likely. Same root." Marla drained her cup and poured another, hands steady, jaw tight. "She came to me not to deliver her babies but to choose between them. That's what midwifery is, sometimes, in the old places. Nobody tells you that."

The kitchen was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a boarder rolled over in bed, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

"Choose," Cyrus said. Her voice came out flat and strange.

"One child for the mountain, one free. That was the shape of the bargain she wanted made. She wouldn't tell me which was which. She'd made up her mind and asked me to help her hold it, and I did, because I was young and believed that mothers know best, and because—" Marla looked at her own hands. "Because she offered me everything she had, and I took it. There. That's my sin, laid out plain. I was paid to help bury half the truth."

"Which child," Cyrus said. "Which one of us went free."

"I don't know."

"You just said—"

"I said I helped make a bargain. I said your mother chose. I never told you I knew the terms, because I didn't." Marla's flint cracked then, just a hairline, just enough. "I delivered two babies in that room above my old shop. Two cries. And then your mother sent me down for warm water, clean as anything, and when I came back up there was one baby in the basket and one bundle of ash on the hearthstone, and your mother standing at the window with frost on her breath, saying goodbye to somebody I couldn't see."

Cyrus's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the table, against the honest grain of it, against thirty years of soup and rent quotes and darned shawls.

"The fire," she said. "You said the fire came first."

"The house burned three nights later. Ashfall burned with it—all of it, in one night, in winter, no wind to carry it. Forty souls gone in their beds, and the mountain road closed by spring, like the world itself was tidying up." Marla's voice went hard again, hammering the crack shut. "Your mother fled with the surviving child and brought her to me. Paid me to raise her unseen, unnamed, unrecorded. Paid me in coin I never counted because I didn't want to know whose face was on it. And I did it. I taught you to be nobody, and you got so good at it I lost track of whether I was protecting you or erasing you, and by the time I wondered, you were seventeen and vanishing on your own, and it was too late to ask forgiveness from anybody."

"Why not ask me?"

Marla laughed—one short, ugly sound with no humor in it at all.

"What would I have said? 'Sorry, dear, your mother sold your sister to the dark under the world and maybe you too, sleep well'?" She pushed the bottle away with one finger, like it was the enemy. "You were happy, Cyrus. Or near enough. You had your roads and your maps and your caravans, and every year you came back here for soup, and I told myself that was enough life to save. People who dig up old graves usually just get dirt under their nails. I never dreamed the grave would send a bill."

*The Undercourt claims what was sold.* On the night of the Choir's singing. Which was—

Cyrus did the arithmetic and felt the numbers go through her like cold water. Eleven days.

"Where is my mother?" she asked. "Now."

"Gone. Weeks after leaving you with me, she went back up the Reach. Told me she had to finish it. Told me if she didn't come back by the thaw—" Marla spread her hands, empty. "No thaw ever brought her down. I lit a candle for her every midwinter for twenty years, and then I stopped, because hope is a debt too, and mine was spent."

Cyrus stood. The chair scraped back and the sound was very loud.

"Sit down," Marla said.

"No." And it was her own turn to use the level voice, the rent-voice, and watch the other woman flinch at hearing it. "You'll forgive me if sitting feels different tonight than it did an hour ago. I learned to sit still in this kitchen. I learned to keep my head down and my name to myself and never ask about the chip in the bowl. You built me like a wall builds a shadow, and now the shadow's been summoned, Marla. By name. In ink older than both of us. So no—I won't sit and drink and let you decide what fragments I get."

Marla rose too, slow, fence-post straight, and for a moment they faced each other across the table like two armies agreeing on where to fight.

"You're going down there," Marla said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to find out who I am before something else decides for me."

"And if it carries you off? If it takes you under and I never see you again?" For one instant the flint broke entirely, and underneath it Cyrus saw something she had never once seen in thirty years of soup and rent: fear, naked and enormous, wearing Marla's face. "Girl, I buried your mother in my heart already. Don't make me bury you in fact."

The words hit harder than Cyrus wanted them to. She let them hit. Then she picked up the ledger, wrapped it back in its oilcloth, and tucked it inside her coat, over her heart, where maps rode.

"Then you'd better hope," she said quietly, "that I'm better at getting out of places than anyone is at getting into them. I've spent my whole life finding roads that don't exist, Marla. Turns out I *am* one. Time I learned where I go."

At the door, Marla spoke once more, to her back.

"There was a choir mistress," she said. "Before your time. Odile Ferrant. Cast out of the Undercourt, they whispered, and living somewhere in Lowmarsh these fifteen years, mad as a wet cat and twice as scratchy. If anyone knows the laws under the mountain—it's her."

Cyrus paused with her hand on the latch.

"Why would you know that?"

"Because your mother asked me the same question, thirty years ago," Marla said. "And because some debts you pay forward, even when it's too late to pay them back."

Cyrus went out into the dark. Behind her, through the shuttered window, she saw the lantern light waver once—as if a woman had finally let herself lean on a table—and then steady.

Eleven days.

She turned toward Lowmarsh, and the rain, and whatever truth was waiting in the person who hated the Undercourt most.

* * *

Lowmarsh began where the good part of town ended and kept going, streets sinking incrementally toward the fen until the houses leaned together like conspirators. Cyrus moved fast through the wet lanes, hood up, ledger against her ribs, turning Marla's fragments over like stones in a streambed.

A twin. The words refused to settle into sense. She searched thirty years of memory for a hollow where a sister should have been and found nothing—no gap, no ache, no shape. Just smooth, seamless solitude, all the way down.

And that was the worst of it, she realized. Not the lie. The seamlessness. Someone had done such thorough work that even the wound was missing.

Ahead, a single lamp burned in a crooked shopfront whose sign hung by one chain: a silver tuning fork, green with verdigris, over lettering worn nearly blank. Music leaked from within—not played, hummed, low and ragged, a woman's voice working through scales like someone sharpening knives.

Cyrus stood in the rain a moment longer, listening.

Then she lifted her hand and knocked.
