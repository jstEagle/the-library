## 4. The Debt-Collector's Offer

Sable Quill had been waiting in the taproom of the Drowned Bell for three hours before Cyrus Vane walked through the door, and he had spent those three hours doing what he did best: learning the room.

The Drowned Bell sat at the crooked elbow of a street that appeared on no honest map, which was precisely why he had chosen it. Its clientele were smugglers, fence-menders, and one customs officer so deep in someone's pocket that his boots had stopped touching the ground years ago. The ale was bad. The stew was worse. The landlord kept a ledger behind the bar with two columns—one for coin, one for favors—and Sable had read it upside down from across the room within ten minutes of arriving. The favors column was longer. It always was, in places like this.

He sat with his back to the wall, a glass of wine he had no intention of drinking, and a ledger of his own open before him. Not paper. Paper could be stolen. His ledger lived somewhere safer, in the place where the Undercourt kept everything it owned: a vault of sound, every contract ever sung preserved as a chord that never quite resolved.

Cyrus Vane entered at half past nine by the church bell, which was wrong, because the church bell in Lowmark rang at half past eight, and anyone who trusted it was already late to everything. She came in wet from the rain, hood back, no attempt at disguise—which interested him immediately. A woman who erased herself from every record in Thornfell should have entered like smoke. Instead she entered like weather.

She ordered stew. She ate it standing up, at the end of the bar, her back to the room but her eyes on the long mirror behind the bottles, watching the door's reflection the way other women watched their own faces. She paid in mixed coin—Thornfell silver, a southern mark, and something old enough that the landlord squinted at it twice. Then she asked him a question too quiet for Sable to catch, and the landlord shook his head, and she nodded as if she'd expected nothing else, and turned for the stairs.

Sable closed his eyes and listened.

This was the trick of his trade that no one understood. They thought debt-collection was about ledgers, about ink, about men with cudgels standing in doorways. Amateurs. Debt was a song. Every obligation had a pitch, a weight, a resonance, and if you knew how to listen you could hear what a person owed the world just by standing near them. Most people hummed with small debts—a borrowed kindness never returned, a promise let lapse, a grave unvisited. The room around him was a dull orchestra of them.

Cyrus Vane was silent.

Not quiet. Silent. As though the ledger of the world had an entry for her and someone had torn the page out. He had encountered silence like this exactly once before, a century ago, on the day the Undercourt first opened its doors beneath the mountain and the first soul walked down singing. He had assumed he would never hear it again.

She reached the foot of the stairs. He rose, straightened his cuffs, and said, pleasantly, "You're being followed."

* * *

She didn't startle. That was the second thing he liked about her. She simply stopped, one hand on the rail, and looked at him in the mirror with the flat assessment of someone pricing a horse.

"By you," she said.

"Technically I've been waiting, not following. Following implies effort." He gestured at the empty chair across from his table. "I've taken the liberty of ordering a second glass. The wine is terrible, but it's terrible honestly, which is more than can be said for most things in this town."

"I don't drink with strangers."

"We're not strangers." He smiled, and let a little of the court come into it—not much, just enough that the candle flames between them leaned toward him like listeners. "We've met before. You wouldn't remember. You were four days old, and I was holding the pen."

Something moved behind her eyes then—the first crack in the composure, quick as a fish under ice. She crossed the room anyway. Of course she did. Curiosity killed more smugglers than customs men ever had, and this one had curiosity the way other people had lungs.

She sat. She did not touch the wine. "Holding the pen," she repeated. "That's a strange way to say midwife."

"It is. Fortunately, I'm not a midwife." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table, aligning it with the wood grain, because precision mattered even in small things. Especially in small things. "My name is Sable Quill. I'm a collector. And I'm here on behalf of an institution you have, until very recently, done a remarkable job of avoiding."

"Institution."

"The court beneath the mountain. The Undercourt, if you prefer the name the surface gives it. We prefer not to be named at all, actually—it saves paperwork—but names are rather the point tonight, aren't they?"

Her hand had gone still on the table. Not clenched. Still, the stillness of a person listening with her whole body. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Sable agreed. "But your face does, and faces are the one thing you've never learned to erase. You erase ledgers, records, parish rolls, caravan manifests—you are, and I say this with genuine professional admiration, the most thoroughly unwritten person I have ever collected against. Do you know how difficult you've made my job? Thirty years of nothing. No birth record. No baptism. No debt, no title, no marriage line, no grave reserved. You've scrubbed yourself out of Thornfell like a stain from silk. I've spent four months trying to find a single document with your name on it."

"And did you?"

"One." He watched her. "A prophecy ledger, a hundred years old, sitting in a bookbinder's shop in Harrowgate because the bookbinder's grandfather bought a crate of ecclesiastical salvage and never read past the first page. A list of dates. Beside last night's date, a name." He paused, savoring it. He was not, by nature, cruel, but he was thorough, and thoroughness sometimes wore cruelty's clothes. "Your name. Your true one. The one nobody has spoken aloud since the night you were born."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; somewhere outside a cart groaned past. Cyrus Vane sat perfectly still, and Sable realized he was doing something he almost never did: he was enjoying himself without any cruelty in it at all. Just fascination. She was a locked door walking around in a coat, and he had spent a century around locks.

"What do you want?" she said finally.

"Straight to business. Good. I'll match you." He folded his hands. "On the night of the Choir's centennial singing—nineteen days from now—the court beneath the mountain will claim a soul that was sold to us. Sold fairly, sold lawfully, sold in accordance with every rule of song and signature we keep. The contract names you. The prophecy confirms it. Two independent instruments of binding, pointing at the same girl. My employers would like me to convey that resistance is possible but inadvisable, and that the mountain is patient in ways you cannot imagine."

"And if I run?"

"You won't." He said it gently. "You could. You're the finest navigator these mountains have produced in a generation—I've read your routes, the ones that officially don't exist, and they're beautiful, truly, little prayers drawn in pencil. You could vanish into Thornfell and I would spend another thirty years chasing a rumor. But you won't. Because you went to Harrowgate. Because you found that ledger. Because you spent today asking questions in three towns—" he counted them off on his fingers, "—Lowmark, Harrowgate, and the mill village whose name I'll spare you—and a woman who wanted to run doesn't ask questions. A woman who wants to run burns the book and changes her name. You want to know."

Her jaw tightened. He pressed on, softly.

"So here is my offer, and I advise you to listen carefully, because I am permitted to make it exactly once. The court will take what it is owed on the night of the singing—that is fixed, written, sung into the stone a century ago, and even I cannot unsing stone. But the manner of the taking is negotiable. If you come down willingly—if you walk through the gate of your own accord, unbound, ungagged, on your own feet—certain courtesies apply. You may speak. You may petition. You may, in the formal language of the court, present terms." He let that sit. "Souls that are dragged below get dragged. Souls that walk down get heard. It is the oldest courtesy we have, older than the lords themselves, and I am authorized to extend it to you."

"And what does the court get out of courtesy?"

"Legitimacy. Consent is the whole architecture of the place, Miss Vane. Everything beneath the mountain runs on agreement—bargains, contracts, songs sung willingly. A soul taken by force is a splinter. A soul that walks in is a cornerstone." He lifted his glass at last and tasted the terrible wine without flinching. "Also, frankly, the lords find it more interesting."

"And if I refuse? If I fight?"

"Then on the nineteenth night, the mountain collects, and you learn what collection means, and I stand in the doorway feeling like a man who delivered a bad verdict." He set the glass down. "I would prefer not to feel that. Call it sentiment. I'm told I have traces of it."

"Why?" The word came out sharp. "Why do you care whether I walk or get dragged? You're a collector. This is inventory."

And there it was—the question underneath the question, the one she'd actually come downstairs to ask, and Sable Quill, who had lied to lords and charmed archivists and talked his way through gates that ate liars, found himself considering telling the truth. It was an unfamiliar sensation, like finding a door in a wall he'd memorized.

"Because I've read your file," he said. "All four pages of it, which is three pages more than exists. Because thirty years ago a bargain was struck over your cradle, and the terms of it have been eating at me since the day I drew the assignment. Because contracts are my scripture, Miss Vane, and I know every clause of yours by heart—and there is a clause in it that doesn't fit. A seam. A stitch out of place. I have been collecting for a very long time, and I have never seen a contract with a seam in it." He leaned forward. "I don't know what it means yet. But I intend to find out, and I suspect you intend to find out faster, and I find that I would rather watch you do it than stop you."

"That's not an offer. That's a confession."

"It's both. The court does love its double entries."

She studied him for a long moment, and he let her, because he knew what she'd see: a man in good wool with careful hands and a voice like a signed document, no weapon visible, no threat displayed. He also knew what she wouldn't see, because no one ever did—the shape of what he'd given up to sit in rooms like this one, wearing a body that wasn't entirely his anymore, answering to a name that had been purchased along with everything else.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "what happens between now and the full moon?"

"Nothing, from me. I'm not your jailer. Go where you like, ask your questions, draw your maps. On the nineteenth night, be at the smuggler's gate below the old toll road—I'll send word of the hour—or send word that you refuse, and I'll deliver your refusal with whatever grace I can manage." He produced a card from his breast pocket, black stock, no name printed on it, only a single musical notation that would look like decoration to anyone who couldn't read it. "This finds me anywhere in Thornfell. Burn it if you like. It reconstitutes. Everything I own does."

She took the card. Turned it over. Her thumb found the notation and rested there, and Sable felt, faintly, distantly, the card sing back to him through her skin—just a thread of contact, just enough to confirm what he already suspected.

She had music in her. Real music, buried deep, the kind the court could hear through stone.

"Well," he said lightly, before she could feel him noticing. "You have nineteen days. Spend them wisely. And Miss Vane—"

"Cyrus," she said. "If you're going to haunt me for three weeks, use the name I chose."

"Cyrus, then." He inclined his head, courtly, precise. "One piece of advice, freely given, no charge against your account. Whatever questions you're chasing—don't chase them alone. The answers beneath that mountain have teeth, and the people who go down alone come back as stories." He smiled, thinner now. "I've collected several of the stories."

She stood, pocketed the card, and looked down at him with something that wasn't gratitude and wasn't trust but might, in a better world, have been either. "Why are you really helping me, Quill?"

"Professional curiosity," he said. "It's the only kind I have left."

She held his eyes a moment longer—checking the sentence for seams, he supposed, the same way he checked her contract—and then she climbed the stairs and was gone, her boots fading overhead, a door closing, water running in a basin.

Sable sat alone with two glasses and the rain.

After a while, quietly, so quietly that not even the landlord's ledger could have caught it, he began to hum. Four notes. The seam from her contract, the stitch out of place, the thing that had bothered him for four months and a hundred years before that. He hummed it and let it resolve wrong, the way it always resolved wrong, and thought about the date on the signature.

The contract bearing her name was dated the day after her recorded birth.

He had told her none of that. Some debts you collect. Some you carry.

Nineteen days, he thought, and finished the terrible wine, and went out into the rain to write his report to the court—every word of it true, and none of it complete.
