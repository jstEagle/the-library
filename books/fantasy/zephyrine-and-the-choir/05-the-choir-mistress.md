## 5. The Choir Mistress

Odile Ferrant kept her hands busy when the memories came. That was the first lesson she had learned in exile, before the lessons about silence and patience and the particular cruelty of remembering a place that did not want you. Hands busy, mind occupied. So on the evening the Vane girl finally came to her door—nineteen days before the full moon, and Odile had been counting, though she would have cut out her own tongue before admitting it—Odile was up to her elbows in the guts of a hurdy-gurdy, pretending very hard that she had not been expecting this visit for four years.

"You're leaking," Cyrus Vane said from the doorway.

"It's called weather. It happens outside." Odile did not look up. "The roof leaks too. I own the leak. I've made peace with it."

The girl stepped in without invitation, which Odile noted with something adjacent to approval. Water ran off her oilskin in threads and pooled on the floorboards Odile never bothered to sweep. She stood there taking inventory of the room the way Odile had known she would: the tools racked on the wall, the seven broken instruments laid out like patients in a field hospital, the single window shuttered against the rain, and—Odile watched her find it, watched her eyes snag and move on too quickly—the small iron frame bolted above the lintel where a tuning fork of Undercourt silver hung wrapped in cloth.

She'd seen it. She'd pretend she hadn't until it suited her to mention it. Good. Odile hated being studied by amateurs, but she hated being underestimated more.

"You've been following me," Odile said. "For eleven days. You walk like a smuggler and you leave no tracks at all, which is how I know you're following me. Innocent people make noise."

"Then you already know what I want."

"I know what you think you want." Odile set down the plectrum and wiped her hands on a rag that had once been white. "Sit down, girl, before you drip on something I care about. There's tea. It's terrible. The water comes off the mountain and tastes of iron and regret."

Cyrus sat. Up close she had the look of someone who had practiced not having a look—a face built for forgetting, ordinary as bread, except for the eyes, which were doing arithmetic. Odile recognized the type. Half the people the Undercourt chewed came out the far end with that same squint, measuring everything for exits.

"Odile Ferrant," Cyrus said. "Former First Soprano of the Deep Choir. Former Mistress of the Lower Rehearsal Halls. Exiled forty years ago for—" She stopped.

"For singing one note wrong on purpose." Odile poured the tea anyway, because ritual was ritual. "Say it. Everyone else does."

"That's not what the ledger says."

The words landed in the room like a dropped glass. Odile kept her face still through long practice, but inside her chest something turned over, the way a body turns over in sleep.

"What ledger," she said.

* * *

The girl told her story badly and precisely, which Odile came to understand was simply how she told everything. A bookbinder's shop in Lowmark. A prophecy ledger, century-old vellum, an entry for a date nineteen days hence—the night of the centennial Song—and beside the date, a name: *Zephyrine*, written in ink that had dried long before the girl was born. Her true name, a name she had never heard spoken. A soul owed to the court beneath the mountain, sold before she could walk.

"And so you came to me," Odile said slowly, "because everyone knows the disgraced choir mistress can get you into the Undercourt."

"Because you're the only person alive who walked out of it," Cyrus said. "And because of the ledger."

"The ledger."

"Your exile entry. It's crossed out. Struck through in the same hand as my name, same ink, same century. Someone amended your record, Mistress Ferrant. Someone un-exiled you on paper forty years ago, and never told you." Cyrus leaned forward. "In the court below, documents are law. Which means somewhere in those archives there is a version of your story with a different ending, and nobody has ever sung it aloud."

Odile put down her cup. Her hands were steady—they were always steady; they had conducted two hundred voices through the Song of Renewal and they did not tremble for anything less than that—but her breath had gone shallow, and she was distantly furious about it.

Forty years. Forty years of scraping a living repairing farmers' fiddles in a village so small it appeared on no map worth owning, telling herself the court had simply discarded her, that her crime had been real, that the shame was hers to carry like a stone she'd chosen. And all the while some clerk's amendment had sat in the archive, struck through her sentence like a debt forgiven and then deliberately left unclaimed.

"Why would you tell me this?" she asked. "You need me. You could have kept it in your pocket. Leverage."

Something moved behind the girl's flat gray eyes—wry, tired, almost kind.

"Because I've spent my whole life being lied to by people who loved me," Cyrus said. "I'm not going to start doing it to someone I need. Besides." A thin smile. "You'd have found out the moment we got below. You'd have gone to the archive yourself. And then I'd have lost you."

* * *

They did not strike the bargain that night. Odile was too old and too much a creature of the court to sign anything on the first reading. Instead she gave the girl a blanket and the good side of the hearth and lay awake on her cot listening to the rain and doing the thing she had forbidden herself for four decades: remembering the Undercourt.

She remembered it the way you remember a lover who ruined you—not the ending, but the middle. The vaulted rehearsal halls carved from living rock, their ceilings threaded with veins of crystal that caught every note and held it, so that the choir sang always in company with its own echoes. The laws made of sound. A contract hummed instead of signed, a promise whispered in a minor key, a lie detected by the flatness of the liar's voice. She had been twenty-two when she descended, brilliant and ambitious, the finest ear in three generations, and she had believed—with the whole stupid furnace of her heart—that music was the truest thing in the world, and therefore the court built upon it must be the truest court.

It took her eighteen years to learn that truth and justice are different instruments played in different rooms.

Her crime, the official one: at the rehearsal of a renewal rite, she had sung a single altered tone, displacing a cadence, buying one borrowed voice a moment's grace before its contract closed. Eighteen seconds of mercy. The court had heard it instantly—of course it had; the court heard everything—and had exiled her not for rebellion but for *imprecision*. For making the music mean something it wasn't written to mean.

That was the story on paper. But if the ledger had been amended—

Odile stared at the dark beams of her ceiling and let herself think, for the first time in forty years, the thought she had buried under the floorboards of her mind: *Someone wanted me out. And someone, later, wanted it recorded that I hadn't really been cast out at all.*

Two hands in the archive. One to erase, one to amend. She knew that handwriting style. Every choir mistress did. Amendments in the deep registers were written in the Hand of Record, and the Hand of Record answered to only one office.

She did not let herself finish the thought. Not yet. Hope was a frequency, and if you hummed it too early, things below the mountain would hear it and come to collect.

* * *

In the morning Cyrus was already up, studying the rack of tools with a professional's eye.

"You tune these by ear alone," she said. "No fork. Except that one." She nodded at the lintel, giving up the pretense at last. "Undercourt silver. You keep it wrapped. Why?"

"Because silver remembers what it's tuned to," Odile said. "And I don't need it remembering out loud." She poured the terrible tea. "Ask your question, girl. You've been holding it since the door."

"The toll gates. The market, the courts, the audience halls. I need the geography. All of it."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to. I know what you're offering, because it's the only thing I have that you want, so it must be the only thing you have that I want." Odile sat across from her and folded her hands. "You're offering me revenge. Or restitution. Or an ending. You're offering to take me back through the door they threw me out of, with standing, with purpose. And you want to know what I'll say, so here is what I'll say: no. Because the last time I guided someone's descent, she didn't come back either, and I am done feeding girls to that mountain."

Silence. Rain on the roof. The leak finding its familiar path.

"Who was she?" Cyrus asked quietly.

"A singer. A runaway with a voice like a knife wrapped in silk, and debts she wouldn't explain." Odile heard her own voice go dry and careful. "She came to me the way you came to me. A name in old ink. A date beside it. I taught her the laws, the shields, the silences. I got her to the gate and I waited on the surface, because my exile forbids the descent, and she went in on the night of the quarter-court and never came out." She met the girl's eyes. "Twenty-six years ago. Her name was Liss. I still cannot say it above a whisper, because names are currency down there and I will not spend her."

Cyrus was silent for a long moment. Then, in a voice stripped of all its careful flatness:

"Mistress Ferrant. The ledger says my soul is owed on the night of the centennial Song. Nineteen days. If I go down alone, with no guide, knowing nothing, I die—or worse, I'm collected, and I sing in that Choir until the mountain runs out of stone. If you refuse me, you're choosing that. Not choosing is choosing. You know it better than anyone—you spent eighteen years watching that court call murder *contract fulfillment*."

"Do not lecture me on my own court."

"Somebody amended your ledger," Cyrus said relentlessly. "Somebody upstairs, or down below, decided forty years ago that your exile should be reversible—and left it sitting there like a loaded instrument. What if that has to do with Liss? What if the reason you were struck through is the same reason she never came back?" The girl leaned forward, and Odile saw for the first time the thing underneath the smuggler's calm: a ferocity banked so deep it looked like frost. "You want an ending to your story. I'm the only person in the world offering you one that isn't the grave. Come with me. Teach me the songs. And if we do this right—if we get to the bottom of whose name is written where—we walk into the archive together, and you read your own record with your own eyes, and whatever's true in it, it's yours again. That's not revenge. Revenge is smaller than that. It's *restoration*. And the court below owes you forty years of it."

Odile looked at her for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere down the valley a dog was barking at nothing, the way dogs do when the ground hums and only animals notice.

"You rehearsed that," she said at last.

"Twelve times," Cyrus admitted. "Walking up your path."

Despite herself—despite everything, forty years of everything—Odile laughed. It came out rusty, a hinge unused, but it was a laugh. The girl smiled briefly, like sunlight getting through a shutter it wasn't supposed to fit.

"Here are my terms," Odile said. "First: we are not friends. We are allies of record, and in the court below even that word binds, so choose to mean it. Second: you obey the laws of sound as I teach them, without exception and without argument, because the day you improvise in front of a lord of the Undercourt, we both become furniture. Third—" and here her voice dropped to the register she had once used to command two hundred voices, and she saw the girl sit up straighter without meaning to— "when we reach the archive, we find Liss's contract. Whatever it costs us. Whatever it delays. That is the price of my guidance, and it is not negotiable."

Cyrus considered her. Odile could see the mapmaker working behind her eyes, plotting routes, weighing loads.

"Agreed," Cyrus said. "One term of mine. Everything you know, you tell me straight. No protecting me. I've had thirty years of people protecting me, and it turns out they were selling pieces of me the whole time. I'd rather have the ugly truth ugly."

"Oh, girl." Odile rose and went to the wall of tools, to the locked case at the end of it that she had not opened in twenty-six years. "You have no idea how ugly the truth gets down there. Sit down. We begin with the first law, which is the only one that might keep you alive past the gate: *silence is a shield, but a held note is a sword*, and the court can always tell which one you're carrying."

She unlocked the case. Inside, wrapped in wool gone gray with age, lay her conductor's baton—Undercourt silver, dense as sin, humming faintly even now with the pitch of a hall she would never conduct again. She had told herself for four decades that she kept it as a warning. She picked it up and felt it wake against her palm, eager, remembering her grip.

Above the lintel, the wrapped tuning fork shivered once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.

"Nineteen days," Odile said, more to the baton than to the girl. "Then we go down. And Zephyrine—"

The girl flinched at the name, and Odile filed that away too, another entry in the ledger she was already keeping.

"—when we reach the deep archive, and you see what's written in your file, remember that I warned you the dates wouldn't fit. Contracts below are never quite what they claim. Especially," she said quietly, "the ones written in the Hand of Record."

Outside, the rain stopped all at once, the way it does when the mountain decides something, and in the sudden quiet both women heard it—faint, distant, rising up through bedrock and root and forty years of exile:

Someone, far below, was warming up the Choir.
