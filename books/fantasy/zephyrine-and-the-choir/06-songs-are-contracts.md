## 6. Songs Are Contracts

Odile Ferrant's house stood at the treeline where the last mapped road gave up and became rumor—a squat stone cottage with a slate roof furred in moss and a door that had been carved from a single piece of heartwood so old the grain had gone black. No sign. No path. The kind of place Cyrus would have walked past a hundred times without seeing, which, she suspected, was exactly the point.

"You'll want to leave your maps," Odile said, not turning around. She had gone straight to the hearth and was feeding a fire that seemed already to be burning before she touched it. "Outside. All of them."

"I don't leave my maps."

"Then you don't go under." Odile straightened, wiped her hands on an apron that had once been white, and faced her. She was smaller than Cyrus remembered from the tavern—diminutive in the way of compressed things, like a coal or a fist. Her eyes were the pale gray of river ice. "Paper is a record. A record is a name. A name, down there, is money in your pocket and a knife at your throat simultaneously. You want to walk into the Undercourt carrying a ledger of every hidden road in Thornfell? Fine. You'll be the richest corpse they've seen this century."

Cyrus looked at her satchel. Eleven maps, each one drawn twice—once for the client, once for herself—and annotated in a cipher of her own devising. Roads no living person besides her knew end to end. Twenty years of work. Her whole self, folded into oilcloth.

"They'd have to read them first," she said.

"Down there, reading is the least of what paper can do." Odile held out her hand. "The mountain eats documents, girl. It digests them into law."

* * *

They worked at a table scarred by candle wax and something that might have been claw marks, in a room lined floor to ceiling with sheet music. Not books—loose leaves, thousands of them, pinned and stacked and strung on cords like drying fish. Some were ancient, brown as autumn. Some looked freshly inked. All of them, Cyrus noticed, had been signed.

"First lesson." Odile dropped a single page on the table between them. On it: a simple melody, four bars, a lullaby by the shape of it. At the bottom, two signatures. "Tell me what you see."

"Music."

"Wrong. You see a contract. Every song is binding. That is the first law of the Undercourt, and it is the law above all the others because it is the law that made them." Odile tapped the page. "This lullaby was sung by a mother to a court official's sick daughter three hundred years ago. Look at the second signature."

Cyrus leaned closer. The name meant nothing to her, but the notation beside it did not: *consideration received — one favor, unspecified, callable at any time.*

"She sang a sick child to sleep," Odile said, "and in doing so sold the court one favor, to be named later. They called it forty-one years after her death. Her grandson paid it. He is still paying it. He works in the foundries now, and he will work there until the mountain is done with him, which will be never." She took the page back. "You hummed on the walk up here. Twice."

Cyrus went cold. "I hum."

"You hummed a walking song your landlady taught you, if I had to guess. Marla Vane always did love that one." Something flickered across Odile's face at the name—there and gone, a shutter. "On the surface, a hum is nothing. Air moving through a throat. Under the mountain, a song is an offer, an acceptance, and consideration, all in one act. If you sing where the court can hear you, you have opened negotiations. And the court," she said, "never loses a negotiation."

"So I don't sing."

"Second lesson: silence is the only shield. But—" Odile raised a finger before Cyrus could exhale in relief. "Silence has its own laws. Total muteness reads as insult, and insult is also binding; you can be challenged over it. Refusing a direct request to sing from someone of rank requires a formal excuse. There are eleven acceptable excuses. Would you like to hear them?"

"Obviously."

"No." Odile smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was a teacher's smile, the kind that comes right before the lesson gets expensive. "Because listing them is itself a recitation, and recitations bind the speaker to accuracy, and if I misremember even one—if I say *twelve* excuses when tradition holds eleven, or name the seventh excuse out of order—I have breached my own contract, and the breach flows downhill to whoever I'm instructing. Which is you." She sat down across the table, folding her hands. "You'll learn them one at a time, in context, when you need them. That's how I learned. That's how everyone learns. The court does not make its rules easy to teach. It makes them easy to break."

Cyrus stared at her. "That's insane."

"That's law," Odile said. "Everywhere. You've just never noticed, because surface law wears robes and sits in buildings, and this law wears melody and lives in your mouth." She leaned forward. "You're a mapmaker. So map it. In the Thornfells, what happens if you cross onto another man's land without knowing the boundary?"

"Fines. Feuds. Sometimes worse."

"And who keeps the boundary stones?"

"The people who benefit from them."

"There it is." Odile spread her hands. "Now you understand the Undercourt. The boundaries are songs. The stones are names. And the people who keep them are the people who wrote them, three hundred years ago, in their own favor."

* * *

The third lesson came after dark, over a supper of smoked trout and bread so dense it could anchor a boat, and it was the lesson that broke something in Cyrus.

"Names are currency," Odile said. She had cleared the plates and laid out three objects in a row on the table: a copper coin, a lock of hair tied with thread, and a strip of birch bark bearing a single word in a spidery hand. "Not metaphorically. Functionally. Watch."

She slid the coin aside. "Money exists down there, but it's for buying things from humans, and there are very few humans. Among the courted folk, transactions run on identity. Give a lord your name"—she touched the birch bark—"and you haven't told him what you're called. You've given him the calling of it. He can summon you with it. Compel small truths from you. Sell it, if he likes, to someone with worse intentions. Your name, once surrendered, is an instrument other people play."

"And the hair?"

"A lesser stake. Blood-adjacent tokens buy small things—a night's lodging, a question answered—but they depreciate. Names appreciate." Odile's voice had gone quiet, almost gentle, which frightened Cyrus more than the content did. "A name that has been held for a century accrues interest. Do you understand what that means for you? Your name is sitting in some archive down there, girl. It has been accruing for thirty years, maybe more. Whatever claims it on the night of the Choir is going to collect something substantial."

Cyrus set down her cup. Her hands were steady—she made them be steady—the same way she made them steady on a cliff face in crosswind. "So I change my name. I've done it before. Six times."

"Surface names." Odile shook her head slowly. "You changed what people call you. You didn't touch what you *are*. The true name isn't the label, Cyrus. It's the hook the world uses to lift you. Yours is written in a contract somewhere beneath us, sealed and dated, and no alias you adopt above ground will dull it." She paused. "Although—"

"Although?"

Odile studied her for a long moment, and Cyrus had the uncomfortable sensation of being surveyed the way she herself surveyed a pass before committing the caravan to it: checking the load, the weather, the odds.

"Although there are loopholes," Odile said. "There are always loopholes. Contracts have margins. But we're getting ahead of ourselves." She gathered the coin, the hair, the birch bark, and swept them back into a drawer. "Tonight you learn the last foundational law, and then you sleep, because tomorrow we practice."

"Practice what?"

"Lying," said Odile Ferrant, choir mistress, disgraced, exiled, "without singing while you do it. It's harder than you think. Everything honest in a human voice carries a tune underneath. Fear especially. Fear wants to be sung." Her gray eyes fixed on Cyrus. "And you, my girl, are going to be more afraid down there than you have ever been in your life."

* * *

Sleep did not come easily, and when it came, it brought the market with it—not the Undercourt market she had never seen, but the one in Lowbridge where she'd bought her first knife at nine years old, except in the dream every stall sold voices. Jars of them. Laughing, weeping, counting, cursing. And at the far end, one jar open and empty, and a vendor saying in a voice like tearing linen, *this one's already been claimed, dearie, paid for before you were born.*

She woke before dawn with the taste of old ink in her mouth and Odile already awake in the next room, and Cyrus lay staring at the black rafters thinking: *I have crossed the Serrated Col in February. I have talked a toll-master out of hanging my client. I have erased myself from six towns' worth of records and ledgers and parish rolls.* She had thought those skills would carry.

But you could outwalk a border. You could outrun a debt-collector. You could forge a seal, bribe a clerk, burn a ledger.

There was no burning a song. A song lived in every ear that heard it. It reproduced itself like ivy. It didn't need paper, didn't need witnesses, needed nothing but breath and agreement, and it bound forever. Her whole life she had trusted lines—roads, borders, contour intervals, the clean grammar of elevation. Lines you could see. Lines that stayed put.

Sound moved. Sound got inside you. Sound was a border that crossed *you*, and no amount of mapmaking would chart it, because the territory was people.

She dressed in the dark and went out to the cold kitchen, and Odile was there with two cups of something bitter and steaming, and a fresh sheet of music laid on the table. Unsigned. Waiting.

"Before anything else," Odile said, "you'll learn the eleventh excuse. It's the only one that works on the lords of the high court, and it will cost you something every time you use it. Sit."

Cyrus sat. Outside, the sun was coming up over Thornfell, gilding a hundred secret passes she knew better than anyone alive, and not one of them led anywhere she needed to go.

"One thing first," she said. "You flinched last night. When I said Marla's name."

Odile's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. For a moment the exiled choir mistress looked, for the first time since Cyrus had met her, genuinely cornered—an expression Cyrus recognized intimately, because she'd worn it herself for thirty years without knowing it.

"I didn't flinch," Odile said. "I listened. There's a difference." She set the cup down, and her voice, when it came, had a crack in it she couldn't quite sing away. "Marla Vane and I have history, girl. Old history. The kind with signatures on it. And if you want my help surviving what's under that mountain, you're going to need to know exactly what kind—because I'm fairly sure the bargain that put your name in that prophecy"

She stopped. Not dramatically. Just precisely, the way a singer cuts a note short rather than let it decay.

"Put my name in what?" Cyrus said quietly.

Odile looked at the unsigned sheet music between them, and then at Cyrus, and whatever calculation she performed behind those ice-gray eyes, it ended in surrender.

"Sit closer," she said. "And don't hum."
