## 7. The Toll Gate of Small Mercies

The smuggler's gate was called the Kissing Door, which Sable Quill had always found obscene. There was nothing tender about it. It sat in a fold of the Thornfells where the rock had been persuaded—never broken, only persuaded—to admit those who knew the right words and forget them immediately after.

Cyrus Vane knew the words.

He watched her work from twenty paces back, arms folded, letting her have this. She crouched at the seam in the mountainside with a stub of chalk and a strip of dried eel skin, drawing marks that were not quite letters and humming something under her breath that was not quite a song. The distinction mattered. Below, in the Undercourt, everything that could be mistaken for music would be treated as music, and every music was a contract. Up here, on the cold grey lip of the world, the rules were looser. But habits formed at gates like this one kept people alive.

"You're staring," Odile Ferrant said beside him.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring." The choir mistress pulled her coat tighter against the wind. She had dressed for the descent in black so old it had gone soft as ash, and she wore it the way other women wore armor. "She's better at this than you expected."

"She's better at this than anyone I've met," Sable admitted. "And I've met everyone worth meeting."

"That's not the compliment you think it is."

"It's never the compliment they think it is," he agreed pleasantly.

Odile made a sound low in her throat—the sound of a woman who had spent thirty years conducting and had learned to say entire paragraphs without opening her mouth. He was beginning to develop a vocabulary for it. This troubled him. He did not intend to spend enough time around either of these women to require fluency.

At the rock face, Cyrus finished her chalk-work and pressed the eel skin flat against the stone. For a moment nothing happened. Then the mountain breathed.

Sable felt it in his teeth before he heard it: a long exhalation of warm air from a seam no wider than a knife blade, smelling of deep water and candle smoke and something older underneath, something like the inside of a violin. The seam widened. Not much. Enough.

"After you," Cyrus said, and there it was again—that flicker of a smile she wore like a knife worn down to a letter opener. Dangerous only if you forgot what it had been.

"Ladies first is traditional," Sable said.

"Everything about you is traditional. That's what makes you easy to read."

Odile went through first, because she was the guide and because pride was a currency she no longer carried. Sable followed. The passage swallowed them by degrees—first the wind died, then the light, then the particular quality of silence that belongs to open sky. He had walked into the Undercourt through four different doors in his long career, and every one of them closed behind him with the same soft finality, like a ledger shutting.

This time, for the first time, he noticed himself noticing.

*Interesting,* he thought, and put the thought away.

* * *

The passage ran downhill for an hour, then an hour more, wide enough for two abreast and lit by veins of pale mineral light that Cyrus called worm-glow and refused to explain further. She walked point. Odile drifted behind her, counting steps under her breath—not for navigation, Sable understood, but for rhythm. A choir mistress kept time even in exile. Especially in exile.

He brought up the rear, which suited him. Rear positions let a man watch faces instead of roads, and faces were where the real maps were drawn.

Cyrus's face, tonight, was doing something complicated. She had been confident at the gate—genuinely, not performatively—and confidence looked good on her the way water looks good on drought country. But now, deeper in, with the air thickening and the walls beginning to carry their footsteps back to them a half-beat late, he watched the confidence start to cost her. Her jaw set. Her breathing shortened. She touched the wall once, lightly, the way a person touches a scar.

"The toll gate is ahead," Odile said quietly. "Half a mile. Perhaps less."

"I know," Cyrus said. "I've mapped three approaches to it. Never used any of them."

"Why not?" Sable asked, though he knew.

"Because using one means paying." She didn't turn around. "Smugglers route around tolls. That's the whole profession."

"And yet here we are," Sable said, "walking directly into one. How the profession has fallen."

"How the profession has *been conscripted*," Cyrus corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference has your name on it."

He inclined his head, conceding the point with the grace of a man who collected concessions the way others collected debts. Which was to say: professionally, and without warmth.

But privately—and Sable Quill's private thoughts were the only property he had never once surrendered—he noted that she was afraid, and that she was walking anyway, and that this combination was rarer than talent. Talent he could buy. Courage he could only witness, and witnessing left a mark.

He had marks enough. He was about to acquire another.

* * *

The Toll Gate of Small Mercies was not a gate. It was a room.

The passage opened without warning into a chamber the size of a modest chapel, its ceiling lost in shadow above the worm-glow's reach, its floor worn smooth as a riverbed by centuries of feet. In the center stood a desk. Behind the desk sat a clerk.

The clerk had been beautiful once, or had been built to resemble beauty by someone who had only heard it described. Its skin was the grey of unsalted paper. Its eyes were two small brass scales, and when it turned them toward the party, Sable felt each of them weighed individually—himself, the choir mistress, the mapmaker—and knew, with the particular dread of a professional recognizing a superior, that the weighing was accurate.

"Three travelers," the clerk said. Its voice was dry and courteous, like pages turning. "One debt. One grudge. One—" the brass scales ticked, ticked, ticked, and something in their balance seemed to puzzle it, "—one absence."

"Charming," said Odile.

"Toll is due," the clerk continued, unbothered. "Passage beneath the mountain is purchased in memory. Small mercies only. The court does not take grief; grief is load-bearing. The court does not take love; love is collateral. You will surrender a small mercy—a comfort, a habit, a kindness you have grown accustomed to carrying—and you will pass."

"And if we refuse?" Cyrus asked.

"Then you will stand in this room," the clerk said, "until you do not refuse. The record shows the longest refusal lasted eleven years. The refuser is still here. You may greet her on your way out, if you choose to leave."

Silence took the room. Sable let it. Silence was his native language, though few believed it—they saw the tailored coats and the silver tongue and assumed noise lived at the center of him. They were wrong. Noise was the packaging. He had learned silence young, in a house where making sound meant being heard, and being heard meant being sold.

"Small mercies," Odile repeated. She stepped forward first, because she was brave in the specific way of people who had already lost everything worth protecting and therefore had nothing between themselves and honesty. "What counts?"

The clerk produced a ledger. Of course it did. Everything below the mountain was a ledger wearing a costume.

"Examples from recent travelers," it said. "A woman surrendered the memory of her mother's lullaby. A man surrendered the taste of bread. A child—" the scales ticked uncomfortably, "—a child surrendered the memory of being forgiven. These were accepted. Grief, love, and identity are refused. Attempting to pay in forbidden coin results in forfeiture of the attempter. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Odile said. "And I know how this works, clerk. I sang at the Court of Ninefold Echo for nineteen years. I know what you are."

"Then you know I am not cruel," the clerk said. "I am thorough. Cruelty is a choice. Thoroughness is a position."

Odile paid first. She laid her hand flat on the desk, and Sable watched the memory leave her—it moved through her like a shiver, like a note held past its proper length and then released. Whatever small mercy she'd given up, she did not look at it going. She turned her face away, jaw tight, and when it was done she stood straighter, the way people do after setting down weight they're ashamed to have carried.

"What did you give?" Cyrus asked softly.

"A kindness," Odile said. "Mine own business which one."

Cyrus went next. She hesitated longer than Odile had—not from fear, Sable judged, but from inventory. A woman who had spent her life erasing herself would have fewer comforts to spare, and each one precious. He watched her lips move slightly, running the count. Watched her close her eyes.

When she opened them, she pressed her palm to the desk.

The taking was quick. She swayed. Caught herself on the desk's edge. Stood there a moment with an expression he had seen on a hundred faces in a hundred transactions—the expression of someone trying to remember a word in a language they used to speak.

"What did you lose?" he asked.

"I don't—" She stopped. Frowned. "There was something. About mornings. I think I liked mornings. There was a ritual. Tea, maybe. Or—" The frown deepened, then smoothed itself away with visible effort. "It doesn't matter. It doesn't matter. Let's go."

It mattered. He could see it mattering, a small hole in the fabric of her, and he filed the sight away in the drawer where he kept things that would later keep him awake.

Which left him.

The clerk's brass eyes settled on Sable Quill, and the scales within them began to tick.

* * *

Here is what the court knew about Sable Quill, because the court knew everything about Sable Quill: it knew he had been human once. It knew the shape of the bargain that had unmade him. It knew, with the loving precision of a creditor reviewing collateral, exactly what remained in him of the man he had been, and it kept that remainder in a vault, and it visited that vault the way misers visit gold—often, and with pleasure.

So when the clerk weighed him, it weighed him knowing precisely which small mercies he possessed. And it wanted a specific one.

"Name your price," Sable said, before it could. Control of the transaction was half the transaction. Always.

"The court does not name prices," the clerk said. "Travelers name payments."

"Then I'll pay in coin. Literal coin. I have a purse that would choke a bishop."

"Coin is not memory. Coin is not mercy. Passage beneath the mountain is purchased in the self."

"Then I'll pay in a memory of my choosing." He smiled, and the smile was excellent, honed across decades of negotiations in rooms like this one. "I choose to forget the taste of olives. I've always found them vulgar."

The scales ticked. The clerk regarded him with the patience of paper.

"The court declines olive-flavored payment."

"Then the memory of my first contract. It was poorly drafted. I'd be improved by losing it."

"The court declines."

"My mother's face."

The room went very quiet. Cyrus had stopped moving entirely. Odile's breath caught audibly, and Sable cursed himself for the slip even as he made it—because it wasn't a slip, it couldn't be, he had offered it deliberately, testing, the way a duelist extends a blade to see if the opponent flinches.

The clerk did not flinch.

"The court declines," it said gently, which was worse than any refusal delivered with force. "The court holds your mother's face in trust, collector. It is spoken for."

And there it was. The first crack.

Sable felt it open along a fault line he had plastered over so many times the plaster had become structural. He kept his posture. He kept his smile. He was aware, distantly, with the strange clarity of a man watching his own house burn from across the street, that both were holding only because they had been holding for so long that holding was all they were made of anymore.

"Then what," he said, and his voice came out level, level, perfectly level, "does the court accept?"

The clerk consulted its ledger. It did not need to. Sable understood, with the sick clarity of the professionally outmaneuvered, that the page had been marked in advance—that someone below had known he would come through this gate, on this night, with these companions, and had left instructions.

"The court accepts," the clerk read, "the memory of the harbor at Bellamere. The morning fog. The boy on the pier who shared his breakfast with a stranger's brother. The court accepts the whole of it."

No.

The word rose in him, vast and wordless, and he strangled it in the cradle, because showing the clerk that it had found anything at all was to show it leverage, and leverage shown was leverage spent. So Sable Quill stood in the Toll Gate of Small Mercies and let his face do nothing whatsoever while the court reached into the one drawer he had never once opened in front of witnesses, and took out the single thing—

Bellamere. Fog off the water, grey-gold, the gulls arguing over nothing. He had been twenty-two and hungry and new to the trade, and his brother Tomas had been fifteen and dying by inches of the lung-rot, and there had been a boy on the pier—some fisherman's son, some stranger—who had split his breakfast roll without being asked, without ceremony, handing half over the way you'd hand a man an oar in rough water. No debt incurred. No contract signed. Just bread, given freely, to a stranger's brother, on a morning when the fog made the whole world feel briefly forgivable.

Tomas had died in the autumn anyway. But Sable had kept that morning. Through the sale of his voice, through the decades of collection work, through every ledger and every locked door, he had kept the fog and the bread and the boy's chapped grin, and he had told himself it was nothing, a trifle, a small mercy, exactly the kind of thing a sensible man surrenders at a toll gate without ceremony—

That was the trap. That was the whole architecture of it. The court had not chosen something precious. The court had chosen something he had convinced himself was cheap, and let him do the convincing, and waited.

"Collector," the clerk prompted. "Do you consent?"

He should negotiate. He should demand terms, amendments, a rider, anything—every instinct of his long career screamed at him to treat, to stall, to find the angle. But he looked up and found Cyrus Vane watching him, and her face held none of the pity he had braced against. It held recognition. She knew what it was to have yourself inventoried by powers that held the pen. She had lived her whole life in that ledger.

He could not let her see him flinch. So instead he did the only thing worse.

He let her see him pay.

"Yes," Sable Quill said.

* * *

The taking was gentle. That was the obscenity of it. The court was never cruel when cruelty would spoil the merchandise. It came into him like fog coming off the harbor—no, like fog coming *onto* the harbor, soft and total, and it lifted the morning out of him the way you'd lift a jewel from a case: carefully, respectfully, leaving everything else in its place so the theft would be hard to notice until inventory day.

When it was done, he was standing in a chapel-sized room in the dark under a mountain, and there was a gap in him shaped like fog.

"Well?" Cyrus asked. Quietly. Too quietly for Odile to hear, pitched for him alone.

"I don't recall," Sable said, and smiled, and the smile worked, it had always worked, it would work until the sun burned out. "Some trifle. The clerk has poor taste in trifles."

She looked at him a moment longer. She had sharp eyes, the mapmaker. Eyes that found the roads other people paved over.

"You're missing something," she said. Not a question.

"Everyone is. That's rather the theme of the venue."

The clerk stamped something—Sable heard the seal bite into wax with a sound like a small bone breaking—and gestured with one grey hand toward the far door, which had not been there before and was now unmistakably there, standing open on a stair that descended into red-dark warmth.

"Passage is granted," the clerk said. "Mind the seventh step. And, collector—" the brass eyes ticked toward him, almost kindly, "—the court sends a message. It says: *the vault remains open. Visiting hours are generous.*"

Sable bowed. It was a masterpiece of a bow, depth without deference, and he held it a beat too long so that it read as irony rather than obedience.

Then he walked through the door, and did not look back at the desk, because looking back was how the court learned what you valued, and he had given it quite enough for one evening.

Behind him, boots on stone: Cyrus first, then Odile, the choir mistress already humming the descending scale that would order their steps on the stair. Ahead, the heat of the deep places rose to meet them, and somewhere far below—miles, perhaps, or only inches, distance worked strangely down here—a voice was singing.

Sable stopped on the landing. Just for a moment. Just long enough.

He did not know why the voice snagged him. It was a rehearsal fragment, thin and distant, a single line of the centennial Song rising up through the rock like water through limestone. He had heard the Song a dozen times. He had helped collect souls that fed it. There was nothing in it for him, nothing personal, nothing owed.

Except that for one suspended moment, standing in the warm dark with his new hollow aching like a pulled tooth, Sable Quill could have sworn the voice sounded familiar—not the melody, but the *timbre*, the grain of it, the way a voice carries the shape of the throat that makes it.

Like a voice he'd heard once on a pier, in the fog, laughing about bread.

"Collector," Odile called down. "Keep up."

He kept up. He was, whatever else might be said of him, always thorough.

But the hollow rode down the stair with him, and by the third turning he had begun—quietly, professionally, with the full rigor of his considerable craft—to draft, in the privacy of his own skull, the opening clauses of a lawsuit against the Undercourt of the Thornfell Mountains.

Item the first: one morning, stolen.

Item the second: damages, pending discovery of what, exactly, he had lost.

He suspected he would find out. The court never released what it truly owned—but Sable Quill had never released what he truly wanted either, and the vault, the clerk had said, remained open.

Visiting hours were generous.

He intended to test that.
