## 8. The Market of Borrowed Voices

Cyrus had mapped a hundred markets in her life. She knew the grammar of them—the way smugglers' bazaars arranged themselves around exits, the way honest markets arranged themselves around churches, the way every market in every mountain town smelled of wet wool and overpriced spice. She had drawn them all, stall by stall, and then erased every drawing, because a map of a market was a map of everyone who shopped there.

The market beneath Thornfell did not follow the grammar. It followed music.

She understood this within thirty steps of entering it, and understanding made her stomach drop the way it dropped on a ledge when the rock told you, through your boots, that it had decided to be something else.

*Every song is a contract,* Odile had said that morning, in the tone of a woman reciting the alphabet to a child who would soon be tested on it. *You hum, you've agreed to something. You whistle, you've signed. If a merchant sings first and you finish the phrase, you have countersigned his price. Keep your mouth shut and buy nothing with a melody in it.*

*How do I buy anything?*

*Badly,* Odile had said, and looked almost pleased about it.

So Cyrus walked the bazaar with her lips sealed and her mapmaker's eyes open, and what she saw rearranged itself slowly, reluctantly, into sense.

The stalls were arranged in spirals. Not around an exit—there were no exits she could find, which was its own kind of obscenity—but around sound. At the center of each spiral stood something that sang: a fountain that hummed three notes over and over; a cage of thin silver holding what she first took for a bird and then, when it turned its head, very carefully did not look at again; a woman seated on a stool with a lute in her lap, playing nothing, wanting nothing, simply existing as a tuning point for the commerce that orbited her.

Everything orbited sound here. The stalls nearest the singers sold the cheap things—candles that burned in time to whatever was being sung nearby, ink that wrote only truths set to meter, bread. Even the bread. She watched a woman buy a loaf by humming four notes at the baker, and the baker hummed back six, and the difference between the two phrases was apparently the price, and the whole transaction took less time than breathing.

*Names are currency,* Odile had said. *But voices are the mint.*

There were voice-stalls.

Cyrus stopped in front of one without meaning to, the way you stop in front of an accident. It was built like a bird-market, rows of small cages, except the cages held no birds. They held mouths. Just mouths—lips and teeth and tongues, floating at the center of each cage like moths pinned mid-flight, and some of them were talking, muttering, singing snatches of songs in voices high and low and cracked and sweet, and a man behind the counter was conducting them with two fingers, drawing one voice forward out of the row like a vendor drawing forward a bolt of cloth.

"Soprano," the man said, to a customer whose face Cyrus could not make herself study too closely. "Unspoiled. Sang only lullabies before we took her. Listen."

He tapped the cage. The mouth inside began to sing, and the voice that came out was a young woman's voice, clear as meltwater, singing something in a language Cyrus didn't know, and the customer closed her eyes and swayed slightly, and Cyrus's whole body went cold with a fury so old-feeling it confused her. She had no history with this place. She had never been here. But her hands had curled into fists without consulting her, and her fists wanted to open cages.

*Don't,* said Odile's voice in her memory, flat as a ledger line. *Whatever you see in the market, don't. The court calls these arrangements voluntary. Whether or not that's true is a question with teeth, and you are not armed well enough to bite anything yet.*

Cyrus uncurled her fingers. One at a time. She made herself walk on.

* * *

Sable found her an hour later, which was impressive, because she had been careful not to be findable. He appeared at her shoulder beside a stall that sold echoes—jars of them, labeled in a script that moved when she read it—and he did not startle her, exactly, but her hand had gone to her knife before her mind caught up, and he regarded the gesture with the mild appreciation of a man grading form.

"Reflexes intact," he said. "Good. The market eats the flinchers first."

"Where's Odile?"

"Arguing with a lamp-seller about whether a wick counts as a promise." He examined the echo jars with professional distaste. "It does, incidentally. Everything here counts as something. That's rather the point of the place."

"What are they?" She nodded at the jars.

"Echoes. Bought from their original locations. There's a stretch of cliff above Greywater where the wind screams if you stand in the right spot—someone owns that now. Sells it by the second." He tilted his head. "You're looking pale for someone who grew up smuggling contraband past toll-wards."

"I've never smuggled anything that used to be attached to a person."

"Ah." He smiled, and it was his courtroom smile, the one that meant he was about to say something true and wanted deniability anyway. "Then let me offer you the comfort of technicality. Nothing here was attached to a person. It *was* the person, or part of one, but attachment ended at sale. The court is very precise about wording. Precision is how they sleep."

"And how do you sleep?"

"I don't own a bed worth the name." He drifted along the stall, reading labels. His composure was back—all of it, seamless—and she might have believed yesterday's crack in it had never happened, except that he kept his gloves on even down here, even in this damp, and one gloved finger kept touching the hollow of his own throat, lightly, absently, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. "Come. You haven't seen the deep rows yet, and you will insist on seeing them eventually, so it may as well be with someone who knows which stalls are traps."

"Which ones?"

"All the ones with good prices."

* * *

The deep rows were quieter. That was the first wrongness. Sound thinned as they walked inward, the way light thins near a cave mouth, until their footsteps arrived late and softened, and Cyrus realized with a lurch that the market was *listening* to them—that the silence wasn't emptiness but attention, thousands of purchased ears hung in the dark like lanterns, all of them turned politely toward whatever passed.

She thought of every secret she'd ever buried in the mountains. She thought of the ledger, and the name in old ink.

*Zephyrine.*

"Don't think loudly," Sable murmured, without looking at her. "I mean it kindly. Some of these are memory-voices. They repeat what they hear near them. Not all of their owners still exist."

Cyrus pulled her thoughts in tight around the name, wrapped it, sank it. Mapmaker's discipline: the treasure goes in the deepest cache, the trail brushed clean behind you.

The deep-row stalls sold stranger stock. A booth where a woman sold last words—guaranteed authentic, spoken into her collection-horn at bedsides across three kingdoms. A booth selling arguments, boxed and ready, *for negotiations, weddings, and sieges.* A booth with no goods at all, just a chair and a sign reading **SILENCE BY THE HOUR**, and Cyrus stared at it longer than she meant to, because Odile had called silence the only shield, and here it was, retail.

"You're wondering what silence costs," Sable said.

"I'm wondering why anyone here would need to buy it."

"The powerful," Sable said, "are surrounded by song from the moment they wake. Every courtesy is sung, every oath, every greeting. The court runs on music the way your surface courts run on paper. Do you know what that means, after a century?" He picked up a jar of echoes and set it down without buying. "It means no one up there has heard actual quiet since before your grandmother was born. Silence is the rarest commodity in the mountain. The lords buy it the way surface lords buy snow from mountaintops—wrapped in cloth, kept in cellars, opened once a year to remember what it felt like."

"To feel nothing."

"To feel *unowned*," Sable said. "For an hour."

They came around a bend of stalls, into a wider dark, and that was where Cyrus heard the voice.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe it to herself and fail, because the description kept collapsing into the wrong words. It was a working voice—not trained, not courtly, roughened at the edges the way a rope goes rough at the coil—and it was singing low, half under its breath, the way people sing when they think no one is listening, a song with no shape she recognized, in no language she knew, and none of that was the problem.

The problem was that her body knew it.

That was the only way to say it. Her body knew it the way her feet knew a pass she'd walked once, twenty years ago, in fog: not the knowledge of the mind but the older kind, the kind that lives in balance and breath and the small muscles around the eyes. Her throat ached. Actually ached, physically, as if the song were a hook set in her sternum and someone had just taken up the slack. And underneath the ache, rising fast and irrational as floodwater, came a longing so specific and so enormous that she had to put her hand against a stall-post to keep upright—a longing shaped like *home*, except Cyrus Vane had never had a home, had erased every address she'd ever slept at, had spent thirty-one years making sure nothing anywhere could call to her by name—

—and something in the dark was calling her anyway, wordless, tuneless, careless, like a person humming while they work, not knowing anyone is standing in the doorway listening.

"Cyrus." Sable's voice, low. "Breathe."

She hadn't noticed she'd stopped. She hadn't noticed her eyes were burning. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at it, and looked down the aisle toward the sound.

A stall at the end, lit by one cold lamp. Cages again—but larger, better-made, the cages of expensive stock. And in the nearest cage, perched on a wooden swing-bar, sat a singer: a young woman, thin, dressed in the grey uniform of court choristry, hair cropped short in the practical way of someone whose hair is maintained by an institution. Her eyes were fixed on the middle distance, on nothing. Her mouth was moving. The song poured out of her, low and looping and heartbreakingly ordinary, the way a person sings when the singing isn't theirs anymore—when it's been assigned, scheduled, owned—and her face, while she sang, was completely empty.

Except that the voice wasn't empty. The voice was *familiar*. It was so familiar that Cyrus took another step, and another, dragging Sable's warning behind her like a coat she'd forgotten she was wearing, and stopped at the edge of the lamplight, and listened, and the song went on, and the ache in her chest went on, and she thought, with the calm of total freefall:

*I know that voice. Whose voice do I know?*

Not Marla's. Not any customer's, any caravan-mate's, any lover's—she had kept those at arm's length too, all of them, every voice catalogued and released. She knew perhaps five voices in the world, and none of them were this one, and this one knew her anyway. It knew her somewhere below knowing, in the place where you learn sound before you're born—in the dark before the dark, in the crowded water where two heartbeats share one room—

The singer stopped.

Mid-phrase. As though something had plucked the thread. Her empty eyes came into focus, slowly, like a lens finding distance, and turned, and fixed on Cyrus with an expression that was not empty at all anymore. It was puzzled. Wounded. It was the expression of someone hearing their own name called from a house they'd been told had burned down.

And Cyrus, throat sealed, contracts be damned, nearly broke her own rule right there—nearly answered, nearly finished the phrase, nearly signed God-knew-what with the only voice she had—because the singer's mouth shaped something silent through the cage bars, a single word, and Cyrus read her lips the way she read contour lines, instantly, helplessly:

*Wren?*

No. That couldn't be. The singer had said it, not asked it—but her brow was furrowed like a question, like the word was strange in her own mouth, like she'd found it written somewhere and was checking it against the world.

"Wren," Cyrus repeated aloud, barely, and the moment the syllables left her the market *moved*—every hanging ear in the deep rows turning at once, every muttering mouth going silent, the whole attentive dark snapping toward her like a compass needle finding north—and Sable's hand closed on her arm hard enough to bruise.

"Don't," he breathed. "Don't say it again. Whatever that word is, it isn't yours to say down here, and the market has already written it down."

The singer was pressing both hands flat against the inside of the cage bars now, staring, mouthing the word again—*wren, wren*—her empty institutional face cracking open into something desperate and young, and behind Cyrus, from the shadows of the stall, came the dry click of a counter-bell being rung, and a voice like oiled hinges said:

"That one's not for sale, surface girl. Choir stock. But you can *look* as long as you like."

Cyrus looked. The singer looked back. Between them hung the word neither of them should have known, and the cold lamp burned steady, and somewhere far above them, muffled through a hundred fathoms of stone, the mountain began—if such a thing could be believed—to rehearse.
