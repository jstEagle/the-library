## 9. Rules of the Court Beneath

I had sworn I would never walk these halls again.

The corridor to Lord Ivoriel's receiving chamber was exactly as I remembered it: walls of pale root-stone veined with silver, ceilings ribbed like the insides of shells, and everywhere—everywhere—the hum. The Undercourt did not have silence the way the surface did. It had a baseline, a low sustained note that lived in the stone itself, and once you had been a singer here you never stopped hearing it, even in exile. Thirty years above ground, and still some nights I woke with that note in my teeth.

"Odile." Sable Quill's voice came soft at my shoulder. He had put away his collector's smile two corridors ago, which told me how seriously he was taking this. "Your feet stopped."

"I know where they stopped."

Cyrus Vane looked between us, mapmaker's eyes cataloging angles, exits, distances. She'd been doing it since we came through the smuggler's gate, and I will grant her this: she was better at hiding fear than most people are at feeling it. "How long do we have?"

"The audience begins when the bells stop." Above us—or what passed for above—a carillon was ringing down through the stone, each tone hanging in the air like something poured. "And when it begins, you speak only when spoken to, you accept nothing without giving something of equal weight, and you do not—" I turned and took her by the collar of her coat, because some lessons require hands, "—under any circumstances, sing. Hum. Whistle. Tap a rhythm. Nothing with pitch leaves your mouth."

"Not even to be polite?"

"*Especially* not to be polite. Politeness here is a trapdoor."

She nodded. Her pulse was jumping under my fingers. Good. Fear would keep her careful; certainty would kill her.

Sable produced a small lacquered case from inside his coat and opened it on his palm. Three thin bands of dark metal, unadorned. "Audience tokens," he said. "Guest-right, provisional. Wear them on the right wrist, keep them visible, and understand that they are not protection. They are *inventory tags*. They tell the court you are accounted for—which means if anything happens to you, someone owes the court an explanation. Preferably that someone is me."

"And who accounts for you?" Cyrus asked.

He smiled, and this time it reached all the way to something that wasn't quite a smile. "No one, Miss Vane. That is rather the point of me."

We took the bands. The metal was warm, faintly throbbing, tuned to some private frequency. I slid mine over my wrist and felt the old shame click shut around it like a shackle dressed as jewelry.

The bells ceased. The silence afterward was worse than the humming—it had texture, expectation, the held breath of an instrument before the downbeat.

"Now," I said. "Walk like you belong to someone. In this court, belonging is safer than freedom."

* * *

Lord Ivoriel's receiving chamber was a bowl of white stone, tiered like an amphitheater, and he sat at its bottom the way a tuning fork sits in its resonator—at the center of everything that made the room work. He was minor nobility, third-rank, which meant he was dangerous the way mid-sized predators are dangerous: hungry enough to prove himself, small enough to need the proof. His skin had the gray-silver sheen they all had, deep-court blood, and his ears were heavy with rings that chimed softly when he turned his head.

He knew me the moment we entered. I watched it happen—the flicker, the recalculation, the slow spreading delight of a man handed an embarrassment he gets to enjoy publicly.

"Lady Ferrant," he said, and the title landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Every eye in the gallery turned. There were perhaps thirty courtiers arranged on the tiers, gray and elegant and utterly still, and all of them were now listening to me with the particular attention of people who had attended my disgrace as entertainment three decades ago and hoped for a revival.

"That rank was revoked," I said. "Along with everything attached to it."

"Was it?" Ivoriel rose, unhurried, rings whispering. "Revocation requires a formal severance song, sung by the revoking authority. Maestra Corvane sang yours—yes, I remember the occasion, I was in the second tier—and she died forty years ago. Tell me, exile." He descended one step, then another. "Who inherited her songs?"

Nobody answered. Nobody ever answers rhetorical questions in this court, because rhetorical questions are usually bait.

But I am old, and bitter, and I had spent the entire climb down rehearsing for exactly this.

"You did," I said. "By succession of voice. Which means, my lord, that until you formally re-sing the severance, my rank is not revoked. It is *held in trust*. By you."

The silence that followed had a different quality than before. Several courtiers on the upper tiers leaned forward by a degree so small it might have been imagined. One of them—I recognized her, Lissandre, fourth cello of the old guard—let out a breath she had clearly been holding since I walked in.

Ivoriel's face did something complicated. To his credit, he recovered quickly. "A technicality."

"All law is technicality," Sable said pleasantly, from behind my shoulder. "It's why we get along so well, my lord. You might check the registry, but registries are tedious, and your guests are standing."

That was Sable Quill's gift: he could say *you're being tested and failing* in a tone that sounded like flattery. Ivoriel's jaw tightened, and then, with the fluid grace of a man converting defeat into theater, he gestured us down into the bowl.

"Then let us honor the technicality. Lady Ferrant presents guests under her standing. Come. Sit. Let us see what the surface sends me tonight."

* * *

What followed was ninety minutes of the most precise conversation I have ever survived, and I include in that count the time I argued contract-law with the Registrar of Names herself.

Ivoriel wanted three things, and he circled them the way a hawk circles, pretending disinterest. First, why we had come—he asked four times in four different forms, each version a slightly different key, and each time Odile's answer, or Sable's answer, or Cyrus's careful non-answer had to resolve in the same chord or the discrepancy became a debt. Second, what we carried—guest tokens declared our existence, but not our purpose, and purpose must be volunteered or extracted, never assumed. Third, and most urgently, he wanted Cyrus to make a mistake.

I could see him wanting it. She was the unknown quantity: surface-born, unregistered scent, no court-tuning in her speech. Everything else about her could be filed. Her presence here was a loose thread in a garment Ivoriel very much wanted an excuse to unravel.

"So," he said, somewhere past the first hour, leaning back with his rings chiming. "The mapmaker. You chart passages that do not officially exist. You erase yourself from records. You come beneath the mountain days before the centennial Singing, carrying a disgraced maestra and a collector whose own ledger shows—" he consulted a sheet of music-paper that lay on the table before him, and I saw Sable go very still, "—considerable irregularity. And you claim no purpose. What *are* you, Cyrus Vane?"

And here is what I will say about Cyrus Vane, whatever else history decides about her: she understood faster than any student I ever taught that silence is a shield.

She said nothing.

Ten seconds. Twenty. The bowl of the chamber amplified every small sound—the chime of a ring as Ivoriel tilted his head, the rustle of a courtier's sleeve. Sable's stillness had gone from professional to absolute. My own heart was a drum I willed quiet, because a racing heart has a rhythm, and rhythm can be read.

Thirty seconds. Forty.

"You find silence rude?" Ivoriel said at last, silk over gravel.

"I find it *expensive*," Cyrus said, "and I've been advised not to spend what I can't afford."

A ripple went through the tiers—not laughter, the court does not laugh, but the sub-audible equivalent, a shifting of weight, a collective intake adjusted by a quarter-tone. Ivoriel studied her for a long moment, and then he did the worst thing he could have done.

He laughed himself. Openly, ringing, delighted.

"Oh, they will *enjoy* you at the Singing," he said. "Yes. Very well. Lady Ferrant holds your guest-right; the debt is hers to carry and hers to answer for. Standard terms: safe passage to the Archive district and back, no detours, no unregistered transactions." His eyes glittered. "And one condition of my own, as holder of your rank, maestra. When the Choir sings the centennial Song, you will attend. In the second tier. Where you sat the night you were cast out."

There it was. Not cruelty for cruelty's sake—worse. *Ceremony.* He wanted me in the seat, wearing my shame like a reserved ticket, while the institution that broke me performed its greatest triumph.

"Granted," I heard myself say, before pride could cost us anything.

* * *

We were dismissed with a gesture that took three full seconds to complete, which in court-time is a small essay on our insignificance, and escorted out by two pages who walked in perfect canon with each other, one always half a beat behind.

Only when the corridor doors had sealed behind us did Cyrus exhale.

"What just happened?" she demanded. "The silence thing—you looked like you were going to pass out."

"Because what you did was either brilliant or fatal, and I genuinely didn't know which until he laughed." I leaned against the root-stone wall. My knees had opinions now that the audience was over. "Silence in a formal audience is not rudeness. It's a *bid*. You were offering nothing, which meant he had to fill the space, and whoever fills the space sets the next term. But if he had decided instead that your silence was a challenge to his rank—" I drew a line across my throat. "Enslavement is the standard penalty. Your voice would be in the market by morning. I've seen it done over less."

"She didn't blink," Sable said. There was something in his voice I hadn't heard before. It took me a moment to identify it as respect. "Not once. I have watched trained negotiators crumble in that room."

"She's a smuggler," I said. "She's spent her whole life not answering questions."

Cyrus was looking at me strangely. "You called yourself *Lady* back at him. You let him think—Odile, what did you trade? Rank held in trust—that means when this is over, he owns your seat. Whatever happens, you owe that room a debt."

"I know what I traded."

"Do you?" Sable's tone was gentle in a way that was almost unbearable. "Because from where I stood, you didn't just hold your rank. You bound yourself to attend the Singing. In the seat where they ended you. That is not a toll, maestra. That is a hook."

"It's also the only way any of us get within a hundred yards of the Choir," I snapped, and pushed off the wall and kept walking, because if I stopped walking I was going to have to feel what I had just agreed to.

They followed. Behind us, the corridor hummed its endless single note, and ahead, at the junction where the noble districts gave way to the Archive quarter, the light changed—colder, bluer, lit by veins of luminous lichen running through the stone in patterns I had once been able to read like staff-lines.

I could still read them. That was the humiliation of it. Thirty years and the walls still spoke to me in notation, and some traitorous part of me was glad.

* * *

The Archive district announced itself by weight. Not sound—weight. Somewhere beneath our feet, in vaults cut into the mountain's oldest bone, every soul-contract ever signed by or against the Undercourt sat in its appointed place, and the mass of all that binding pressed upward through the stone until you felt it in your joints, like weather.

"We rest here," I said, stopping at the mouth of an alley between two archive-houses. "The district seals at this bell"—the carillon overhead began a descending figure, eight notes falling to one—"and opens at the rising figure. Nothing moves between archives during seal. Even collectors sleep."

"Most collectors," Sable murmured.

"Even you?"

"For the duration of our arrangement," he said, "even me."

Cyrus had gone quiet again, but it was a different quiet than the audience-chamber. She was staring up at the archive-house across the alley—at the lintel above its sealed doors, where a line of names was carved in the old convention, ledger-style, the founding donors of the vault. I watched her lips move, reading. Watched her go pale in the blue lichen-light.

"Odile." Her voice was very steady, which is how I knew. "The contracts in there. Are they indexed by name?"

"By true name. Why?"

She didn't answer at first. Her thumb moved over the guest-token on her wrist, back and forth, back and forth, a rhythm she didn't seem to know she was keeping—and I thought, absurdly, *stop that, someone will read you*—and then she looked at me with an expression I had last seen on the face of a young chorister, decades ago, the moment before she opened a score she already knew would break her heart.

"Tomorrow," Cyrus Vane said, "I'm going to find my name in there."

She turned toward the sealed doors of the archive, and the carillon above us finished its descent, and the last note hung in the blue air like a held breath—

—and then it resolved, unexpectedly, into a chord I hadn't heard sung in thirty years. A summons-chord. An assembly of the Choir.

Somewhere below us, in the great rehearsal vaults, the centennial Song had begun to practice.

And underneath it, threading through the harmony like a silver wire through gray cloth, ran a voice I did not recognize—and Cyrus Vane, frozen on the steps with her hand pressed flat against the archive door, clearly did.
