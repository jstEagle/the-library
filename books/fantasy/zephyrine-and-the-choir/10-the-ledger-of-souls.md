## 10. The Ledger of Souls

The soul-archive sat beneath the Court of Small Hours like a stone heart in a stone chest, and every map Odile had ever drawn of it was wrong.

"You're certain," Cyrus said for the fourth time, crouched in the service corridor where the walls were grown, not built—pale root-stone veined with something that pulsed faintly, slow as a sleeping pulse.

"I'm certain I was dragged down this corridor in chains," Odile said. "Whether the archive has moved since, or whether my memory of it was edited on my way out, is a question I'd rather you not ask me again."

Cyrus let it go. She had learned, in six days beneath the mountain, which questions had edges.

She unrolled her own work instead—the map she'd been building since they passed the toll gate, inked on smuggler's vellum that didn't take enchantment well. That was why she used it. Everything in the Undercourt could be rewritten by anyone with standing; her maps couldn't. They were the only honest surfaces down here, and she trusted them the way she'd never trusted anything she hadn't made herself.

The corridor branched three ways ahead. According to Odile's memory, left led to the archive's antechamber. According to the draft in the air—cold moving against the current of warm breath from the court above—it came from the right.

"Right," Cyrus said, and went right, and forty paces later stood before a door with no handle, no hinges, no seam except the one her fingertips found: a line of silence so absolute it felt like pressing a thumb into a blind eye.

Songs are contracts. Silence is the shield. Odile's first lesson, and still the truest. Every corridor in this place hummed with some low binding melody, ward-songs woven into the stone, and you walked through them at your peril because to hear them was to begin agreeing with them. But silence—true silence—was nothing the court had ever thought to claim. It belonged to no one. Which meant it could be entered by anyone.

Cyrus pressed her palm flat against the door and did not sing, did not hum, did not think in rhythm. She emptied herself the way she'd once emptied ledgers, striking her name from page after page until even the ink forgot her. The silence recognized a fellow citizen. The seam breathed open.

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and cold iron and, underneath both, something sweetly rotten—like fruit left too long in a sealed room. Her eyes adjusted to a gloom that wasn't entirely dark. The shelves gave off a light of their own, a grey phosphorescence rising off ten thousand spines, ten thousand ledgers, ten thousand souls filed in order.

* * *

They were arranged alphabetically by name.

That detail hit her harder than the scale of the place did. She had expected horror—jars, cages, screams in bottles. Instead there was bookkeeping. Row upon row of identical volumes, each spine bearing a single word in silvered script, and the words were names. Ordinary names. Peasant names, merchant names, a few noble ones shelved apart in a glass case like vintage wine. Souls, catalogued. Souls, cross-referenced.

Somewhere in this room was a ledger with *her* on the spine, and the thought made her skin try to leave her body.

*Vane,* she reminded herself. *You're looking for Vane.*

Except she wasn't. Not anymore. Six days of Odile's instruction had taught her that the name on a contract was the binding one, and Marla had sworn—and broken, and confessed, and wept—that the name her mother had signed away was not Vane. It was the other one. The true one. The forgotten one. The name Cyrus had spent twenty-six years not knowing she lacked, and which now sat inside her like a swallowed coal.

She'd told no one the name. Not Sable, whose curiosity she trusted least when it was most sincere. Not Odile, who had gone strange and quiet when the prophecy was first read aloud, in a way Cyrus had logged but not yet decoded. The name lived only in her mouth, unspoken, and in the old ink of a ledger in a bookbinder's shop a hundred miles and one world away.

But contracts have copies. That was the whole architecture of the Undercourt—every song a contract, every contract recorded, every record enforceable. If her mother had sold a soul here, the original instrument was on one of these shelves.

She started walking the aisles, reading spines, keeping her breathing shallow. Names slid past: *Alder, Ashdown, Bellamy.* She counted shelf-ends to keep her bearings, mapping as she moved, because that was what her hands did when her mind was afraid—they drew.

She found the section for V-names near the back, in the newer stacks, where the bindings were less cracked. *Valen. Vance. Varo. Vashti.* No Vane.

Of course not. Vane was the name she'd built, brick by brick, over a lifetime of erasure—a name chosen precisely because it attached to nothing. The court wouldn't hold a contract on a fiction.

Which meant the contract bore the other name. And she would have to speak it—or worse, think it clearly enough to find its shelf—to look for it here.

She stopped between two shelves and pressed her back against the cold and let herself be afraid for exactly ten breaths. Then she spent the name.

Not aloud. Into the dark, silently, deliberately, the way you spend coin you can't get back: *Zephyrine.*

Nothing happened. No bell rang, no ward flared. But somewhere in the grey-lit rows, a book seemed—she couldn't swear to it—seemed to lean toward her, the way a candle flame leans when a door opens far across a room.

She followed the feeling. Third aisle from the east wall, second stack from the floor, between *Zabala* and *Zenoir.*

*Zephyrine.*

The spine was old. Older than any other binding in the row—the leather gone soft and dark as plum skin, the silver lettering faded to a ghost of itself. The books around it were recent, decades old at most. Hers predated them all. A century-old contract, filed among the newborns, waiting.

Her hands were shaking. She took the ledger down anyway.

It was thin. Contracts, apparently, were brief things; whole eternities fit in a page or two. The cover bore a court seal she didn't recognize—a circle of eight notes, closed upon themselves—and beneath it, in the formal script Odile had drilled into her:

**INSTRUMENT OF SALE — ONE SOUL, ENTIRE AND INDIVISIBLE**

She turned to the first page and read it three times, because the first two times her eyes refused to assemble the words into sense.

*Be it known in the Court Beneath, on this day and date inscribed, that the bearer does convey unto the Court one (1) soul entire, being that of the child Zephyrine, born of Lys Corvane, midwife attending, in consideration whereof the Court shall suffer the said child's twin to live and thrive beyond the mountain, unclaimed and unrecorded, forever.*

Cyrus read it a fourth time. A fifth.

The world tilted and she put a hand on the shelf to steady herself, and the shelf—she felt this distinctly, though no one would ever believe it—felt steadied by *her.*

*The child Zephyrine.*

Not her sister. Not Wren. The contract named *her*. The sold soul, the bargain's price, the thing owed to the court tonight at the Choir's singing—it was hers. The prophecy had been right. Marla's fragments had been wrong, or half-wrong, or wrong in the way grief makes things wrong. Her mother had sold *Cyrus*, and bought the freedom of—

The twin. The surviving twin. Wren.

So the story ran. So the ink said.

And then she saw the dates.

* * *

Contracts in the Undercourt were precise about time. Odile had been merciless on this point: *dates are load-bearing, girl. A contract's power lives in its calendar.* Cyrus had mocked her for it once, early, and earned a stare that could have curdled milk at forty paces.

Now she read the dates the way she read contour lines—with everything she had.

**Date of birth of the child Zephyrine, per record of the parish of Thornfell Lower:** the third of Harvestmonth, in the year the old king died.

She knew that date. It was carved on the marker Marla kept under loose floorboards, the little stone she'd never explain, the one Cyrus had assumed was her parents' grave-marker. Third of Harvestmonth.

**Date of signature:** the fourth of Harvestmonth.

One day later. Fine. Reasonable. A midwife, a desperate mother, a deal struck fast—signed the day after the birth. That fit.

Except.

Below the signature block, in smaller script, the standard attestations. And the first attestation read:

***Witnessed and sealed in the presence of the infant party, being of sound mind and full understanding, who signs her mark below.***

There was a mark. A small, wobbly, unmistakably childish scrawl at the bottom of a century-old contract, attested as the signature of an infant one day old, *of sound mind and full understanding.*

Cyrus sat down on the floor. The archive's grey light pooled around her. She was distantly aware that sitting on the floor of the soul-archive was probably fatal if discovered, and equally distant from caring.

Infants don't sign contracts. Infants don't have sound minds or full understanding. Unless the attestation wasn't describing the signing. Unless the mark wasn't made at birth at all—unless it had been *added later,* by the party herself, when she was old enough to hold a pen. Amended. Updated. Reaffirmed.

She turned to the last page, hunting for amendment clauses, and found one. A single line, appended in different ink—fresher ink, ink no more than a few decades old:

***Reaffirmed by the party, being come of age, freely and with knowledge.***

No date on the reaffirmation. No age given. Just those words, *freely and with knowledge,* sitting there like a knife someone had left in a doorway.

Freely. With knowledge. Someone had come of age, learned what the contract held, and signed it again.

Marla hadn't mentioned that. Marla's confession—wrung out of her through a scrying mirror while she wept like a woman confessing a murder—had been all fire and flight and a mother running, a bargain made in desperation over a newborn. Nothing about a second signature. Nothing about *knowledge.*

Unless the second hand wasn't her mother's.

Cyrus looked at the childish mark again. Looked at her own hand, holding the book. She had always written fast and ugly, letters leaning like drunks, the tail of her capital letters looping shut. The mark on the contract looped shut.

It could be coincidence. Handwriting echoed down families; she'd seen it in a hundred ledgers.

Or.

Or the party to this contract had signed twice—once as an infant, impossibly, and once as a grown woman, knowingly—and the grown woman's hand looked like hers because the grown woman *was* her, in a way that made no sense, in a year that hadn't happened yet, or had happened differently, or—

*Stop.* She made herself breathe. Made herself do the thing she did with impossible terrain: stop arguing with it and start measuring it.

Facts. One: the contract named Zephyrine, not Wren, as the sold soul. Two: the consideration—the payment—was the twin's life and freedom beyond the mountain. So Wren was the one saved, and she was the one sold. Three: the signature postdated the birth by one day, but the attestation described an infant signing knowingly, which was either fraud or impossibility. Four: someone had reaffirmed the contract later, freely, with knowledge, in handwriting that matched her own.

Five: the prophecy had named her. Had called her down here by the true name, for tonight, when the Choir sang and the court claimed what it was owed.

If she was the sold soul, the prophecy made clean, terrible sense. The debt was due. She was the debt.

But the dates didn't fit. And Cyrus Vane had survived twenty-six years in the smuggling trade on exactly one principle: *when the numbers don't fit the story, it's the story that's lying.*

Someone had forged something. Or amended something. Or—her mind snagged on Odile's face when the prophecy was read, that strange stillness, that look of a woman watching a trap she remembered setting—

Or the contract had been rewritten once already, by someone with standing, and the version in her hands was not the original at all.

She flipped back to the cover, hunting for a registration number, the court's filing marks, anything that would tell her whether this was the master instrument or a copy. And there, embossed in the lower corner, so faint she'd missed it twice: a small stamped sigil, half worn away.

Not the circle of eight notes.

A bird. A small, plain bird, wings folded—*a wren*—struck through with a single diagonal line.

Erased. A mark of erasure, the kind archivists used for stricken entries. Except it wasn't on an entry. It was on the *binding.*

The archive went very quiet around her—which it had been all along, and yet somehow it got quieter, the silence tightening like a held breath.

Behind her, at the end of the aisle, someone cleared their throat.

Cyrus was on her feet with a knife in her hand before the sound finished, the ledger pressed to her chest, and she already knew—who else moved that softly down here, who else wore silence like tailored cloth—but knowing didn't stop her heart from slamming.

Sable Quill stood at the mouth of the aisle, elegant as ever in his grey coat, his ruined voice folded away behind the smile he wore the way other men wore weapons. In his gloved hands he carried a small lacquered tray, and on the tray, absurdly, domestic as a servant in a nightmare, sat a pot of tea and two cups.

"You're sitting," he said, "on the floor of the most dangerous room under the mountain, holding a document that could buy the court or burn it." He set the tray down on a shelf-end, unhurried, and poured. "I have questions. But first—" his pale eyes lifted to hers, and for the first time since she'd met him there was nothing charming in them at all, only an attention like a blade laid flat against skin, "—first I want to know why the ledger you're holding just changed its own dates."

Cyrus looked down.

On the first page, in ink that was suddenly, horribly fresh, the date of signature had begun to move—digits crawling like insects, rearranging, settling—into a new date.

Tonight's date.
