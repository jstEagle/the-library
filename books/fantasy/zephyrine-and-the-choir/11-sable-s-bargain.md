## 11. Sable's Bargain

Sable Quill did not sleep. This was not a boast; it was simply a fact of his anatomy, like the too-smooth throat or the way candlelight passed through him a half-second late, as though deciding whether he deserved to be illuminated. So while the others bedded down in the smugglers' waystation—Odile against the far wall with her back to the room, Cyrus curled near the dead fire with her pack buckled to her wrist like a woman who expected the world to pickpocket her—he sat by the window and watched the mountain.

Thornfell at night was a held breath. The stars came down close enough to read by, and the peak wore its snow like a collar turned up against the century. Somewhere under all that stone, the Choir was rehearsing. He could feel it the way a man feels a tooth: low, constant, impossible to ignore. A hum in the rock. A tuning before the performance that would renew the court's lease on everything it owned.

And it owned so much. Fields and fault lines. Names and voices. One disgraced choir mistress who thought her hatred made her invisible. And one mapmaker who had spent thirty years erasing herself from every ledger in the range—who had, through diligence Sable found genuinely admirable, unmade herself so thoroughly that even his masters had needed a prophecy written in old ink to remember she existed.

He heard the girl stir. Of course he did; he heard everything now, in the muffled, underwater way he heard anything. Her breathing changed from sleep to pretending.

"You've been watching me for an hour," Cyrus said to the fireplace.

"I've been watching the fire," Sable said. "You're merely between me and it."

"Lucky me." She rolled over, sat up, kept her hand on her knife with the casualness of a woman adjusting a sleeve. "Debt-collectors don't watch people sleep for professional reasons unless they're planning to collect."

"Your debt isn't due until the full moon. I'm punctual, not greedy."

"That's a distinction without much difference from where I'm sitting."

He smiled. He had a good smile; he'd bought it secondhand decades ago from a dying actor, and it still performed. "Ask your question, Miss Vane. You've been circling it since the toll gate. You circle questions the way you circle mountains."

The fire popped. Odile didn't move, but Sable noted the change in her breathing—the shallow attention of a woman listening while pretending to sleep. Let her listen. Some truths were better overheard than offered twice.

Cyrus came and sat across from him, cross-legged, knife loose in her lap now, which he took as a compliment or a dare. Up close her eyes were the grey of pencil lead, and they studied him like terrain.

"At the toll gate," she said. "You paid in memories. Three of them. The gatekeeper took them and you—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide to be precise, because precision was her weapon the way music was Odile's, "—you flinched. Not when you handed them over. After. When you tried to remember what you'd given up and couldn't. You stood there doing arithmetic on your own face. What did you lose?"

"Ah." Sable looked back at the mountain. "There it is."

"You don't have to answer."

"No," he agreed. "But you've paid your own tolls to get here, haven't you? Pride, mostly, and the comfortable lie that nobody knows your name. And I watched you pay those without flinching. It seems rude not to match you."

* * *

He had been born, he told her, in the fen country south of Thornfell, in a year she would not find in any record because he had personally removed himself from all of them. He knew the trick of it. That was why he'd been assigned to her case—no one else in the Undercourt's service understood self-erasure from the inside.

"There were two of us," he said. "My brother Tam and I. Our mother died of the marsh fever when I was nine, and there was no one, so we did what fencrawlers do. We ran cargo. Nothing wicked at first—tobacco, untaxed brandy, letters for people who wanted letters to arrive quietly. By fourteen I was drawing maps. Good ones. I could smell a pass before I saw it, the way some people can smell rain."

Cyrus's mouth twitched. "Familiar."

"Everything about this story will be familiar. That's rather the point." He folded his hands. His hands were beautiful; he'd kept them even after everything else went. "Tam couldn't map but he could sing. Marsh songs, boat songs, the long keening things the fen women sang at funerals—and oh, he could *sing*, Miss Vane. When Tam opened his mouth, gulls came down to listen. Men put down their oars. Once, in Saltmarket, a woman gave him her wedding ring just to hear him finish a verse. We laughed about that for a week. We ate well on songs."

He paused. The pause lasted longer than he intended. Cyrus, to her credit, let it.

"When he was seventeen he coughed blood into his cupped hand, and we both pretended he hadn't. Fen fever. Slow one. There's a cure, if you're rich, and we weren't, and I was fifteen and desperate and I knew—I *knew*, the way you know where a pass is before you see it—that the stories about the Undercourt were true, because I'd mapped three gates to it without knowing what I was mapping. Smuggler's doors. Toll gates. Places where the mountain takes payment and gives you passage."

"The market," Cyrus said quietly. "The Market of Borrowed Voices."

"Word travels. Yes. There's a stall there—well, it moves, stalls do—but there's a broker who buys and sells in the raw. Voices, years, luck, first memories. And there's a desk behind the stall where contracts are drawn, and behind the desk sits a clerk who has never once looked up from the page, and if you sign, the court pays in whatever currency you've asked for. Gold is the cheap option. Miracles cost more."

"You sold your voice."

"I sold my voice to buy my brother's cure." Sable said it flatly, a fact laid on the table like a coin. "Not a miracle. Just medicine. The best physicians in Saltmarket, paid in Undercourt gold that no tax assessor ever questioned. Tam lived to be sixty-one. Married a fisherman's daughter. Had four children, one of whom has my mother's crooked smile. I attended none of it. I signed also a term of service—standard, they told me, standard—collecting debts for the court until such time as the court considered its ledgers balanced." He spread his beautiful hands. "I have been balancing them for forty-three years."

The fire had burned down to a red eye. In the window, the mountain kept its silence, but the hum in the rock seemed, if anything, closer.

"What happened to your voice?" Cyrus asked. "Did they keep it?"

"It's here." He touched his throat, that too-smooth throat. "They don't destroy what they buy. They *hold* it. Somewhere below us, right now, there's a shelf—or I imagine there's a shelf; I've never been permitted in that archive—with a small jar, or a locket, or whatever vessel fashion dictates this century, and inside it is a nineteen-year-old boy singing boat songs to make gulls come down. That's how I think of it. It helps to think of it as somewhere specific."

"And you've never tried to buy it back?"

Sable laughed. It came out of him wrong, the way all his sounds came out of him wrong—pitchless, frictionless, a laugh with the music surgically removed. "Buy it back. Miss Vane. I collect for them. I know their books better than any clerk alive. There is no price listed for what I own, because there is no price. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Everything else tonight—the prophecy, the descent, Odile's grand plans—they're all built on the same error. The belief that the Undercourt is a marketplace. It is not. It is a *hoard*. Markets want flow; hoards want weight. They sell the small things to bait the large ones. Your voice, my voice, a memory of your mother's face—we're gravel to them. But a soul sold before birth, named in a prophecy, walking willingly through a toll gate?" He shook his head slowly. "That's treasure. And no hoard lets go of treasure."

Across the room, Odile's breathing stayed carefully, theatrically even. Sable raised his voice just enough.

"You may as well hear the rest, Mistress Ferrant, since you'll need it for whatever scheme you're building."

A beat. Then Odile sat up, unhurried, as if she'd merely decided the moment required it. "Continue, then."

"The warning is simple." Sable leaned forward, and for once he let the actor's smile drop entirely, and what was underneath was just tired—a very old tiredness wearing a young man's borrowed face. "The court never releases what it truly owns. It releases what it *thinks* it owns, gladly, because mistakes amuse them and the correction costs more than the original sale. But true ownership—contracted, witnessed, sung into the law—they will defend the way a body defends blood. If you go down there expecting to bargain your way out with cleverness, you'll leave with less than you came with. Possibly with nothing. Possibly with less than nothing; they can take the nothing you would have been."

"So what do we do?" Cyrus asked. "You're our guide as much as Odile is. Don't give me doom; give me terrain."

He looked at her—at this woman who had mapped every hidden road in the range except the one out of her own story, who asked for terrain instead of comfort—and felt something shift in him that he hadn't felt in forty-three years of service. It took him a moment to name it, because he'd sold the voice that used to name things easily.

It was hope. Ugly, unauthorized hope.

"Terrain, then," he said. "First: the court's power is its Song, renewed each century. Contracts are sung. Laws are sung. Even the stone obeys because the stone has been sung into obedience. Which means the law itself is *sound*, and sound—" he tapped the ledger-scarred table between them, "—can be misquoted. Second: they are literalists. Every clause, every comma. They cannot act outside the letter of an agreement, only within it. Their whole civilization rests on never having met anyone who reads more carefully than they do. Third—"

"Third," Cyrus said slowly, and he watched the cartographer wake in her eyes, the thing that looked at impassable cliffs and saw contour lines, "a contract is a kind of map. Terms. Boundaries. Passages marked and passages closed."

"*Passages*," Sable repeated softly. "Yes. Now you're reading the country instead of the road."

Odile had risen and come to the table, arms folded, her face lit from below by the coals. "You're telling us the law has seams."

"I'm telling you I've spent forty-three years collecting on contracts, and in all that time I've seen exactly one thing the court fears." He let the silence sit. Neither of them filled it; good students, both. "Amendment. A contract amended by a party with standing, executed properly, binds the court as surely as the original. It has happened twice in their recorded history. Both times, the clerks destroyed the records afterward." He smiled again, and this time the smile was entirely his own—thin, dangerous, and purchased from no one. "Which is why I know about them at all. I destroyed one of the records myself."

Cyrus stared at him. "Then you know how it's done."

"I know how it failed," Sable corrected. "Both times, the amendment was attempted alone. One signer, one claim, one voice arguing its own case. The court ate them—not killed them; *kept* them. Standing requires standing, Miss Vane. You cannot amend a contract you're merely trapped in. Someone bound to you by blood or bargain must join the instrument, must *consent*—" his voice caught on the word, pitchless and strange, "—must choose it freely, with full knowledge of the terms."

The fire collapsed into embers. Above them, faint through fifty feet of rock and a hundred years of law, the Choir hummed its centennial hum.

"Blood or bargain," Cyrus repeated. Something moved behind her face—some calculation she wasn't sharing yet, some line she was drawing on a map none of them could see.

"Why are you telling us this?" Odile demanded. "You serve them."

"I served them," Sable said, "for forty-three years, on the promise of balanced ledgers. Tonight, at the toll gate, I checked my account." He touched his smooth throat once more, gently, the way you'd touch a scar you'd stopped hating and started merely knowing. "The balance hasn't moved in twenty years. They were never going to release me. I was gravel, and gravel doesn't get refunds. So yes, Mistress Ferrant, I serve them—right up until I don't."

He rose, crossed to the window, and looked at Thornfell's black mass against the stars.

"Two days to the gate," he said. "Sleep. Both of you. Because tomorrow I'm going to teach you contract law the way they sing it below—and Miss Vane, you'll want to be rested for that, because I suspect you already have an idea. And if your idea is what I think it is—"

He turned. In the ember-light, for just a moment, the shape of a younger man surfaced in his face like something rising through dark water.

"—then you're not descending into the mountain at all. You're breaking into the archive of fate itself. And fate, unlike the court, has never once employed a decent bookkeeper."

From her bedroll, Cyrus Vane smiled—a smuggler's smile, sharp as a blade slipped between ribs—and began unbuckling her pack.

"Get me ink," she said, "and paper that won't burn. Tomorrow we start drafting."
