## 12. Rehearsal for the End of the World

The Undercourt had no sky, but it had a schedule.

Cyrus learned this on her third day below, when the lamps along the Vaulted Way dimmed to a deep amber and every goblin-clerk, moss-tender, and bone-lanternist in the district turned as one toward the distant hum of the Choir Hall. Even the market stalls closed early. Even the toll-keepers put down their ledgers. The whole mountain oriented itself around the rehearsal like iron filings around a lodestone, and Cyrus stood in the crowd pretending she didn't feel the pull in her own chest—that low, aching resonance, as if someone had plucked a string buried under her sternum.

"Rehearsals run until the Night," Odile had told her that morning, pressing a folded scrap of gray cloth into her hands. "Every day. Every hour they can spare. The centennial Song has to be perfect or the whole court comes apart at the seams—literally. Half the wards down here are stitched into the music."

"And the other half?"

"Stitched into the singers." Odile's face had done something complicated. "Which is why you're going to be a stagehand and not an audience member. Audiences get *looked at*. Stagehands are furniture."

The gray cloth was a smock. It smelled of tallow and old rope. When Cyrus pulled it on over her coat, Odile studied her for a long moment, then reached up and smeared two fingers of lamp-black across Cyrus's cheekbone.

"What's that for?"

"Beauty is identity down here. Ruin the first one and you blur the second." A thin, humorless smile. "You're welcome. You look terrible."

* * *

The Choir Hall was the largest carved space Cyrus had ever seen, and she had mapped caverns that swallowed entire caravans. It wasn't built so much as *excavated into obedience*—the walls rose in smooth black curves that no chisel could have made, surfaces worn glassy by a century of sound. The ceiling vanished into darkness hung with hundreds of suspended lanterns, each one a caged flame that burned without smoke, and beneath them stretched a stage of white stone so vast that forty singers standing across it looked scattered, lonely, small.

They did not look small for long.

Cyrus kept her head down and hauled crates of resin-candles along the eastern wing, counting doors, sightlines, exits—the mapmaker's reflex that had kept her alive for thirty years. There were four ways off the stage level: the singers' gallery, the lords' entrance, the freight ramp she'd come up, and a narrow stair behind the resonating pillars that even the stagehands seemed to avoid. She marked all four in memory, the way she'd mark water sources on a desert route.

Then the conductor raised his hands, and she forgot how to count.

He was a tall figure in dove-gray, face obscured—not masked exactly, but *unremarked*, the way certain people in the Undercourt simply declined to be perceived. When his fingers came down, the forty voices of the Choir opened together, and the sound hit Cyrus physically, a warm pressure against her ribs like a wave she hadn't braced for.

It wasn't beautiful. That was the first thing she understood, standing frozen with a crate of candles in her arms while the Song rolled over her. Beautiful was too small a word. It was *load-bearing*. She could feel the mountain settling around the music the way a house settles around its beams, stones shifting a half-inch closer to their appointed places. The lanterns above brightened in time with the phrases. The air itself thickened, sweetened, became something you could almost chew.

And threaded through all forty voices was *her voice*.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. Hers—the particular grain of it, the slight roughness at the bottom of her register that Marla used to say sounded like gravel in honey. Cyrus had heard her own voice recorded once, years ago, in a singing cave near Greywater where echoes came back altered; she'd hated it then, hearing herself from outside. This was worse. This was herself from outside, refined, disciplined, made glorious—and coming out of someone else's throat.

She found the singer without meaning to.

Third row, center-left. A woman standing with the easy, rooted posture of someone who had never once had to think about where her feet were. Dark hair pinned up carelessly. Eyes closed. And her mouth shaping notes that belonged, by every law of nature Cyrus understood, inside Cyrus's own body.

The crate slipped. Resin candles scattered across the stone with a crack like snapping bones, and forty heads turned toward her in eerie unison—including the singer's.

Her eyes were brown. Ordinary. Furious.

"Stagehand." The conductor's voice cut through the silence, mild and cold. "The candles will be replaced. The interruption will not be repeated."

"No, sir." Cyrus knelt and gathered candles with shaking hands, keeping her face angled away, letting the lamp-black and the gray smock do their work. But she felt the singer's gaze on her back the whole time—a stare with weight to it, curiosity sharpening into something that felt uncomfortably like recognition.

*She heard it too,* Cyrus thought. *Whatever I am to her voice, she's something to mine.*

* * *

At the mid-rehearsal break, the Choir dispersed into the galleries and the stagehands clustered by the freight ramp to eat bread and pickled root from shared tins. Cyrus sat at the edge of the group, saying nothing, which required no effort at all—silence, Odile had taught her, was the only shield that couldn't be confiscated.

"You dropped my candles."

The voice came from behind her shoulder, and Cyrus's spine went rigid before her mind caught up. The singer stood there, still in her performance robes, holding a tin of her own. Up close the resemblance was unbearable—it wasn't just the voice now, it was the set of the jaw, the way her left eyebrow sat fractionally higher than the right, a flaw Cyrus saw every morning in her own reflection.

"I'll replace them," Cyrus said, pitching her voice low, flat, anonymous.

"With what wages? Stagehands are paid in meal tickets." The singer dropped onto the bench beside her, close enough that Cyrus could smell the strange clean scent the Court put on its own—cold stone and something floral, like night-blooming stock. "You're new. I know every face on this crew. I make a point of knowing faces."

"Do you."

"It's a habit. Faces are the only things down here that stay themselves." The singer bit into her bread and chewed thoughtfully. "Yours doesn't want to be known. You've got the look of it—the smear, the slouch. Good technique. Bad luck."

"How's that?"

"Because I've spent twenty-nine years listening to voices," the singer said, "and yours just did a thing. When you said 'How's that'—the bottom of it caught. Gravel in honey. There's exactly one other person in this mountain who sounds like that, and she's been dead for a long time, or the Court says she has." She turned and looked at Cyrus straight on, and her ordinary brown eyes were anything but ordinary. "So either you're a ghost, or you've got the most interesting throat in Thornfell. Which is it?"

Cyrus's mind raced through options—deny, deflect, vanish—but Odile's voice was in her head: *In the Undercourt, a lie about your name is forgery. A lie about anything else is just conversation.* Names were currency. Everything else was negotiable.

"I'm nobody," she said carefully. "A stagehand. Ask the conductor."

"I'm asking *you*." The singer leaned in. "That voice of yours—it's a key. You must feel it. When the Choir sings, don't tell me you don't feel the pull in your chest like something's trying to find its socket." Her hand rose, almost unconsciously, to her own sternum. "I've felt it since I was a child. Like half of me is somewhere else, humming."

The words landed in Cyrus like stones dropped down a well—she heard them fall and fall and never heard them land. *Half of me.* Somewhere in the archive below, in a ledger of souls, a contract bore her name and a date that didn't fit, and Odile had gone quiet in a way that meant she knew more than she'd say, and Sable had started watching her with something that wasn't quite pity.

"Maybe you ate something bad," Cyrus said. "Court food."

Something flickered across the singer's face—hurt, quickly buried, then anger. She stood, brushing crumbs from her robes with sharp little motions. "Fine. Be furniture." She paused, and her voice dropped, and it was Cyrus's own voice speaking from outside again, gentle now, which was somehow the worst thing yet. "The rehearsal tomorrow runs the Third Movement. The binding verses. If you're going to keep sneaking looks at us, at least come for that one. It's the part of the Song that decides what everyone in this room is worth."

She walked away, and Cyrus sat very still among the eating stagehands and did not let herself shake.

* * *

Sable was waiting outside the freight ramp when the rehearsal ended, leaning against the corridor wall in his immaculate charcoal coat, examining his cufflinks as though he'd been there for hours and found the wait tedious. He always managed to look like a man between appointments rather than a man lurking.

"Well?" he said.

"The Choir sings. The mountain holds. Very impressive."

"Cyrus." He used her assumed name, the false one, the one that was currently a small crime in progress every time he spoke it. His eyes tracked over her—the smock, the lamp-black, the tremor she hadn't fully hidden. "You heard her."

It wasn't a question. That was the trouble with Sable Quill; he collected debts and information with the same instrument, and the instrument was attention.

"I heard a choir," Cyrus said.

"You heard *a voice*." He pushed off the wall and stepped closer, lowering his tone to match hers. "I've watched you navigate smugglers' gates and minor lords and Odile Ferrant's moods without blinking. And tonight you come out of that hall looking like someone showed you your own funeral. So let me save us both some time." His voice softened further, and underneath the elegance Cyrus heard something old and tired. "I told you the court never releases what it truly owns. I should have added: it also never loses track of what it owns. And whatever is standing on that stage in the third row—" he tapped his own chest, once, "—it knows you. The way a lock knows its key. I felt it from the corridor, and I sold my voice a long time ago, so believe me when I say my hearing is excellent."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Odile knows. Doesn't she. Whatever this is—she's known since the ledger."

"Odile has known since before you were born, I'd wager, and she'll tell you when telling you serves her revenge better than her caution. That's not malice. That's just Odile." Sable glanced back down the darkened corridor toward the hall, where faintly, impossibly, the Choir had begun rehearsing again—even during the break, even off-key, some section running phrases over and over like a prayer. "Tomorrow. The Third Movement. If you're going to hear it anyway, hear it deliberately. And afterward, ask Odile why the prophecy ledger lists your name beside a birth date that comes *after* the signing of your soul-contract."

Cyrus went cold. "I never told you about the dates."

"No." Sable smiled, and it was his collector's smile, but tired at the edges, almost kind. "You didn't have to. I read that same ledger a hundred years before you did, girl. I've had a very long time to wonder who would finally come down here and finish reading it."

Down the corridor, the phantom rehearsal swelled—the Third Movement's opening phrase, rising and falling, rising and falling, a voice like gravel in honey singing to a mountain full of stones.

Cyrus turned toward it, dread and recognition braided so tightly in her chest she could no longer tell which was which, and began walking back toward the light of the hall.
