## 13. The Sister Who Was Erased

Odile Ferrant had spent eleven years learning how to lie with her face, and she was good at it—good enough to sit through a hundred audiences in the Undercourt while lords sang threats at her in three-part harmony, good enough to smile through the tribunal that stripped her rank, good enough, even now, to watch Cyrus Vane sleep and keep her expression neutral.

But lying to yourself was a different discipline. That one she had never mastered.

They were holed up in a disused tuning chamber two levels above the soul-archive, a room where the court's instrument-makers once tested pitch against the mountain's own resonance. The walls were scored with grooves that hummed faintly when anyone spoke above a whisper. It was the quietest place Odile knew in the Undercourt, which meant it was the safest place to tell the truth.

Cyrus sat across from her on an overturned resonance bench, elbows on knees, hands laced so tight the knuckles had gone white. Sable Quill stood by the door with his arms folded, doing his elegant statue act, though Odile noticed he hadn't touched the wine he'd poured himself. When Sable didn't drink, something was wrong.

"Say it again," Cyrus said. "The part about the dates."

"The contract in the archive bears your name." Odile kept her voice level, pitched below the humming threshold of the walls. "Zephyrine. Signed by your mother. Dated the day after your recorded birth."

"And you said that's wrong."

"I said it's *impossible*. There's a difference." Odile rose and began to pace, because pacing was easier than looking at her. "The Undercourt doesn't accept contracts for souls that haven't drawn breath. It's not squeamish—it's legal. A soul has to be *present* at the signing. Witnessed, weighed, entered into the ledger. If the contract is dated the day after your birth, then whatever was signed for wasn't you."

"Then what was it?"

Odile stopped pacing.

Here it was. Thirty years of other people's secrets, and every one of them had landed in her lap eventually—that was the curse of being the person who kept records. She had carried Marla's guilt, the mother's flight, the midwife's ledger-entry she'd found years ago in a surface parish book and never understood until this week. She had told herself she would deliver the truth gently, in pieces, the way you fed a rope down to someone at the bottom of a well.

But Cyrus Vane was already at the bottom, and gentle was just another word for slow.

"You had a twin," Odile said.

The room's grooves caught the words and hummed them back, faintly, twice.

Cyrus did not move. For a long moment the only sound was the walls' low resonance and Sable's breathing, carefully even, carefully nothing.

"That's not funny," Cyrus said finally.

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't anything close to funny."

"People don't have twins and then just—" Cyrus's hands came apart, gestured at nothing, laced again. "There would be records. I erased myself from every record in Thornfell. I would *know* how hard it is to erase a whole person. You can't do it alone. You can't do it in a day. Someone would remember a second baby."

"Someone does remember," Odile said quietly. "That's rather the point."

She crossed to the wall where she'd hung her old choir satchel—the one possession they'd let her carry out of the court, eleven years ago, along with her disgrace. From its inner pocket she drew a fold of oilcloth, and from the oilcloth a single sheet of paper, brittle at the creases, copied in her own hand from the parish registry of Lower Thornfell.

She held it out. Cyrus took it the way people take verdicts.

Odile watched her read it. Watched her lips move over the entry. *Born this night to one E. Vane, two daughters, living.* And beneath it, in different ink, added days later: *one daughter recorded.*

"One daughter recorded," Cyrus read aloud. Her voice came out flat as slate. "Not one daughter born. One daughter *recorded*." She looked up. "You're telling me someone walked into a parish house with two babies and walked out with one in the ledger."

"I'm telling you that's what the ink says. I'm telling you the contract in the soul-archive says what it says. And I'm telling you," Odile said, because there was no kind way around it, "that I've stood in the Choir's rehearsal gallery four nights running, and I've heard the voice you heard in the market. The lead singer. The one who sounds like you."

"Wren." Cyrus said the name like she was testing whether it cut. "Where did you get that name?"

"It's written into the Choir's role-book. Every singer's true name is inscribed when they're bound to the centennial Song. It's how the Song holds them." Odile paused. "Hers is the only name in the book with no family line attached. No parentage, no birthplace, no date of arrival. Just *Wren*, and beneath it, in the margin, in the registrar's hand: *erasure complete.*"

The word dropped into the chamber like a stone into still water. The grooves hummed it back. *Erasure. Erasure.*

Cyrus laughed—one short, ugly note with no humor in it anywhere.

"Erasure," she repeated. "I know a little something about erasure. I built myself out of it. Do you know what it costs? Do you have any idea what it takes to scrub a person out of Thornfell? Fifteen years. Fifteen years of bribes and burned papers and forged ledgers and never sleeping in the same valley twice, and I did it *alone*, because I thought—I thought I was keeping myself safe from—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "From a prophecy. From a bargain my mother made. From a debt I thought was mine."

"It was never yours," Odile said.

"That's worse." Cyrus was on her feet now, moving, restless as water finding cracks. "Don't you understand? If it was mine, at least it would be *fair*. At least I'd be paying for something. But if some other girl—if my sister—" The word snagged in her throat. She swallowed past it. "My sister has been singing in a cage under a mountain for thirty years so that I could walk around up there free. Erasing herself wasn't even her choice. She doesn't get maps. She doesn't get roads. She gets a role-book and a margin note."

Sable spoke from the door for the first time. "You don't know that she suffers."

"Oh, *forgive me*," Cyrus snapped, wheeling on him. "Please, collector, tell me how pleasant it is to be owned."

"She's the lead singer of the Choir," Sable said, unmoved. "In the Undercourt, that is not a cage. It's a throne with strings. There are lords in this mountain who would trade their names for her voice."

"There are lords in this mountain who would trade their *mothers*," Odile said. "That doesn't make it freedom."

"No," Sable admitted. "It doesn't." Something moved behind his composure, brief as a fish under ice, and was gone. Odile had seen that flicker before—at the toll gate, when he'd paid his memory and walked away too quickly. She filed it away, as she filed everything.

Cyrus had gone still again, which was somehow worse than the pacing. When she spoke, her voice had dropped to something small and terrible.

"When I heard her in the market. That voice. I couldn't stop following it. I told myself it was familiar because all voices sound alike in that place, because the acoustics are strange, because—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "It sounded like me. It sounded like listening to myself sing from outside my own body. And I thought, *that's impossible,* and I walked away, and I've been walking away from it ever since, because walking away is what I do."

"Cyrus—"

"Do you know the worst part?" She lowered her hand. Her eyes were dry, which surprised Odile; she'd expected tears, and the absence of them frightened her more. "The worst part is that some part of me knew. In the archive, when the dates didn't fit—some part of me already knew, and I kept reading anyway, because I wanted the answer that kept me alive. I wanted the debt to be mine. Because if it's mine, I can pay it. I've been paying things my whole life. But if it's *hers*—" 

Her voice broke on the last word, and Odile understood suddenly that this was the thing Cyrus could not do. Not weep. Not rage. What she could not do was stop being the center of her own story. Twenty-nine years of survival had taught her that every road led back to herself—and now the road led somewhere else entirely, to a stranger with her own voice, and Cyrus Vane had no map for that country at all.

Odile crossed the chamber and did the one thing she'd sworn off doing since her exile: she put her hands on another singer's shoulders, the old gesture, mistress-to-chorister, steadying before the downbeat.

"Listen to me," she said. "I conducted the Choir for six years. I know what the court does to the people it owns, and I also know what it cannot do. It cannot make Wren forget what she is. Whatever they've taken from her—her history, her surface, her *name*—the voice remembers. Voices always remember. When you heard her in the market, she may have heard you too. Sound runs both ways in this mountain. Always has."

Cyrus stared at her. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying that a girl who has been erased might be looking for the shape of her own absence." Odile let go of her shoulders. "And I'm saying you should be careful, because hope is a currency here too, and the court mints counterfeit."

* * *

They did not speak for a while after that. Sable produced bread and dried fruit from somewhere in his immaculate coat, and they ate without appetite, mechanically, the way travelers eat when the road ahead has become a cliff edge. The walls hummed their faint idiot harmony. Somewhere far below, muffled by strata and stone, the Choir was rehearsing, and even here, two levels up, Odile could feel the centennial Song gathering itself like weather—a pressure behind the ears, a sweetness in the throat that meant nothing good.

It was Cyrus who broke first, because Cyrus always broke first; she simply did it in the direction of work.

"The contract," she said. "The one with my name on it. If the soul it claims isn't mine—if it's hers—then when the Song comes due tomorrow night, the court will call *her*. They'll take her up onto the stage and finish whatever my mother started thirty years ago."

"Yes," Odile said. "That's what the prophecy promises. A soul sold long ago, claimed on the night of the Choir's singing. Everyone assumed it meant you, because your name is on the paper. But paper lies. Ink lies. Names especially lie—they're only handles, and handles get switched." She hesitated. "Which raises a question I've been avoiding since the archive."

"Ask it."

"If the contract names Zephyrine but owns Wren—then the name and the soul are separable. The court's own law proves it. Which means a contract can be *amended*." Odile watched the idea land, watched it move through Cyrus like current through wire. "If someone with standing brought the right argument, at the right moment, before the right audience—"

"Prophecies are contracts," Cyrus breathed. "Contracts can be redrawn."

"Don't." Odile's voice cracked like a conductor's baton coming down. "Do not finish that sentence in this room. The walls here hum. Everything spoken in this mountain travels toward the ears that want it, and if the court learns you're planning to rewrite its oldest agreement, they will move the claiming up tonight, or dissolve the Choir, or simply erase *you* properly this time—no margin note, no sister, nothing left to argue with." She held Cyrus's gaze. "If you mean to try this, you plan it in silence, you write it in cipher, and you sing it exactly once, at the exact moment the Song demands its due. One chance. One note. Miss it and you lose everything, including her."

For a moment Cyrus just looked at her, and Odile saw something shift in the smuggler's face—not gratitude, nothing so simple, but recognition. Two women who had been cast out of the same court for the crime of knowing too much.

"You've thought about this," Cyrus said. "Amending contracts. Undoing the court's law."

"I've had eleven years to think about revenge," Odile said. "This is what it looks like when it grows up."

* * *

Near what passed for morning in a place with no sky, Cyrus slept at last—curled on the resonance bench like a child, one fist closed tight around the parish record, as if the paper might be taken back. Odile stood watch by the door because sleep wouldn't come, and because watching was a form of penance, and penance was the only prayer she had left.

Sable drifted to stand beside her. Below them, faint as a pulse through stone, the Choir sang its endless preparation.

"You knew," he said softly. "Before the archive. Before any of it."

Odile didn't deny it. "I suspected. The voice in the market confirmed it. I needed to see the dates for myself before I said it aloud." She glanced at him. "Why? Feeling righteous?"

"Feeling curious." His gaze stayed fixed on the middle distance. "You gave her the truth all at once, knowing what it would cost. Most people feed it out in pieces. Kinder, that way. Slower."

"Kinder," Odile said, "is how courts talk people into cages. You offer the truth slowly, and by the time the whole of it arrives, they're grateful. They thank you for the chain." She turned to look at him fully. "You of all people should know that, collector."

Something crossed Sable Quill's elegant face then—something older than charm, older than the mask, raw as an unhealed wound—and for one unguarded instant Odile Ferrant, who had read faces in front of singing lords, saw it plain.

He knew. He knew exactly what it was to be handed the truth too late, in pieces, by people who called it kindness. He knew what the court kept, and what it never released, and what it felt like to learn that the story of your own life had a twin version running underneath it, silent, all along.

"Sable," she said carefully.

"Don't," he said. It was barely a whisper, and beneath the word, almost lost in the walls' faint hum, Odile heard it—the ghost of a voice that had once been human, reaching for a note it no longer owned.

Then he straightened, and smoothed his coat, and was porcelain again.

Down in the dark, the Choir swelled toward the end of rehearsal, and threaded through the hundred harmonized voices, clear as a bell struck in an empty church, came the lead singer's line—Wren's line—rising alone over the mass of sound.

And this time, Odile noticed, Cyrus was awake, and sitting up, and listening with her whole body, tears finally sliding down her face without her permission, because there was no mistaking it now, no explaining it away:

The voice rising out of the mountain sounded exactly like her own—

—and it was singing her name.
