## 14. Wren

The first thing Wren knew every morning was the note.

It lived under her sternum before she woke—a low A, steady as a second heartbeat—and when her eyes opened the note rose through her body and out of her mouth as a single sustained tone that filled her cell in the singers' wing. The stone drank it gratefully. The stone always drank.

She held the note while the brazier kindled itself, while the wardrobe opened its brass throat and offered her rehearsal robes, while the small window unshuttered onto the Vault of Chandeliers where the centennial stage stood half-built like a ship's skeleton. She held it until the Choir's answering hum came up through the floor—two hundred voices tuning themselves to hers—and only then did she let it go.

*Good morning, First Voice,* said the walls, in the court's way of saying things: not sound exactly, more a pressure against the ear.

*Good morning,* Wren answered, and dressed.

She had no memories that predated the mountain. This had never struck her as strange. The Undercourt kept no portraits of childhoods; it kept scores. Her earliest memory was of Mistress Odile—before the disgrace, before the exile—pressing a tuning fork against Wren's collarbone and saying, *You were born knowing this pitch. Don't let anyone tell you different.* Wren had been, by her own best accounting, eight years old. Everyone in the court was some age or other until they weren't. She had never wondered what came before, because in the Undercourt *before* was a vulgar word. The court dealt in now, and in next, and in the hundred-year renewal that was now eleven days away.

So it was a genuine inconvenience, on the morning of the twelfth day before the Song, when a stranger was waiting in her rehearsal chamber claiming to be her past.

* * *

Wren saw her before the woman saw Wren—from the gallery above the chamber, where Wren liked to stand and listen to the room breathe. The stranger stood at the center of the floor with her hands jammed in the pockets of a surface coat, all road-dust and wrong angles, and she was looking at the chamber the way people looked at things they intended to steal.

There were three of them. The disgraced choir mistress, Odile Ferrant, whom Wren remembered in fragments and hated in whole—the traitor who had sung false at the last renewal, who had been stripped of her rank and cast up the long stair, whose name was forbidden in the singers' wing. The elegant one, Sable Quill, debt-collector, all lacquer and patience, standing by the door like a man guarding an exit he didn't intend to use. And the stranger.

The stranger touched the center of the floor—the conductor's mark, the place where Wren stood—and Wren felt it like a hand on her throat.

She came down the gallery stairs fast, and her voice went ahead of her, cracking off the stone. "Get off the mark."

The stranger stepped back as if scalded. Up close she was younger than her coat, with map-scarred hands and eyes that did something unbearable: they widened. They *recognized*. They moved over Wren's face like fingers over a familiar page.

"I'm sorry," the stranger said. "I didn't—it's just, I've been looking at maps my whole life, and I've never once seen a room built to hold a sound this size." As if that explained the way she'd been standing there. As if that explained anything.

"Then you know better than to stand on it," Wren said. "This is a closed rehearsal. First Voice privileges. However you got past the door-wardens, Odile, I assume—you can get back past them."

"Wren—" Odile began.

"First Voice," Wren corrected, and heard the Choir's assent shiver through the walls. Two hundred voices below the stone, murmuring her name back into the architecture. *First Voice. First Voice.* The court respected rank above everything except a well-signed contract. Even exiles had to bow to it.

Odile bowed. It was beautifully done, and done with such open irony that it was worse than insolence. "First Voice. May I present a traveler from above. She has questions about her origins."

"Everyone above has questions about their origins," Wren said. "That's why they come down here to sell them."

"Not these ones." Odile's voice did the thing it had always done, even when Wren had been eight years old and hanging on it: it made the room smaller, quieter, private. "These ones are yours."

And the stranger said, "My name is Cyrus Vane. I think—I think we're twins."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted four full seconds, which in the Undercourt is a geological age. Four seconds is enough time for the walls to take a full inventory of everything spoken in their hearing, and file it, and price it.

Wren laughed. It came out ugly. "Twins."

"Yes."

"You're going to have to be more specific about the joke, because I'm not finding the shape of it."

"There's no joke." The stranger—Cyrus—pulled something from her coat. A folded paper, travel-worn, handled so often its creases had gone soft as cloth. "A ledger page. Prophecy registry, Thornfell, dated a hundred years back. It lists tonight's date—or eleven days from now—and it lists a name. Zephyrine. Sold to the Undercourt before her first breath, by her own mother. That name should have been mine. But the dates are wrong. The signature is dated a day after a recorded birth, and the recorded birth—" Her voice snagged. She steadied it the way a climber steadies on rock. "The recorded birth is mine. There were two of us. Someone traded one of us to save the other. Everything about it says it was you."

Wren looked at the paper. She didn't touch it. Touching things was how the court got its hooks in you; she'd learned that at eight years old too, along with the scales.

"So you found some old ink," she said, "and you decided it was me."

"I decided it was us."

"I don't have an *us*." The anger came up fast now, faster than she could sing it down, and the chamber's acoustics took her fury and multiplied it, gave it a cathedral's worth of edges. "I have a pitch. I have two hundred voices who answer me when I call. I have the centennial Song in eleven days, which I will sing because I am the best singer this court has produced in a century, and I have no memory of any surface, any mother, any *twin*, because I was born under this mountain knowing one note, and the woman who taught me that—" she jabbed a finger at Odile, whose face had gone very still— "*she* told me not to let anyone say different. So either you're lying, or she's lying, or the ink is lying, and in this court, surface-girl, ink doesn't lie. Contracts do. People do. Which are you?"

Cyrus didn't flinch. Wren hated her for that most of all. She'd expected the stranger to flinch.

"I'm a mapmaker," Cyrus said quietly. "I don't draw things that aren't there. I found your voice in a market six days ago, before I knew anything. I heard one singer out of three hundred and I stopped walking, because I thought someone was playing a trick with my own voice. That's not ink. That's blood talking."

"The market sells tricks," Wren said. "That's what markets are for."

"Wren." Sable Quill spoke for the first time, from the doorway, and his voice was silk drawn slow over a blade. "Child. May I?"

"No."

He came in anyway, of course. Debt-collectors. He stopped an arm's length from her—respectful, exactly respectful, a man who knew precisely how close he could stand to a First Voice without owing her an apology—and looked down at her with something she had never once seen him wear in ten years of collecting around the court's edges.

Pity.

"When I bought my brother's freedom," Sable said, "the court gave me a receipt. Do you know what was on it? Not his name. Mine. The court keeps what it truly owns written down nowhere, First Voice, because unwritten things can't be forged. You want to know if this woman is your sister? Ask yourself why the court has never once, in your entire life, let you see the terms of your own engagement."

The floor seemed to tilt. Wren reached for her note—the low A, the anchor—and found it, but it trembled.

"Get out," she said. "All of you. Rehearsal is closed."

* * *

They went. That surprised her. She'd braced for argument, for the collector's lawyering, for Odile's terrible gentle persistence. But Cyrus looked at her one more time—that unbearable recognition, that awful *hope*, hope aimed at Wren like a weapon—and then turned and walked out, shoulders square, and took the others with her, and the door sealed behind them with a sound like a held breath released.

Wren stood alone in the chamber for a long time.

Then she did the thing she had never done. She walked to the conductor's mark, and instead of standing on it, she knelt, and pressed her palm flat against the stone, and asked—not the Choir, not the walls, but the archive beneath them, the deep ledger-room where the court kept its signed truths—

*Show me my engagement.*

Nothing. Of course nothing. She had no standing to ask; asking without standing cost standing, and she'd just spent some. The court would bill her for the question. Fine. Let it.

But that night, in her cell, the note under her sternum would not settle. It slid. A minor second above, a tritone below, restless as water finding a crack. And in the space between one failed breath and the next, Wren remembered something that had no business existing in a mind born under the mountain:

Rain.

Not the cistern-rain the court piped through the upper galleries for atmosphere. Real rain, cold and sideways, hammering green leaves, and a woman's voice singing against it—singing *over* it, badly, joyfully, a lullaby in no key the court would recognize—and a weight on her chest that was another person, small and warm and breathing in counterpoint, and—

The note shattered. Wren doubled over on her cot with the broken tone dying in her throat, and the walls came alive with concern, *First Voice? First Voice?*, two hundred voices rising anxious through the stone, and she waved them down with a gesture she was grateful none of them could see.

She sat up in the dark.

"They erased me," she said aloud, testing it. "Both of us. They erased both of us, and gave me the song instead."

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. She had spent her life believing she was the court's treasure, its First Voice, chosen and crowned. But treasures get displayed. She had never once been shown the terms of her own sale. She had been *filed*. A twin sold before breath, a sister scrubbed from every record, a girl grown into a voice that held up a mountain—and neither of them ever asked for any of it.

Fury came again, but slower now, and colder, and worse: grief arrived right behind it, wearing Cyrus's recognizing eyes.

Eleven days. In eleven days she would sing the centennial Song, and the Song would renew the court, and whatever the contract said she was, the contract would collect.

Wren rose. She knew the singers' wing's blind corridors better than anyone living—the maintenance stairs behind the resonating shafts, the routes that existed for tuning, not traffic. She knew, too, where the surface delegation was quartered, because of course she knew; she was the First Voice, nothing moved through this court unheard.

If the stranger wanted to steal her story, fine.

Wren was going to break into her own rooms and find out what else had been stolen, and from the look on Cyrus Vane's face when she kicked that door open—no knock, no courtesy, the stranger frozen mid-motion over scattered papers, and Odile Ferrant rising behind her with a warning hiss—there was plenty left to find.

"You again," Wren said. "Sit down. All of you. You have until the midnight bell to tell me everything the court made sure I'd never hear—and then you're going to tell me why you think either of us can survive what happens in eleven days."

Cyrus looked at Odile. Odile looked at the ceiling. Somewhere far below, the Choir hummed in its sleep, holding up the mountain.

And Sable Quill, from the shadows by the window, said softly: "Ah. Now we can begin."
