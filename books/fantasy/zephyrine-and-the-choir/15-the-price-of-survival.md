## 15. The Price of Survival

Wren had a system for strangers.

They came to her in waves—after rehearsals, after performances, after the Song settled into the stone and the court grew generous with its attention. They came with petitions, with gifts, with requests dressed as compliments. *Lady Wren, first voice of the Choir, would you intercede—would you sing at my daughter's naming—would you ask the Lord of the Ninth Stair for leniency.* She learned young that every stranger wanted something, and that the fastest way through an audience was to name the want before they did.

So when the stagehand with the wrong hands cornered her in the cold gallery behind the rehearsal hall, Wren didn't wait.

"You want something," she said, not turning from the window. Below, the market of borrowed voices was closing for the deep-hours, vendors folding their stalls of hanging throats and bottled lullabies. "Everyone who finds me alone wants something. Save us both the dance and say it."

"I want you to leave."

Wren turned.

The woman from the rafters—the one with sawdust in her hair and a stagehand's badge that sat crooked on her collar like it had been pinned by someone who'd never worn one—stood with her back against the door she'd just pulled shut. Dark hair cut short and practical. Eyes the color of wet slate. And a face—

A face Wren knew.

Not from the Undercourt. Not from any of the hundred years of faces that moved through the mountain. From somewhere older and stranger, from the place behind her eyes where dreams kept their inventory. Wren had dreamed that face before, whole nights of it, and woken with her throat sore as if she'd been shouting.

"You," Wren said slowly. "You're not a stagehand."

"No."

"Odile Ferrant vouched for you." Odile, disgraced mistress of the Choir, returned after thirty years of exile wearing humility like an ill-fitting coat. Wren had let her back in because the old woman still knew harmonics no living singer could match—and because Wren was a fool for anything that smelled of the surface. "She said you were crew. She lied for you."

"She did." The woman crossed the gallery, boots silent on stone that hummed faintly with the Song's perpetual bass note. "And I'm asking you to forgive that later. My name is Cyrus Vane. I'm a mapmaker. I came down here because—" She stopped. Started again. Wren watched her rebuild the sentence like someone rebuilding a bridge mid-collapse. "Because a contract in your archive has a name on it. And I think it's mine. And I think the voice it bought belongs to you."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own small weight.

"The archive," Wren said.

"The Ledger of Souls."

"You broke into the Ledger of Souls."

"It wasn't locked as well as they think it is."

Wren laughed—a short, sharp sound she hadn't made in days. Rehearsals for the centennial Song ran fourteen hours now; the Lord of the First Stair wanted perfection, and perfection, this close to the singing, meant bleeding into the music until you couldn't tell where your breath ended and the mountain's began. She had no time for madwomen with stolen badges and impossible stories.

But her heart had gone strange and fast, the way it did when the Song shifted key without warning.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

Cyrus Vane carried the forgery wrapped in oilcloth, which told Wren she'd been raised above ground where rain mattered. In the narrow alcove off the singers' corridor—Wren's private territory, the one place in the Undercourt where no lord could demand her voice—she unrolled pages covered in cramped, precise script.

"This is the original," Cyrus said. "Or a true copy of it. Sable Quill verified the seal. The contract of sale, dated the night of my birth—or what I thought was my birth. One soul sold to the court beneath Thornfell, payment: safe passage and a hidden life for the child named Zephyrine." Her finger stopped on a line of ink gone brown with age. "That's me. Zephyrine. Except Zephyrine died, or never existed, or—I don't know. Because look here."

A second page. A record Wren recognized the format of, because she'd seen her own entry once, when she'd asked the Archivist why she couldn't remember her mother's face. *Acquired, infant female. Designation: Wren. Provenance: surface birth, twin of Zephyrine.*

"Twin," Wren read aloud. The word felt foreign in her mouth, a word from a language she'd studied but never spoken. She looked up. "I don't have a twin."

"You do. You have me."

"That's not how twins work." But even as she said it, the dream-face made sense, and the ache she'd carried since childhood—the one the healers called resonance strain, the one that flared every time she sang the high passages, as if part of her voice lived somewhere outside her body—that ache rearranged itself into a shape she understood. "Thirty years. You're telling me there's been another person walking around with my face for thirty years while I sang in a hole in the ground."

"Not walking around. Hiding. That's the point of the bargain. Your life bought mine. My mother—our mother—sold you so I could be free." Cyrus's voice cracked on *free*, and Wren heard, underneath the crack, thirty years of a lie being dismantled. "I didn't know. Until three weeks ago I didn't know you existed, didn't know I had a real name, didn't know any of it. Then I found the prophecy ledger, and it named me, and everything I built my life out of turned out to be paper over a hole."

Wren set the pages down carefully, as if they might go off.

"So you came to rescue me."

"I came to get you out. Both of us. There's a way—I've been mapping it since we entered. The smugglers' gate, the toll road, up through the passes before the centennial Song finishes. We could be on the surface in two days. You've never seen the sky. You've never—" Cyrus gestured at the stone around them, at the humming dark, and her voice went fierce with a conviction Wren recognized, because she'd heard it a hundred times from petitioners: the certainty of people who thought their solution was simple if only everyone else would stop complicating it. "You shouldn't be here. None of this should have happened to you. Come with me and it stops."

And Wren, who had spent her whole life being polite to strangers who wanted things, found that politeness had finally run out.

"Sit down," she said.

* * *

Cyrus sat. It was, Wren noted distantly, the first sensible thing she'd done.

"Do you know what the Song does?" Wren asked. "Not what they tell stagehands. What it actually does."

"It renews the court's power. Every hundred years. Odile explained—"

"Odile told you the version lords tell children." Wren rose and pressed her palm flat against the corridor wall. The stone answered, as it always did, a vibration too low to hear and too deep to ignore, the heartbeat of the centennial working already underway. "The Song holds the mountain shut. Do you know what's under the Undercourt? Under *us*? There are levels below the Ninth Stair that even the lords don't map. Old things. Hungry things. The first choir built the Song to cage them, and every hundred years it has to be sung again or the cage opens. That's the ceremony. Not a coronation. Maintenance."

"All right," Cyrus said slowly. "Then sing it and come with us after."

"After isn't the problem." Wren turned around. This was the part she'd rehearsed in her head a thousand times, in the sleepless hours between rehearsals, the argument she'd never expected to need. "The Song needs a lead voice anchored inside the court during the full singing. That's always been the first soprano. Me. If I leave—if I'm not standing on the dais when the final verse lands—the Song doesn't fail gracefully. It doesn't fade. It *tears*. Odile knows. Ask her what happened the last time a lead voice abandoned the verse. Ask her why she's really exiled."

Cyrus went very still. "What happened?"

"A village. On the south slope. Ninety-one years ago, the lead singer fled rather than finish her term—there are stories about why; the court's stories and hers don't agree. She got four verses out of twelve before the Song collapsed. For eleven minutes, the mountain was open." Wren's hand was still on the wall. She could feel the thing beneath, patient and vast, pressing up against the music like a tongue against a tooth. "Eleven minutes. When it sealed again, Thornhollow was gone. Two hundred people. Not killed—*taken*. Some of them are still down here. You can see them in the market sometimes, voices in jars, singing fragments of songs they loved when they had mouths."

The silence stretched.

"There are more villages now than there were then," Wren continued, quieter. "The surface has grown. Refugees come to Thornfell when the border wars push them out—they live on the slopes, they farm the valleys, and every one of them lives inside the radius of the Song. Four thousand souls, maybe five. If I walk out that gate two days before the singing, the number in Thornhollow won't be two hundred. It'll be everyone."

"They're not your responsibility—"

"Neither were you," Wren said. "Until an hour ago."

She watched it land. Watched Cyrus Vane—Zephyrine, the name still didn't fit her, too ornate for those practical hands—absorb the collapse of her mission. Wren knew exactly what was happening behind those slate eyes, because she'd felt it herself when she'd learned the truth of her own acquisition: the vertigo of discovering that the story you'd organized your pain around had a second floor you'd never known about, and the second floor was worse.

"You want to know the cruelest part?" Wren said. "Your freedom cost mine. Our mother sold me so you could live unowned. And now you're offering to undo it—but the only way to undo it fully is to do the same thing in reverse. Take my freedom to pay for theirs. Five thousand strangers' lives for one sister you met an hour ago." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "You can't win this. There's no version of the map where both of us get out clean. Welcome to the family business."

"Don't." Cyrus stood. "Don't do that. Don't make it tidy. I didn't ask to be bought—"

"Neither did I!"

The word rang down the corridor, and somewhere in the walls the Song shivered, picking up her anger the way it picked up everything, weaving it into the bass note. Wren closed her eyes and breathed until her voice came back level. Control. Always control. The court had taught her that if nothing else: a singer who lost her temper lost her leverage.

"I have spent thirty years," she said, "being the price. The infant on the invoice. The line item. And it turns out the person the invoice protected didn't even know I existed—which means I was sacrificed for *nothing*, which means either our mother was a monster or the world is, and I've made my peace with both options, and I will *not* stand here while a stranger with my face tells me my thirty years were a rounding error in her rescue plan."

Cyrus had gone pale. Good, some petty thing in Wren thought. Let her feel it.

But then Cyrus did something Wren didn't expect. She didn't argue. She didn't soften. She reached into her coat and drew out a battered notebook—paper, real paper, water-stained and soft with handling—and opened it on the alcove's stone shelf.

"Then don't come," she said. "If the cost is real, if tearing the Song kills thousands, then stay. Sing your verses. I'm not going to drag you, and I'm not going to pretend your math is wrong, because it isn't. But I'm not leaving yet either. Because there's one thing you haven't considered."

Wren folded her arms. "Which is?"

"Contracts can be amended." Cyrus tapped the forged pages, the ones she'd shown Wren earlier, the ones with new clauses drafted in a hand that trembled only slightly. "Prophecies are contracts. Souls are collateral. Debts can be restructured—it's all law down here, Odile says, everything is law, and laws have text, and text can be rewritten by someone with standing." Her eyes came up, and Wren saw it in them now, the thing that must have kept her alive thirty years as a smuggler in mountains that officially didn't exist: not hope exactly. Something harder. Audacity with its sleeves rolled up. "I gave up on escaping invisible the day I learned your name. So here's my new plan. I'm not stealing you out of the mountain. I'm going to rewrite the contract that put you in it. Both of us walk free, the Song gets its chorus, and nobody pays. It's impossible. Every rule says it's impossible."

"And if you fail?"

"Then I fail loudly, in front of the entire court, on the night of the centennial, and we're both no worse off than we are now." Cyrus smiled, and it was a terrible smile, exhausted and bright. "Actually that's a lie. We'd be much worse off. But I've been worse off before, and I'm still here, so."

Wren stared at her—at this woman assembled from her own missing pieces, stubborn and reckless and utterly certain that the world could be redrawn like one of her maps.

It was insane. It was arrogant. It treated the oldest powers under the mountain like clerks at a counting desk.

It was also, Wren realized with a sensation like a floor giving way, the first time in thirty years anyone had offered her anything except a choice between cages.

"Why?" she asked quietly. "You could take the archive proof and run. Expose the court. Live your life. Why burn it all on someone you met an hour ago?"

Cyrus looked down at the contract copy, at the brown ink, at the name.

"Because you're the only person alive who remembers our mother's face," she said, "and I've been trying to recall mine for three weeks, and I can't. Because whatever else she was, she was ours. And because—" She stopped, jaw working, and Wren waited, and finally it came out rough and unwilling, like a splinter drawn from skin. "Because when I heard you singing in the market, before I knew who you were, I stopped walking. In the middle of a bazaar full of things that want to eat me, I stopped, because your voice sounded like home. I don't know what that makes us. But I'm not leaving it down here."

The Song hummed in the walls. Below, the mountain breathed its slow stone breath.

Wren picked up the forged amendment and read it properly this time, every clause, her trained eye catching what lay beneath the legal architecture—not an escape plan but a composition, risky and strange, built to be sung.

"If we do this," she said, "you'll need to be on that dais with me. The amendment only binds if it's performed, and it can't be performed by an outsider. You'll have to sing. In front of the lords. In front of the things under the Ninth Stair." She held Cyrus's gaze. "Can you sing?"

"I can hold a tune."

Wren almost smiled. "That," she said, "is the worst answer I've ever heard, and we have six days to make it less true. Come on. Odile owes me thirty years of explanations, and you owe me a childhood. We'll start collecting tonight."

She pushed open the alcove door—and stopped.

Two figures stood in the corridor. One was Sable Quill, elegant and expressionless, his collector's seal already in his hand. The other was a page in the livery of the First Stair, breathing hard, holding out a card of black velvet edged in silver.

"Lady Wren," the page panted. "The Lord of the First Stair requests your presence. Immediately. He says—" The boy swallowed. "He says he knows about the mapmaker."
