## 16. Marla's Confession

The mirror came to Marla in the way most things came to Marla: by trade, half-accidental, unexamined. A tinker had owed her three months' rent and paid instead with a tarnished hand-glass he swore was Undercourt work, silver-backed and cold to the touch no matter how long it sat near the stove. She'd hung it in the pantry behind the flour sacks, where it stayed for eleven years, reflecting nothing but shelves.

Tonight it reflected Cyrus.

Marla had been washing dishes when the glass began to hum—a low note, like a wet finger on a rim—and she'd known before she pulled it out what she would see. There was only one person left alive who would know how to call her through a thing like that, and Marla had spent thirty years waiting for her to learn.

So she sat down at the kitchen table, set the mirror against the vinegar crock, and lit no lamp, because if this was going to be a confession it could be done in the dark like an honest thing.

"Cyrus," she said.

The girl's face swam up out of the silver, gray-lit, drawn. Behind her, Marla could see stone walls that belonged to no building on any surface map, and the edge of someone else's shoulder—broad, dark-coated, keeping a careful distance. The debt-collector. Of course. He'd always been the one who knew where bodies were kept.

"You can see me," Cyrus said. It wasn't a question, and there was something raw under it—the flat, careful voice of a girl who had decided not to cry in front of witnesses.

"I could always see you. That was never the problem." Marla folded her hands on the table so they'd have something to do besides shake. "Ask me what you came to ask."

"You knew." Cyrus's voice didn't rise. That was worse. "You knew about the prophecy. About the court. You raised me under a mountain's shadow and never once—"

"Yes."

"—never told me I had a *name* in somebody's ledger—"

"Yes."

"—and now you'll say you're sorry and I'm supposed to—what? Forgive you? Go home? Make supper?"

"No," Marla said. "None of that. You asked me once, when you were nine, why you didn't have a birthday. Do you remember?"

The silence on the other side of the silver went long. Then: "You said the records burned."

"The records didn't burn." Marla closed her eyes. Thirty years of not saying this, and now the words came out like teeth being pulled, one at a time, roots and all. "I burned them. Every one. And then I told you they'd burned, which is the same as burning them twice."

* * *

She started, as she had always known she would, in the middle—because the beginning was the part she could not look at directly, even now, even here.

"Your mother's name was Lark Vane. She came down out of the high passes in the winter of the year you were born, alone, seven months gone and carrying like nine, and she knocked on my door at two in the morning with snow in her hair and a knife in her belt and a look I've seen maybe four times in my life. The look of somebody who has already decided what they're willing to lose."

Cyrus said nothing. In the mirror, the shoulder in the dark coat shifted slightly closer.

"She needed a midwife who didn't ask questions and a house that wasn't on any registry. I was both. That was my whole profession back then—unregistered rooms for unregistered people. Half the smugglers in Thornfell learned their first safehouse from me." Marla laughed, briefly, and it came out wrong. "You think you invented erasing yourself from maps, girl. You learned it at my table."

"I learned it from nobody," Cyrus said. "That's rather the point."

"No," Marla said. "It isn't. But we'll get there."

"Lark gave birth in my best room in a February storm. Two girls. Twins, identical down to the whorl of their hair, and both of them screaming like the world had done them a personal injury. Healthy. That's what nobody tells you about these stories—that there's nothing *wrong* at the start. No sickly child, no bad omen. Just two healthy babies and their mother crying, and I thought at first it was the good kind of crying."

"It wasn't," Cyrus said quietly.

"It wasn't." Marla's hands had gone still on the table. "Because the court had found her. I don't know how—I never learned how—but a collector came while the afterbirth was still being dealt with. Stood in my doorway with snow melting off his shoulders and made his offer polite as a tax notice. One soul for passage. Standard terms. She'd sold something to them years before, your mother—something that had come due, and the debt had interest, and the interest was due *that night*, and the penalty clause—" Marla stopped. Swallowed. "You've been learning how their contracts work down there. You tell me what a penalty clause means when it comes due on a woman alone in February with two newborns."

In the mirror, Cyrus's face had gone very pale. Behind her, faintly, the debt-collector's voice: low, confirming something. *Standard terms. Yes. That's standard.*

"She couldn't pay," Cyrus said. "And she couldn't refuse."

"She couldn't refuse," Marla agreed. "But she could choose *which*. That was the mercy in it, if you want to call it that, and the court did call it that, they always do, they put the word mercy right in the paperwork so you sign faster. One soul of the two. Their choice or hers. And your mother looked at those two babies, and she looked at me, and she asked me the question I have been answering every night since."

Marla opened her eyes. The kitchen was dark around her; the only light was the silver glow of her own guilt, which seemed about right.

"She asked me which one."

* * *

"I want to be very clear about what happened next," Marla said, "because you deserve the ugly version, not the version I've rehearsed in my head for thirty years where I come out of it confused but well-meaning."

"Marla—" Cyrus started.

"No. Let me. I've earned the floor." Marla leaned forward toward the glass. "I chose. Not her. *Me.* She couldn't do it—her hands were shaking too badly to even touch them both at once, and I understand that now, I didn't then—and so I stood over two infants in my best room and I played arithmetic with souls. Which one weighed more. Which one had the stronger grip. As if you can tell, at an hour old, anything worth telling."

"What made you choose—" Cyrus stopped. Started again. "Why me?"

"That's the part I've never said out loud." Marla took a breath. "There was no reason. That's the truth, and you can hate it, and you should. A coin would have been kinder than me, because at least a coin doesn't pretend. But I looked at your face and then at your sister's face and I picked one, and the only thing I can tell you about how is that your sister was sleeping. You were screaming. And some fool voice in me said, *the loud one will fight, the quiet one will manage.*"

The silence that followed was so complete Marla could hear the stove ticking.

"The quiet one will manage," Cyrus repeated.

"Yes."

"And so she managed. For thirty years. Down there."

"I know," Marla said. "I've known every day. That's the sentence I built my whole life inside of, Cyrus. *The quiet one will manage.* I told myself it meant you'd be all right up here. I knew, somewhere I didn't let myself look, that it meant exactly what it said."

Cyrus's voice, when it came, was almost gentle, which was somehow unbearable. "What happened to my mother?"

"Gone by morning. Left a letter I burned with the rest, because—" Marla pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Because I told myself it protected you. Because I was the one who'd made the choice and I couldn't bear to let her explain it, in case she explained it better than I could. In case you loved her for it. In case you hated *me* for it. Both, if I'm honest. Both at once."

"And the contract. My name in the ledger. The date."

"Written that night, in my presence, by the collector's own pen. Except—" Marla frowned, reaching for the detail the way you reach for a stair in the dark, a stair you've stumbled on before. "He wrote the name wrong. Or I thought he wrote it wrong. He inked it and I read it and it said *Zephyrine*, and I corrected him—out loud, God help me, I corrected a court official on his spelling—and he smiled at me the way they smile, and he said, *Madam, I have written precisely what was purchased,* and he turned the ledger so I could see, and the signature line was dated the day after the birth. One day after. I assumed a clerical formality. Debts of that kind are dated at sunset, he told me, and the girls were born after midnight, so technically—"

"*Technically* the debt attached to a day that hadn't happened yet," Cyrus finished. Her voice had changed. Marla heard it happen—the sound of a mapmaker finding a river that runs the wrong direction. "Marla. When you corrected him. What name did you *say*?"

"I said Zephyrine."

"And which baby were you holding when you said it?"

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls of Marla's old house, a beam settled, the way beams do when a house has stood a long time on top of things it would rather not hold.

"I don't remember," Marla whispered. "Cyrus. I swear to you on every honest map ever drawn—I have tried to remember which child I was looking at, and I cannot. Thirty years, and the one thing I cannot give you is the one thing you need."

"No," Cyrus said slowly. "It's better than that. Don't you see?" And there was something in her face Marla hadn't seen since the girl was small and used to narrate her own climbing, bright and fierce and certain. "If the correction happened while you held one of us—if the court heard the name *spoken* over one child and *written* for another—then the contract might be ambiguous. Ambiguous contracts can be contested. Odile says everything down here is language. If the name in the ledger and the name at the birth don't bind to the same body—"

Behind her, the debt-collector's voice cut in, sharp with something like awe: "Then the court has been enforcing an instrument with a defective title for thirty years. They'll have buried it in re-amortization. They *hate* when that happens. Girl, if you're right, this is better than forgery. This is *precedent*."

Marla listened to them plan above her own confession, and found—to her astonishment—that she was smiling, wet-faced, in the dark.

* * *

They talked another hour. Technical things mostly; Marla answered what she could and confessed what she couldn't, and somewhere in the middle Cyrus asked, without preamble:

"Do you regret it? Choosing me?"

"Yes," Marla said. "Every day. Not because I chose wrong. Because I chose. Somebody should have refused to, and there was nobody in that room brave enough, and I've had thirty years to become her and I did it thirty years too late." She paused. "You don't have to forgive me. I want that on the record too. I'm not asking. Forgiveness isn't a toll, girl, it's a gift, and I've got no standing to collect gifts."

"I know," Cyrus said. And then, softer: "Wren sings like you humming. Did you know that? Same key. I heard her across a market full of bought voices and I knew her, and I didn't know *how* I knew her, and it turns out it's because she sounds like Sunday mornings in your kitchen."

Marla put her hand flat on the table, over nothing, over the shape of two infants thirty years gone.

"Bring her home," she said. "Both of you. I'll burn the old ledgers properly this time, and I'll write new ones, and the first page will have two names on it and a real date."

When the mirror finally went dark, Marla sat a long while without moving. Then she rose, went to the pantry, moved the flour sacks aside, and began—methodically, by candlelight, the way she'd done everything important in her life—to take down every jar, every tin, every loose board, until she reached the cavity in the wall where, thirty years ago, she had hidden the one record she had never been able to bring herself to burn.

Lark Vane's letter. Unopened by anyone but its writer, who had sealed it with wax and a smear of afterbirth-blood and the words, in a shaking hand: *For whichever daughter survives me.*

Marla carried it to the table, laid it beside the dead mirror, and waited for dawn, because some letters should only be broken into in daylight.

On the front, beneath her mother's seal, the address had faded almost to nothing—but Marla had read it once, thirty years ago, and had never once been allowed to forget it.

*To Zephyrine — or to whoever she turns out to be.*
