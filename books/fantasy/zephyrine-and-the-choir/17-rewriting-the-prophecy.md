## 17. Rewriting the Prophecy

Cyrus didn't sleep. She sat in the borrowed room beneath the Undercourt's guest-galleries—Odile's old rooms, reclaimed by Odile's old rank and a bribe of two memories Cyrus could spare—with the soul-contract unrolled across the table like a map of a country that wanted her dead.

She had read it forty times.

The language was the problem. No—the language was the *answer*. She just hadn't seen it yet, because she kept reading it the way a frightened woman reads a death sentence instead of the way a cartographer reads terrain.

She spread her hands flat on the parchment and made herself look at it coldly.

*One soul, sold in good faith, to be claimed upon the night of the Centennial Song: the daughter of Liria Vane, born under the sign of the Hollow Star.*

A name. A date. A consideration given—a mother's bargain, whatever Marla had confessed through the scrying glass, sealed and witnessed. Terms of performance. Conditions of default. Penalties.

"That's not a prophecy," Cyrus said aloud, to no one. "That's a bill of sale."

The candle guttered as if offended. She ignored it.

Every contract she'd ever forged—and she had forged dozens, tariffs papers, transit writs, a bishop's passport once, and none of them ever traced back to her—had three weaknesses. The parties. The terms. The signature. Change any one of them with sufficient craft, and the document meant something new while still meaning itself.

Prophecies were supposed to be different. Prophecies came from above, or below, or from wherever the mountain kept its deep machinery, and everyone knew you couldn't argue with fate.

But this wasn't written on sky-stone. It was ink on calfskin, in a ledger, in a hand. And down here, where songs were contracts and names were currency, a prophecy wasn't a vision at all. It was an instrument. Filed. Enforceable. Which meant—

Which meant it could be amended.

Her heart started going fast and stayed there.

* * *

"You're either brilliant or about to get us all rendered down into choir-boys," Sable said.

They had gathered in Odile's antechamber past the dead hour, when the mountain's corridors went quiet enough that even the walls seemed to listen. Sable stood by the door out of habit, elegant as ever in his grey coat, but his eyes kept flicking to the contract on the table with something that was not quite fear and not quite hunger. Odile sat with her hands wrapped around a cup of bitter root-tea, her silver rank-chains laid out beside her like surgical instruments.

"Say it properly," Odile said. "If you're going to commit treason against a hundred-year-old instrument of the Undercourt, say it so I can hear the shape of the crime."

"Prophecies are contracts." Cyrus tapped the parchment. "This one has parties—my mother, whoever held the court's pen that night. It has consideration—one life given, one life spared. It has terms of performance—claiming my soul on the night of the Song. And it has execution clauses, right here—" her finger found the cramped column of small script near the seal "—which nobody reads because everyone assumes prophecy is weather."

"And?" Sable said.

"And every enforceable instrument in Thornfell law—surface or Undercourt, and I've forged both—permits amendment by mutual consent of the parties, or by unilateral act of a party with standing." She looked up. "My mother is one party. She's alive. She signed."

Silence. Somewhere far above, water moved through the mountain's stone throat, patient as everything else down here.

"The court will never consent," Sable said at last, and his voice had lost its silk. That was how she knew he was taking her seriously. "You're proposing to renegotiate with a creditor who owns your soul and has for thirty years. They'll hear the word 'amendment' and take the whole debt plus interest."

"They don't have to hear it," Cyrus said. "Not as a request."

Odile set down her cup. "Explain."

"A rider." Cyrus pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began sketching as she talked, quick cartographer's strokes, boxes and lines. "Contracts carry riders all the time—supplementary provisions executed alongside the primary instrument, incorporated by reference. If a rider is drafted correctly, signed by someone with standing, and *performed publicly within the terms of the original contract*, it becomes part of the contract. You can't strike it afterward without voiding the whole instrument. And they can't void the whole instrument." Her finger came down on the clause she'd read forty times. "Because the Song depends on it. Chapter fifteen of their own law, Odile—you told me. The Centennial renews their power *because* the claim is performed. If the claim fails on a technicality, the Song fails. If the Song fails, every refugee under this mountain loses the binding that keeps the wards up."

"So they'd never let it fail," Odile said slowly. "Even if it failed the way we want it to."

"They can't afford to be careful," Cyrus said. "They have to perform exactly as written. And if what's written, by tomorrow night, includes an amendment—" she drew a line connecting two boxes, "—then performing as written means honoring it."

Sable laughed once, short and disbelieving. "You want to smuggle a clause into a century-old prophecy and let the court execute it against its own will. That's not law. That's forgery of fate."

"I'm a forger," Cyrus said. "It's the only honest work I've ever done."

* * *

The problems came at her like rocks off a cliff face, and she catalogued them the way she catalogued hazards on an unmapped pass.

Problem one: standing. Who had the legal right to amend?

"Your mother," Sable said. "Obviously. But Liria Vane is thirty years gone and unreachable, and even if she weren't, the court would smell the fraud before she finished signing."

"She doesn't have to sign," Odile said. She had gone very still, tea forgotten. "There's another party with standing. The beneficiary."

Cyrus frowned. "The beneficiary of the original bargain?"

"The person whose life was purchased." Odile's eyes lifted. "You, girl. The contract gave you your freedom. Every year of it. You are the living consideration received. Under court law—and I taught this law for twenty years before they took my voice and my name—an injured or benefited party may petition amendment when the performance of the instrument causes harm contrary to its spirit." Her mouth twisted. "I lost my case on that doctrine. But I know every syllable of it."

"But I'm also the thing being claimed," Cyrus said. "Can the collateral amend its own contract?"

"It's been done." Sable's voice was quiet now. He hadn't moved from the door, but he had stopped leaning on it. "Once. Before my time. A vintner's son in the eastern galleries bought back six months of his own servitude by filing a grievance-of-performance—he argued the court had breached its duty of care toward him, and the arbitrators let him trade the grievance against his debt. It nearly worked." His jaw tightened. "Then they found out he'd learned the procedure from me, and they took it out of my brother's account instead. Six months became sixty years." He looked at Cyrus. "So yes. It's possible. And yes, they punish audacity. I want that understood between us before we go further."

"Noted," Cyrus said. "Understood. Proceed anyway."

Something shifted in his face—not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. One professional acknowledging another's willingness to burn.

Problem two: form. An amendment had to be drafted in proper instrument-language, witnessed, sealed, and—this was the part that made Cyrus's stomach drop—*sung*. Down here, nothing bound unless it was voiced. A written rider was a corpse without breath.

"That's why you need me," Odile said. She rose and went to the chest at the foot of her bed, and from beneath folded robes she brought out something wrapped in oilcloth: a thin rod of dark wood, banded in silver. "A cantor's baton. Confiscated when I fell. I stole it back on my way out, which is the only crime of mine the court never discovered." She weighed it in her palm like a woman greeting an old wound. "With this, and my rank-forms—which are still valid; disgrace strips your privileges, not your training—I can give any text we write the musical authority of a filed instrument. They'll hear it as law before they realize what it says."

"And the wording?" Cyrus said. "It has to survive their scrutiny. They'll have centuries of practice reading traps."

"Then we don't trap them," Sable said.

Both women turned. He had left the door at last and come to stand over the table, and when he spoke again it was in the low, rapid cadence of a man reciting something he'd memorized long ago and hated.

"The amateur move is obfuscation—hide the poison in the small print. The court eats amateurs for breakfast; they've had ten thousand years of small print. No." He laid one pale finger on the contract's opening line. "You go the other direction. You draft in perfect, blinding clarity. You make the amendment so plainly lawful, so scrupulously fair, so *generous to the court* that refusing it makes them look like what they are." His eyes came up, and there was something almost gentle in them, which was worse than the predatory charm. "Courts survive on legitimacy. Even this one. Especially this one. Give them a version of events they can accept without admitting defeat, and they'll take it, because the alternative is a public breach on the night of the centennial in front of every lord they need to impress."

"What version?" Cyrus asked.

He smiled, and it was the first smile of his she'd seen that reached all the way up. "That's the beautiful part. We offer them a substitute performance. The contract demands a soul surrendered at the Song. It doesn't demand *whose*—not anymore. Your mother's bargain named 'the daughter of Liria Vane.' Singular. Unspecified. Thirty years ago, everyone assumed it was you. But Wren is also Liria Vane's daughter, and Wren is already theirs, already singing, already—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—accounted for. So we amend: the claim shall be satisfied by the surrender of a daughter of Liria Vane *already in the court's possession*. No loss to them. No breach. The Song performs perfectly. And you walk."

The room tilted. Cyrus gripped the edge of the table.

"That's trading Wren," she said. "That's ratifying what my mother did. That's handing them my sister on a plate with a ribbon."

"For now," Sable said. "Clause two."

"Clause two?"

"An amendment can contain multiple provisions, cartographer. Provision one satisfies the claim with a soul already held—no prejudice to the court, no argument. Provision two establishes a redemption schedule: any soul thus held may be ransomed by a party of standing, payment to be negotiated in good faith, terms to be fixed at the time of performance." He spread his hands. "We don't ask them to free Wren. We ask them to *name a price*. Publicly. Under their own law, in front of their own lords, on the holiest night of their century. And once a price exists, debts can be paid. Names can be traded. Songs can be sung. You told me yourself what Odile taught you—everything down here is negotiable. We're just making them negotiate on the record."

Cyrus stared at him. "And if they refuse the redemption?"

"Then they've refused their own law in open court, and the instrument is tainted, and Odile's grievance-doctrine comes into play." He shrugged elegantly. "But they won't refuse. Pride is cheap; precedent is expensive. A lord who refuses a lawful ransom sets a precedent that could someday be used against *him*."

Problem three: the signature. Standing, form, strategy—none of it mattered without a hand on the line that the court must honor.

"It has to be mine," Cyrus said.

They both looked at her.

"The beneficiary signs. Me. In my true name." She said it and felt the strangeness of it, the weight of letters she'd spent thirty years avoiding. "Zephyrine Vane. On paper. In front of witnesses. Forever." She laughed, short and unsteady. "Do you understand what that costs? Everything I built. Every erased record, every unmapped road, every year of being no one. The moment I sign, I exist. Permanently. In their archives, on their instruments, in their memory. I can never disappear again."

"No," Odile said quietly. "You can't."

Cyrus looked down at the blank sheet waiting for ink, and thought about the mapmaker's oldest rule: *every line you draw, you're responsible for.* She had spent her whole life drawing lines that led away. This was the first line that led through.

"All right," she said. "Then we do it properly. Odile—rank-forms and the baton. Sable—draft me the redemption provision in language so clean a lord could eat off it. I'll handle the authentication. There's a reason the court's own seals use cartographic sigils, did you know that? Territory and law are the same discipline down here. I've been forging territorial authority since I was fourteen." She pulled the inkwell close. "We have until the rehearsal ends and the stagehands change shift. Roughly four hours."

"Four hours to redraft fate," Sable said.

"Three and a half, if we want time to rehearse the singing." Odile was already unwinding the silver bands from the baton, her fingers remembering their old work. "And we will rehearse. If Zephyrine's voice cracks on a single syllable of the invocation, the whole instrument collapses and takes us with it."

* * *

They worked as the mountain breathed around them—Sable dictating clauses in a murmur, crossing out and restoring words with a jeweler's precision; Odile setting each sentence to its required modal contour, humming fragments under her breath, correcting meter; Cyrus writing fair copies until her hand ached, then copying again, because down here a flawed text was a flawed soul.

Somewhere past the third hour, Cyrus realized she was happy.

Not comfortable. Not safe. Her hands shook and her mouth was dry and somewhere above them the Choir was rehearsing the end of the world with her sister's voice at its center. But underneath all of it ran a current she recognized from the best days of her old life: the feeling of a passage opening where every map said *impassable.* The feeling of drawing a road that didn't exist yet and knowing, with total certainty, that by morning it would.

"Again from the invocation," Odile said.

Cyrus cleared her throat and sang:

*"Let the instrument be amended: the claim herein named shall be satisfied by a daughter of Liria Vane already in the court's keeping, without diminution of the court's due; and let a price of redemption be named for that daughter, openly, at the hour of performance, payable by any party of standing—"*

Her voice caught on *standing.* Not from nerves. From the sheer absurdity of it—her, standing, after a lifetime of erasure. She steadied and finished the phrase clean.

Odile lowered the baton. "Better. Again."

Outside the antechamber door, boots passed in the corridor—two guards, unhurried, their lantern-light sliding under the door and away. The mountain hummed its endless note. The contract lay on the table between them, old and patient, certain of its rights.

Cyrus dipped her pen and began the final fair copy.

She was halfway down the page, the true name—*Zephyrine*—still unwritten, when Sable went rigid by the door and raised one hand for silence.

From somewhere in the walls, faint and getting closer, came singing. Not the Choir's rehearsal. Something smaller. A single voice, moving through the service corridors, singing a scrap of melody Cyrus knew better than her own heartbeat—a lullaby from a fire-lit room above a bookbinder's shop, a tune Marla used to hum.

Except Marla was a hundred miles away behind a scrying mirror.

The voice stopped outside the door. And then, gently, mockingly, it sang one more line—in harmony with the exact phrase Cyrus had just been rehearsing.

Someone had heard them. Someone had been listening the whole time.
