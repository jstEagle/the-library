## 18. The Night of the Choir

The stolen role fit worse than the stolen dress.

Cyrus stood before the cracked silver mirror in Wren's dressing alcove, tugging at a bodice stitched with notes instead of beads—actual notation, inked onto silk so that the garment itself was a contract. Which meant she was wearing a legal document. Which meant every seam was a clause.

"Don't fidget," Odile said from the doorway. She had dressed for war in her old formal regalia: the slate-and-silver of a choir mistress, moth-eaten at the cuffs but unmistakable. Thirty years of exile and the Undercourt still recognized its own. "The robe reads you. If your pulse spikes too hard, it'll register on the hem."

"Then we should have picked a calmer suicide."

"We did not pick this one at all. You did." Odile crossed the room and took Cyrus's chin in her hand, tilting her face to the light the way she'd once appraised a hundred singers. "Again."

Cyrus sang it. The first phrase of the amendment, folded inside Wren's opening line of the centennial Song—a counterfeit melody with a smuggled cargo, three bars of rewritten contract hidden in the throat of an official performance. She'd practiced it until her voice went raw, until Sable said she could recite it under torture, and then made her practice more.

"Better," Odile said. "You still sing like someone who learned music as a second language."

"I did."

"Yes. Well. Tonight everyone else does too." Odile stepped back, and something moved across her face that wasn't cruelty or contempt—something older. Fear, maybe, wearing a mistress's posture. "When the Song calls the name, there will be one breath of silence. One. The whole court will lean toward you whether they mean to or not; that's how the law works. That is where you sing. Not before. Not after. In the silence, Cyrus. Silence is the shield. It's the only lesson I ever taught that mattered."

"And if I miss the breath?"

"Then the court claims a soul it already owns, the Song renews for another hundred years, your sister sings herself hollow, and I watch everything I hate survive again." Odile adjusted one of Cyrus's collar-clasps with unnecessary force. "So don't miss the breath."

* * *

The corridor to the great hall filled as the hour turned. Choir members drifted past in their notation-stitched robes, none of them looking at Cyrus—or rather, all of them looking at her exactly the way they looked at Wren, because that was the trick of it. The role she wore was real. Odile had pulled strings that hadn't existed since her disgrace, favors called in from choristers who still owed her, and the ledger of the Choir now listed its lead singer's understudy as *present*. Names were currency. Tonight, for the first time in her life, Cyrus was spending.

Sable found her at the mouth of the performers' gallery. He was dressed as what he was—an accredited collector of the Undercourt—and he wore it beautifully, which somehow made it worse. His hair was combed. His cuffs were clean. He looked like a man attending his own funeral and pleased about the catering.

"You're pale," he said.

"It's powder. Wren's complexion runs lighter than mine."

"It's terror."

"It's both." She checked his hands. Empty. No ledger, no pen. "Where's your copy?"

"In my head, same as yours." He said it lightly, but his eyes went somewhere else for a moment—to a cell, maybe, or a brother, or the toll gate where he'd paid a memory and pretended it cost nothing. "Cyrus. Listen to me, because Odile won't say this part. Courts don't amend contracts out of generosity. They amend them out of advantage. Your forgery is good—it's better than good—but the amendment only binds if the court believes accepting it profits them more than refusing. So when you sing, you don't sing like a girl begging for her life. You sing like a merchant closing a deal. Give them your name. Make them want it."

"My name is worth nothing. Nobody's ever known it."

"That's why it's worth everything." He smiled, thin and sharp. "A name no one has spoken in thirty years is an unclaimed title, an unminted coin. Every lord down here has spent a lifetime hoarding names and they have never once had the chance to own one that history forgot. You aren't offering them a soul tonight. You're offering them a rarity. Act like it costs you."

"It will cost me."

"Yes," Sable said. "That's what makes it valuable." He offered his arm, absurdly formal, and she took it because her hands needed something to hold. "Come along, Vane. Or whatever your name is. Let's go make you legible."

* * *

The Hall of the Long Note had no ceiling anyone could find.

It rose into darkness the way a well rises, except upward—stone walls climbing past torchlight, past reason, into a black that hummed. The acoustics were the architecture; the room was shaped like the inside of a held breath. Tiered galleries ringed a stage of white stone, and above the stage hung nothing at all, an emptiness that waited the way an ear waits.

The lords gathered on the highest tiers, and Cyrus made herself look at them, because Odile had said fear feeds on vagueness. They were not what the stories promised. Some were beautiful in the way frost is beautiful; some had let beauty go entirely and kept only the shape of having once had it. A lady whose gown was a slow waterfall of sound. A fat, genial creature with rings on every finger, each ring whispering a different debt. And at the center, in a seat carved from a single chord, the Lord of the First Voice—old beyond old, his face smooth as paper that has never been written on.

Waiting to be written on, she thought. They're all waiting. That's the whole court. A library that eats.

The Choir assembled below the galleries, forty voices in notation silk, and Cyrus slid into the second row among them, hood up, standing where Wren would stand during the procession while Wren herself—

He was safe. She had to believe he was safe. Locked in the archive antechamber with Odile's old key on the door, furious, singing to himself in the dark—the only person in the mountain who could unravel the amendment by refusing to harmonize, and so the only person who had to be elsewhere. She had kissed her sister's forehead before she shut the door and Wren had spat at her, and Cyrus had closed it anyway, and she carried the sound of that door like a stone in her chest.

*Forgive me after,* she had said. *Or don't. But be alive to hold the grudge.*

The horns began.

They came from nowhere—from the walls themselves, from the humming dark above—and the hall changed temperature. Every courtier leaned forward by exactly one degree. The Choir began to move, processing up the white steps, and Cyrus walked with them, one foot, then the other, terror transmuting in her chest the way ore transmutes in fire—not disappearing, becoming something with an edge.

This is just a road, she told herself. The most dangerous road I've ever mapped. But I have never once lost a caravan.

She reached her mark. The Lord of the First Voice raised one finger, and the hall inhaled.

* * *

The centennial Song began the way water begins—everywhere at once, inevitable.

Forty voices braided together beneath the solo line, and the solo line belonged to Wren's position, which belonged, tonight, to Cyrus. She came in on the third measure exactly as rehearsed, wearing her sister's melody like a borrowed coat, and the hall received it without comment. The deception held. Of course it held. She was Wren's twin; the blood knew the tune even if the tongue stumbled.

But the Song did not stay a song.

By the seventh movement it had become a ledger. Cyrus could feel it—the music reaching outward through the hall, touching every courtier, every chorister, taking inventory. This was the renewal Odile had described: a century of bargains audited in harmony, debts confirmed, ownership reaffirmed, the whole vast machinery of the Undercourt oiled and set running for another hundred years. The refugees in the deep Warrens, sheltered by the Song's long echo. The contracts in the archive. The souls on the shelves. All of it renewed by this sound, this night, this voice.

Her voice. Wren's voice. Whichever—the Song didn't care, and that was the crack in the wall she intended to drive through.

Movement nine. Movement ten. Her legs ached from standing in one perfect posture. Sweat ran cold between her shoulder blades, and the notation on her bodice seemed to tighten with every verse, clauses cinching. In the third tier she found Sable's face, composed and watching, and beside him Odile, who had no ticket to be in this hall at all and was there anyway through sheer accumulated entitlement, and the sight of them steadied her the way a cairn steadies a climber: *someone passed here. Someone survived.*

Movement eleven. The audit deepened. The Song began calling names—quiet ones, murmured into the harmonies, and each named soul shuddered slightly wherever it stood or sat or hung in its jar. A century's interest, coming due.

Then the music shifted underneath her, the way ground shifts before rockfall, and Cyrus felt it come.

The demand.

The melody opened like a hand, like a mouth, like a page turning to a marked place, and the whole court leaned—one degree, every courtier at once, exactly as Odile had promised—and the Song spoke into the silence it had made:

*"Zephyrine."*

Her true name. Spoken aloud for the first time in thirty years, in a hall with no ceiling, by a voice made of law.

The silence after it was the largest thing she had ever stood inside.

One breath. Odile's voice in her memory: *not before, not after.* Sable's: *sing like a merchant.* Wren's, from the last night they'd argued across an archive table: *If you do this, do it all the way, because I will not be rescued twice.*

Cyrus lowered her hood.

She let the court see her face—Wren's face, unhooded, unknown, a stranger standing in the lead singer's mark—and she stepped forward to the lip of the stage, into the light of the noteless dark, and she breathed in.

Then she sang the amendment.

Three bars of forged law, delivered clean, her smuggler's voice carrying it into every corner of the hall: the terms laid out in melody as binding as any contract in the archive. *The name Zephyrine Vane, unspoken for thirty years, unminted, unowned—offered freely to the court, in perpetuity, in exchange for the release of all claims on the souls of both daughters of Marla of Thornfell. Both. Not one. Both.* Her voice didn't shake. Later she would wonder at that. Tonight it simply obeyed, the way her hands obeyed on a cliff face, terror burned down to its purest form, which is focus.

The hall erupted—or tried to. Sound broke against the law of the silence the Song had made, and the silence held, and in that held silence the Lord of the First Voice rose from his seat carved from a single chord.

His face was still blank paper. But every other face in the high tiers had turned toward her, and she saw it there, the thing Sable had promised: hunger. Thirty years of name-hoarders hearing an unminted coin ring on the floor.

The Lord descended. It took no time at all and all the time in the world, and when he stood before her on the white stage, his voice came from everywhere, including from inside her own chest.

*"A name,"* he said. *"Freely given. The court accepts much, little mapmaker. But contracts require—"*

*"Harmony,"* Cyrus said. "Yes. I know the terms."

And into the silence behind her, a second voice began to rise.

Not from the choir rows. From the doors of the performers' gallery—thrown open, unlocked from outside, because Odile had never given Cyrus the only key. Wren walked down the aisle of the great hall in the clothes she'd been locked in, head high, eyes blazing, singing the counter-melody of the amendment in a voice that was Cyrus's own voice returned to her from a mirror—and the two lines met above the white stone and twined, and the Hall of the Long Note, built to hold a single sound forever, shuddered around a chord it had never heard before.

Two sisters. One contract. Sung in harmony, as the law demanded.

The Lord of the First Voice tilted his blank face upward, listening, and Cyrus sang on, and did not yet know—and could not yet know—what the court would ask in return for saying yes.
