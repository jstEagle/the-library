## 19. The Song That Names You

The Undercourt had never seen a full house like this.

I knew because I'd spent the last hour memorizing it from the worst seat in the cavern: behind the great organ of bone, in the stagehand's gallery, wearing a borrowed smock and another woman's face-paint and a role stolen three nights ago from a dressing room that smelled of cold iron and lilies. Below me the amphitheater rose in tiers of living rock, and every tier was full. Lords in crowns of antler and glass. Ladies whose gowns were made of suspended notes—I could see them humming, faint gold threads drifting off the silk. The refugee galleries at the very top, where the mountain's saved souls sat pressed shoulder to shoulder, kept alive by the Song the way a lamp is kept alive by oil.

And everywhere, everywhere, the expectation. The court had been rehearsing this night for a hundred years. The centennial renewal. The Song that would be sung once and then hold for another century—the Song that kept the mountain standing, the refugees breathing, the courts fed, the contracts bound.

The Song that demanded its due.

I checked the amendment one last time. It rode against my sternum, folded into eight squares, hidden under the stagehand's smock where a heart would be if I still had one I trusted. Eight squares of vellum. Odile's hand for the old script. Sable's knowledge for the legal architecture—he'd spent two days explaining to me how a prophecy is just a contract with better posture, and how every contract has an amendment clause if you know where the bodies are buried, and Sable Quill always knew where the bodies were buried because half the time he'd buried them himself.

*Let it hold,* I thought at the paper. *You only have to hold until I sing.*

Below me, the Choir assembled.

Two hundred voices filing into the crescent tiers of the stage, robed in grey so pale it looked like fog given patience. They arranged themselves without instruction, without a conductor—there was no conductor, there never had been, the Song organized itself the way water organizes itself downhill—and at the center of the crescent stood Wren.

My sister. The words still didn't fit right in my mouth. My sister. A woman my own height, my own bones, a face I'd seen in tarnished mirrors all my life without knowing I was looking at someone else's reflection too. She wore the lead singer's crown of woven silver and she stood the way I stood when I was about to do something dangerous: weight forward, chin level, eyes already mapping the exits.

She hadn't agreed to anything. That was the flaw in the plan, the crack running through the whole forged foundation of it. Wren had told me what leaving would cost—the refugees, the hundreds of lives held up by her voice like a roof held up by one pillar—and she had refused me flat, furious, final. So the amendment couldn't free her by force. It could only open a door and trust her to walk through it.

Trust. There's a currency they don't trade in beneath the Thornfells.

A hush went through the cavern like wind through wheat. The lights dimmed—not extinguished but *lowered*, as if the mountain itself were leaning in. In the front tier, the Lord of the First Veil settled onto his throne of fused glass, and his eyes, black and patient as wells, lifted toward the stage.

Toward me, though he didn't know it yet.

The first note came from nowhere and everywhere. Not from any single throat—from the Choir entire, from the stone, from the marrow of the mountain. It was a low tone, felt in the teeth before the ears, and when it bloomed into melody I understood why the court called it the Song instead of *a* song. It wasn't music the way I understood music. It was law set to pitch. Every note landed like a seal pressed into wax.

I gripped the rail of the gallery and did what Odile had drilled into me for six sleepless days.

*I don't hear it,* I told myself. *I'm not listening.*

Silence is the only shield. But you can't stand inside a centennial Song and simply not hear it; that's like standing in a river and deciding not to get wet. What Odile taught me was worse and better: give the Song nothing to grip. A nameless thing, a thing erased from every record, is hard for the law to hold—but the Song was about to fix that, wasn't it? Tonight the Song would speak my true name before the whole court, would write me back into existence in the act of claiming me. Zephyrine. Sold a century before my birth by a bargain I never signed.

Unless I amended the terms first.

The Song climbed. The Choir swayed like kelp in a current, and their voices braided into harmonies that made the air above the stage visibly shimmer, threads of light pulling taut between the singers like the strings of some vast instrument. I saw lords weeping openly. I saw refugees in the top galleries holding each other, faces turned up, drinking the sound like rain after drought. This was the terrible arithmetic of the place: the Song enslaved and saved in the same breath. You couldn't cut the pillar without dropping the roof.

The first movement ended. The second began, faster, brighter, and now the melody started doing something that raised every hair on my arms.

It was counting.

I couldn't have said how I knew—it was in the shape of the phrases, the way each one closed a little shorter than the last, syllables falling away like pages torn out. A ledger being read aloud. An account being tallied. And at the end of the tally, the Song would arrive at its sum.

Its due.

Sable's voice in my memory, dry as old vellum: *The contract names a soul. The Song will call that soul by name. When it does, everyone in this cavern will know who belongs to the court—and the court will reach out and take possession. Your only window is between the naming and the taking. Perhaps ten seconds. Perhaps five.*

*And if I miss the window?*

*Then you'll spend a very long time learning to sing.* He'd smiled his collector's smile, the one that never reached whatever he kept in place of eyes. *You have a good voice, Vane. It would be a shame. It would also be an eternity.*

The third movement opened, and the temperature of the cavern changed. The lords leaned forward. Even the light seemed to gather itself closer to the stage. This was the movement the century had been waiting for—the Claiming, Odile called it, her jaw tight as a trap. The moment the Song renewed itself by collecting what it was owed.

The melody descended, note by note, down and down, a stairway built of sound, and at the bottom of the stairway—

It spoke.

Not sang. Spoke. The whole cavern became one mouth, and out of it came a woman's voice, ancient and mild and absolutely without mercy, and it said:

"*Zephyrine.*"

My name. My true name, the forgotten one, the sold one, ringing off ten thousand facets of living rock, written into the air before the entire Undercourt. Heads turned. Lords stirred. Somewhere in the tiers a whisper ran like fire through dry grass—*the debt, the old debt, the soul from the surface bargain—*

Every eye in the cavern swept the audience, looking for the named one rising from her seat.

They didn't look up. They didn't look at the stagehand's gallery behind the organ of bone.

Which meant, for exactly the length of one held breath, no one was watching me step over the rail.

I dropped to the second-tier catwalk, then down the ladder Odile had shown me—the maintenance way, the smuggler's way, the road that officially does not exist even inside a mountain—and came out onto the edge of the stage while the echo of my own name was still dying against the ceiling.

Two hundred singers turned toward me. Two hundred pairs of eyes. And in the center of them, Wren, going white around the mouth, her hands clenching in the folds of her robe: *what are you doing, what are you doing—*

The Song paused. Not ended—paused, the way a predator pauses, the way a magistrate pauses when the accused stands up uninvited. The mild ancient voice hung in the air, waiting. Curious.

In that silence I did the bravest and stupidest thing of my life.

I began to sing.

My voice came out rough—I hadn't sung since I was a child, hadn't dared, song being contract and contract being chains—but it carried. Smuggler's lungs, mapmaker's discipline. I sang the amendment.

Not the words on the paper; those were dead ink, evidence, a thing to be produced later if the court demanded proof. I sang the *substance*, the way Odile had taught me to translate contract-law into melody: the old clause recited backward, the debt acknowledged, the debtor identified, the terms rewritten in real time before the assembled law of the Undercourt.

*I am Zephyrine.* The notes shook and steadied. *The name is mine. Take the name.*

The court erupted. Not noise—something worse, a shifting, a rustle of attention, ten thousand gazes focusing on me like heat through a lens. The Lord of the First Veil rose from his glass throne, and his well-black eyes fixed on me with something I had not expected and had no armor against.

Recognition. And below it, faint but unmistakable: delight.

"*Zephyrine,*" the Song said again, tasting it. "*The name returns. The debt stands. The soul is owed.*"

"No," I sang, and poured everything into the next phrase, the heart of the amendment, the part Sable had argued over with me until dawn—*"Read the instrument. Read the dates."*

This was the blade we'd spent six days sharpening. The original contract bore my mother's signature dated the day after my recorded birth—which meant it could not bind me, because I had not existed to be bound when the ink dried. The soul named in it was another's. And the other—the twin, the erased sister, Wren—had been claimed not as payment but as *consideration*: given in exchange, held in service, never purchased outright. Held, but not owned. A lien, not a title.

And a lien, under the oldest law of the Undercourt—the law older than the Lords, the law the Songs themselves obeyed—could be discharged by tender of equivalent value.

Equivalent value.

"I offer," I sang, and my voice cracked and I let it crack, because Odile said the court trusted cracked things, cracked things were honest things—"I offer the name itself. Zephyrine. Freely surrendered, fully transferred, irrevocably given. A name freely offered outweighs a soul compelled. Let the debt be paid in identity. Let the record show the creditor satisfied."

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats. In those three heartbeats I felt myself start to come apart.

Because here is what they don't tell you about invisibility, about erasure, about thirty years of scrubbing yourself out of every ledger and register and memory in the Thornfells: it becomes you. Cyrus Vane, the woman who isn't anywhere, the road that doesn't exist, the name that isn't written—I had built her plank by plank, and she had kept me alive, and giving her up felt like peeling off my own skin in front of ten thousand strangers.

The Song took the offered name. I felt it go—a drawing-out sensation, like a splinter leaving flesh, except the splinter was everything I'd ever been. The road that doesn't exist. The passes only I could walk. The landlady's girl. The mapmaker. All of it gathered up, weighed, appraised by a voice older than the mountains it lived under.

And then the Song did something I will remember on my deathbed, however far away that bed turns out to be.

It laughed.

Low, and warm, and horribly pleased. "*Clever,*" it said. "*The surface sends us clever ones. The tender is heard. The tender is weighed—*"

The pause stretched. The lords had gone utterly still. Wren was staring at me with an expression I couldn't parse—horror and hope fighting for the same ground.

"—*and found sufficient,*" the Song finished, "*in part.*"

*In part.* The two words hit me like cold water.

"*One name discharges one claim,*" the Song continued, mild as milk. "*The contract holds two signatures' worth of debt. The mother signed twice. Who speaks for the second signature?*"

The floor tilted under everything. Twice. My mother had signed twice—Marla had told me fragments, Odile had confirmed the shape of it, but the full arithmetic had never landed until this moment, in front of the entire court, with my own name already halfway out of my possession. One signature for the child freed. One signature for the child given. Two debts, two souls, and I had brought enough coin to buy out only one.

Wren stepped forward.

The silver crown caught the light. Her voice, when she used it, was the most beautiful sound in that whole cavern of beautiful sounds, and it was aimed at me like a drawn blade. "You absolute fool," she sang—harmony threading into my broken melody whether I willed it or not, our voices tangling the way our faces tangled, "you didn't think you could carry both signatures alone?"

"Wren—"

"The amendment requires—" and here she faltered, just slightly, because she'd read the forgery too, of course she had, she'd had three nights and a sharp mind and every reason to study the document that promised her freedom she didn't want—"it requires *harmony*. Two voices. One per signature. You knew that."

I had known it. I had hoped she'd never realize it in time.

The Song waited, patient as stone, its mild voice hanging over us like a held axe: "*Who completes the amendment? Who tenders the second name?*"

Wren drew breath to answer—to give her own name, to pay her own debt, to walk out free and leave the refugees' roof pillar-less—

And the Song moved first.

"*Or,*" it said, and the warmth drained out of it all at once, leaving something vast and cold and juridical, "*or the court offers a counter-term. For the second signature, the court will accept not a name.*"

The lights guttered. Every lord in the amphitheater leaned forward at once, ten thousand thrones creaking, and I watched the black eyes of the Lord of the First Veil shine with something that had no warmth in it at all.

"*The court will accept a chorus,*" the Song said. "*New voices. Willing voices. To carry the burden the lead singer lays down. Find me a chorus willing to be bound—or the second signature stands unpaid, and the elder sister leaves alone.*"

The echo died. The whole cavern stared at us—two identical women on a stage, one stripped of her name, one crowned in silver, the amendment hanging unfinished in the air like a bridge missing its far span.

Wren looked at me across the distance of one lifetime.

"Well," she said, in plain speech, no music at all, her voice shaking with fury and fear and something underneath both that I recognized because it lived in me too. "Now what?"
