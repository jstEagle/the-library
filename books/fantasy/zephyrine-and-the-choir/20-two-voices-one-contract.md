## 20. Two Voices, One Contract

The Song turned on me mid-phrase.

I had felt it coming for weeks—the way the centennial music shifted under my feet like ice deciding whether to hold. Tonight, on the dais of the Undercourt, with every lord of the deep places arrayed below in their galleries of bone and amberlight, the Song finally asked its question. It reached into my chest where my voice lived and found a name that was not mine.

*Zephyrine.*

The name rolled through the vaulted dark like a stone dropped down a well. Ten thousand of the courtly kind went still at once—not the stillness of an audience, but the stillness of creditors.

And my sister stood in my place in the Choir's formation, wearing my stolen role, her mouth opening to answer.

Everything in me wanted to scream *no*. I had spent three days hating this stranger who claimed my story as hers, and one impossible hour learning she hadn't claimed it—she'd been erased so I could be kept. One hour learning that everything I sang, I sang for her sake too. That the Song holding ten thousand refugees safe beneath Thornfell's roots was braided through my voice like a wire through wet clay, and if I walked out, it all came down.

Now she was about to give herself away for me. The same trade our mother made thirty years ago, made again by the wrong sister, in the wrong direction, forever.

No. Not again.

I stepped forward before Zephyrine could sing a word.

* * *

You have to understand what harmony means down there. It is not prettiness. It is not two voices agreeing to sound nice.

Harmony is contract law.

Odile had drilled it into us both in the stolen hours before the performance, pacing the stagehand corridors with her conductor's hands carving the air: *A single voice makes a claim. Two voices make terms. The court cannot refuse a true harmony, because the court itself is built from one—a thousand singers bound into a single instrument. When you offer harmony, you are offering them something they recognize as their own bones.*

"But it has to be willing," she'd said, stopping mid-stride, looking at me with those gray eyes that had watched this court cast her out. "Both voices. Both *choosing*. A forced duet is just slavery with better acoustics, and the court knows the difference. They built the difference."

So there I stood, having stepped out of formation, in front of the assembled deep lords, my heart going like a trapped bird, offering my hand to a woman I had known for a day and hated for most of it.

"Sing with me," I said.

Zephyrine stared. Up close she looked like a mirror tilted wrong—my jaw, my brow, but weathered differently, sun-browned where I was cavern-pale, scarred across one knuckle where my hands were unmarked. Her eyes were doing what mine did when I was calculating odds against terror. I knew that look from the inside. It was the strangest thing that had ever happened to me, seeing my own face lie to itself.

"The amendment," she said quietly, so only I could hear—or so she thought; in the Undercourt, quiet carries. "It's written for one signatory. My name in place of either soul."

"I heard it in rehearsal. Through the wall." I had. Four nights ago, crouched among the stage machinery, I'd heard her whispering the forged clauses to Odile, and the shape of the words had gone through me like cold water, because I understood then what she meant to do. "It's badly constructed. Your collector friend's legalese is elegant but the consideration clause collapses if challenged—you're offering your name, but your name only has standing if you survive to hold it. You've written yourself a loophole and a noose in the same line."

Her mouth opened. Closed.

"I'm the lead voice of the Choir," I said. "I have standing. And I know how to carry a counter-melody." I lifted my chin toward the lords' gallery, where the Lord of the Ninth Vein sat like a vein of midnight given a crown. "They won't take a solo amendment. But a harmony—" I swallowed. "A harmony rewrites everything. Terms, not claims. We can build the escape into the music itself."

"You hate me," she whispered.

"I don't know you." My voice cracked, and I let it; down here, honest cracks carried further than polished notes. "But you're the only person in two worlds who knows exactly what it cost to be me. Sing with me or don't. But I'm not letting you pay alone."

For one heartbeat—one long, suspended beat, the kind Odile taught us could hold an entire hall—I thought she would refuse.

Then Zephyrine Vane took my hand, and her palm was calloused in all the places mine were soft, and she nodded once.

* * *

What happened next, the court's own chroniclers would fail to describe, because we broke their instruments of description.

We began with the Song itself—the centennial renewal, the great binding melody I had sung since childhood, that I knew the way you know your own pulse. Zephyrine knew it too, now; Odile had crammed a lifetime's training into her in six desperate days. But where the Song demanded its due—*a soul sold, a debt collected, the ledger closed*—Zephyrine diverged. She sang the amendment. Her name offered up. Her invisibility surrendered. Herself, made legible to every power in that hall, marked and findable forever, in exchange for both sisters walking free.

It should have collapsed. A single voice making terms is a claim, and the Lord of the Ninth Vein was already rising from his seat to deny it—

And I came in underneath.

Not opposing her. Not echoing. Underneath, the way bedrock sits under soil. I took her melody and gave it somewhere to stand. Where she sang *I surrender my name*, I sang the old refugee-clause, the mercy-thread woven into the Song's foundations a century ago by the first choir, the ones who built this whole arrangement to shelter the fleeing thousands of the Border Wars. Where she sang *let the contract close*, I sang *let the contract pass on*—and I turned toward the Choir behind me, two hundred voices who had been watching their lead singer do something unprecedented, and I beckoned.

The Song needs a chorus. That was always its weakness, the flaw Odile saw the night they exiled her and hated themselves for: it binds whoever sings it, but *whoever* was never specified. Only willing.

Mistress Corvin of the second section met my eyes. She had a daughter among the refugees in the lower halls. She stepped forward.

Then Halven. Then the whole of the altos, because altos have never once in history declined to be useful. Then the tenors, then the seconds, and the centennial Song—that vast machine of obligation that had held my voice in its gears for twenty-three years—slid free of me and settled onto two hundred throats who chose it, freely, in front of the entire court, making the transfer not theft but *gift*, which no law in any world has ever managed to forbid.

The Lord of the Ninth Vein stopped rising. Sat back down. His expression did not change, but his silence changed quality, and in the Undercourt, that is how you know you have won.

Zephyrine and I kept singing. We had to; the amendment wasn't accepted until the final cadence. And somewhere in the second-to-last phrase, without planning it, without deciding, her line bent toward mine and mine rose to meet it, and for eight bars we weren't negotiating with a court of ancient powers at all.

We were just singing together. Two parts of one chord that had been split at birth and finally found its root.

I have no words for what her voice felt like against mine. It was like hearing my own heartbeat from outside my chest. It was like being introduced to myself. When we hit the final interval—the fifth, the open, unresolved-yet-complete sound that Odile called "the only honest ending in music"—the hall's amberlight flared white, every soul-ledger in the archive below rang at once like struck glass, and the contract bearing our mother's signature tore itself in half somewhere in the dark, and we heard it.

Both of us heard it. Zephyrine grabbed my hand again, and this time neither of us pretended it was strategy.

The Lord of the Ninth Vein spoke last, as was proper. "The amendment stands," he said, and his voice was the sound of a door closing gently on a room no one would enter again. "Two souls released. One Song transferred by consent. The ledger closes clean." A pause, and then, in a tone I would spend years deciding whether it was mockery or respect: "The court has not been amended in nine hundred years. We had hoped someone would try."

* * *

Afterward, there was chaos, but the good kind—the chaos of an audience that got more than it paid for. Lords departing in columns of drifting light. The Choir dispersing to tell everyone they had ever met what they'd done. Odile Ferrant standing at the edge of the dais with her arms crossed and her face carefully neutral, which on Odile meant she was fighting tears and losing.

"You changed my bridge section," she accused when we reached her. "The modulation in the third clause. It was elegant."

"It was *correct*," I said. "Your version modulated too early. You always modulate too early. It's why they exiled you—they couldn't stand being right about you."

Odile made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and pulled us both into an embrace that smelled of chalk dust and old velvet, and for a moment the three of us just stood there in the emptying hall, conspirators, survivors, choir.

Sable Quill appeared beside us the way he always did, as if he'd been standing there since before the room existed. He looked different. Lighter. I didn't yet know why—didn't yet know that the amended contract had dissolved his own ancient sale, that his voice was his again, that he was turning over in his mind whether he even remembered how to use it. All I knew was that he studied the two of us standing hand in hand, and said, with genuine wonder:

"Fascinating. Thirty years in the debt trade and I've never seen a creditor say *thank you*."

"Get used to it," Zephyrine told him. "There's a dawn upstairs with our names on it. Well. One of our names. We're workshopping."

* * *

We surfaced near midnight into the smuggler's gate, and the mountain let us go the way a hand opens.

But that part belongs to another chapter, and there is one moment from inside that I want to keep here, because it is mine.

In the corridor below the dais, while Odile argued logistics with Sable and the Choir's new lead voice—Corvin, steady, willing, chosen—Zephyrine caught my sleeve.

"Marla," she said. "Our— the landlady. She's up there. She's been waiting thirty years thinking she damned one daughter to save the other."

"I know. I heard the scrying." I had listened through the mirror with the whole fury of my ignorance, and then past the fury, to the truth underneath: a terrified midwife making an impossible arithmetic in a burning house. "Zeph. She didn't choose between us. She just couldn't save both. Nobody can be blamed for the shape of a fire."

"She'll think I came to punish her."

"Then we shouldn't go up separately," I said. "One story, two voices. It worked on the court. It'll work on an old woman in a kitchen." I hesitated, and then said the thing I had been carrying since the final cadence, the thing I would never be able to unsay: "I've sung alone my whole life. I didn't know it was loneliness until tonight."

Zephyrine looked at me—looked at her own face wearing a different life—and smiled the smile I suddenly realized I'd seen in my own reflection forever without knowing where it came from.

"Then we harmonize," she said. "Everything. Starting with her."

Above us, far up the shafts of the mountain, dawn was hours away, and a kitchen light was burning, and a woman who had regretted for thirty years was about to learn that her arithmetic had finally come out even.

We climbed toward it together.
