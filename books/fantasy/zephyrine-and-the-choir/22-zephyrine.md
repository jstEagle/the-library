## 22. Zephyrine

The shop had been a cobbler's once, and before that a chandler's, and before that—according to the oldest deed, which Zephyrine had tracked down out of professional habit—a counting house for a wool merchant who had died in debt and been forgotten by everyone including his creditors. She liked that lineage. A building that kept changing its name felt like family.

The sign went up on a Tuesday, because Wren insisted that beginnings needed ordinary days attached to them or they got ideas about themselves. It read, in letters Odile had painted with the same steady hand that had once ruled a choir of three hundred:

**VANE & SISTER — HONEST MAPS, TRUE SONGS**

"Vane," Zephyrine had said when she first saw it. "I don't go by Vane anymore."

"You do and you don't," Wren said, hanging the last loop of rope over the hook. "That's the point of it. Nobody looks twice at Vane. But *Zephyrine & Sister* sounds like a law firm." She'd stepped back, head tilted, that assessing singer's squint she used on everything from sheet music to loaves of bread. "Besides. You spent thirty years hiding behind a name you chose to be invisible. Now you've got two names and you're standing in a window. Balance."

Zephyrine could not argue with her sister's arithmetic. She had stopped trying, mostly.

The shop was narrow and deep, like all the buildings on Lamplighter Row, with a counter of scarred oak at the front and worktables in back where the light came through south-facing glass. The shelves held maps—not the smuggler's kind, not the erased kind, not the kind drawn to be memorized and burned. Honest maps. Roads marked as they were. Rivers named as the people who lived beside them named them. The passes of Thornfell rendered plainly, every one of them, including the ones that officially did not exist, because Zephyrine had decided, in the long slow weeks after coming down out of the mountain, that a map with omissions was just a lie with good posture.

There had been consequences to that decision. There were always consequences.

The surveyors' guild in Coldharbour had sent a letter, then a delegation, then a lawyer, each more offended than the last. A woman appeared at the shop one afternoon with guild colors on her collar and said, "You understand you've invalidated forty years of official cartography."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "Isn't it wonderful?"

The woman left without buying anything, but she came back a week later and bought three maps and did not mention the guild again, and Zephyrine understood that something in her had been hungry for plainness the way Zephyrine had once been hungry for it herself.

* * *

Wren ran the singing lessons in the back room, mornings and evenings, and Zephyrine still had not entirely gotten used to hearing them through the wall.

They were small lessons. Six students, then nine, then twelve. A miller's daughter who wanted to sing at her own wedding without anyone in the family falling into a bargain-trance. An old soldier who had lost his voice at a toll gate decades ago and wanted to learn what his throat could do now that it belonged to him. Two children from the row who had wandered in for the novelty and stayed because Wren taught them rounds, simple circles of song with no hooks in them, no clauses, no fine print.

"No binding," Wren would say, every lesson, the way other teachers said *breathe from the diaphragm*. "A song should hold you the way a bridge holds you. You cross it, and it lets you go, and it's there again tomorrow if you need it. If a song won't let you off, it isn't a song. It's a cage with a melody."

She had learned that under the mountain, in a century of singing the Song that kept the refugees of Thornfell safe in their valleys while it hollowed her out note by note. Except it hadn't hollowed her, quite—it had filled her with something else, and the unspooling of that something was taking longer than any of them expected. Some nights Zephyrine woke to hear her sister in the kitchen, humming fragments of the old centennial Song, and some mornings Wren couldn't remember what day it was, or whether doors were things that opened. The Undercourt had released them both—the amended contract had done that much, cleanly and legally, because Odile had drafted it and Sable had argued it and the court's own lords had found themselves bound by their own rules—but release, it turned out, was not the same as being finished.

The refugee-sustaining Song itself had passed to a new chorus, as the amendment specified: volunteers this time, singers who came to it freely, who rotated the burden among thirty voices instead of stacking it on one. Odile trained them. Odile had gone back under the mountain without hesitation, disgraced exile no longer, reinstated choir mistress by a court too embarrassed by its own ledger of her case to refuse her, and she wrote letters that arrived irregularly and read like dispatches from a war she intended to win.

*The lords are learning humility,* one letter said. *Slowly. Like glaciers. I have taken the liberty of rewriting the entire contract canon. They hate it. They also cannot find a procedural grounds to stop me, which is my favorite kind of hatred.*

And Sable—

Sable had chosen exile, as he had promised himself he would, freed by the amended contract and owing nothing to anyone for the first time in two hundred years. He had stood with them in the dawn above Thornfell, written his name in the margin of Zephyrine's map, and then walked down the mountain alone toward the lowlands and whatever a man became when he was no longer a debt.

He turned up in the shop in the third week.

Zephyrine looked up from a survey of the eastern passes and there he was in the doorway, dressed in traveling gray instead of court black, holding a hat that had seen weather, looking faintly astonished to exist in daylight.

"You're supposed to be in exile," she said.

"I am." He came in, touched the counter as if checking it was real. "This is me, exiled. One learns that exile is largely a matter of where you put your feet." He set a folded paper on the oak. "I've been reviewing your guild correspondence. Your lawyer is incompetent. Also, I have nothing to do, and it turns out two centuries of contract law leaves one with very few marketable skills outside of, well." He gestured at the paper. "Fine print."

Zephyrine read it. It was a legal dismantling of the surveyors' guild's objections so thorough that it ended with a clause suggesting the guild might owe *her* damages.

"You want to work here," she said slowly, "in my honest map shop. As a lawyer."

"I want to be somewhere," Sable said, and for a moment the old predatory charm slipped entirely, leaving something rawer underneath, "where people say what things cost. Out loud. Up front." He straightened his hat. "Also your sister threatened to write a song about me if I kept wandering. I've heard her songs. I'd rather have employment."

So the sign stayed **VANE & SISTER**, and the back office acquired a desk, and the shop became three things at once, which Zephyrine decided was also part of its lineage.

* * *

The customer came on an ordinary Thursday, because that was when ordinary things happened now.

She was nobody remarkable—a carter's wife from two valleys over, wanting a map of the river roads for a delivery run, the kind of purchase that paid the rent and asked nothing of anyone. Zephyrine wrapped the map in brown paper, quoted the price, took the coin, made change. All of it out loud. All of it plain.

The woman lingered at the counter, though. Fidgeted with her change. Looked at Zephyrine with the particular sideways attention that Zephyrine had learned to recognize since the night of the Choir: someone who had heard the story. Everyone had heard the story, by now, distorted through a hundred tellings—the sisters who sang down the Undercourt, the smuggler who rewrote a prophecy, the mountain whose masters changed their laws overnight. Half of it wasn't true. The true half sounded less believable.

"They say you're the one," the carter's wife said. "From the mountain."

"I'm the one from Lamplighter Row," Zephyrine said. "I sell maps."

"Mm." The woman turned her coin over in her fingers. She was quiet a moment, working up to something. Then: "Doesn't it frighten you? Being found?" Her voice dropped, confessional. "My grandmother's family, they came out of the hills for exactly that reason. Names changed, records burned, whole line scratched out so nobody could come collecting. That's why we're carters and not hill folk now." She glanced toward the window, the street, the wide visible world. "You gave all that up. Everything they say you traded away—your hiding, your erasing, all of it, just—gone. Anyone can look at you now. Anybody who ever wanted to find Cyrus Vane knows exactly where she stands." She lowered her voice further. "Doesn't it frighten you?"

Through the back wall, faintly, Wren's voice rose above her students, leading them through a round, six voices braiding and unbraiding, none of them caught, all of them free to drop out whenever they liked. Somewhere behind the office door, Sable muttered at a guild contract in the tone of a man sharpening knives. On the shelf by the window sat the newest map, the one of the road down from Thornfell, the road that now existed, marked clearly, leading home.

Once, Zephyrine would have answered yes without thinking. Once the whole architecture of her life had been built on that fear—every record burned, every alias worn smooth, every friendship kept shallow enough to abandon. She had thought invisibility was safety. She had learned, in the deep places of the world, that the erased are the easiest of all to collect: no one comes looking for someone who was never there.

"No," Zephyrine said.

The woman blinked. "No?"

"No." Zephyrine folded the woman's map along its creases, neat and square, and slid it across the counter. "It took me thirty years to learn the difference between being hidden and being safe. Hidden, I could vanish any morning, and no one would notice, and no one would come after me either—which is the trap, you understand? Erasure doesn't keep you from being taken. It just guarantees nobody looks." She tapped the counter, the shop, the plain glass window with her name on it facing the street. "Now, if someone tries anything, there's a sister who can sing a door shut, a lawyer who can sue the shadow off their wall, and a former choir mistress under the mountain who considers it a personal hobby. And beyond them—" she nodded at the round drifting through the back wall, at the students' voices, bright and unbound "—there's a whole street full of people who know my name and would notice by suppertime if I stopped answering to it. That's not exposure. That's the strongest fortification I've ever stood inside."

The carter's wife considered this. She was quiet for a long moment, turning the words over the way she'd turned her coin.

"My grandmother," she said at last, "never let us write the family name down anywhere. Not once. Not on anything." She tucked the wrapped map under her arm. Then she set a second coin on the counter. "For the next one. When I'm ready to buy it."

"Buy what?"

"A map," the woman said, moving toward the door, "with our name back on it."

The bell chimed. She was gone into the lamplight of the row, and Zephyrine stood a moment with the extra coin warm under her palm, and then put it in the drawer, and then—after a breath—took it back out and set it on the windowsill, face up, where she could see it. A promise with a shape. She had spent her whole life learning to read the marks people left; this one she intended to keep in view until it was cashed.

Wren appeared in the doorway to the back room, wiping chalk dust from her hands, that singer's squint already running its assessment.

"That was the carter's wife," she said. "The one who's been circling the shop for a fortnight. What did you tell her?"

"The truth."

"Ugh." Wren wrinkled her nose. "You and your honesty. It'll be the death of the business." She leaned against the frame, and her expression softened into something older and less defended. "Dinner soon? Odile's letter came—she says the new chorus has the Song stable through midwinter, and she wants us to come down for the solstice. Both of us. She says—" Wren paused, and Zephyrine watched her sister decide to say it plainly, because they had agreed, all of them, to say things plainly now. "She says the court keeps a room for us. Officially. In the ledger and everything. In case we ever want to visit."

"Do you?"

"I don't know yet." Wren shrugged, and it was an honest shrug, unburdened, the shrug of someone who actually got to choose. "Ask me at midwinter." She pushed off from the doorframe. "Set the table, will you? Three plates. Sable pretends he isn't staying but he's already alphabetized the drawer of pens, which is what he does instead of saying thank you."

She vanished back through the doorway, and a moment later her voice rose again with the students', the round catching her up and letting her go, and Zephyrine stood alone a moment longer in the front of the shop, in the window, in full view of the street.

Below Lamplighter Row, the town went about its evening. Above it, far off and blue in the dusk, Thornfell held its silence—not empty now, just quiet, the way a house is quiet when everyone in it is finally sleeping well. And pinned to the wall beside the door hung the newest map, still drying, the ink still sharp: the whole range drawn honestly, every pass marked, every road named, and in the corner, small and clear and signed, the legend that told any traveler everything they needed to know.

*Here be roads,* it said. *All of them. Ask for anything.*

Zephyrine turned the lamp higher, so that anyone out there could see in.
