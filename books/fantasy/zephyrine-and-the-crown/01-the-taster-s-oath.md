## 1. The Taster's Oath

The spoon was silver, older than the palace, and it had never once been washed.

Lena held it the way he'd been taught at eleven years old—cradled in the left palm, bowl tilted toward the heart, so that the metal could drink the warmth of his skin and the poison, if there was any, would have to pass through him first before it ever touched a king. Nine years of that. Nine years of silver warming against his palm while the kitchens roared around him like a river in flood, and still, every morning, the first thing he felt was the spoon's small cold weight becoming his own.

"Rice porridge," said Maren, sliding the tray across the tasting bench. "Almond milk, saffron, a whisper of honey from the southern hives. Cook swears on her mother's grave nothing's touched it but her own two hands."

"Cook swore the same thing the day the honey came laced with oleander," Lena said. "Her mother has died four times now."

Maren snorted and turned back to her ovens. That was the rhythm of the morning kitchen: steam and shouting, knives keeping time, and at the center of it all one narrow bench where a boy with no family name worth speaking sat alone with a spoon and ate the king's breakfast so the king wouldn't have to die of it.

He took the first mouthful plain. Rice, almond, the ghost of saffron—expensive, careful, ordinary. He counted to ten. Then he dipped the tip of one finger into the vial at his belt and touched it to his tongue: the reagent, bitter as burnt hair. His gums tingled faintly, the way they always did when arsenic was near, even in quantities too small to name. Not in the porridge. In the *air*, maybe, or in his imagination, which after nine years had become its own kind of instrument.

He ate three more spoonsful, slowly, letting the kitchen watch him do it. That was part of the ritual too. A taster who gulped in private was a taster who could be bribed in private. Everything about his office lived in daylight, in front of witnesses, in the open mouth.

"The seal," Maren said, and pressed warm wax to the tray's edge, and Lena set his thumbprint into it beside hers, and just like that the king's breakfast became safe by law.

The tray went up the service stair on a footman's shoulder. Lena climbed behind it, because he always did—not because anyone required it anymore, but because the walk through the palace was the part of the day he loved, and he was honest enough with himself, in the privacy of his own skull, to admit it.

* * *

The palace woke in layers, like a body coming back from sleep. First the servants' corridors, all stone and candle-smoke, where the walls sweated in winter and the flagstones knew your footsteps better than your friends did. Then the gallery with its windows of colored glass, where dawn came through in pieces—a red shard across the floor, a blue one climbing the wall—and the portraits of dead monarchs watched you with the patience of people who had won their arguments centuries ago. Then the wide stair, the guards in Zephyrine blue, the smell of beeswax and stone polish, until finally the doors of the morning chamber opened onto carpet thick enough to swallow sound whole.

Lena knew every seam of that journey. He knew which floorboard sang under the fourth window, and which portrait—the third king, the one with the weak chin—had a draft behind it that made the canvas breathe. He knew the guards by name and the maids by their coughs. Nine years ago he had come up these stairs half-starved off the Thornfell roads, a smuggler's kid with quick hands and a palate sharp enough to tell hemlock from parsley blindfolded, and the guild had looked at him and seen not a child but an instrument: something expendable, precise, and grateful for bread.

They hadn't been wrong. That was the shameful part. He *was* grateful. The palace fed him, warmed him, taught him letters and poisons in equal measure. It had given him Bram, who was the closest thing to a father the roads had ever let him keep. And in exchange, all it asked was that he be willing to die quietly, without ceremony, somewhere between the kitchen and the king's fork—his body carried out through the servants' door like spoiled fish, his name entered nowhere, his grave unmarked, because a taster's death was a security failure and security failures embarrassed everyone.

He loved this place. It would let him die without ceremony. Both of those things were true at once, and he had made his peace with the contradiction the way you make peace with weather.

In the morning chamber, the king sat at the long table with the porridge steaming in front of him, flanked by the chamberlain and two secretaries and a chaplain whose prayers had the cadence of a man reading shipping manifests. Lena stood at his post against the wall, hands folded, invisible in the way that nine years had made him: furniture that breathed.

King Aldous the Third was not an old man, but he had governed long enough to be carved down by it—gray at the temples, lines around the eyes like cracks in fired clay, hands that trembled slightly unless he gripped them still. He had been, in Lena's earliest years here, a tired and decent sort of tired; he complained about the mutton, thanked the kitchen staff by name twice a year, and once, memorably, had asked Lena whether the arsenic tingle hurt, and when Lena said it only tickled, the king had laughed and said, *Then we are both liars, my boy.*

That king had not laughed in some time.

This morning, Aldous looked at the porridge for a long moment. Then he looked up—at Lena, directly, which was itself unusual; kings did not see tasters—and smiled.

It was a good smile. That was what unsettled. It reached the eyes, crinkled them warmly. But it arrived a half-beat late, like thunder after lightning, like a man remembering the order of his own face.

"You've tasted it," the king said.

"I have, Majesty."

"And I am safe."

"As safe as I can make you."

The smile widened a fraction. The king picked up his spoon and ate—heartily, ravenously, with an appetite that had grown strange over the past month, enormous and undiscriminating, as though hunger itself had moved into him and taken rooms. He finished the porridge in a quarter of the usual time and asked for more. When the second serving came he ate that too, and asked after the smoked eel, and the black bread with drippings, and the pickled things, eating everything set before him with the same deep mechanical pleasure, and Lena watched and felt the morning go subtly wrong around him, the way a room goes wrong when you notice the clock has stopped.

Kings were supposed to be finicky. Kings were supposed to push food around, complain, grow bored. This one ate like a man who had forgotten how eating worked and was practicing.

"He hasn't blinked," said a voice at Lena's shoulder, low and gravel-worn. "Watch. Go on. Watch him."

Bram had come up without Lena noticing, which was nearly impossible; the old taster moved through palaces the way fog moves through a harbor. He was seventy if he was sixty, built like a barrel stave, with a cook's burn-scars down one forearm and eyes that had watched four monarchs eat and outlived three of them. He stood beside Lena now, ostensibly supervising, actually staring at the king with an expression Lena had never seen on him before.

Bram was afraid. Lena catalogued it the way he catalogued flavors—with precision, and a rising sickness.

"Majesty blinks," Lena murmured. "I've seen him blink."

"When?"

"This—" Lena turned back to the table and watched, and counted, and the porridge disappeared spoonful by spoonful, and the king chewed, and swallowed, and smiled his late-arriving smile at the chamberlain, and answered a secretary's question about grain tariffs in a voice pitched perfectly, correctly, and—

Lena lost count somewhere past four hundred seconds.

The eyelids never moved. The eyes stayed wet and fixed, rolling smoothly from speaker to speaker like marbles in oil, and not once—not for tariff questions, not for the chaplain's blessing, not when a secretary dropped an inkwell and the whole room flinched—did Aldous the Third, King of Zephyrine, Defender of the Marches, close his eyes.

Men blinked. Lena had spent his life studying bodies the way Sera Vane studied maps, though he hadn't met her yet and didn't know it: the flush of fear, the sweat of lies, the pupil blown wide by poison. Blinking was the body's most basic honesty. You could train away a stutter. Nobody trained away blinking.

"Three days," Bram said quietly. "I counted the first night, thinking I was getting old and stupid. Second night I asked the night porter. He says the same. Three days, no blink, no rub of the eye, nothing." He paused. "And the appetite. Boy, I've fed five royals through this kitchen. Appetites change with grief, with love, with bad livers. This isn't appetite. This is a belly learning the shape of itself."

"What are you saying?" Lena asked, and heard how thin his voice came out.

Bram didn't answer. He was watching the king finish the eel—every bone stripped clean, the head left staring—and whatever the old man saw in it closed his face like a door.

"Eat your own supper tonight before you serve it to anyone," Bram said instead. "Sleep in the kitchen, not your bunk. And Lena—" his hand landed heavy on the boy's shoulder, calloused and warm, the one point of heat in a morning gone cold at the edges, "—if the wards on the border fall tonight, you run. Don't ask me how I know to say that. Just remember it."

"The wards? Bram, the wards have stood since the first king—"

"Aye," said Bram, and took his hand away. "Since the first king. That's rather what frightens me."

At the table, the king set down his spoon, aligned it precisely with the tray's edge, and rose. The chamber bowed in a rustle of silk and knee-joints. And as the monarch passed the wall where Lena stood—invisible, breathless, a boy holding a silver spoon against his heart like a small shield—the king turned his head.

Not quickly. Slowly, smoothly, the way a weathervane turns when the wind changes. The wet unblinking eyes found Lena's and held them, and the late-arriving smile arrived, and the king said, in a voice pitched perfectly, correctly, kindly:

"Your porridge was excellent today. Tell the kitchens I tasted every bit of the care in it."

Then he walked on, robes whispering, chaplain trailing, and Lena stood very still among the bowing courtiers and understood, with the certainty of a man who has just identified a poison by its smell, two things at once.

The king did not taste anything anymore. And the king should not have known his taster's work had care in it—because the taster's whole art was to be unseen, unnamed, unfelt.

The palace bells rang the hour. Somewhere far to the east, beyond the walls, beyond the city, beyond the mountains where the Thornfell wards had slept for three hundred years, the sky hung low and gray and waiting, full of stars that had fewer than twelve hours left to live.
