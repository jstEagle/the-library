## 2. Night Without Stars

The kitchens slept the way old animals sleep: half-awake, one ear turned toward the door. Lena Lockridge had learned to sleep that way too, nine years of poison spoons and midnight summonses having burned the habit into him deeper than any scar. So when the summons came—soft footsteps on the stair, the particular knock the under-steward used for *urgent but not yet screaming*—Lena was already reaching for his boots before the second rap fell.

He paused at the narrow window of his room above the scullery first. Habit. He always looked at the sky before a night tasting; Bram had taught him that in his first year. *The stars tell you what season of trouble you're in, boy. Clear and cold means politics. Clouded and warm means someone's been to the coast for shellfish.* It was a joke, mostly. But it had made Lena a reader of skies the way other men read ledgers.

Tonight there were no stars at all.

Not clouded. Not veiled. *Gone.*

He stood at the window with one boot in his hand and counted, the way you count after a blow to the head, waiting for sense to return. The sky over Zephyrine's capital was a smooth, unbroken black, like the inside of a covered pot. No moon. No constellations he had known since childhood—the Miller's Wheel, the Weeping Sisters, the long pale scar of the Thornfell Reach along the southern horizon. The darkness wasn't deep, exactly. It was *flat*. A ceiling instead of a heaven.

Below him, the city should have been dotted with watch-fires and lantern-glow. It was not. There was the palace, ringed in its own torchlight like an island, and beyond it a blackness so total that Lena could not tell where the rooftops ended and the world began.

"Master Lockridge?" The under-steward's voice through the door, thin as a whisker. "His Majesty requires a tasting. The west battlement. They've—" A hesitation, the sound of a man deciding how much truth would get him slapped. "They've laid a supper up there. On the wall itself. You're to come at once."

"Who laid a supper on the battlements?"

"The King did, sir. With his own hands."

* * *

Lena climbed the west stair with his kit against his ribs and his mind running the corridors of possibility the way it always did when a task refused to make sense. Kings did not dine on battlements. Kings did not serve themselves. In nine years he had tasted every meal the monarch took—seventy-two bites a day, each one logged in the tasters' ledger with the hour, the dish, the sensation—and not once had Aldric IV lifted so much as a spoon without ceremony, without the chamberlain announcing the course, without the small theater of power that kept a court a court.

The stairs were wrong too. Servants pressed against walls with candles cupped close, whispering. Somewhere below, the alarm bells of the river garrison began to ring—not the measured toll of a drill but the ragged overlapping clangor of towers answering each other in panic. Lena heard fragments as he passed: *the wards are down*, *Thornfell's gone dark*, *the border stones are cold*, *no stars, have you seen, no stars anywhere.*

He stopped once, at the arrow-slit window on the fourth landing, and looked south.

The Thornfell border wards were older than the kingdom. Older than the dynasty, if you believed Sera Vane's maps, and Lena had believed them once, before her disgrace. Nine great standing stones strung across the mountain passes like beads on a wire, each one singing a note too low for human ears, the whole line humming a boundary into the world: *here ends law, here begins wilderness, and nothing hungry may cross.* Every child in Zephyrine grew up under that hum the way they grew up under weather. You didn't hear it. You felt it, the way you felt your own heartbeat—a thing whose absence would be louder than its presence.

Lena felt the absence now. It was like a tooth pulled from the jaw of the world. The mountains stood unguarded against a blackness that no longer bothered to pretend to be sky.

And the soldiers—he saw them from the slit, torchlight spilling from the south gate in a wavering river—were marching into it. Thirty men, then fifty, their torches burning normally in the courtyard and then, at some invisible threshold, simply *dimming*, the flames shrinking as though dipped in water, until the rearmost soldiers watched their vanguard walk into a darkness that swallowed fire whole. Lena heard the captain shouting for lanterns, more lanterns, oil, anything that burned—and heard under the shouting the thing he would remember longest: the silence of the men. No panic. Just the quiet obedience of soldiers following orders into a place where orders might mean nothing, because the world's rules had come loose from their moorings and nobody had told the chain of command.

He made himself climb the rest of the way.

* * *

The west battlement supper was laid out on a campaign table that must have taken six servants to carry up the spiral stair: a roast quail, glazed and glistening; bread still steaming from the ovens; honey, figs, a cup of dark southern wine. All of it arranged with care. All of it arranged, Lena realized as he came level with the parapet, by the hands of the man standing behind it.

King Aldric IV stood alone at the wall, facing out over the starless dark, and he was smiling.

Lena had tasted for this man for nine years. He knew the king's face better than his own reflection—the heavy jaw, the grey coming into the beard at the temples, the small scar through the left eyebrow from a hunting lance in his youth. He knew the king's moods the way a wife knows weather: the thunderclouds of council days, the thin sunshine of feasts, the long grey drizzle of grief since the queen's passing three winters past. He had never loved the man. You didn't love the man you died for, professionally speaking. But he had studied him with a devotion that amounted to something adjacent, and what stood at the parapet tonight was wearing all of it correctly and none of it truly.

The face was right. The smile was wrong. Not cruel—nothing so useful as cruelty. It was the smile of a man remembering a joke told in another country, aimed at nothing, informed by nothing, hanging on those familiar features like a portrait hung slightly crooked.

"Your Majesty." Lena bowed, low enough that his eyes went to the flagstones, and when he rose the king had not moved. Had not, Lena noticed with a slow cold spreading under his sternum, *turned around*. "You requested a tasting."

"I did." The voice was the king's voice. The cadence was almost the king's cadence. Almost. There was a pause where breath should have shaped the words, a fractional gap between the syllables, as if each one were being selected from a drawer somewhere behind the teeth. "Taste it for me, Taster. All of it. I want to know it's safe."

Lena opened his kit. Hands steady—that part was trained so deep it operated independent of terror. He took the ritual spoon from its velvet slot, broke the bread, took the mandated bite. Waited the sixty heartbeats. Nothing. The wine: a swallow held under the tongue, then swallowed, watching his own fingertips for the flush of arsenic or the numbness of hemlock. Nothing. The quail, carved at the breast, dressed with its glaze. Nothing.

"Safe," he said. "All of it, Majesty. Clean as temple bread."

"Good." The king turned then, and Lena mastered himself, because the movement was *slow*—not weary, not deliberate in any human way, but slow the way a millstone is slow, with a weight behind it that had nothing to do with muscle. The starless dark reflected in the king's eyes, and Lena understood with a lurch that it was reflecting nothing, that those eyes were not catching the torchlight at all, that they were simply *painted* with the same flat black as the sky. "You taste everything for me," the king said, smiling his crooked-hung smile. "Everything I take into myself. Isn't that your oath?"

"It is, Majesty."

"Then taste the night."

For one vertiginous moment Lena thought the thing meant it literally—that it wanted him to lift a spoonful of that flat black dark and swallow—and his body reacted before his mind could mock him: a full flinch, spine curving, hand going to the spoon at his belt like a knife. The king watched the flinch with an expression of gentle interest, the way a scholar watches an insect navigate a maze.

"You're frightened," the king observed. "Good. Fear is honest. I've had so little honest material lately." He gestured at the table with a hand that bent at slightly too many joints, or perhaps Lena only imagined that, would spend years trying to decide if he'd imagined it. "Eat, Taster. Sit with me. The view is remarkable. Everything is finally quiet."

Lena looked out at the darkness swallowing torchfire at the south gate, and thought of Bram, and said the only safe sentence available: "I'm needed below, Majesty. If the wards have fallen—"

"They haven't fallen." For the first time, something moved in the painted eyes. Something like appetite. "They were *paid*. Do you know the difference, Taster? A wall falls. A debt is honored." The smile widened by a degree. "Someone has been keeping accounts."

Then, distantly, from somewhere beneath them in the palace, a bell rang the pattern Lena knew better than his own name: the kitchen bell, four strikes and a fifth, calling the taster's guild to emergency muster. Bram's call. Bram would have heard about the battlement supper. Bram, who had been saying for a week that something was off, that the king hadn't blinked in three days, that the oaths renewed at court each evening had begun to sound less like ceremony and more like *feeding*—

"Go," the king said, still facing the dark, still smiling. "Run along to your guild, little spoon. Taste what needs tasting." And then, as Lena backed toward the stair with his kit hugged to his chest and his bow scraped shallow and fast: "You'll want to be careful in the corridors tonight. The dark came inside with us. It likes the low places. It knows the servants' ways."

Lena fled. He did not run—running invited questions, and questions invited attention, and attention tonight felt like blood in water—but he took the spiral stair at a pace that shamed his dignity, and behind him he heard, faint over the bells and the wind, the sound of the king laughing at nothing, alone on the wall, in a voice that got the pauses between the syllables exactly, precisely wrong.

* * *

The kitchens were awake and terrified and pretending otherwise, which is to say they were working. Forty people in motion: fires stoked high, oaths muttered over rising dough, the ancient superstitions of the guild dragged out of storage like armor. Salt at every threshold. Iron knives pointing north. Cooks who had spent thirty years sneering at folklore now laying iron and salt with the fervor of converts, because the sky was empty and everyone in the building could feel the hole where the wards' hum used to be, a silence pressing on the ear-drums like altitude.

Bram found him at the washing trough, scrubbing the tasting spoon with a violence that had nothing to do with cleanliness.

The old man looked ten years worse than he had at supper service. His hands shook. Not with fear, Lena thought—with the particular tremor of a man who has seen something and is holding it behind his teeth at enormous cost. He waited until they were alone in the spice closet, among the hanging bundles of hyssop and sage, and even then he spoke barely above breath.

"You tasted him."

"On the wall. Yes."

"And?"

Lena set the spoon down. His reflection swam in the basin water, broken by drips. "Nothing touched me. Nothing will ever touch him either—I watched him stand an arm's length from the parapet in this wind and his hair didn't move. Bram. His hair didn't move." He heard his own voice climbing and forced it down. "What happened tonight? The wards, the stars—all at once, like snuffed candles. What does that?"

Bram lowered himself onto the flour barrel, an old man's movement, joints protesting, and for a long moment he looked at the closed door, listening. Then he reached into his coat and brought out a folded scrap of parchment and handed it over. Lena unfolded it. It was the oath registry page from last night's court renewal—the master list, the one the chamberlain signed, the one the tasters' guild received copies of because guild law required them to know who had access to the royal table. Routine paperwork. Except that every name on the page was written in the same hand.

Every name. Two hundred courtiers, ministers, guardsmen, each with their own signature for forty years of renewals—and last night, every single signature identical. Not forged. *Identical.* The same down to the ink-blot at the descender of the final letter, repeated two hundred times, as if two hundred pens had been guided by one hand.

"The stars didn't go out," Bram said quietly. "That's what nobody understands yet, shouting about omens. Stars don't go out all at once, boy—it doesn't work like that. Light takes years to fall. Whatever we were looking at up there for all these centuries, it stopped being shown to us at midnight. Like a curtain drawn." He rubbed his face with both shaking hands. "And the wards. Sera Vane tried to tell them, three years ago. The wards aren't stone, she said. They're contract. Old, old contract. Paid-for protection, generation after generation, and the payment—" He stopped. Looked at Lena with eyes that held something worse than fear: recognition. "The payment has always been sworn at the crown's own table. Who do you think carries the tribute to the border stones every solstice? Who carries the sealed dishes?"

The floor seemed to tilt. Lena gripped the edge of the basin. Nine years of meals. Seventy-two bites a day. The ledger, the ritual spoon, the salt and the silverleaf and the solemn theater of the tasting—and threaded through all of it, invisible as arsenic, something else being served, carried, *delivered*, by the very people trained never to notice what they carried.

"Bram," he said slowly. "The supper on the battlements. He laid it himself. Why would a king lay his own table?"

Bram looked at him for a long time. Outside, the alarm bells had finally gone silent, and the silence was worse—thirty towers' worth of panic resolving into the held breath of a city waiting to be told what the dark wanted. When the old taster finally spoke, his voice was the one he used over deathbeds.

"Because whatever is wearing Aldric's face isn't celebrating a victory, lad. It's *famished*. The wards bought it nine kingdoms' worth of restraint and it paid with the king's oath, and now it's hungry in a way that has no bottom, and everything it eats next—" He glanced again at the door, at the palace above them where two hundred identically-signed names lay sleeping, contented, fed. "It eats loyalty, boy. That's the dish. That's always been the dish. Oath by oath, signature by signature. And tomorrow morning the whole court lines up to renew."

Lena stood in the spice closet with his spoon clean and his ledger accurate and nine years of perfect professional service behind him, and understood that the rules had not merely broken. They had been *collected*, quietly, like dishes after a feast—and the hand that gathered them wore the face of the man he had sworn to die for.

Above the palace, through the smoke-vent, the sky stayed black. And somewhere down in the low places, in the servants' corridors, in the dark that had come inside with them, something patient began to walk.
