## 3. What the King Swallowed

The summons came at dusk, carried by a page who would not meet Lena's eyes.

Not that pages ever met anyone's eyes—they were trained out of it early, the way hawks were trained to hoods—but this boy's gaze skittered sideways like something hunted. His livery was rumpled. His hands shook around the folded card with its black wax seal.

"Taster Lockridge," he recited, voice thin as onion skin. "His Majesty requests your presence at the private supper. The *private* supper." He said it twice, as though repetition might make the words mean less. "In the Amber Room. Alone."

Lena set down the mortar he had been grinding—foxglove and chalk, a mild sedative for the Lord Chancellor's insomnia—and wiped his hands on his apron out of habit rather than need. Nine years in the palace had taught him that every summons had a flavor. This one tasted of copper.

"Alone," he repeated.

"Alone," the page confirmed, and then, so quietly it barely qualified as speech: "They're saying the chamberlain hasn't come out since yesterday. They're saying the king ate in front of him and the chamberlain just... watched."

*Watched.* Everything in the palace these days was reduced to watching. Watching the king who did not blink. Watching the sky where no stars hung. Watching for orders that explained what everyone was too frightened to ask.

Lena pulled off his apron, folded it over the chair back, and followed the boy into the darkening corridors.

* * *

The kitchens had been his kingdom once, or the nearest thing to one. He passed through them now the way a man passes through a house he grew up in after the funeral—everything familiar, nothing his. The night shift worked in silence, which was wrong. Kitchens were never meant to be silent. They were meant to roar and clatter and curse, to be a warm animal body of noise at the palace's cold heart. Tonight the cooks moved like sleepwalkers, and the only sounds were knives on boards and water boiling over unattended until someone remembered, distantly, to lift the pot.

Bram caught him at the door to the service stair, one hand closing on Lena's sleeve with the strength of a man who had spent forty years hauling carcasses.

"You're not going up there." Bram's whisper was gravel. He was an old wall of a man, gray-stubbled, scarred from a lifetime of kitchen fires and sharper things, and right now his face held something Lena had never seen on it: fear without shame. "Tell them you're ill. Tell them you fell down the stairs. Tell them anything."

"It's a direct summons. With seal."

"I don't care if it's written in fire on the sky." Bram glanced over his shoulder at the silent cooks, and lowered his voice further. "Three days, Lena. Three days and he hasn't blinked. I timed him last night—I stood at the back of the hall with a wine tray for two hours, and I counted. You know how many times the man blinked? None. Not once. And when the Duke of Calloway made his little joke about the harvest, the king laughed four seconds too long. Everyone heard it. Nobody breathed."

"The king is grieving," Lena said, because it was the official line, because grief was the one poison no taster could draw off. "He lost his son."

"He lost his son eight months ago and wept exactly once, at the funeral, into a handkerchief that came away dry." Bram's grip tightened. "I've fed three kings, boy. I know what hunger looks like, and I know what *appetite* looks like, and whatever sits on that throne now has appetite for nothing we understand. And you—" he jabbed a blunt finger at Lena's chest, "—you are the one they send to taste for him. You think about what that means. Whatever he is now, whatever he wants—you're the first mouth it touches."

"Bram—"

"They'd let you die without ceremony." The old man's voice cracked, quiet and terrible. "You know that. Nine years, and there's no medal for a taster, no grave with a name. There's a line in the ledger. *Spoilage.*"

Lena did know. He had known since his first week, when Taster Morrow coughed blood into a napkin at the Duke of Vess's banquet and was carried out through the servants' door while the string quartet played on. Morrow had survived—the dose was light—but the ledger entry existed; Lena had seen it, out of morbid curiosity, in the steward's office. *One unit spoilage, Tuesday seating.*

And he had stayed anyway. That was the wound under all his wounds: he had stayed. Because the palace fed him, clothed him, gave him a place in the order of things. Because the ritual spoon felt, some nights, like a purpose. Because belonging, even the kind that killed you slowly and filed you under *spoilage*, was sweeter than the freedom of the streets where he'd learned to eat garbage and call it dinner.

"I'll be careful," he said.

Bram looked at him for a long moment, and Lena understood that the old man was memorizing his face, the way you memorize a face at a graveside.

"Eat nothing up there," Bram said. "Swallow nothing. Not wine to rinse, not bread to clear the palate. If the king offers you food from his own plate—" he stopped, jaw working. "Just don't. Promise me."

"I promise."

It was the easiest promise he had ever made and, before the night was out, the only thing that would save his life.

* * *

The Amber Room earned its name at dusk, when the low sun came through the western windows and turned everything the color of honey held to flame. But the sun was setting into a starless sky now—it had done so for three nights, sinking into a darkness that did not glitter, and the room's amber had gone the color of old urine, of illness, of things left too long in their own juices.

The table was small. Intimate. Set for two.

The king sat at the far end with his back to the windows, and the failing light did strange things to him—or perhaps the strange things were simply the truth, visible now in certain lights, the way bruises show better by candle than by day. He wore midnight blue. His hands lay on either side of his plate with perfect, deliberate symmetry, like objects arranged rather than limbs at rest. His face was the king's face—that heavy jaw, those deep-set eyes, the nose broken in a hunt twenty years ago—and yet assembled slightly wrong, as if a very skilled painter had copied it from memory and gotten every measurement correct but the *spirit* incorrect by a hair.

"Lena Lockridge." The king said his full name, and his voice was the king's voice, pitched and paced correctly, and still wrong. It was like hearing a beloved song played by a musician who had learned every note from a transcription and never heard the original. "Come. Sit. You've been with us nine years, haven't you? Such faithful service."

"Nine years in spring, Your Majesty." Lena bowed the exact depth required and took the offered seat, placing himself carefully across the table rather than beside. Distance was a language. He wanted to speak fluently tonight.

The dishes arrived by dumbwaiter, each sealed with the royal kitchen's wax, each carried to the table by the king himself—no footmen, no cupbearers, no witnesses but Lena. This was the private supper indeed. The chamberlain, presumably, had been witness to yesterday's version and was now somewhere being quietly un-witnessed.

"Your work precedes you," the king said, lifting the first cloche. Under it sat a small bird—quail, glazed dark, stuffed with morels and chestnuts—and beneath the glaze, beneath the stuffing, tucked into the meat like a secret, Lena smelled it before he saw it: arsenic. A generous quantity. Enough that the fumes alone should have stung his eyes.

His stomach dropped through the floor.

Because *he* had prepared this dish. Or rather, he had prepared the tasting portion of it, three hours ago, in the kitchens, following the standing order that came down after the border wards fell: *all the king's meals to be tested at double strength.* Double strength. He had laid the arsenic in himself, careful as embroidery, and sent the quail up with its seal, and the quail on this table was that same quail—he knew the particular char on the breast, the way he knew his own handwriting.

The king lifted the bird in both hands, like a lover, and bit into it.

He chewed slowly. He closed his eyes. He made a sound—a soft, wet exhalation of pleasure, the sound Lena's grandmother used to make over stew on winter nights—and swallowed.

"There," the king murmured. "There it is. Do you know, Lockridge, that I can taste it? Every grain. It blooms on the tongue like frost. Like snowfall inside the skull." He opened his eyes, and they found Lena with the slow deliberation of a compass needle finding north. "The cooks think I don't notice. They think the doses are secrets. But I taste *everything* now. The rat poison in Wednesday's pottage. The hemlock in the wine. The nightshade they brushed over the sugared plums—oh, that was elegant, that was almost respectful. Someone in my kitchens has been trying to save me, in the only language they have left."

The room tilted. Lena kept his face smooth through nine years of practice, but inside him something was screaming in a very small voice.

*Double strength.* He hadn't poisoned the king. No one had. The standing order was his—the tasters' guild's—meant to protect the king by testing everything harder. And the king knew. The king had eaten every test, every doubled and tripled dose, for three days, and thanked no one, and blinked not once.

"Your Majesty honors me with his confidence," Lena said. His voice sounded fine. Distantly he was proud of it.

"Honor." The king smiled. The smile arrived in pieces—mouth first, eyes several seconds later, like thunder after lightning. "Eat, Lockridge. You've barely touched yours."

Lena looked down at his own plate. He had not noticed food being placed before him. That was wrong—he *never* failed to notice food; it was his profession, his religion, the architecture of his survival. Yet there it sat: a portion of the same quail, carved and steaming, arranged with tenderness.

"The same preparation?" he asked lightly.

"Of course. From my own plate. Nothing at this table is poisoned." The king's laugh, four seconds too long. "Except everything at this table is poisoned. Isn't that amusing? You must tell me, expert to connoisseur—can you taste the difference between a toxin and its absence? Which fork of the tongue knows truth from mercy?"

"I was instructed not to eat during a tasting, Majesty." The rule was real. Bram's promise sat behind the words like a wall. "Protocol."

"Protocol." The king rolled the word around his mouth as if tasting it too. "Yes. You people love your protocols. Protocols are how the invisible stay safe, aren't they? Stand in the exact spot, bow the exact depth, swallow exactly what you're told, and no one ever has to see you." He leaned forward, and the candle flames—all of them, simultaneously, though no door had opened—bent toward him like courtiers. "But I see you, Lena Lockridge. I have been *made* of seeing. I drink oath and attention the way you drink broth. Every soul in this palace bows to me nightly and pours itself a little deeper into my keeping, and do you know what you are to me?"

Lena did not answer. His heart was slamming against his ribs hard enough that he feared it might be audible, might be *appetizing.*

"A gap," the king said softly. "A hole in the fabric. Nine years of swallowing poison and bowing and holding your tongue, and the oaths slide right off you, because you never gave one. Not truly. You gave your labor, your palate, your life by the spoonful—but never your *fealty*. Isn't that remarkable? The most loyal man in the palace, measured in doses endured, and the only one I cannot taste." Something moved behind the king's face then, something with hunger in it, and for a moment the mask slipped far enough that Lena saw what wore it: not a face at all. An arrangement. A hollow shaped exactly like want. "I have been thinking about your gap, Lockridge. Thinking about how to fill it."

The king reached across the table.

His hand was not fast. That was the horror of it—it was *slow*, slower than any living movement, the fingers unfolding like something underwater, like time-lapse flowers, like a thing that had forgotten bodies take effort. And in that slowness, in the terrible leisure of it, Lena understood everything at once, the way a man falling understands the whole height of the tower:

Nothing inside that thing was alive. Poison did not touch it because poison needed a body to poison—needed enzymes, needed breath, needed the busy machinery of a living cell to sabotage. The king had eaten arsenic and hemlock and nightshade with relish the way a stone eats rain. The stars had gone out and the wards had fallen and the man on the throne had not blinked in three days because blinking was a living reflex, and whatever had answered the vacancy left by a dead king was not alive, was not dead, was *hungry*, and it had just told Lena—in the politest possible terms—that it intended to eat his emptiness next.

He was moving before the decision reached him. Nine years of servant corridors lived in his legs; he was through the service door and into the passage beyond before the king's fingers finished crossing the tablecloth.

Behind him, unhurried, came the scrape of a chair. Then footsteps. Slow ones.

* * *

The servants' passages were his second native country. He ran them now in the dark, palms brushing walls he knew by texture—plaster, then rough stone, then the seam of oak paneling behind the queen's gallery—and the whole time the slow footsteps followed, patient as tide.

They shouldn't have been audible. The passages swallowed sound; he had hidden in them half his life, unheard. But each footfall came through the walls themselves, through the wood and plaster, a pressure more than a noise: *step. step. step.* Unrushed. Certain. A creature that knew the palace had nowhere else for him to go.

*Loyal man in the palace,* it had said. *Measured in doses endured.*

Nine years. Nine years of ritual spoons and ledger entries, of Morrow coughing blood while the quartet played, of a palace that would file his death under *spoilage*—and he had called it belonging. He had loved it. That was the betrayal underneath all betrayals: not that the crown had never seen him, but that he had mistaken being useful for being seen, and the thing upstairs had looked straight through nine years of devotion and found only a gap. A hole to fill. The invisibility he had thought was his armor, his craft, his cleverness—it had never been his at all. The palace had made him invisible so that it could use him up unseen, and now the monster in the king's skin had noticed the hole because holes were precisely what it ate.

He burst out through the linen chute access on the ground floor, crossed the scullery where the night shift still moved in their trance—*run*, he wanted to scream at them, *don't you feel it?*—and slid out through the delivery gate where the ash-cart had left the door unlatched, as it always did, as it had the night he'd first crept in nine years ago, a starving boy following the smell of bread.

The outer yard. The sky above it was a bowl of absolute black, starless, moonless, a ceiling instead of a heaven. Against that blackness the battlements stood silhouetted, and on them, at intervals, the wardens walked their posts—and Lena saw, with a lurch, that their lanterns cast no glow backward. The light went out of the lamps and simply stopped, as if the dark drank it.

*Step. Step.*

From somewhere above and behind, through six floors of stone, the slow footsteps continued descending. Or perhaps they didn't. Perhaps the sound was only in his head now, which would be worse.

Lena pressed himself into the shadow of the ash barrels, pulled the hood of a carter's coat over his gold taster's collar, and ran—not toward the great gates, which would be sealed and warded and full of soldiers marching into a darkness that swallowed torchlight, but west, toward the low wall by the tannery ditch, toward the gap in the palisade that only smugglers, deserters, and kitchen boys knew.

Thornfell. The old roads. Nine years since he'd run them, and every mile of them still lived in his feet.

As he dropped over the wall into the ditch, he looked back once, one breath, no more—at the palace blazing with light it could not push outward, at the starless sky pressing down on it like a lid, at the high window of the Amber Room where a candle-flame silhouette stood watching. Even at this distance, even in the dark, he felt it find him. Felt the attention settle on his back like a cold hand.

It wasn't chasing. That was the thing that turned his marrow to ice-water as he scrambled up the far bank and into the black fields. It wasn't chasing, because it already knew where the gap would run.

It was simply letting him carry his hunger-filled emptiness out into the world—like a message. Like an invitation.

Like the first course of something long.
