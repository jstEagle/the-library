## 4. Thornfell Roads

The city sealed itself at dawn, and Lena Lockridge was already in the walls.

He had learned the servant corridors the way other children learned prayers: by repetition, by fear, by the slow accumulation of small mercies. Nine years of carrying trays through passages no architect had bothered to draw had taught him that a palace was not a building but a body, and like any body it had veins and bowels and blind little capillaries where a man could hide if he made himself small enough. The kitchens were its heart. The throne room was its mouth. And somewhere behind its eyes, the thing wearing King Aldric's face was still chewing.

Lena moved through the dark behind the larder walls with his stolen satchel pressed to his chest and listened to the city close around him.

First came the bells — not alarm bells, which rang sharp and scattered, but the low, deliberate tolling of the seal, struck once from every tower at the same instant, so that the sound arrived everywhere at once like a held breath finally released. Then the gates. He felt them rather than heard them, a shudder through the stone as the portcullises dropped, as the great oak doors swung shut on their iron hinges, as Zephyrine became a closed fist. By noon, he knew, the wardens would be going door to door with the king's new decree: no one leaves, no one enters, all loyal subjects will present themselves for the renewing of oaths.

All loyal subjects. As though loyalty were a ration to be collected, weighed, stamped.

Lena pressed his palm against the cold wall and let himself feel it — really feel it, for the first time since he'd fled the royal supper chamber with his heart hammering and his mind refusing to finish the thought it had started. *Nothing inside him is alive.* He had tasted poison for nine years. He knew what death looked like from the inside, what it did to a tongue, a pulse, the wet brightness of an eye. The king had eaten arsenic the way a child eats honey, and there had been nothing behind his smile to kill.

Grief came for him then, in the dark between the walls, and he let it, because there was nowhere to run from it anyway.

It wasn't grief for Aldric — or for whatever Aldric had been before the crown hollowed him. It was grief for the palace itself, for the warm bread smell of the morning ovens and the ritual spoon worn smooth by a hundred years of tasters' thumbs, for Bram's gruff voice calling him *boy* when they both knew Bram thought of him as something closer to a son. He had loved this place the way a dog loves the hand that feeds it, knowing full well the same hand held the leash and the whip. The palace would have let him die without ceremony. A taster's funeral was a line in a ledger and a spoon returned to the drawer. And he had loved it anyway. That was the poison no immunity could touch.

He wiped his face on his sleeve, breathed until the shaking stopped, and kept moving.

* *

The Thornfell roads began where the maps ended, and Sera Vane — wherever she was now, disgraced and exiled — would have wept at how thoroughly they ended.

There were no smuggler paths marked on any chart of the capital, because smuggler paths were not built but *negotiated*, argued into existence over generations by people the city preferred to forget. A taster learned them the way one learned poisons: not from books but from necessity, from the guild's quiet instruction in case a taster ever needed to carry evidence out of a palace that had decided to eat him too. Old Master Corvin, dead ten years now, had drilled the first route into him over a single bottle of plum wine: *If the day comes, boy, you go down through the ash chute, along the culvert under the tannery wall, up the bone-dry well behind the chandler's, and you don't stop moving until the stones under your feet smell of pine instead of smoke. The moment they smell of pine, you're in Thornfell, and Thornfell belongs to no king.*

The ash chute was a throat of greasy black stone that dropped thirty feet into darkness. Lena went down it hand over hand, the satchel strapped tight across his back, its seal — the crowned spoon of the royal kitchens, pressed in red wax — riding against his spine like a second heartbeat. Inside the satchel was the king's supper. Every dish, untouched, wrapped in oilcloth against the cold: the roast quail glazed with honey and nightshade extract, the wine cut with foxglove, the candied figs rolled in powdered hellebore. A dose enough to kill a garrison. Eaten without a flicker. He had carried the evidence out because evidence was the only weapon a servant owned, and because some stubborn, unkillable part of him believed that truth, properly preserved, could be made lethal.

The culvert under the tannery wall ran ankle-deep in water that smelled of rot and lime, and Lena waded it bent double, listening. Above him, muffled through stone, he could hear the wardens moving through the streets — not shouting, not running, but walking. Walking in step. Hundreds of boots striking cobbles in perfect unison, a rhythm with no drummer, steady as a metronome. It was wrong in a way he couldn't name and didn't want to. Soldiers marched; soldiers also stumbled, cursed, fell out of line. These men walked like water finding its level.

He came up through the dry well behind the chandler's into a grey morning sky, and stopped, and stared, because the sky was the same color as everything beneath it.

No stars last night. No stars, and now a sun that gave light without giving warmth, hanging behind cloud like a coin seen through milk. The whole city seemed dimmed, colors leached to the memory of themselves. Lena had spent his life learning to read subtle things — the bitterness in a broth that meant hemlock, the sweetness that meant something worse — and every instinct he had was reading the light now and returning the same verdict: *something has been taken out of the world, and nothing has been put back.*

He pulled his hood low, crossed the chandler's yard, and slipped into the warren of streets beyond the merchant quarter where the city forgot to watch itself.

That was where the wardens found him.

Not all of them. Two. They came around a corner ahead of him with the unhurried certainty of men who already knew where he was going, and Lena froze in the shadow of a dye-house awning and looked at their faces and felt his stomach turn to ice water.

Their eyes reflected nothing.

He'd seen animals' eyes flash green in torchlight, seen drunkards' eyes shine, seen the king's eyes — the false king's — gleam wet and empty in the candlelit supper chamber. But these two stood in open grey daylight and their eyes drank the light and gave back starless black, two coins of absolute dark set in faces that were otherwise ordinary, weathered, human. Warden's uniforms. Warden's boots. One of them had a scar Lena almost recognized from the gate guard rotations. Everything human except the eyes.

"Subject," the nearer one said. Not a question. Not even quite a word — more the shape of a word, spoken by a mouth remembering speech from the outside. "Present yourself for renewal."

Lena did the only thing his life had trained him for. He reached for the nearest weapon he hadn't planned to use.

The dye-house vat sat steaming at the alley's edge, madder-root and alum and a dozen fixatives Lena couldn't identify but could smell — and smell was enough, because underneath the plant-stink ran a thin mineral thread he knew intimately. Copper salts. Not lethal, not to a healthy man. But in the eyes, in the lungs, in the soft wet places — it burned like a personal insult. He tipped the vat with his shoulder, all nine years of kitchen muscle behind it, and walked into the screaming crimson flood as it sheeted across the cobblestones toward the wardens, and turned, and ran while they clawed at faces that could not blink.

He didn't look back to see whether it slowed them. He suspected nothing slowed them. But their footsteps lost their rhythm for eleven heartbeats, and eleven heartbeats was enough to reach the well, and the well was enough to reach the wall, and the wall —

The wall was the problem. Thirty feet of dressed granite, patrolled, topped with iron spikes, and beyond it the pine-smell of Thornfell that Master Corvin had promised him a decade ago. Lena crouched in the shadow of a tanner's shed with blood singing in his ears and counted his options and found he had exactly one coin left in his purse and no idea what it might buy.

Then he heard the cart.

* *

It came groaning out of a hidden gate in the wall's base — a smuggler's gate, older than the patrols, known to maybe forty living people — drawn by two shaggy ponies and piled high with hides, dyestuffs, and people who did not want to be counted. A caravan of outcasts: a debt-bonded laundress with her two boys tucked among the bales, an old soldier missing an arm, a girl with a lute and no shoes, a fat, quick-eyed man in a fur-collared coat who was clearly the one being paid to make this crossing possible. The gate ground shut behind them, and the cart rattled down the forest road toward the border hills, and Lena stepped out of the treeline directly into its path with his hand raised and his last copper coin shining between his fingers.

The fat man reined in and looked down at him for a long moment.

"Well," the fat man said. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a man the city's done chewing." The fat man spat over the wheel with great precision. "Name's Ferro. I move goods south through Thornfell. Goods," he added, with a glance at the laundress's boys, "being a generous term. Passage costs a silver."

"I have a copper."

"Passage costs a silver," Ferro repeated, without malice, the way a mountain repeats its own height.

Lena looked at the coin in his palm. His last. In nine years of service he had saved almost nothing — tasters were paid in room and board and the peculiar honor of being trusted to die correctly — and this copper was meant to buy bread in whatever town lay far enough away to be safe. He looked at the cart, at the frightened faces pretending not to watch him, at the wall receding behind them with its black-eyed walkers striding along the parapet like punctuation marks.

Then he opened the satchel, tore off a corner of candied fig, and ate it.

Hellebore bloomed across his tongue — bitter, numbing, familiar as an old argument. Around him the outcasts flinched; the laundress gasped; one of the boys made a small horrified sound. Lena chewed, swallowed, and stood there letting the poison find nothing in him to kill, the way it had found nothing in the king, and hated himself for knowing exactly how that worked.

"I'm the king's taster," he said quietly. "I can tell you what's poisoned in your cargo, your water, your next three meals. I can taste anything in Thornfell and live. That's worth more than a silver, and we both know it."

Ferro's quick eyes went from the satchel's wax seal to Lena's face and back, and something behind them recalculated. Smugglers, Lena had heard Bram say, are honest about exactly one thing: value.

"A taster." Ferro turned the word over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "Running *from* the palace. With the palace's own supper on your back." He grinned suddenly, gold tooth flashing. "Oh, that's a story. Stories cost extra. Ride."

Lena climbed up beside him, and the cart lurched forward into the pines, and the city fell away behind them like a bad dream losing its grip.

They traveled south through the rest of the day and into a dusk that never quite became night, because there were no stars to become night *with*. The forest road narrowed and climbed. Thornfell rose around them — old growth, black-trunked, smelling of resin and rain — and with every mile the stones underfoot gave up the last of the smoke-smell and the air grew clean enough to hurt. The laundress shared dried apples with her boys. The one-armed soldier whittled. Nobody spoke above a murmur, and everyone watched the road behind.

Lena sat with the satchel in his lap and let the grief finish its work.

It had come into the walls with him that morning as a raw, shapeless wound, but grief is a patient alchemist, given hours on a road. By the time the first ridge swallowed the last view of Zephyrine's towers, it had begun to change state — cooling, clarifying, settling into something with edges. He tested those edges carefully, the way he tested any new substance, and found they held.

He loved the palace. The palace had fed him to a monster's table and called it honor. Bram was either dead or oath-bound or worse, and the kitchens that raised him were stirring pots for a thing that wasn't alive. Every person in that city — the laundresses and lamplighters, the guards on the wall whose eyes might already be going dark, the children who would be asked to sign their names in candlelit rows — every one of them was inside the fist, and the fist was closing, and no decree or army was coming to pry it open.

But he had the supper. He had the seal. He had a tongue that could taste truth in anything, and nine years of surviving every poison the crown could brew, and a road through Thornfell that no king's map had ever bothered to draw.

Resolve, Lena discovered, tastes like hellebore. Bitter at first. Then it simply becomes the flavor of staying alive.

"Ferro," he said, as the cart crested the ridge and the border hills opened below them, dark and trackless and free. "The people you move goods for. The fences, the forgers, the ones the crown ruined. Do any of them know the old archives? The flooded ones, under Thornfell?"

The fat man glanced sideways. "Might. Why?"

"Because I need a mapmaker," Lena said, "and I'm told there's one hiding somewhere on this road who owes the crown a very large bill."

Ferro laughed, a big rolling laugh that startled the ponies, and shook the reins, and the cart rolled down into the dark toward the inn at the crossroads — where, though neither of them could know it yet, a disgraced royal cartographer was drinking alone, and recognizing seals, and about to change everything.
