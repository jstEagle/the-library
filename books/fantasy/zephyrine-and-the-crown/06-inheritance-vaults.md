## 6. Inheritance Vaults

The water in the archive had not moved in a hundred years.

Sera Vane stood at the top of the spiral stair with her lamp held high and watched it breathe anyway—a slow, glassy swell that rose against the stone and sank again, as if the flooded vault below were a sleeping animal taking its ease. The stair descended into black water and did not emerge on the other side. That was the first lesson of Thornfell's deep archive: you could not see where you were going. You could only know.

She knew. She had spent three years of her disgrace memorizing every survey fragment, every drowned chart, every half-burned folio that mentioned the vaults beneath the mountain—because when the Crown College of Cartographers struck her name from their rolls for the crime of drawing a border too honestly, they could take her commission, her rooms, her stipend, and her good name. They could not take what she had already copied into memory.

"You're certain it's shallow enough to walk?" Lena Lockridge asked behind her. He had his satchel—the stolen satchel, the king's seal still unbroken on its buckle—slung across his chest like a bandolier, and he looked at the black water the way a man looks at a rival: professionally, assessing its poisons.

"Shallow enough," Sera said. "The old builders graded the floor to slope toward the cisterns. It rises to here—" she touched her collarbone "—at the deepest gallery. The shelves are above the flood line. They built the archive to survive drowning."

"Why?" Ivo asked.

He had insisted on coming, the deserter warden, though his hands hadn't stopped shaking since Ashmarket, since he'd told them what he'd seen peel off the king like a coat. He stood now with his back to the stair wall, positioned—as wardens always were—with a sightline to both exits. Some habits outlive the institutions that carve them.

"Because whoever built this place," Sera said, "knew that someday someone would need to read what's down there after everything else went under."

She lifted the hem of her coat, tucked the lamp into the wire cage she'd rigged for exactly this purpose, and stepped down into the water.

It was cold enough to ache and clean enough to drink, snowmelt from some vein of the mountain they would never see. The steps were broad and shallow-risen, made for processions rather than flight, and each one was carved with script that vanished beneath her boots as the water swallowed it. She heard Lena follow, then Ivo, then the small sounds of two people deciding not to ask questions until they absolutely had to.

The water rose past her knees. Past her thighs. Her lamp threw a trembling coin of light across the ceiling far overhead, where painted constellations had flaked into ghosts—and she heard Lena stop breathing for a moment when he saw them.

"Stars," he said quietly.

"The founders' sky," Sera said. "Painted four hundred years ago. Every constellation that hangs over Zephyrine." She paused, one hand on the slick rail. "Every one except the ones that have gone missing this month."

They walked down into the drowned dark, and the painted stars watched them go.

* * *

The main gallery announced itself by smell before sight: paper and mineral, wet stone and old ink, the particular sweetness of vellum that has been underwater and refused to rot because the water was too cold and too pure to let anything live in it. The gallery itself was a long barrel-vaulted hall, and the flood had stopped precisely at the level of the reading tables—stone slabs rising from the water like the backs of grazing animals, their surfaces dry, their legs submerged. Above them, tier upon tier of niches climbed into darkness, each niche sealed behind a pane of cloudy crystal, each pane furred with frost from the inside.

Lena waded to the nearest table and put his palm flat on it, as if greeting it. Sera found herself watching him do it. There was something in the gesture—not reverence, exactly. Recognition. A man who had spent nine years in kitchens greeting a work surface.

"How do you know all this?" he asked. "Truly. Not the innkeeper version."

So she told him, standing hip-deep in the founders' water with her lamp between them.

"I was eleven when I was apprenticed to the Royal Survey. Do you know what a cartographer actually does? Everyone thinks we draw coastlines. We don't. We draw *claims*. Every line on a crown map is an argument about who owns what, and the crown wins every argument it draws. My whole trade is the monarchy's handwriting." She turned, walking the table's length, water dragging at her skirts. "Third year of my commission, I was assigned to re-survey the Thornfell grants. Land titles. Boring work, deadly important—wardens collect taxes off those lines. And in the grant records, over and over, in different centuries, in different hands, I kept finding the same marginal note. Same phrase, copied like a prayer no one understood anymore."

"What phrase?"

"*By the original seal, in the original hall.*" She said it the way it had been written, formal and dead, and then said it again the way she had come to understand it. "Not a motto. An address. Everything about Zephyrine's succession—the laws, the wards, the oaths, the whole architecture of obedience—refers back to a place. A specific room under a specific mountain. This room."

Ivo had climbed onto one of the stone tables and sat there like a gargoyle, boots dangling above the waterline, refusing to be caught in the flood twice in one lifetime. "Wards are fed," he said flatly. "That's what the oath-keepers say. Fed with loyalty. I always took it for liturgy."

"It's accounting," Sera said. "And I can prove it, or I could prove it once we open the right niches." She pointed up into the dark tiers. "Second gallery, northern face. The founder's folios. But first—" she waded to the end of the hall, where a stone lectern rose alone from the flood like a pulpit, "—first you should see the contract."

* * *

The lectern held a book the size of a door.

It lay closed, bound in something that was neither leather nor metal but had the grain of one and the weight of the other, and the water had never touched it. Frost feathered across its cover in patterns that were not random—Sera had studied them for years in fragments and copies, and she knew them for what they were: clauses. The entire founding charter of Zephyrine, rendered in frost-script, legible only in miniature, illegible in full, so that no one could ever read the whole of what they lived under.

"Open it," Lena said.

"No," Sera said, and enjoyed, briefly, the small shock on both their faces. She was tired of being the woman who knew things and said them carefully. "You don't open it. No one opens it. Look at the binding." She traced the spine without touching. "There's no clasp because there's no lock. It isn't closed to keep people out. It's closed because it's *holding something shut*. The book doesn't contain the contract, Lockridge. The contract is contained the way a wound is contained—by skin."

Lena's face did something complicated. Nine years of tasting other people's food gave a man a certain fluency in the language of things that looked fine and weren't.

"So read me the parts you've copied," he said.

So she did. She recited from memory, standing in the founders' water, her voice carrying strangely under the vaulted dark:

"The first clause. In exchange for the mountain's keeping and the lowlands' peace, Aldric Zephyr, called First of That Name, binds himself and his heirs to the land, season upon season, until the debt of crowning be paid in full." She let the archaic rhythm sit in the air. "That's not a coronation oath. That's a promissory note. The founder didn't win the kingdom—he borrowed it. From the land itself, if the old words mean what I think they mean. And the collateral—"

"Is the bloodline," Lena said softly.

"Is the bloodline." She nodded. "Every heir renews the note. Every coronation is a refinancing. The wards on the borders, the oaths of the court, the loyalty that keeps a kingdom knitted together—all of it runs on that original signature, drawn down century by century like water from a well. And here is the part the College burned cartographers over." She turned to face him fully, lamp-high, and saw Ivo lean forward on his table in the periphery. "A contract can be discharged. Paid off. Or—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the word she had carried alone for six years, "—*unwritten*. Returned to blank. But only in the hall where it was signed. Only by the hand that holds the founding claim. And only—"

She stopped.

"Only," Lena prompted.

"Only with royal blood, freely given." Sera heard how it sounded in the flooded dark, how it rang against the frost-furred book like a bell with a price tag hanging off it. "The succession secret, Lockridge, the thing under everything: the crown cannot be broken from outside. No assassin, no rebellion, no usurper can void it, because none of them hold the claim. To unwrite a monarch—to dissolve the contract that feeds your starless thing in the palace—you need someone of the founder's own line to stand in this hall and consent to end it. Willingly. The vault reads intent the way a taster reads poison. Coerced blood sours the whole page."

Silence, except for the water breathing against the stone.

"That's why the wards fell," Ivo said at last, slowly, working it through the way he must have worked through the shadow peeling off the king, night after sleepless night. "Someone fed them. Someone inside paid them with the king's oath—but if the oath runs off this contract, then whatever took the king didn't break in. It *foreclosed*. It collected on the debt."

"And couldn't finish collecting," Sera said, "because the collection requires the founder's line, freely consenting, in this hall. So it wears the king's face and unwinds the kingdom decree by decree, erasing law after law, trying to return everything to blank the slow way—because it can't get to the fast way." She gestured around the drowned gallery, at the niches of preserved truth, at the great closed book. "This is the fast way. And it needs blood this vault will accept."

Lena had gone very still. It was a kitchen stillness; Sera had seen Bram's apprentices do it when a sauce broke and there was one chance to save it. "Then we find an heir," he said. "Some lost cousin. Some bywater branch. There are always lost cousins."

"There are," Sera agreed. "The College kept genealogies. I copied the relevant ones before they struck my name—partly out of spite, partly because I don't know how to leave information uncopied." She began to wade back along the tables, and her lamp threw their shadows huge across the painted stars. "But understand what you're asking, Lockridge. Whoever we find—if anyone of that line still lives—we won't be handing them a rescue mission. We'll be handing them an executioner's bargain. The contract ends only if they consent to end it, and consent means standing here, in the founders' water, giving up whatever claim protects them while a thing that eats oaths comes looking for exactly that claim. Hope with a bill attached. That's all this room sells."

"Most things worth having," Lena murmured, "come with arsenic on the side."

He smiled when he said it, the crooked professional smile of a man who had swallowed worse than despair for nine years, and Sera found—to her irritation—that she liked him better for it.

They reached the second gallery as the lamp oil ran low. The niches here bore seals older than any living house, and when Sera pressed her copied signet-ring of office—struck from her, yes, but wax remembers—against the frost-crusted pane of the founder's folio, the crystal cleared like breath fading from glass, and inside, dry as the day it was written, lay the genealogy of the line of Aldric Zephyr.

She lifted it out with both hands. Vellum, soft as cloth. Names in column after column, four hundred years of them, each generation's entry closing with the same formulaic line: *bound, renewed, bound, renewed.*

Until the third-to-last page. Where the columns thinned. Where a clerk's hand—one clerk, hurrying, the ink blotted—had drawn a single line through a mother's name, and beside it written, small and almost ashamed:

*Removed to service. Line continued, unrecorded. Let no register carry it.*

Below, a date. Below that, a household: the palace kitchens.

Sera read it twice. Then a third time, her lips moving, doing the arithmetic of decades, of service rosters she'd seen in her survey years, of a name that appeared in palace records with suspicious completeness for a servant—birth, apprenticeship, appointment—all of it tidy, all of it *placed*, like furniture arranged to hide a door.

"Lockridge," she said.

He was checking the oil in his lamp, half-listening. "Mm?"

"When were you born?"

He looked up. The lamplight moved on the black water between them, and somewhere far above, faint as memory, the painted stars hung in their ancient order—minus the ones the sky had lost.

"Ninth of the Hollow Month," he said slowly, hearing something change in the air. "Why?"

Sera looked down at the vellum in her hands, at the clerk's blotted, guilty line, at the words *let no register carry it*, and felt the whole weight of the flooded archive settle onto one impossible point.

"Because," she said, "I think I'm holding your family tree."
