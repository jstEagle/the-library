## 7. Company of the Unlawful

Ashmarket smelled the way Lena remembered it and nothing like he wanted it to: coal smoke and wet wool, frying onions gone rancid in old oil, the mineral tang of the canal where the tanners dumped their lye. A town that had never once been asked for its opinion of the crown and had stopped expecting to be. He'd forgotten how loud poverty was—the hawkers, the dogs, the argument on every corner—and how quiet it made you feel, walking through it with a dead king's supper in a satchel against your ribs.

Three days out of Thornfell and the satchel still hadn't left his side. He slept with it. He ate with one hand so the other could rest on it. The bread inside was stale now, the roast gone grey, the sugared plums weeping syrup through their cloth—but it was the only proof he had that the man on the throne of Zephyrine did not eat, did not live, did not blink. Proof needed witnesses. Witnesses needed persuading. And persuasion, in Ashmarket, cost exactly what Lena had left: nothing but himself.

He started at the bottom of the world, which in Ashmarket meant the Undertow—a cellar tavern beneath a chandler's shop, where the ceiling was low enough to brush your hair and the lamps burned whale oil smuggled up from ports that didn't officially exist. It was there, nine years ago, that a starving boy named Lena Lockridge had learned that a tongue could be a weapon and silence could be a trade. It was there he went looking for Doss.

* * *

Doss was playing solitaire with a deck missing four cards and winning anyway.

She hadn't changed—small, wiry, hair cropped to a stubble she shaved herself with a razor she used for other things too, if the stories were true. Her hands were what people remembered: long-fingered, scarred at every knuckle, always moving, always touching something they shouldn't. Right now they were moving cards. They did not stop when Lena sat down across from her.

"If you're here to sell me something," she said, "I'm broke."

"I'm not selling."

"Everyone's selling." She turned over a card, scowled at it, turned it back. "Look at you. Palace fat on your bones, palace boots on your feet, palace seal stitched into your collar where you tried to unpick it and gave up halfway." Her eyes came up. They were the color of cheap tin. "Taster Lockridge. Come down from his mountain to visit the worms."

"Doss."

"Don't 'Doss' me." The cards went down flat, all at once, like a small door slamming. "Four years I ran routes with you. Four years you never lied to me once, and then the guild's men come to the Undertow asking after my associate, and you're gone. Up the mountain. Wearing a seal."

"They offered me food," Lena said quietly. "And a bed that wasn't shared with three other people. You'd have done the same."

"I'd have done it faster." That almost earned a smile. Almost. "So why are you back? Because if you've brought palace trouble down into my cellar, I'll drown you in the canal myself and bill your guild for the rope."

Lena unbuckled the satchel. He laid the bread on the table first, then the cold roast, then the plums, arranging them like an offering, like evidence at a trial, because they were both. "Tell me what you see."

"A bad picnic."

"Taste it."

"I don't eat strange food, Lena, that's how I've lived to be thirty-one."

"Taste it," he said again, "and know that I have tasted everything else served to King Aldric for nine years, and that this—" he pressed his finger to the bread "—was set before him three nights ago, and he ate it, and he smiled, and he asked for more. Bread laced with widow's-bane. Roast stuffed with nightshade and arsenic both, which should taste of bitter almonds and rot. Plums dusted with salts of lead. I watched him eat all of it. Nothing happened to him, Doss. *Nothing happens to him.* Not poison. Not sleep. Not blinking."

The cellar noise seemed to recede, the way noise does when someone has said something the room needs to think about.

"That's a story," Doss said. But her hands had gone still on the table, and Lena knew Doss well enough to know that stillness was her version of fear.

"It's a story I need you to help me tell to people who can act on it. And I need your hands for locks, because the road I'm going down is full of them."

"Why me?"

Because you were fined twice for theft you didn't commit and once for theft you did. Because your brother died in the wardens' cells of a cough they wouldn't treat because treating prisoners costs money the crown would rather spend on gilding. Because you have been robbed by the law so thoroughly that outlawry is just bookkeeping to you. Lena said none of this. He said instead:

"Because you owe the crown nothing, and I can't afford anyone who does."

Doss looked at the bread a long moment. Then she tore off a corner, chewed it slowly, deliberately, holding his eyes the whole time—and spat it into her palm with a grimace. "Widow's-bane," she confirmed. "Bitter as sin. So either the king of Zephyrine has a death wish, or he isn't anybody anymore." She swept the cards into her sleeve, stood, and pulled her coat off the chairback. "I'll want double rates. And when this is done, Lockridge, you're buying me a real dinner, with nothing in it."

* * *

Mother Quill received them in the back of her shop, which appeared from the street to be a stationer's and sold, in fact, some paper, some ink, and an entire second economy folded into drawers and false spines.

The old woman sat behind her counter like a spider who had grown comfortable in her web—vast, silver-haired, fingers stained black to the second knuckle from decades of handling forged documents while the ink was still wet. Two lamps burned. Neither was for light; they were positioned, Lena knew, so that anyone entering cast two shadows, and Mother Quill had spent fifty years reading shadows.

"Lena Lockridge," she said, before he'd cleared the curtain. "You've lost weight and gained enemies. Sit. Both of you. Touch nothing."

"Mistress Quill—"

"You'll call me Mother or you'll get nothing, same as when you were fourteen and thought you could haggle." Her eyes, small and quick as a moneylender's, flicked to Doss, to the satchel, back. "Word reached me yesterday that a taster fled the capital by the Thornfell roads carrying the king's supper. Word travels fast when the wardens offer gold for it. Gold," she added, with delicate distaste, "which tells me the crown wants this quiet. Crowns only want things quiet when the thing itself is embarrassing."

"The crown wants it dead," Lena said. "Quiet is just how it prefers its deaths."

"Mm." She drew a ledger toward herself but didn't open it. It was an old habit, a gesture that meant *I am deciding your price.* "So. You want information, passage, contacts. You want me to spend reputation I've built for thirty years on a story about a king who doesn't eat. What do I get?"

"Truth first. Then decide." Lena laid out the food again, and told her—all of it. The stars going out like snuffed candles. The wards falling the same midnight. Bram's face when he'd whispered *he hasn't blinked in three days.* The king on the battlements, smiling at nothing. The private supper, the relish, the head turning too slowly as Lena fled through the servants' corridors, past portraits of monarchs whose painted eyes suddenly felt like company.

Mother Quill listened the way she read shadows: completely, without a single interruption.

When he finished, she was silent for the length of ten heartbeats. Then she opened the ledger, licked her thumb, and turned to a page near the back. "Three nights ago," she said, "a broker came to me. Crown coin, though he dressed it in merchant's clothes. He bought route information. Thornfell roads. Which smuggler paths a fugitive from the palace would likely take." She looked up. "He was buying your roads, Lena. Someone is selling you already."

Lena's mouth went dry. "Did you sell?"

"I told him I needed to consult my books." Her smile was thin as paper. "My books say the price wasn't high enough yet. My books also say that if the crown falls—or changes ownership without warning—every debt instrument stamped with the royal seal becomes worth whatever the new owner says it's worth. And if there is no new owner, they become worth nothing at all." She closed the ledger. "I am an old woman with a great many ledgers, boy. I cannot afford a kingdom that stops meaning anything. So I will give you what you ask—not for loyalty. Loyalty is for people who've never counted. I'll give it for self-interest, which keeps much better."

"That's the only currency I have," Lena said.

"Then we understand each other." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and began pulling documents from the false spines—maps, warrants, a warden's rotation schedule stamped CONFISCATED and worth more than the shop. "One more thing. If you're crossing Thornfell proper, you'll want someone who knows the wardens from the inside. Their posts, their habits, their blind spots." Her ink-stained finger tapped the rotation schedule. "There's such a man drinking himself stupid at the Salt Door. Deserter. Ran the night the stars went out. The others want him flogged for it. I want him found first."

"What did he see?" Doss asked.

"He hasn't said," Mother Quill replied. "That's what frightens everyone. Men who desert over nothing brag about it. Men who desert over something go quiet." She fixed Lena with her small bright eyes. "His name is Ivo Renner. Be gentle with him, or don't bother going at all."

* * *

The Salt Door was less a tavern than a place where alcohol was stored near people. It sat at the canal's edge, and the canal smell got into everything—the ale, the walls, the silences between conversations. Lena found Ivo Renner at the end of the bar, the seat furthest from the door and nearest the wall, which told Lena the man still thought like a soldier even if he'd stopped being one.

He was big—not heavy the way Mother Quill was heavy, but built, with the deep-chested solidity of someone who'd carried armor for a living. He wore it badly now. His uniform was gone, replaced by fisherman's oilskin, but his posture hadn't gotten the message: spine straight, shoulders squared, hands resting on the bar in front of him like a man at attention who had forgotten how to do anything else. His knuckles were split. His eyes were the problem.

They were red-rimmed and unfocused, the eyes of a man who had stopped sleeping properly, and when the barmaid leaned close to refill his cup he flinched—flinched, a warden, a trained killer flinching from a woman with a jug—and didn't drink after.

Lena took the stool beside him and ordered nothing. For a long time neither of them spoke. The canal lapped at the pilings below the floor.

"You're staring at me," Ivo said finally. His voice was low and rough, gravel rolled in wool. "Everyone stares. Trying to figure if I'm the coward who ran or the liar who says he saw something. You want to know which?"

"No," Lena said. "I want to tell you what I saw, and see if it matches."

Ivo turned. Up close his face was worse—hollowed, unshaven, a week of terror worn openly. "Nobody wants to hear it."

"I do. I need to." And Lena told him. The stars, the wards, the supper, the thing that turned its head like a door on rusted hinges. He kept his voice level, the way he'd been trained to describe poisons to physicians—precisely, without drama, because drama obscured symptoms. When he finished, the tavern noise went on around them, oblivious, blessedly ordinary.

Ivo was crying. Not sobbing—just leaking, tears running down into his beard while his face stayed perfectly still, a body doing one thing while the man inside did another.

"Night the stars died," he said, "I was posted on the king's corridor. Inner keep. Night watch, third bell." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, mechanically, like clearing rain from glass. "I heard the oath ceremony let out late. Whole court, renewing their oaths of loyalty—everyone called it routine, the chamberlain's office does it quarterly, signatures and kneeling and a few words over the crown. But it ran past midnight, and the hall went quiet, and I saw him come down the corridor to his chambers. The king." His jaw worked. "Except he stopped under the lamp outside his door. And he—"

He stopped. His hands flattened on the bar.

"Take your time," Lena said.

"There's no time to take. That's the whole point." Ivo laughed, one broken note. "He reached back over his shoulder, and he *took hold of his own shadow*, the way you'd take hold of a coat. And he peeled it off. Slowly. Like it hurt. Like he was tearing a bandage off a wound that went all the way down. And what was standing there when it came away—" His voice dropped to almost nothing. "It was wearing him. Same face. Same hands. But the shadow it left on the floor, it didn't match. It was *crown-shaped*. Just the crown, lying flat in the lamplight where his shadow should have been. And it stepped over it, into the bedchamber, and closed the door, and I stood there until dawn pretending I'd seen nothing, because I'm a warden and wardens don't see things, and then the stars went out, and then I ran."

Silence. The canal lapped.

"The oaths," Lena said quietly. "They renew them nightly now. Every signature—"

"—goes somewhere," Ivo finished. "Feeds something. I signed mine eleven times a year for twelve years. Whatever it built with all that, I helped build it." He looked at his own hands as if they belonged to the monster. "I deserted because I couldn't stand guard over it one more night. Not because I'm brave. Because I'm *weak*, and I know the difference."

"I'm not asking you to be brave." Lena turned on his stool to face him squarely. "I'm asking you to be useful. I need a man who knows warden rotations, signals, chokepoints on the Thornfell pass. I need you to walk back toward the thing you ran from."

"Why would I?"

"Because you flinched when the barmaid touched you, and you haven't drunk a drop since you sat down, and a man that frightened doesn't want safety. He wants absolution." Lena held his gaze. "I can't give you that. Nobody can. But there's a road east, and at the end of it there may be a way to unmake the thing wearing the king, and every step you walk on that road is one more than cowardice required. That's all absolution ever is, I think. Steps you didn't have to take."

Ivo stared at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, he pushed his untouched cup away, stood, and settled his shoulders the way a man settles armor.

"Double rates," Doss said from the next stool, without looking up. "Everyone's getting double rates."

"Triple," Ivo said. "I'm told I'm worth more alive than the others."

* * *

They left Ashmarket before dawn, four of them on the salt-road heading east: a taster carrying a dead man's dinner, a lockbreaker with cards up her sleeve, a fence's maps folded into her coat, and a deserter who walked at the rear and checked behind them every forty paces without seeming to.

Lena felt the strangeness of it, the wrongness and the rightness together. Nine years in the palace he had believed in the machinery of loyalty—the oaths, the rituals, the great chain of duty that ran from kitchen boy to crown. He had loved that chain, even knowing it would hang him someday. This was not that. Nobody here had sworn anything. Nobody here believed in anything except each other's grievances, and somehow that held tighter than faith ever had. A family assembled from injuries, fitted together along the broken edges.

They camped the first night in a quarry pit off the salt-road, no fire, cold rations. Doss slept with one eye open. Ivo took first watch without being asked and sat facing east, toward the starless dark where the capital glowed faintly on the horizon like an ember that refused to die.

Lena lay awake under a sky with nothing in it, the satchel under his head, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. Mother Quill's maps put the inheritance vaults six days' hard travel beyond the pass. The wardens were behind them. The usurper's brokers were ahead of them, buying roads. And somewhere between here and there was whatever Ivo had seen in the king's corridor, following them like a shadow that had learned to travel without its owner.

Near midnight, Ivo's hand closed on Lena's shoulder—gentle, but a soldier's grip, the kind that woke you whether you wanted waking or not.

"You need to see this," Ivo whispered.

Lena followed him to the lip of the quarry. On the far ridge, small in the distance but unmistakable in the light of no moon at all, a line of figures moved along the crest. Wardens' formation, spaced for pursuit. Moving without torches. And as Lena watched, the lead figure stumbled on the scree, caught itself, and kept pace without breaking stride—an animal rhythm, tireless, mechanical, wrong.

"They don't rest," Ivo said quietly beside him. "I've been watching for two hours. They don't rest, and they don't use lights, and they're not losing us because they never needed to find us." He turned to Lena, and his eyes reflected the fireless dark. "They're herding us, Lockridge. Somebody wants us going exactly where we're going."

Lena looked east, toward the vaults, toward the answer waiting at the end of the road like bait on a hook.

"Then we'd better find out," he said, "who's holding the line."
