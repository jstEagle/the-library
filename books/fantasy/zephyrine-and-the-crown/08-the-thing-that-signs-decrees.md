## 8. The Thing That Signs Decrees

I will tell it plainly, because plainness is all I have left, and because I have learned in these last weeks that the truth is treated like contraband—smuggled in whispers, traded in dark corners, worth killing for. So: plainly. My name is Ivo Renner. I was a warden of the second watch for eleven years. I deserted my post on the night the stars went out. And I saw what I saw.

I have said those words to three people now. To Doss, who checked my hands for wardens' calluses and then believed me anyway. To Mother Quill, who priced my story at a cup of plum wine and a long look. And to Lena Lockridge, who listened the way a man listens to his own death warrant being read, and nodded, and asked me to say it again slower.

So I will write it here, slower. Sera gave me paper. She said testimony wants a fixed form, that a thing written down is harder to starve than a thing spoken. She is a cartographer; she believes in fixing things to surfaces. I am a warden; I only ever believed in standing between.

This is what stood on the other side of the king's door.

* * *

The royal bedchamber of Aldermere Palace sits at the top of the Ivory Stair, behind a door of black oak banded with silver—silver, everyone said, for beauty. Wardens of the second watch learn otherwise in their first month. The silver is old work, older than the dynasty that supposedly commissioned it, and it is worked into shapes no jeweler would choose: knots without ends, letters in no living alphabet. It is not decoration. It is a lock of the other kind, the kind that works on what is inside the room rather than outside it.

We were never told this directly. Wardens are never told anything directly. But the old sergeant, Hesk, who trained me, had a habit of touching the doorframe with two fingers every time we took the post, quick as a superstition, and once—only once, when he was deep in his cups—he told me why the night rotation changed at moonrise instead of midnight.

"Because the king sleeps badly when the moon is down," he said. "And because you don't want to be the one standing there if he wakes before it's up."

I asked what happened if the king woke. Hesk looked at me for a long moment with his ruined eye—the one he'd lost to a smuggler's blade in Thornfell, back when Thornfell was still just roads—and said, "Nothing has happened yet. Keep it that way."

Eleven years. Nothing happened for eleven years. I counted the king's breathing through the door the way shepherds count sheep, four thousand nights of the same slow rhythm, in and out, in and out, the most boring sound in the kingdom, and I came to love that boredom the way soldiers come to love bad food and worse weather—as proof that you were still somewhere ordinary, where the rules held.

On the ninety-first night of autumn, the rules stopped holding. I want to be exact about the date because exactness is all testimony has. The ninety-first night of autumn. There was frost on the battlements and no wind, which the old-timers call a listening night, though I never knew what they thought it listened to.

* * *

The first wrongness was small, the way the worst things always are. The king's breathing stopped.

Not paused. Stopped. I stood at my post with my ear near the silver-banded door and heard nothing where there had always been something, and my body knew before my mind did—I felt my hackles lift like a dog's, felt cold move up my spine in a slow wave, because eleven years of counting breaths had taught me the shape of their absence.

I did not open the door. Understand that. Whatever else I am guilty of, whatever cowardice they can charge me with for running afterward, I did not open the king's door. It opened itself.

There was no sound of latch or hinge. There was a sound I still cannot name—a soft, wet giving-way, like ice releasing a drowned thing from a river—and the door swung inward six inches, and warm air came out, and it smelled wrong. Not foul. Wrong the way a note played slightly flat is wrong. The smell of the king's chambers had been lavender and lamp oil for eleven years. This was the smell of a cellar after rain, of earth where earth should not be, of something underneath.

I looked. God help me, I looked, because looking is the job, watching is the whole of the work, and I had watched that room through every hour of eleven years.

The bed was empty. The coverlet lay thrown back on one side as if someone had risen quickly—or as if something had been pulled out from under it. And across the room, silhouetted against the tall window and the moonlight, stood King Aldric of Zephyrine, upright, barefoot, facing away from me.

Except it was not the king. Not yet. Not quite. I want to describe this accurately and I don't know if language will do it, but I will try, because Sera says try, because accuracy is the only candle we have.

The figure by the window was the king in outline but too still within the outline, the way a garment is still on its hook. And then the figure moved—and the movement was this: it reached up, both hands, and gripped its own shadow where the shadow fell across the moonlit floor, and it *pulled*.

Like a man removing a coat. Like a woman drawing off a wet cloak after rain. The shadow came away from the floor and came up the body like hemmed darkness rising, and where it passed, the body inside changed—thinned, loosened, emptied. I saw the king's face fold inward around the eyes. I saw his shoulders collapse like emptying sacks. The shadow peeled off him the way bark peels from a dead tree, one slow curling sheet of it, and beneath the shadow there was nothing wearing the king's shape, and above the shadow, gathering it into itself, folding it over its arm like a servant receiving linen—

Something stood there in the king's skin, and it fit badly, and it smiled.

I have been asked, since, what it looked like. People want a face for horror; it makes the horror manageable. But it looked like the king. That is the whole unbearable truth of it. It looked exactly like King Aldric—same grey at the temples, same broken knuckle on the right hand from the hunting accident nine years ago, same tired kindness around the mouth. Every detail correct. All of them together, wrong. The way a portrait can be perfect and still not breathe.

It turned toward the door—to me—and it did not move the way bodies move. It arrived at the new position without traveling through the ones between. One moment it faced the window; the next it faced me, and the six-inch gap in the door might as well have been six miles and six inches at once.

It put one finger to its lips.

Then it drew the door closed, gently, the latch clicking home like a kind word, and I heard the bedclothes rustle, and in a moment the breathing resumed—in and out, in and out, the most boring sound in the kingdom—but now I knew it was a recitation. A memorized thing. A lullaby the thing sang to the palace, and to me.

I stood my post until dawn. I do not know why. Fear, probably, wearing duty's uniform—it had worn mine often enough. When the day watch relieved me, Sergeant Hesk looked at my face and went grey, and pulled me into the stairwell, and I told him everything, every word I have written here, and he listened with his ruined eye fixed on some point past my shoulder as if the past itself were coming up the stairs.

"I saw one before," he said finally. "Thirty-one years ago. In the north garrison, under old King Marren. A chamberlain. He signed requisitions for months, and every signature fed it, and by spring half the north march belonged to it on paper." He rubbed his mouth. "You know how they're made?"

I didn't.

"Crowns," Hesk said. "Not the metal. The office. All that kneeling, all those oaths, year after year, generation after generation—oaths are a substance, boy, they weigh something, they go somewhere. They pool under the throne like rain under a roof. And sometimes—" he shrugged, helpless, a soldier trying to explain weather to a fish— "sometimes something drinks."

He made me swear silence that night, and I kept it, because he told me what happened to the garrison that spoke. And then, four days later, Hesk walked into the White Pool with his armor on and did not come out, and I understood that my silence had stopped protecting anyone, including him.

That was eight days before the stars went out. I counted. Exactness is all testimony has.

* * *

Now I must speak of the oaths, because this is the part that matters, the part I would shout from the capital's towers if towers carried sound further than knives.

Every soul sworn to the crown of Zephyrine renews the Oath of Fealty once each year, on the anniversary of their swearing—wardens, ministers, guildmasters, judges. It is a small ceremony. You kneel in the Oath Hall, you sign your name in the great ledger, you speak the words, a priest of the Concord witnesses it, done. I have knelt twenty-two times in my life. It means about as much to most people as paying a toll. Words, ink, a stiff knee. You forget it by supper.

After the night of the ninety-first day of autumn, the renewals stopped being annual.

They began nightly.

The summons came to the barracks on a Tuesday: all household wardens to the Oath Hall, evening dress, full kit. We assumed a state occasion. What we found was the court of Zephyrine arranged in candlelit rows like an orchard, kneeling in batches of twelve before the throne, and the king upon it—not the king, the thing in the king's skin, wearing the crown slightly askew in a way Aldric never had, Aldric who adjusted it constantly like a man aware of the weight—and the great ledger open on its stand, and the pages turning, turning, turning.

Here is what I saw, and I ask you to believe that a warden's eyes are trained instruments, that noticing is my craft the way tasting is a taster's:

Each time a pen touched the ledger, the crown brightened.

Not reflected light. There were a hundred candles in that hall and the gold caught all of them and more besides; it shone the way coals shine, from within, a low hungry red under the yellow. And with each signature the kneeling signer's face changed. Slackened. A contentment moved over them like oil over water, total and terrible, and they rose from their knees smiling, smiling, and returned to their seats, and sat with their hands folded, waiting their turn to be happy again tomorrow.

Chancellor Merev signed. I watched his lips move through the words—he had spoken those words to the true king with fire in them, twelve years ago, at his own investiture; everyone remembered it, it had been famous—and now he recited them like a child reciting a lesson, flat and pleased, and when he rose, he rose lighter, as if something had been taken from him and he was grateful.

Lady Corane signed. General Ashk signed. The Master of Coin signed. The Dean of the Concord signed, which broke something in me quietly, because if the church could not tell, then the church had never been looking.

Row by row. Night after night. I stood my post along the hall wall in the rotation, and I watched the whole court of Zephyrine kneel its way into contentment, one signature at a time, feeding the thing above them sip by sip, and no one noticed except the servants.

That is the detail I cling to, the splinter of hope I carry: the kitchen folk noticed. The cooks and scullions and tasters, the people whose hands touched actual matter every day—dough, blood, salt, heat—they flinched from the king. They crossed themselves with the old pre-Concord signs, thumb to breastbone, that the priesthood banned two centuries ago. A scullery girl fainted clean away when the king passed her in the corridor, and the thing wearing his face stopped and looked down at her for a long moment, and then continued on, and I saw its head turn—too slowly, far too slowly, like a weathervane deciding—tracking her as she was carried off.

Those who work with bread know what bread should smell like. Those who only handle decrees never learn what a decree should smell like. And the decrees had begun to stink, if you had the nose for it: edicts dissolving old protections, striking laws from the books, unwriting this charter and that guarantee, each one sealed with the signet and each one, I understand now, another clause fed back into the blank contract sleeping in the vaults beneath Thornfell. The kingdom was being eaten from the paperwork outward.

I could not stop it. I want that in the record without excuse. I stood at walls my whole life and on the night it mattered, I ran. I told myself it was to bear witness, and maybe that is even true, but witness is what cowards call survival when they've had time to embroider it. I deserted on the night the stars went out. I climbed the wall at the Thornfell gate while every bell in the capital rang and the darkness above ate the last constellation, and I dropped into the smuggler roads beyond, and I did not look back at the palace, because I knew—if I looked back, I would see it standing on the battlements, smiling at the empty sky it had made.

* * *

Which brings me to the last thing I must set down, the reason Sera pressed this paper into my hands and said, *write it so it survives you.*

The oaths are still being renewed. Nightly, still, in the Oath Hall—but not only there. On my way out of the capital I passed warden stations, tax houses, the guildhalls, and in every window: candles, kneeling figures, the soft recitation. The thing is not satisfied with the court. It is swallowing the country's word of honor town by town, oath by oath, and each swallowed vow makes it heavier, more real, harder to unseat—and makes the people lighter, emptier, easier.

But an oath, Hesk told me, is a substance. It weighs. It goes somewhere. Which means it can be *refused*—and refusal, freely given, is the one coin the thing cannot mint.

I did not understand what that meant until I met the company I now travel with. Until I watched Lena Lockridge taste a spoonful of stolen palace broth and spit it into the fire and say, very quietly, "It's not poisoned. That's what's wrong with it." Until I heard Sera Vane explain contracts older than the dynasty and Mother Quill name a price without shame and Doss laugh in the dark like a man picking a lock with his teeth.

They are all, every one of them, people the crown's oaths never managed to hold. Smugglers, debtors, deserters, disgraced women, a taster whose whole profession is licensed lying-by-mouthful. Unsworn. Unfed-on. The blank spaces in the ledger.

Tonight we make for the mountain. Tonight I finish this testimony, and Sera seals it, and if we do not survive the vaults, then whoever finds these pages will know at least this much: the king has been dead since the ninety-first night of autumn, the crown is drinking the country's loyalty one kneeling signature at a time, and the thing that signs the decrees once put its finger to its lips and hushed me like a mother hushing a child—which means it can be frightened, and whatever can be frightened can be fought.

There is one more thing. I said I would be exact, so.

As I went over the wall at Thornfell gate, in the last light before the stars died entirely, I heard the bells of the palace kitchen tower ringing the shift-change for the night cook. Ordinary bells. Ordinary work. And beneath them, thin and clear, a single voice singing a work song—the kind tasters sing over their mortars, the kind with a counting rhythm, one verse for each ingredient.

Someone in that kitchen was still working. Still handling real things. Still unsworn.

If there is any justice left un-eaten in this kingdom, that song and our road are headed for the same place.
