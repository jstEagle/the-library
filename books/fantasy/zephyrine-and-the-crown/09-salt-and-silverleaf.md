## 9. Salt and Silverleaf

The Marrow River did not deserve its gentle name.

Lena had crossed it twice in his smuggling years—once at the ford below Harrow's Mill, once farther north where the water ran white over broken slate and a man could lose his footing and be carried ten miles before anyone found what was left of him. The northern crossing was faster. It was also, in Doss's professional opinion, "a place where rivers go to eat people."

"We take the ford," Lena said.

"Of course we take the ford," Doss muttered. "Why would we take the bridge like sensible folk."

"Because the bridge has wardens on it." Ivo said it without looking up from the far bank, his grey eyes narrowed against the dusk. He'd been watching the crossing for an hour, motionless as one of the standing stones on the ridge behind them. "Six of them. Maybe eight. Standing too still. Not talking. Not stamping their feet against the cold, which they should be, because it is cold enough to crack teeth out here."

The company crouched among the reeds on the southern bank while the light drained out of the world. There were no stars to take its place. There never were, anymore—not since that first night, nine days gone, when the sky had gone black as a closed eye and stayed that way. Lena had stopped expecting them back. What he could not stop doing, every time he looked up, was flinching at the absence. A sky without stars felt like a face without eyes. You kept waiting for it to notice you.

Mother Quill sat on a fallen log with her hands folded over her cane, radiating the patience of a woman who had survived four kings and intended to survive whatever came next by the simple method of refusing to be impressed by any of it. Sera Vane knelt beside her pack, unrolling the oilskin that wrapped her maps even now, even here, because Sera protected paper the way other people protected children.

"The ford it is," Sera said. "Shallow water. Fast approach. If they're waiting for us there anyway—"

"They're not waiting for us," Ivo said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. He had that way of speaking that made you lean in whether you wanted to or not, a warden's habit, delivering truth like bad news to a family that already knew.

"I know how they stage a watch," he went on. "I did it for eleven years. You stagger posts. You set fires. You complain about your boots. Those six on the bridge aren't a watch, they're bait—they want us to see the bridge guarded so we go to the ford instead. And the ones at the ford will not be standing around getting cold." He finally turned his head. His eyes caught no light at all; Lena had noticed that days ago and told himself it was a trick of exhaustion, and had stopped telling himself that somewhere around the fourth day. "They'll be in the water."

* * *

They came at moonrise, when the only illumination left in the world was the small ring of their own firelight reflected off the current—and even the fire burned strangely now, its flames low and colorless, as though the darkness overhead leaned down to drink from every flame in the kingdom.

Lena saw them first, or rather he saw the water move wrong. A smooth stretch of shallows upstream of the ford shifted in a way water does not shift when fish pass beneath it—a long, coordinated ripple, six bodies deep, moving against the current.

"Wardens," he breathed. "In the river. Upstream."

Ivo was already turning. "Down!"

The crossbow bolts came out of the dark with a sound like tearing linen. They struck where the company had been standing half a heartbeat earlier, thudding into the reed bank, one of them pinning Doss's hat to the log Mother Quill had vacated. Then the water erupted and the wardens rose out of the Marrow like things birthed by it—six shapes in soaked grey cloaks, faces pale as drowned men, eyes reflecting nothing, absolutely nothing, two holes of starless black in each face.

Lena had seen hollow-eyed men before, in the palace corridors, in the oath-lines Sera described. Seeing them up close, wet and fast and coming at him with drawn steel, was different. It rearranged something in his chest. Fear stopped being abstract.

"Doss—the ropes!" he shouted, and then the world dissolved into noise and cold and violence.

Doss was already moving. He had spent the last hour rigging the crossing with lines and counterweights—"for convenience," he'd claimed, and nobody had believed him, because Doss never rigged anything for a single purpose—and now he threw himself at the anchor post, hauling, and the trip-line they'd strung taut across the ford's downstream edge snapped upward out of the current and took two wardens at the knees. They went down in the white water. They did not thrash. That was the worst part. They simply sank, upright, like cut marionettes, and did not surface.

Sera fought the way she drew maps: precisely, economically, with no wasted line. She had a boat-hook she handled like a surveying instrument, catching a warden's sword-arm mid-swing, pivoting, using the man's own momentum to fold him over the hook's iron lip and dump him gasping into the shallows. She stamped her heel down once, hard, on the wrist holding the sword, and Lena heard bone give. She didn't look at what she'd done. She was already scanning the banks for the next threat, cataloguing, the way she catalogued everything.

Mother Quill did not fight at all. Mother Quill stood on the high bank with her cane raised, and when a warden scrambled dripping up the stones toward her, she brought the cane down across his temple with a crack that echoed off the ridge, and then she stepped back and let him fall, and adjusted her shawl, and said, "Eleven years I paid protection to the Wardens' Guild. This is the thanks."

A blade found Lena's ribs. Not deep—the warden's grip slipped on his wet haft—but enough to lift him off his feet with pain, enough to slam him down into the shallows where the river filled his ears and mouth and the taste of slate and silt flooded everything. The warden loomed over him, raising the sword for the second stroke, and its face hung above him pale and empty as a plate, and Lena thought with strange clarity: *it isn't angry. None of this costs it anything.*

Then Ivo hit the warden from the side like a falling beam.

He did not use his sword. He used his shoulder, his elbows, his whole history of brawling in barracks yards, driving the thing backward into the current, and then his knife came up under the warden's jaw in one short, sickening arc. The body dropped. Ivo stood over it, chest heaving, water streaming off him, and looked down at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

"That's the third of mine I've killed since the stars died," he said, to no one. To the river. "Three."

Lena got an arm under himself and coughed the Marrow out of his lungs.

And that was when he smelled it.

* * *

It came to him faintly, under the iron reek of blood and the mineral stink of the water—a sweetness. Thin, green, almost pleasant, like crushed stems in springtime. Any other nose would have missed it entirely, buried as it was beneath river and rain and gore. But Lena Lockridge had spent nine years training his nose the way archers trained their draw, and there was no sweetness in the world he could not name.

He knew it. He knew it the way you know a voice heard once through a wall, years ago, in the middle of the night.

"Stop," he rasped. "Everybody stop. Don't touch the water where they fell. Don't touch them."

The company froze—Sera halfway to a fallen warden's pouch, Doss with his fingers hovering over a bolt to pull it free and salvage the head. Even Ivo stilled.

Lena waded to the nearest body. Up close the wardens were worse than they'd seemed in the fighting: their skin had the waxy slackness of candles left near a fire, and where the river had split the knuckles of one hand, no blood came out. Only a slow seep of something grey. But the smell was strongest at their collars, at the hoods soaked through, and he pressed his fingertips there and raised them to his face, and closed his eyes, and let nine years of swallowed poison speak to him in its native tongue.

"Bittercress extract," he said. "Cut with marsh distillate to keep it soluble. Salted with silverleaf ash to make it bind to skin and cloth." He opened his eyes. "Coated on their cloaks. Every cloak. It's not armor they're wearing—it's a delivery."

Sera went very still. "Delivery of what?"

"Palewater." He hadn't said the word aloud in years. It tasted like the guildhall cellar, like Bram's voice reciting the ledger of toxins the way priests recited psalms. "The palace calls it King's Mercy when it's written in the stores inventory. It doesn't kill quickly. It doesn't kill loudly. You breathe the vapor off wet cloth for an hour, maybe two, and three days later your lungs fill quietly while you sleep, and everyone says what a tragedy, so young, must have been his heart." He looked at the black-eyed dead man at his feet. "They weren't just sent to kill us. They were sent to poison the crossing. Poison the ford, poison the bank, poison anyone who comes through after us. Every smuggler, every refugee, every farmer who waters a goat in this river tomorrow."

"But how—" Doss began, and stopped, because he had followed the same chain of reasoning and arrived where Lena had already arrived, and his face had gone the color of the river foam.

"Silverleaf ash," Lena said. His own voice sounded far away. "Do you know where silverleaf grows? Nowhere wild. It can't survive outside a tended bed. There are exactly three gardens in Zephyrine that cultivate it, and two of them are ornamental. The third is behind the royal kitchens, walled, locked, under the Tasters' Guild seal." He held up his wet fingers. Nine years of memory clung to them like the smell itself. "Bittercress extract is a palace preparation. Marsh distillate is a palace preparation. I have tasted both. I helped catalogue both. This—" he gestured at the ruined bodies, the poisoned river, the starless dark pressing down on all of them, "—this was mixed in my kitchen. By someone with access to my storerooms. The thing wearing the king's face isn't commanding the army of Zephyrine. It *is* the army of Zephyrine. It's using the crown's own hands, the crown's own stores, the crown's own poisons. Everything I ever tasted for it, it's tasting back."

Nobody spoke. The river ran on, indifferent, carrying its invisible burden toward villages downstream full of people who trusted their king.

It was Mother Quill who broke the silence. She lowered herself onto a rock with a wince, laid her cane across her knees, and said, "Well. Then we'd best move fast, boy, and get to those vaults before it empties the whole pantry on the kingdom. Grief is a fine meal but a poor plan."

* * *

They made camp two miles south, in a hollow of birch and stone that Ivo chose with a warden's instinct for ground that could be defended and a deserter's instinct for ground that wouldn't be looked for. Doss rigged alarm-strings in concentric rings, muttering apologies to each one as he tied them, as if the strings might resent being asked to work. Sera spread her maps by the light of one shuttered lantern and began, in her precise cartographer's hand, to mark the crossing, the ambush, the direction the wardens had come from.

Lena did not sit down. He built a fire small enough to hide and then he built a second thing on top of it: a working. He laid out the tools of his old life on a flat stone—his mortar, his little brass scale, the vials he'd carried from the palace the night he fled, and the satchel of the king's untouched food, which had been riding against his spine like a guilty conscience for nine days.

"You're going to do something with all that," Sera said, settling beside him. It wasn't a question.

"The cloaks." He nodded back the way they'd come, though the crossing was dark and far now. "Silverleaf binds to cloth. Which means cloth holds the recipe. If I can strip a sample clean—if I can separate the carrier from the active—I can read the proportions. Every mixer has habits, Sera. Heavy hand with the salt, light with the leaf, a particular order of addition. Bram taught me that poisons have handwriting." His jaw tightened. "I want to see whose handwriting is on those cloaks."

"And if it's the palace's?"

"It will be the palace's. That's not the question." He worked as he talked, the motions coming back to his hands like a language never fully forgotten—measure, steep, decant, hold the liquid to the lantern and watch the color tell its story. Amber. Then amber shading toward green at the meniscus. "The question is *which* palace preparation. Because there are two bittercress extracts in the royal stores. The coarse one, for vermin control in the granaries. And the refined one, triple-filtered, which exists for exactly one purpose: it was made for me. For my training. So that a child of eleven could be fed death in teaspoon doses and live to be fed more." The green liquid trembled slightly. He realized it was his hands. "This is the refined. This came out of *my* cabinet, from the stock that was procured *for me*, which means whoever brewed it signed the requisition with my name or Bram's, or forged them, or—" He stopped. Breathed. "Or it means nothing, and grief is making me read handwriting in smoke."

"It means something," Sera said quietly. She put one hand flat on the stone beside his work, not touching the vials, just near them. An anchor. "You survived it. All of it. Every dose they gave you, you survived, and now you're the only person in this kingdom who could stand in a poisoned river and *name the vintage.*" Her mouth curved, thin and fierce. "They built their weapon out of the one thing they never counted as a weapon. You."

For a long moment Lena couldn't answer. The lantern hissed. Somewhere upslope, Doss swore softly at a knot and Ivo's low voice answered, and Mother Quill laughed at something—one short bark of a sound, there and gone. Strange, Lena thought, how a thing could assemble itself without your noticing. Three days ago these had been strangers bound by grievances and geography. Tonight they had fought back to back in a freezing river, and Sera was sitting with him in the dark guarding his work with her presence, and none of them had discussed it, because some things are decided in the marrow before the head is consulted.

"How long?" Sera asked, nodding at the vials.

"Hours, for a clean read. Days, if I want the full profile." He exhaled slowly. "But I don't need the full profile to know one thing already. Look." He tilted the sample toward her. In the green liquid, tiny grains had settled along the bottom of the vial in a faint bright line, like frost forming on glass. "See that? Silverleaf ash doesn't fully dissolve. It always leaves a residue line. And the residue *always* sits crooked unless someone adds salt first to level the solution." He tapped the vial. "There's salt in this mix. Coarse salt. Grey salt."

"So?"

"So the kitchens haven't stocked grey salt in four years. We switched to white sea crystal under the old king's physician. Grey rock salt is older. Cheaper. It's what we used when I was a boy." He looked up at her, and his voice came out lower than he intended. "This poison wasn't mixed recently, Sera. Or if it was, it was mixed by someone following recipes twenty years old. Someone who learned them a generation ago and never updated. Someone who has been standing in that kitchen the whole time, going through the motions, brewing from memory."

Sera stared at him. The lantern-light moved in her eyes, the only eyes in the world that still reflected anything.

"You think it's one of yours," she said. "One of the tasters."

"I think," Lena said, "that the thing in the crown eats oaths, and no one in Zephyrine swore deeper oaths than the guild that taught its children to dine on death and call it duty. I think it didn't corrupt my kitchen from outside." He capped the vial with hands that had gone steady again—steady the way they went steady over a poisoned spoon, when steadiness was the only thing between a king and the grave. "I think it grew there. And until I know which pair of hands stirred this batch, I don't trust anyone who ever wore a guild apron—including the memory of my own teacher."

Across the camp, Mother Quill had risen and was looking east, downriver, toward the dark lands where they were headed, and her face in the failing firelight was unreadable as a ledger with the sums torn out.

"Lena," she called, lightly, too lightly. "Come rest, boy. Morning's a long way off, and roads are shorter for the sleeping."

He rose, tucking the vials into his coat, and did not go to her. He stood a moment longer, watching her watch the eastern dark, and told himself that an old woman looking at the road ahead meant nothing at all.

Behind them, far off, a bell began to toll in some unseen village—the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one—and then, midway through the fourth repetition, it stopped, as if the hand pulling the rope had simply let go.
