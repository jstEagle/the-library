## 10. Mother Quill's Price

The rain came down on Ashmarket like a debt collector—patient, thorough, indifferent to who suffered under it.

Mother Quill had lived through forty-three winters in the city of Zephyrine's making, and she had learned long ago that weather, like law, was simply another instrument the powerful used to remind the powerless of their place. Rain flooded the low quarters and left the high terraces dry. Law hanged a boy for stealing bread while a magistrate pocketed the bakery entire. She had stopped expecting fairness somewhere around her fortieth birthday, when her husband died in the salt mines for a debt of six silver marks and the crown sent his widow a bill for the cost of his shackles.

So she did not flinch when the broker came to her door on the third night of rain.

She was sitting by her brazier, mending a cloak that belonged to no one yet—everything in her shop belonged to no one yet; that was the art of it—when she heard the knock. Three soft raps. The kind that said *I know you know why I'm here.*

"Closed," she called, not looking up from her needle.

"The sign says otherwise." The voice was silk stretched over something harder. "The sign says Quill's Curiosities, Est. By Appointment."

Mother Quill set down her needlework with the deliberate slowness of a woman who had spent decades learning that haste killed more smugglers than soldiers ever had. She crossed the shop, unbarred the door, and let in a gust of wet air and a man in a grey coat too fine for the district.

He was nobody. That was what made him dangerous. No scar, no swagger, no story written on his face. Just a smooth-cheeked functionary with rain beading on shoulders worth more than her whole inventory, holding a ledger under his arm the way other men held scripture.

"Madam Quill." He inclined his head. "May I come in? I find the streets of Ashmarket damp this time of year."

"You find them damp every time of year." But she stepped aside, because refusing an audience was its own kind of confession, and Mother Quill had survived this long by never confessing to anything except profit.

* * *

He sat in her single customer's chair like he'd been fitted for it. His boots left small dark crescents on her floorboards, and she found herself disliking him for it—not for the trespass itself, but because he didn't notice he'd made one. Men who noticed their own messes could be bargained with. Men who didn't were the kind that burned things down and called it weather.

"I'll be direct," he said, opening the ledger across his knees. "You're fencing goods out of Thornfell. You've moved contraband through the old salt roads for twenty years without once paying the guild tithe. And in three days' time, you intend to guide a certain company through the Starless Pass along routes you have known since you were a girl running messages for your grandmother."

Mother Quill poured herself tea. She did not offer him any. "That's a lot of words to say nothing worth hanging for."

"Everything is worth hanging for now." He smiled without warmth. "There's a new administration. New rules. Or rather—" he turned a page, "—no rules at all, which is worse for people like us. People who live in the margins laws used to define."

She drank her tea. It was bitter, oversteeped, the way she liked it. Bitterness kept a mind sharp. Sweetness made women complacent, and complacent women ended up in ledgers.

"What do you want?"

"A name. A route. A day." He said it lightly, as if asking after her health. "The company you've been sheltering. Where they mean to cross, and when."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we do this the hard way, and I want to assure you"—he raised both hands, palms out, a gesture so rehearsed it might as well have been printed on parchment—"that I take no pleasure in the hard way. There are wardens in the capital who would very much like to speak with a fence of your reputation. They don't ask questions anymore. They just look at you until you start answering, and then they keep looking until you stop being able to."

Outside, the rain drummed against the shutters like fingertips on a coffin lid. Mother Quill thought about her granddaughter.

Her name was Tansy, and she was eleven years old, and she had her mother's stubborn chin and her grandfather's habit of humming when she was frightened. She was indentured to the Harrow Street chandler works until her nineteenth birthday—a debt inherited from a father who'd gambled away his own daughter's freedom over three nights of dice and drowned himself before the ink dried. Debt-bondage, they called it. Legal. Sanctioned. Stamped with the crown's seal by clerks who went home each evening to wives and children of their own.

Tansy dipped wicks twelve hours a day in a room where the tallow smoke ate your lungs by inches. When Mother Quill visited—once a month, if the roads allowed—the girl's hands smelled permanently of rendered fat, and there was wax in the creases of her knuckles that never washed out, and she hummed.

She always hummed.

"Sixty gold crowns," Mother Quill said.

The broker didn't blink. "Fifty."

"Sixty. Enough to clear the bond, pay the release fees, and buy passage south where the chandler's guild can't follow with new papers. Sixty, or you walk out that door and tell your masters the old woman wouldn't talk."

"Fifty-five," he said, "and I'll throw in the release documents drawn proper. Guild-sealed. No loose ends for some clerk to unpick later."

Mother Quill looked at him for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the back of the shop, and returned with a bottle of plum brandy older than the broker's career. She poured two cups.

"To sealed bargains," she said.

They drank. The brandy burned going down, warm and clean.

"Now," Mother Quill said, setting down her cup, "ask me again what you want to know."

* * *

She told him about the pass.

Not everything—she wasn't a fool, and fifty-five gold crowns bought information, not devotion—but enough. The route through the Starless Pass. The timing: they meant to cross within the week, ahead of the first snows. The composition of the company: Lockridge, the taster; Vane, the mapmaker; Doss; the deserter Ivo. She gave him the shape of their plan the way a fishwife gives a customer the shape of a fish—with the head cut off and the guts already cleaned out.

What she kept back was the thing that mattered most, though she couldn't have said exactly what it was. Perhaps it was Lena's face when he'd arrived at her door, hollow-eyed and smelling of smoke and fear, saying *Auntie, I need the old roads.* Perhaps it was the way Sera Vane had spread her maps on the floor and traced the mountain passes with fingers that trembled—not from cold, but from memory of what waited behind them. Perhaps it was only the simple animal truth that Mother Quill had spent twenty years selling other people's secrets and had somehow never sold her own.

But she gave him enough.

When the broker left, she counted the coins twice, wrapped them in oilcloth, and put them in the false bottom of her tea chest, beneath the dried chamomile and the letters from her dead husband she still couldn't bring herself to burn.

Then she sat by the brazier until dawn, listening to the rain, waiting to feel ashamed.

* * *

Shame never came.

What came instead, two mornings later, was a girl.

Tansy arrived at the shop door soaked to the bone and laughing, actually laughing, her chandler's smock still buttoned wrong because she'd dressed in such a hurry, clutching guild-sealed release papers in hands that finally smelled of nothing but soap and rain. Behind her stood a hired carter with a wagon, and on the wagon were two trunks and a birdcage with a finch inside—Tansy's finch, Pip, the one creature besides her grandmother she had loved in that tallow-smelling room.

"They said I could go," the girl breathed, half-hysterical with joy. "They said the bond was cleared, all paid, Grandma, they said—I'm free, I'm free—"

Mother Quill held her granddaughter and felt the girl's heart hammering against her ribs like a bird against a window, and she felt nothing but relief. Not pride—that would come later, or wouldn't. Not guilt—that was a luxury for women whose grandchildren were safe. Just relief, vast and simple as sunrise.

"Well," she said into Tansy's hair. "Pack light. We leave for Southmarch on Thursday."

* * *

The company found out on the fifth day.

They came to her shop in the gray hour before dawn—Lena first, then Sera with her maps rolled tight under her arm, then Doss and Ivo bringing up the rear with the wary silence of men who had learned to read rooms for exits. Mother Quill had been expecting them. She'd laid out tea and the last of her honey-cakes, and she'd worn her good shawl, the one with the embroidered hem, because whatever came next deserved ceremony.

Lena knew the moment he crossed the threshold. She saw it land in his eyes—the way he read the room the way she'd taught him years ago, back when he was a kitchen boy learning the fence trade: the tea set for five, the shawl, the false bottom of the tea chest sitting open and empty.

"You told them," he said quietly.

It wasn't a question. Lena Lockridge had spent nine years tasting poison for a living; he knew how to detect the bitter thing in a cup before it touched his lips.

Mother Quill poured the tea anyway. Five cups. Her hand did not shake, because she had trained it not to, sixty years ago, when shaking hands got girls sold.

"I did," she said.

Doss's knife was out of his sleeve before anyone else drew breath. Ivo caught his wrist—not gently—and held it. Sera Vane closed her eyes as if praying, or counting.

"How much?" Lena asked.

"Fifty-five crowns." She met his gaze steadily. "Enough to buy my granddaughter out of a chandler's works on Harrow Street, where they'd have worked her lungs to lace by nineteen. Enough to move her south where no guild seal can reach her. She's free, Lockridge. Free as of Tuesday morning. Humming again, instead of coughing."

Silence. The brazier ticked. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past through the wet streets.

"She's eleven," Lena said.

"I know how old she is. I was there when she was born." Mother Quill lifted her cup and drank, unhurried. "I also know how old I am, and how old the kingdom is, and which of those is going to outlast the other. You want me to apologize, boy?"

"No," Lena said slowly. "I want to understand."

"Then understand this." She set down the cup with a click that sounded final as a lock turning. "I have buried a husband the crown billed me for. I have buried two sons—one to fever, one to a magistrate's rope for a theft he didn't commit. I have spent my whole life watching this kingdom eat children and call it law. And when the chance came to pull one child out of its teeth, I took it. I didn't hesitate, I didn't haggle beyond the price, and I will not apologize."

Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it—it stayed level, conversational, the tone of a woman discussing the price of thread.

"You're doing a grand thing, Lockridge. Unbinding the crown, restoring the stars, all of it. Grand and righteous and likely to get everyone in this room killed. Maybe it even works. Maybe the world comes out the better for it." She leaned forward. "But I'm an old woman, and I won't live to see your new world. My granddaughter will. So you can keep your righteousness and I'll keep my Tansy, and we'll see which of us bought more good with our sins when the ledger closes on us both."

Sera Vane spoke first. "The wardens know the route through the pass."

"Some of it," Mother Quill admitted. "I gave them the road, the timing, the names. I did not give them the salt caves below the tree line, or the shepherd stair above the frozen falls, or the fact that the pass forks at the Cinder Cairn and only one fork leads anywhere but death." She shrugged. "Fifty-five crowns buys the front of the book. The rest costs more than any crown pays."

"So we can still outrun them," Doss said, lowering his knife at last.

"You can outrun anything if you're willing to leave things behind," Mother Quill said. "That's the whole secret of running, boy. Ask your feet."

Lena stood very still. She watched him work through it—she could see the machinery turning behind his eyes, the taster weighing bitterness against sweetness, deciding whether this cup was drinkable. Finally he said:

"Why tell us at all? You could have taken the gold and vanished. Southmarch is far."

"Because I'm done lying to people I love," Mother Quill said simply. "It's the one luxury I've saved all these years, and I find I want to spend it." She looked around the room—at the thief, the deserter, the mapmaker, the taster. At the found family she'd helped stitch together out of grievance and grief. "Also, because you'll need someone to hold the wardens at the Cinder Cairn fork, and none of you knows the ground like I do. I'm coming with you as far as the fork. After that—" her jaw tightened, almost imperceptibly, "—after that, I go south to my girl."

"You'd guide us," Ivo said flatly. "After selling us."

"I'd guide you," she agreed, "because guiding you is the only coin I have left to pay, and I pay my debts. Even the ones I chose freely." She rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and began gathering cloaks and provisions from the back room. "Now sit down and finish your tea. The wardens will ride at first light, and I intend to have us over the wall before they've finished saddling."

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Lena sat, and lifted his cup, and drank.

One by one, the others followed—Sera, Doss, Ivo—even Ivo, whose eyes stayed hard as river stones but who drained his cup to the dregs, because a soldier learns early that sometimes you swallow what you're given and march anyway.

Mother Quill watched them drink and permitted herself, for exactly one breath, to feel the weight of what she'd done lift slightly off her chest. Not gone. Nothing was ever gone; that was the lesson of her whole life. But lighter. Bearable.

She turned to the window, where the sky was graying toward dawn—still starless, still wrong, but lightening nonetheless—and began to plan the day's march.

Behind her, Lena spoke quietly. "The fork at the Cinder Cairn. How far?"

"Two days, if the weather holds." She pulled her hood up. "And it won't. Which means one day and most of a night, and every one of you moving faster than you think you can."

She opened the shop door onto the wet street, onto the smell of coal smoke and coming snow, onto the road that led either to the mountain or to ruin, and stepped through it first—because that was what you did, when you'd sold the road and meant to walk it anyway.

"Keep up," she said, "or keep quiet about which of us owes whom."

And the company of the unlawful followed her out into the starless dark, toward the pass, toward the fork, toward whatever waited for them at the top of the world.
