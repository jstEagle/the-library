## 11. The Hollow Court

They took her at the ford, three days out of the flooded archive, when her boots had finally begun to dry and she had allowed herself, foolishly, to hope.

Sera Vane would remember afterward not the hands that seized her—though they were strong, and cold as river stones—but the silence of it. No shout, no challenge, no oath of arrest. The wardens simply rose out of the mist on the far bank like a correction to a drawing, as if the world had noticed she did not belong in it and sent someone to erase her. She reached for her satchel, for the maps rolled tight against her ribs, and a warden's fingers closed over her wrist with the gentle, terrible patience of a thing that had all night and no heartbeat.

"Cartographer," it said. Not a question. It knew. Somewhere in the capital, someone had read her name off a ledger and known.

They bound her hands in front of her—an oversight, she thought later, or perhaps arrogance—and put her in a wagon with slatted sides through which she watched Thornfell's hills fold themselves away like hands closing over something precious. The stars were still gone. Six nights now without them, and she had stopped expecting them back. The sky above the wagon was not black, exactly; black was a color, and this was an absence wearing the shape of one, a velvet hole where the universe should have been. She found that if she looked too long, her eyes watered and her thoughts went soft at the edges, like paper left in rain.

So she looked instead at the road. A cartographer's habit: know where you are, even when you don't know what you are.

And the road was wrong too. The milestones along the king's highway had been freshly painted—the royal seal bright, the distances crisp—but beneath the new paint she could see the ghost of older markings, chiseled by hands two centuries dead, and the numbers did not agree. Someone was redrawing the kingdom. Not conquering it. *Editing* it.

She pressed her bound hands against the satchel strap beneath her coat and thought of Lena Lockridge, somewhere ahead of her with stolen bread and a thief's map of smuggler roads, and made herself a quiet promise: *I will get out of this city. Whatever it costs, I will carry what I saw out of this city.*

It was a good promise. She would spend four days learning what it cost.

* * *

The capital announced itself by smell before sight—or rather, by the absence of smell. Any city of eighty thousand announced itself from a league away with woodsmoke and sewage and horse and fish and the sweet rot of a market day ending. This city smelled of nothing. Scrubbed. Scoured. As though some enormous hand had passed over it and wiped the living residue of humanity from its walls.

Then the walls themselves, white in the starless dark, and the gates standing open—not forced open, not broken, but standing wide as if the idea of closing them had been forgotten, the way one forgets a word in another language.

Inside, the streets were full and quiet. That was the worst of it. Markets ran. Children chased a hoop down a cobbled lane. A woman leaned from a window shaking out a rug, and the small percussion of dust was the loudest sound Sera heard in the first quarter mile. Everyone moved with a kind of unhurried smoothness, faces pleasant, eyes—

She made herself look. She was a surveyor; looking was her trade; she would not flinch from fieldwork now.

Their eyes were contented. That was the only word. Not vacant—the vacant-eyed woman selling turnips still made change correctly, still counted coins—but contented in a way that had no texture underneath it, no weather. Every face wore the same mild satisfaction, like a room where every painting is the same landscape.

"They've signed," said one of the wardens, to no one, in the tone of a man remarking on weather. "Nearly all of them have signed."

The wagon rolled on, up through the terraces of the old city, past the fountain of the Nine Daughters—which had run dry, Sera noted, its basin scrubbed so clean the marble shone—and up, and up, toward the palace that crowned the hill like a growth.

They did not take her to the dungeons. She had braced herself for the dungeons, had composed, in the privacy of her terror, several dignified things to say under interrogation. Instead they took her to a guest chamber in the east wing, third floor, with a view of the inner courtyard, and locked the door from the outside, and left her a tray of food she did not touch.

The room was comfortable. That frightened her more than irons would have. Someone wanted her *kept*, not broken. Someone considered her part of the paperwork.

Through the leaded window, below, the courtyard filled at dusk with candlelight.

* * *

They came in rows.

Courtiers first—lords and ladies in silk gone slightly shabby, because no one had ordered new cloth since the stars went out, and no loom worked in a kingdom whose lord did not blink. Then officers of the guard, helmets under their arms. Then clerks, gray and brown, carrying ledgers against their chests like infants. Then servants, and soldiers, and ordinary citizens in work clothes who had no business inside a palace courtyard at all but came anyway, summoned by something, filing into rows with the smooth obedience of water finding level.

At the head of the courtyard stood a table. On the table lay a book.

Sera had spent nine years among the royal charters; she knew every binding style the palace scriptorium used. This book matched none of them. It was old the way mountains are old—its cover not leather but something paler, scarred like bark, and its pages, when a clerk opened it, were not ruled. They were blank. Vast stretches of blankness, vellum after vellum, and yet the signers bent over those empty pages as though reading.

One by one, they signed.

There was no pen visible that she could see—or there was, but it moved strangely, hesitating between signers as if choosing. Each person stepped up, bowed their head, and their shoulders eased in the same moment, a little sigh running down the row like wind through wheat. Some smiled. One elderly countess laughed, softly, delightedly, at whatever emptiness she had just written her name into, and walked away lighter than she'd come.

And above them, on the balcony of the great hall, stood the king.

Sera had seen King Aldous twice in her career, once at a distance during the survey presentations and once close enough to watch him argue a border concession down to the furlong. He had been a sharp man, a tired man, a man whose attention had weight, like a hand on the back of your neck.

The figure on the balcony had his face the way a glove has a hand's shape. It stood too still. When it moved—turning its head to follow a signer, the motion arriving in pieces, like a clock's hand ticking—it smiled, and the smile arrived separately from the eyes, which stayed fixed on the middle distance, on some appointment it was keeping elsewhere.

*That is not the man I mapped,* Sera thought, and the thought steadied her the way coordinates steady a sailor. She had drawn this kingdom's every river with her own hand. She knew what belonged in it. This did not.

The line moved. The candles burned. And in the fourth hour of watching, huddled at the window with her chin on her folded arms, Sera Vane saw the thing that saved her sanity: at the edge of the courtyard, in the shadow of the buttery stairs, a kitchen boy stood with his tray of wine and did not go forward to sign.

He stood there the way a man stands in cold water. Shoulders hunched. Jaw set. Watching the line with an expression that had weather in it—fear and disgust and defiance, all the textures the other faces lacked.

And behind him, two more. A scullery maid. An old woman in an apron with flour ground so deep into the weave it would never wash out. Kitchen people, holding their ground at the courtyard's rim like swimmers refusing one more stroke out from shore.

*Those who handle food daily sense the wrongness.* Lena had said something like that once, years ago, at some interminable feast where they'd both been working—she with her maps displayed, he with his tasting spoon—and she'd dismissed it as kitchen superstition. *You can't lie to a cook, my lady. We put our hands in everything. We notice what doesn't sit right.*

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass and watched the kitchen staff stand their small, unwitnessed ground, and wept for the first time since the ford, briefly and furiously, because the whole hollow edifice of the crown had been punctured not by lords or generals but by people who kneaded dough.

* * *

On the second day, they came for her testimony.

Not torture—paperwork. Two clerks, a notary, a comfortable chair, and a form. Her name, her station, her reason for travel, and then, at the bottom, a line for her signature above the words, in beautiful copperplate: *In renewal of fealty to the Crown of Zephyrine, everlasting and undivided.*

"It's routine," said the senior clerk. He had kind eyes, or the memory of them. "Everyone signs. His Majesty prefers matters settled promptly."

"And if I decline?"

The clerk's face did something complicated and sad, the way a house does when its foundation shifts. "No one declines."

She asked for a pen, and when it came, she wrote—in the signature line, in her best drafting hand, small enough to fit and legible enough to be undeniable—*This crown is a contract, and contracts require parties. To what am I swearing?*

The notary read it. The clerks read it. For a moment, in the notary's face, Sera saw something flicker that was almost thought—a fish surfacing in a pond and going under again—and then it smoothed away, and he said, gently, "We'll note the objection," and they escorted her back to her room, and doubled the guard outside her door, and tripled the sweetness of the meals they left, which she fed to the gutter through a gap in the shutters.

They were going to make her sign eventually. She understood that now. There would be no execution; executions produced martyrs, and martyrs had edges, and edges were the one thing this place could not abide. No—they would wait until she was soft and starved and lonely, and then the pen would feel like mercy, and she would write her name into the blankness like everyone else, and smile.

Three days, maybe. Perhaps less.

So when the kitchen boy came through the servant door at shift-change with his tray and his terrified, magnificent face, Sera Vane was already standing by the window with her boots laced and her satchel strapped across her chest, and she said, before he could stammer past his first sentence:

"I know. I've been counting guards for two days. Which stairwell?"

He stared at her. "You're the map lady."

"I'm the map lady," she agreed. "The kitchens sent you?"

"The head cook." He swallowed. "She says—she says the food knows. She says whatever wears the king up there, it can't touch anything that passes through a kitchen honest. She says—" his voice dropped, awed and ashamed at once, "—she says it's the only place left in this palace where people are still people, because we're always making something, and it can't unmake what's being made."

Sera felt the truth of it land in her chest like a plumb line finding vertical. Of course. Of course. A thing that fed on oaths, on signatures, on the *giving-over* of selves, would starve in a room where people gave each other bread.

"Tell your head cook," Sera said, "that I will draw every one of their names in every archive I ever reach, if it's the last thing I do. Now show me the way."

* * *

The servant corridors were Lena Lockridge's country, not hers, but she had mapped their bones. They went down through the warm arteries of the palace—the laundry, the stillroom, the long low bakehouse where forty ovens breathed and no one had signed anything, ever, she'd have bet her drafting kit on it—and the kitchen people passed her along like a dish at service, each pair of hands delivering her to the next with a nod and no questions.

In the bakehouse, waiting under a sack-shelf while a patrol's lantern-light slid past, she saw the decree list.

It hung, of all places, on the wall by the flour bins, pinned there by a scullery hand—whether as warning or trophy she couldn't tell. It was a fair copy, clerk-neat, of the usurper's current docket: the orders issued under the king's seal since the stars died. She had time to read perhaps half before the patrol passed and the scullery hand tugged her deeper into shadow, but she read with a surveyor's hunger, and what she read rearranged the world.

*Decree the Eleventh: the Forest Charter of Corvane, being contrary to the unity of the realm, is void, and all common rights of grazing, gleaning, and woodcutting therein revert to—* nothing. Not to the crown. Not to any lord. The clause simply ended, the rights reverting to *no party named*, dissolving into the air like smoke.

*Decree the Fourteenth: the Debtor's Relief Act is annulled; all bonds recorded thereunder are—* erased.

*Decree the Nineteenth: the Warden's Oath, in its present form, is superseded; wardens shall serve—* whom? The line trailed into a smudge, as though the very ink had grown tired of pretending.

It wasn't tyranny. That was the revelation that made her hands shake against the flour-dusted shelf. Tyrants accumulate; this thing subtracted. Law by law, right by right, protection by protection, it was unwinding the entire legal fabric of Zephyrine back toward some original state—not toward despotism but toward *nothing*, toward a blank page, toward the vault-condition Sera had seen described in the flooded archive beneath Thornfell. Justice perverted not into cruelty but into clerical erasure. Every evil law and every good one, abolished with the same indifferent pen. The orphan's shield and the tyrant's sword, deleted together, and the people left smiling and signing and undefended.

*It thinks it's restoring,* she thought. *It thinks the kingdom was the injury.*

The scullery hand—a girl of perhaps twelve, with burn scars on both forearms—caught her staring at the decree list and misread her expression entirely. "Don't worry, mistress," the girl whispered. "Cook says it can't erase us. It can only erase what was written down. And nobody ever wrote down what kitchens know."

*Gods above,* Sera thought, *let her live to be a hundred,* and then the girl was pushing her through the coal chute, and the night air of the capital hit her face like cold water, and Sera Vane, royal cartographer, disgraced and hunted and furious beyond the reach of language, began to walk south.

* * *

She went out through the fish gate, because the fish market still opened before dawn and its crowd was the densest and least contented in the city—fishwives arguing prices with real heat, real edges, blessed ugly human friction—and then along the river path where the fog gave her cover, and then she ran when she could and walked when she couldn't and hid in culverts when the wardens' lanterns passed, their lights burning a shade too steadily, a shade too white.

By the second day she was starving; by the third, feverish; on the fourth, with Ashmarket's chimneys smoking on the horizon like promises, her legs simply resigned their commission, and she crawled into the lee of a fallen milestone with the satchel clutched to her chest and reviewed her assets with a dying surveyor's discipline.

She had: the decree list copied out in charcoal on the back of her prison inventory, every erased law rendered line by line. She had the succession secret of the vaults. She had a name, learned from the bakehouse gossip in a single overheated sentence: *Lockridge*, the taster who fled, the one the wardens couldn't find because he knew the roads that weren't on anyone's maps.

She had, in other words, the truth. All of it. Contraband of the highest order.

"They'll want it in Ashmarket," she said aloud, to the milestone, to the starless sky, to whoever was out there still unsigning themselves free. "Someone in Ashmarket will know what to do with it."

The sky did not answer. It never did anymore.

But the chimneys smoked, and the road went on, and Sera Vane put her shoulder to the dirt and pushed herself upright, one hand's breadth, then another, toward the town where the outlaws gathered—carrying, folded against her heart like the last unburned map of a country being erased from existence, the paper proof that justice had been murdered by paperwork, and buried in blank books, and signed over by everyone, smiling.

Behind her, the capital glowed faintly against the starless dark, candlelit and contented and hollow as a bell with no tongue.

Ahead, in Ashmarket, someone was cooking.
