## 12. Reunion in Ashmarket

Ashmarket smelled the way Lena remembered it: woodsmoke and wet wool, tallow and river mud, the faint sweetness of rotting apples nobody had bothered to gather since the stars went out. It was a town built on what could be carried—smuggled over passes, hidden under floorboards, traded twice before noon—and it had always kept its doors half-open to people who arrived with nothing but a story.

Lena had been three days in Mother Quill's sister's cellar, cataloguing the stolen satchel of the king's untouched food while Doss drank too much and Ivo didn't drink at all, sitting by the window with his back to the wall, watching a sky that held no moon, no constellations, no anything. The dark up there was not the friendly dark of clouds. It was an absence with weight. Lena kept catching himself looking for the Shepherd's Belt, the Kettle, the long pale smear of the Queen's Veil—and finding only the places where they used to be, like gaps in a smile.

He was grinding moonflower root when the shouting started in the street above.

"Found her at the ford," someone was saying, voice cracking with cold and fear. "Half in the water. She's got palace seals all over her, Quill—palace seals, and she keeps saying a name."

Lena was up the ladder before he decided to move, flour still on his knuckles.

* * *

Sera Vane looked smaller than a person should look. She lay on the fence's back-room table among bolts of contraband silk, soaked to the bone, lips gone the blue-gray of old pewter, and around her—clutched so tightly that her fingers had to be pried open one by one—a leather document case stamped with the sun-and-crown seal of the royal cartography office.

"She was breathing when we pulled her out," Mother Quill said. Her granddaughter Pella hovered behind her with towels, eyes enormous. "Barely."

Lena put two fingers under Sera's jaw. Her pulse came slow and stumbling, like a drunkard feeling along a wall. He bent close and caught it beneath the river-smell: bitter almond, faint as a memory, threaded through something greener and sharper underneath. His stomach dropped with recognition even before his tongue confirmed it.

"Hemlock watered down with hellebore," he said. "Coated in wax so it would dissolve slow. Someone wanted her to die tired, somewhere far from help." He was already pulling open his kit, laying out vials in the order his hands knew better than his mind did. "Boil water. Every drop you have. Pella—the blue jar, second shelf, bring it carefully, don't shake it."

"You're sure you know what's in her?" Quill said.

"I'm sure I've tasted it." He almost laughed, and it came out wrong. "Nine years of tasting. Turns out it was training for this."

The work took him out of himself, which was a mercy. Antidote craft was the mirror of his profession, the part of the taster's art nobody sang about: for every poison a palate learned, a countermeasure lived in the memory of the body. Hemlock surrendered to a strong infusion of moonflower and salt-of-ash. Hellebore fought harder—it wanted the heart, wanted to slow it down into the earth—but silverleaf tincture, bitter enough to make his own eyes water, coaxed the rhythm back toward morning. He talked while he worked, because Sera's eyes had opened to slits and he needed her to stay on this side of the dark.

"Sera Vane. Cartographer. You drew the Thornfell roads so well the wardens copied your maps and arrested you for it—that's the story, anyway. You're stubborn. Stubborn people don't drown quietly in a ford. Stay stubborn. Stay here."

Her mouth moved. No sound. But her hand found his wrist with surprising strength and held on.

It took four hours. When it was done, Sera slept—not the waxy sleep of poison but real sleep, ragged and snoring—and Lena sat on the floor beside the table with his hands shaking now that they had nothing to do, and realized he had been more frightened in those four hours than on the battlements the night the stars died.

That was worth examining later, when he had the luxury. Not now.

* * *

She woke at dusk, ravenous, which Lena took as the best possible sign. He fed her broth in stages the way he'd feed anyone recovering from hellebore—thin first, then thicker, watching her color come back from pewter to parchment to something approaching a living woman.

"You're the taster," she rasped. "The one who ran. They showed your likeness at the gates."

"They showed it in the kitchens too. Terrible likeness. My nose isn't half that big." He eased another spoonful into her. "You crossed half of Thornfell poisoned to bring me something. Or to bring someone something. Which?"

Sera's eyes went to the document case, which sat on the chair where Pella had placed it with the reverence due a reliquary. "To whoever would understand it," she said. "I hoped—" She stopped, swallowed, started again. "I was wrong about everything, Lockridge. I thought the wards failed because someone paid them off, fed them the king's oath. That was just the door opening. What came through the door—" Her breath caught, and Lena couldn't tell if it was the poison or the thought. "Read the decrees. I stole them out of the chancery. Read them in order."

He unlatched the case. Inside, papers lay in a neat stack, each bearing the crown's seal and the king's signature—except that Lena had watched the king's hand for nine years across the ritual table, and these signatures were wrong in a way he felt in his teeth before he understood in his head. Too regular. Each letter identical to its siblings, like a line of soldiers. A man's hand trembles. This hand didn't.

"Decree forty-one," Sera said. "Start there."

Lena read aloud, slowly, translating the legal thicket as he went. "*By royal decree, the Bread Assize of Harlow is repealed. Weights and measures pertaining to grain shall no longer be enforced by the crown.*" He frowned. "That sounds almost harmless."

"Keep going."

"*Forty-two: the Crown Granaries are dissolved. Stores revert to the original holders.* Forty-three: tolls on the Ash Road abolished. Forty-four—" He slowed. "Forty-four dissolves the Wardens' Charter. The whole thing. Warden courts, warden oaths, jurisdiction, everything." He looked up. "Ivo's own charter. Why would the usurper dissolve its own enforcers?"

"That's not the strangest one. Keep reading. Fifty-one."

"Fifty-one repeals the Poor Laws. Sixty-one dissolves the Crown's claim to the Thornfell passes—*all claims*, it says, *in perpetuity, reverting to no holder*." Lena set the page down, and his hand wasn't quite steady. "These aren't conquest decrees. A conqueror takes things. This is—" He groped for the word and hated the one that arrived. "This is *unwinding*. Clause by clause. Everything the crown ever claimed, it's giving back. Or unclaiming. Unmaking."

"Now read the last one. Page ninety. The oldest paper in the case—I pulled it from the restricted archive under the chancery, and I'll tell you what it cost me to get it after you finish being amazed."

Page ninety was not a decree at all. It was older than the others, drawn on vellum gone amber at the edges, and the script on it was the founding hand—the hand from the histories, King Aldous First-of-His-Name, though Lena noticed even this ancient signature had that same terrible regularity. No. Not the same. Similar. As if the newer signatures were imitating the older one.

He read it twice before the shape of it stopped sliding out of his understanding.

"It's a contract," he said. "A blank contract. Look—every obligation has been struck out. Every grant, every oath, every promise the first king made or received. Scratched away so cleanly it might never have been written." He turned it toward the candlelight and saw, beneath the scratches, ghost-lines of ink: words murdered centuries ago, their shapes still faintly visible. "But it says at the bottom—*this bond shall hold until all terms are restored to blankness*. And then it says the crown itself is—"

"The crown is the pen," Sera said quietly. "I spent six years mapping borders, Lockridge. Borders are just lines people agreed to bleed over. So I started asking where the first lines came from. And the answer is that vault under Thornfell. The monarchy isn't a family or a flag. It's a *contract*, and the crown is the instrument that writes and rewrites it. Whoever holds the crown holds the pen."

Lena sat back. In the next room he could hear Doss arguing with Pella about card rules, the ordinary noise of ordinary people, and it seemed impossibly far away.

"So the usurper," he said slowly, "isn't stealing the kingdom."

"It's emptying it. Every law repealed, every claim released, every obligation struck. It's working backward through nine hundred years of legislation, unwriting everything down to the foundation." Sera pushed herself up on one elbow, and there was fever-brightness in her eyes now, and something worse underneath it: grief. "And here's the part that kept me walking north instead of lying down in that river to die. Read the decrees again. Really read them. The Bread Assize? It protected bakers from being hanged for short weights—protected *bakers*, not lords. The Poor Laws were corrupt; I documented it myself for a commission nobody read. The Warden Courts hanged a boy in my home village for poaching his own lord's land, except the land had been his family's before the crown gave it away." Her voice cracked. "Almost every clause it's erasing deserved erasing. Some of them I've dreamed of erasing."

The silence stretched. Lena looked at the scratched-out vellum and felt the ground tilt beneath an idea.

"You think it believes it's doing justice," he said.

"I think—" Sera pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I stood in that court and watched a hundred people renew their oaths with faces full of *contentment*, Lockridge. Bliss. Whatever wears the king's face told them something true enough to make surrender feel like relief. And its decrees aren't cruelty. They're *correction*. It found a kingdom full of broken promises and it's honoring the contract the only way a perfect thing knows how: by returning everything to zero. To the blank page. No laws means no unjust laws. No crown means no cruel crown." She lowered her hands. "It thinks it's setting us free."

"By unwriting us," Lena said. "A blank page is free the way a corpse is painless."

"Yes." She laughed, short and joyless. "That's the terrible clarity I crawled through a river to deliver. Our enemy may be the only honest creature in Zephyrine. It read the original contract—all the lies, all the theft, all the blood the crown was signed in—and it agreed with every radical who ever cursed the monarchy, and it's fixing everything, and the fix is oblivion. It's not conquering us. It's *restoring* us. Backward. All the way back."

Lena rose and walked to the window. Below, Ashmarket moved through its starless evening: lamps lit, shutters closing, a watchman calling the hour out of pure habit though no law required him to anymore—decree sixty-four had dissolved the requirement, along with the watchman's pay. People kept doing lawful things out of love for the shape of them, and somewhere beneath the mountain a perfect patient thing was scratching those shapes away one by one.

"Bram said something once," Lena murmured. "Old Bram, my mentor. He said a taster learns there are two kinds of poison. The kind that wants to hurt you, and the kind that doesn't know you're alive." He turned back to Sera. "I tasted the king's supper three nights ago. Nothing inside him is alive. Nothing in him notices us. But you're right—there's intent in the decrees. Judgment. It's not malice, it's—" He stopped.

"What?"

"*Standards,*" Lena said. "It's correcting our work. Like a master checking an apprentice's botched attempt and sweeping it off the table to start clean." The thought settled into him with a coldness that had nothing to do with the season. "Sera. If it's restoring the contract to blankness—if that's its purpose—what happens when the page is finally blank? All the way blank?"

Sera was quiet for a long moment. The candle guttered, straightened.

"A contract needs parties," she said at last. "Two sides who agree to be bound. Blank out all the terms and eventually you blank out the parties too. The crown, the kingdom—" Her eyes met his, and he watched her arrive at the same ledge he was standing on. "And the people. Nine hundred years of oaths, Lockridge. Every deed, every debt, every marriage line and border stone descends from that signing. We are all of us *terms*. When the page goes blank all the way down—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

* * *

They gathered everyone in the back room past midnight: Doss picking his teeth, Mother Quill with her ledgers, Ivo standing sentry-straight by the door as if desertion hadn't yet finished leaving his body. Lena laid the decrees out on the table in order and let them read. He watched Doss's face go through disbelief, then anger, then the strange slack look of a man finding his own grievances quoted in an enemy's handwriting.

"The Warden Charter," Ivo said finally. His voice was gravel. "It dissolved it. After what I saw it do to the wardens—the black eyes, the marching—it *frees* them?" He shook his head slowly. "No. It empties them. Same thing it did to the king, done to an institution. Wear the shape until the shape is all that's left."

"Then we go to the vault," Doss said. "We've known that since the flooded archive. We go down, we find the original contract, and we—" He mimed tearing paper.

"And it lets us?" Quill said dryly. "A thing that unwrites laws will blink at a lockbreaker?"

"We don't know what stops it," Lena admitted. "But we know where its heart is. Where the pen began." He gathered the pages back into their case, and his fingers paused on the scratched vellum, on the ghost of the first king's murdered promises. "Whatever it thinks it's doing—whatever twisted idea of justice drove it—we're the ones still alive in here. Still bleeding, still hungry, still arguing over cards in the next room. That has to count for something. That has to be worth defending against a blank page."

Sera, propped on her pillows, gray but alive, spoke up. "There's something else. Something I found in the archive and didn't understand until tonight." She beckoned Lena closer, and when he leaned in, she lowered her voice so only he could hear it. "The contract can't simply be torn. The archive was clear on that. It can only be *rewritten*—by consent, freely given, by someone of the founder's own line." Her eyes searched his face. "Everyone thinks Aldous's line ended four generations ago. But the archive keeps a sealed genealogy, and I broke it open before I fled the city. There's a branch it doesn't advertise. A servant registered in the palace rolls, nine years ago, whose entry was annotated in the founding hand."

Lena's mouth had gone dry. "Annotated how?"

"With two words," Sera said softly. "*Heir preserved.*"

Above Ashmarket the sky stayed empty, and somewhere far to the south, in a candlelit court, a hollow thing dipped its pen.
