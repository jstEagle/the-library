## 14. The Starless Pass

Ivo had spent eleven years learning to read the sky, and the sky had stopped speaking.

That was the thought that kept circling him as they climbed, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. Above Thornfell's shoulder the night hung like wet slate, featureless from horizon to horizon, and where the stars should have pricked through—the Shepherd's Yoke over the eastern peaks, the pale smear of the Milkroad that every warden learned to navigate by before he learned his own mother's face—there was nothing. Not cloud. Cloud had edges, texture, the smell of coming snow. This was absence, smooth as the inside of a bell, and it pressed down on the pass the way a hand presses on a lid.

He missed the stars the way you miss a language. Not because you need it to survive, but because without it the world stops meaning anything in particular.

"You're doing the thing again," Doss said beside him. The lockbreaker walked light on the scree, barely printing the frost, a habit from twenty years of doors that didn't care for visitors. "The staring-at-nothing thing."

"I'm reading," Ivo said.

"Read me something good, then."

Ivo tilted his chin at the black overhead. "No wind at altitude. Cold enough that the frost should be squeaking under us and isn't. And the wardens are four hours behind instead of two." He paused. "Also, we're going to die up here, but that part was true last week too."

Doss grinned, quick and crooked. "Good. Consistency. I hate a forecast that changes."

Ahead of them the company moved in a loose line along the old smuggler shelf—Lena at the point with Sera Vane close behind her, their heads bent together over a scrap of map by the light of a shuttered lantern, Mother Quill riding the mule like an empress in exile, and little Tam Quill, the granddaughter, wrapped in three blankets and asleep against her grandmother's back, her breath fogging in small silver puffs. Nine of them now, counting the mule. A kingdom's worth of grievance, Lena called it, and laughed the way people laugh when the alternative is weeping.

The pass rose ahead of them into the dark, a white seam between two shoulders of mountain, and somewhere beyond its crest lay the descent into the vault country, the drowned archive, the end of everything any of them had been running toward or from.

Ivo checked the backtrail one more time. Nothing. That was the worst part.

Wardens who pursued left signs. It was doctrine—*the hunt is a conversation*, his first captain used to say, *and every step is a word*. Broken crusts of snow. The gleam of a buckle. The sour smell of men who'd been marching since morning. But the things following them up out of the lowlands left nothing at all. No tracks in powder soft enough to swallow a horse to the hock. No firelight, though no living patrol crosses Thornfell in this cold without one. Just, occasionally, far below on the switchbacks, a suggestion of movement, like shadows detaching themselves from other shadows, patient as water finding its level.

They did not tire. They did not freeze. On the third night Ivo had watched one of them stand hip-deep in the freezing shallows of the Greywater for six hours without shifting its weight, waiting for them to come back for a dropped satchel, and when the thing finally turned away it turned all at once, smoothly, the way a weathervane turns.

He had been a warden for eleven years. He knew what his own body could do and what it couldn't, and everything behind them on that road was a list of things it couldn't.

* * *

They made the high camp at what should have been dusk, though dusk had become a rumor—no stars to mark it by, only the slow thickening of the dark until walking became stupid. Sera called it *starset*, half-joking, and the name stuck the way names do when people are frightened and need small things to own.

They sheltered beneath an overhang of granite that some ancient flood had undercut, and Lena got a fire going—small, shielded, almost apologetic—and cooked.

This was the ritual none of them named aloud. Every camp, whatever else was happening, Lena cooked, and everyone ate, and for the length of the meal the company became something sturdier than fear. Tonight it was barley and salt pork and a handful of dried silverleaf thrown in at the end, which tasted of pine and something sweeter underneath, and Ivo ate his portion slowly and watched the others do the same, and felt the strange guilt of the well-fed in a starving world.

"The chokepoint is tomorrow," Sera said, when the bowls were scraped. She unrolled her map on a flat stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. Her hands were steady; her voice wasn't quite. "Here. The Needle's Eye. The trail narrows to single file for forty yards, cliff on one side, drop on the other. After that it opens onto the saddle and there's a dozen ways down the far side. If they catch us before the Eye, we're dead. If they catch us after, we scatter and most of us live."

"And if they catch us *in* it?" Mother Quill asked.

Sera looked at the map. She was very good, Ivo had noticed, at looking at maps when she didn't want to look at people. "Then whoever's in front gets through and whoever's behind doesn't."

"No," Lena said.

Everyone turned. The cook hadn't looked up from banking the fire, and the light moved on his face like something alive.

"No," Lena said again, quietly. "Nobody's behind. Somebody holds the Eye while the rest go through, then follows. One person can hold that gap for an hour against anything that has to come at it single file. Two, if the second one is lucky."

"And which of us," Mother Quill said, in the tone of a woman laying a card face-down, "is volunteering to be lucky?"

Silence. The fire ticked. Up on the black shoulder of the mountain the wind made a sound like someone trying to remember a song.

"I am," Ivo said.

It came out level. He was surprised by that. Inside, everything was louder.

Lena looked at him across the fire, and Ivo saw him start to speak—the refusal, the arithmetic, *you're the only one who knows how wardens move*—and Ivo spoke first, because if he let the argument begin he might lose it, and he had already decided, three nights ago, lying awake under the starless dark, listening for footsteps that made no sound.

"I deserted my post," he said. "Eleven years I stood gates and bedchamber doors and city walls, and the one night it mattered, the night the stars went out, I ran. I've been running since. Everyone in this company is running from something the crown did to them." He nodded at Doss, at Mother Quill, at sleeping Tam. "You were robbed. Cheated. Sold. Me—I just left. There's a difference and I've felt it every day like a stone in my boot." He fed another stick to the fire. "A man can't outrun a stone in his boot. He has to stop somewhere and take it out. Let it be here. Let it be a gap in a wall, since walls are what I know."

"Ivo—" Lena began.

"It's not a sacrifice," Ivo said, and found, examining the words as he said them, that they were true. "It's arithmetic. You said it yourself once: the taster's whole art is knowing which poison to take so no one else has to. I'm just borrowing your math. Wardens don't tire. I do. So I'll spend my tiredness where it buys the most."

Doss opened his mouth, and Mother Quill put one ringed hand flat on his knee, and whatever the lockbreaker had been going to say died there, which was unlike him, and Ivo understood suddenly that Doss had liked him, in the sideways way thieves like honest men, and that this was going to make the morning harder than the fighting would be.

Sera rolled up her map. Her eyes were bright and furious, the fury of a woman who calculates everything and hates the answers. "An hour," she said. "You hold it an hour, then you follow. That's the term. You swear it."

"I swear it," Ivo said, and both of them heard the irony, and neither smiled, because oaths had gone strange in Zephyrine lately. Oaths were food for something. But this one sat in his chest warm and human, an oath with nobody feeding on it, and that was worth having.

Above them the starless dark pressed down like a held breath.

* * *

They reached the Needle's Eye at what Sera called midmorning, though morning was another rumor now—a gray lessening rather than a dawn, the world lit by some diffuse grudge of light that cast no shadows and showed no sun.

The gap was exactly as the map promised and worse than the map could say. Forty yards of trail cut through living rock, wide enough for one man, or two pressed close. To the left the cliff went up sixty feet of sheer gray. To the right the mountain fell away in a series of ledges and then simply stopped being mountain at all, dropping into cloud, into distance, into the white noise of air. Wind funneled through the Eye with a low moaning note, a single held tone like a struck glass, and Ivo thought: *if I die here, at least the place will already be singing.*

Lena stopped him at the mouth of it.

The cook's face was raw with cold and with the effort of not saying what he wanted to say. He pressed something into Ivo's hand: a small stoppered vial, clay-sealed, warm from his pocket.

"Bittercress and queen's bane, cut with ashroot," Lena said. "Not a cure. A delay. If you're cut or shot, pour it in the wound and you'll buy yourself another hour of standing. Don't drink it straight unless you mean to stop entirely." His jaw worked. "And this." A twist of cloth, smelling of salt and silverleaf. "Eat it when they're close. All of it. It won't save you. It just—" He stopped. Started again. "When I taste truth in a dish, I feel it here." He touched his own sternum. "Whatever those things are now, they were men. Men remember. Make them remember."

Ivo took both. "An hour," he said. "Then I follow."

Lena held his eyes a moment longer, and then did the thing that undid Ivo more thoroughly than any speech could have: he gripped Ivo's forearm, warden-fashion, the grip of the watch-relief, *your post, my trust*, and said, "Relief's coming," which was a lie and a blessing and both of them knew it.

Then they were through, single file, Doss first with his crossbow, then Sera, then Mother Quill with Tam a sleeping weight on her back, then the mule complaining beautifully, then Lena last, turning once at the far side to raise one hand—not waving, just open, just there—and then the gray swallowed them, and Ivo was alone in the singing gap with the wind and the vial and the bread of truth in his coat.

He drew his sword. Old habit. Then, deliberately, he set it down in the snow at his feet, within reach, and picked up instead the short warden's spear he'd carried since the Greywater, because the gap was narrow and spears were for gaps, and because he wanted, in whatever time remained, to do the work with the tools of the man he'd been trying to stop being.

He waited.

* * *

They came at what might have been noon.

He heard nothing—that was the obscenity of it, that after eleven years of training his ears he needed his ears and they gave him nothing—but the wind changed. It had been blowing through the Eye from the west at his back, carrying the smell of the far side, snow and stone and faintly, impossibly, the green scent of the flooded archive country. Now it stuttered, reversed, and brought him the smell of the trail behind: cold iron, and under the iron, faintly, the smell of a snuffed candle.

They rounded the bend in single file, because the trail allowed nothing else, and Ivo counted them the way you count anything terrible: quickly, because looking long makes it worse. Eight. Then four more. Then, after a pause that had intention in it, one more, walking last, wearing the uniform of a warden-captain, the braid still crisp, the boots still polished, as if the thing inside the uniform were keeping the uniform's dignity even now.

Their faces were the worst of it. Not hollow—he'd expected hollow, had braced for hollow. They were *content*. Slack and easy and faintly smiling, faces of men dozing by a fire, faces of men who have signed something and feel good about it, and Ivo recognized the expression with a lurch because he had seen it on the courtiers in the capital, row upon candlelit row, and he had seen it, God forgive him, in his own shaving mirror some mornings, before the desertion, before the stars died, the contentment of a man who has given his weight to something larger and no longer has to carry himself.

The lead warden raised its crossbow with perfect calm.

"*Zephyrine's oath binds you*," it said, and the voice was a sergeant's voice, bored and certain, the voice of ten thousand musters. "*Return to your post.*"

"I had a post," Ivo said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Eleven years. And then I saw what my king keeps under his face, and I learned that the post was a feeding trough." He set the butt of his spear against stone. "So no. This is my post now. And it's occupied."

The bolt came. He swayed inside it—it was slow, or the wind was kind, or dying men get one gift of speed—and then they were coming, all of them at once in the narrow throat of the rock, and there was no more room for thinking.

What followed was not glory. Glory is a word officers use. It was work, ugly and close, the work he'd trained for and hated and now, at the end, performed with a strange tenderness, because every stroke bought seconds for people who smelled of woodsmoke and bad jokes and barley soup, people who had let him walk among them without asking him to be anything but what he was. The gap was narrow. Spears are for gaps. He planted, thrust, shifted, planted, gave ground inch by inch and took it back, and the wind sang its one note over it all, and twice he went down and twice the vial—queen's bane and bittercress, poured screaming into a gash along his ribs—bought him back to his feet, because Lena Lockridge's poisons kept their promises even here at the top of the world.

He counted. It was the old drill, *count your strokes, count your breaths, counting is a wall against panic*. At two hundred strokes the first wave lay broken on the trail or over it, tumbled down the ledges into the cloud, and the second wave came stepping *over* them without expression, and Ivo understood that these things felt no horror at walking on their fellows, and felt, absurdly, a flash of pity. Whatever oaths had fed them had eaten that too. They were men who had signed away the parts of themselves that flinch.

At three hundred strokes the spear shaft split. He drew the sword after all. At three hundred and fifty the captain came for him personally, moving with that smooth wrong grace, and its crossbow bolt took him high in the chest, and the bittercress was spent, and Ivo sat down against the cliff with the blood coming warm and fast and thought, with a clarity that felt like grace:

*I stood my ground.*

Not a gate that belonged to someone else. Not a door for a king who never learned his name. A gap he had chosen, for people he had chosen, and the choosing was the whole of it, the entire difference, and it was enough. It was, he thought dimly, watching the captain advance through the singing wind, more than enough. It was the first thing he had ever done that was entirely his.

His hand found the twist of cloth in his coat. Salt and silverleaf. *Make them remember.*

He stuffed it into his mouth and chewed, and the truth went into him like sunlight into water, and he looked up at the contented face above him and smiled with bloody teeth.

"Your king," Ivo said, "is *dead*. And you know it. And some piece of you, deep under the signature, has been screaming about it ever since." He spat red. "*Listen to it.*"

The captain stopped. For one instant—one held note of the wind—the contentment cracked, and behind it was a man's face, young, terrified, a sentry's face, remembering.

Then the mountain shook.

Not the wind, not the fight—a deep subterranean groan, rolling up through the rock under Ivo's knees, through the bones of Thornfell itself, and far below, beyond the saddle, beyond the descending trails, something vast exhaled. The starless dark overhead shivered like the skin of a drum. And down the far side of the pass, faint with distance but unmistakable, carried on the reversing wind, came the sound of many voices crying out at once—in wonder, or alarm, or grief—and beneath them, rising through the ground and the air and the marrow of the world, a sound Ivo had never heard and knew instantly, the way you know your own name:

A vault door, opening.
