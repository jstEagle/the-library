## 18. The Last Dish

The vault had a hearth.

Lena had not noticed it when they came in—he had been looking at the open door, at the thing waiting inside it—but of course it did. Every contract has a table. Every table needs a fire. The first king had signed away his kingdom somewhere, and men sign nothing worth signing on an empty stomach. Somewhere beneath nine hundred years of law and ward and oath, there had always been this: a stone hearth, black with old ash, waiting for someone to light it again.

Lena lit it with the stub of a tallow candle Sera carried, and the flame took to the dry wood Ivo's sacrifice had bought them—Doss had gathered the dead man's kindling from the chokepoint pass without being asked, without speaking, his hands shaking the whole time—and the small fire threw its light against the vault walls, where the original contract lay unrolled across the floor like something flayed.

Blank now. Or nearly. Lena's refusal had burned through it clause by clause while they stood there, ink lifting off vellum like scum off milk, and what remained was a single line in an alphabet older than the kingdom, still glowing faintly, like a coal that would not die.

The usurper stood on the other side of the hearth and watched him work.

* * *

It had offered him everything. That was the part Lena kept turning over in his hands as he moved through the motions of cooking—the memory of it, the terrible gentleness of it. *Taste for me forever,* it had said, in King Aldric's voice, with King Aldric's smile, the one that used to make the whole court feel forgiven. *One dish a day, brought to my hand, and no one you love will ever want for anything. Comfortable houses. Full larders. Your mother's debts paid, your friends made harmless, your enemies forgotten. You will never be invisible again.*

And it had meant it. That was the horror. It was not a lie dressed in kindness; it was kindness, genuine as hunger, offered by something that had eaten the difference between generosity and consumption long ago and found both flavors pleasing.

"No," Lena had said.

And now: dinner.

He worked the way Bram had taught him, thirty years of ritual compressed into every motion. Clean surface. Clean hands. Clean intention—though that last one was new, that last one was his own invention, and it tasted strange in his mouth even unspoken.

The satchel of the king's untouched food had been confiscated in the Hollow Court; Sera had seen it carried into the palace stores. But the palace stores had come to them, in a sense, because the usurper wanted this feast, and everything the usurper wanted arrived. Servants' carts had followed them down the mountain road for two days—warden-driven, silent, eyes full of starless black—and unloaded at the vault mouth like mourners leaving offerings at a grave: flour and salt, cream and marrowbones, dried orchard fruit, a wheel of white cheese sealed in wax, jars of honey dark as old blood.

And poison. Of course. A crate of it, packed in straw like fine glassware. The palace's own stores, the ones Lena had spent nine years learning the way other men learned scripture. Arsenic from the rat-catcher's ledger. Hemlock tincture labeled in the steward's careful hand. Nightshade berries preserved in syrup. Antimony, aconite, mercury distilled sweet with coriander to fool the tongue. Nine years of the guild's quiet inventory, delivered to his hearth by the thing that had fed them all to him one spoonful at a time.

"It wants you to cook," Doss had said, staring at the crates. "Why does it *want*—"

"Because it can't imagine any other way to be met," Lena said. "It only knows tables."

* * *

He built the dish the way you build anything worth building: slowly.

A base first. Marrow bones roasted in the hearth until the fat ran clear and the smell filled the vault with something almost human—almost home, almost winter, almost the kitchen under the east stair where he had grown up between sacks of onions and the low murmur of working people. He deglazed the pan with cider Sera had hauled down out of pure stubbornness, and the steam rose up and for one moment the great blank contract on the floor seemed to flinch from it.

"Beautiful," the usurper said. It sat across from him now, in a chair that had not existed an hour ago, wearing Aldric's body the way a hand wears a glove three sizes wrong. "My mother made something like this. Before." It smiled. "You see? I remember him. All of him. Every meal he ever loved is in me. When you feed me, you feed him too. Isn't that a kind of mercy?"

Lena didn't answer. Answering was how it fed. He had watched it happen in the Hollow Court—every oath, every assurance, every *yes, majesty*, swallowed down and turned to substance. Words were its grain. Belief was its bread.

So he cooked instead, and let the cooking be the argument.

The arsenic went in second, folded into the paste of the sauce—a white powder fine as priest's dust, the first poison he had ever tasted professionally, at fourteen, kneeling on cold flagstones while Bram held his shoulder and counted his heartbeats aloud. *Two hundred and forty,* the old man had murmured. *Good lad. You'll live.* Lena remembered the taste even now: faintly sweet, faintly of nothing, like lying to someone gently. He measured it with a spoon and doubled it, and then doubled it again, because the dose that would kill a king barely warmed him, and tonight he was not cooking for a king.

The hemlock next, steeped into the cream. The night after that, the syrup of black nightshade, glossy and dark, stirred in slow figure-eights until the sauce went the color of a starless sky—which was to say, the color of the usurper's eyes, and he saw it notice, and he kept stirring.

"You're poisoning it," the usurper said. Not accusing. Delighted. "Oh, that's *good*. That's the whole story of this kingdom in one pot. Do you know how many kings have been fed exactly this way? Slowly, lovingly, by people who called it duty?" It leaned forward, and Aldric's handsome face caught the firelight. "I am only doing what the crown has always done, Lena. I consume what is given to me. They gave me a whole country."

"You didn't take the country," Lena said quietly. He sliced the candied orchard fruit, laid it in overlapping rows. "You took the man who wanted to put it down. There's a difference, and it's the size of everything."

"He *begged*," the usurper said, and something moved behind its face then, some old grief not its own, and Lena's hands went still for half a heartbeat because for just a moment it sounded like Aldric—like the real one, the tired one, the one who had knelt in his own kitchens decades ago and asked a guild of poison-eaters to help him disappear. "Every night for six years. *Make me nothing. Let me stop.* So I did. I am keeping his last wish. Is that not loyalty?"

"It's appetite wearing loyalty's coat," Lena said. "I've tasted the difference. It's the whole profession."

He worked the silverleaf in last.

Sera had carried it wrapped in wax paper inside her coat since Ashmarket—the last of the truth-leaf, the herb that stripped the tongue of every evasion, that had once been burned by the cartload when an old queen tried to silence the archive towns. It looked like frost. It smelled like the first breath after a lifetime underwater. Lena crumbled it over the finished dish in a fine snowfall, watching each curl dissolve into the warm glaze, and with every flake that vanished he felt the air in the vault change, felt it go thin and bright, the way a room changes when someone finally says the thing everyone has spent years not saying.

The usurper stopped smiling.

"That will hurt," it said.

"Yes," Lena said.

* * *

He plated the dish himself. Of course he did. Presentation was half the art; Bram had drilled that into him alongside antidotes. The plate was plain tin, scavenged from the warden carts, and he wiped its rim clean with his sleeve the way he'd done ten thousand times before a service, and set it on the table between them with the soft click of a key turning in a lock.

Then he sat down across from the usurper, and folded his hands, and waited.

The thing looked at the food. Steam rose off it carrying marrow and apples and arsenic and nine years of swallowed nights, and beneath all of it, so faint you'd swear you imagined it, the green sharp breath of the silverleaf.

"If I eat this," the usurper said, "and there is truth in it—truth goes into me, and truth does not sit still in me, it *unmakes*—" It stopped. Aldric's throat worked. Aldric's hands, flat on the table, trembled very slightly, and Lena understood suddenly that the trembling was not fear of death. The thing did not die. It feared the other thing. The thing that happened to oaths when they were spoken falsely in front of witnesses. The thing that happened to stories when someone told the ending out loud.

"You could refuse," Lena said. "Push the plate away. Sit hungry at a table forever. That's the other option."

"I could take you instead," the usurper said. "Consume the claimant. Tear the inheritance out of your blood and be legitimate by morning." But it said it the way a man describes a door he has already tried, and found painted on the wall. The refusal had changed something. The blank line of the contract pulsed on the floor between them, dimmer now, and Lena felt it too—that his *no* had gone into the world's ledger and could not be taken back out.

"You could," Lena agreed. "But you'd be eating a man who died refusing you, and that would be in you forever. Like the leaf." He nudged the plate an inch closer. "Whereas this—" he touched the rim of the tin, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal— "this is just dinner. Made well, by someone who knows you. That's more care than anyone has ever shown you, isn't it? Even the king who made you only ever asked you to take things *away*."

The usurper stared at him.

"You cannot poison me," it said. "Nothing in me is alive."

"I know," Lena said softly. "That's why I didn't bring poison."

He picked up the fork and held it out across the table, handle first—an offering, a courtesy, a taster's final courtesy, the gesture he had performed every day of his adult life: *after you, majesty; I have tested it; it is safe.*

It was the safest dish he had ever served. That was the joke nobody would laugh at. Every ingredient in it had already passed through him and been survived; he had seasoned it with his own endurance; the only new thing, the only untested thing, the only lethal thing—

was the truth.

The usurper took the fork.

* * *

It ate slowly. That surprised him. He had expected gulping, rage, violence—expected the mask to drop mid-bite and the starless dark underneath to simply reach across the table and unmake him. Instead it ate like Aldric had eaten on the good nights, before everything, when the king still bothered to send compliments down to the kitchens: attentively, with real pleasure, closing his eyes at the first taste of the marrow sauce like a man hearing an old song.

"This tastes like being human," it said wonderingly, around the second bite. "Is that what it—"

The silverleaf reached it on the third.

Lena watched it happen and knew he would see it in the dark behind his eyelids for the rest of his life. Truth entered the usurper the way venom enters a body that has never learned to suffer it: catastrophically, totally, with swelling and blackout and the wild panic of a system turning on itself. Aldric's face flushed gray. Aldric's eyes flew open—and for the first time they reflected the firelight, reflected *anything*, two small flames burning in all that starless depth like the first stars coming back to a sky.

"*Oh,*" the thing said. And then, in a voice that was suddenly, horribly, only one voice—old and tired and cracked at the edges: "*Oh, what have I—what did I agree to—*"

"That's him," Sera whispered from the doorway, her hand pressed over her mouth. "Lena, that's *him*, that's actually—"

"The true statement is:" the usurper said—or the thing said, or the knot of them said, both voices braided now, coming apart strand by strand—"the crown was never mine, the body was never mine, I have eaten nine thousand loyal hearts and they are *screaming* in me, they have always been screaming—" Its spine arched. The fork fell. The tin plate rang on the stone like a small bell, a rehearsal, an omen. "Take it *out*, take it *out*, I only ever wanted to put it *down*—"

"I know," Lena said. He stood. His own hands were steady, which felt like the strangest mercy of the night. "I know. I tasted it years ago, in the soup course, on a Tuesday. Nobody believed me but Bram." He came around the table. He put his hand—taster's hand, poison-scarred, immune to nearly everything—on the shoulder of the thing wearing the king, the way Bram had once held *his* shoulder on the cold flagstones, counting heartbeats.

"You're safe," he lied, gently, deliberately, feeding it one last false comfort, because a lie was the only anesthetic he had left to offer. "Swallow. You're safe."

The usurper swallowed.

And the contract on the floor began, clause by clause, to burn.

The blank line flared white, then gold, then a color Lena had no name for, and the fire did not spread to the walls or the floor or the crates of poison—it spread *outward*, up through the stone, out through the mountain, following the roots of every ward and oath and binding in the kingdom like flame running along a fuse nine hundred years long. The vault filled with a sound like a great book closing. Then another. Then hundreds.

The thing in the chair came apart the way mist comes apart at noon—not violently, not screaming, but *relieved*, unraveling into threads of shadow that thinned and thinned until all that remained was an empty robe, folded around a shape of ordinary dust, and on top of the dust a face Lena recognized from portraits: the first king, young and exhausted, wearing at last an expression of perfect, finished sleep.

The stars came back one constellation at a time. Through the vault mouth, far above, Lena heard Doss shout something—incredulous, joyful—and heard, rolling down the mountainsides from every direction, the sound of wards falling like rain, of oaths dissolving, of bells in distant villages ringing without anyone pulling their ropes.

He looked down at the empty robe. At the tin plate, scraped clean.

"Service," he said quietly, to no one, to the dust, to the man who had begged his guild to make him nothing. "That's what we should have offered you. Instead of the spoon."

Behind him, the last clause of the oldest contract in the world curled up like a moth wing and went out—and in the sudden, enormous dark, Sera caught his arm and said, in a voice that shook:

"Lena. If the crown is gone—who's holding up the kingdom?"

Above them, the stars kept returning. Below them, in the deep foundations of the world, something vast shifted its weight, as if waking—as if it had been holding the whole arrangement up from underneath all along, and had just been told, at last, that it was free to let go.
