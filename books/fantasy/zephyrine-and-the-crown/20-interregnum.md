## 20. Interregnum

The bell that wasn't a crown had not yet been cast, and in its absence the kingdom rang with nothing at all.

Ivo felt the silence of it most at dawn, when he walked the capital's walls as he had once walked them under oath, and found no order to guard. The wards were down. Not broken—*released*. Sera had explained it to him twice, once in the flooded archive and once over cold mutton in Ashmarket, and both times he'd understood only this: for two hundred years, Zephyrine's borders had been held shut by a contract written in a king's consented suffering, and now that contract was ash, and everything it had sealed was loose. Trade caravans rolled through mountain passes no soldier had patrolled since the founder's reign. Refugees came up from the southern valleys where lords still claimed their land by right of old signature. And above it all the sky did its slow, astonishing work—one constellation returning each night, like a lamp being relit in an enormous dark house.

The Ram had come back first. Ivo remembered standing on the wall with three hundred wardens who didn't know yet whether they were wardens or just men with spears, watching a scatter of pale stars assemble themselves into horns and haunches, and hearing someone behind him start to cry without any shame at all.

He had cried too. He'd waited until his watch ended.

Now, four months on, dawn was his favorite hour because the city was honest then. By noon the great square would fill with the Assembly—the word capitalized now, half-jokingly at first, until it wasn't—and the shouting would begin, and the throwing of turnips, and the occasional knife. But at dawn the capital belonged to bakers and lamplighters and the women who swept the shrine steps, and Ivo could walk through it feeling something he had no clean name for. It took him weeks to find the word, and when he found it he laughed alone in the warden barracks, because the word was *unused*. The city was unused. Two hundred years of obedience had been lifted off it like a heavy coat, and underneath, the streets were new.

New and chafing. That was the trouble.

* * *

The Assembly met in the old throne room, which everyone agreed was either perfect irony or terrible judgment, depending on the day. They had taken out the throne itself—carted it down to the vault-archive in pieces, where Sera catalogued each fragment of gilded oak with the tenderness of a woman dissecting a corpse she'd hated—and left the dais empty, a raised platform of bare stone that speakers climbed at their peril.

Ivo attended every session. Not because anyone asked him to, but because he had learned in twenty years of warden service that peace was not the absence of argument; it was argument that hadn't started throwing things yet. So he stood at the back of the throne room, unarmed now except for a short truncheon he kept mostly out of habit, and watched the free people of Zephyrine govern themselves with all the grace of a tavern brawl that occasionally produced miracles.

Today's miracle was the grain dole. Today's brawl was the millers' guild.

"The southern roads are open," Master Corvel was shouting from the dais, a fat man with flour in the creases of his knuckles and righteous fury in every line of him. "Open! Do you understand what that means? Any farmer can take his crop to any buyer. The old crown price is *dead*, gentlemen. You cannot fix what has been unwritten."

"And so prices float like scum on floodwater," snapped a weaver named Aldith, small and gray and immovable, "and the poor eat scum. The Assembly exists to protect—"

"The Assembly exists to do *nothing well*," Corvel roared, "because it does everything by committee and committees are how cowards say no!"

There was a scattering of laughter even from his own faction, because it was true and everyone knew it was true, and this was the strange alchemy Ivo was still learning to trust: that they could say such things to each other and not hang for them. A man had stood on that dais in the third week and called the whole Assembly a den of thieves, and instead of the block, he'd been handed a ledger and made treasurer, on the theory that thieves know best what thieves steal. The treasury had never run cleaner.

"Order," said Mother Quill, from her seat near the front, and the room obeyed her the way rivers obey stone—not instantly, but inevitably. She was the closest thing the Assembly had to a speaker, elected by acclamation after she'd stood up during a riot over market stalls and said, in the voice she'd used for decades on fences and smugglers: *"Sit down, all of you. My granddaughter is watching."* Her granddaughter sat beside her now, eleven years old and debt-free, taking minutes in a round childish hand that would someday be worth more than gold to historians.

They argued the grain question for two hours. They resolved nothing. They scheduled another session. And when the crowd filed out grumbling but not bleeding, Ivo caught Quill's eye across the emptied room, and she beckoned him down to the dais with one crooked finger.

"You look tired, deserter."

"I look employed."

"That too." She studied him with eyes that had priced stolen goods and human hearts in the same ledger and always gotten both right. "Word from the villages?"

Ivo leaned against the dais edge. "Thornbury's council meets in the tithe barn and nearly burned it down arguing about wells. Marlow split in two over whether to keep paying the ferryman—the ferryman being, apparently, the last man in Zephyrine who misses the crown. Hallowford wrote us a letter." He paused. "It's mostly insults. It ends with a request for a schoolteacher."

Quill snorted. "And the harvest councils? Are they feeding themselves?"

"They're feeding each other. Badly, sometimes. There was near-famine logic in Duncarrow before they worked out the seed-sharing." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "It's messy. It's loud. Half of them want to reelect a lordling because at least a lordling tells you what to do and you can hate him honestly."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is arguing about wells." He looked at her. "You've heard the other thing too. Don't pretend you haven't. People come to you first—they always have."

Mother Quill was quiet for a moment. Outside, the city noise swelled and broke against the throne room walls like a tide against a seawall: cart wheels, hawkers, a fiddler tuning up for the evening crowds.

"They want him," she said finally. "Some of them. More every week. The winter was hard, Ivo, and freedom doesn't bake bread. They remember that under the false king there was order, and they forget that the order was eating them alive, clause by clause. So they look around at all this—" she gestured at the empty dais, the bare stone, the vast echoing room built for one man's voice and now holding hundreds of smaller ones, "—and they think: if only someone would sit there. Someone good. Someone who fed poison to tyrants for nine years and never broke."

"Lena won't do it."

"I know what Lena will do. I'm telling you what the people will *ask*." She rose, joints cracking like green wood. "A petition's circulating. Three hundred names already. They mean to bring it to the Assembly at midwinter session. 'Restore the Crown of Zephyrine to its rightful heir.'" Her mouth twisted around the words. "Rightful heir. As if that boy was ever anything but invisible until the vault spat the truth at us."

Ivo said nothing. There was nothing to say that Quill didn't already know, and saying it aloud would only make it heavier. He watched her shepherd her granddaughter toward the doors, and then he stood alone for a long while in the throne room, looking at the bare stone dais, at the place where a hollow thing had worn a beloved face, at the place where a good man might soon be begged to wear a real one.

Both thrones, Lena had said, in the vault, in the dark, with the contract unraveling around them like burning rope. Both thrones deserve to burn. He'd refused the usurper's bargain and his own birthright in the same breath, and the refusal had unmade two centuries of binding law.

Ivo wondered, walking back to the barracks through the gathering dusk, whether refusal could hold forever—or whether it was like a dam, admirable right up until the water rose.

* * *

The petition reached three hundred names by the first frost, and a thousand by midwinter, and Ivo read every name.

That was his penance, or his discipline—he wasn't sure which. Each evening after the watch reports, he sat in the barracks with the growing scroll unrolled across three trestle tables and read the signatures: farmers and ferrymen, widows and guildsmen, a whole streetful from the Tanner's Quarter pressed thumbprints in charcoal because half of them couldn't write. Some names came with notes. *He saved my daughter's fever with willowbark tea when no physician would come.* *My brother starved under the old king; this one knows hunger.* *Let someone decent carry it, just for a while, and then we'll take it back.* That last one was from Aldith the weaver, who wanted no king at all and had signed anyway, and Ivo understood her better than he understood almost anyone: hope and dread wearing the same coat.

The winter was hard, as Quill had promised. Harder than anyone had planned for, because planning was exactly what the unwritten kingdom hadn't figured out yet. Under the crown, granaries had been filled by decree and distributed by ration-book; now granaries were filled by whoever thought of it first and distributed by argument. In the river districts, children went hungry in November while the Assembly debated whether emergency powers violated the spirit of the Refusal. The phrase *spirit of the Refusal* was spoken so often that autumn it became a joke, then a curse, then a prayer, cycling through all three in a single session.

Ivo kept the peace. That was the whole of his office now—wardens had rewritten their own charter in a barn outside the walls, forty of them drunk on cider and terror, and what they'd produced was one page long. *We answer to the Assembly. We carry no blades within the walls. We may detain no one longer than sunrise. We walk our rounds in pairs and we listen more than we speak.* Old Warden-Captain Voss had called it suicide and resigned on the spot, predicting the city would eat itself by spring. Instead, something else happened. Without swords, the wardens had to talk. Without cells, they had to solve. Disputes got mediated on doorsteps by men who used to kick those same doors in. Crime fell—not vanished, never that, but fell—and when it rose again in the hungry weeks, it was theft of bread, and the punishment was that the thief's family ate at the warden barracks until the Assembly sorted the dole.

"We're not governing," Ivo told his wardens at the midwinter muster, standing in the snow in the yard where he'd once drilled them in riot formation. "Stop thinking you are. We're holding a door open. What walks through it belongs to them."

A young warden—Pell, seventeen, who'd been born under the crown and couldn't quite believe the sky was real—raised his hand like a schoolboy. "And if they ask for a king, Captain?"

"Then they'll ask loudly, and argue about it, and maybe fight about it, and take years deciding, because that's what freedom costs. Tyranny is fast. That's its whole virtue." He looked at their faces, young and old, scarred and soft. "We don't decide this for them. We decided enough things for people, once."

He didn't say: *and some of us peeled off our own shadows and fed on oaths.* He didn't need to. Every man in that yard had served the thing in the crown. Every man had signed the nightly renewals with a hand that felt warm and a mind that felt pleasantly empty. They were all, in their various ways, doing penance with truncheons and patience.

* * *

The petition came to the Assembly on the last day of the midwinter session, carried by a delegation of forty from the Tanner's Quarter, led by a widow named Maren Cole whose son had died in the border fighting two kings ago. Ivo stood at the back as always, and watched them process up the aisle between the packed benches, and saw the Assembly's factions—all of them, Corvel's merchants and Aldith's weavers, the ex-lordlings and the radical cartwrights—go silent with the specific silence of people watching something inevitable arrive.

Maren Cole was seventy if she was a day, and she climbed the bare dais steps slowly, leaning on a stick, and turned to face the room with the scroll held out before her like an offering.

"A thousand and sixty-two names," she said. Her voice carried; grief had given it practice. "We don't ask for tyranny. We lived under it. We know its smell." She let that sit. "We ask for one person, chosen not by blood-right but by *demonstration*—a person who has already proven he'd rather die than rule unjustly. We ask for Lena Lockridge, for a term of five years, subject to recall by this Assembly at any session. Not a king. A steward. With chains on the office that we forge ourselves, this time, out in the open, where everyone can see the links."

The room erupted. Of course it erupted. Corvel was on his feet shouting that stewardship was monarchy with better manners; Aldith was shouting back that chains forged openly beat power seized secretly; somewhere in the middle a fistfight started and was stopped, remarkably, in under a minute, by two wardens who talked instead of striking.

Through all of it, Ivo watched the doors at the far end of the throne room.

Because Lena Lockridge was there. Ivo had seen him slip in late, as he often did these days, standing along the wall in his cook's apron still dusted with flour from the palace kitchens, where he fed the city through the hungry winter from stores meant for one man's table. He stood now with his arms crossed, watching the widow make her case for him, and his face—

His face was the thing Ivo couldn't stop looking at. Not anger. Not temptation, exactly, though Ivo had seen temptation wear kinder masks. It was older than both. It was the expression of a man watching a wave come in and knowing he could not argue with the sea, only decide, when it reached him, whether to stand or be moved.

The vote wasn't held that day. The Assembly did what assemblies did: it argued until the candles guttered, tabled the matter, scheduled a special session, insulted each other's parentage, and adjourned. But everyone walking out into the snow knew the arithmetic. The petition had the weight. The winter had the weight. The empty dais had a gravity all its own, and gravity, unlike crowns, could not be unwritten by any vault.

Lena found Ivo in the colonnade afterward, breath steaming, flour still in his hair. For a while they just stood there, two men who had fed and guarded monsters, watching the Assembly stream past them into the blue evening.

"You could say no," Ivo offered. "Publicly. End it tonight."

"I could." Lena's voice was quiet. "And next month they'd draft a softer petition. And the month after, a harder one. Ivo, I refused the throne in the vault with the truth in my throat, and it was the easiest thing I've ever done, because refusing a *thing* is easy. This—" he nodded toward the dispersing crowd, the widow Cole being helped down the steps by children who adored him, "—this isn't a thing. It's a thousand people who are cold and frightened and asking me to stand between them and the dark. Saying no to that doesn't feel like freedom. It feels like abandonment wearing freedom's clothes."

"So what will you do?"

Lena was quiet a long time. Snow began to fall, fine and dry, catching in both their hair.

"I'm going to cook for them," he said at last. "The special session. All of them, every faction, the whole quarrelsome lot. One long table, my food, and nobody speaks of crowns or petitions until the plates are empty." He smiled faintly, and it was the smile of a man who had weaponized intimacy once already and knew its cost. "You can't swallow poison on a full stomach of bread you watched being baked. Let's see what a thousand arguments look like to people who've eaten together."

"And if it changes nothing?"

"Then at least they'll argue warm." He pushed off the column, then paused. "One more thing. The vault-keepers sent word this morning. Sera's been translating the original contract fragments all autumn, the ones we couldn't read before the unwriting. There's a clause she says we missed—a final one, at the very bottom of the founder's page, in handwriting that isn't the founder's." His breath fogged the air between them. "Ivo—it's addressed to whoever refuses the throne. Word for word. The founder knew. Two hundred years ago, someone in his own house was going to stand where I stood and say no. And they left him—or her—a message."

The snow thickened around them, erasing the square, the benches, the whole arguing, stumbling, magnificent mess of the free city, until there was only the two of them and the words hanging in the cold.

"What does it say?" Ivo asked.

"I don't know yet," said Lena. "Sera opens the seal tomorrow. She wants us both there."
