## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trick to stealing a memory is not to be clever. Cleverness is for the city thieves, the ones who work crowds and pickpockets' alleys where a hundred eyes might catch you. In the valley, the fog does most of the work. You just have to be patient enough to let it.

I sat across from Widow Serafina Hale in her kitchen while her tea went cold between us, and I waited.

Her cottage smelled of rosemary and old wool and the particular staleness of a house where one person has stopped bothering with the second chair at the table. The second chair was there, though. She'd polished it that morning—I could see the wet ring where the cloth had missed a spot on the seat. Forty years of marriage, six months of widowhood, and she still polished his chair. People are like that. They keep setting places for the dead. It's the cruelest habit we have, and the most human, and I have built an entire living out of its wreckage.

"You said you could help," she said. Her hands were wrapped around the teacup like it was the last warm thing in the world, which, given the season, it possibly was. "The tinker's wife said you helped her sister. After the fever."

"The tinker's wife has a generous mouth," I said. "What did she tell you?"

"That you take things away. The heavy things." Serafina looked up at me then, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady, which is the worst combination. Steady grief means someone who has decided. "I don't want all of him gone. Just—" Her voice caught, steadied. "Just the beginning. The first kiss. Everyone says you never forget your first kiss, and I'm sixty-three years old, Rafael, and I can't keep carrying it."

Here is what nobody tells you about this trade: they always ask. I have never once had to convince anyone. They come to me the way people come to a tooth-puller—hating the necessity, grateful for the hand, and afterward swearing they feel lighter even as their tongue keeps going back to the gap.

"Once it's gone, it's gone," I told her, because I say this every time, and because it's true, and because saying it lets them stop me if they're going to. "You won't know what you lost. You'll just know there's a shape missing. Some people find that worse than the memory itself."

"And some people find it's the only sleep they get," she said. "Do it."

* * *

Let me explain how the valley works, since you'll need to understand it before you understand anything else about me.

The valley is called Merelowe by the people who live here and nothing at all by the maps, because no cartographer has ever thought us worth the ink. It sits low between two shoulders of the Greymass Mountains, and twice a day the fog rolls up from the river flats and swallows everything—the orchards, the bell tower, the crooked lane of shops, the whole gray-brown sprawl of us. And the fog doesn't just swallow light. It swallows magic.

Everywhere else in the world—or so I'm told; I've never been, and you'll learn why—magic runs through things like water through a sponge. Witches charm their doorways, hedge-mages cure fevers with a word and a pinch of ash, kings keep court wizards who can pull lightning out of a clear sky. Here, none of it works. A spell cast in Merelowe dies before it finishes leaving your mouth. An enchanted object crosses the valley line and goes as dumb as a stone. The fog eats it. Nobody knows why. There are theories—the mountains leeching it away, an old god sleeping under the riverbed, a bargain struck so long ago the terms have rotted out of every record—but theories are cheap and the fog doesn't care about any of them.

Which makes the valley the one place in the world where a person like me can exist without being noticed by the things that would notice.

Because my particular talent isn't magic. Not exactly. Or—it is, but it's a kind that slips under the fog like smoke under a door. I can take memories. Touch a person's temple, find the thread of what they want gone, and draw it out. It comes away warm. It comes away tasting of whatever it was: saltwater, bread, another person's skin. And once it's out of them, it's mine, folded up and stored somewhere behind my own thoughts like letters in a drawer I never open.

In the cities, people who do what I do are called memory-thieves, and they are hunted, hanged, or worse—recruited. Here, I am Rafael Marchetti, who mends shutters and sells eggs and occasionally helps grieving folk set down their heaviest stones, for a fee. The fog hides me even from myself, in a way. In Merelowe, I am unremarkable. I am safe.

I am also, if I'm honest on the days I allow honesty, alone in a way that has teeth.

But that's later. Right now there was a widow across the table, and a job to finish.

* * *

"Close your eyes," I said.

She did. Her face went slack with trust, which is the part I hate most, if I'm ranking the parts. The taking itself is easy. The trust is what costs.

I reached across and laid two fingers against her temple, left side, where the oldest memories live closest to the surface. Finding the right thread is like sorting yarn in the dark—you go by texture. Grief has a texture. It's coarse and damp and it pulls toward everything. But underneath the grief, wrapped in it like a stone in wet cloth, was the thing she'd asked for: a first kiss. Hers. His. I won't describe what I found there, because it isn't mine to describe anymore than it's mine to keep, but I will say this: it tasted of cider, and rain, and a boy's nervous laughter, and it was so bright after all that coarse gray grief that I flinched.

Then I drew it out.

It came free with a small sound she made—not a word, more the sound a knot makes when it slips—and I held it cupped in my palm like a moth I'd caught. Warm. Faintly glowing, if glow is the word for something only I could see. Sixty-one years old and still burning. That's the thing about first kisses. They don't fade. Everything else fades around them, and they sit there being perfect, which is exactly why they become unbearable.

Serafina opened her eyes. She looked at me, then past me, then down at her tea, and her brow creased in the small way I've learned to recognize: the shape of a missing thing. Like reaching for a stair that isn't there.

"Do you feel it?" I asked quietly.

"I feel—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum. "Lighter. Yes. Like setting down a basket I forgot I was holding." Then, uncertain: "Was it a good one? The thing you took?"

They always ask that. I've never figured out whether they want the truth or comfort, and I've stopped trying to guess.

"It was good," I said. "That's why it was heavy."

She nodded slowly, and something in her face settled, and I stood up before she could ask anything else, because the other rule of this trade is: leave before they start building new memories of you. Gratitude is fine. Attachment is dangerous—for them, mostly. For me too, if I'm being thorough, but I try not to be thorough about myself.

She paid me in coin counted out of a tin on the mantel, and a heel of dark bread wrapped in cloth besides, pressed on me over my protests, which were sincere, and which she ignored, which is also a pattern. Widow logic: you feed the thin young man who took your first kiss, because feeding people is the last ritual you have left.

At the door she caught my sleeve.

"Rafael." My name in her mouth sounded strange, like she was testing whether it fit. "Will I see you again? At market, I mean. Ordinary seeing."

"Ordinary seeing," I agreed. "Market days. I'll be the one buying too many onions."

She laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and closed the door, and I walked down her garden path into the fog feeling like a man who has done a kind thing and a monstrous thing and cannot locate the seam between them.

* * *

The walk home takes twenty minutes if you take the lane and forty if you take the ridge path, and I took the ridge path, because the ridge path is empty and empty is where I do my thinking.

Merelowe from above is a handful of roofs drowning in white. The fog had come up early, the way it does in autumn, and the village was just suggestions: the bell tower's top like a drowned mast, chimney smoke smearing upward until the fog ate that too. Somewhere down there, Serafina Hale was polishing a chair she no longer knew why she polished. Somewhere down there, four hundred people lived their whole lives inside a bowl of cloud and called it the world.

And here's the confession, since we're alone up here and the fog keeps secrets: sometimes I envy them.

Not the grief. Never the grief—I've seen enough of it stored behind my own eyes to last several lifetimes, and I haven't even opened the drawer. I envy the *shape* of their lives. Serafina had sixty years of a man, and losing him broke her, and the breaking proved the having. Every person down in that fog is stitched into the fabric of the place. Old Toma at the mill remembers everyone's debts and grudges going back forty years. The baker's daughters argue about who got the bigger share of their grandmother's temper. Even the feuds are a kind of belonging. They are all of them *known*, thoroughly, tediously, wonderfully known.

And I am Rafael Marchetti, thirty-one years old, and if you asked anyone in Merelowe to describe me, they'd say: quiet fellow, does odd jobs, keeps to himself, hard to recall the face actually, now that you mention it. Hard to recall the face. I've worked at that. Unremarkable isn't an accident; it's a discipline. Plain clothes chosen to be forgettable. A voice kept level. No stories told, no debts owed, no favors banked. I have spent my entire adult life sanding myself down into a shape the world slides off of.

It works. Gods, it works. No one looks at me twice. No one from outside ever finds me. The fog swallows magic, and I am made of something adjacent to magic, and so the fog swallows me too, gently, daily, and I am safe.

Safe. Alone. The two words come joined at the hip, like twins, and you don't get to adopt one without the other.

I sat on the ridge with the bread in my lap and ate it slowly, watching the valley fail to see me, and I told myself the thing I always tell myself, which is that this is a fair price. That invisibility purchased with loneliness is a bargain, not a sentence. That a person who erases themselves has no one to blame but—

Well. That's the thing, isn't it. Blame implies a choice, and lately, on nights I don't examine too closely, I've wondered whether I ever chose any of this. Whether the talent chose me. Whether something older than the fog decided my shape before I was born, the way a potter decides the clay.

I shook that thought off. It's a bad thought. It leads nowhere except to the edge of the valley, and I never go to the edge of the valley.

* * *

Home is a cottage at the end of Larkspur Lane with a leaky roof I keep meaning to fix and a single room downstairs that serves as kitchen, workshop, and everything else. On the table inside, under a cloth I keep weighted with river stones, is the only valuable thing I own: my ledger. Not money. Memories. Dozens of them, taken over eleven years of trade, each one wrapped in a scrap of my intention and filed away in the dark behind my thoughts—but the ledger records them, because a thief who can't account for his inventory is an amateur.

First kiss, Serafina Hale, taken this day, paid in full plus bread. I wrote it out in my careful hand. Entry number two hundred and six.

Two hundred and six memories. Two hundred and six lives, in fragments, carried around inside me everywhere I go. Sometimes, late at night, when the fog presses against the windows and the house creaks, I swear I can hear them. Murmuring. Not words—never words—but a rustle, like a drawer of letters shifting in a draft. I've learned to ignore it. Mostly. The alternative is to wonder what happens to all those stolen pieces of people when I die, and that way lies insomnia, and I already have enough of that.

I banked the fire, hung up my coat, and was reaching to snuff the lamp when the knock came.

Three raps, uneven, weak. The kind of knock made by someone leaning on a doorframe.

Nobody knocks on my door after dark. That's not paranoia; that's bookkeeping. In eleven years, the after-dark callers number exactly zero, because I have arranged my life so that there should be zero, and arrangements like that don't break by accident.

I blew out the lamp anyway and stood in the dark, listening.

Another knock. Softer. And then a voice, papery and cracked, saying words that I would think about for the rest of my life, though I didn't know it yet:

"Marchetti. I know you're awake. I'm dying, lad, and I've brought you something worth more than coin—and I need you to take a memory I can't afford to die holding."

I stood there in the dark with my hand still raised toward a lamp that wasn't lit, and every instinct I owned—the ones that had kept me invisible, unnamed, unfound for thirty-one years—told me not to open the door.

I opened the door.
