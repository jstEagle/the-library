## 2. A Book Bound in Bark

He was older than the doorframe. That was my first thought, absurd as it sounds—that the wood had grown around him sometime in the last century and he'd simply been standing there ever since, waiting for me to let him in.

Tobias Wick. I knew the name the way everyone in the valley knew it: the peddler who'd walked the ridge roads for fifty years, selling pins and prayer cards and stories nobody believed. He'd been old when my mother was young. Now he was dying of it, propped against my doorpost like a coat someone had forgotten to hang up, his cart gone—he hadn't brought a cart—and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth clutched to his chest with both hands, the way you hold a thing you're afraid will be taken.

"Tobias," I said, because using people's names was a habit I permitted myself. It cost nothing and made them trust you, and trusting me was about to be the worst thing that ever happened to him. "You shouldn't be out in this fog."

"That's the point of fog, lad." He smiled, and his teeth were very few and very yellow, like a picket fence after a flood. "It's for things that shouldn't be seen." His eyes went past my shoulder into the dark of my kitchen, then came back to my face with terrible precision. "May I come in? Cold's in my bones now. It doesn't ask permission anymore."

Every rule I lived by said no. No visitors. No witnesses. No one who could later say *I was in Rafael Marchetti's kitchen on the night of—* whatever night this was. My house was known to exactly two neighbors as a place where a quiet person sold quiet services, and neither of them knew more than that. Thirty-one years of careful nothing, and here was a dying man on my step wanting in.

But he was dying. That was the trouble with me; I've never been able to refuse the dying anything. They've already got one foot through the only door I can't follow them through, and they know it, and there's a kind of peace in their faces that makes liars of everyone left standing on this side.

I stood aside. Tobias Wick shuffled past me smelling of rain and tallow and something else underneath—something faintly scorched, like a doused fire—and settled into my second-best chair as though he'd sat in it before. Perhaps he had. Peddlers sit everywhere.

* * *

"I want you to take her face," he said, once he'd drunk half my tea and refused the rest.

I kept my hands flat on the table. "You know what I do."

"I know exactly what you do. Better than most." He set down the cup with a care that suggested his hands were negotiating separately from the rest of him. "That's why I came to you and not to some hedge-witch with her needles and her herbs. You're clean. You take a thing whole, don't you? No tearing. The widow Hollis still smiles at sunsets since you took her grief, and she doesn't know why she stopped crying. Clean work."

The widow Hollis. Word traveled even in a valley built for forgetting. I filed that away to worry about later, in the crowded room where I kept all my later worries.

"Faces are different from grief," I said. "A face is everywhere. It's in mirrors and doorways and other people's smiles. I take the memory, but the world keeps offering her back to you. Every dark-haired girl at the market. You'll feel the loss every time and not know its shape. Grief is heavy but it sits still. A face wanders."

"You think I haven't thought of this?" And there, under the papery voice, under the ruin of him, was something hard and old and clear—the voice of a man who had crossed mountains for a living. "I've thought of nothing else for six days. She's been dead nine years, lad. Nine years I've carried her face, and it's the carrying that's killing me faster than the dying. Every morning I wake up and the first thing I see, behind my eyes, is her at four years old with jam on her chin, and I have to lie there and be her father all over again before I can get up and be nobody. I'm tired of being her father. I want to die light."

I looked at him for a long time. This is the part of my trade nobody pays me for: the looking. Deciding whether the person across from you understands what they're asking, or whether they'll understand it tomorrow, when it's too late and the thing is gone.

"You won't remember you had a daughter," I said. "There will just be a hole. A hole with her name missing from it. You'll know something's gone because men like us always know—but you won't know what, and you won't be able to mourn it properly, because mourning needs a shape."

"Aye." He nodded slowly. "And won't that be a mercy. A hole I can't fill is better than a face I can't put down."

I should have said no. I want that on the record somewhere, in whatever ledger the valley keeps—I should have said no, because he was old and dying and not in his right weight of mind, and because taking a daughter from a man's memory felt like a theft even I couldn't dress up as kindness. But he counted out the coin himself, wrapped in a handkerchief embroidered with small blue birds, and his hands shook less while he did it, and I told myself the story thieves always tell: *it's his choice, not mine.*

"Look at me, then," I said, and he did.

Taking a memory is like lifting a purse in reverse. You reach in—not with hands, with attention, with a kind of listening—and you find the thread of the thing and you pull, gently, the way you'd draw a splinter. Tobias's memory of his daughter's face came out warm. That's the detail I've never told anyone: memories have temperatures. Grief is cold. Joy runs hot. Hers ran hottest of anything I'd ever lifted, a small fierce heat like a coal cupped in palms, and when it settled into me it didn't fade the way stolen things usually do. It sat behind my breastbone and glowed.

Her name was Maren. The memory told me that much without words—a face comes with its context clinging to it, like burrs. Four years old, jam on her chin, laughing at something Tobias was doing with a wooden spoon. I saw her the way he saw her, which meant I saw her the way only a father sees a child: entirely, hopelessly, every smudge beloved.

I gave him the tea again and let him drink it while the hole opened up inside him. He didn't cry. He just went very quiet, the way houses go quiet when someone leaves them, and after a while he said, "Did I ever have children?" in a tone of mild academic interest, and I said, "No, Tobias. You were always alone," which was the kindest lie available, and he nodded and thanked me for the tea.

Then he unwrapped the bundle he'd carried through the fog, and pushed it across my table, and the floor of my life quietly gave way.

* * *

"It's not payment," he said. "Payment was the coin. This—" he patted the oilcloth like a horse he was fond of "—this is a burden. I've carried it forty years, and I'm done carrying. You take burdens, Marchetti. Everyone says so. You take what others can't hold."

Inside the oilcloth was a book.

I want to describe it honestly, because honest description is all I have left of that night. It was bound in bark—actual bark, grey and ridged, from some tree I didn't recognize, stitched with sinew along a spine that curved wrong for a book, curved more like a rib. It was heavier than its size. When I touched it, the scorch-smell I'd noticed on him strengthened, faint and sweet, like the air over a candle that's just been pinched.

"There's a buyer in the lowlands would give you a house for this," Tobias said, watching my hands on it. "Or there would have been, once. Not now. Now it only wants you."

"Now it only wants—Tobias, what does that mean?"

"It means I bought it off a dying woman in the pass in the famine year, and she said it would find its reader, and I've spent forty years making sure it didn't find me." He stood, slowly, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "I read three pages, lad. Forty years ago, three pages, and I shut it and I never slept right again. But I read your face in the doorway tonight and I recognized something. Same look she had. The look of a person in a story who hasn't been told they're in it yet."

He gathered his coat around him and made for the door, and I let him go, because what was I going to do—keep him? Warn him? Against what? At the threshold he paused, and turned, and for a moment his eyes were clear and ancient and terribly sorry for me.

"You take care how you read it," he said. "Books like this aren't read. They open."

Then the fog took him, and I was alone with the thing on my table.

* * *

I did not open it that night. I want credit for that too. I put it in the pantry, behind the flour, under a folded sack, and I went to bed, and lay there listening to my own heart behaving normally, one beat at a time, like a well-behaved thing.

I lasted until the second night.

Understand: I am a professional. Curiosity is a hazard of the trade and I've trained against it the way sailors train against drowning. But there was heat coming off that book. Faint, deniable, the kind of warmth you'd blame on a sun-struck shelf—but I could feel it through the pantry wall, a small insistence, like a cat sitting on the other side of a door. And underneath the warmth, when I held it, something else: the same glow as Maren's face, the stolen coal behind my breastbone, leaning toward the book like a plant toward a window.

My thefts had never done that before. Nothing I'd stolen had ever *done* anything before. In the valley, magic can't follow—it dies in the fog like a moth in smoke—which is precisely why I live here, why generations of my kind have lived here, why the whole valley is a haven of people who want to be ordinary by force. What I do isn't magic. It's just... attention. Listening. Anyone could learn it if they weren't afraid to. That's what I tell myself. That's the story.

So when the stolen warmth of a dead child's face began straining toward a book bound in bark, I did what any sensible thief does with evidence that contradicts his story.

I opened the book to make the evidence stop.

The first pages were recipes. Actually recipes—for breads and salves and one alarming instruction involving a black hen and a Tuesday—written in three different hands across what felt like centuries. Someone's grandmother's grimoire, mundane and homely, and I laughed aloud in my empty kitchen at myself, at Tobias, at forty years of dread over a cookbook.

Then I turned a page and the handwriting changed.

It wasn't ink anymore. Or it was, but ink that moved—no, that's wrong, I need to be precise, because precision is all I have. The ink didn't move. The ink *waited*. It sat on the page with a density the other pages didn't have, black as the underside of a stone, and when I tilted the page toward the lamp the letters cast shadows slightly longer than letters should.

It began, in a language I shouldn't have been able to read but could, the way you can hear your own name through a wall of noise:

*In the turning of the season, when the debt comes due, the Ember shall call home that which was pawned.*

*And the pawned thing shall walk up the mountain wearing a borrowed life, a thief with no shadow and no name.*

*And the court beneath shall know its own, for invisibility is the livery of the Ember's household, and the thief shall pass unseen among the caged fires, being already a flame that has forgotten it is burning.*

*A thief with no shadow and no name,* I read again, and laughed, because half the valley would qualify. Half the people who come to me at night are fleeing toward namelessness like it's a country with better weather. I've spent thirty-one years building mine, brick by invisible brick. No family visitors. No debts. No portraits. I don't sign things. There are maybe four people alive who could pick my face out of a crowd, and I've been meaning to reduce that number for a decade.

Then I turned the page, and stopped laughing.

There was a portrait on the leaf facing the prophecy. Ink, dense and waiting, drawn with a confidence no forger would bother faking. A figure standing in a doorway with a lamp not yet lit, hand raised toward the lamp, caught mid-hesitation. Thin. Watchful. Hair that grew back from the temples like mine does, in the two places mine has always thinned first, the places I check in the mirror without admitting I'm checking. A face drawn from a slight angle, three-quarters, unflattering, exact—the angle of someone who has watched you often and closely and from the shadows, and finally decided to commit you to paper.

It was my face. It was my face the way Maren's was her father's memory of her face: entirely, hopelessly, every line beloved—or claimed. Possessed. Drawn by something that had been looking at me my whole life from somewhere I couldn't turn around fast enough to catch.

And beneath the portrait, in smaller script, in that waiting black ink, a single line I read perhaps forty times before I understood that reading it more times would not change it:

*Rafael Marchetti. Sold before birth. Payment owed at the solstice.*

Eleven days away.

I sat in my kitchen for a long time. The lamp burned. The fog pressed at the windows, doing its faithful work, swallowing all magic, keeping me safe, keeping me nothing. Somewhere behind my breastbone, the warm coal of a dead girl's face flickered—as if in sympathy, or recognition, or greeting.

I have spent my whole life being unremarkable on purpose. I have told myself it was freedom: no name worth stealing, no shadow worth following, no self worth finding. Tonight I learned the truth, which is that I was never hiding. I was stored.

Someone had written me down long before I was born, and the book had simply been waiting—for me, for this kitchen, for eleven days from now.

And the last thing I noticed, before I put it behind the flour again and sat with my head in my hands, was this: on the page after the portrait, at the bottom margin, in fresher ink, there was a second figure sketched. Small, quick, half-finished. A pair of hands.

Ink-stained fingers, holding a pen.

Drawing a map.
