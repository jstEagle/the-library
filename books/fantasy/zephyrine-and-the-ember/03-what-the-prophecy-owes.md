## 3. What the Prophecy Owes

I did not sleep.

This will surprise no one who knows me, since I have made a quiet career out of not sleeping — out of being awake in doorways, awake at windows, awake to the small sounds a house makes when someone is moving through it who should not be there. Sleep is a luxury for people whose faces belong to them. But that night the sleeplessness had a different texture. It wasn't professional. It was the particular white-eyed vigilance of a woman who has found her own portrait in a century-old book and cannot stop looking at it.

The book sat on my table where the peddler's bundle had left it, splayed open to the page with my face on it. My face, or something so close that the difference didn't matter. Ink does something strange to a person. It flattens you, makes you permanent, makes you a fact. I had spent thirty-one years ensuring I was not a fact anywhere — not in a ledger, not in a parish record, not in the memory of anyone who might live long enough to mention me twice. And here was a book that had been bound before my grandmother's grandmother cut her first tooth, saying: *this one. This exact one.*

The candle guttered. I turned the page.

* * *

Here is what I knew about translation: nothing, formally. But a thief learns to read the shape of things without knowing their names. Locks, ledgers, letters left half-burned in a magistrate's study — I'd learned six alphabets by ear, picking out which symbols repeated, which ones only appeared at beginnings, which ones clustered around money. The book's script was old High Vale, the kind carved over church doors and ignored by everyone, and I could worry out perhaps one word in five without help.

One in five is more than enough when the words are the right ones.

*...sold before the breath...* That took me an hour, working backward from a marginal sketch of a swaddled infant with a hollow where its chest should be. *Sold before the breath.* Before breathing. Before the first cry, before lungs, before any of the machinery a person uses to prove they're alive.

There was a word I kept circling and failing to land — a long, coiled thing that recurred seven times on the page, always near references to *the court*, always near *the mountain*, always near a symbol like a flame drawn inside a cage. Seven repetitions. I counted them the way you count the locks on a door you're about to go through, wanting very much for the number to be smaller than it is.

Seven times the book said *soul*.

* * *

You want to know about my mother. Everyone who ever got anything out of me wanted to know about my mother eventually, because I let slip, once per acquaintance, that she was from up-mountain, and then they'd lean in with that soft-faced curiosity people reserve for tragedy. She came down before I was born. She died shortly after. She spoke rarely and when she did it was mostly warnings, delivered in an accent the valley children mimicked until I learned to stop repeating anything she said.

She taught me three things before the fever took her. One: how to hold a face still while someone searches it for a reaction. Two: never sleep facing a door. Three — and this she said only once, gripping my wrist hard enough to leave marks, when I was seven and had asked why we never went to the midsummer festival —

*"Nothing of yours is yours. Remember that, and remember I'm sorry."*

I had filed it under mothers-being-strange. Mothers are strange. Mine was strange in the way of someone performing normalcy from written instructions, but I was seven, and seven-year-olds do not audit their parents. They eat what's put in front of them and assume the fear in the house is weather.

Now, sitting over a book that said my soul had been *sold before the breath*, my mother's grip on my seven-year-old wrist arrived in my memory with the force of a debt collector kicking in a door.

*Nothing of yours is yours.*

She hadn't been being strange. She had been being precise.

* * *

By the third candle I had the shape of it, if not every clause. The passage ran like this, as best I could render it, filling gaps with inference and hating every gap:

*A bargain struck beneath the boughs of the world. For grain against the starving years, for the valley kept, the woman heavy with child gave over that which the child would carry: the flame unlit, the soul unsung, to be held in pledge at the Ember Throne until the turning of the season, when the debt comes due and the pledged one ascends.*

The starving years. I knew those, secondhand — the famine of sixty-odd years past, the one the valley still didn't talk about, the one that emptied four farms and filled the graveyard behind the chapel with stones instead of names because nobody had the strength to cut letters. Every valley family had its version of that winter. Ours was that my mother came down the mountain during it, already pregnant, already alone, and married a tanner with kind eyes within the month.

I'd always thought she was fleeing the famine. It had seemed tidy. Things flee toward safety; that's the whole grammar of refugees.

But the book proposed a different grammar. Not fleeing the famine. *Settling* it. Trading something for grain against the starving years — and the something wasn't hers to trade, or rather, it was hers precisely because it was inside her, the way you can pawn a ring on your own hand.

The soul unsung. Mine. Pledged before I drew breath, held at the Ember Throne — the court beneath the mountain, the place the valley's fog existed specifically to keep out, the place my whole careful architecture of invisibility had been built, apparently, without my knowledge, as a fortress around property that was not mine.

I laughed. I'll admit it. One short, ugly sound, alone at my table at what must have been three in the morning, laughing at a book. Because the alternative to laughing was putting my fist through the table, and I needed the table.

* * *

Here is the thing about discovering your identity was never yours: it doesn't feel like grief at first. Grief requires that something be gone. This was worse. Nothing was gone. Everything was exactly where it had always been — my hands, my habits, my talent for being forgotten — it was simply *not mine*, the way a rented room contains your whole life while belonging to someone else. Thirty-one years of being Rafael Marchetti, thief, unremarkable, safe. And the book said: tenant. Occupant. Caretaker of a soul that had a lien on it older than my name.

Vertigo is the honest word. I gripped the edge of the table like a sailor in weather.

And under the vertigo, fury — not the hot kind, which passes, but the cold kind, which organizes. I was furious at my mother, and then immediately furious at myself for being furious at a dead woman who had gripped my wrist and said *I'm sorry* and meant, all along, *this*. She hadn't betrayed me. Or she had, but the way you betray someone when the alternative is watching a valley starve, which is to say: she had done arithmetic, and I was the remainder. I could feel both truths at once, the anger and its retraction, and they ground against each other like millstones, and between them I suppose some understanding would eventually be ground out, but not that night. That night there was just noise.

Because there was one line left. The last line of the passage, the one I'd saved for last the way you save the worst lock for last, and when I finally worried it loose, the candles were down to stubs and the fog outside the window had gone grey with almost-dawn.

*Eleven days before the solstice, the debt awakens.*

I looked at the calendar chalked beside my door. I keep one because a thief who loses track of dates is a thief who walks into a magistrate's appointment. I counted twice, the way you count money twice when the amount frightens you.

Eleven days.

* * *

*

I did the sensible thing, which is to say I tried to disprove it.

I spent the morning doing what I do when a job goes sideways: gathering corroboration, checking angles, looking for the flaw in my reading. I took the book to Old Marta, who pretends to sell herbs and actually sells gossip, and asked her, casually, what she knew about the famine. She gave me the standard version — bad winters, blighted grain, the mountain silent — and then, unprompted, because Marta cannot keep a secret longer than a sneeze: "My gran always said it ended too quick, you know. One day everyone's burying children, next day the wagons come up from who-knows-where, full to the boards. Never did find out who paid for it." She cackled. "Probably the devil."

Close enough, Marta.

I walked the fog line at the valley's edge, where the mist stands like a wall that has agreed to be weather, and I felt — for the first time in my life — that the fog was not protecting me. It was *containing* me. A larder. A pen. All these years I'd thought of myself as a fox gone to ground, and the book suggested I was goods in storage, kept cool and dark until collection.

I even tried the simplest test. There's a game thieves play, a superstition: look at your shadow at noon and see if it looks back. Valley children do it for luck. I stood in the empty market square at high noon and looked down, and my shadow was there, faint as everything about me, thin grey on pale stone — and I hated it, suddenly, ferociously, this smudge that had followed me my whole life, this proof that I cast *anything* at all. The prophecy called its subject one *with no shadow and no name*. Well. I had a shadow. Thin, but present. Maybe the ink had flattered me. Maybe the whole thing was a peddler's forgery, a dying man's last con, trading a cursed book for the sweetest memory he'd ever taste—

Except I had watched him touch that book. I had watched his cracked fingers smooth the cover the way you'd stroke a sleeping child's hair. Nobody strokes a forgery like that.

* * *

*

Evening found me back at the table with the book and a bottle of plum brandy I'd been saving for a celebration that now seemed unlikely ever to occur. I had moved from denial into negotiation, which in my case meant planning. If the debt comes due in eleven days, what does *collection* look like? Does something come down the mountain? Do I walk up? The passage said *the pledged one ascends* — ascends, present tense, an instruction, not a threat. Which meant either the book expected me to go willingly, or it had never considered the possibility that I wouldn't, and both readings offended me equally.

I poured the brandy and did not drink it, because I wanted my hands steady, and read the passage again, and again, hunting for loopholes the way I hunt for wards. *Until the turning of the season.* Debts have terms. Terms have conditions. Somewhere in the seven repetitions of the word *soul*, somewhere in the clauses I couldn't yet read, there was a lock, and every lock opens, and most of them open from the inside.

That was the plan, then, forming itself in the cold part of me that doesn't feel vertigo or fury, only geometry: learn the rest of the language. Learn what I owed and to whom and whether the contract could be — the word arrived in my head with a thief's grin already attached — *renegotiated*.

I was reaching for the book to turn the page when I heard footsteps on the stair. Unhurried. Familiar. The particular light tread of someone who has been told, more than once, that the third stair creaks, and who takes it precisely wrong anyway, out of affection rather than carelessness.

Wren.

Dinner. Tonight. I had said yes to dinner nine days ago, back when my largest problem was rent and my face belonged to me, and Wren — punctual as sunrise, stubborn as ink — was climbing my stairs carrying, I could hear, the ceramic clink of a borrowed dish, probably stewed something, probably still talking to themselves about contour lines.

I looked at the book. Open on my table. Portrait of my face staring at the ceiling. Prophecy spread out beneath it in letters like scars.

I am, by profession, excellent at hiding things in the four seconds before a door opens. But my hands wouldn't take the book off the table. They stayed flat on the page — on the hollow-chested infant, on the seven coils of the word *soul* — as if holding down a map in wind, as if letting go meant the whole future it described would blow away and scatter, and God help me, after eleven days of counting, some traitorous part of me wasn't sure it wanted it to.

The knock came.
