## 4. The Date That Isn't

There are three things I do well: vanish in a crowd, lift a memory without leaving a mark, and ruin a good thing before it ruins me.

The third one I've been practicing the longest.

Tonight I was supposed to be practicing none of them. Tonight Wren Vale was coming to dinner—Wren, who maps coastlines for a living and has hands that are permanently stained to the second knuckle with iron-gall ink, who once argued with me for an hour about whether the western fog line had moved forty paces east over the last decade, and was right. Wren, who laughs like they're slightly surprised by it every time.

I'd spent the afternoon doing everything wrong on purpose. Burned the onions because I kept drifting to the window. Set two places at the table and then stood there staring at the second plate like it might bite me. Two plates is a claim. Two plates says *I expect you to come back*, and expectations are debts, and I have recently learned—I have *always* known, in the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked—that debts are how I end.

So there's the context for my behavior this evening, if anyone ever asks: a man with a book of prophecies hidden under a loose floorboard, eleven days left on his own soul, and a pot of onions burnt black because love makes me careless.

*Not love,* I told myself firmly, scraping the pan. *Infatuation. A chemical accident. It passes.*

It had been passing for eight months.

* * *

Wren knocked at dusk, which was punctual for Wren, whose relationship with time was best described as diplomatic. I opened the door and there they were, backlit by the last grey light, ink on their fingers and flour somehow on their collar—had they baked something? They'd baked something—and that open, unguarded expression people get when they're glad to see you and haven't yet decided whether to hide it.

"You smell like a fire," Wren said.

"I cooked."

"Tragically, from the sound of it." They held up the basket. "I brought bread. In case of catastrophe."

"Catastrophe averted, then. Barely."

They came in the way they always did, like the house was already half theirs—coat over the chair, comment about the crooked picture frame, the same comment they make every time, because the frame *is* crooked and I keep not fixing it because it's the only flaw in my house I can afford. I watched them move through my rooms and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *this person is going to be in the story of my life. I just don't know yet whether as a chapter or an epitaph.*

We ate. The bread saved the meal; my competence ended at theft and onions. Wren talked about a commission—a survey of the old shepherd paths above the treeline, which nobody had mapped since before the valley stopped letting people up there, and which Wren had accepted against everyone's advice.

"Above the treeline," I said. My voice came out strange.

"It pays well."

"It kills cartographers. The last three surveys—the maps come back blank or the surveyors don't."

"They come back *blank?*"

"Fogged. Exposed. Like the mountain develops them and doesn't like what it sees." I smiled to take the edge off, and it didn't work, and Wren was looking at me with those eyes that noticed things for a living. "Don't take it."

"It's money, Rafael."

"It's a mountain."

They laughed. "You're being strange tonight. Stranger than usual."

And here's where I should tell you about the book, because it was under the floorboard six feet from where we sat, and I could feel it the way you feel a tooth that needs pulling. All day I'd been turning pages in my head, reading and rereading the passages the peddler's dying translation had unlocked for me, and every rereading made it worse, and I will tell you why.

*The thief comes with no shadow and no name.*

That part was mine. That part I'd already swallowed.

But there was a second figure in the prophecy. I'd missed them the first time, skimming in panic, hunting only for my own face. The second time I'd read it properly, by candlelight, with my heart going like a fist on a door:

*And the one who draws the way shall walk beside the shadowless,*
*a guide with ink-stained hands,*
*who leads the thief to the court below the mountain*
*and cannot be led home again.*

Ink-stained hands.

I looked at Wren's fingers, dark to the second knuckle, wrapped around my cup, and felt the floor drop out of the evening.

* * *

I want to be honest about the next hour, even though honesty is not my native language. For one whole hour I did nothing. We washed dishes shoulder to shoulder. Wren told a story about a client who wanted a map of a place that didn't exist and refused to accept the refund. I laughed in the right places. And all the while some cold clerk in the back of my skull was running figures.

Option one: tell them. Say *there's a book, it says you die, or worse—it says you lead me somewhere and never come back.* But telling them made it real, made them part of it, tied a thread between us that prophecy could pull taut. Maybe the prophecy needed witnesses. Maybe saying it aloud was signing it.

Option two: push them away. Tonight, cruelly, cleanly. Be the coldest version of myself, the one I keep oiled for exactly these emergencies. If they weren't beside me, they couldn't draw the way. If the prophecy wanted a mapmaker, I would see to it the mapmaker never got near me again.

Option two was safer. Option two was also unthinkable, and I knew it was unthinkable, and I hated myself for knowing it, because that meant I'd already chosen, hadn't I? Longing had outvoted self-protection months ago, quietly, without calling a vote.

The clerk in my skull noted the result and filed it under *liabilities.*

"Rafael." Wren was drying the last plate, watching me. "You've gone somewhere."

"Nowhere."

"Liar. You do this thing where your face goes very still and your eyes go somewhere else. You've been doing it all night." They set the plate down. "Since before I came in, actually. There's flour on the windowsill and ash in the sink and you flinched when I said *mountain.* I draw terrain for a living, Marchetti. I know a landslide when I see the ground start to go."

God, they were observant. Of course they were observant. Prophecies recruit well.

"There's something I've been reading," I said. Which was true. "An old text. It's—strange. Superstitious. Probably nonsense."

"But?"

"But it mentions people. By description. People who might be—" I stopped. Careful, Rafael. "People I might know."

Wren waited. Wren was good at waiting; maps take patience.

And here is what I finally understand about myself in that moment, standing in my own kitchen with the best thing in my life drying cups across the counter from me: I have spent thirty years believing invisibility was safety, but invisibility is just loneliness wearing armor. Every memory I steal leaves a hole in someone, and I patch my own holes with other people's scraps, and I call it survival. It is survival. It is also the smallest possible life, and Wren standing there with ink-stained hands and flour on their collar was the largest thing I'd ever been offered, and the prophecy said *cannot be led home again*, and—

And the solstice was eleven days away, and after it none of this might matter anyway.

"The mountain," I said. "Something's up there. Something older than the valley. And whatever it is, it's coming to collect something from me at the turn of the season, and the text says—" my voice did something embarrassing, so I steadied it—"it says whoever walks with me goes up and doesn't come down. So if anyone ever asks you to climb it, Wren. Don't."

Silence. The fire ticked. Wren looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for mockery, or fear, or the gentle careful voice people use when they've decided you're ill.

What Wren said was: "Is that why you haven't slept? You look like you owe money."

"That is—yes. Essentially, yes."

"To whom?"

"That's the part I can't say yet."

They nodded slowly, and then did the worst thing they could possibly have done, which was believe me. No argument, no demand for proof. Just Wren leaning against my counter with their stained hands folded, saying, "All right. Then here's what we're not going to do: you're not going to handle it alone, and I'm not going to pretend I wasn't going to take that commission regardless. So either you let me help, or you spend the next while lying badly to someone who reads lies for a hobby too."

"We're not the same profession."

"No. Mine's honest." A pause, and then, quieter, with the humor stripped out: "Rafael. What happens at the turn of the season?"

And I looked at them—at the ink and the flour and the terrible stubborn warmth of them—and I made my choice, the coward's compromise, the half-truth I'd defend later as protection: I told them enough to warn them and not enough to bind them. A debt. A deadline. A place beneath the mountain. I kept back the book, the portrait with my face, and most of all I kept back the words *ink-stained hands*, which sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

Because here's the arithmetic I'd settled on, the lie I told myself while looking them in the eye: if Wren never hears themselves in the prophecy, maybe the prophecy can't find them. If I keep them ignorant, maybe ignorance is a kind of fog, and the fog keeps things out. It was a bad theory. Even then I suspected it was a bad theory. But longing had won the vote, and cowards write very persuasive minority reports.

* * *

Wren stayed late. Later than usual. We sat by the fire and they sketched while I pretended to read—actually I watched their hand move, quick and sure, laying down lines on paper the way other people lay down bets. They drew my street. They drew my crooked window. They drew, absently, without seeming to notice, a shape at the top of the page: a mountain, and beneath its peak, small and precise, a doorway.

"Wren," I said. My mouth was dry. "Why did you draw that?"

They looked at the page, surprised at themselves. "Habit. Terrain wants drawing." Then they frowned at their own fingers, at the ink. "Odd. I've never drawn that mountain before. Nobody has. There's no reliable survey of it anywhere in the guild archive—it resists mapping, did you know that? Every attempt comes back wrong. Distances that don't hold. Paths that redraw themselves." They looked up at me, and their eyes had gone bright with the particular fever I'd seen when they talk about impossible coastlines. "Which means nobody's ever drawn it *first*. Do you understand what that would be worth? To be the first map of a place no map has survived?"

"Do you hear yourself? *No map has survived.*"

"That's just it," Wren said softly, looking at the little doorway under the peak. "Survived by whom? Maybe the maps came back fine and the *surveyors* didn't." They tapped the page. "Someone's going to map that mountain eventually, Rafael. It might as well be someone who knows what they're doing."

I should have taken the sketch and fed it to the fire. That's the version of me the prophecy was counting on never appearing—the one who burns the page, breaks the date, moves towns, starts over. I have done it before. It takes four hours and a good alibi.

Instead I said, "Finish your tea," like a man negotiating with fate over the price of a cup.

When Wren finally left, near midnight, they paused in the doorway—my crooked doorway, under my crooked frame—and said, "Whatever you're carrying. Eleven days, you said?"

"Something like that."

"When it gets heavy enough to share, share it. That's all." And they were gone into the dark, footsteps sure even on the unlit lane, because of course they were; Wren Vale has never once needed light to find their way.

I went inside and pulled up the floorboard and took out the book and read the line again, though God knows I had it memorized:

*a guide with ink-stained hands, who leads the thief to the court below the mountain and cannot be led home again.*

Then I turned the page, hunting desperately through the marginalia, the annotations, the cramped scholarly hand of some dead translator, for any loophole. Any conditional. Any *unless.* And that's when I found the note I hadn't seen before, squeezed into the gutter in brown faded script, so small I'd read past it three times:

*The way in is drawn twice. Once by the guide. Once by the mountain itself, which also holds a pen.*

Underneath, someone long dead had added a single word in a shakier hand:

*Run.*

Outside, far off toward the ridge, something answered—a sound too low to be thunder and too rhythmic to be anything alive, like a heartbeat under the floor of the world.

Eleven days. Ten, now.

And Wren had already promised to take the commission.
