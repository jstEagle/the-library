## 5. Eleven Days

I have drawn the coastlines of four kingdoms, the riverbends of two empires, and the underside of a bridge in Lower Marchen that no one else has ever bothered to map, mostly because they were wise enough not to look at it twice. I am, by trade and temperament, a person who notices where things are. Where things are *wrong*.

Rafael Marchetti was wrong in eleven distinct ways by the time the soup was on the table.

I counted. It's what I do.

One: the book on the shelf, spine out, bound in bark that no tannery in the valley would touch. Two: the way Rafael hadn't touched it all evening but had touched the shelf beside it twice, like checking a wound through cloth. Three: shadows under the eyes deep enough to survey. Four: salt instead of sugar in my tea, which Rafael noticed one sip too late and apologized for with the particular horror of someone who prides themselves on precision. Five: the clocks. All three of them, wound forward or back, I couldn't tell—just that none of them agreed, and Rafael kept glancing at them like conspirators.

Six through ten were smaller things. A thumb pressed white against a mug. A laugh arriving half a beat late to its own joke. The window shut against a mild night. The knife set down crosswise on the bread board, which I have never once seen Rafael do, because Rafael arranges cutlery the way other people pray.

Eleven: Rafael said my name twice in one sentence. *Wren, Wren, pass the pepper.* People only do that when they're memorizing you.

I know what it looks like when someone is memorizing you, because I've done it. From maps. You hold the shape of a coastline in your mind so hard that when it changes, some part of you changes with it.

"Pepper," I said, passing it. "Rafael. What's the book?"

The spoon stopped halfway to Rafael's mouth, which told me everything before any answer could. I watched the whole calculation happen behind those careful eyes—the weighing of what to tell me, what to spare me, what lie would be kindest and whether kindness was even the right currency tonight. Rafael was very good at this. That was the problem. Nobody gets that good at lying without practicing on themselves for years.

"It's nothing," Rafael said. "A peddler's junk."

"I've seen peddler's junk. Peddler's junk doesn't make people wind their clocks wrong."

"You don't know I wound them wrong."

"Wren's rule of cartography," I said. "If every landmark disagrees with your chart, the chart is wrong. Or—" I tapped the table, "—the landmarks are hiding something."

For a moment I thought Rafael would laugh. That was the old Rafael, the one I'd met over a mislabeled survey of the eastern fens, the one who laughed like something being let out of a cage briefly, then put back. But the new Rafael, the eleven-days Rafael, just set down the spoon and looked at me with an expression I'd never had directed at me before. It took me a moment to name it, because I'd mostly seen it from the outside, in mirrors, before I got better at mirrors.

It was goodbye.

"All right," I said, before anything could be decided on my behalf. "Before you do whatever this is—I want it on record that I'm not stupid."

"I never said—"

"You didn't have to. You've spent the whole dinner deciding what I can handle, which is a thing people only do to people they've already measured for a coffin." I pushed my bowl aside. The soup was oversalted anyway. "So. Show me the book, or tell me to leave, but pick one, and pick it now, because I've had a long day of drawing coastlines and I'd rather be frightened than managed."

Silence. The clock that was wound wrong ticked in a rhythm that suggested it had opinions about all this.

Then Rafael got up, took the bark-bound book off the shelf, and put it in my hands as if handing me something live.

* * *

The portrait was me.

No—not me. Me the way prophecies mean people: close enough to hurt. Ink on bark-paper, faded to the color of weak tea, a figure with ink-stained fingers and a satchel of charts, standing at the mouth of a road that coiled up a mountain I knew only as a silhouette everyone in the valley pretended not to look at. Beneath it, in a hand older than the valley's oldest house:

*the mapmaker who draws the way.*

"There's more," Rafael said quietly. "There's a lot more, and none of it is good, and most of it is about me, and I was going to handle it alone."

"You were going to *handle* it alone," I repeated. "Like weather."

"Like everything. It's what I'm good at."

"Rafael." I set the book down very carefully, because my hands wanted to shake and I refused to give them the satisfaction. "How long have we been—whatever this is? Dinners, walks, the thing with the lanterns at the harvest fair that we agreed not to define because defining it felt like jinxing it?"

"Eight months," Rafael said, instantly, and then looked surprised at the instantness.

"Eight months. And in eight months, have I once needed you to decide what I can handle? Have I ever given you the impression that I fold under information?"

"No," Rafael admitted. "You gave me the impression you catalogued information and filed it somewhere terrifying."

"*Exactly.* So here is what I've catalogued in the last ten minutes." I stood, because thinking on my feet is the only way I think, and started pacing the little kitchen, gesturing the way I gesture at drafting tables. "One. Whatever that book says, it says the mountain is involved, and the mountain has been involved in exactly zero valley affairs in living memory, which means this is unprecedented, which is either terrible or the most interesting thing that will ever happen to us. Two. You haven't slept in—I'd guess—four days, based on the shadow depth, which means whatever's coming has a date attached. Three. You keep looking at the door when you think I won't notice, and you keep looking at me when you think I also won't notice, and those are the looks of someone planning a departure they feel guilty about."

Rafael opened their mouth. Closed it. I'll treasure that expression until I die.

"Four," I said, softer now, stopping in front of the chair. "You were going to leave me a note, weren't you? Not even a note. A *map*. Something with the route to safety marked on it, so I'd follow it and never know why."

The silence confirmed it. Of course it did. Rafael thought in maps too; we just used them for different kinds of rescue.

"I hate you," I said, which was a lie, and both of us let it stand for a second because it was holding the door closed against truer things. "I hate you so much. Sit down. We're going to talk about the mountain."

"We're not going anywhere near the mountain."

"We?" Rafael said. And there it was—that single pronoun doing more damage than the whole prophecy.

"Yes, *we*," I said. "Because I've read the rest of that page, Rafael, even upside down, even while you were watching me, because reading things quickly is another thing I'm good at. It says the mapmaker draws the way. Which means if anyone alive is written into this disaster, it's me, and I would rather find out what that sentence costs while sitting next to you than alone with a note."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being seen: it feels exactly like being caught.

I'd built my whole life out of being useful and unremarkable, the same as Rafael, though I'd never had the vocabulary for it until I watched Rafael do it with infinitely more skill. I drew maps. Maps are humble things. They say *here is the world, arranged, manageable*, and nobody asks the map how it feels about the territory. I dated rarely and left gracefully. I kept my fingers ink-stained and my history unremarkable, and I told myself that a life well-surveyed was a life well-lived.

And then Rafael slid that book across the table, and read me the parts about a soul sold before birth, a debt due at solstice, a thief with no shadow and no name—and I sat there and listened to the person I loved describe their own annihilation in the flat voice of someone reading someone else's obituary, and every instinct I had said *run*. Not from Rafael. From the knowing. From the terrible weight of being inside something instead of charting it from a safe altitude.

But Rafael looked up at the end of it, at me, waiting—actually waiting—for me to bolt. I could see the brace in their shoulders, the years of practice at being left.

"Say something," Rafael whispered, "even if it's goodbye."

"The mountain," I said. "Tell me about the mountain."

"That's not—"

"Everything starts with terrain, Rafael. Always. Before strategy, before courage, before any of it—you learn the ground. So tell me the ground."

So they told me. The court beneath it, the Ember, the throne, the turning season. Eleven days. Their voice steadied as they went, the way voices do when they finally stop circling the thing and land on it, and I listened the way I listen to travelers' accounts, which is skeptically, hungrily, taking notes in my head.

When they finished, the wrong-wound clock ticked through a full minute of nothing.

"There's no route," Rafael said finally. "I checked. Nobody climbs it. Maps that go up there come back blank or don't come back. The fog line stops at the valley's edge like it hit a wall, and past that wall is the court's country, and the court doesn't invite surveyors."

"Mm," I said, and got up, and fetched my satchel.

"What are you doing."

"My work," I said, kneeling on the kitchen floor and unrolling paper across the flagstones. "Sit down. This is going to take a while and my knees need witnesses."

I pinned the corners with the salt cellar and the pepper mill and began to draw from memory: the valley, the fog line, the mountain rising out of it in the hatched strokes I use for places no one has measured. When I reached the slopes, my pen slowed. Not because I didn't know the shape. Because I did—better than I should, better than any map had ever taught me, in the way you know the layout of a room you've dreamed about.

"Wren," Rafael said slowly, "why does your mountain look like the drawings in the prophecy?"

"Because," I said, hatching the ridge above the tree line, "for the last six years I have had recurring dreams about climbing a mountain I've never climbed, and being the sensible person I am, I filed that under 'too much cheese before bed' and moved on with my life. And now there's a book that says I draw the way." My pen found the summit and stopped. There was, I discovered, a door there. In my dream there had always been a door. "So either the prophecy is true, or my subconscious has excellent taste in literature, and honestly at this point I'll take either."

"That route doesn't exist," Rafael said. "No map survives it."

"Right," I said. "Good. I've always wanted to draw one that does."

And then I made the mistake of looking up.

Rafael was looking at me the way—no. Let me get this right, because I want it recorded accurately, since accuracy is the whole of my religion. Rafael wasn't looking at me with gratitude, or relief, or any of the soft things I might have expected. Rafael was looking at me with *fear*. Raw, undiluted, the fear of a person who has just realized that the thing they were protecting by leaving alone is now coming along, and that love, it turns out, is not a shield but an exposure. Every secret you keep keeps you safe. Every person who sees you is a door the world can walk through.

I know, because I'd been afraid of the exact same thing for eight months. Being seen is the risk you take. It's the toll on every bridge worth crossing.

"Eleven days," I said. "That's enough time to draft three routes, test the fog-line crossings, and argue with you about gear, which I'm looking forward to enormously. It's not enough time to be gentle about it, so I'll say the plain thing once and never again: I'm coming with you. Not because a book says so. Because you were going to leave me a map instead of a conversation, and I intend to personally supervise every inch of the real one."

Rafael laughed then—the caged-laugh, but the cage door was open, and something came out of it that might have been crying or might have been laughing wearing crying's coat. They crossed the kitchen and knelt down on the cold flags next to me, among the salt and pepper and the unfinished soup, and put their forehead against mine.

"You understand," Rafael said, very quietly, "that whatever's up there, it knows about you now. You're in the margins. You drew yourself into this."

"I was always in it," I said. "I just hadn't finished the survey. There's a difference between walking into a trap and mapping one, Rafael. One of them gets to bring instruments."

We stayed like that a while, kneeling on the floor of a small house in a valley that forgets, two people who had made an art of being overlooked, looking directly at each other for possibly the first time. It was terrifying. I recommend it.

Then Rafael pulled back, wiped their face with the heel of their hand, and became practical, which is my favorite of their disguises.

"The fog line," they said. "Dawn crossings are safest. If we're really doing this—"

"We are."

"—then tomorrow we walk east and see how close the edge lets us get. I want measurements before you commit ink to anything."

I grinned and turned my paper over to the clean side, and my pen was already moving, laying down the first true line of the route—valley floor, fog line, the black shoulder of the slope—when I noticed something that cooled the sweat on my neck considerably.

The mountain I'd drawn had paths on it. Faint ones, hatched light, the kind I use for trails reported but never verified. And I had not drawn them.

They had come out of the pen anyway—eleven days early, in my own hand, in ink I hadn't chosen—as if somewhere under the mountain, something had felt the point of my nib touch the paper, and had begun, patiently, to draw back.
