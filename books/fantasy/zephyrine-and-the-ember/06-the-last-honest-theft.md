## 6. The Last Honest Theft

There is an art to choosing a mark, and the first rule of that art is: never choose someone you know.

I broke that rule on a Tuesday, because Aldous Ferren was the richest man in the valley and because I was eleven days from needing a small fortune, and because — if I'm being honest with myself, which I try to avoid as a general policy — because he deserved it.

Aldous owned half the mill rights, all of the toll roads, and, through a chain of loans so tangled no one had ever successfully followed it to the end, most of the valley's debts. He was not cruel in the way of storybook villains. He didn't kick dogs. He simply regarded every other human being as a line item, and line items, as everyone knows, exist to be reduced. Three families had lost their farms to him in the last five years. The widow Serafina — whose first kiss I had carried off for the price of two weeks' bread — had nearly been the fourth, saved only by her late husband's foresight in hiding the deed somewhere Aldous's clerks never found it. She'd told me about it while I worked, the way lonely people do, trusting me with everything except the thing I'd actually come to take.

That's the trouble with knowing your mark. You learn things you can't unlearn.

But here is what I told myself, walking up the gravel drive of Ferren House under a sky the color of dishwater: I have never once stolen from anyone who didn't hand me the knife. People give me their memories freely. That's the whole trick. They *want* to be rid of them — the grief, the guilt, the shame, the ten minutes of weakness they'd rather die than confess. I don't take anything anyone wasn't already desperate to lose.

Aldous Ferren, I reasoned, would be desperate to lose something. Rich men always are. And whatever he paid me would be worth more than whatever I took, because rich men always overpay to feel light again.

I've told that story to myself several times now. It gets less convincing with each telling.

* * *

Getting into Ferren House required no magic at all, only patience and a delivery cart. The valley's richest man employed exactly one housekeeper, a near-deaf woman named Petra who waved me through the service door because she'd been expecting the wine merchant, and the wine merchant and I both wore caps and both had the good fortune of being invisible to anyone who wasn't looking for us specifically. Which is, I've come to understand, my entire nature. In the fog of the valley, nobody sees Rafael Marchetti unless I make myself seen. I used to think this was a talent.

Now I knew it was a leash.

The house was enormous and mostly empty, which is the way of rich houses. Rooms furnished beautifully and never entered. Hallways built to be long rather than useful. Aldous had outlived his wife — there had been a wife; even I hadn't known that — and his children had scattered to cities where the fog couldn't reach and neither could he, apparently, because the portraits in the gallery were dusty in a way that suggested no one stood in front of them anymore, least of all him.

I found him in the study at the back of the ground floor, asleep in a leather chair with an open ledger sliding off his knee. Seventy, maybe older. Liver-spotted hands. A face like a collapsed tent — the structure still visible, but nothing holding it up.

Here is the technical part, since you're wondering how it's done. It isn't done with tools. There's no needle, no incantation, nothing you could confiscate. You sit close enough to smell the person's breath, you put your fingers against their temple like a physician taking a pulse, and you *listen*. Memories have texture. A first kiss is silk. A funeral is wet stone. A betrayal has edges, fine as paper cuts, and people carry those around for decades without realizing why they bleed.

You listen until you find the thing they're holding at arm's length. Then you ask it, silently, to come away. And because it wants to — because it has wanted to for years — it does.

I sat down across from Aldous Ferren and put my fingers to his temple, and I listened.

What I expected: guilt about the farms. Some specific cruelty, some winter when he'd turned a family out into the snow, some moment he replayed at three in the morning trying to convince himself they'd deserved it. That's the usual currency of rich men. I would take it, he would pay me triple what it was worth, and Wren and I would leave this valley with our packs full and our pockets heavier than they'd ever been.

What I found instead was a door.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left now. Every memory I've ever touched has been *human*. Small, warm, mortal. Even the worst ones — even the memory of a man watching his brother drown and doing nothing — even that was made of ordinary materials. Grief and fear and shame, the common clay of a life.

The thing inside Aldous Ferren's head was not made of those materials.

It sat behind the ordinary memories like a vault behind a wallpapered wall. When I brushed against it, it did not have texture. It had *temperature*. Heat, specifically — a deep, banked heat, like pressing your palm against a stove door. And behind that heat, faintly, something was moving.

I should have stopped. Let me say that plainly, because whatever else happens in this account, I want it on record that I knew. The book bound in bark was sitting on my kitchen table eleven miles away, and its ink portrait wore my face, and I had read — three times, in increasing horror — the words describing the court beneath the mountain, the halls of ember-light, the throne where stolen souls burn in rows. I knew what lived under this valley. I knew it wanted me.

And I opened the vault anyway.

Because that's what I am. Before I'm a coward, before I'm a victim, before I'm whatever the prophecy says I'll be — I am a thief. And a thief, shown a locked door in a rich man's head, will pick it every time. Curiosity is just greed wearing a better coat.

* * *

The memory came away clean. They always do, when they want to go, and this one *wanted* — God help me, it strained toward my hands like a dog toward the one person who ever fed it.

Aldous woke as it left him. He made a sound I have never heard a human being make before or since — not a cry, more like the note a door makes when its hinges finally give. Then his face went smooth, blissfully smooth, the way all my marks' faces go, and he blinked at me with the mild confusion of a man who has misplaced something and cannot remember what, and knows only that he is lighter.

"Who are you?" he asked, pleasantly. "Are we finished?"

"We're finished," I said, and my voice came out wrong. Scraped. "You'll find payment in the usual way. Triple."

He nodded, serene, and went back to sleep sitting up, and I sat there in the lamplight of a dying man's study holding a memory that was burning my hands where there were no burns, and I looked into it.

This is what Aldous Ferren saw, forty years ago, when he was young and hungry and followed a rumor about gold in the mountain's roots farther than any sensible man should have gone:

He saw a crack in the rock, breathing.

Not wind. *Breathing* — in and out, slow, patient, the respiration of something vast asleep or pretending to be. He squeezed through, because Aldous Ferren has never in his life declined to squeeze through a crack if there was value on the far side, and he came out into —

Light. Not firelight. Not sunlight. A light that had never known either, amber and deep and endless, rising from below in columns, and he understood dimly that each column was a *cage*, and each cage held a flame, and each flame was — and here forty years of forgetting had worn the word down to a nub, but I could feel its ghost in the memory, the shape of what he'd known and fled —

Each flame was a person. Or had been. Souls, hung in the dark like lanterns in an orchard, thousands of them, burning in ordered rows, and between the rows moved figures in moth-colored silk who paid him no attention at all, because he was nothing, because he was beneath notice, because he was a mouse who had wandered into a cathedral.

And at the center of it all, past the orchard of burning souls, a throne. A brazier tall as a church tower, iron and black, and within it a fire that did not flicker like fire flickers. It pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically. Like something keeping time with a heart it remembered having.

Aldous — young, greedy, already turning to run — heard a voice. Not spoken. *Felt*, the way you feel thunder in your ribs:

*You brought nothing. You owe nothing. Go, and forget, and count your coins, little man. But tell no one, or the debt comes due in ways you cannot pay.*

So he went. And he forgot — or thought he had forgotten, buried it so deep under ledgers and land deeds and forty years of acquisition that even he didn't know it was there, a coal under the floorboards of his life, warming the whole house of him in a way he never understood and never examined. No wonder he bought everything in sight. No wonder he lay awake at night. Some part of him had been trying to fill a room-shaped hole with money, and the room was in a mountain.

* * *

I walked out of Ferren House through the front door, because after that the servant's entrance seemed absurd. Nobody stopped me. Nobody ever stops me.

Outside, the fog was coming down the valley in its evening procession, swallowing the fields one fence post at a time, and I stood on the gravel drive with my hands shaking and understood three things with terrible clarity.

First: the court beneath the mountain is real. Not prophecy-speak, not metaphor, not the fever dream of some bark-bound madman centuries dead. Real, populated, and lit by souls in cages — plural, thousands — which meant the debt coming due for me was not even the worst thing happening under that mountain. It was merely the most personal.

Second: the court lets people see it. Aldous walked in and walked out alive. A mouse in a cathedral, and they let the mouse go. That is not carelessness. That is a *message*, sent forty years ahead of time to whoever was fated to go digging through Aldous Ferren's skull — and I knew, standing there in the failing light with my skin crawling, exactly who the message was addressed to. The thief with no shadow and no name. Me. They had put a signpost in a rich man's memory the way you'd leave bread crumbs on a trail, and I had eaten the bread crumbs, and I was going to walk into the cathedral with my hat in my hands like a good little mouse.

Third, and worst: I wasn't even angry about it. Some vast, cold, patient thing had arranged my whole catastrophe like a dinner party, and my dominant emotion, standing on that gravel drive, was *relief*. Because the memory was proof. Proof there was a throne, proof there was a soul of mine hanging in an orchard of souls, proof that if I climbed fast enough and stole well enough, I might get *myself* back. I had spent thirty years being nothing to everybody, and now the universe itself had confirmed it: I was something. I was somebody's property. And I wanted it back so badly my teeth ached.

Greed, curdling into dread, curdling into greed again. That's the recipe. Write it down.

* * *

Wren was waiting at my cottage when I got home after midnight, which was not the plan, because Wren didn't know about the plan, because I had decided — wisely, I thought — to tell Wren nothing. Cartographers draw borders. They don't get marched across them.

They were sitting on my doorstep with a lantern and a paper-wrapped parcel of honey cakes, and when they saw my face they stood up slowly, the way you stand when you've spotted the edge of something in a map that shouldn't be there.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," Wren said. "Or robbed one."

"I robbed a rich man," I said. My voice was steady. I was proud of that. "We have money for the journey now."

"The journey," Wren repeated. Not a question. Just holding the word up to the lantern light, examining it, the way they examined everything.

I should mention that Wren's hands are always ink-stained. It's the first thing I noticed about them, months ago, at the market — those stained fingers, that patient squint, the way they talked about the valley's fog lines like they were old friends with inconvenient habits. I should also mention that I have spent my entire adult life making sure no one looks at me too closely, and that Wren looks at everything closely, professionally, *constantly*, and that this is either the best or the worst trait a person could have, depending entirely on how much longer I intend to keep lying to them.

"Rafael," they said quietly. "There's a book on your table I can see through the window. It's been sitting there untouched for four days. You haven't let me past the doorstep in four days. You keep touching your chest like something hurts, and last night I heard you reading aloud in a language I've never heard, and I make maps for a living, so believe me when I tell you—" they lifted the lantern, and their eyes were very kind and very unfooled, "—I know what it looks like when someone is planning a route they don't expect to survive."

The honey cakes sat between us, going cold. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and gave up. Eleven days. I had eleven days, a fortune in Aldous's coin, a stolen memory of an orchard full of burning souls, and one cartographer with ink-stained fingers standing on my doorstep refusing, with maddening gentleness, to be protected from the truth.

"Come in," I said, opening the door. "I need to show you something in a book."
