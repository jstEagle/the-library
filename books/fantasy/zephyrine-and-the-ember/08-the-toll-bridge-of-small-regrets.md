## 8. The Toll Bridge of Small Regrets

The mountain had a throat, and we were walking down it.

Not literally—not yet. But the path Wren had drawn for us, the one that shouldn't have existed because no map of this mountain had ever survived long enough to be useful, led us along a ledge where the rock curved inward like the inside of a cheek, and the air changed. It tasted of struck flint and old smoke. Behind us, the fog of the valley was a gray memory. Ahead, the stone itself seemed to be listening.

I don't want to talk about what it felt like when magic came back to me at the valley's edge. Wren described it once as watching someone surface from deep water, gasping. I'll only say that a thief's invisibility turns out to feel different when you know it's not yours—when you know it's a leash someone else clipped to your collar before you were born. I'd spent my whole life proud of it. Now every step I took unremarked, unnoticed, unfelt, felt like walking around wearing my owner's brand.

Wren walked beside me anyway. That was its own kind of miracle. Wren saw me. Had always seen me, apparently, ink-stained fingers and terrible cooking and all.

We'd been climbing since dawn when the path simply ended.

I say *ended*, but that's not right. It *arrived*. The ledge widened without warning into a span of black stone that crossed a chasm I could not see the bottom of, because the bottom was not dark so much as it was *elsewhere*—a depth that suggested it went down further than geography permitted. And in the middle of the bridge sat a figure on a small stool, mending what appeared to be a fishing net made of hair.

"Oh good," Wren said faintly. "A person."

"A toll," I corrected. Because that's what it was. You don't put a bridge-keeper in the middle of nowhere unless somebody's expected to pay.

The figure looked up. I will do my best to describe it and I apologize in advance for failing. It was roughly woman-shaped, roughly old, with skin like birch bark and eyes like two coins left too long in a fire—not melted, just softened, their faces rubbed smooth. Her hands were strong and knotted and moved through the net's repair with the boredom of ten thousand years' practice.

"Two to cross," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and patience. "Toll is one regret apiece. Small ones preferred. Large ones jam the mechanism."

"The mechanism," Wren repeated.

She lifted the net. Strung through its mesh, where fish should have been, were small glinting things. Shapes. A first bicycle, fallen and scraped. A cruel word said to a sibling, thirty years gone. A wedding where the wrong person was chosen. Each regret hung in the net like a caught silverfish, still twitching faintly, still *feeling* itself be felt.

"They keep," she said, tapping the net. "Better than most things. Regrets don't rot. That's the tragedy of them, if you ask me. People carry them for decades thinking they're carrying something heavy, and really they're carrying something *preserved*."

"We don't have money," Wren said, which was sweet, and wrong.

"Money." The keeper smiled, and her teeth were like a key ring. "This is the court road, children. Down there—" she pointed her needle at the chasm, and something in the chasm pointed back, though I couldn't see what— "the only currency is pieces of the self. You've been paying all day, by the way. Didn't you notice? Every mile past the fog line takes a little something. Stamina, mostly. Appetite. The color yellow, for some people. You two got off cheap."

I believed her, which frightened me more than the net did. Since dawn I'd been trying to remember the smell of the bread at the bakery on Coil Street, and I'd assumed it was the climb scrambling me. It wasn't the climb.

"How does it work?" I asked. "The payment."

"You think of a regret. You give it to me honestly—I'll know if you're padding—and I take it out of you the way a splinter comes out. There's a moment of relief, then a moment of loss, then you go across and mostly forget which one was which." She went back to her mending. "Small ones preferred. Large ones jam the mechanism."

"What happens to them?" Wren asked. "After."

The keeper's needle paused.

"Nowhere, yet. They're carried down. Paid upward, eventually. The court runs on them." She looked up, and those burnt-coin eyes found me and stayed, which no one's eyes had done in twenty-nine years without my permission. "You especially, thief. They've been waiting on your account a long while. Best start settling."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a memory-thief: you get very good at inventory. I could close my eyes and walk the shelves of myself—every memory lifted, every face borrowed, every grief held in escrow for strangers who paid me in bread money to stop hurting. I knew what was mine and what wasn't, to the ounce. It was the only fortune I'd ever built, and I'd built it out of other people's leftovers, and I had told myself for years that this made me honest. A fence for feelings. Nothing stolen that wasn't already abandoned.

The keeper held out her hand, palm up, patient as erosion.

And I stood there doing arithmetic I hated. Because the smart play—the thief's play—was obvious. Give her a regret about something trivial. Something with no weight, no connection, no thread leading anywhere near the mountain or the prophecy or the mother-shaped hole at the center of everything. Pay the toll with a coin so worn it barely counted as currency.

But she'd said *honestly*, and her eyes said she'd know, and besides—

Besides, I was already paying in pieces of the self. Every mile. If the court was going to take from me regardless, then maybe the only choice I had left—the last one—was choosing *what*.

"I have one," I said. "It's small. It's mine. I want you to understand it's actually mine, because almost nothing is."

The keeper nodded slowly, like a tree agreeing to lose a leaf.

It was this: When I was nine—no. When I was nine *that I know of*, which by then was a different thing entirely—there had been an afternoon behind the tannery where the fog pooled thickest, and another child, a girl with a burn scar on her wrist, had shared her plum with me. Half a plum. We'd sat on the loading dock and eaten it in the silence of children who both understood what it was to go unseen, and when she left she'd said, "Same time tomorrow?"

And I hadn't come.

Not because anything happened. Not because I was afraid, even—or no, that's a lie, exactly because I was afraid. Because I was already learning the trade. Already learning that every attachment was a handle someone could grab me by, every friend a witness who could describe me. I'd sat behind the tannery the next day, hidden, and watched her wait, and watched her leave, and I had made myself into a smaller and smaller thing until by evening there was nothing left for anyone to find.

I'd carried that half a plum for twenty years. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. It was the smallest regret I owned and also the truest, because it wasn't imposed on me by any prophecy or any court. I had done that. Me. Free and clear, before anyone's fate touched me: I had chosen the fog over the plum.

"That one," I told the keeper. "Take that one."

Her hand closed gently around nothing, and pulled.

Have you ever had a splinter come out of a place you didn't know was wounded? That was the sensation—a sliding release, twenty years of low-grade pressure I'd stopped noticing because it had always been there, gone between one heartbeat and the next. Relief flooded me, actual physical relief, my shoulders dropping, my breath coming easy.

Then the loss arrived. Not of the memory—I could still narrate it, tannery, dock, plum, the scar like a small brown comma on her wrist. But the feeling under it was simply *gone*. I reached for the shame and found smooth walls. I reached for the ache and found a shelf, empty, dusted, labeled with nothing. Somewhere inside me a room had been emptied out and I would never be able to prove it had ever held anything, except that I now knew the exact dimensions of the hole.

"Oh," I said. My voice sounded strange to me. "Oh, that's what they take."

"That's what they take," the keeper agreed, threading my plum-regret into her net, where it gleamed and shivered among the others. A small silver shape. Half a fruit. "Feel lighter?"

"Yes."

"That's the trick of it. Lighter isn't better. Lighter is *less*." She turned her burnt-coin eyes to Wren. "Your turn, mapmaker."

* * *

I want to record what happened next precisely, because later it mattered, and because I have thought about it every day since.

Wren stepped forward without hesitation—that was Wren all over, wading into rivers with the map still wet—but I saw the hesitation arrive a moment late, the way it does with brave people. Wren's hands were steady. Their jaw wasn't.

"I need a moment to choose," Wren said.

"No you don't," the keeper said, not unkindly. "You chose before we started talking. You've been choosing since the fog line. Take your time anyway. The bridge isn't going anywhere. The bridge has never gone anywhere. That's the whole trouble with bridges."

So Wren took their time. And I stood there realizing, with the particular horror of a person who has just been handed evidence in their own trial, that I did not know what Wren was going to give. That I had no right to know. That intimacy, real intimacy, the kind I had spent my life treating as a liability to be managed, apparently included this: standing beside someone while they paid a piece of themselves, and not asking for the receipt.

That's not what I wanted to do. What I wanted to do was grab their wrist and say *don't*, the way I wanted to grab the wrist of everyone who ever traded me a memory, because I knew better than anyone what the empty rooms look like afterward. Twenty years of taking had taught me the cost of giving. And here was Wren, about to pay my toll on *my* road to steal back *my* soul, and the ledger between us was already so lopsided I could hardly breathe under it.

But Wren had asked for a moment, so I gave the moment. It was the hardest currency I've ever handed over.

"I know which one," Wren finally said quietly, to me, not to the keeper. "And I'm not going to tell you. Not because you don't matter. Because if I tell you, you'll spend the rest of the climb trying to give it back, and you can't, and then it'll be your empty room too. Let me have one private thing. Even from you. *Especially* from you."

"Wren—"

"It's fair," they said, and there was the cartographer's certainty in it, the voice of someone who has measured terrain and found it passable. "It's a fair price. Better than fair. You should hear what I was considering."

And they held out their hand to the keeper.

I watched the taking. I watched Wren's breath catch, and their shoulders drop, and their eyes go briefly somewhere very far away and come back with one less window in them. I watched the keeper thread something new onto the net—a shape I couldn't make out, small and bright, turning slowly like a fish in clear water—and I felt, absurdly, jealously, the way you feel looking at a sealed letter addressed in handwriting you almost recognize.

"What was it?" I asked. I'm not proud of it. "What did you pay?"

"No," Wren said.

"Wren."

"Rafael." They smiled, and it was the same smile as always and it was missing something I couldn't name, and somehow that made it kinder. "Ask me in a year. Maybe I'll have grown it back."

The keeper snorted—an avalanche in miniature—and stood, joints cracking like green wood, and gathered her net of small regrets over one shoulder. It chimed faintly. All those preserved hurts, singing together.

"Cross," she said. "Both of you. And listen—" the needle pointed at me again, "—when you get where you're going, and they offer you everything, and they will, because they know exactly what you're hungry for, you remember the weight of the plum. You remember that lighter isn't better. Whatever they're selling, thief, check the price against what you just paid, and ask yourself if you can afford to be less."

Then she was simply not there, the way the fog isn't there, and the bridge lay open before us, black stone humming faintly underfoot like a struck bell that hadn't decided to finish ringing.

We crossed. The chasm below us breathed. Halfway across, Wren's hand found mine, ink-stained fingers lacing through mine, and we didn't speak, because some transactions are private even when witnessed.

On the far side, the path plunged downward into warmth, into ember-light leaking up through cracks in the stone like lava seen through honeycomb. The court was close. I could feel it the way you feel a fire in the next room—heat without light, presence without sound.

"There's a door ahead," Wren said, unrolling their sketchbook one-handed, refusing to let go of me. "There's always a door ahead. I drew it last night."

And there was. But carved into the lintel, in letters that were warm to the touch and rearranged themselves when I blinked, was a line I recognized from the book bound in bark, a line I had read eleven days ago and prayed I'd misread:

*The mapmaker who draws the way in, and cannot draw the way out.*

Wren's grip tightened on my hand. They had seen it too.
