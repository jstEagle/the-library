## 9. The First Courtier

I have been making maps for eleven years, and in that time I have learned exactly one universal truth: every mountain lies about its size.

This one lied the most. From the valley floor it had looked like a day's climb, a gray shoulder slouching against the sky. Two days in, we were still on it. The path Rafael remembered from other people's memories turned out to be a goat trail that the goats themselves had apparently disowned, and by the third morning my boots had developed opinions about me that I did not care to hear.

"You're limping," Rafael said, without turning around.

"I'm *pacing myself.*"

"Your left boot is sulking."

"My left boot has earned the right." I caught up to them at the edge of a shelf of rock where the wind came sideways and smelled faintly of struck matches. That was new. For two days the air had been getting warmer instead of colder as we climbed, which violated everything I knew about mountains and most things I knew about weather. "How far to the court?"

Rafael didn't answer right away. They stood with their hands in their pockets, looking up the slope into haze the color of old pewter, and when they spoke their voice had that flatness they got sometimes—the one I'd started privately calling their ledger voice, the voice of someone entering a number in a column.

"The memories say three more hours. But the memories are forty years old and belonged to a man who was fleeing."

"Fleeing down or fleeing up?"

"That's the interesting question."

It was always the interesting question with Rafael. Everything was interesting questions now, asked in that careful voice, while underneath it something ticked like a clock with a crack in it. Eleven days until the solstice. Eight of them spent. I had watched Rafael count them without meaning to—watched their lips move over breakfast, watched their fingers twitch through some silent arithmetic—and I had decided somewhere around day four that I would not point it out, because there is a kind of cruelty in naming someone's fear out loud when they're working so hard to keep it folded small.

So instead I said, "Then we'd better make those hours count," and took out my notebook to sketch the switchbacks behind us, because that was what I did now. What we did. Every hour, I drew the way we'd come—not because we needed a map back, but because since the door beneath the root, since the stone had *moved* for my pencil, drawing felt less like recording the world and more like negotiating with it.

And every time I drew, I felt it again: that faint tug behind my sternum, like a hook set gently in me, reeling.

*The mapmaker who draws the way in, and cannot draw the way out.*

I hadn't told Rafael about the margin note. Not yet. There is an art to telling terrible things, and I am a professional believer in preparation, and I needed another day or two to figure out how to say *the prophecy thinks I'm disposable* without watching Rafael do something reckless about it. They already carried enough debt. I refused to be one more entry in their column of obligations.

We climbed. The haze thickened but did not cool. Somewhere above us, I began to hear a sound I couldn't name—a vast, slow whispering, like silk dragged across silk, or ash sifting down through still air.

"There," Rafael said suddenly, and stopped so fast I nearly walked into their back.

On a ledge ahead—impossibly, because the ledge was narrow and bare and we had seen nothing living for a day except a moth the size of my thumbnail—stood a woman.

She was dressed in gray silk that moved wrong. That's the only honest way I can put it: the fabric shifted against itself even when she was still, rippling in patterns that didn't match the wind, and after a moment I understood why. It wasn't silk. It was wings. Thousands upon thousands of moth wings, layered and overlapping into the shape of a gown, each one pale as bone dust and trembling with a life of its own. Her hair was silver-white and pinned up with what looked, at this distance, like needles. Her face was beautiful the way winter light through a window is beautiful: precise, luminous, and entirely uninterested in your comfort.

She smiled at us.

Every instinct I owned, all eleven years of reading terrain and people, told me the same thing: *that smile has been practiced.*

"Well," she said. Her voice carried down the slope effortlessly, warm as a hand held near a fire—not touching, just warming. "The thief and the mapmaker. You're later than the book said."

Rafael went very still beside me. I felt them doing the thing they did—checking exits, counting shadows, weighing whether the stranger was a threat or merely a nuisance—and I did the thing I did: I looked at her feet.

No footprints. The ledge around her was dusted with fine volcanic grit, and there wasn't a mark on it. Either she'd been standing there since before the last snowfall of ash, or she hadn't walked here at all.

"You know us," Rafael said. Flat. Careful.

"I know *of* you. There's a difference, and you of all people should appreciate it." She descended toward us without appearing to take steps, the way a thought arrives—you simply notice it has. Up close, the wing-gown resolved into detail I wished it hadn't: each moth was whole, intact, sleeping, their little bodies curled among the folds. A dress made of a thousand small sleepers. "My name is Vesperine. I keep the mountain's shoulder. And I knew your mother."

That landed. I saw it land—a flinch so small most people would have missed it, the kind Rafael had spent a lifetime learning to hide and could not quite manage anymore, not with eight days left and their own face waiting for them under a mountain.

"Liar," Rafael said softly.

"Almost certainly true of everyone you'll meet above the fog line, so let's establish a better standard than truth." Vesperine tilted her head, and the needles in her hair caught what little light there was. "Ask me something only someone who knew her would know. Something small. Small things are harder to counterfeit."

I watched Rafael wrestle with it. Watched them want to refuse and be unable to, because this was the first sentence anyone had spoken on this entire mountain that contained their mother, and Rafael Marchetti would walk into fire for a scrap of her—even, I suspected, especially, if the fire was also a trap.

"What did she smell like?" Rafael said finally. "Not perfume. Just—her."

Vesperine's smile changed. It softened by a degree, and for one flickering instant the performance fell away and something older and wearier looked out through the winter-light face.

"Clove smoke and river mud," she said. "She used to steal apples from the orchard above the mill and swear they were payment for services rendered, though no one ever established what services. When she laughed too hard she pressed the back of her wrist against her mouth, as if joy were something she'd been caught doing." A pause. "She bit her nails. She'd have hated you knowing that."

Rafael made a sound I will remember for the rest of my life. It wasn't a word. It was barely a breath. It was the sound of a locked door opening onto a room you'd forgotten existed.

I reached out and put my ink-stained hand over theirs, and they didn't pull away. Progress. We were keeping score in tiny increments now; it was how we survived each other's honesty.

"You're wondering," Vesperine said briskly, and just like that the softness was gone, folded away like a letter, "why a courtier of the Ember is standing on a cold mountainside talking to thieves. The answer is simple: because I choose to be. Because the court beneath this mountain is a wheel, and wheels turn, and I have lived long enough to know that the clever place to stand is not on the wheel but beside it." She spread her hands, and a hundred moths stirred in their sleep, a ripple passing through her gown like wind through wheat. "You are going to the throne. You are going to attempt the single most audacious theft in the history of theft—I've seen the prophecy's margins, child, don't look surprised, the court wrote that book and the court keeps copies. And you are going to fail."

"Comforting," I said.

"It's meant to be. Failure is survivable. Success, as written, is not—but then, prophecies are contracts, and contracts have loopholes, and loopholes require guides." Her eyes moved to me, and I felt them the way you feel a draftsman's compass touch paper: precisely, coldly, at a chosen point. "Which brings me to you, cartographer."

"I'm flattered."

"Don't be. Flattery is for people who need persuading. You need only informing." She produced—from nowhere, from the same nowhere her footsteps came from—a folded square of gray silk and laid it on the rocks between us. "There is a path to the lower halls that avoids the toll bridge, the wardens, and the gallery of mouths. Your route, as drawn"—she nodded at my notebook, and something in my stomach dropped through the mountain—"dies at the third gate. All routes die at the third gate. Except mine."

"And the price," Rafael said. Their voice had gone very quiet, very level. Ledger voice. "Nothing on this mountain is free. What does guidance cost?"

Vesperine's smile returned, and this time I saw the teeth in it plainly—metaphorically speaking, since her actual teeth were perfectly normal and somehow that was worse.

"A favor," she said. "To be named later. Unspecified. Owed by both of you, jointly and severally, redeemable once, at a time of my choosing, before the solstice passes." She let that sit in the cold air between us like a coin on a table. "That is the whole contract. No hidden clauses. I don't deal in hidden clauses; hidden clauses are for amateurs and kings."

"And if we refuse?" I asked.

"Then you die at the third gate, the Ember keeps what it was promised, and in a hundred years another thief with no shadow climbs this mountain and fails in exactly the same way, and I stand here again making the same offer to someone else who smells of clove smoke and stubbornness." She shrugged, and the moths shivered. "I am very patient. It's my defining virtue. Ask anyone who's outlived me. No one has."

I looked at Rafael. Rafael looked at the silk-wrapped packet on the rocks, and I watched them run the arithmetic—they were always running arithmetic, always converting everything to cost and payment—and I watched them arrive, with visible distaste, at the same answer I had.

A favor owed to a creature like this was a knife with the handle facing her.

But a corpse owes nothing, and dead people make terrible thieves.

"We accept," Rafael said.

"No," I said, at exactly the same moment.

We looked at each other. Vesperine looked at both of us with the expression of a woman watching a play she'd funded.

"Jointly and severally," I said to Rafael, low. "You heard her. If she calls the favor and you refuse, I'm bound anyway. If I refuse, you're bound. We don't get to split this. So we decide it together or not at all."

"Wren—"

"Eight days, Rafael." I kept my voice gentle, because the words weren't. "We don't have the luxury of you protecting me from the paperwork."

Something moved behind their eyes—anger, fear, and underneath both, that terrifying relief they never quite managed to hide, the relief of being *seen*, of being argued with by someone who intended to still be there tomorrow. They exhaled slowly through their nose.

"Together," they said. "Fine. Together."

"Together," Vesperine repeated, pleased as a cat presented with a cream jug. She nudged the silk packet toward us with one toe—it left a print in the ash this time, I noticed, now that she wanted it to. "The route inside. Follow it exactly. Deviate at your peril; the mountain rearranges itself for guests it doesn't like, and it has never liked anyone." She began to turn away, gown rippling, and then paused, and looked back over her shoulder at Rafael with an expression I couldn't read at all.

"One more thing, free of charge, because I am feeling generous and generosity makes better leverage than cruelty, whatever the court believes." Her voice dropped, and the whispering sound I'd been hearing all morning—the silk-over-silk, the ash-through-air—suddenly resolved, horribly, into what it actually was.

It was the mountain breathing.

"She didn't sell you because she didn't love you," Vesperine said. "Remember that, in the days coming, when you learn what the bargain bought. She loved you the way only a woman selling her child's soul can afford to: completely, and in advance, and against everything."

Then she stepped off the ledge into empty air, and did not fall so much as become unavailable, and the moths of her gown scattered upward like a handful of thrown snow, and we were alone on the shoulder of the mountain with a folded piece of silk and the sound of something enormous inhaling beneath our feet.

* * *

Rafael didn't speak for a long time.

We sat with our backs against a boulder that gave off heat like a banked stove, and Rafael held the silk packet unopened in their lap, turning it over and over, and I pretended to update my sketches and watched them instead over the top of the page.

"Wren." Their voice came out rough. "What she said about my mother. About loving me in advance."

"Yes."

"Do you believe her?"

I considered lying. I considered it seriously, the way you consider a shortcut across a frozen lake. Then I put down the pencil.

"I believe she wanted you to believe it," I said. "Which isn't the same thing. But—" I turned my hand palm-up on my knee, showing the stains, the calluses, the pencil groove worn into my middle finger from twenty years of holding the same tool. "I draw coastlines for a living. Do you know what I've learned? Every coastline is a lie the land tells the sea. The real edge is somewhere in between, changing with every tide. People are the same. Whatever the truth of your mother is, it's not one thing, and it isn't hers alone anymore—it's yours to survey." I hesitated. "And for what it's worth: whoever sold a soul to save a valley probably thought she was buying something too."

"Buying what?"

"Time, maybe. A chance. A thief with no shadow who might, against all odds, climb up here and steal it back." I tapped the packet in their lap. "Open it. Let's see what we bought."

Rafael unfolded the silk.

Inside lay a strip of gray cloth embroidered in thread that glowed faintly, like embers seen through a closed door—a route, drawn in stitchwork, winding down through the mountain's bones. And tucked against it, a slip of paper in handwriting I recognized instantly, because it was mine—or rather, it was *like* mine, the way a forgery is like a signature, close enough to pass at a glance.

It was a copy of the page I'd torn out of my own notebook two nights ago and burned. The margin note. The line I hadn't shown Rafael yet.

*The mapmaker who draws the way in, and cannot draw the way out.*

Beneath it, in fresh ink, someone had added a single word:

*Unless.*
