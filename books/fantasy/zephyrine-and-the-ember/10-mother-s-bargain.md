## 10. Mother's Bargain

Vesperine's memory-taste is different from mine.

I should explain that, because it matters. When I take a memory from someone, it comes away clean—like lifting a coin from a pocket without disturbing the lining. The donor feels a small absence, a word on the tip of the tongue, and then nothing. I've always been careful. Surgical. It's the one craft I've ever mastered and I mastered it out of terror.

Vesperine doesn't lift. She *pours*. She set two fingers against my temple in the shadow of her pavilion on the mountain's shoulder, said, "This will feel like drowning in warm water," and it did exactly that—and what she poured into me was not her memory at all.

It was my mother's.

* * *

I was twenty-two when she died, and I thought I had grieved her properly. I'd stood at the graveside in the fog with my hands empty and my face arranged into something appropriate, and I'd felt—the thing I always felt. A door closed from the inside. I loved her the way you love a wall that has kept the weather off you: gratefully, distantly, without ever once asking what the wall wanted, or what it cost to stand there.

Here is what I remembered of her, before tonight: flour on her forearms. A habit of humming songs that stopped mid-phrase, as if she'd forgotten how they ended. The way she flinched—not visibly, but I learned to see it—every time I did something too clever. Every time I stole something small and perfect and invisible. She never caught me. I used to think that meant she wasn't paying attention.

She was paying attention the way you pay attention to a fuse.

The memory Vesperine gave me begins in winter, in a house I almost recognize—a version of our cottage with a younger roofline, before the lean-to, before the years put their weight on it. My mother is pregnant. Round as a harvest moon and half as patient, standing at the hearth stirring a pot of something thin, because the pantry behind her holds three turnips and a bag of meal and nothing else, and this is month four of the famine.

I know about the famine the way everyone in the valley knows about it: the year the wheat came up gray, the year the river ran shallow as an apology, the year the churchyard got so much use they had to open the far corner of it. Eleven months. Two hundred dead, which in a valley our size is not a number, it's a census. They blamed blight. They blamed weather. They blamed each other, briefly and badly, and then they stopped blaming anything because blame takes energy and energy was food.

In the memory, my mother is stirring the pot and talking to me. To the me that isn't born yet.

"You'll be stubborn," she says. "I can tell already. You argue with everything, even my digestion." A pause. Her hand goes still on the spoon. "I'm sorry about that. About all of it. You're going to come into a world that's already spent you, little thief, and you won't even get a receipt."

Little thief. Even unborn, apparently, I had a reputation.

Then the door opens and the cold comes in with it, and so does a man who is not a man.

I want to describe him accurately because accuracy matters here, but he's difficult to hold in description the way certain dreams are difficult to hold past waking. Tall in a way that suggested the ceiling should have objected. Gray, but not the gray of ash or stone—the gray of a thing that had been burned down to its essential shape and found that shape acceptable. His eyes were ember-colored, and they didn't reflect the firelight. They competed with it.

He did not introduce himself. Courtiers don't, I understand now. Introductions are for people who can be met more than once.

"You've been asking," he said. "At the crossroads. With the old words."

My mother didn't deny it. In the memory I could feel her body from the inside—her heartbeat, her cold feet, the specific exhaustion of carrying a child on three turnips a day—and under all of it, a fear so large it had stopped feeling like fear and started feeling like furniture. She was afraid the way the house is afraid of wind. Constantly. Structurally.

"The valley is dying," she said.

"The valley is *negotiating*," he said, and there was something in his voice that I recognized with a lurch, because I've heard it in my own mouth across a hundred stolen memories: the tone of someone explaining terms they consider generous. "Rains fail. Wheat grays. Children go hungry. These are not accidents, goodwife. These are *arguments*, and the court beneath makes them patiently. Your valley sits on land we claim. Your fog keeps us out—" and here something moved through his face that might have been distaste, "—but fog does not feed anyone, does it?"

"What do you want?"

"Nothing from the valley." His ember eyes went to her belly, and my mother's whole body clenched around me like a fist. "The valley can keep its dirt and its gray wheat. We want what's owed. A debt was spoken over this land generations ago and never paid, and debts accrue interest, and interest accrues famine, and famine accrues—" he gestured at the thin pot, the empty pantry, the whole gray dying year, "—this."

"Name the price," my mother said. And God help me, in the memory, I could feel that she already knew. Some part of her had known since the crossroads. Since before the crossroads. She asked anyway, because asking made it real, and making it real was the only way to survive it.

"The child," he said. "Not its life—we don't deal in death, death is vulgar and cheap. Its soul. Held in trust by the court until the season turns enough times. Then it comes home to us, willingly or otherwise, and the debt closes, and your valley eats."

And here is the part I will spend the rest of my life turning over like a stone with something alive under it:

My mother did not hesitate.

I need you to understand—I needed *her* to hesitate. I needed a long terrible silence, a bargaining, a *please, anything else*, some evidence that selling me had been the last door in a burning house rather than simply the nearest exit. But the memory shows me the truth with a thief's clarity: between his sentence and hers there was exactly the pause of a woman doing arithmetic she'd already done at the crossroads, alone, with the old words, weeks ago.

"How long?" she said.

"Until the solstice turns. Twenty-some years. It will live its life in your fog, safe from us, safe from everything. We ask only that you keep it unremarkable. Keep it *small*." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Though I suspect that won't require effort. Our kind leaves a mark on what it touches, even secondhand. It will hide whether you teach it to or not."

*Our kind.* He said it like a relative saying *the family nose.*

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the famine continues, and worsens, and in three winters there will be no valley left to keep anything safe." He spread his gray hands. "I am not threatening you, goodwife. I am describing a ledger."

My mother looked down at her belly. At me. And in the memory—which is hers, remember, filtered through her eyes, her heart, her guilt—I felt what she felt looking at me, and it was not the simple clean love I'd always assumed mothers carried. It was love tangled up with terror tangled up with a decision already made, and underneath all of it, steady as a floorboard, this:

*Better owned by something below than buried by something above. Better a soul in pawn than a grave in the far corner of the churchyard. Better it hates me alive than loves me dead.*

"I'll pay it," she said. "But I want terms written. Not spoken. Written, and sealed, and a copy in my keeping."

Something flickered across the courtier's face—surprise, maybe, or the courtly equivalent, whatever that is. Probably mild professional interest.

"You drive a hard bargain for a woman with no leverage."

"I have the only leverage there is," my mother said. "I'm the one saying yes."

* * *

The memory ended there—or Vesperine ended it there, folding it closed like a letter—and I came back to myself sitting on the floor of her pavilion with my face wet and no memory of starting to cry.

"That's not fair," I said. My voice came out sanded down to nothing. "You can't just—that's not *fair*, giving me that. That was hers. That was private."

"She gave it to me," Vesperine said, "a long time ago, to hold. She said—" and here the moth-winged courtier did something I hadn't seen her do yet, which was look away from me, "—she said, *when it comes asking, and it will come asking, let it see why.*"

"When it comes asking." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "She talked about me like a debt collector."

"She talked about you like a mother who knew her child would grow up clever enough to find the truth and angry enough to survive finding it." Vesperine crouched in front of me, silk whispering, ember eyes level with mine. "Do you understand what you saw? Truly?"

"I saw her sell me."

"You saw her buy two hundred lives with one. You saw a woman walk to a crossroads in a famine winter and offer up the only thing she had that the court wanted, and then—" Vesperine's voice sharpened, "—and then *negotiate*. Do you know how many people say yes to us? Everyone says yes. Nobody says *written terms, sealed, a copy in my keeping.* Your mother haggled with the court beneath the mountain while pregnant and starving, and she won concessions. The fog itself, thief. Why do you think the valley's edge holds magic out? That clause was *hers*. She traded your soul and bought a border with it."

I sat with that. The pavilion was quiet except for the sound of the mountain breathing, which is a real sound, if you've never heard it—slow, mineral, patient.

Because here's the thing. Here's the thing I couldn't say out loud yet.

For eleven days I had built a story out of the prophecy and the peddler's book and my own fury, and the story went like this: I was robbed. Before I drew breath, someone sold the best part of me, and every invisibility I'd ever cultivated, every careful erased trace, every lonely unremarkable year—it was all fallout from a theft committed against me before I could consent to anything. I had a villain. The villain was my mother. It was a workable story. Anger is very load-bearing if you build your house out of it carefully.

And now the house had a second floor I hadn't known about, and the second floor was this: she didn't sell me *instead of* loving me. She sold me *as* the act of loving me, the largest one available, the only currency the court accepted. She looked at a choice between her child's soul and her neighbors' children's lives and she chose, and she chose fast, and then she spent twenty years watching me for signs of the court's mark and flinching every time I was too clever, because every trick I pulled was proof the debt was real and coming due.

Keep it small, the courtier had said.

So she kept me small. Or tried. And I mistook her whole life's work—my safety—for neglect, and my inheritance of hiding for a personality, and her flinches for disapproval, when they were forecasts. She was reading weather I couldn't see. She was watching the sky over her sold child and counting down seasons.

"I hate this," I told Vesperine. It was the truest sentence I had. "I hate that she's both. I had her sorted. Villain, or victim, pick one, I can work with either. But she's—" My hands were shaking. "She's the person who betrayed me and the person who shielded me and it was the *same act*, it was one act, and I don't know where to put that."

"Nowhere," Vesperine said. "That's the honest answer. Some things don't fit anywhere. You carry them instead." She rose, smoothing her silk, and the charm slid back over her like a visor. "Feel free to hate me for the delivery. It's traditional."

"Why give me this now? What does it buy you?"

"Context," she said, "is worth more than gold in the halls you're walking into. The Ashen King will tell you a version of your mother's bargain. The throne will show you another. I'd rather you walked in knowing the truth, so that when they lie to you, you'll recognize the shape of the lie." A pause, delicate as a moth landing. "Also, I collect gratitude. It compounds beautifully."

I got to my feet. My legs held, mostly. Outside the pavilion, Wren was crouched over their maps in the last light, ink-stained fingers moving, drawing the mountain the way only Wren draws things—as if the paper were listening. They glanced up at me and whatever they saw in my face made them start to rise.

I held up a hand. *Later.* There was a later-shaped conversation coming, the biggest one of my life, and I couldn't have it yet. First I had to do what I do. What I was apparently *made* to do, court-made, contract-made.

I had to steal something.

Because here's what the memory gave me besides grief, besides the reframing, besides the terrible new architecture of my mother's love: it gave me a detail. One small, precise, thief-relevant detail that Vesperine either didn't notice or didn't think mattered.

When the gray courtier spoke the terms—*until the solstice turns, then it comes home to us, willingly or otherwise*—my mother made him write a clause. One more line, added in the old words at the bottom of the contract, and in the memory I watched her read it aloud to make sure it was exact:

*"And the child may take back what is its own."*

Take back. Not be given. Not inherit. *Take*—the verb of thieves, the verb of the valley, the verb she'd watched me practice my whole life without understanding she'd planted it in my contract like a seed.

The court thinks the prophecy delivers me to their throne. The Ashen King thinks he's collecting a debt.

But somewhere in those halls, on a throne of fused souls, sits something that is legally, contractually, in the old binding words—

Mine.

"Wren," I said, and my voice didn't shake anymore, which surprised us both. "How fast can you draw a throne room?"
