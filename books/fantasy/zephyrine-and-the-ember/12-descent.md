## 12. Descent

The door beneath the root did not open so much as admit us, the way a throat admits a swallow. One moment I was standing in Wren's carefully drawn corridor of blind stone, lantern held high, and the next the mountain had simply decided to include us in itself, and the wall was behind me, and there was no going back that wasn't also falling.

"Tell me you meant to do that," Wren said. Their voice came out thin and strange in the new dark, ink-stained fingers pressed flat against the stone as if checking it for a pulse.

"I never mean to do anything," I said. "It's my defining virtue."

The dark ahead was not entirely dark. Far down the throat of the passage—down, always down; everything about this mountain was down—there was a light that didn't behave like light. It didn't fall on things. It rose off them, the way heat rises off a summer road, except it was red-gold and slow, and it breathed. That's the only word for it. The light breathed, in and out, like something enormous sleeping under a blanket of rock.

*The Ember*, said every stolen memory I carried, all at once, in a chorus of dead men's voices. *The Ember. The Ember.*

I have spent my life carrying other people's memories like coins in a purse, spending them when I must, jingling them at night when I couldn't sleep. They had whispered since we crossed the valley's edge. But here, this deep, they weren't whispering. They were *pointing*.

"That way," I said, before Wren could unfold one of their maps.

"You know the route?"

"No. But three hundred people whose heads I've picked seem to."

Wren looked at me for a long moment—the look they gave the fog line, the look they gave impossible things before deciding to map them anyway—and took out their charcoal, and wrote: *Route confirmed by consensus of ghosts.*

"There are days," they said, "when I remember why I love you, and today it's because you're horrifying."

We went down.

* * *

The passage opened without warning into vastness, and I stopped understanding where I was with my eyes and started understanding it with my skin instead.

Halls. Plural. A cathedral's worth of halls, folded into each other like hands, ceilings so far above us that the ember-light rising off the floor died halfway up and left the heights in a soft black nothing, so that the whole court seemed to hang in its own darkness like a coal in ash. Pillars of black stone veined with gold—no. Not veined with gold. *Cracked* with gold, the way a log cracks when fire is inside it trying to get out.

And everywhere, everywhere, the embers.

They hung in cages. That was the first thing I understood, and the last thing I stopped understanding. Small cages of black iron filigree, delicate as birdcages made by someone who loved birds very much and kept them anyway, each one holding a fist-sized coal that glowed red-gold-red-gold in a rhythm almost but not quite like breathing. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Strung along the walls, clustered on pedestals, suspended from chains lost in the dark above, filling the halls with their slow collective pulse until the air itself seemed to have a heartbeat, and it was not mine.

Each one a soul. I knew it the way you know your own name, which is to say: I knew it, and then, a heartbeat later, I understood what knowing it cost, and wished I didn't.

Because some of them were flickering in rhythms I recognized.

There. Near the western pillar. A caged ember pulsing quick and skittish, quick and skittish, and underneath my ribs something answered it, an old borrowed memory of a boy named Aldric who had sold me his first kiss's worth of grief outside a chapel twenty years ago, and the rhythm of that ember was the rhythm of Aldric's laugh, which I still carried somewhere in the purse of myself.

"They're people," Wren whispered. "Rafael. They're *people*."

"Yes."

"And the courtiers just—" Wren's hand rose, pointing, and I followed the finger, and saw.

Courtiers. Drifting through the halls the way dust drifts through a sunbeam, except these sunbeams moved and wore silk. Tall figures in colors I don't have names for—colors like the moment after a match goes out, like the inside of a closed eyelid—robed in moth-wing sheen, faces smooth and lovely and wrong in the specific way of things that have practiced having faces. They drifted between the soul-cages without looking at them. Some paused to warm their hands at a cage the way you'd warm your hands at a hearth, and the soul inside would flare, dim, flare, and the courtier would sigh with pleasure and drift on.

My whole body wanted to be sick. My thief's training overruled it. *Never vomit on the merchandise,* old Halva used to say, and even though Halva had been talking about pickpocketing drunk merchants, I felt the advice applied.

"We need to not be seen," I murmured. "Stay behind me. Walk like you belong to the furniture."

"Belong to the—"

"Wren. Trust me. I have never once been seen when I didn't wish it. It's the one thing I'm actually good at."

So we walked into the heart of the Ember's court, two small soft creatures crossing a hall full of tigers, and the courtiers drifted past us like we were drafts of cold air.

Except.

Here is the thing I noticed, maybe forty steps in, the thing I will carry in whatever purse I have left after all this is done. A courtier passed within arm's reach of me—close enough that I could see the moth-dust shimmer of its skin, close enough to count the ways its face was almost right—and it did not glance at me. Not a flicker. And I realized, with the particular vertigo of a floor dropping out for the second time in one season, that it hadn't glanced at me because there was nothing to glance *at*.

I know invisibility. I've worn it my whole life. It's a thing you do—a slouch, a forgettable face, a way of moving that tells every eye *nothing here, move along*. It takes effort. It's a performance with no intermission.

This wasn't that. I wasn't performing. I was simply *unseen*, the way the cages' light was simply warm, the way the mountain was simply deep. It sat on me like a garment tailored to my exact shoulders.

And I knew the tailoring.

* * *

Let me tell you how a thief knows their own work, even secondhand.

When I lift a memory from someone, I leave nothing. No scar, no seam, no little hole where the memory used to live. People walk around for years with gaps in them that I cut and they never once put a hand to the empty space and wondered. That's craft. That's the difference between me and some hedge-witch with a rusty talent—I don't take memories, I *edit* them out, so cleanly that reality itself agrees to the revision. The world looks at the edited person and says *yes, this is complete, this was always thus*.

That agreement—that clean, seamless, world-deceiving cut—is a signature. Every thief has one. Mine is invisibility: I edit myself out of being perceived, and the world agrees, and eyes slide off me like rain off oiled cloth.

And now, walking through the court beneath the mountain, wearing that invisibility like a fine coat, I finally recognized the cloth.

Court-magic. The same weave. The same seamless *agreement* that makes the world say *nothing here, move along*. The same technique, scaled up, refined over centuries by things that had been editing perception since before my valley's fog learned its business. My gift—the one thing I had ever owned, the single trait I'd built a self around, *I am the one who cannot be seen*—was not mine.

It was inherited. Like eye color. Like debt.

A courtier drifted past, close enough to touch, and I watched its eyes pass over me with perfect, untroubled blankness, and I understood that it wasn't failing to see me. It was seeing exactly what the court's magic told it to see, using the court's magic that lived in my own skin, and what it saw was *property, unremarkable, already ours*.

I stopped walking.

"Rafael?" Wren, half a step behind, low voice.

"Don't stop," I said, and started again immediately, because the first rule of any heist is keep moving. But my hands had gone cold, and not mountain-cold.

All those years in the valley. All those years congratulating myself on my cunning, my craft, my careful constructed nothing-of-a-life. The fog swallowed magic, everyone knew that, magic couldn't follow into the valley—and so I'd believed my talent was somehow native there, a flower that grew only in that soil, *mine*, rooted in the place that rejected all other enchantment. I had thought myself the exception. The valley's one small domestic miracle.

But the fog doesn't swallow magic. I know that now, standing in a hall lit by ten thousand caged souls. The fog *hides* magic. It's the same edit. The same seamless lie, laid over a whole valley: *nothing here, move along.* The court reached into that valley whenever it liked—my mother's bargain had proven that much—and it had left one thing behind in the womb of a woman who owed it everything. A mark. A brand shaped like a talent. A leash braided to look like a gift.

I was invisible to the courtiers not because I was clever.

I was invisible because I was *theirs*, and you don't stare at your own coat hanging in the closet.

"Rafael." Wren caught my sleeve, drew me into the shadow of a pillar whose gold veins pulsed like slow lightning. Their eyes searched my face with the terrible competence of someone who has spent weeks learning to read it. "You've gone the color of the stone. What happened?"

"I know how my trick works," I said. My voice came out level. I'll always be proud of that. "The not-being-seen. I always thought it was mine. It's not. It's theirs. I'm not hiding from the court, Wren. I'm *wearing* it."

Wren was quiet a moment. Charcoal fingers tight around my sleeve.

"Okay," they said. "So it's a uniform."

"What?"

"A uniform. You put it on every day of your life and you thought you chose it, and it turns out somebody handed it to you. That's—" they exhaled. "Rafael, half the things I am, I got from my mother's side. The ink habit. The terrible knees. If someone handed you your one good thing, then fine. It was still your hands that used it. Nobody else's."

For a moment—one moment—I let that be true. It helped the way a candle helps in a cathedral: not much light, but it told you where you were standing.

Then a bell rang.

* * *

It was not a bell like any bell in the valley. It came from everywhere, out of the walls themselves, out of the gold veins in the stone, a low bronze toll that I felt in my teeth and in the purses of other people's memories I carry, and every soul-cage in the hall flared at once—tens of thousands of coals flaring together—and the light they threw turned the whole court the color of the inside of a heart.

The courtiers stopped drifting. All of them. Mid-stride, mid-sigh, mid-warming-their-hands-at-the-caged-dead. Stopped like puppets with a hand on their strings, and turned—not toward us, thank every saint I've ever pickpocketed—all turned in the same direction, down the great axis of the hall, toward the far end where the light rose highest and reddest and oldest.

Toward the throne I had come to rob.

"The audience," Wren breathed. "It's starting. The solstice—" They had their map out, the one of the inner halls, and their eyes flicked between it and the sea of frozen silk and moth-wing faces. "Rafael. If they're all going to the throne room—"

"Then the wards are being opened," I finished. Every door opens for a court occasion. Every lock, every ward, every seal—because an Ashen King holding audience wants his court gathered, and a gathered court means passages cleared, means the defenses of the deepest hall relaxed for exactly the length of one ceremony.

Every thief in creation knows that the best time to rob a palace is during its own party.

"Or," Wren said, with the flat calm of someone doing arithmetic they hate, "every courtier in this hall is about to be standing in one room, and if your invisibility stops working in there—if it's *their* magic, if they can end it whenever—then we'll be walking into the one place in this mountain where we are most visible, surrounded by ten thousand witnesses, to steal from the throne itself."

"Yes," I said.

"That was supposed to talk you out of it."

"It did the opposite, didn't it."

"It did the opposite," Wren admitted, and folded the map away, and did the thing I love most and fear most about them: smiled. Ink-stained fingers, ember-lit grin, terror worn like a well-cut coat. "Then we go now, while the doors are open and the halls are emptying. Stay behind me. Walk like you belong to the furniture."

The bell tolled again, and the court began to flow toward its king, and we slipped into the current going the other way—or rather, I slipped, and pulled Wren with me, unseen, unseeing, marked and branded and *owned* down to the trick that kept me alive.

One thought rode with me down the last corridor, keeping pace like a third traveler:

Somewhere ahead, at the center of all this light, my soul hung in a brazier waiting for its audience. And the thing wearing my invisibility—the court's magic, the family silver—was going to carry me straight to it, whether the Ember knew I was coming or not.

The doors at the corridor's end stood open. Heat rolled out of them like a greeting.

I stepped through.
