## 13. The Ember Throne

The courtiers never saw me.

I want to say that was a comfort, that thirty years of being forgettable had finally paid off in the one place it mattered. But it wasn't comfort. It was worse than being seen. Because as I slipped between them—drifting figures in silk the color of banked coals, their faces beautiful and half-remembered, like people you'd met at a funeral and couldn't place—I understood *why* they didn't see me. It wasn't my skill. It wasn't Wren's chalk marks or the stolen shadow I'd worn across three counties.

It was that I belonged to the house. And furniture doesn't get noticed by its own household.

I kept that thought at arm's length, the way you keep a hand away from a stove you already know is hot. There would be time to feel sick about it later, if there was a later. Right now there was only forward: down the long throat of the hall, past caged embers that murmured as I passed.

They hung from chains of black iron, hundreds of them, thousands—a bazaar of small fires, each one burning in its own little cage, and each one *saying something*. Not words, exactly. Closer to the way a sleeping person says a name in the dark. When I walked beneath them they leaned toward me, all of them at once, flames bending sideways like grass under wind, and the whole hall sighed.

Wren's hand found mine. Ink on ink. We didn't look at each other, because looking at each other would have meant admitting what we were doing, and neither of us could afford that yet. You can steal anything, I've always said, so long as nobody—not even you—believes it's real until it's already gone.

* * *

I should back up. The descent deserves description, but description is a thief's way of stalling, and I've been stalling since the door beneath the root.

We came in through a passage no map had ever survived—which is to say, a passage Wren had drawn into being, their pen moving over paper while the stone ahead of them rearranged itself to match, grudgingly, like an old servant obeying a new master. The prophecy had said *the mapmaker who draws the way in*, and it hadn't lied, though it had been conspicuously quiet about the rest of the sentence. I knew about the rest of the sentence now. So did Wren. We were both carrying that knowledge the way you carry broken glass: carefully, quietly, cutting yourself anyway.

The halls opened as we descended. Small ones first, rooms like knuckles, then galleries where the ceiling rose into dark that swallowed torchlight. Everything was warm. That's the thing I hadn't expected—the warmth. I'd imagined the court beneath the mountain as cold, because cruelty is supposed to be cold, but this place was warm the way a fever is warm, the way a hand closed around your throat is warm. Every surface gave off heat like the mountain itself was a body with a temperature and a pulse.

And everywhere, the embers. In cages, in braziers, in lanterns of pierced silver, in walls where the stone had been hollowed into honeycomb cells and each cell held a flame. Some burned steady. Some guttered. One, near the second gallery, flickered in a pattern I recognized before I understood why I recognized it—the particular double-stutter of someone who had spent their life flinching. A widow's memory, maybe, or her mother's. Someone from my valley, carried down here decades before I was born, still flinching.

"Don't count them," Wren whispered. They'd caught me slowing.

"Why not?"

"Because you'll want to count them all, and we don't have that kind of time." A pause. "And because you'll find one you know."

Too late for that last part.

* * *

The throne room announced itself the way cathedrals do: not with a door but with a change in pressure, a sense that you had stopped walking through a building and started walking through an opinion.

It was vast—I'll give the Ashen King that. Vast and dim, the ceiling lost somewhere above in a haze of ember-light, the floor polished obsidian-dark and so still it looked less like stone than like water deciding not to move. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, motionless in that drifting way they had, and at the far end, raised on a dais of fused glass—

The brazier.

It was smaller than I expected. That's the detail that keeps catching in my memory like a fishhook. After eleven days of prophecy and dread, after halls full of caged souls and a mountain with a heartbeat, I expected the Ember itself to be enormous, a bonfire, an insult of light. Instead it sat in a bronze brazier no bigger than a washbasin, plain as a cookfire, and it burned low and steady and *orange*, the exact orange of the lamp my mother used to leave lit when she thought I was asleep.

And it was flickering in the rhythm of my heart.

I know how that sounds. I stood there and checked it the way I check everything, twice, three times, professionally suspicious of my own perceptions—because a thief who trusts her eyes dies young. I pressed two fingers to my wrist. Beat. Beat-beat. Rest. The flame matched it. Not approximately. *Exactly.* The little stutter I get when I'm frightened—and I was, make no mistake, frightened down to the marrow of me—the flame stuttered too, faithfully, honestly, a small fire doing an impression of my chest.

"Rafael," Wren said softly.

"I see it."

"It's seeing you."

That was the true thing, and the terrible one. Because the flame wasn't just flickering at me. It was *reaching*. I don't mean it leaned. I mean that across forty feet of polished floor and four hundred watching courtiers, I felt something extend toward me the way a hand extends in the dark—not grasping, not desperate, just *sure*. Certain of where I was. Certain that I was the thing it had been reaching for since before I had lungs.

Recognition. That's the only word for it, and it's insufficient. It was like meeting a twin you'd never known existed—like looking at your own face in the old book's portrait, except this face was made of fire and it had been waiting a hundred years, patient as only a caged thing is patient.

My soul. Mine. Sold before my birth, kept alive in a bronze dish, fed on god-knows-what for twenty-nine years, and the first thing it did upon finally seeing me was not rage, not accusation, not even fear.

It warmed. I felt myself warm in answer, someplace I don't have anatomy for.

*Oh,* I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *Oh, you're real.*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing: the hardest part has never been the taking. The taking is easy. Locks are just puzzles, guards are just people, people are just doors left carelessly open. The hardest part is the moment *after* you have the thing in your hands and you realize it is heavier than it looked, or lighter, or warmer, or it has a name scratched on the bottom, and suddenly it isn't loot anymore. It's a grief. Every theft I ever committed ended the same way: me holding somebody's most precious thing and understanding, far too late, exactly how much it was worth.

Standing in that hall, I understood that I had been stolen from before I drew breath, that the thief had been my own mother, that the fence had been a king of ash, and that the goods had spent twenty-nine years alone in a dish, flickering in time with a stranger's heartbeat because it was the closest thing it had to a companion.

And I understood—I felt it land in me like a dropped coin—that every theft I'd ever committed had been practice. Thirty years of lifting kisses and daughters' faces and dead sons' laughter, and it had all been rehearsal for the only heist that mattered: stealing myself back.

"You're crying," Wren murmured.

"I don't cry."

"You're crying right now, though."

"Then the air in here is disgraceful," I said, and Wren made the small sound that is their version of a laugh in dangerous places, and the sound steadied me more than any plan had.

Because here was the other thing, the thing I had to look at sideways, the way you look at the sun: the flame was *happy* to see me. Twenty-nine years of captivity and its first impulse was warmth. Whatever the court had made of my soul—and God knows what rituals, what feedings, what slow atrocities—it had not managed to make it bitter. It reached for me the way you reach for the window in a stuffy room. Instinctively. Gratefully.

And I reached back, which was the mistake, or the beginning of the mistake, or possibly the only honest thing I've ever done.

I let the reach happen. Just for a second. Just to feel it.

The connection opened like a door in a house I'd lived in my whole life without knowing had a cellar. And what came through it wasn't words. It was *knowing*. It was twenty-nine years compressed into a single instant of shared attention: darkness, warmth, the rhythm of a distant heart it had learned to love the way a prisoner loves the moon. It knew my name before I offered it. Not *Rafael*—the other name, the one I didn't have, the one the prophecy called *no-name*, the one that must have been written in some ledger down here since before my birth. It knew my mother's hands. It knew the bargain. It had *heard* the bargain, I realized—it had been present, small and newly-lit, when she made it, and it had spent its whole short furious existence trying to forgive her.

Which was more than I had managed.

And underneath all of it, threaded through everything, one simple message, repeated the way you'd repeat something to a child, or to someone drowning:

*I'm here. I'm here. I'm here.*

"Well," I said, out loud, in a hall of four hundred enemies, "that makes one of us."

* * *

A courtier turned its head.

Just one. A tall figure near the dais, moth-winged silk—no. Not Vesperine; someone older, greyer, their face like a page written on too many times. Their eyes found the space where I stood, and tracked along it, and stopped. Not quite on me. Near me. The way a dog stares at a spot where a ghost might be sitting.

I pulled my invisibility close, that old familiar cloak, and felt it settle around me—and felt, for the first time, its *texture*. It had never had texture before. In the valley it was nothing, a quality of being overlooked, fog given personality. Here it had weight and weave, here it hummed faintly against my skin, and here—this was the part I made myself look at—it answered to the same heartbeat as the flame.

My gift. My precious lifelong talent, the thing that had kept me fed and hidden and alive. It wasn't mine. It was a leash. It was property marking itself, a brand worn on the inside, the court's claim stitched into me so deep I'd mistaken it for a self.

The courtier lost interest, or seemed to. Its gaze slid on. But my hands were shaking now, and Wren saw, because Wren always sees.

"Talk to me," they breathed.

"It's mine," I whispered. "The Ember. It's—" I swallowed. "Wren, it's *glad*. Twenty-nine years in a dish and it's glad to see me. What kind of—" My voice did something humiliating. I pushed through it. "What kind of creature forgives the people who caged it?"

"The kind you'd raise," Wren said simply.

And that—that undid me more thoroughly than any horror in this mountain could have. Because Wren said it without poetry, without ceremony, just as fact, the way they draw coastlines: this is where the land ends, this is where the water starts. The kind you'd raise. As if my soul were a child I'd sent away and it had come home kind anyway.

The flame in the brazier flared. Not much. A fingertip's worth of extra height, a brightening like a held breath. Across the hall, several caged embers swung on their chains, turning toward their uncaged sibling like flowers toward an illegal sun. Somewhere high in the haze, something metal groaned.

And on the dais, in the great chair of fused glass that faced the little bronze dish—the chair I had somehow, until this moment, failed to properly look at, the way you fail to look at the one thing in a room you cannot bear—

Something moved.

Not the King. Not yet. This was smaller: a stirring among the courtiers nearest the dais, a ripple through the ranks, the whole beautiful congregation leaning a degree or two toward the brazier, the way the embers leaned toward me in the corridors. As if the entire court were one organism, and it had just felt a change in its weather.

The temperature climbed. The obsidian floor underfoot went from warm to hot, gently, gradually, the way a bath goes wrong. The murmur of the caged souls rose from sighing to something with intent in it.

Wren's fingers found mine again and gripped hard enough to hurt. "Rafael."

"I know."

"It knows you're here."

"No," I said, watching the flame stand up straight in its dish, tall and bright and unmistakably, defiantly *aware*, watching it turn—not metaphorically, actually turn—toward the empty glass throne and the figure now rising from behind the dais, tall as a doorway, grey as the far side of a fire. "It doesn't know I'm here."

The Ashen King lifted his head.

"*It called me.*"

He looked directly at the space where I stood. Not near it. At it. At me. Four hundred courtiers turned as one, and every caged ember in the hall bent toward me like grass under wind, and the man of ash smiled the smile of a landlord greeting a tenant who is many years behind on rent.

"There you are," he said, and his voice was warm as fever, warm as my mother's lamp, warm as the thing in the dish that loved me. "Little heir. Come and sit. We have kept your seat warm for a very long time."
