## 14. Audience with the Ashen King

There is a particular quality to being announced that I have never gotten used to, mostly because it has never once happened to me.

In the valley, doors open for other people. Names are called across squares; hands are shaken; chairs are pulled out. I have spent thirty years on the other side of all that, sliding in through kitchens and slipping out through gardens, taking what I needed and leaving nothing behind but a small, inexplicable gap in someone's memory—a first kiss here, a dead mother's voice there—that they would notice only later, the way you notice a tooth missing.

So when the doors at the end of the ember-hall swung open on their own hinges, both of them, with a groan of old stone, and a voice like cooling ash said, *She comes*, I confess my first instinct was to look behind me.

There was no one behind me. There is never anyone behind me. That has been the entire architecture of my life.

Wren's hand found mine—found *it*, rather; found my elbow, because we had agreed in the blind passages that touching hands was too dangerous, that if one of us flinched the other would feel it—and squeezed once. Our signal. *I'm here. Don't do anything stupid.*

It is a very versatile signal.

"You should wait here," I breathed.

"I absolutely should not," Wren breathed back, which is what I'd expected, and loved them for, and resented, all at once.

The courtiers drifted aside to let me pass. That was the thing about them—the not-seeing. A hundred of them stood in that hall, moth-winged silk and candle-colored skin, and not one pair of eyes turned as I walked through their midst. I had spent three days telling myself this was my own doing, my own craft, the thief's invisibility I had honed since childhood. It had been a comfort, that story. I had been proud of it.

Then Vesperine's face had gone still when she looked at me—actually *looked*, the first courtier ever to manage it—and she had smiled like a door opening onto a cellar, and told me the truth I have been carrying in my chest like a stone since: that I did not hide from the court. The court simply does not see its own possessions. My gift was never mine. It was a brand worn on the inside, a collar stitched into my soul before I drew breath, and every year of my careful, invisible, unremarkable life had been nothing more than inventory moving quietly through a warehouse.

I walked down the hall anyway. What else was there to do? The throne was ahead. My heartbeat was ahead. Eleven days early or not, the debt had come looking for me, and you cannot outrun a creditor who lives inside your ribs.

* * *

The Ashen King did not look like a king.

He looked like the last hour of a bonfire. He sat—not on the throne itself, I noticed; on a plain stone bench beside it, the way a farmer sits beside his best horse rather than riding it at market—wrapped in a robe the color of cooled coals, gray shot through with dying orange veins. His face was long and kind and terribly tired, the face of a man who has been awake for a century listening to other people sleep. When he saw me coming, he rose. He *rose*. And he inclined his head.

"Thief," he said. "You're shorter than the portrait."

"The portrait lies," I said. "It's the first thing I learned about portraits."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a smile had been considered and found too expensive. "Sit, then. If you won't be flattered, at least be comfortable." He gestured, and a chair appeared—or rather, I noticed a chair that had been there all along, which in this place may amount to the same thing. It stood at a respectful distance from the Ember-throne, close enough to feel its heat on the back of my neck like a hand.

I sat. I did not look at the throne. This took discipline I want credited somewhere.

Behind me, the flame that lived in the brazier flickered, and I felt the flicker in my own chest, the way you feel a mirror-image heart beating out of rhythm with yours and then, horribly, back into rhythm. My soul. Caged in fire for thirty years, and aware, and reaching. Every second I sat there it pressed against me like a child against a window. I am going to be honest: it took everything I had not to climb into the brazier right then, consequences be damned, and I want it noted that I didn't, because everything afterward depends on the fact that I didn't.

"You came early," the King said. "The contract said solstice. Most debtors spend their eleven days running."

"I don't run well uphill."

"No," he agreed mildly. "You steal well, though. Better than any heir we've crowned. The widow's grief in the eastern valley. The rich man's golden afternoon. The peddler's daughter—" he paused, delicately, "—you kept that one, I think. Her face. You carry it still."

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "How—"

"This court holds every memory that was ever taken in the valley's shadow. Did you think your little trade went unwitnessed? We watched you work for thirty years, Rafael Marchetti. You were our finest instrument, and you never once knew it." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty would have given me something to push against. He said it the way you tell a fish about water. "And now you've come home."

"I haven't." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I've come to take something back."

"Yes. Yourself. I know." He folded his hands. His fingers were long and gray and gentle-looking, the hands of someone who had done a great deal of comforting and a great deal worse. "But first—I promised myself I would make you an offer before you made your choice. Not a threat. Threats are for people who have something to lose, and I'm told you've arranged your life so as to have nothing." His tired eyes glinted. "Though I notice you brought the cartographer."

"Leave Wren out of this."

"They wrote themselves in. Or were written. The margins are unclear even to me." He waved this off, and I hated how easily he waved it off, how a person's fate could be a marginal note to him. "The offer, then. Hear it whole before you spit. You owe me that much for the climb."

I owed him nothing. But the heat on my neck was steady and patient, and my soul was listening through my ears, and so I nodded.

"You have spent your life invisible," the King said. "Not by choice—I know that now matters to you, and I'm sorry for it, though sorry is cheap coin here—but by design, after. You erased yourself beautifully. No debts, no attachments, no trail. Do you know what I saw, all those years, watching you work?"

"A tool."

"An artist," he said, "starving in a valley that couldn't feed her. Every memory you took, you took alone. You carried a hundred lives inside you and shared none of them. You sat in taverns full of people whose dearest moments you had held in your own hands, and you could not say *I know you*, because knowing them was theft. Tell me I'm wrong."

I didn't say anything. Which he took, correctly, as agreement.

"Here is my offer." He leaned forward, and the dying embers in his robe brightened. "Come to the court willingly. Take your place. You would be seen, Rafael—truly seen—for the first time in your life, by a hundred courtiers who have waited three decades for their heir. You would learn what your gift is actually *for*. What you have been doing in the valley with a spoon, we do here with tides. You could hold a stranger's wedding day in your palm and give it back improved. You could walk into any mind in creation and be welcomed. Nothing taken again—only given, traded, shared. You would never be invisible another day of your life."

The heat on my neck pulsed. Somewhere under my sternum, my soul flared like a struck match, and I felt—God help me—I felt *yes* move through it like a shiver through wheat.

"And the price?" I made myself ask, because there is always a price; it is the first thing a thief learns, that free things are bait.

"The succession," he said simply. "In time. Not tomorrow. Not for years—the throne burns slow. But eventually you sit where I sit, and the Ember passes to you, and you keep what I have kept."

"Which is what?"

"Everyone." He said it softly, reverently, and gestured at the hall—at the thousand caged embers overhead, each one a soul, each one burning in its own private rhythm, a sky full of stolen hearts. "Every soul in this hall was taken from someone who chose safety over feeling, or survival over a child, or a bargain over a famine. They are mine as those memories are yours. I keep them. I warm them. And in return, the court endures, and no one below ever needs to know what was paid." His eyes came back to mine, and they were kind, and that kindness was the sharpest thing in the room. "You would never be alone again. You would have a hundred lifetimes of other people's loves to keep you company. Isn't that what you've always wanted? To stop standing outside the window?"

* * *

Here is the thing I have not told anyone, not even Wren, not even in the dark passages where we told each other everything else.

For about four seconds—four honest seconds, maybe five—I wanted to say yes.

Not the power. Power has never tempted me; power is just weight you have to keep lifting. But *belonging*. Being seen. Thirty years of walking through crowds like a draft through a room, of eating dinner alone, of knowing the intimate geography of a hundred strangers' hearts while none of them knew the color of my eyes—and here was an old gray king offering me the exact shape of my loneliness, filled in, warmed, lit with a thousand patient flames. He hadn't threatened me. He hadn't needed to. He had simply walked to the deepest wound I carried and poured himself into it like wax into a mold, and the mold fit perfectly, and that is how I knew the offer was a lie.

Because that's the tell. Real belonging doesn't fit your loneliness exactly. Real belonging is awkward. It involves a cartographer with ink on her fingers who squeezes your elbow too hard and refuses to wait by the door. It involves being known in pieces, over years, badly. What the King offered was loneliness's perfect mirror image—which is to say, more loneliness, gilded. A hundred courtiers waiting to see their heir. Not *me*. Their heir. Their possession, come home to the display case.

And underneath it, quiet as a ledger line: every one of those welcoming courtiers would see me because I would finally be marked as property. The collar made visible at last. That was the whole gift. *We will see you, once you belong to us openly.*

I looked past the King, at last, at the throne—the brazier, and the flame inside it that beat with my heart. And the flame flared when my eyes met it, eager, hopeful, *reaching*, and I understood suddenly that my soul had been offered the same bargain from the other side of the glass, and had been refusing it for thirty years, and had sent me here—not the prophecy, not the contract, not my mother's ghost—*my soul had called me here*—to break the glass.

"No," I said.

The word dropped into the hall like a stone into a well. Courtiers three aisles away turned their heads—felt it, some frequency of refusal traveling through the ember-light. The King's kind face did not change, but the orange veins in his robe dimmed, just slightly, like a held breath.

"No," I said again, and stood, and it felt like standing up out of a grave. "Thank you. Genuinely. That was the cruelest thing anyone has ever offered me, and you dressed it beautifully, and I need you to know I saw the stitching." I stepped back from the chair, toward the throne, toward the fire that was mine. "You're right that I wanted it. You were wrong about why. You think I stole a hundred lives because I wanted company. I stole them because taking is the only way I ever learned to touch anything. And you want to make that permanent. You want to give me everything in exchange for becoming exactly what you are—a keeper of a prison, calling it a family."

"Child," the King said, and for the first time his voice had edges, ash grinding on ash, "if you refuse the succession, the Ember dies when I do. And everything in this hall dies with it."

"Then I suppose," I said, drawing the thief's stillness around me like the fog of home, "we'll both have to be very clever, very quickly."

And behind me, the flame that had beaten with my heart for thirty years went suddenly, blindingly bright—and in its light I heard, from somewhere near the great doors, Vesperine's voice, silk over a blade:

"Well. Since we're all being honest at last—"
