## 15. Vesperine's Favor

I had always assumed that if I ever died by violence, it would be something mundane—a cart accident, a fall down a badly designed staircase, the sort of thing you could put on a map with an asterisk and a note about structural negligence. I had not expected to die of arithmetic.

Vesperine did her sums in moth-wing silk, which is to say: beautifully, and with a faint dusting of powder that got into everything.

"Sit," she said, in a chamber off the third gallery where the walls were veined with ember-light like marble holding a secret fire. The furniture was low and black and looked as though it had been grown rather than made. I sat, because my legs had opinions of their own and none of them involved running.

She poured two cups of something that steamed violet. I didn't touch mine. In my experience, courtiers who pour you drinks are either poisoning you or courting you, and Vesperine was far too efficient for the second.

"You've been drawing," she said.

"I'm a mapmaker. It's considered traditional."

"On the mountain." She smiled. Moth-wing silk, moth smile—all shimmer and no teeth visible until it was far too late. "Your lines move stone, little cartographer. Do you know how long it's been since anything moved the stone of this mountain that wasn't the mountain's own will?"

*I know exactly what my lines do,* I thought. *I found out four days ago, when a passage I'd sketched in frustration opened like a mouth.* I said nothing. Silence is also a kind of map; you chart what the other person fills it with.

Vesperine filled it with violet steam and patience, which frightened me more than threats would have. Threats have edges. You can navigate around them.

* * *

Here is what I had been doing instead of sleeping, in the eleven—no, nine, now eight—days since Rafael told me the truth with their hands shaking around a book bound in bark: I had been mapping.

Not the honest way. Not the way I'd learned, with chain and compass and the patient accumulation of error. On the mountain, my maps had begun to make themselves true. I would draw a corridor and hear, distantly, stone shifting to accommodate it. I drew a stair once, idly, in the margin of a page, and the next morning there was a hairline seam in the rock shaped exactly like my hatching strokes, as if the mountain were a sketchbook and I'd pressed too hard.

This should have delighted me. A person spends their whole life being told their talent is for *accuracy*, which is a polite word for *irrelevance*, and then one day they draw a door and the world says *yes*. But the prophecy had gotten there first. *The mapmaker who draws the way in, and cannot draw the way out.* I had read that line so many times the ink had worn soft under my thumb, and every time I read it, I heard the same cold sentence underneath: *you are a tool, and tools are used up.*

So I had done what any sensible person does with a fatal prophecy. I kept it from Rafael, and I worked on the problem alone, at night, while they slept badly three feet away and I pretended to be asleep beside them, listening to their heartbeat like it was a metronome I could set my courage to.

And Vesperine, apparently, had noticed.

* * *

"The favor," she said, on the second cup—the one I still hadn't touched—"is small."

"Everything is small right up until it's yours."

She laughed, genuinely, and I hated that I liked the sound. "You're wasted on the valley. Very well. Straight dealing, since you insist." She set her cup down and unfolded her hands, and for the first time since we'd met her on the mountain's shoulder, she stopped performing. What was underneath the performance was worse. Underneath, she was hungry in the old way, the way that has outlasted whatever it was originally hungry for.

"The throne room has wards. Old ones. Older than the King, if you can believe anything here is older than the King. They are woven into the architecture—not painted on, not carved, *woven*, thread over thread, and the threads are not thread. I cannot see them. I have lived six hundred years in this court and I cannot see them, because they were built to be invisible to those who belong here." Her eyes came up to mine. They were the color of a moth's wing backlit by flame. "But you don't belong anywhere, do you, Wren? And things built to hide from the court are not hidden from you. Your drawings have already brushed them. Don't look surprised. Stone remembers what touches it, and I can read stone."

My mouth was very dry. The violet steam smelled, now that I paid attention to it, like the moment before a storm. "You want me to map the wards."

"I want you to *draw* them. Mapping is your word for it, and it flatters you, so use it. Draw me the weave of the throne room, thread by thread, and I will give you the thing you want most." She let that sit. Courtiers always let things sit; it's how they get you to fill the silence with your own worst wants. "The unguarded hour. The King distracted. A path through the halls that even the court's eyes will slide past. Everything you need to take your thief up to that throne and take back what's theirs, before the solstice turns and the debt collects itself."

"And what do you need it for?" I asked, though I knew. Of course I knew. "The wards. Why do *you* need them mapped?"

"Why does anyone climb?" Vesperine said lightly. "To see what's at the top."

"Liar," I said, before I could stop myself, and then, because I was going to die anyway, possibly today, I said the rest of it: "You want the Ember. You want the throne. You've wanted it for six hundred years and you need someone who can see the lock because you were never given a key."

The silence afterward had weight and temperature. Somewhere beyond the chamber wall, the caged embers of the great hall hummed their thousand-note chord, each one a soul, and I thought: *each one a person who once sat in a chair like this one, across from someone in silk, being offered something small.*

"Yes," Vesperine said simply. "That is exactly what I want. Isn't it refreshing, in a court, to hear it said plainly?" She leaned forward. "Now. Here is the arithmetic, mapmaker, and I'll give it to you slowly, because unlike most people who come through this court, you seem capable of counting."

* * *

The arithmetic was this:

One: Rafael's soul hung on the throne, and the debt came due at the solstice, and refusing the debt meant the court took it by force, which meant—per chapter and verse of a prophecy written in ink that predated both of us—that Rafael died or worse, or accepted the Ashen King's offer and became the court's heir, which was death wearing better clothes.

Two: the only plan anyone had was mine. The real one. The one I hadn't told Rafael yet, because telling it aloud meant admitting what it cost, and I wasn't ready to watch their face when they understood. My plan needed time, and access, and above all it needed us alive long enough to reach the valley's edge with the Ember in hand.

Three: Vesperine knew about my drawings. Which meant she knew about the passages I'd already sketched, the blind routes, the seams I'd cracked in the mountain's skin. If I refused her, she could close those doors behind us with a word to the King—or simply open the right door at the wrong time and let the court find two thieves standing in its nursery, hands in its cradle.

Four: if I agreed, I would be handing a six-hundred-year-old predator the layout of the last defense around the souls of everyone in this hall, including Rafael's, and hoping I could betray her faster than she could betray me.

"That's not arithmetic," I said when she finished laying it out. "Arithmetic has answers."

"Arithmetic has *consequences*," Vesperine said. "Answers are a comfort people invented for children. Count it however you like, but count it honestly: refuse me, and I am your enemy with all I know. Accept, and I am your enemy with a delay in it. One of those gives you days. The other gives you hours." She slid a sheet of paper across the table—real paper, cream-colored, soft as skin—and a stick of silverpoint. "Begin with the eastern arch. That's where the oldest thread runs."

And here is the part I think about when I can't sleep, which on this mountain is most nights: I picked up the silverpoint.

I told myself it was strategy. I told myself the words *double agent* and *long game* and *the only way to protect Rafael is to appear to cooperate*, and all of those things were true, in the way that a map is true—it shows the terrain, but it doesn't tell you where you'll fall. Because underneath the strategy, cool and quiet as groundwater, was something simpler and uglier: I wanted to live. I wanted Rafael to live. And Vesperine was offering me the one currency this court accepted for both—complicity—and when the choice is between your love's survival and your clean hands, I learned something about myself in that chamber that I have not yet told anyone: my hands went dirty without hesitating. They were drawing before my conscience finished clearing its throat.

Silverpoint doesn't erase, incidentally. Cartographers choose it for permanence. Every line I laid down on that cream paper—thread over thread over thread, the ward-weave of the throne room blooming under my hand like frost on glass—I chose knowing it couldn't be taken back. The oldest thread ran through the eastern arch, silver-white and humming. The second crossed beneath the dais steps. The third was coiled around the brazier's housing like a sleeping snake, and when my stylus touched its line, I felt the touch *echo*, somewhere far below us, the way you feel a footstep in a floorboard through your soles.

The wards felt me feeling them.

I don't know how else to say it. Something vast and patient turned over in its sleep beneath the mountain, and for half a heartbeat every ember in the hall flickered in unison, a thousand flames blinking like one enormous eye opening.

Vesperine went very still. "Careful," she murmured, and for the first time, she sounded mortal.

* * *

I gave her three-quarters of the truth. That's a specific skill, lying by proportion: you draw the weave accurately up to the point where accuracy becomes betrayal, and then you bend it—one thread misplaced, one crossing drawn as parallel, one anchor shifted six inches left. To anyone reading the map, it would be perfect. To anyone *using* the map, at the critical moment, it would fail—gently, plausibly, like weather. Like bad luck.

It's the same trick I use on survey commissions, honestly. Every cartographer lies a little; the earth is round and the paper isn't, so somewhere, something must give. I just never thought the thing giving would be the security of a soul-prison.

While I drew, Vesperine talked. That was our arrangement—her voice covering the scratch of my stylus, the way you hum over the sound of breaking something.

"Your thief," she said. "Do they know what you've become?"

"A mapmaker."

"A maker whose maps *make*." She watched my hand. I kept it steady through sheer professional spite. "Stone moves for you. Wards show themselves to you. You were born in a valley where magic cannot follow, and the magic followed anyway, across the fog, to find *you*. Doesn't that suggest anything? Some doors don't open unless they were cut to fit a particular key."

"I've read the margins, thank you. I know what the prophecy calls me."

"The prophecy is a contract, not a fortune," Vesperine said idly, and my heart stopped against my ribs like a bird hitting a window. She noticed—of course she noticed—but misread it, or pretended to. "Everyone here treats it as scripture. It isn't. It's paperwork. Drafted, witnessed, filed. Which means, little mapmaker, that paperwork can be *amended*—by whoever holds the pen." She smiled at my drawing. "Hold the pen. That's free advice, and worth more than the favor I'm buying."

*Paperwork.* I put the word away carefully, the way you pocket a key you find in a locked house. If the prophecy was a contract, then maybe it could be breached, renegotiated, voided. Maybe *cannot draw the way out* was a clause, and clauses had conditions, and conditions had loopholes, and loopholes were just doors nobody had bothered to draw yet.

I finished the eastern arch. I bent the third thread. I added, from memory, a service passage behind the dais that did not exist—yet—and felt, distantly, deep in the bones of the mountain, stone beginning to consider whether to exist.

"There," I said, and turned the paper toward her. "Thread by thread. As promised."

Vesperine studied it for a long time. Moths, I remembered too late, are drawn to light, not to truth; she examined the glowing lines of the weave and never once glanced at the shadows where I'd hidden my lies. Six hundred years of hunger, and hunger only looks forward.

"Adequate," she said, which from her was rapture. She folded the map into her sleeve and rose, silk settling around her like wings folding. At the door she paused. "One more thing, freely given. When the moment comes—and it will come sooner than you've planned—you will have to choose whom to save first, because you will not have time to save both. The thief or the way out. Everyone in this court eventually learns the same lesson." Her smile was gentle as ash falling. "The mountain keeps the ledger. It always balances."

Then she was gone, and I sat alone in the ember-veined dark with my untouched violet cup and my shaking hands, doing the sum she'd left me.

Because here is what she didn't know—what nobody knew but me and the cream-colored paper now hidden in the false bottom of my map case, beneath three layers of harmless coastline surveys: I had already chosen. Weeks ago, in the margin of a page, at three in the morning with Rafael's heartbeat keeping time against my shoulder blades.

I wasn't drawing a way in for Vesperine. I had never been drawing a way in.

Every stroke I'd made since the fog line—every passage, every seam, every stair I'd conjured out of stubbornness—was one leg of a single continuous line, a route with a shape no one had noticed because no one had seen the whole map at once. A route that began at the throne and ended at the valley's edge. A way to carry the Ember out through the fog, where magic starves and courts cannot follow.

There was only one flaw in it, and I had written it myself in the smallest letters I owned, in the corner of the page where I keep the things I can't say aloud: *someone carries it the whole way. it burns the whole way.*

The mountain keeps the ledger. Fine. Let it balance.

But I would be the one who decided what went on the scale—and tomorrow, over breakfast, with the solstice three days off and the court's civil war already smoldering in Vesperine's sleeve, I was going to spread the whole map across the table and show Rafael what I'd really been drawing.

I just hadn't yet figured out how to tell them the price.
