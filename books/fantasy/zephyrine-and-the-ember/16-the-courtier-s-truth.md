## 16. The Courtier's Truth

Vesperine kept rooms in the mountain the way other people kept secrets: carefully, and where no one would think to look. Wren found them for me—a spiral stair behind a tapestry that wasn't a tapestry, a door that opened onto warmth instead of stone, a chamber lit by three embers floating in glass like wasps in jars. I didn't ask how Wren knew. My cartographer had spent two days with their nose against the walls of this place, drawing lines that moved when you weren't watching, and I had learned not to interrupt them mid-thought unless the building was on fire.

The building, as it turned out, was about to be.

She was waiting for us. Of course she was. Moth-winged silk, that smile like a key turning in a lock, seated in a chair carved from something pale and rootlike, drinking from a cup that steamed violet. She'd been waiting since before we climbed the stair. Possibly since before we were born.

"Rafael," she said, warmly, as if we were old friends. "And the mapmaker. Sit. Both of you. What I have to say goes better sitting down."

"I'll stand," I said.

"You will." Her smile widened a fraction. "That's very much in character. Very well. Stand."

Wren took the second chair. I noticed they positioned it at an angle—half-facing Vesperine, half-facing me—and I understood, with a small cold turn in my stomach, that they were placing themselves between us. Not obviously. Just geometrically. That's what love looked like on Wren: sightlines and escape routes.

I got to business the way I do everything. Sideways, and with my hands in someone else's pockets.

"You knew my mother," I said. "You unlocked her memory for me. You've been generous. Generous people want things, and I'd rather hear the price now than after I've paid it without noticing."

"Mm." She set down her cup. "You are your mother's child. She also hated owing anyone anything. It's why she owed everyone everything." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Very well. The truth, since you've earned a piece of it, and since the King is about to give you the rest in a shape designed to break your heart. You should hear it from someone who wants something *else* from you first."

"That's not reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be. Reassurance is for people whose problems can be soothed." She folded her hands. "Tell me what you know about the prophecy. The book. The one bound in bark, written by a dead peddler's hand, describing a thief with no shadow and no name."

I told her. The portrait that was my face. The solstice debt. The line about the mapmaker who draws the way in and cannot draw the way out—Wren flinched when I said that part, and Vesperine's eyes flicked to them with interest, filing the flinch away somewhere.

"And what did you conclude?" she asked. "When you read it?"

"That I was fated to come here. That some fortune-teller a century ago scribbled my face into a book and set my life on rails, and that I've been walking those rails my whole miserable life without knowing it."

"Yes." She nodded slowly. "That is precisely what you were meant to conclude."

* * *

The room went very quiet. Even the caged embers seemed to dim, listening.

"Prophecies," Vesperine said, "are how the court does its paperwork."

Wren laughed—one short, disbelieving note—and then stopped, because Vesperine wasn't smiling anymore.

"A prediction requires a future that hasn't happened yet," she went on. "The court doesn't deal in futures. Futures are unreliable. Futures require *hope*, and hope is the one currency we cannot mint. No—the court deals in agreements. Contracts. Terms and conditions, signed in advance, sealed in blood or soul or both. A prophecy is simply a contract that has been dressed up in poetry and hidden in a book, and left somewhere it will be found by the person who signed it."

"I never signed anything."

"Your mother did." She said it gently, which made it worse. "Before your birth. And you—" she pointed one pale finger at my chest, at the hollow place behind my ribs where a soul should sit, "—you signed it too. In your mother's blood, in the first hour of your existence, when the contract required an heir and the heir's own consent could be... presumed. Infants are very agreeable signatories. They don't read the fine print. Neither, as it happens, did your mother. She read the famine clause. She skipped everything else."

The floor did not actually tilt. It just felt that way—the particular vertigo of standing on ground that turns out to be paper, drawn over a pit.

"So the book," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "The peddler. He didn't find it. He was *sent*."

"He was dying and he wanted his daughter's face remembered. The court arranged the meeting. The court arranged the price. The court, Rafael, arranged *you*—every step of the road you walked to get here was drafted, clause by clause, in a hand far older than mine. The fog that hid you. The valley that forgot. The gift of vanishing that kept you alive and alone. All of it provisions. All of it clauses. You have spent your whole life executing a document you've never read."

I thought of every theft I'd ever committed. Every memory lifted, every trace erased, every year spent being nobody on purpose. I had built that life myself. I had *chosen* invisibility, chosen the valley, chosen the fog—my one act of authorship, my single original decision.

Except it wasn't. It was a term of the contract. I was living out the fine print.

"The thief with no shadow and no name," I said. "That's not a prophecy. That's a *job description*."

"Now you're beginning to understand why you were paid so well."

* * *

Wren had gone very still in their chair, the stillness of someone doing arithmetic fast. I watched their ink-stained fingers curl, uncurl. "Then the way in," they said quietly. "The route I mapped. The wards I can unravel. That's in the contract too?"

"Clause nine," Vesperine said, almost kindly. "'The mapmaker who draws the way in.'" A beat. "And cannot draw the way out."

"Why?" Wren's voice didn't rise. That's the thing about Wren—anger makes them quieter, more precise, like a blade being narrowed. "Why put that in writing? What's the purpose of telling someone they're going to die in a place?"

"Because fear shapes behavior. Because a person who believes they cannot leave stops planning exits and starts planning *use*. Because the court wanted a cartographer who would map with nothing held back, nothing reserved, no secret route home tucked in a sleeve." Vesperine spread her hands. "You drew honestly because you believed you had nothing to survive for but the work. That was the intent of the clause. Whether it worked—" her eyes moved between us, and something unreadable passed through them, "—remains to be seen."

I should have felt fury. I did feel fury—it was there, banked and black—but underneath it, colder and heavier, was something else. The realization that the question I had been carrying since the bark-bound book—*is the prophecy a cage or a choice?*—was itself a lie. A false binary, drafted by lawyers. There was never a fate to defy or accept. There was only a document, and documents have loopholes.

"Enough," I said. "The throne. The succession. The Ashen King offered me a place at court—heir, belonging, every memory I could steal. What I want to know is why a king needs an heir *now*. Kings don't recruit. They inherit."

"Ah." Vesperine picked up her cup again, and drank, and I understood I was finally asking the question she'd been waiting for all evening. "Yes. That. Would you like the version the King will tell you, or the version that's true? They're nearly identical. The difference is only in what he leaves out."

"True. Always true."

"The Ember holds souls. A thousand caged flames, each one a person entire—the hall around you is a prison, and the Ember is its lock and its keeper and its fuel all at once. The Ember does not burn wood, Rafael. It burns *will*. Souls feed it willingly or it consumes them anyway, and the willing ones burn slow and bright and painless, and the unwilling ones—" she shrugged elegantly, "—well. The halls are full of the screaming kind. You've heard them. You thought it was wind."

I had thought it was wind.

"The Ashen King is not a man who wears a crown," she continued. "He is the crown's current holder—an office, a function, the soul who agreed to tend the fire and keep the terms. And the terms are expiring. Contracts do. Even ours. Especially his. His tenure ends at the turning of the season, and when it ends, either a successor accepts the office *willingly*—" she let the word land like a coin on a counter, "—or the Ember loses its keeper, and an unkempt Ember does not sleep. It starves. And when the Ember starves, it eats what it holds."

The cold came up through the floor then. Through my feet, my spine, my teeth.

"Every soul in the hall," I said.

"All thousand. Every flame guttering on those walls. They go out—not peacefully, not into rest, but *into* the Ember, devoured to buy it another century of hunger. Your refusal would be felt in this mountain like an earthquake, and heard in it like a feast."

* * *

I stood there in the moth-lit room and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was all I had left. I had spent my life taking things from people—a kiss here, a face there, a daughter from a dying man—and I had told myself the cost was contained. Small evils, fairly traded. But this—this was the ledger beneath all ledgers. If I walked away, if I chose freedom, if I did the brave romantic thing and refused the cage—

A thousand people burned. People with mothers. People with first kisses that some valley thief hadn't gotten to yet.

And if I accepted—if I sat on the throne and took up the office—I lived. Comfortably. Eternally. Fed on willing souls, tended and warm, with Wren beside me if they could be persuaded and a court bowing to the thief they had drafted into godhood.

The moral floor didn't just tilt. It dropped out entirely, and beneath it was not bedrock but another floor, and beneath that another, and I understood suddenly why the court built downward.

"There's no third option," I said. "In the contract. There's no clause for refusing and surviving."

"Not in the contract." Vesperine's smile returned, thin as paper. "But you are a thief, Rafael Marchetti. And contracts are only words, and words—" she lifted her cup in a small toast, "—can be stolen. The King needs your willing acceptance. Which means the one thing standing between him and the Ember's survival is your consent. Consent cannot be forced. But it *can* be forged, or feigned, or given falsely—or withdrawn, once given, at the worst possible moment. Ask any lawyer what terrifies them most about a signature." Her eyes glittered. "It's the hand that signs it."

Wren spoke up softly. "You're telling us to trick the King."

"I'm telling you what I want," Vesperine said. "Which is the Ember. Whole, unbroken, and in my hand. The King's crisis is my opportunity, and your performance is my instrument. Help me take it, and I will see you both out of this mountain alive—with your souls, such as they are, and your mapmaker's exit clause torn up with my own fingers."

"And the thousand souls?" I asked.

"Are fuel," she said, "and will remain fuel, whoever tends them. Sentiment is heavy, Rafael. You cannot carry a thousand lives and your own. Choose your own."

I looked at Wren. Wren looked back at me, and I saw the same arithmetic running behind their eyes—and something else, something they were hiding even now, some figure they'd already solved and folded away. Their fingers twitched toward the satchel of maps, then stilled.

Later, I promised myself. Later I would find out what they were carrying.

For now I turned back to Vesperine, and put on the best face I owned—the one I'd worn through a hundred heists, the one that says *yes* while the hands are already moving.

"Fine," I said. "The audience is tomorrow. The King expects his answer." I let a pause stretch, long enough to be dangerous. "So tell me exactly how one goes about stealing a fire."

Vesperine leaned forward, moth-silk whispering, and began to explain—

And that was when the doors burst open, and a courtier I'd never seen stood trembling in the entrance, ember-light guttering across its face, and announced that the Ashen King would receive the thief *tonight*.
