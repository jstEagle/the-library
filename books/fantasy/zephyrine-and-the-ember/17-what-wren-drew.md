## 17. What Wren Drew

I have been lying to Rafael for eleven days.

Not the loud kind of lie—the kind with shouting and slammed doors and someone storming out into the fog. No. My lie was quiet. It lived in my satchel, rolled up in oilcloth between the bread knife and the spare ink, and it grew a little larger every night while I sat hunched over it by candlelight, drawing lines that no honest map has any business containing.

Cartographers have a rule. It's the first rule, actually, older than projection or scale: *you draw what is there.* The land tells you what it is, and you write it down faithfully, and if the land lies to you, that's the land's sin, not yours. I built my whole life on that rule. Every map I'd ever made was a small act of devotion to the truth.

Which made what I'd been doing at night either the worst betrayal of my profession or its finest hour. I hadn't decided which.

* * *

It started, properly, on the third day out from the valley—though if I'm honest, the seed of it was planted back when I first read the margins of that bark-bound book and found my own name waiting for me like a trap I'd walked into years before I was born. *The mapmaker who draws the way in, and cannot draw the way out.*

Rafael had gone pale as unbleached paper when I showed them that line. They'd started talking immediately—too fast, too many reassurances, the particular torrent of words that people use to bury something they can't bear to look at. But me? I did what I always do with terrible information. I sat down, I sharpened a pencil, and I thought about it geometrically.

Here is what I saw, once I stopped panicking long enough to look:

The prophecy said I could draw the way in. It also said I couldn't draw the way out. Everyone—including, for a while, me—read that as doom. A one-way door. Wren leads them in; Wren never leaves.

But I am a cartographer, and cartographers know something that prophets apparently don't: *a map is not the territory.* The prophecy described the mountain's magic, not mine. The mountain's magic says nothing leaves. Fine. But the valley's magic says nothing magical enters—or endures. Two laws, colliding at the fog line like two weather fronts.

And what happens at the boundary of two immovable laws?

Nothing. Everything stops. Magic fails. Both magics fail, caught between each other, canceling out into ordinary foggy air where a person can simply *walk*, unmolested by destiny.

The prophecy bound me inside the court's law. So I would take Rafael—and whatever we stole—to the edge of the other law, and let the two laws fight it out over a strip of fog while we slipped through the gap.

You cannot draw the way out *of the mountain*. Nobody said anything about drawing a way through the place where the mountain's power runs out.

I know. It's a technicality. It's the kind of reading that would get you thrown out of any respectable guild of prophecy-scholars, if such a thing existed. But technicalities are how I've survived my whole life, and besides—I checked the wording four times. Prophecies are contracts, and contracts live and die on their exact words. I had spent three nights copying the relevant passages onto separate sheets just so I could lay them side by side and squint at them, and every time, the same loophole grinned back at me.

So I drew. Not the route in—that part was done, folded away, already serving its purpose. I drew the way *through*. The fog line, mapped to the width of a hair. The dead ground beyond it where the court's reach thinned to nothing. The precise distance a soul carried in bare hands could travel before—

Well. Before the part I hadn't been able to make myself calculate honestly. More on that later. Or possibly never.

* * *

The trouble with secret plans is that they require secrecy, and secrecy requires acting normal, and I have never once in my life acted normal around Rafael Marchetti. I flirt by arguing about contour lines. I show affection by correcting their pronunciation of valley towns. Normal, for me, means telling Rafael everything, immediately, at volume. So for eleven days I had been walking beside them saying nothing—which is to say I had been screaming.

They noticed, of course. Memory-thieves notice everything; it's the whole trade. On the evening of the eleventh day, sitting in the lee of a granite shoulder with the court's ember-light bleeding red through the cracks in the stone above us, Rafael finally put down their bowl of trail food and said, "You've got your lying face on."

"I don't have a lying face."

"You do. Your mouth goes very serious, like it's holding the lie down with both hands." They tilted their head. "You've had it on since the bridge."

Since the bridge. Since they'd paid a regret to the toll-keeper and watched it evaporate, and then asked me which regret I'd paid, and I'd said *none of your business* with a smile, and they'd let it go, because Rafael lets things go the way other people hold on. I wondered, not for the first time, whether the regret I'd paid had been visible to them somehow—whether memory-thieves could smell a missing thing the way sailors smell a storm.

"It's nothing," I said.

"Right," said Rafael, in the voice of a person who has heard 'right' mean 'everything' roughly nine hundred times.

And here is the thing about love, which nobody warns you because everyone who knows it is too busy being embarrassed: it makes liars of careful people. I had planned to keep the map hidden until the last possible moment, on the reasonable theory that hope is heavier than fear and Rafael was carrying enough weight already. That was the plan. That was a good plan.

Then Rafael looked at me across that little fire with their too-quiet eyes, this person who had spent twenty-odd years being nobody so hard that they'd almost convinced even themselves, who was two days from walking into a throne room to steal back their own soul, and who trusted me—actually trusted me, the way you only trust someone after you've watched them pay a toll in regrets without flinching—and I thought: *if this goes wrong tomorrow, and they find the map afterward, rolled up in my satchel among my bones, they will spend whatever's left of eternity believing I hid it because I didn't believe in them.*

I would rather die with them angry at me than have them grieve me politely.

"I need to show you something," I said, and reached for the oilcloth.

* * *

I unrolled it between us on the flat rock, weighting the corners with stones, and watched Rafael's face do several things in quick succession.

First: confusion, because what they saw looked like the valley—not the mountain, the valley, the fog line drawn in careful double strokes, the fields, the town, the little crooked roofline of Rafael's own street rendered in the shorthand I use when I'm tired.

Second: recognition, because they were a thief, and thieves read maps the way priests read scripture, and I could see them tracing the route with their eyes—out of the court, down the blind passages, along the mountain's western flank, down, down, to a single point at the fog line marked with my personal sign for *here the rules change*.

Third: understanding. And with understanding came the stillness. Rafael went so still that for a moment I thought the court had already taken them, some ward plucking them silent out of the air.

"You've known," they said finally. "Since—" Their hand moved, indicating everything: the book, the margins, the whole cursed architecture of our lives. "How long?"

"Since the night I read my own name in the margins." I kept my voice level. Professional. This was, after all, a briefing. "Everyone reads that line as a death sentence. *Cannot draw the way out.* But prophecies are written by lawyers, Rafael. And lawyers make mistakes, or rather—they make assumptions. They assume the reader wants to escape the mountain. They never imagine anyone might want to leave *differently*."

"Differently." Rafael's eyes hadn't left the map. "Explain."

"The court's law ends at the fog line. The valley's law begins there. At the seam, both fail. Not gradually—all at once, like a candle going out. I've charted it." I tapped the double-stroked line. "This isn't guesswork. I measured it the morning we left. Walked right up to it with my compass spinning and wrote down exactly where sense gave up."

"And?"

"And so we don't run out of the mountain. Running gets you chased. Chasing is what the Ashen King is *good* at—he's had a thousand years of practice hunting anything that tries to leave his law." I pulled the pencil from behind my ear, an old habit, and immediately wished I hadn't; it made my hands look less steady than my voice. "Instead we *walk*. Slowly. Respectfully, even. We walk out the front, down the pilgrim road, like we're leaving under parley. And at the fog line—" I traced the final stretch, the short one, the one I'd drawn over and over until the paper shone— "the Ember dies. The court's power fails. Whatever we're carrying becomes just—an object. A warm rock. Nothing worth pursuing, because pursuit requires power, and power stops at that line like water at a dam."

Rafael was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere above us, deep in the mountain, something vast shifted in its sleep, and a fine sift of stone dust drifted down through the ember-light.

"That's the plan," Rafael said at last. "That's the whole plan. It's good. It's—" They laughed, short and disbelieving. "Wren, it's better than good. Why have you been hiding it? Why have you been looking at me all week like you were composing a eulogy?"

And there it was. The question I'd been rolling toward for eleven days, the way a cart rolls downhill, inevitably, gaining speed.

I looked at the map instead of at them. At the fog line. At the little annotation in my own handwriting that I'd tried three times to erase and finally left, because erasing it felt worse than writing it had.

"A map has to be honest," I said. "Every line. Even the ones you hate. Especially those." I swallowed. "There's a problem with the plan, Rafael. There's one thing standing between us and that fog line, and it isn't the Ashen King."

* * *

Here is the problem, stated plainly, the way I should have stated it eleven days ago:

The Ember is a soul. A thousand souls, fused into one flame, and Rafael's soul at the heart of it like the seed of a fruit. And souls raised in the court are creatures of the court's law. Take the Ember past the fog line, and the law dies—but the Ember doesn't die instantly. It dies the way a fire dies when you carry it out into a rainstorm: fighting the whole way, consuming whatever it can reach, trying to feed itself on anything that burns.

And the only fuel it can reach, held in bare hands across six miles of dead ground, is the hands that hold it.

I had done the arithmetic at three in the morning, four days ago, by the light of a stolen candle. Six miles. The rate at which the court's fire consumed what fed it—I'd gotten the figure from Vesperine, indirectly, from a careless sentence she'd let drop about how the throne must be fed. I'd run the numbers the way I run any survey, twice, and then a third time hoping I'd made an error.

I hadn't made an error. The Ember could be carried to the fog line. It could cross. It would survive—barely, guttering, but survive, because Rafael's soul at its core was born half-valley and would remember how to breathe ordinary air.

But whoever carried it would be burned down to match. Not killed—killed would be clean. Consumed. Fed into the flame mile by mile, memory by memory, self by self, until the fog line took what remained and the Ember crossed into safety riding on the ash of the person who'd carried it.

Unless the carrier had enough of themselves to give. Enough to feed it *instead* of being fed from.

I didn't know how much was enough. That was the honest horror of it. Maybe a hundred memories. Maybe a lifetime.

Maybe exactly one life, precisely measured, and the measurer was me.

* * *

"There's a price," I said.

Rafael waited. They were good at waiting; invisibility teaches patience the way rivers teach valleys.

"The Ember won't survive the crossing on its own. It's court-born. Past the fog line it starts to starve, and it feeds on whatever's nearest." I finally made myself look up. "Whoever carries it pays. In themselves. Memories, first—it eats outward, oldest to newest, or maybe newest to oldest, I don't know, the direction hardly matters—"

"No." Rafael said it fast and flat, the way you say *no* when you already understand everything and are trying to outrun the understanding. "No. You're not carrying it."

"Someone has to—"

"*I'll* carry it. It's my soul. It reaches for *me*. You saw it at the throne, it practically sang—"

"And it will burn you alive, Rafael, because your soul might be at its heart but the rest of you is valley-raised, fog-fed, and the Ember will treat you like kindling the entire way—" I heard my own voice crack and hated it, hated the crack, hated the honesty of it. "I've done the sums. Four times."

"Then do them again!"

"They come out the same!" And now I was on my feet, because sitting felt unbearable, because the firelight was suddenly too much like ember-light, because eleven days of silence had rotted through me and come out all at once. "They come out the same every time, and the same is: somebody walks six miles holding their own funeral pyre, and I have spent every night this week trying to design a plan where that somebody isn't you, and there isn't one, and I am *not* letting it be you."

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

Rafael stood slowly. They came around the fire, and they looked down at the map—at my map, my honest, treacherous, loving map—and their finger came to rest on the fog line, and then moved, deliberately, to the small annotation beneath it. The one I'd failed to erase.

*carrier pays in full,* I had written, in the neat hand I use when I don't want to feel something.

"Whose hand were you planning to be in?" Rafael asked quietly.

And because I had committed myself, eleven days ago, to a map that told the truth, I said: "Mine."

"No."

"Yes."

"No." They grabbed my shoulders—not gently, not angrily, but with the fierce precision of a person handling something breakable. "Listen to me. You want to talk sums? Let's talk sums. If I carry it, I burn. Fine. But here's what your arithmetic missed, cartographer: I'm a thief. Stealing is the one thing I do better than anyone alive. And you want me to stand there holding my own soul while it eats *you*?" Their voice dropped, went rough. "I spent my whole life taking memories from strangers because I couldn't stand to lose my own. Do you think I can watch it happen to you? Do you think I could walk six miles doing that?"

"Yes," I said. "If the alternative is me watching it happen to you."

We stood there, gripping each other's shoulders like two people fighting over the last rope on a sinking ship, and I realized we weren't arguing about arithmetic at all. We were arguing about love, which is the same argument wearing different clothes, and it has no solution, only choices.

"We could split it," I said, because despair invents compromises the way rot invents mushrooms. "Carry it together. Alternate—"

"It doesn't work that way. Your own map shows it. Contact has to be continuous or the Ember gutters and the King feels it and comes running." Rafael closed their eyes. When they opened them, something had settled in their face—something that frightened me more than the Ashen King, because it looked like decision. "Then there's another answer."

"Don't."

"You said the Ember eats what's nearest. Oldest first." They smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd ever seen, bright and terrible, the smile of a person about to commit magnificent theft. "A hundred lives' worth, Wren. That's what I'm carrying. Every memory I ever stole, every job, every stranger's first kiss and last goodbye—they're all in here." They touched their own temple. "Let it eat those. Let it eat a century of other people's lives before it gets within a mile of mine. Or yours."

"That's still—you don't know what that costs, giving up that much of yourself—"

"I know exactly what it costs." For a moment the brightness went out of their face, and underneath it I saw the thing I'd seen the night they told me about the book: not fear. Loneliness. Twenty years of it, banked like coal. "But it's a price I can pay, and it buys the crossing, and it keeps the two people I—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "It keeps you safe. That's the whole plan now. Everything else is negotiable."

I wanted to argue. Believe me, I catalogued arguments the way I catalogue landmarks—dozens of them, ranked and ready. But I looked at the map, at the fog line, at my own honest handwriting, and I understood that Rafael had just done to me what I'd spent eleven days failing to do to them: told the truth, all of it, and stood in the open space the truth made.

"One condition," I said.

"You're not in a position to bargain."

"Nobody ever is. That's what bargaining is." I picked up the pencil. "You carry the Ember. You feed it your stolen memories. And I walk beside you the entire way, and I write down everything—every name you give it, every memory you feed it, every piece of yourself it takes. All of it. In full. Because if the Ember's going to eat a hundred lives, then a hundred lives are going to exist somewhere besides inside a flame, and I am going to be that somewhere. You don't get to vanish, Rafael Marchetti. Not while I'm holding the pen."

Rafael stared at me. Something moved through their face like weather—grief and relief and something bigger than either, the thing people mean when they say a word they're too embarrassed to say.

"You'd spend the crossing writing?" they asked. "While the world's ending?"

"I'd spend the apocalypse writing," I said. "It's what I'm for. Didn't you read the prophecy?"

That startled a laugh out of them—short, wet, real. Then they looked west, toward the fog line, invisible in the dark but present on the map between us like a promise. Two days' walk. One theft. Six miles of burning.

"All right," Rafael said. "All right. Tomorrow we steal my soul. Tonight—" They nodded at the map. "Show me the route. Every line. Leave nothing out."

So I knelt on the cold stone beside the person I loved, and I showed them the way out—the real one, the one no prophet had imagined, drawn in graphite and stubbornness on the back of a lie I no longer needed. Outside, the mountain breathed its ember-light against the stars. Inside the stone, something vast turned over, restless, beginning—perhaps—to dream of us.

By dawn, the court would know we were coming.

Neither of us said it aloud. Neither of us had to. We just rolled up the map together, four hands on one truth, and went to sleep facing the door.
