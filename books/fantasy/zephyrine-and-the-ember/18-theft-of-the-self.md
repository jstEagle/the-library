## 18. Theft of the Self

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing from a king: it is much easier when he wants to give you everything.

The Ashen King stood before his throne with his arms open, ash-grey robes pooling around him like smoke that had given up on rising, and made his offer for the third time. Belonging. Power. A hall full of souls I could pluck like ripe fruit whenever loneliness got too loud. Every word of it aimed, with surgical cruelty, at the part of me that had spent thirty years eating dinner alone.

"You were made for this court," he said. "Look how well you wear its colors."

I looked down at myself. In the ember-light, my hands had gone translucent at the edges, the way they always did when I worked — except here it wasn't work, it was just *me*, unspooling faintly into the air like thread off a spool. Court-magic. My invisibility, my whole life's careful craft, wasn't mine at all. It was a brand. Property marks its goods.

"Generous offer," I said. "Let me think about it."

Which was true. I was thinking about it very hard, just not about the thing he thought I was thinking about.

*Wren,* I thought. *Tell me you're ready.*

Somewhere behind the eastern wall of the throne room — behind stone that Wren had spent three days mapping with charcoal and stubbornness — there was no answer. There was never an answer. That was the arrangement: I would feel nothing, see nothing, hear nothing, and trust anyway. Trust, as it turns out, is the one thing I never learned to steal. You have to be given it. Wren had given it to me so many times I'd finally learned the shape of it in my hands.

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because the plan deserves better than to die unwitnessed inside my own head.

We had gathered in Vesperine's chambers, which smelled of moth dust and old promises. She'd laid out her coup like a woman dealing cards she'd already counted: her faction of courtiers, disgruntled, hungry, ready to rise the moment the wards flickered. All she needed was Wren's maps of the throne room's defenses.

"And when the wards fall," she said, silk wings rustling, "I take the Ember, I take the throne, and you two take whatever scraps of freedom remain. Everyone eats."

Everyone eats. I've heard that pitch from every con artist in three valleys. The tell was always the same: *everyone* meant *me*, and *scraps* meant *you*.

But we needed her chaos, so Wren agreed, all ink-stained fingers and guileless eyes, and handed over maps that were beautiful and wrong.

That's the part I want carved on something, someday. The maps Wren gave Vesperine showed every ward in the throne room — truthfully, accurately, lovingly rendered — and also showed them all connected in ways they weren't. Vesperine believed that unraveling the outer ring would collapse the whole lattice. It wouldn't. The real lattice, the one on Wren's hidden copy, ran inward, coil by coil, toward the throne itself, and only someone standing where I was going to stand could pull the last threads.

"The mapmaker who draws the way in," I'd quoted at them, "and cannot draw the way out."

"I've been thinking about that line," Wren said, and their chin did the thing it does when they're terrified and refusing to blink first. "Prophecies are drafts. Contracts get amended. Watch." They turned their own map over, and on the blank back, in quick sure strokes, drew the throne room again — but this time the lines didn't describe the room. They *argued* with it. And the stones of Vesperine's floor shifted, faintly, with a sound like a sigh.

Small magic. Their own magic, not written by anyone's contract. The court hadn't planned on Wren. That was our entire strategy, really: the prophecy had accounted for a thief, a courtier, a king. It had not accounted for a cartographer who took notes in pencil because everything deserves the chance to be erased.

* * *

Now the audience hall, and the moment itself.

The Ember burned on its brazier at the heart of the throne, and within it — I will say this plainly, because I earned the right to plainness — a flame beat in the rhythm of my own heartbeat. Not near it. Not echoing it. *It.* When my pulse stuttered, it stuttered. When I breathed, it flared.

And it was reaching for me. I don't mean mystically; I mean I could feel it the way you feel a hand hovering an inch from yours in the dark, warmth without contact, patient without hope. Thirty years old. Sold before I drew breath. It had been waiting in this cage since before I existed, and it knew me the way you know your own reflection, and it was *glad*, and I nearly broke right there. Nearly walked to the throne, sat down, accepted the whole rotten inheritance, if only to stop it reaching.

The Ashen King saw my face change and misread it beautifully. "You feel it," he said softly. "Of course you do. It has been calling you home your entire life. Every door that closed for you, every face that forgot you — that was the court keeping what is ours safe until you came of age." He extended a hand the color of cooled ash. "Take your place, thief. Stop being the absence in every room. Be the room."

Temptation aimed precisely at my loneliest wound — he'd had a century to aim it. And here is the terrible confession: for one full second, I wanted it. One full second of imagining walking back down the mountain wearing a crown instead of a debt, of never having to vanish again because I'd finally matter enough to be seen.

Then I remembered Wren's face when they'd said *love stated as a plan*, and the second passed, and I got to work.

Because that's the other thing about theft that nobody tells you: the best time to steal is during a gift. A king handing you his kingdom is looking at your face. He is not looking at his throne.

I let my edges blur — court-magic, my brand, my leash, and tonight, my crowbar. Thinning out the way I'd thinned out of ten thousand rooms, I drifted sideways along the dais, insubstantial as heat-shimmer, while the Ashen King watched the place where I'd been standing and waited for his heir to answer. The courtiers saw nothing. They were built not to. The wards felt nothing. I was already inside their definition of permitted absence.

Three steps to the brazier. Two. The heat should have been unbearable; it was the opposite, it was *recognition*, warmth pouring into the hollows of me like water finding cracks.

*Hello,* said the flame. It didn't use words. It used the memory of my mother's laugh, which I had never possessed, delivered straight into the center of my chest. *You came.*

"I came," I whispered. "Hold still. This is going to feel like being stolen."

It flared — joy, unmistakably joy — and I reached past the brazier's iron lip into the fire itself, and did the thing I had done ten thousand times to ten thousand strangers, and did it now to the only self I had ever been owed.

I took it.

* * *

Stealing a memory feels like lifting an egg from under a sitting hen: warm, resistant, guilty. Stealing a soul is nothing like that. Stealing a soul feels like being born.

The flame came up out of the brazier and into my hands, and my hands remembered how to hurt, and my chest remembered it had been a hollow all these years — a rented room, a stage set, a shape where a person was supposed to stand. The Ember sank into me like a key turning. Light went through my veins and lit me from inside, and for the first time in my life, in any life of mine, I cast a shadow.

A long, dark, gloriously ordinary human shadow, thrown huge across the dais by the thousand caged embers of the hall.

Every soul in those cages saw it. I heard them notice — a rustle through the hall like wind through wheat, a thousand small flames leaning toward me at once. The property, marked and branded, casting a shadow. Casting a *self*. Somewhere in the lattice, a ward registered the impossible and began, very quietly, to fail.

The Ashen King turned.

I will give him this: he didn't shout. He looked at me standing there blazing, holding my own soul in cupped hands like water I'd carried across a desert, and he said, with genuine grief:

"That was the only version of this where anyone survived."

"See," I said, "I think that's the sales pitch talking."

And then the world ended, on schedule.

* * *

It began as a sound like tearing cloth, vast and slow. Vesperine's people hit the outer wards with everything they had, exactly as her stolen maps instructed — and the maps were wrong in exactly the way Wren had drawn them, so the outer ring didn't collapse the lattice. It *unraveled sideways*, peeling off the walls in ribbons of dying light, and each ribbon that fell freed a little more of the hall's attention from the throne, and each degree of freed attention was a degree the courtiers spent looking at Vesperine instead of me.

Chaos is a generous word. What happened was civil war in a room made of tinder. Courtiers who had smiled at each other for centuries drew knives of grey glass. Vesperine rose above it all on moth-winged silk, radiant and ruthless, shouting claims to the empty throne — empty, because I was standing next to it holding its heart.

And through it all, the walls moved.

I felt it before I understood it: the eastern wall bulging, then folding, then opening like a book to a page that hadn't existed an hour ago. Wren's map, arguing with the mountain, winning. A corridor of raw new-drawn stone, lit by nothing, leading away.

*I can't draw the way out,* they'd said, afraid. Well. Apparently you can't. But you can draw a way out for somebody else, which might be braver, or might be the same thing wearing different ink.

"WREN'S CORRIDOR!" I screamed into the chaos, knowing they couldn't hear me, trusting anyway — trust, given freely, the one thing I never had to steal. "THIRD PILLAR, THEN DOWN!"

The lattice's last coils snapped somewhere overhead with a sound like a harp breaking, and I felt it go — felt every ward between me and the valley's fog become simply stone, simply distance, simply a long hard climb. Nothing magical left in my way. Only the King, and the court, and the price.

The Ashen King came off the dais like ash off a log, robes streaming, and the hall bent toward him even mid-war, because some gravity is older than rebellion. His face had stopped being grief and become something I recognized from mirrors, from bad nights, from the bottom of every lonely year: hunger wearing patience as a mask it had dropped.

"You cannot carry it across the fog," he said. "It dies at the line. You know this. Your soul dies at the line, and you will have murdered yourself for a mapmaker's pretty arithmetic—"

"Been threatened by better," I said, which was a lie, and I lit the lie with my own soul's light so it looked true.

He lunged. I ran.

* * *

Running through a collapsing court with your own soul in your arms is exactly as dignified as it sounds. The halls were coming apart — Wren's unraveled wards had been load-bearing in more than metaphor, and the mountain was renegotiating its architecture in real time. Corridors folded. Stairs became ramps became waterfalls of dust. Caged embers swung on their chains as the ceiling groaned, and every one of them watched me pass, and every one of them *hopeful*, and I couldn't carry them all, I could barely carry me, and their hope was heavier than the soul was.

Third pillar. Down. The corridor Wren drew opened ahead of me, impossibly smooth-walled, smelling faintly of graphite and rain, and I threw myself into it as the throne room's roof came down behind me in a long white roar.

For one blessed moment: silence, and running, and the weight in my arms beating like a second heart.

Then the wall beside me tore open, and the Ashen King stepped through it, because of course he could; it was his mountain. But he didn't lunge. Worse. He held out his hand, palm up, gentle as a priest.

"Keep it," he said. "Keep the soul. Walk away. Live your small fog-bound life, thief. Just leave the Ember on the ground behind you, and I will forget the valley exists. I will forget *you* exist." A pause, delicate as a blade. "No. Better: I will make sure no one ever forgets you again. Every name in your ledger, returned. Every face remembering you. Isn't that what you want? To be *seen*?"

My feet slowed. Damn him. Damn him for doing his homework.

Behind him, deeper in the ruin, I could hear Vesperine screaming her faction forward, and the particular crackle of moth-winged silk catching fire — her coup becoming her funeral in real time, though I didn't know that yet, not for certain. Ahead of me, the corridor bent downward toward cold air and the smell of pine, and somewhere down that dark, a person with ink-stained fingers was waiting, having drawn a way out they themselves could not take.

Two doors. Be forgotten and free, or be remembered and owned.

I had been the first thing my whole life. It had never once kept me warm.

"No," I told the Ashen King, and my soul flared in my arms and threw his shadow backward down the hall. "But ask me again in a mile. I'm told I'm persuasive when I'm tired."

I ran, and the mountain shook, and the King's roar came after me down Wren's drawn dark like winter itself in pursuit —

—and ahead, at the corridor's end, a lamp. Small. Steady. Held by someone who had never once, in all my life of taking things, asked me to steal anything for them.

"Rafael!" Wren's voice, cracked with terror and relief in equal measure. "The fog line's two miles — tell me you've got it!"

I tightened my arms around my own burning heart and answered the only honest way I knew how.

"I've got it," I said. "Now run."
