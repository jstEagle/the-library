## 19. The Long Way Down

**Rafael**

Here is a thing they never tell you about stealing from gods: the hard part isn't the taking. The hard part is the running.

The Ember sat in my hands like a heart that had decided to keep beating out of pure spite, and the throne room came apart around us in slow, burning pieces. The caged souls overhead swung on their chains, each one shedding light like a struck bell sheds sound, and the whole vast hall groaned—a sound I felt in my teeth, in the hollow where my soul had lived for twenty-six years before I took it back.

Wren grabbed my wrist. "Stairs," they said, with the calm of someone reading a map only they could see. "Left of the dais. The one that shouldn't exist."

"The one you drew into existence this morning."

"Maps are suggestions," Wren said. "The mountain just doesn't know it yet."

We ran. Behind us the Ashen King rose from his ruined throne—or didn't rise, exactly; he *reassembled*, ash pulling itself back into the shape of a man the way water pulls back into a puddle. His voice followed us down the first flight of stairs, and it was terribly, horribly gentle.

*"Thief."*

"Not today," I said, to a king, over my shoulder, while fleeing with his entire kingdom in my arms. If I was going to die, I was going to die insufferable.

*"You hold the sum of everything I am,"* he said. *"And you run through my halls as if they are not also yours. Rafael. Child of the bargain. Heir of the Ember. Stop running and take what was always meant for you."*

The stairs went down and then, in defiance of every reasonable expectation, kept going down. Wren's map-unmade-stone shimmered at the edges, passages opening ahead of us like eyes blinking awake. I could feel the wards unraveling behind us—Wren's ink, somehow, working backward through the mountain's bones, erasing as fast as we moved, closing doors that had stood for a thousand years.

"How long can you keep that up?" I gasped.

"Ask me when we stop," Wren said, which meant *not long*, which meant *run faster*.

The King's voice again, drifting down through stone that should have swallowed it: *"I offer you the throne. Not as a cage—as an answer. You have spent your whole life invisible. Here, you would be eternal. Every memory in this court would open to you like a book. No more trading kisses for bread. No more being no one."*

And here is the thing I will not pretend, even now: it landed. It landed in the loneliest room in me, the one I'd built my whole life around, and it turned the key.

"I'm listening," I called back, because honesty costs nothing when you're already planning to betray the listener.

Wren shot me a look.

"I'm *listening*," I repeated. "Doesn't mean I'm agreeing. There's a difference. It's a very important difference. Keep drawing."

* * *

**Wren**

Three things I knew as we ran:

One: the mountain was dying around us, and it knew who to blame.

Two: Rafael was listening to the King, and I couldn't fault them for it, because I'd seen the shape of their loneliness from the outside for months, and if someone offered me eternity as an antidote to that, I'd have paused too.

Three: my hands were bleeding.

Cartography, it turns out, has a physical cost when you do it live. Each passage I pulled out of the mountain's blind places cost me a line drawn in something other than ink. I'd mapped us four miles of corridor that shouldn't exist, and my fingertips were split and red, and I was wiping them on my coat and thinking, distantly, *this is going to ruin these pens*, which is what my mind does instead of screaming.

"Right here," I said. "There's a landing—there's going to be a landing—in thirty steps."

"There is *nothing* there," Rafael said.

"There will be."

There was. Stone unknitted itself into a wide shelf overlooking a hall so deep the embers below looked like a city seen from the air at night. Which, in fairness, it was.

Below us, the court was tearing itself apart. Vesperine's coup had caught the way fire catches in dry grass—courtiers against courtiers, moth-winged silk blazing, cages swinging loose from the ceiling and raining light. Somewhere in that glow Vesperine was hunting the Ember she'd sent us to map the way toward, and somewhere above us the Ashen King was done talking gently.

The ceiling of the great hall cracked. Not metaphorically. A fissure ran its length, and through it poured the King's voice, no longer warm at all:

*"Then hear me, thief. Give back what you stole, or I will follow you past the fog. I will walk into your valley. I will find every door you ever hid behind, and I will open them, and I will start with the cartographer's."*

Rafael went very still beside me.

"He can't cross the fog," Rafael said. But their voice did the thing it does when they're checking arithmetic they already hate.

"He thinks he can," I said. "That might be worse."

Because that was the shape of it now, wasn't it. The prophecy had been a contract, the contract needed a willing heir, the heir had refused, and refusal left a king holding an empty brazier and a millennium of momentum. Men like that don't grieve. They escalate.

"Come on," I said, and drew us a stairway out of nothing with two fingers and blood, and we kept falling downward through the body of the mountain.

* * *

**Rafael**

He stopped threatening somewhere around the fourth descent. That frightened me more than the threats had.

Instead his voice found us in the dark between landings—soft, worn, almost human—and he said: *"I was stolen once too."*

We both stopped. Not because we wanted to listen. Because the words had weight, and weight slows you.

*"A thief came for the Ember, long ago, and got me instead—the keeper, the vessel, whatever word you prefer. The crown is not a crown, child. It is a sentence. And I have been serving it since before your valley had a name. Take the throne and free me. That is all succession is. One prisoner handing the chains to another."*

"Is that true?" Wren whispered.

"It doesn't matter if it's true," I said.

"It matters."

"It matters *after*," I said, "when we're not standing in a collapsing hallway deciding whether to feel sorry for the thing chasing us. Ask me at the bottom of the mountain. I promise I'll be sympathetic at the bottom of the mountain."

But I carried it anyway. That's the thief's curse—you steal everything, including arguments against yourself. Somewhere under my ribs, next to the soul-shaped absence that wasn't absent anymore, a small traitorous voice noted that I had been sold before birth by a mother trying to save everyone, and that kings and mothers and thieves are all just people making bargains with the available exits, and that maybe mercy was an exit too.

I told that voice to shut up and run.

* * *

**Wren**

We came out onto a bridge—a span of black stone across a chasm full of rising embers, freed souls streaming upward like sparks off a bonfire—and Vesperine was standing in the middle of it.

She had been beautiful the way moths are beautiful, and she had lost none of it even now, even with silk scorched at the hems and ash in her hair and her eyes fixed on the bundle in Rafael's arms with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it on maps: the look of someone who has finally found the coast after years of drawing coastlines they'd never seen.

"The favor," she said.

"We're in the middle of—" I started.

"*The favor.*" Her voice was silk over broken glass. "You mapped the wards, mapmaker. You opened the way. The Ember is *right there*. Give it to me, and I let you both walk out of my mountain alive. Refuse, and—" she spread her hands, gracious, terrible, "—well. Bridges are so fragile."

"You want to be the King," Rafael said. "That's all this was. The guidance, the memories, the mother's story—it was all just you clearing your own path to the chair."

"I want to *hold* it," Vesperine said. "There's a difference. Ask your king—he'll understand." She took one step forward. The bridge hummed. "Or give it to him, if you prefer. Either way it stays in the family. Either way it stays *here*. You were never going to carry it out, thief. Souls raised on ember-light cannot survive the fog. Your own prophecy says so. Your own plan dies at the valley's edge, and all of this"—she gestured at the burning, gorgeous, dying hall beneath us—"was for nothing."

And that's when I saw Rafael smile.

It was not a nice smile. It was the smile of someone who has been carrying a secret the size of a plan, and I thought, with a lurch, *they know something I don't, they've known it since the throne room, oh no, oh good.*

"You're right," Rafael said sweetly. "It dies at the edge. Everything does. Watch how fast I run anyway."

"No," said Vesperine, and lunged—

* * *

**Rafael**

She was fast. Court-fast, silk-and-smoke fast, and she had the reach, and for one clear moment she had her hands on the Ember and her hands on my wrists and all three of us were burning.

Because it does burn. Nobody warned me properly. Holding your own soul is like holding your hand over a candle and finding out the candle knows you—finding out it wants things from you, warmth and fuel and *witness*, and takes them whether you offer or not.

Vesperine held on. She was stronger than me. She'd had centuries and I'd had eleven days, and the bridge cracked under the weight of our wanting, and I remember thinking, absurdly, of the toll-bridge keeper, of the regret I'd paid, wondering if this counted as getting my money's worth.

Wren didn't hesitate. That's the part I keep. When I tell this story later—and apparently I will be telling it later, page by page, to someone with patient eyes—I will keep this part: Wren drew, one-handed, bleeding, a line straight across the bridge's map-self, and the bridge *obeyed*, and the span beneath Vesperine's feet simply stopped being true.

She fell reaching.

That's the image the mountain gave me to carry out, since I wouldn't carry the throne: Vesperine in her burning moth-silk, going down into the rising river of freed souls, her arms stretched up toward the Ember in my hands—not clawing, at the last. Reaching. The way you reach for a door in a dream. The way you reach for anything you spent your whole life walking toward and understand, too late and all at once, that it was always going to be the thing that killed you.

She burned the whole way down. Moth to the last.

"Don't look," Wren said.

"I'm looking," I said. "Somebody should."

* * *

**Wren**

After the bridge there were halls I only half-remember mapping, and after the halls there was a chute of raw stone I drew out of sheer desperation—*down, please, down, faster*—and after the chute we were in the deep ways, where the mountain's blood runs cold and nothing glows except what we carry.

Which was a lot. Rafael glowed now. They tried to hide it, cupping the Ember against their chest inside their coat, but light gets out of Rafael the way truth gets out of drunks: completely, and at the worst times.

We stopped in a vein of tunnel to breathe, and in the quiet I heard it—far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone—the sound of the Ashen King. Not pursuing.

*Begging.*

*"Give it back,"* floated down, thin as smoke through a keyhole. *"Please. You don't understand what happens when it leaves the hall. You don't understand what I am without it. Please. Please. I'll give you anything. I'll give you your mother. I have her, thief—I have the memory of her, whole, the way she sounded, the way she said your name—please—"*

Rafael's face did something I will never be able to draw accurately, and I draw for a living.

They knelt on the cold stone with a kingdom in their arms and listened to a king beg them for their own soul back, using the one currency Rafael had never learned to refuse: other people. Their mother. Her voice. The name.

"Rafael," I said quietly.

"I know."

"He's lying."

"I know," they said again. "Probably. Maybe. Wren, he knows how she said my name. I don't. I was a *line item* in a bargain. I don't even know what her voice—"

The tunnel shuddered. Dust sifted down. Above us and behind us the mountain was coming apart at the seams, and every second we spent feeling things was a second the mountain spent rearranging itself on top of us.

"Bottom of the mountain," I said. "You promised yourself sympathy at the bottom of the mountain. Collect it there. Run now, feel later—that's the deal."

"That's a terrible deal."

"It's the only one on offer." I held out my bleeding hand. "Come on. I've got three more passages in me and then I'm empty, so let's spend them well."

Rafael took my hand. We ran.

* * *

**Rafael**

The last passage Wren drew let out onto a slope of scree under a sky gone grey with dawn, and behind us the mountain breathed out a column of dust and dying light, and somewhere inside it a king was still calling *please* in a voice frayed down to thread.

We ran down through scrub and shadow, and the Ember burned brighter with every step—no, that's wrong. It burned *hungrier*. Like it knew. Like it could smell fog on the wind coming up from the valley, and understood, the way a drowning man understands a shore, that magic ends at that white line and souls raised on ember-light might end with it.

Vesperine had said it. The prophecy said it. Everyone, it turned out, but me.

"Wren." My voice came out strange. "When we cross—"

"I know," Wren said, and their face was doing something terrible and resolved, some arithmetic they'd finished days ago and hadn't shown me, and I was too breathless, too burnt, too *full of running* to read it in time.

The fog line rose ahead of us, pale and patient, the valley's long white answer to every question the mountain had ever asked.

The Ember flickered in my arms, in the rhythm of my heartbeat, my oldest self, my newest theft.

It was starting to gutter.

"Hold on," I told it—told myself, told the truth of me, told the flame that had reached for me across a throne room like a twin across a crowded hall. "Hold on. We're nearly home."

Behind us, the mountain screamed. Ahead of us, the fog waited to find out what we were made of.

I put my head down and ran into the white.
