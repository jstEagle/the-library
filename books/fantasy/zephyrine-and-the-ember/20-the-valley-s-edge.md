## 20. The Valley's Edge

The mountain was trying to keep us.

I don't mean that poetically. The halls behind us had begun to *lean*, stone groaning like a ship in a storm, corridors rearranging themselves with the petulant slowness of a bureaucracy that has decided you are no longer welcome. Doors we had already opened stood closed when we passed them again. Stairs that had gone up now went down, or sideways, or into walls. Somewhere far below—or above; the court had never committed to a direction—Vesperine's coup was still burning itself out, and the whole mountain rang with it, a bell the size of the world struck once and left to moan.

And behind all of it, patient as arithmetic, came the Ashen King.

He wasn't running. That was the worst of it. I kept glancing back down the tilting galleries and seeing him there, always the same distance away, walking through chaos that parted around him like water around a stone. Sometimes his mouth moved. I couldn't hear the words over the roar of collapsing wards, but I could guess at them. He'd tried *the throne is yours*. He'd tried *I will unmake your valley*. He'd tried, in the last gallery before the root-door, simply standing still and looking old, which was its own kind of argument.

Wren had my elbow. Wren, who had never run from anything slower than them, ran like someone who had drawn this route forty times in their head, because they had. Every turn they took was a turn on the map that didn't exist yet—the map they'd been drawing in secret for days, ink and intention braided together until the mountain couldn't tell the difference.

"Left," Wren gasped. "No—right. It changed. Right, Rafael, *right*—"

"I know. I felt it."

That was the other thing. Since I'd taken my soul off that throne, I could feel the court the way a fish feels water—every current, every shift, every hungry little eddy of magic looking for something to hold. Including me. Especially me. My soul had been raised here, steeped in ember-light like tea leaves in hot water, and now that it was back where it belonged, the court recognized it. Recognized *me*. The wards didn't snag Wren. They snagged me, gently, constantly, a hundred small hands plucking at my sleeves saying *stay, stay, you live here, you have always lived here.*

We burst out of the root-door into snow and moonlight, and I heard Wren make a sound I'd never heard from them before—something between relief and terror—and then we were running down the mountainside with the whole sky open overhead, which after hours beneath that roof felt less like freedom and more like being flung.

The fog line waited at the valley's edge, exactly where it had always been. A gray wall, soft-looking, indifferent. The boundary of the only country where magic could not follow. The place I'd been born behind, raised behind, hidden behind. For twenty-six years I had thought of it as a cage.

It's remarkable how quickly a cage becomes a door when something is chasing you.

* * *

We hit the fog at a dead sprint, and it closed around us like a held breath.

Here's what I had forgotten about the valley's edge: the fog isn't weather. It's an absence. Walking into it is like walking into a room where someone has just finished sweeping—every loose thing gathered up and carried off. Sound went first, then color, then the sense of the mountain at our backs. Magic unraveled off my skin in long gray threads and simply stopped existing, and I have never in my life felt so clean or so naked.

Wren staggered beside me, coughing. I kept hold of their hand because letting go seemed, in that moment, like a thing the universe would take advantage of.

And then the Ember screamed.

Not aloud. Not in any way Wren could hear. It screamed along the inside of my arms, up through the bones of my wrists where I carried it cradled against my chest like the world's most catastrophic infant. We had crossed maybe thirty paces into the fog when its heat stuttered—actually *stuttered*, the way a candle gutters in a draft—and I looked down at my own hands and saw the light inside it flicker in the rhythm of a heart that was missing beats.

"No," I said. Which is the traditional opening move of every negotiation ever lost.

I knelt right there in the wet grass, Wren dropping beside me, and turned the Ember over in my cupped hands. Up close, away from the court's vast ambient glow, I could finally see it properly: not a coal, not a jewel, but something older and stranger—a knot of light with a texture to it, dense and folded, like a flame that had been burning so long it had developed layers. Like a soul that had learned to be a fire because no one ever came to be a soul instead.

Except now someone had come. And the someone had carried it out of the only atmosphere it could breathe.

"The fog," Wren said. Not a question. Wren had seen the map of this in their head before I had, I think. "The court can't reach in here. Its magic dies at the line."

"And it *is* the court's magic." My voice came out very flat, the way mine does when the alternative is screaming. "Every soul in that hall. Raised on ember-light. Fed on it." I swallowed. "Take it past the line and you don't free it. You suffocate it."

The Ember flickered again. And—I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left—it reached for me. Not with heat. With recognition. The same reaching I'd felt across the throne room, the twin-pulse thing, *there you are, there you are, I have been waiting in the dark for so long.* It pressed against the edges of itself, against my hands, and dimmed another degree, and somewhere in the hall we had fled, I knew without knowing how, a thousand caged flames guttered in sympathy.

Behind us, out in the clear night beyond the fog's far side, the Ashen King had stopped at the line. I could feel him there the way you feel a draft under a door. He wasn't shouting. He didn't need to. His silence said everything the walk down the mountain hadn't: *If it dies, they all die. If it dies, you did this.*

Wren's hand found my shoulder. Ink-stained fingers, steady even now. "Rafael. Talk to me. There's a way through this. There's always a way through, we've—" They stopped. Looked at my face. Wren has spent their life reading terrain, and terrain includes people, and whatever map they made of me in that moment, they clearly did not like the elevation changes.

"There's a way," I said. "There's a stupid, obvious, exactly-like-me way."

"Those are usually your best ones."

"It's a thief's problem, Wren. That's all it is." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You can't carry fire through water. But a thief knows—you don't carry the fire. You feed it. You give it something to burn that isn't itself."

"Fog doesn't burn."

"No. But memory does."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stolen memories: they have weight. Real weight. I'd been carrying a hundred lives' worth of them for years—harvested griefs and first kisses and childhood dogs and the smell of a bakery in a town none of these valley people had ever visited—and I had always told myself they weighed nothing because they weren't mine. That was the thief's comfort. That was the lie I'd built my whole invisible life on top of.

They weighed everything.

I opened the inner doors—all of them, every cabinet in the dark house of myself—and let the fog see what was inside.

They came up whispering, the way they'd started whispering the moment we crossed out of the valley two days ago, when magic flooded back into me like cold water and every stolen thing I carried woke up and remembered it had once belonged to someone. The widow's first kiss, warm and nervous, tasting faintly of plums. The richest man in the valley's boyhood, all gravel banks and river eels. The peddler's daughter's face—gods, the peddler's daughter's face, which he'd sold me with shaking hands and which I'd promised myself I would someday find a way to give back. A hundred lives. A hundred someones' best and worst hours, hoarded in the dark by a man who was too much of a coward to keep anything of his own.

The Ember turned toward them like a starving thing smelling bread.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, *Rafael.*"

"It works." I was talking fast now, the way I talk when I'm selling something, because apparently even at the end of the world I am a salesman first. "Think about it. Memory is the most flammable thing there is—it's *made* of meaning, and meaning is the only fuel souls run on. The court feeds its prisoners on ember-light; fine, but the exchange runs both ways. I've felt it since I picked this thing up. It's been—reading me. Tasting the edges." I swallowed. "It likes the widow's kiss. It hums when I get near the bakery."

"You feed it a hundred lives," Wren said slowly, working the arithmetic, hating the answer the way I hated it, "and it burns bright enough to cross the dead air on its own."

"For a while. Long enough. Long enough to get it somewhere safe and figure out the rest."

"And you?" Wren's voice didn't break. Wren's voice did many things that night but it did not break, and I will love them forever for that if for nothing else. "What happens to you? Those aren't fuel cells, Rafael, those are *people*. Whole lives. If you pour them into—"

"Wren."

"—into a fire, what's left of the person carrying—"

"*Wren.*" I took their face in my hands, careful, the way I handle everything valuable, and their skin was cold from the fog and their eyes were doing the thing where they go bright and furious at the same time. "Look at what I'm holding. Look at what it costs to let it die. A thousand souls, minimum. Maybe more—maybe everything that court ever swallowed, going back further than the valley has records. Against that—" I shrugged, and my shrug came out steadier than I deserved. "Against that, I'm offering memories I stole. Things that were never mine. It's not even a sacrifice. It's restitution."

"That's a beautiful lie and we both know it."

"Of course it's a lie. I'm a professional."

But they weren't laughing, and neither was I, and the Ember between us had gone very quiet and very still, listening, the way it listened, the way it had *always* been able to listen, because it was not a coal, it was not a jewel, it was a soul, and souls know when someone is deciding on their behalf.

I looked down at it. At the small folded light guttering in my hands, flickering in the rhythm of my heartbeat because it had learned my rhythm across a throne room, because we were twins, because it had been waiting in the dark under the mountain for twenty-six years for the person the prophecy promised would come for it, and here I was, and the prophecy had said nothing—nothing at all—about what happened next. The prophecy had written me as a thief, as an heir, as the debt come due. It had never once imagined I might simply give something away.

For the first time in my life, nobody had written my lines for me.

"All right," I told it. Quietly. Just us. "Dinner's served."

* * *

I won't describe it fully. Not because I'm sparing you—because it doesn't compress. Memory doesn't translate into language any better than music does; you can note the key and lose the song. But I'll give you the shape of it.

I started with the ones I was proudest of, because you pay your best coin first when you're buying something that matters. The baker's memory of his wedding day, which he'd traded me for a debt he couldn't pay, and which I'd carried for six years feeling like a pickpocket at a funeral. It went into the light and the light *leapt*, and for half a second I was inside a wedding I'd never attended—saw the crooked archway, the nervous groom, the rain that everyone agreed afterward was good luck—and then it was ash and the Ember burned brighter, and somewhere out in the world a man slept a little poorer and never knew why.

Then the rest. All the rest.

The rich man's river eels. The widow's plum-sweet kiss—she'd wanted it gone, she'd *paid* me to take it, and I told myself that made it clean, and the Ember took it anyway, gently, like accepting a gift. A hundred lives, one hundred times a hundred moments, poured out of me in a long bright stream, and here is the thing I had not planned for, the thing no thief plans for: I felt each one leave. Not the memories themselves—I had no more right to mourn those than a fence has to mourn stolen silver—but the *shape* they'd left in me. Every stolen thing had carved a little hollow where it sat, and the hollows were mine, and they emptied one by one like rooms in a house with all the windows open on a windy day.

Somewhere in the middle of it I realized I was crying, and somewhere after that I realized I'd forgotten why, and both of those seemed like fine, efficient progress.

Wren held onto me the whole time. Both hands on my shoulders, grounding me, muttering things I couldn't hear over the rush of the burning—which had become enormous, which had become a sound like a library on fire read aloud, which had become, briefly, joyously, *beautiful*. The Ember rose off my palms. It hung in the fog like a second moon, blazing white-gold, throwing shadows where no light should live, and the dead gray absence all around it recoiled and thinned and *parted*, and through the gap I could see the far hills of home, and the Ember moved toward them like a ship finding its channel, dragging its own weather behind it.

A thousand souls in a hall under a mountain felt it. I know because I felt it for them—one vast indrawn breath, one instant of shared astonished hope, and then the tether between me and my own soul sang taut as a bowstring and held.

The Ember drifted ahead of us into the valley. Bright. Alive. Carried now by its own burn, fed on a century of other people's best days.

I got to my feet. The ground seemed further away than usual, and I noted this with mild academic interest, the way you note things when the part of you that would care has recently been emptied out for parts.

"Rafael." Wren was standing in front of me, gripping my forearms, searching my face with the expression of a cartographer watching a coastline change in real time. "Rafael, look at me. What's your name?"

"Rafael," I said.

Nothing happened. No echo. No weight. The word came out of my mouth sounding like someone else's luggage—familiar, handled, empty. I frowned at it. Tried again. "Rafael Marchetti."

It was a fact. It was not a memory. There is a difference, and I was learning it at speed.

"Your mother," Wren said, relentless, gentle, terrible. "Her name."

"I—" The hollow where the answer should have been yawned open, wind whistling through it. "She sold me. Before I was born. She saved the valley and damned me and she used to sing, I think, she must have sung, everyone's mother sings—" I stopped. Listened to myself. Heard all the rooms standing open and bare. "Oh," I said. "Oh, it's worse than I told you. I gave it everything. Not just the takings. The *house* too."

The fog was thinning around us, ordinary now, just weather, just the valley breathing its nightly breath. Ahead, small and gold and steady, the Ember floated down toward the sleeping village, and I understood distantly that I should follow it, that someone would need to explain to a thousand newly-freed souls why the sky smelled like weddings, and that someone appeared to be me, whoever that was going to be.

Wren didn't let go of my arms. Wren looked at me—at the stranger wearing their lover's face, at the thief with no shadow and no name, which is what the prophecy had called me all along, though it had turned out to be less a description and more a receipt—and Wren's jaw set in the particular way it set when they'd decided the map was wrong and they were drawing a new one.

"Okay," Wren said. "Okay. Then we start from the beginning. Do you remember me?"

I searched. Honestly, thoroughly, the way I search a room before a job. And the strangest thing happened—not a memory, not quite, but a groove worn deep in something, a habit of looking, so old and so practiced that it survived even the sweeping.

"No," I said. "But I think I'd like to."

Wren made a sound that was half laugh and half the other thing, and pulled me forward into the valley, following the little gold light down toward home—and behind us, at the fog line, the last thread of ember-glow sank into the grass, and the dark where the mountain stood said nothing at all.

I didn't remember my name. But I was about to learn how heavy a name can be when someone gives you one.
