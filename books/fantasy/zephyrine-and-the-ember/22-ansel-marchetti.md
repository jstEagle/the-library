## 22. Ansel Marchetti

A year is a strange unit of measurement for a person who doesn't have one.

I know this because I've been counting anyway. Three hundred and sixty-some mornings of waking in a bed that Wren made, in a house that Wren found, beside a window that looks out on the fog line where the valley ends and the world begins. I counted the days at first the way you count stitches in a wound—to make sure nothing was still bleeding. Somewhere around day two hundred I stopped counting to check and started counting to keep. That's different. That's almost the same thing as having a future.

The house sits where the fog thins to lace, close enough to the valley's edge that the mist comes visiting most evenings like an old dog that remembers being fed here once. It can't come in. Nothing from the old life can come in. The valley forgets, and beyond the valley magic dies, and I live in the seam between them, in a house with no shadow on its doorstep and a person inside it with no name that anyone gave them honestly.

Well. Almost no name.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is apparently something I do. Wren says I narrate my own life like a heist in progress—*first the kettle, then the cup, then the tea, clean getaway*—and I've decided to take it as a compliment, since I don't remember enough of my past to be embarrassed by anything in it.

This morning there was frost on the window and the book on the table.

* * *

The book bound in bark came back to me in the spring, which surprised me, because I'd assumed it had burned or drowned or been confiscated by one of the dozens of people who would have had excellent reasons to want it gone. It arrived wrapped in oilcloth in a peddler's cart—the same cart, if the wheel-ruts meant anything, the same creaking axle—and the peddler who handed it over was young, a girl with her grandfather's eyes, and when I paid her she looked at me too long and said, "He told me to bring it back to the thief with no shadow. He said you'd know why."

I didn't. I still don't, entirely. But the dying man whose daughter's face I once took for the price of this book—he got his memory's worth out of it, it seems. Some transactions keep ripening after they're done. I find that comforting in a way I can't explain without sounding like a fortune-teller, so I don't try.

The book sat on my table all summer. I dusted around it. Wren, who has the patience of a cartographer and the curiosity of one too, which is to say infinite but methodical, never touched it. "It'll open when you're ready," they said, in the tone of someone describing weather. "Books are just maps of the insides of people. You don't read a map by force."

"You've never met a map you didn't interrogate within an hour of owning it."

"That's different. Maps consent." They inked a line on whatever coastline they were inventing that week and added, mildly, "Also, you flinch every time you walk past the table. So does it. I'm choosing not to think about what that means."

So: this morning, frost on the glass, woodsmoke smell, the cat—we have a cat now, orange, disreputable, named Ember by Wren in a joke so obvious I've decided to find it funny—the cat sitting on my chest at dawn demanding breakfast, and when I came out to provide it, the book lay open.

Not fallen open. Open, pages face-up, as deliberately as a hand extended.

I gave the cat her fish first. Priorities matter even at the end of the world, or after it, whichever this is. Then I sat down across from the book the way you sit down across from someone you owe money to, and I read.

* * *

The old prophecy was still there, of course, the one that ruined my life in the best possible way. *The thief with no shadow and no name shall climb into the halls of the Ember.* I remembered the shape of those words better than I remembered my mother's voice, and I resented them for that, and I read them again anyway, because grief is a habit and habits survive amnesia. You don't need memories to mourn. You only need the space where they were. I have a lot of well-kept space.

But the last pages weren't what I left behind them. The ink had changed. I want to be careful how I say this, because I know how it sounds: the ink had *changed*, had rearranged itself overnight the way ice rearranges itself on a pond, and where the old contract—I know now it was a contract, drafted in a court beneath a mountain by lawyers with too many joints in their fingers—where the old contract ended, new lines had grown.

Prophecies, it turns out, aren't predictions. I learned that the hard way, at the cost of everything I had stolen and everything I was. They're drafts. Someone writes the world a certain way and the world, lazy, obliging, signs at the bottom. The court wrote me as their heir and I nearly was. Vesperine tried to write herself as queen and burned for it mid-reach, her moth-wing silk going up like autumn. And Wren—

Wren drew a map instead. Drew the way out of a story written by kings, and maps, unlike prophecies, don't ask permission.

The new lines were in no hand I recognized. Not the court's cramped script, not Wren's precise drafting letters. Something older and looser, like the handwriting of the mountain itself, or maybe of the thousand souls we carried out in that brazier-flame, all of them signing the ledger of their own rescue at once. It took me three readings to stop shaking long enough to parse it, and then I laughed, alone, in my kitchen, with frost on the window—a real laugh, ugly and relieved.

The final line said:

*The thief who stole themselves free.*

* * *

Here is what I did after the fog line, though I've told it badly before and will probably tell it badly again.

The Ember was dying in my hands. Everything the court was, all its stolen heat, all its caged light, could not survive the crossing into the valley where magic cannot follow—and I stood at the border holding a brazier's worth of souls in my bare hands, watching them gutter, and Wren was shouting something at me that I couldn't hear over the sound of a mountain collapsing behind us, and I understood, the way you understand things at borders, simply and completely, what had to be done.

A thief's whole craft is knowing what you can carry and what you must leave. I had spent my life leaving things: names, traces, faces, footprints in snow. I had spent my career taking other people's memories and hoarding them like a miser, a hundred lives' worth of first kisses and dead daughters and sunlit afternoons that belonged to strangers, all of it packed into me like cargo. The richest man in the valley's whole gilded childhood. The widow's kiss. Every theft I ever committed to stay invisible, every payment I ever accepted, all of it was fuel, and the fire needed feeding, and there was only one thing in my pockets.

So I paid. One hundred lives' worth, one memory at a time, into the flame in my hands. I felt each one go—not torn, given; there's a difference, and the difference is the whole point—and the Ember brightened as I dimmed, and by the time I crossed the line into the valley's forgiving fog I was carrying nothing at all. No stolen past. No name. Not even my own memories, which turned out to be stored in the same vault as everyone else's, and burned just as warm.

The Ember lived. It lives still. It's small now, small as a candle, and it sits in a lantern by our hearth, and some evenings it flickers in the rhythm of a heartbeat, and I've stopped finding that eerie and started finding it companionable. My soul, or the part of it that was raised on ember-light, kept a little of the warmth. Most of me crossed into the fog hollow and blinking.

And Wren was there on the other side. Of course Wren was there. Wren, who paid a regret at a bridge and never told me which; Wren, who was written as a sacrifice and refused the part; Wren, who watched the person they loved walk out of the mist with empty hands and an empty head and did not flinch, did not grieve usefully and move on, but sat down on the cold ground with me and opened a notebook and said:

"Tell me your favorite color."

"I don't know."

"Then we'll try them all until one fits. Blue?"

"No."

"Green?"

"...Maybe."

"Green," Wren wrote, in their beautiful drafting hand, at the top of a fresh page. "Rafael likes green." And then, after a pause: "That's your name, by the way. Rafael. I'm lending it back to you. Interest-free."

They wrote me back into existence that winter, page by page. Not the old me—I wasn't the old me, the old me was ash on a mountain and fuel in a lantern—but a new one, assembled out of facts and preferences and small daily evidence, the way you map a country no one has surveyed. *Rafael takes tea too strong. Rafael hums while chopping onions, tunelessly, songs they cannot possibly remember. Rafael talks to the cat. Rafael is afraid of the cellar and won't say why and that's allowed.* A self made of words instead of stolen recollections. Secondhand, handwritten, entirely mine.

* * *

Which brings me, circling like the fog outside, to the name in the book.

Because the new prophecy's last lines weren't only about the thief. There was more, a few lines of benediction or warning—I genuinely couldn't tell which, and I've stopped assuming those are different things—and then a signature block, the way contracts have, except this one wasn't signed by the court. It was signed by the freed. A thousand names in a thousand hands, crowded down the margin like guests at a wedding, and among them, unmistakable, in drafting letters so neat they could only belong to one person:

*Rafael — drawn free by the mapmaker, who asks in return only this: choose.*

Choose. Choose what? I sat with the frost melting off the window and the cat auditing my lap and the question sitting across from me like the book had, patient, open-handed. Choose the name? Choose the life? Choose whether the prophecy had been a cage or a choice?

I already knew the answer to the last one. I'd known since the bridge of small regrets, since the throne room, since the moment I fed myself to a flame to keep a promise I hadn't consciously made. The prophecy was a cage built by people who believed no one would rather break it than inherit it. They were wrong about me. They were wrong about Wren. Being wrong about the two of us was, collectively, the best mistake anyone ever made.

But the name. That was harder, and it took me until the kettle whistled to admit why.

There's a version of me that wants to be Rafael forever—wants it fiercely, wants it the way you want to keep a coat that still smells like someone you lost. Rafael was my mother's son's name, or daughter's, whatever I was to her; Rafael was the name I hid under, the name I spent a lifetime keeping small and unremarkable and safe. Keeping it would be loyalty. Keeping it would be carrying her bargain—all of it, the betrayal and the shield both—into every room I entered for the rest of my life.

And there's another version of me that understands something the dying peddler understood when he sent the book back: that some things ripen after the transaction closes. That the person who crossed the fog line isn't the person who went in, and dressing the newcomer in the old clothes is a kindness to a ghost, not to the living.

Wren came downstairs while I was thinking, hair flat on one side, and put their arms around my shoulders from behind and read the open page upside down, which they're very good at, years of reading my scrawls across a shared table.

"The thief who stole themselves free," they murmured. "Show-off."

"It wrote itself. I was asleep."

"Mm. And the signature line?"

"It says choose." I turned my head and looked up at them, ink-stained fingers and all, my cartographer, my co-conspirator, the one who draws the way. "Choose what?"

"You know what." Wren kissed the top of my head, unbothered, and went to feed the cat, because Wren has always understood that enormous moments and breakfast occupy the same morning and refuses to pretend otherwise.

* * *

So here is what I chose, and I chose it with my eyes open, which is the only way I do anything anymore.

I kept a name. Not Rafael—that one I folded up carefully, the way you fold a letter from someone gone, and put away somewhere safe, and I take it out sometimes at night and hold it and put it back. It served. It saved the valley once, through my mother's terrified arithmetic, and it sheltered a thief for thirty-odd years, and it deserves rest more than reuse.

The name I kept is the one Wren wrote at the top of the first page, the one they gave me over that winter of reconstruction, half as a joke that stopped being one: the name on the spine of the book Wren has nearly finished writing, the account of everything that happened, set down so that neither of us ever has to be forgotten again.

Ansel Marchetti.

Marchetti because Wren said, sensibly, that I should keep the surname—it was the one honest thing in my old life, inherited fair, stolen from no one—and Ansel because, Wren claims, it means something like *protected by god* in some language they refuse to specify, and when I pressed them they admitted they might have invented the etymology entirely, and when I pointed out that you can't just invent meanings they said, "Cartographer," with tremendous dignity, "I invent coastlines professionally. One more word is nothing."

Ansel Marchetti. A person with no past, a present measured in strong tea and bad humming and a cat with delusions of grandeur, and a future that nobody wrote down anywhere—not in prophecy, not in contract, not in any ledger kept by any king with too many joints in his fingers. A name chosen at a kitchen table, witnessed by frost, ratified by cat. Mine. Honestly mine. Which makes it, by the standards of my entire former career, the single greatest theft I ever pulled: I stole it fair and square from nobody, out of an unlocked vault, in front of the owner, who was me.

* * *

Tonight the fog is thick against the windows and the lantern on the sill burns small and steady, and Wren is at the table adding mountains to a map that will never be printed, and I have done the thing I do every evening now, the ritual I invented myself, which means it's the only tradition I own outright.

I light the small warm flame in the window.

It's not much to look at. A candle-flame, amber and ordinary, the kind of light you'd pass in any village window without a second glance. But I know what's in it. A hundred lives' worth of borrowed brightness, a soul that learned to burn before it learned to be, and one thief's entire hoard, spent gladly, glowing there against the dark like a promise the dark agreed to keep.

Sometimes, on nights like this, I swear the flame leans toward the glass. Toward the valley. Toward whatever's left under the mountain now that the court is scattered and the throne is cold and the Ashen King's begging voice has faded, at last, from the corridors of my well-kept empty spaces. Sometimes I think something in that fire remembers being royal.

Mostly I think it's just glad to be let out.

Wren looks up from the map. "You're doing the brooding-at-the-window thing."

"I'm admiring my handiwork."

"You're narrating yourself brooding at the window."

"Guilty." I turn from the glass, and the flame holds steady behind me, and the house is warm. "Anything good on the horizon?"

Wren taps the pen twice—the tell I've learned means *yes, and you're going to pretend to be annoyed*—"Peddler came through the fog line this afternoon. Says there are travelers asking, down in the low country, about a flame that walked out of a mountain. Says they're calling it a relic. Says someone's offering money for the mapmaker who draws the impossible ways." A pause, perfectly calibrated. "Also there's a rumor the mountain's regrowing. Roots moving. Doors reopening."

I look at my partner. My partner looks at me. The cat knocks something off a shelf, on principle.

"Well," I say, reaching for my coat, feeling—for the first time in a year, in a lifetime, in any life I can prove I lived—the particular lightness of a thief with nothing left to hide and a whole dark road ahead. "It would be rude to keep them waiting."
