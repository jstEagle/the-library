## 1. The Ledger of Small Thefts

The Vellum Guild kept no clocks. August Galloway had never needed one.

Time announced itself in other ways here — in the way candlelight thickened to amber in the third hour of a shift, in the soft settling of the vaults below like an old man turning over in bed, in the exact shade of blue the tall windows turned before dawn, when the last entries were meant to be finished and the ink meant to be capped. August knew all of it the way a sailor knows weather: not by measurement but by the body's slow accumulation of hours spent in one place, doing one thing, well.

And August did this one thing very well indeed.

The ledger lay open beneath their hands, its pages the color of old teeth, and August's pen moved across them in a hand so precise it looked printed — each entry squared to the margin, each figure balanced, each memory assigned its number, its weight, its classification, its shelf. This was the work. It had been the work for nineteen years, since Master Corvane had set a pen in August's callow fingers and said, *You have an honest face, Galloway. That is worth more than you know. People will tell you true things hoping you'll lie about them later.*

August had not lied about anything since. Not once. Not even to themselves.

*Entry 4,417,* they wrote. *First kiss, stolen from a cooper's daughter in Marrowfield, age sixteen, spring. Sensation profile: rain on a tin roof, yeast-smell from the workshop, the metallic click of a hatpin coming loose. Emotional valence: terror shading into wonder at approximately the four-second mark. Condition upon acquisition: intact, still warm. Shelf: Rose Vault, Tier Nine. Price on resale: negotiable.*

The jar itself sat on the felt pad beside the ledger, small as a fist, its glass fogged from within as though it were breathing. August held it up to the lamplight and watched the memory move inside — a smear of gold and grey, the suggestion of two figures leaning close, the rain rendered as sound rather than water, because that was how first kisses remembered themselves: not as images but as weather. The cooper's daughter had sold it that morning. She was forty-one now, August knew from the intake docket, recently widowed a second time, and she had told the acquisition agent she wanted *the part of me that could still be surprised taken out and put somewhere safe.*

August catalogued the request without comment, as the Guild required, and did not let themselves wonder where on a person the part that could be surprised was located, or what shape the hole left behind might be.

They capped the jar, carried it through the sliding door at the back of the counting room, and set it in its place among nine hundred others in the Rose Vault, Tier Nine — kisses, mostly, though the tier also held three apologies and one declaration of love that had been returned unopened by its intended recipient and resold twice. August knew every jar on the tier by its number. They could walk the aisle blindfolded. Sometimes, on bad nights, they did.

Then back to the desk, where the next item waited under a glass bell like something sick.

* * *

*Entry 4,418.* This one was larger, a jar the size of a churn, and it took both of August's hands and a wheeled trolley to bring it into position. Widow's grief, acquired whole. Sixty years of marriage compressed to a single emotional event — the morning after the funeral, when the widow had opened her husband's coat closet and found his smell waiting for her like an ambush.

Grief was the Guild's most reliable commodity. It never depreciated. Gods wanted it, though August did not let themselves think about that particular line of the business; gods were the province of the acquisition side of the house, and August kept the books, and the books only said what the books said.

They uncorked the sampling valve — a needle-thin aperture Corvane himself had designed, drawn from some pre-war text August was never permitted to see — and let one drop of the memory touch their tongue, because classification required verification. This was procedure, not indulgence. Procedure.

It hit like cold water.

A kitchen. A woman's hands around a mug going cold. A closet door that would not stay closed, swinging open again and again as though the house itself were trying to tell her something. And underneath everything, so loud it was almost a taste: *he hung his coat there every night for sixty years and I never once thought about it until now.*

August wrote the classification with a steady hand. *Emotional valence: grief, matrimonial, acute. Secondary notes: guilt (mild), tenderness (severe), coffee gone bitter. Recommended buyer: none at present. Hold.*

They held things. That was another thing August did well. They held six hundred griefs and eleven hundred loves and a soldier's courage that had come in just before dusk, Entry 4,419, extracted from a deserter who claimed — weeping, the intake note said — that he didn't want to be brave anymore, that courage had marched him toward guns three times and he'd finally worked out that it wasn't his, it had never been his, it belonged to whatever was behind him shouting, and he wanted to sell it before it killed him.

The courage sat in its jar like a struck match, all orange light and pressure. When August verified the sample it tasted of tin, boot leather, and a prayer someone else had written. *Recommended buyer:* August wrote, and then stopped, pen hovering, because the standard notation was *military interests,* and tonight the word *military* looked different on the page than it ever had. Thinner. Like a word someone had used too many times until the meaning wore through it.

They wrote it anyway. They always wrote it anyway. That was the arrangement: August recorded, others decided, and August's hands stayed clean in the only way hands in this building could be — by being merely the instrument of the record rather than the author of the trade.

*I have never stolen for myself,* August thought, capping the ink. Nineteen years. Not one drop siphoned, not one jar misplaced, not one memory pocketed on the stairs. Other clerks had tried it. August had watched two of them leave with their own ledgers read aloud to them in Corvane's mild, terrible voice, watched them discover what the Guild did to people who confused custody with ownership. August had never envied them their little thefts. Pride, maybe, was the honest name for what August felt — the clean, cold pride of a locked door.

It was a good pride. It filled the space the way furniture fills a room: usefully, without warmth.

* * *

By the deep-blue hour, the counting room was silent except for the scratch of the pen and, faintly, from below, the sounds the jars made when the building dreamed.

People who did not know the Guild assumed the memories slept. August knew better. Stored feeling settled and shifted like sediment in wine; a vault full of longing developed currents. On quiet nights the whole building hummed at the frequency of other people's lives, and August had long ago made their peace with falling asleep inside the murmur of strangers' hearts. It was company, of a kind. It asked nothing of August. It did not notice that August had no stories of their own to contribute.

Because that was the truth August kept in no ledger: nineteen years of meticulous custody, and August Galloway owned exactly four personal memories. A red door. Snow on a specific street whose name they'd lost. The sensation of being carried. And a voice — a woman's voice, warm and half-lost — saying words August could no longer hear, only feel, like a footprint in a floorboard worn by someone who had moved away.

Four memories. Everything else had been sold off early, before August came to the Guild, before Corvane, before the pen. August had signed the inventory of their own childhood at fourteen without understanding it, because there had been debts, and because children are not consulted in matters of accounting.

They did not think of this often. Thinking of it was like pressing a bruise to confirm the leg still worked. But tonight — perhaps it was the widow's grief still on their tongue, or the deserter's courage humming on the desk — the silence between entries felt less like professionalism and more like what it was.

August lit a fresh candle. There were eleven items left in the night's intake. Work was the answer to most things. Work had always been the answer.

They drew the next jar from the tray, checked the intake tag against the manifest, and froze.

The tag was wrong.

Not damaged, not mislabeled in any way a supervisor would flag. The accession number was valid. The date of acquisition was twenty years old — older than nearly everything in the deep vaults. The provenance field contained the single word *estate.* All of it correct. All of it ordinary.

Except the contents line. Except the description, written in a clerk's hand two decades dead, which read:

*One childhood, partial. Subject: Galloway, A.*

* * *

The counting room had never felt cold before. August had worked through winter nights when frost ferned the windows and the vaults breathed warm, and never once felt cold. Now the chill came up through the floor as if the building had opened an eye.

They did not pick up the jar. That was the first thing — they noticed their own hands making a careful, deliberate business of not touching it, hovering over the glass the way one hovers over a sleeping animal. Protocol said verify. Classification required verification. Nineteen years of procedure sat on August's shoulders like a yoke, and the yoke said: sample it, weigh it, enter it, shelve it.

But the ledger entry had not yet been made, and already August knew what it would say, because August knew their own handwriting better than they knew their own face, and the manifest did not lie. The manifest said *Galloway, A.,* and the date said twenty years ago, and the debts of a desperate family said the rest.

Somewhere below, among nine hundred breathing jars, something shifted its weight.

"Wrong tray," August said aloud. Their voice sounded thin in the high room. "Clerical error. Items get mis-trayed."

Items got mis-trayed. Of course they did. In nineteen years, precisely never, but the mechanism existed, and error was human, and August was — August was what? August looked at the tag again. *Subject: Galloway, A.* The letters sat on the card with the calm of facts.

They should fetch Corvane. That was protocol too: anomalous provenance escalated upward, always, no exceptions, especially — Corvane had been explicit once, in a voice like a door closing — *especially anything touching the staff.* He had said it nineteen years ago, August realized. He had said it the week August arrived, standing in this same room, holding this very manifest book in his long hands, warning them about the deep vaults the way a man warns a child about deep water.

*You have an honest face, Galloway. Guard it. Some things down there used to belong to faces.*

August picked up the jar.

It was lighter than it should have been. That was the second wrongness — a partial childhood, even a partial one, should have had weight, the way grief had weight, the way courage struck its match against the glass. This jar weighed almost nothing, as if whatever was inside had thinned itself out over twenty years of waiting, stretched itself like breath over a cold window.

Through the fogged curve, August could see color. Green, mostly. Green and a scatter of gold. A garden, some part of them understood without knowing how — not saw, understood, the way you understand a smell is your mother's before you can name the spice. Green and gold and, briefly, the hem of a woman's dress moving between leaves.

The four memories August owned stirred like fish in a shallow pool. The red door. The snow. Being carried. The voice.

The voice.

August's hands began, without instruction, to shake.

They were professionals about it. That was the horror of the next several minutes — how professional they remained. They set the jar on the felt pad. They aligned it square to the desk edge, because alignment was what their hands did when their mind had left the country. They reached for the sampling valve with fingers that trembled only slightly, only at the very tips, and told themselves the tremor was the candle-draft, and knew it was not.

One drop. Verification required one drop. Twenty years was within the preservation window. Procedure would take less than a minute and then the thing would be classified and shelved and August would carry the knowledge of its shelf number the way they carried everything — accurately, at a distance, untouched.

Their fingertip rested on the valve.

From inside the glass, softly, the way a voice carries through a wall — through twenty years, through debt and signature and silence — something spoke.

Not words. Worse than words. It was the cadence of August's own name, *August,* spoken in the warm half-remembered woman's voice, in the tone reserved for calling a child in from the garden before dark. The jar did not open. Nothing escaped but a breath of pressure against the valve, against August's skin, and it smelled — absurdly, impossibly — of green things growing, of turned earth and evening, of a garden August had never seen and recognized completely.

August Galloway, who had catalogued eleven hundred loves and six hundred griefs and never once stolen so much as a sigh for themselves, sat down slowly in their chair and discovered that they were crying.

They let it happen the way one watches weather. It did not feel like grief. It felt like inventory — a stocktaking conducted without consent, an audit of a self that had been sold off in lots before it could object. *Item:* one childhood, partial. *Condition upon acquisition:* intact, still warm. *Price:* whatever the family owed. *Current location:* on my desk, whispering to me in my mother's voice.

When the tears stopped, August did what August had always done. They reached for the ledger. They dipped the pen. And they wrote the entry in a hand so precise it looked printed:

*Entry 4,420. Childhood, partial, subject unknown. Contents: unverifiable. Provenance: irregular. Recommendation:*

The pen hovered. Ink gathered at the nib like a held breath.

Twenty years of procedure said: *escalate.* Say the name. Write *Galloway, A.* and carry the manifest to the master of the Guild and let the machinery do what machinery does, because August kept the books and the books did not decide.

August looked at the jar. Green and gold behind the fog. The hem of a dress between leaves.

They wrote: *Recommend hold.*

Then, in the smallest hand they had ever used, squeezed into the margin where no auditor would look and every auditor had been trained not to:

*Do not report. Do not open. Find out why.*

Below the floor, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the vaults shifted their weight again — and this time, August could have sworn, the murmur of nine hundred sleeping memories changed pitch, the way a crowd changes when someone important enters the room.

The candle burned blue. Somewhere upstairs, a door closed — Corvane's tread, unhurried, crossing the hall toward the counting room at an hour when the master of the Vellum Guild had no honest reason to walk.

August capped the ink, covered the jar with the glass bell, and folded their hands atop the ledger like a lock.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.
