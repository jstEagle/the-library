## 2. A Prayer Answered

The chapel of Saint Verre had been drowning for sixty years, and it drowned beautifully.

Zephyrine came to it the way other women went to graves — out of obligation, out of grief, out of a stubborn refusal to let a thing be finished when her heart insisted otherwise. She descended the moss-slick steps at dusk, when the tide withdrew far enough to expose the nave like the ribcage of some enormous patient beast. Water still stood ankle-deep across the floor, black and mirror-still, holding inverted reflections of the broken clerestory windows. The saint's statue rose from the flood at the far end, waist-deep now, its upraised hands crusted white with salt. Saint Verre, patron of glassmakers and of prayers that pass through what seems solid. The sea had taken everything else and left the hands.

She should not have come. She knew this the way she knew the ache in her knees on cold mornings: completely, uselessly.

* * *

Her order had been disbanded eleven years ago by decree of the Concordat — the great reconciling council that had ended the God Wars with paperwork instead of victory. *The gods are dead,* the decree read. *What remains are echoes, superstitions, and the grief of those who cannot accept silence.* Every temple was secularized. Every reliquary catalogued and sold. Every priestess given a choice: renounce or be renamed, and the renaming was worse than renunciation. They did not call them apostates. They called them *delusional*, gently, in official documents, and the gentleness was the cruelty. You cannot argue your way back into respectability from inside a diagnosis.

Zephyrine had signed. Of course she had signed. She'd had a mother in a hospice bed and no trade but prayer, and prayer paid nothing once prayer was declared impossible. She signed the renunciation with the same pen they used for property transfers, and she kept the vestments anyway, folded in oilcloth beneath the floorboards of her rented room, because there are surrenders the mouth makes that the body refuses to ratify.

So she prayed. Alone. In secret. In ruins. Out of habit, she told herself. Habit was the only word that didn't sting.

Tonight the tide had been generous. She waded down the center aisle, skirts hitched above her calves, and took her place before the statue where the water ran shallowest, over the worn hollow in the flagstone where generations of knees had made a basin for themselves. She knelt in it. The cold climbed through the wool of her trousers and she welcomed it; pain made the prayer feel earned.

"Saint Verre," she began, and her voice came out rusty, a door long unopened. "Glassmaker of heaven. Passer-through of walls."

There was no liturgy anymore. She had burned hers, officially, in the courtyard of the seminary along with two hundred other copies, and so what remained was what she'd had as a child before the teachers got hold of her: plain words, said plainly, to someone she hoped was listening.

"I don't ask for anything. I know better." The water shivered around her knees. A fish — some pale blind thing the floods had brought — brushed past her shin and vanished into the dark under the pews. "I only wanted to say that I remember you. That's all. Nobody else does. Somebody should."

That was the whole prayer, most nights. *I remember you. Somebody should.* Eleven years of it, spoken into drowned stone, and eleven years of the particular silence that follows such words — not an empty silence but a full one, the silence of a held breath.

She bowed her head. She waited the customary span. She began to rise.

And the chapel answered.

* * *

It did not answer with words. Not at first. It answered the way spring answers winter — a pressure change, a shift in the quality of the dark. The standing water across the entire nave trembled at once, every reflection breaking simultaneously, and Zephyrine felt it through the soles of her feet before her ears caught anything: a low vibration, subterranean, patient, like roots splitting stone somewhere far below the foundations, below the harbor mud, below any depth a map would bother to chart.

Her first thought was that the tide had returned early. Her second thought died before it formed, because then came the voice.

It came up through the water, through the stone, through the hollow her knees had worn. It was not loud. It did not need to be. It was vast the way a mountain is vast without raising its voice, and it spoke in the register of geology, of things that measure time in millennia and find a century an eyeblink.

***"Little listener."***

Zephyrine did not move. Later, examining the memory the way she examined everything now — clinically, at arm's length, as evidence belonging to someone else's life — she would marvel that she hadn't screamed. But in the moment there was no room for terror yet. There was only recognition, arriving like sunrise, undeniable and total.

She knew that voice.

Not its words — it had never spoken words to her before. Its *texture*. The deep harmonic underneath it. For nine years of her novitiate she had knelt five mornings a week in the dry chapel of the Order of the Quiet Ear, and she had been taught that what she felt during those hours was merely devotion, merely the mind's own warmth reflected back. But she had always known better, the way you know better about the sun being warm. Something had been present in those chapels. Something had leaned close when she prayed. And it had gone silent the year of the Concordat, and she had grieved it privately the way you grieve a friend everyone insists never existed.

"You're dead," she whispered. The words fell into the water and sank. "You were declared dead. I signed the—"

***"Paper,"*** said the god of Saint Verre, and something that might have been amusement moved through the flooding dark like a slow fish. ***"We were declared dead on paper. How very like you all. As if we lived there."***

"What—" Her voice cracked. She steadied it against the statue's salt-crusted foot, laid her palm flat on the cold plinth. "What are you?"

***"Thirsty,"*** it said.

The word landed strangely. Not metaphorical. She heard the truth of it in the way the vibration thinned at its edges, the way a starving man's voice goes bright and brittle. The god was thirsty. The god had been thirsty for eleven years, drinking nothing, while the country above it poured its longing into new vessels — banks, armies, the Vellum Guild with its bottled years and stolen memories, though Zephyrine knew nothing of the guild yet, nothing except that the world had found substitutes for everything the old faiths used to hold.

"We fed you," she said slowly, remembering her theology now, dredging it up from the silt of the disbanding. "The rites. The offerings. The—"

***"Attention,"*** the god said. ***"You fed us attention. Feeling, given freely and named. That is the whole of the sacrament; the rest was architecture."*** A pause, long enough that the water stilled again, and when it resumed there was something new threaded through the vastness — something she would spend a long time learning to name, and longer wishing she hadn't heard it. Hunger, yes. But underneath the hunger: confusion. A god waking after a century declared dead, finding the world rearranged, finding itself thirsty in a desert of its own funeral. ***"Someone has been feeding us again, little listener. Someone who is not you. And what they feed us — tastes wrong."***

"Who? Who's feeding you?"

The pressure in the water deepened. Somewhere overhead, in the ruined tower, loose stones ground against each other with a sound like teeth.

***"I do not know its face. I know its taste. Longing, but caged. Grief, but bottled. Years of feeling, harvested rather than given —"*** the voice roughened, roots splitting stone, splitting something larger, ***"— stolen. We are being fed stolen things. And stolen things are making us strange."***

Then, before she could speak again, softer — and the softness was the worst part, because a mountain should never sound uncertain:

***"You heard me. No one has heard me. Tell me, little listener — am I the last?"***

And Zephyrine, kneeling in sixty years of floodwater in a church that officially contained nothing but damp and superstition, understood with perfect clarity that she could not answer honestly, because she did not know. Because the Concordat had declared them all dead together, in one document, with one pen stroke, and if one had survived then—

Then the decree was wrong. Then the disbanding was murder-by-paperwork. Then eleven years of her own quiet private prayer had not been delusion but correspondence, unanswered until tonight. Then everything she had surrendered to keep her mother comfortable in her last season had been surrendered to a lie.

She opened her mouth to say *I don't know*, and what came out instead was the oldest reflex of her training, the reflex they had never managed to legislate away:

"I'll find out."

* * *

She fled.

Not immediately — she wanted to say that later, in her own defense, to the empty room where she kept the vestments. She stayed long enough to promise the god she would return, long enough to press her forehead briefly against the statue's flooded knee in a gesture so old her body performed it without consulting her. But when the tide turned and the water began creeping back up the aisle, reclaiming the nave inch by inch, some animal ledger in her finally balanced fear against awe and tipped, and she went.

She climbed the outer stairs two at a time with the sea hissing at her heels, and she did not stop at the clifftop. She walked fast through the salt grass with her heart slamming and her clothes soaked to the thigh, past the leaning fence posts and the shuttered customhouse, all the way to the lamp at the head of the harbor road before her legs would consent to shaking.

The village of Verre-sur-Brume slept around her, forty houses and a fish-smokery, ordinary as bread. Lamplit windows. A dog complaining somewhere. Smoke rising straight from chimneys into windless air. Everything exactly as it had been an hour ago, when she had descended into the flood believing herself alone with her habit and her grief.

Everything exactly as if nothing underneath the world had just cleared its throat.

She stood under the harbor lamp and made herself think it through, because panic was a luxury her training had priced out of reach.

Possibility one: hallucination. Eleven lonely years, the disbanding, the diagnosis waiting politely for her to confirm it. The Concordat's physicians had described her condition in detail — auditory phenomena, stress-induced conviction experiences, the devout mind's talent for hearing its own needs in ambient noise. She recited the description to herself under the lamp like a rosary. It fit. It fit distressingly well.

But the water had moved. All of it, at once, against the tide. And the fish —

No. Stop. Evidence, not argument. She pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw constellations.

Possibility two: it was real. And if it was real, then she alone had heard it, in sixty years of the chapel drowning, among all the villagers who passed the ruin daily, among all the former clergy scattered across three provinces who still — she was certain of this, though none of them admitted it — kept small private observances behind locked doors. Why her? What was a *little listener*? And why, of all nights, tonight?

Unless it wasn't of all nights. Unless the waking was spreading, moving through the buried country like thaw through frozen ground, and Saint Verre was simply the first root to break surface near her. The god had said *we*. Had said someone was feeding *them*. Plural. Many. Fed stolen feeling by an unknown hand until they grew strange.

Strange gods. Hungry gods. Gods taught to want by whatever was bottling grief and selling years — and she had seen, in the market towns, the guild's gray wagons with their sealed jars, and had assumed, as everyone assumed, that the impossible cargo was a rich man's eccentricity.

She looked back down the cliff path. Below, the tide had fully returned; the chapel was underwater now, its roof a dark suggestion beneath the swells, its saint holding up those salt-white hands to a heaven that had spent a century insisting on its emptiness.

*I'll find out,* she had promised. She was already regretting it with her whole body. Already knowing she would keep it.

Because here was the thing the Concordat had never understood, the thing you could only learn kneeling: a prayer doesn't end when the words stop. It ends when it's answered. Hers had been eleven years long, and tonight, at last, in the drowned dark, something ancient had answered — and the answer changed the shape of everything, the way a key changes the shape of a lock.

She turned toward her rented room, toward the floorboard and the oilcloth and the vestments she would take out tonight whether she meant to or not. Behind her the sea kept its counsel. Above her the stars hung indifferent and mapped.

She did not yet know that eastward, beyond hills whose names had been quietly struck from the atlases, a whole erased country lay dreaming under its erasure, full of voices. She did not yet know that within the month she would cross a border that maps refused to draw. She knew only this: she had heard the dead speak, and they were not dead, and they were hungry, and she was the only one listening.

She walked home through the sleeping village, wet to the knee, carrying the beginning of everything.
