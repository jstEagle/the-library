## 3. Inventory of Impossible Things

The summons came at dawn, which was wrong in itself. The Vellum Guild did not conduct its business at dawn; dawn was for the people who still believed their days belonged to them.

August Galloway stood in Master Corvane's study with ink still drying on their cuffs and watched him turn a small brass key over and over between his fingers, the way he did when he was deciding how much truth a thing could bear.

"A full inventory," Corvane said. "Every vault. Every shelf. Every jar counted, weighed, and entered."

"Master, the last full inventory took eleven months—"

"The last full inventory was eleven years ago." He set the key down on the desk between them, deliberately, like a card played face-up. "Things have been moving that I did not authorize. Or things have been sitting where they should not sit. Either way, I want to know what we own. All of it. And I want *you* to do it, Galloway, because you are the only hand in this building I trust to write what is actually there instead of what ought to be."

It was, August knew, both a compliment and a cage. They inclined their head anyway. "When do I begin?"

"You began the moment you said yes." Corvane smiled his thin, courteous smile — the smile of a man who had once been kind and had found kindness too expensive to maintain. "Start with the deep vaults. Work downward. And Galloway—" he paused, and something flickered behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice, "—some of the older holdings are fragile. If you find anything that unsettles you, catalog it. Do not open it. Cataloguing is not the same as knowing."

"No, Master," August said, because it was expected, and because it was true, and because they had built twenty years of professional life on the narrow space between those two words.

* * *

The deep vaults of the Vellum Guild descended through the bones of the city like a throat swallowing slowly. August had walked them a thousand times and knew their geography the way a blind woman knows her own kitchen: by touch, by temperature, by the particular hush each level kept.

The upper vaults held the ordinary miracles. First kisses in stoppered glass, glowing faintly rose-gold, warm to the palm even after decades. Courage, which always smelled faintly of iron and rain. Grief, which was heavier than its volume suggested and had to be shelved on reinforced oak lest the shelves bow. August catalogued these with the ease of long habit: *Item 4,411. One (1) first kiss, harvested from a ferryman's daughter, quality excellent, provenance clean.* Their pen moved. Their heart did not. That was the discipline. That was the pride.

The middle vaults held the stranger stock. Bottled years — whole twelve-months decanted from lives that could no longer afford them. A widow's thirty years of marriage, sold in a single desperate afternoon, the jar so dense with feeling it hummed when August brushed past it. Names, occasionally. Accents. One shelf devoted entirely to the sensation of being forgiven, which was the rarest commodity in the building and the most expensive per ounce.

August worked. The lamp burned. The inventory grew in their neat, bloodless hand.

It was on the fourth day, in the seventh sub-vault, that the work stopped being work.

* * *

Sub-vault seven was the oldest part of the guild, older than the guild itself if the architecture was to be believed — walls of stone that predated the city, carved with symbols no one living could read. The jars here were different. Older glass, older seals, wax stamped with sigils from houses that no longer existed. These were the deep holdings, the legacy acquisitions, the things the guild owned but did not advertise.

August moved down the rows with their ledger open, recording dimensions and seal-marks, and it was almost meditative, almost peaceful, until they reached the third row and saw the label.

The labels in sub-vault seven were written in an old clerk's hand, faded brown ink on yellowed vellum tags. Most read like the others: *harvested*, *quality*, *provenance*. But this one —

This one read:

**GALLOWAY, AUGUST. Age six through nine. Childhood, assorted. Sold by maternal consent. Year of acquisition: [twenty years past].**

August stood very still.

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the stairwell and righted itself. Somewhere far above, the guild went about its morning — clerks arriving, ledgers opening, the great machine of theft grinding on without them. None of it reached this room. In this room there was only the label, and the jar beneath it, and the sound of their own breathing, which had gone strange and shallow.

They read it again. Then again, as though repetition might dissolve it, the way you say a word too many times and watch it come apart into meaningless sounds.

*GALLOWAY, AUGUST.*

Their name. Their own name, in the inventory. Not as keeper of the inventory. As *stock*.

The jar itself was unremarkable — quart-sized, thick glass, sealed with black wax bearing the guild's oldest mark, the moth-and-key. Through the glass August could see nothing. That was the nature of stored memory: it did not glow like a first kiss or darken like grief. It simply waited, opaque as river-stone, holding whatever it held. A childhood. *Assorted.* Six through nine.

Three years. Three years of someone's life, and the someone was them.

August's hand rose toward the jar and stopped, hovering, trembling faintly in the lamplight. Twenty years ago. They tried to reach back for those years and found — they had always found — a smooth blankness, a stretch of fog between the smell of their mother's kitchen (did she have a kitchen? had there been a mother? there must have been, everyone began somewhere) and their first day as a guild apprentice at ten years old, sharp-eyed and hungry and grateful, so grateful, to be given work and purpose and a place where forgetting was a profession rather than a wound.

They had thought the fog was natural. Everyone's early years thinned. Everyone's childhood compressed into a handful of scenes.

But scenes. They had *scenes*. A garden — sunlight through leaves, the green smell of it. A voice reading aloud. A door painted blue. Fragments, worn smooth as sea glass from handling. And now here sat the rest of it, three whole years of it, corked and labeled and filed under *assets*, and August understood with a vertigo so complete it was nearly physical that the fragments were all that remained *outside* the glass. Everything else had been poured out of them and sold.

Sold by maternal consent.

They thought of their mother — the shape of her, the idea of her, a woman seen mostly in retrospect and mostly from behind. What had the guild paid her? Enough to eat? Enough to leave? Had she wept while she signed, or had she already stopped?

The ledger-keeper in August — the cold, meticulous, proud professional who had never once stolen for themselves — looked down at the entry forming in their own hand and saw it clearly for the first time:

*Item 9,207. One (1) childhood, assorted, ages six through nine. Provenance: the subject. Quality: unknown. Status: held in trust against future use.*

They were also an entry. They had always been an entry. Every careful line they had ever written in these vaults had been written by a person who was herself a line item, filed and shelved and waiting to be spent.

The lamp hissed. August became aware that they had been standing there for a long time — an hour, perhaps more — and that their face was wet, which was absurd, because they could not remember the last time they had cried and could not, just now, have said precisely what they were crying about. That was the cruelest arithmetic of it: they were grieving a loss they could not feel, mourning memories they could not access, weeping over a jar the way you might weep over an urn of ashes belonging to someone you'd never met.

Someone they had never met. Someone named August Galloway, aged six through nine.

They wrote the entry. Their hand shook only a little. *Catalogued,* they noted. *Not opened.* And beneath it, in a smaller script they would later pretend they hadn't written: *I am sorry. I don't know yet what for.*

* * *

Corvane was waiting for them at the top of the stairs.

August should have expected it. Of course he knew what was in sub-vault seven; he knew everything the guild held, the way a spider knows every thread of its web. But seeing him there, framed in the doorway with the grey morning light behind him, his hands folded with terrible gentleness — seeing him *waiting* — told August that this moment had been arranged. Perhaps the whole inventory had been arranged. Perhaps the point had never been the counting.

"You found it," Corvane said. Not a question.

August said nothing. There was no answer that wasn't a confession.

"I wondered whether you would," he went on, walking with them — steering them, really — away from the stairwell, down the corridor toward his study, his voice low and reasonable and horribly kind. "Most keepers find their own jars eventually. It's almost a tradition. The guild takes from everyone, Galloway; even its own. Especially its own. We deal in what people can spare, and children spare more than they'll ever know."

"My mother sold me," August said. The words came out flat, factual, ledger-language. It was the only language they had.

"Your mother sold *a portion* of you. Let us be precise; precision is your gift." He opened the study door and gestured them in, poured two glasses of the good plum brandy though August had not asked, and pressed one into their hand. "Ages six through nine. A difficult span, often — the years when the world stops being magic and starts being rules. Some parents prefer their children skip it entirely. She was offered a fair price and a fresh start in a country with kinder winters. She took it. I have never judged her for it. I have never judged *you* for it either, which I hope you understand."

"Do I get it back?" August asked. "Ever?"

Corvane smiled, and for one unguarded instant the smile was sad, and that was somehow worse than everything else.

"That depends," he said, "entirely on you."

He drank. August did not. Outside the window, the city went about its unmapped morning, and somewhere beneath it — though August did not know this yet, would not know it for another week — something old turned over in its sleep and dreamed of being fed.

"There are things stirring in this world, Galloway," Corvane said quietly. "Old things. Things our order declared dead a century ago because dead things are safe things. They are waking, and when they finish waking, every soul in this nation will need the guild — will need *order*, will need *record* — more than it ever has. I am preparing for that. The inventory is preparation. Your jar is preparation." He set down his glass. "So hear me, and hear me gently, because I am saying this as nearly a friend: do not open it."

"Why not?" August's voice cracked on the second word. "It's mine. You said it yourself — provenance, the subject. It came *out of me*."

"It did. And it has been aging in the dark for twenty years, and what comes out of a jar after twenty years is not always what went in." Corvane crossed to the window, silhouetted, his shadow long across the floorboards. "Memory is a living thing, Galloway. It ferments. A childhood left uncorked that long, in a vault that deep, among holdings we do not speak of — opening it might give you back three years. Or it might give you back three years *and everything that has been dreaming around them.*"

"Dreaming."

"The vaults are old. Older than us. Things settle into stored feeling the way flies settle into honey." He turned, and his eyes in the grey light were patient and bottomless. "One day, when the time is right — when the work is finished, when the guild stands secure — I will open that jar for you myself, with proper safeguards, and you will have your childhood back entire. That is my promise. Until then: catalogue. Count. Write what is there. And let sleeping years lie."

It was a good speech. It was, August would think later, a masterfully constructed speech, every clause load-bearing, every reassurance placed exactly where a frightened person needed it. And it worked, mostly. August finished the brandy because refusing seemed dangerous, thanked him because silence seemed more dangerous, and walked back down through the vaults to resume the counting.

But one splinter stayed lodged, working deeper with every hour: *when the work is finished.* When the work was finished. Corvane had spoken of the waking gods the way other men spoke of weather — as a fact to be endured — but he had spoken of *the work* the way men spoke of a harvest they intended to reap.

And August, descending into the dark with their lamp and their ledger, realized they had just been handed the only clue they would ever be given freely: somewhere in these vaults, in some entry they had not yet reached, was the record of what the work actually was.

They turned to a fresh page and wrote, in their small precise hand, the only honest sentence of the day:

*Begin inventory at sub-vault one. Read everything.*

Below the deep vaults, August knew, there were older ones still. Vaults even Corvane's clerks did not enter. And ledgers — August was certain of it now, certain the way one is certain of a heartbeat — ledgers written in a hand very much like their own.
