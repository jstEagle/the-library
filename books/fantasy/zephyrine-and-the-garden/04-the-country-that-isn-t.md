## 4. The Country That Isn't

Zephyrine had been walking for three days when the road began to apologize.

She noticed it first as a softness underfoot — the packed clay giving way to something spongy, mossed, half-hearted — and then in the way the milestones thinned out and finally stopped altogether, as if the road itself had grown ashamed of going where it was going. By the fourth morning there were no milestones at all, no boundary stones, no faded paint of any surveyor's hand. Just the road, narrowing, and the voice beneath it.

*Down here,* it said, when she pressed her ear to the ground at dusk like a madwoman. *Down. Down.*

"You are not real," she told it, for the hundredth time. Her voice came out flat and cracked from disuse. "You died. All of you died. The Synod said so. There was a document."

The voice did not argue. It never argued. It only continued, patient as water working at stone, and Zephyrine followed because the alternative was to stop, and stopping meant admitting that three days of her life had been spent chasing a sound.

The drowned chapel of Saint Verre was behind her now, a day's walk back and sinking further into memory with every step. She tried to hold on to her terror of that night — terror was sane, terror was the correct response, terror was what kept a person from becoming the kind of woman who heard voices in old stone and believed them. But terror, she was discovering, does not keep. It fades like ink left in sun, and what remains underneath is only the thing itself: the fact of having knelt in the flooded nave with the water around her knees, and having spoken the dawn office out of habit, and having been answered.

Not echoed. Answered. She knew the difference the way a widow knows the difference between a knock and wind against the door.

She crested a low hill at midday and saw, below her, a town that should not exist.

It sat in a river valley the color of tarnished silver, perhaps two hundred roofs, a market square, a bell tower with no bell. Smoke rose from chimneys in ordinary gray threads. Laundry hung between windows. And Zephyrine, who had spent eleven years as a cartographer's apprentice before the cloth took her, who could read a map the way other people read scripture, stood on the hilltop and understood with a cold sliding feeling in her stomach that this town was not on any map she had ever seen. Not because it was new. The architecture said otherwise — the leaning gables, the church buttressed in a style two centuries dead. It was old, and inhabited, and absent.

"Saint Verre," she whispered, testing herself. "Corvane's Crossing. Aldermoor. Thistledown." She named the towns of the region aloud, one by one, all the towns that appeared on the survey maps in the seminary library, and none of them was this one. She made up names for the gaps and none of the names fit. The valley below held its silence like an accused prisoner.

She went down into it anyway. Of course she went down into it. The voice had grown warmer here, or she had stopped being able to tell warmth from want.

* * *

The town was called nothing, or its name was a thing people's mouths refused to hold.

"The name," the innkeeper said, polishing a cup that was already clean. He was a broad man with a burn scar down one cheek and the particular stillness of someone listening to a question he has heard many times. "We had one."

"And?"

"And we had one," he repeated, gently, the way one corrects a child. "You'll want the room at the back. It doesn't face the square."

She took the room. She ate bread and a stew of some root vegetable she couldn't identify in the common room, and she watched the townsfolk, and they watched her watching them, without hostility but with a weary patience, the look of people accustomed to strangers arriving and then not quite arriving.

Because that was the wrongness at the center of everything, she realized as the evening wore on. Strangers arrived here and then did not arrive. A peddler by the fire kept telling a story about a wedding he had attended in a village whose name slipped him each time he reached it; his listeners nodded along, helping him past the gap every time, the way you steady a drunk man past a hole in the road. An old woman knitted and unpicked the same row of blue. Two children played a game with rules that changed whenever one of them was losing, and neither child minded, and Zephyrine understood after a while that this was not because they were easygoing but because winning required remembering what the point had been.

Nobody here could hold a grudge, she thought. Nobody here could hold much of anything.

"I'm looking for the source of a voice," she told the innkeeper when the room had emptied. She didn't bother disguising it. If they thought her mad, well, madness was nearly the truth.

He set down the cup. "Beneath things," he said. Not a question.

"You've heard it."

"We all hear it." He said it the way another man would say *we all have bad backs.* "Louder lately. My grandfather said his grandmother remembered when it went quiet — after the war." He pronounced the word carefully, a coin handled with tongs. "Nobody remembers which war. That's rather the point of us."

"Of you?"

"Of here." He gestured vaguely, taking in the inn, the square, the dark window, and somehow all of it — the valley, the hills, the road that had apologized its way into nothing. "There was a country. Now there isn't. We're what's left when a country is unwritten carefully enough." He smiled, and the smile had decades of practice behind it. "You crossed the border today, priestess. I saw it in how you walked. Everyone walks differently for the first week."

"How did you know I'm a priestess?" She wasn't, anymore. The order was disbanded, the vestments burned, the Synod's decree nailed to every church door in the province: *the gods are dead, and those who claim otherwise are dead to the congregation.*

"You have prayer in your shoulders," he said. "And you flinched when I said the war." He gathered the cups. "Sleep. The voice is loudest before dawn, if you're bound for wherever it's pulling you. Most who follow it don't come back, but most who don't follow it don't either, so." He shrugged. "The arithmetic's not encouraging either way."

* * *

She slept badly, in the manner of someone sleeping inside a struck bell.

Before dawn she gave up, dressed, and went out into the square. The town at night was worse than the town by day — houses stood at angles their foundations didn't justify, and twice she saw a street that she had certainly walked that afternoon and that now led somewhere else entirely, a dead end where a bakery had been, a bakery where the well had stood. The town rearranged itself not dramatically but drowsily, like a sleeper shifting position, and she understood that this was what happened to places that were forgotten: they lost their grip on their own arrangement. Memory was mortar. Without it, everything drifted.

At the center of the square stood the bell tower, and beneath the empty belfry, the ground spoke.

She knelt. She hadn't decided to; her knees simply went, the way they go at funerals. The cobbles were cold through her skirt and the frost of early morning silvered them, and she put both palms flat against the stone because that was what you did when you prayed to something underground, something buried, something that had been pushed down and covered over, and she hated herself for knowing the gesture, hated the way her body remembered offices her mind had renounced.

"I'm listening," she said. "Against all wisdom, I'm listening. What do you want?"

The pause that followed was so long she thought the voice had gone, and then it came, and up close — kneeling above it, skin to stone — she could feel it in her teeth and sternum, a vibration like roots splitting rock, slow and immense and unbearably patient.

*You hear us,* it said. Wonderingly. As if the miracle ran the other direction.

"There's more than one of you." Her breath fogged the air. "I felt that. More than one voice."

*A choir needs a throat,* the voice said, or one of the voices said, and underneath it others moved like fish under ice. *We have been under so long, little listener. Under and under. They wrote us out of the sky and then out of the earth and then out of the words, and we sank, and sank. But a written-out thing is not an ended thing. We are here. We are waking. And we are so hungry.*

"Hungry for what?"

Silence again — but this time she recognized it as effort, as a vast attention gathering itself around her small kneeling shape, examining her the way a giant might examine a moth that has landed on its sleeping hand.

*You are one of ours,* it said at last. *Verre's girl. We remember your vows even if your Synod burned them. You prayed to us for eleven years and we could not answer, and you kept praying anyway. Do you know how rare that is? To be fed without knowing you feed anything?*

Her eyes stung. "That wasn't faith, then. That was just — throwing prayers down a dry well."

*No well is dry,* said the voice. *Some are only deep. Verre sleeps deepest of all of us, and yet your prayers reached her, year upon year, thin as thread and strong as cable. She knows your name, Zephyrine. She kept it safe when the maps let go of everything else.*

Hearing her own name in that subterranean thunder broke something loose in her chest. Eleven years since the disbanding — eleven years of being a woman who had wasted her life on a superstition officially retracted, of nodding politely when sensible people explained about the gods the way one explains weather to a child — and now, kneeling in a forgotten square in a country that did not exist, the whole ledger of her devotion was read back to her by the debtor himself. Not wasted. Received. Filed. *Kept.*

And that was the moment belonging arrived — unearned, unasked-for, terrifying — and Zephyrine understood why the innkeeper's people stayed. Why anyone stayed. It was the same gravity that had pulled her across the border: the terrible sweetness of being known by something larger than yourself, in a world where being known was the rarest currency there was.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice shook. "No, I know what this is. I know how this feels from the inside. This is exactly how it starts."

*How what starts?*

"Faith." She stood, wiping her palms on her skirt, putting distance between herself and the stone. "And I've seen where faith goes when the thing it trusts turns hungry. I served a god, whatever your Synod says. I watched my order hand itself over piece by piece — first we asked permission to doubt, then we forgot doubt was ever allowed, and then the Synod burned the vestments and called it reform. Hunger dressed as holiness is still hunger." She was arguing with the ground, in a frozen square, before dawn. She did it anyway. "What do you eat? Tell me that first. Before I take one more step after you. What do you eat?"

The choir beneath her shifted, and for a heartbeat she heard all of them at once — not one voice but a discordant multitude, some murmuring thin and reedy as starved things, others rolling fat and satisfied — and the fat ones frightened her far more than the thin.

*Everything you are,* said the deep voice, gently. *Which is why you should run, if you're wise. And why we hope you won't.*

Then it withdrew — not gone, she could feel it, folded down into the dark like a giant settling back into sleep — and the square was just a square, and the frost was just frost, and the sun was coming up over hills whose names had been unwritten.

Zephyrine stood there a long time, shaking.

* * *

She should have run. She inventoried her reasons for not running over breakfast, and found them all suspect.

Because the land itself was testimony, that was one reason, and she was enough of a scholar to be seduced by evidence. Over the following days, moving from town to town through the unmapped interior, she pieced together what the official histories had amputated. There had been a war — everyone agreed on that much, and on nothing else. Ask ten people and you'd get ten shrugs wearing different faces. The war had ended not in treaty but in erasure: the victors had not annexed this country, they had *deleted* it, striking it from surveys and registers and parish rolls, redrawing trade roads around it as if the land were a stain. The borders weren't guarded because officially there was nothing to guard. You got in by not noticing where you were going, and you got out — she asked — by rarely getting out.

"They say the forgetting works inward too," the peddler told her, in a second nameless town. He was the same peddler, or a peddler very like him; she suspected all these towns shared a few souls among them, passed around like a communal coat. "First the maps forget you. Then the neighbors forget you. Then your cousins across the border stop writing, not from cruelty — the letters just go astray, always astray. Then you start losing yourself. Small things first. Your mother's face. The taste of the food you grew up on. Why you chose your wife." He smiled his practiced smile. "We write things down here. Everything. It's our great industry — not trade, not craft. Remembering. Every house keeps its own book."

"Does it work?"

"Ask me what my daughter's name was." His smile didn't waver. "Don't ask me that, actually."

She stayed, in the end, for none of these reasons. She stayed because on her sixth morning in Vhalen — and yes, the name surfaced eventually, from an old woman's half-eroded memory, surfacing like a body, *Vhalen*, and hearing it made several people in the room weep quietly without knowing why — she woke to find a sprig of rowan laid across her threshold, tied with white thread in the knotting style of her old order.

Someone here knew the rites. Someone here still tended the old devotions, secretly, in defiance of a decree issued by a Synod in a country that had never heard of this one. She carried the rowan sprig for the rest of the day like a coal she was afraid to drop, and that night she dreamed of the drowned chapel, and in the dream Saint Verre's glass eye opened underwater, and looked at her, and was glad.

On the seventh day, on a ridge road between two villages whose names were written only in house-books, she smelled woodsmoke where no chimney stood, and crested the rise, and saw the campfire below — and beside it, hunched over a leather case with the careful greed of a professional, a stranger in travel-stained clerk's clothes, counting jars.

Jars. Dozens of them, packed in straw. Each one glowed faintly from within, the way a lamp glows through closed shutters, and each bore a paper label in a precise, cramped hand.

The stranger looked up. Their eyes met.

Zephyrine had spent eleven years reading faces in confessionals, and what she read in the half-second before the stranger's expression shuttered was not the guilt of a thief caught stealing. It was the guilt of someone caught carrying something they could not put down.

"Don't," the stranger said, rising slowly, one hand on the case. "Whatever you're thinking — don't."

Behind Zephyrine, deep under the ridge, something vast turned over in its sleep, and listened.
