## 5. Thief and Priestess

Zephyrine had learned the roads of Vhalen the way other people learned grief: slowly, unwillingly, and with no hope of ever being finished.

The old roads did not forgive. They tolerated. They wound through fields of grey wheat that whispered in a language older than wheat, past wayshrines whose carved faces had been sanded smooth by a century of official denial, and they shifted — subtly, constantly — for anyone the country did not recognize. Zephyrine had crossed the border three nights ago and still felt the land testing her at every mile, like a dog circling a stranger, sniffing for a lie.

She recognized herself to it each morning. *I am Zephyrine of the Order of Saint Verre. I served the god Verre before the disbanding. My mother baked bread on a street called Ashlane in the city of Vell.* The land would pause, consider, and let her pass — but only just. Only because she was half-forgotten too, and forgotten things recognized their own.

It was on the fourth morning, in the hollow below the ruined toll-arch at Kesswick Cross, that she saw the smuggler.

They came down the old road at first light, when Vhalen's fog lay thickest over the ditches, and they moved wrong for these parts. Too straight. Too certain of the ground beneath them, as if roads were things that stayed where you left them. A tall figure in a grey travelling coat, leading a mule whose panniers clinked with a sound Zephyrine knew from her nightmares: glass against wool. Bottles. Jars.

Her hand went to the knife at her hip before her mind had finished deciding anything. Guild goods. Guild glass. In Vhalen, where the guild had no right to walk, no right even to exist on any map that mattered.

She let them pass her hiding place, counted their steps — eleven, twelve, thirteen, the mule's hooves squelching in the wet clay — and stepped out onto the road behind them.

"Turn around," she said, "slowly, or I'll open your throat and read your ledgers while you bleed."

The figure turned slowly. Not from fear, Zephyrine thought. From courtesy. Under the coat's hood was a face that was neither young nor old, sharp-boned and pale as vellum itself, with eyes the colour of ink left overnight — dark, settled, faintly glittering at the bottom.

"Well," the smuggler said. Their voice was dry and precise, an archivist's voice. "You're not a hunter."

"No."

"Hunters announce themselves. It's policy." The hooded head tilted. "A priestess. Of the old order, by the cut of that collar. Saint Verre?" A flicker of something — not mockery, exactly. Recognition. "You shouldn't be here. Your whole church was unmade in the disbanding."

"And your whole guild was never made at all," Zephyrine said, "so we're evenly matched in impossibility. Open the panniers."

The smuggler's ink-dark eyes considered her. Then, with the same unhurried courtesy, they unbuckled the strap and folded back the wool.

Jars. Dozens of them, packed in rows, each one glowing faintly with its own interior weather — a gold haze here, a bruised violet there, one jar near the top pulsing a soft arterial red that made Zephyrine's stomach turn. Stolen memories. Stolen years. Enough human living to fill a cathedral, bottled by the Vellum Guild and carried through a country that didn't officially exist.

"You're smuggling this out," she said. Not a question.

"I'm transporting it." The smuggler's voice was very even. "There's a difference."

"Where?"

The pause was small, but Zephyrine had spent nine years listening for the silence under words, and she heard it. "South."

"South is the sea."

"South is the sea," the smuggler agreed pleasantly, "and you have a knife at my throat, so let's not pretend either of us owes the other the truth yet."

* * *

August had faced down customs men, guild hunters, a customs officer who was secretly a guild hunter, and once, memorably, Master Corvane himself in one of his quiet rages that made the candles gutter. August had never lost composure. Composure was the ledger-keeper's armour; it was written into the first page of the first book August had ever kept, in a hand younger and rounder than the one they used now: *Never show them what the numbers mean to you.*

But this priestess was looking at the jars the way August had never dared to look at them — not as inventory, but as wounds — and it was making the armour itch.

"Who are you?" she asked. Her accent was Vhaleni, old-Vhaleni, the vowels rounded the way they were before the erasure flattened them out of every schoolbook. She hadn't sheathed her knife. It wasn't pointed anywhere in particular; it simply existed between them, patient as weather.

"August Galloway. Ledger-keeper, third rank, Vellum Guild." August gave the title flatly, the way one gives a name to a border guard. "And you're a long way from your drowned chapel, priestess. Word travels, even through countries that don't exist. There's a woman who prays to dead gods and claims they answer."

Something crossed her face — quick, raw, gone. "Claims," she repeated.

"That's what the word would be in my ledgers." August gestured at the jars without touching them. "These need to reach the vaults at Corvane's southern annex. If you let me pass, there's money in it. Guild scrip converts anywhere."

"Money." She said the word like she was tasting spoiled milk. "You carry stolen lives in glass, and your offer is money."

"Stolen is a strong word—"

"They're *glowing*, Galloway." And there it was — the crack in her voice, the fury underneath, aimed not at August but at the jars themselves, at the red one especially, which pulsed faintly like something dreaming of battle. "That one's a soldier's courage. I'd stake my life on it. You can see the shape of the man in the light if you look properly. Who was he? Does your ledger say? Does it say whether he sold it, or whether someone took it while he slept?"

August looked at the jar properly. It had been in the inventory for two years, catalogued under number 4471, provenance uncertain, and August had never once looked properly.

"The guild doesn't steal from sleepers," August said, and heard how thin it sounded even as it left their mouth.

"No," the priestess said quietly. "You just buy from desperate people and call it trade, and when desperation runs short, I imagine the definition of *willing* gets... flexible." She lowered the knife — not away, but down, resting, like a sentence suspended. "Here is what happens now. You're going to tell me why a third-rank ledger-keeper is running guild goods south through a country erased from every map, alone, without hunters, without escort. Because that's not transport, Galloway. That's flight."

The fog moved between them. Somewhere in the wheat, the whispering rose and fell.

And August, who had never in nineteen years of service shown anyone what the numbers meant, heard themselves say: "If I tell you, you'll know things it isn't safe to know."

"I prayed to a god last week and it answered," she said. "I'm already unsafe. Try me."

* * *

They walked south together because Zephyrine refused to let the jars out of her sight and August could not stop her — the old roads bent toward her when she walked, August noticed, straightened themselves under her feet like servants hearing footsteps. For August the road wandered, doubled back, delivered them twice to the same leaning fence-post. For her it went where she told it.

That, more than the knife, was why August bargained instead of bolting.

"I know where the guild keeps everything," August said, on the second evening, as they made camp in the shell of a toll-house. "Every vault. Every annex. Every memory it has bought or taken in ninety years, including the ones nobody knows it has. You want to hurt the guild? I'm the map."

"And what do you want, ledger-keeper?"

August fed a splintered pew-back — some chapel had died here once — into the small fire. "Out," they said. "There's a door. There are stories about a door."

Zephyrine stared at them across the flames. "The Door of Unmaking," she said slowly, and watched August go carefully, deliberately blank. "Yes. I've heard it too. In hymns they burned after the disbanding. You'd need—" She stopped herself.

"I'd need what?"

"Nothing. Old songs." But she looked at August then with an expression August could not catalogue, could not enter under any heading, and it was worse than the knife.

They travelled four days like that. Suspicion had a rhythm to it, August learned: her watching August's hands at every rest stop, August watching her lips move in prayers to gods that were supposedly dead. At night they slept on opposite sides of the fire, the mule between them like a neutral nation. Zephyrine touched none of the jars except once — the red one — laying two fingers against the glass and closing her eyes, and whatever she found in there made her silent until noon.

On the fifth day, hunters crossed their trail.

* * *

Zephyrine smelled them before she saw them: tallow and clean paper, the guild's signature scent, the smell of things filed rather than lived. Three riders on the ridge behind, moving in the guild's formation — point, flank, anchor — and moving *correctly*, which on Vhalen's shifting roads meant only one thing.

"They have a piece of the country," she said. "A token. Something that remembers them."

August's face did the thing it did — closed like a book. "Then we split. You go east, I go south, they follow the goods."

"The goods are the reason they're following you at all, you idiot."

"They'd follow me regardless," August said, and there was something under the words, some cold gravity, and Zephyrine, who had spent nine years listening beneath words, heard it and filed it away. "Splitting is the smart play."

"Smart," Zephyrine said, "is what got you carrying stolen soldiers' courage through a haunted country alone. We don't split. We burn."

August actually laughed — one syllable, startled out of them. "Burn what?"

"The ledgers you're carrying in your coat. Don't look shocked, Galloway, I've watched you touch that breast pocket eleven times a day like a man checking a wound. Paper. Ledger-paper, guild-stock, and I'd wager my last vow it's written in more than ink." She held out her hand. "Hunters track ledgers the way sharks track blood. Burn them, and the trail goes cold."

For a long moment August did not move. The fire cracked. Out in the dark, the wheat whispered, and far to the north Zephyrine felt — faintly, horribly — something vast turning over in its sleep, attentive, listening the way the whole country had begun to listen since the voice in her chapel.

"These aren't inventory records," August said finally. Quietly. The archivist's voice stripped down to joists. "These are the true ledgers. Blood-written. Everything the guild is, everything it has done — names, prices, provenances, the *people*. If I burn them—"

"What happens to the memories in the jars?"

August blinked. That, clearly, had not been the objection expected.

"No," Zephyrine pressed, stepping closer, into the firelight. "Think it through with me. What happens to them? Are the jars bound to the books? If the books burn, does the glass go dark?"

"...No." August's hand had gone to the breast pocket again, the twelfth time. "No, the jars stand alone. The ledgers record. They don't hold."

"Then they're only paper."

"They're *proof*." And there it was — the crack in the armour, hairline, blazing. "Ninety years of theft, itemized. Every name. If those books survive, someday, someone could be *reunited*. Could be given back what was taken. If they burn, it's all just — glow in glass, forever, orphaned. No one will ever know whose is whose."

The wind came up out of the wheat, smelling of rain and old iron. Behind them, miles back, hoofbeats — or perhaps only the country shifting its weight.

"Yes," Zephyrine said softly. "Someone could be reunited. If the books survive. Now tell me, Galloway — in nineteen years of perfect bookkeeping, how many reunions has the guild arranged?"

August opened their mouth. Closed it. The firelight moved across a face suddenly stripped of all its careful architecture, and underneath, Zephyrine saw, was not coldness at all — only a person who had spent a lifetime believing that precision was the same as purpose.

"None," August said. "None. Ever."

"So burn them."

"It's not that simple—"

"It never is. That's what makes it a choice."

* * *

Later — much later, when the hunters' trail had gone cold behind a wall of burning paper and the country's suspicion had softened into something almost like welcome — August would try to reconstruct the moment and fail.

What they remembered was this: kneeling at the toll-house hearth with the true ledgers stacked before them, blood-scripted pages that hummed faintly against their fingertips like sleeping wasps. Zephyrine standing watch at the door, knife drawn, facing north. The smell of tallow and paper growing closer, closer. No time. No time at all.

And the strange, vertiginous relief of striking the flint.

Page one caught reluctantly — blood resists flame, August had not known that — and then all at once, eagerly, as if it had been waiting nineteen years for permission. The fire ran down the stack like something starved finding a table laid. Names went up. Prices went up. Number 4471, soldier's courage, provenance uncertain, went up, and August watched the entry that should have shamed them dissolve into honest heat, and felt — there was no ledger-category for it — *lighter.*

The smoke rose through the broken roof and the country breathed it in. Zephyrine swore softly at the door. "The road just changed. Ahead of us. It's — Galloway, the road is *opening*."

August stood, ash on their hands, ash on their cuffs, nineteen years of meticulous complicity curling grey toward the stars. The hunters crested the ridge to find only a cold hearth and a mule-track vanishing south, and by then the road had already forgotten where it had been.

They walked until dawn. When they finally stopped, in the lee of a wayshine whose smooth-worn face might once have been Verre's, Zephyrine built a new fire and, wordlessly, handed August the first share of the bread.

Respect, August thought, tasting char and salt. So that's what it feels like from the inside.

"There's something you should know," Zephyrine said, after the silence had gone on long enough to become a different kind of speech. "About why the road opens for us now. Why the country is letting us pass." She turned her hands to the flame, and August saw they were trembling — not with cold. "When I was in the chapel. When the god answered me. It didn't say what you'd expect. It didn't say *worship* or *obey* or any of the things gods say in scripture."

"What did it say?"

Her eyes came up, green-grey and terrified and certain.

"It said, '*Bring me the keeper of the blood-ledgers. I remember their name.*'"
