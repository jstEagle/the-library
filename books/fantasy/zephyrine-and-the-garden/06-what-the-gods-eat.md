## 6. What the Gods Eat

The cipher took me four days to crack.

Not because it was difficult — I have broken harder codes before breakfast — but because every hour I worked at it, I hoped I was wrong. There is a particular kind of dread that comes from being good at your job. The better you are, the faster you arrive at the thing you were afraid of.

It began, as most of Corvane's sins do, with a discrepancy.

I keep the ledgers the way a surgeon keeps a body: everything accounted for, nothing wasted, no part unexamined. Every memory the Vellum Guild buys or steals passes through my hands in ink. A first kiss from a ferryman's daughter in Lowmarsh, sold for eleven crowns and a pardon. The last words of a dying midwife, bottled in blue glass because grief keeps better cold. Forty years of a fisherman's mornings, decanted into a jar the size of a fist, leaving him a man who wakes each day uncertain whether he has ever loved anything.

I record them all. Purchase price, provenance, vessel, shelf, buyer. That is the whole of my trade: I am the man who writes down what was taken from you, so that when you come asking — if you ever come asking — we can quote you a fair price to give it back. Most never come. Most don't remember what they've lost well enough to miss it.

Four days ago, I found an entry that did not balance.

It was a small thing, the kind of error that would escape anyone but me. A withdrawal recorded without a corresponding deposit. Six jars removed from Vault Nine — the deep vault, the one behind the door that requires two keys and a word spoken backward — logged as *transferred*, with no recipient named. In twenty-two years of keeping these books, I had never once seen *transferred*. Everything is bought or sold or destroyed. Nothing is given away. The Guild does not give.

I pulled the thread. It is what I do. And like all threads in this building, it led down.

* * *

Vault Nine opens only on Corvane's authority, but the ledger room opens to me, and the ledger room remembers everything the vault would rather forget. There is a second set of records — not mine, older than my employment, written in a hand that slants hard to the left as though fleeing something — kept in a locked cabinet beneath the floorboards under my own desk. I found it years ago and said nothing, because saying nothing is the first skill they teach you here, and I learned it so well they gave me keys.

The cabinet held forty years of Corvane's private accounting. And it was not written in ink.

Blood-ledgers. That is what the old clerks called them, back when there were old clerks, back before they stopped being old or clerks or anything much at all. Pages of vellum cured in something thicker than tallow, inscribed in a brown-red script that seems to move slightly when you look away and return. My blood went cold in a way that felt less like fear and more like recognition, though I could not have told you why. Not then.

The entries were coded — a substitution cipher keyed to psalms, which should have been my first warning, because the Guild does not pray. But I sat down with the Song of Ascents and a pot of cold coffee, and by the third night the code came apart like wet paper, and I began to read what Master Corvane has actually been doing with the things we steal.

We are not merely selling memories, it turns out.

We are feeding them to gods.

* * *

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only prayer I know.

The entries begin fourteen years ago. Jar after jar, year after year, catalogued in Corvane's slanting hand with a care that made my stomach turn, because it was *my* kind of care — meticulous, exhaustive, proud. He recorded each delivery the way I record each sale. Vessel type. Contents. Condition of the feeling: fresh, aged, crystallized. And then, in place of a buyer's name, a symbol I initially took for a merchant's mark.

It is not a merchant's mark. I spent a day and a night convincing myself it was, comparing it against guild seals in the historical registers, finding near-matches, telling myself a story about some defunct trading house whose sigil it resembled. Near-match. Not match. Because it is not a sigil. It is a *name* — a name in the old Vhaleni script, the script of the country that isn't, the script that was officially abolished along with everything else official about Vhalen a century before either of us was born.

A name that means, as best I can render it, *the one who waits below the orchards.*

A god's name.

They told us the gods died. All of us — every schoolchild in every province that used to touch Vhalen's erased borders — they taught it like arithmetic: the war came, the gods died, the country was struck from the maps, and good riddance to all three. The Church of the Quiet Hours built its whole doctrine on the silence. Zephyrine's order — whatever remains of it — was disbanded for preaching otherwise. I remember thinking, distantly, when I'd heard of her: how absurd, a priestess of dead gods, a woman keeping vigil at an empty grave and insisting the ground is warm.

I am not laughing now.

Because according to fourteen years of Corvane's private books, the ground is very warm indeed. The gods did not die in the war. They went *under* — beneath Vhalen, beneath the erasure itself, sleeping in the wound the country left in the world. And they are waking. They have been waking slowly for decades, starving, blind, half-formed, hungry for the one thing they were always made of:

Us. Our feeling. Our remembering.

That is what gods eat. I know it now the way I know the weight of my own ledgers. A god is not power wearing a personality; a god is longing given somewhere to go. Every prayer ever whispered into Vhalen's air pooled downward like rain, and the things below grew fat on it, and when the country was unmapped and its prayers cut off at the source, the things below began to starve. A century of famine. A century of shrinking.

And then Corvane found them.

* * *

Here is where I must lay out what I read, flat and clean, because if I let myself feel it while I write it I will stop writing.

Corvane has been delivering memories to the waking gods for fourteen years. Not selling — the entries make clear no payment moves in that direction. Delivering. Feeding. And he has been *selective*. That is the horror of it, the specific, engineered horror. He does not send them random stolen years. He curates.

There is a section of the cipher — I think of it now as the diet book — where he logs his reasoning. A god fed only courage becomes reckless; noted and adjusted. A god fed too much grief turns inward, goes quiet, sleeps again; useless. A god fed love — pure, undiluted, stolen love, hundreds of jars of it, wedding days and christenings and the moment a mother first counts her baby's fingers — a god fed love becomes *devoted*. Devoted to whom? To the hand that feeds it.

He is shaping them. Jar by jar, feeling by feeling, he is deciding what the old gods of Vhalen will be when they finish waking. He is raising them the way a man raises hunting birds — hooded, starved, rewarded — so that when they open their eyes fully, the first face they see will be his, and the first word they learn will be *obey*.

One entry, dated three winters ago, I have read six times. I will transcribe it here from memory because I cannot bring myself to copy it out again:

*The orchard-thing responds beautifully to maternal material. Fed it the Kellaway batch (forty-one jars, first-loves and lullabies) and it sang for six hours. When I spoke the binding syllable it went silent instantly and inclined toward me. It is beginning to understand ownership. Another decade of disciplined feeding and we will have a god that kneels.*

*A god that kneels,* he wrote, in the same even hand a grocer uses for inventory.

And beneath it, smaller, almost tender, the sentence that finally made me put the book down and walk to the window and stand there watching the rain with my heart going like a trapped bird:

*What is a kingdom compared to a kneeling heaven?*

* * *

You will want to know what I felt. Everyone always wants to know what the ledger-keeper felt, as though the man who writes down the fire cannot also burn.

I felt cold. That is the truth of it — not rage, not tears, just a spreading cold, like frost climbing a pane, starting at my hands where they held the pages and moving inward toward whatever I keep in place of a heart. Because here is the thing I could not argue my way out of, sitting alone in the lamplight with forty years of complicity stacked under my desk:

Every jar Corvane fed to those starving things passed through my books first.

My hand recorded the widow's grief before it was poured down a god's throat. My notation system tracked the soldier's courage from acquisition to Vault Nine to the deep delivery, and I never once asked where the deliveries went, because asking questions is bad bookkeeping and I have always been *such* a good bookkeeper. Twenty-two years of flawless entries. Twenty-two years of making theft respectable by giving it tidy columns. And now those tidy columns read like a recipe — like a butcher's chart, like a feeding schedule — and every line bears my initials.

I used to tell myself the Guild's sin was the taking. That I stood apart from it, a neutral instrument, a scale that weighs but does not choose. I told myself I never stole for myself, and I believed that cleanliness meant something. But neutrality in a house like this is not innocence. Neutrality is the mortar between the bricks. Corvane could not have raised a single obedient god without a man willing to write *received in good condition* beside the name of every stolen childhood.

Received in good condition. God forgive me. I wrote it beside a jar containing someone's mother's voice.

* * *

There is one more entry. I have saved it because I do not know what to do with it, and I am a man who knows what to do with everything.

It is dated nineteen years ago — before the feeding began, before the diet book, back when the deep vault was still just a vault. It records an acquisition. Not a purchase. An *acquisition*, which in the old terminology meant taken rather than bought, and acquisitions were rare even then, reserved for special materials. The entry lists the vessel: one jar, green glass, sealed in wax. Contents: childhood memories, early years, approximately ages four through nine, quality exceptional, emotional saturation high. Provenance: *voluntary surrender by next of kin, payment rendered.*

Payment rendered. Nineteen years ago. Ages four through nine.

I was four years old once. I have always known this the way you know the far side of the moon — as fact, not landscape. My earliest certain memory is of the Guild itself: standing in the intake hall at perhaps ten years old, holding a slate, being told I had a gift for numbers. Before that, nothing. A smooth white wall where other people keep a garden. I assumed, the way orphans assume, that whatever came before was simply gone — faded, ordinary, unworthy of keeping.

The entry gives the child's initials. Two letters. *A.G.*

I sat very still for a long time after that. The lamp burned. The rain kept up its small applause against the glass. Somewhere below us — below the ledgers, below the vaults, below the foundations and the clay and the old drowned chapels — something vast turned over in its sleep, fed for another season on other people's tenderness, dreaming whatever dreams Corvane has chosen for it.

My childhood is in this building. It has been in this building for nineteen years, green glass and wax seal, filed and shelved and — I checked, God help me, I checked the transfer logs twice — drawn from storage eight times in fourteen years.

Eight times. Drawn, transferred, returned. Not sold. Not destroyed. *Used.*

I do not yet know for what. I do not know whether my four-year-old laughter is in some god's belly, or whether Corvane kept it closer for reasons of his own. But I know the taste of the cold in my hands now, and it has a name, and the name is not horror. Horror is for other people's losses. This is something worse. This is the vertigo of reading your own name in an inventory — of discovering that the ledger-keeper is also an entry, that the man who writes down what was taken was himself, once, a line in someone else's tidy column.

* * *

I should run. I understand that. Any sensible thief or clerk would take the blood-ledgers' knowledge and run for the border, except that the border runs through a country that isn't there, and I have spent my whole life indoors.

Instead I did the only thing I know how to do. I closed the cabinet. I replaced the floorboard. I sat at my desk, and I opened my own current ledger, and I began — in my neat, upright, blameless hand — a new set of books.

A true set. Every delivery Corvane ever made, cross-referenced, dated, totalled. Every god named in the old script, with every feeling he has fed it, itemized. If he is going to raise a kneeling heaven out of stolen love, then someone, at least, will hold the receipt.

Let it be said I learned my trade from a monster. Fine. Then let the monster's own methods be turned on him — because a man who feeds on secrets can be starved by exposure, and every empire of Corvane's kind rests, in the end, on paper. And paper burns.

I was three lines into the new ledger when the lamps flickered — not the wind; there is no draft this deep — and from far below, faint as a pulse through stone, I heard something that was either the settling of the foundations or a voice.

It sounded like roots splitting rock. It sounded, impossibly, like it was singing.

And then, nearer — directly beneath my chair, beneath the floorboard, beneath the cabinet of blood-written pages — a knock. Three soft knocks, patient as a creditor.

No one knows I am down here. No one should be able to find me.

The knocking came again, and with it, muffled through old stone, a woman's voice — low, urgent, and utterly unfamiliar:

"Ledger-keeper. I need to see your books."
