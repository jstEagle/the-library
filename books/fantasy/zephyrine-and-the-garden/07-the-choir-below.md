## 7. The Choir Below

The catacombs beneath Vhalen's capital did not appear on any map, which was fitting, because neither did the capital itself.

Zephyrine had been walking down into the dark for what felt like hours, following a sound she could not yet name. The stairs were cut from black stone that sweated moisture even in the driest season, and her candle — a stub of tallow she'd bought from a woman who sold candles and nothing else, in a shop that sold nothing else, in a street that changed its name when you weren't looking — guttered with every step as though the air itself were reluctant to burn.

She had come to the capital because the voice in the drowned chapel of Saint Verre had not stopped.

It had followed her for three days now. Not in her ears — she'd learned to distinguish that particular madness early in her novitiate, when the older sisters taught her which voices were prayer and which were hunger — but beneath her feet, in the soles of her boots, in the bones of her ankles. A low vibration like roots splitting stone, patient and immense, and it grew stronger with every mile she walked toward the heart of the erased country. By the time she reached the capital's outskirts — a sprawl of grey houses whose windows all faced inward, as if the buildings themselves were shy — the vibration had become a hum, and the hum had become something almost like words.

*Down,* it said. Or she thought it said. *Down, little listener.*

So she went down.

The stair ended at a landing where the architecture changed. Above, the stonework had been human — chiseled blocks, mortar, the honest clumsiness of hands. Here the walls curved in ways stone should not curve, smooth as the inside of a throat, veined with pale mineral light that pulsed faintly, slowly, like the breathing of something very large and very far away. Zephyrine held up her candle and watched its small flame lean toward the passage ahead, drawn by a draft that flowed *outward*, from below, carrying a smell she knew from her childhood in the cloister: cold incense, wet earth, and underneath it something sweeter and more terrible, like fruit rotting in a golden bowl.

She should have turned back. She was a disgraced priestess in a country that officially did not exist, standing at the mouth of a passage no human hand had carved, following a voice that had already been declared dead by an ecclesiastical council before she was born.

Zephyrine Verre-who-was-no-longer-Sister-Verre adjusted the strap of her satchel, checked that her knife was loose in its sheath, and walked on.

Because here was the thing they never understood, the thing the Synod had refused to hear when she stood before them at nineteen years old and told them the gods still breathed: faith is not a garment you can be stripped of. It is marrow. You can break the bone around it, but the marrow goes on making blood.

* * *

The first god she heard clearly was starving.

She knew it the way she knew hunger in a beggar's voice — by its texture, its desperation, the way it scraped at the edges of itself. The sound came from a side chamber off the main passage, a hollow like a bell jar of stone, and when Zephyrine pressed her palm against the wall she felt the vibration sharpen into something almost articulate.

It was singing. That was the only word for it. A long, looping, threadbare melody with no beginning she could find, sung in a language older than the country above, older perhaps than countries altogether. But the song kept faltering. Every few phrases it would thin to almost nothing, the way a fire thins when the wood runs out, and then rally, and then thin again.

And Zephyrine, who had spent eleven years learning to listen — real listening, the discipline the order called *anima audire*, the hearing-with-the-whole-of-the-soul — felt the meaning of it arrive in her chest whole, the way grief arrives, without translation:

*I am forgotten. I am forgotten. I am forgotten, and forgetting is a slow starvation, and I have been starving for a hundred years.*

Her eyes stung. She pressed her forehead to the cold wall.

"They're still here," she whispered. "You're still here."

The singing paused. Then, softly, tentatively, like a bird answering a whistle it hasn't heard since fledging, the god sang one phrase directly at her — at *her*, unmistakably, the vibration focusing through the stone into her palm, her wrist, her ribs.

*Someone hears. Someone hears. Someone—*

And then the voice cracked, or the stone did, and the moment broke, and the god sank back into its threadbare loop of forgotten-forgotten-forgotten, and Zephyrine stood in the dark with her hand pressed flat against the wall and wept without making any sound, because she had learned that skill too, in the years after the disbanding, when crying in public marked you as one of *those* — a god-touched, a relic-hugger, a priestess of a dead faith, pitied and avoided and quietly unemployable.

She wept, and then she wiped her face on her sleeve, and she kept walking, because the deeper sound was still calling.

* * *

The second god was glutted.

She heard it before she saw anything, a rich rolling chord of a voice, fat with harmony, singing in luxurious overlapping lines that made her teeth ache. The chamber it occupied was vast — a collapsed cistern, maybe, or a tomb complex swallowed by the hill — and its walls glistened with a substance she did not want to identify. The light here was different: warmer, amber-gold, pulsing faster than the pale veins elsewhere, and the air was thick enough to chew.

This god's song had *words*. Fragments of them. And the fragments were wrong.

*A wedding — the smell of bread — my daughter's laugh — the last summer — forgive me — I never told him — the sea in October — her hands —*

Human things. Human memories, worn smooth as river stones from handling, strung together into melody. The god was singing other people's lives, and it sang them beautifully, gorgeously, with a gluttonous relish that turned Zephyrine's stomach. This was not a god remembering the world. This was a god *consuming* it, digesting stolen feeling into song, growing fat and resonant and utterly estranged from anything of its own.

"Who fed you?" she asked aloud. Her voice fell flat in the chamber, absurdly small. "Who *did* this to you?"

The chord swelled, pleased, mistaking her question for praise. It offered her a fragment like a dog offering a stick: *a garden — someone's mother's voice — a boy —*

She flinched away from it. The fragment snagged on something in her — some half-formed sympathy — and for one vertiginous second she *saw*: green light through leaves, a woman humming, small hands in dark soil. Not her memory. Someone else's childhood, leaking out of a god's mouth like juice from a punctured fruit.

"No," she said, and her voice came out harder than she intended. "That isn't yours. None of this is yours."

The god's song stuttered. For just a moment, beneath the fat harmonies, she heard something else — something thin and frightened, the same note as the starving god's refrain, buried under a hundred years of stolen feeling like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Then the gluttony closed over it again, and the rich chord resumed, and Zephyrine backed out of the chamber with her heart hammering, understanding two things at once with terrible clarity.

First: the gods had not woken on their own. Something had been *feeding* them. Something with access to human memories in quantities no ordinary thief could gather.

Second: she recognized the trade goods. Bottled years. Stolen feeling. The inventory of the Vellum Guild.

* * *

She meant to leave then. She swore to herself afterward that she had truly meant to leave — that she had turned back toward the stair with every intention of climbing into the grey daylight, finding August Galloway, and telling the ledger-keeper exactly what their meticulous bookkeeping had been feeding.

But the deep voice rose to meet her before she'd taken ten steps.

It came up through the floor like heat, like floodwater, and it was not one voice. It was many. Dozens. Perhaps hundreds. They sang from everywhere below her at once, a vast discordant choir filling the bedrock of the capital the way blood fills a body, and no two of them sang the same thing. Starving gods keening their forgetfulness. Glutted gods rolling in their stolen harvests. Gods singing rage in rhythms like siege drums. Gods singing lullabies in keys that made her fillings ache. Some of the songs were beautiful. Some were obscene. Most were both at once, and none of them agreed, and the whole of it together was the most terrible music Zephyrine had ever heard, because it was not chaos.

It was a choir that had lost its conductor. A hundred divine voices, each singing alone, each singing *hungry*, each shaped — she could hear it now, could not stop hearing it — each shaped by whatever it had been fed. The guild hadn't just woken the gods. The guild was *composing* them. Choosing their diets. Growing gods to order, the way you'd grow a pear against a wall, training the branches flat.

She stood at the center of the catacomb junction with her candle burning low and let the choir wash over her, and she did the only thing her eleven years of training had prepared her for.

She listened. Properly. *Anima audire.* She opened herself the way the old sisters had taught her — breath slow, spine straight, the soul spread wide as a net — and she began, methodically, agonizingly, to sort the voices.

Most were strangers. Gods of rivers that had been rerouted, of festivals no longer held, of professions that had vanished, of a border that had been redrawn a century ago by men in a room she would never see. The erased country's erased pantheon, buried alive with it.

But one voice —

One voice she knew.

It came from far below, deeper than the others, and it was singing quietly, almost apologetically, a melody Zephyrine had heard every morning of her childhood: the Dawn Verses, sung at first light in the chapel of Saint Verre, the tune the sisters used to wake the novices gently, the tune her own grandmother had hummed while kneading bread, because the god of small mercies had always preferred to be sung in kitchens rather than temples.

*Santiel,* she thought, and her knees nearly gave.

Santiel. God of small mercies. Of warm bread and mended fences and the mercy of being unnoticed when you wept. The gentlest of the pantheon, the one the old sisters said loved humans best because he asked for so little — a crumb, a kind word, a remembered name. He had been the first god Zephyrine ever served and the reason she'd taken her vows at sixteen, young and certain and stupid with devotion.

And his voice — his dear, familiar, kitchen-warm voice — was *wrong*. Threaded through the Dawn Verses now ran a second melody, a counter-melody of appetite, notes that bent toward hunger the way a sapling bends toward a window. He was singing her childhood hymn in a voice that had been taught to want.

They were feeding even Santiel. Even him. The god of crumbs and kindness, force-fed on stolen longing until his gentleness had gone strange, until he was becoming something that wore his voice like a mask over a mouth.

Zephyrine slid down the wall until she was sitting in the dust, candle between her feet, and she put her face in her hands, and for a long time she simply grieved.

Not the clean grief of a funeral. The other kind — the grief of watching something you love be *used*. She had felt it once before, at nineteen, when the Synod declared the gods dead and melted the bells and sold the reliquaries, and she had thought that was the worst wound her faith would ever take. She had been wrong. A faith killed outright can be mourned and buried. But a faith *weaponized* — a gentle god fattened into an instrument, a hymn rewritten into an appetite — that you had to carry living, day after day, the way you carry a wounded thing that keeps struggling.

"They were going to make you obedient," she said to the floor, to the choir, to Santiel somewhere below in the dark. "That was always the plan, wasn't it? Not worship. *Ownership.*"

The choir sang on, discordant, enormous, oblivious.

And Zephyrine sat in the dust of a country that officially did not exist, and something in her finished breaking — and something else, in the same moment, finished hardening.

She thought of August Galloway, with their ink-stained fingers and their careful ledgers and their proud insistence that they had never stolen for themselves. She thought of the way the ledger-keeper had looked at the burning accounts that night on the road — not with guilt, exactly, but with the dawning horror of a person reading their own handwriting on a confession they don't remember signing.

"You knew," she murmured. "Some part of you knew. That's why your books are written in blood. Everything true costs."

She rose. She brushed off her habit. She took the stub of candle in her fist like a weapon she had no idea how to use.

Below her, Santiel's corrupted voice sang the Dawn Verses into the dark, and Zephyrine did the bravest and most foolish thing she had done since the day the Synod struck her name from the rolls.

She answered.

Quietly at first, her voice cracking with disuse and grief, she sang the counter-line — the response the sisters would chant at dawn, the human half of the verses, the part that had always been the congregation's to give. *We remember you at the breaking of bread. We remember you at the mending of fences. We remember your name.*

The choir faltered.

All of it — hundreds of voices, starving and glutted and raging — stumbled on the same beat, the way a room falls silent when a door opens. And from far below, thin and strained but unmistakable, one voice found the melody again and tried, *tried*, to sing it the old way, the kitchen way, the merciful way — fighting through the appetite threaded into its throat like a man trying to speak around a gag.

*Zephyrine,* the god of small mercies sang, using the only human word left in its repertoire. *Zephyrine, Zephyrine, Zephyrine.*

Not a summons. A plea.

She stood alone at the crossing of a thousand buried songs, holding a candle stub, and she understood at last what she had been walking toward since the night in the drowned chapel. Not answers. Not safety. Purpose, ugly and clear as a blade: somewhere above her, someone was keeping books on the feeding of gods, and that someone had recently grown a conscience, and conscience was the only weapon this war had ever required.

She climbed toward the daylight, and the choir sang her up the stairs, and by the time she emerged into the grey streets of the unmapped capital, her grief had cooled into something that would keep.

She needed to find August Galloway.

And she needed to find them before whoever was conducting this choir did.
