## 8. Corvane's Offer

The summons came at the hour when the guild's lamps burn lowest — that gray interval before dawn when even the bottled memories seem to sleep in their jars, their light dimmed to embers. A boy in guild livery, no older than I had been when they bought me, stood at my door with a folded card of black vellum. He didn't meet my eyes. None of them ever did. The Vellum Guild taught its children early that looking too closely at a thing was the first step toward wanting it.

*Master Corvane requests the pleasure of your company,* the card said, in ink that shifted color depending on the angle — a small vanity of his, a demonstration that he could afford inks that did not exist anywhere else in the world.

There was no pleasure in it. There never was. I dressed carefully, because Corvane noticed clothing the way other men noticed lies, and I took the long stairs down through the vaults rather than the lift, because I wanted time to think, and because I wanted to pass the shelf where the jar sat.

I had not opened it. Not yet. But I had taken to visiting it the way one visits a grave — or a wound you keep deciding not to scratch. It sat on the third tier of Vault Nine, between a merchant's stolen courage and a child's fear of deep water, and it glowed with a light I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting: *that* gold, the particular warm amber of a summer afternoon that no alchemist could counterfeit because no alchemist had been there. My label was still on it, in my own careful hand, catalogued twenty years ago by a clerk who did not know what she was writing:

**GALLOWAY, AUGUST — AGES 4–11. MISC. CONTENTS. PROVENANCE: PARENTAL SALE. CONDITION: EXCELLENT.**

*Excellent condition.* Seven years of a life, sealed and preserved, and the ledger called it excellent the way it might call a vintage wine excellent, or a bolt of silk.

I stood before it for exactly eleven breaths, which was all the time I allowed myself, and then I went down to Corvane.

* * *

His office occupied the top of the guildhouse like a crown occupies a head — visibly, uncomfortably, and with the constant suggestion that it might fall. Books climbed every wall, but none of them were ledgers. Corvane kept the ledgers elsewhere, in a place only I knew, because the ledgers were written in my blood and he liked to say that a man should keep his heart close but his debts closer. The books in his office were histories. Old ones. Histories of wars, mostly, and of the countries that wars unmake.

He was standing at his window when I entered, watching the city go gray-gold below us, and he did not turn around.

"August," he said. "Do you know what today is?"

"Inventory reconciliation," I said. "Quarter's end."

"Quarter's end." He smiled at the glass. "You always know the calendar better than I do. That's why I keep you." Now he turned, and his eyes were kind in the way that a well-made trap is well-made — everything fitted, nothing wasted. "Sit, please."

I sat. The chair across from his desk was lower than his own; it always had been. I had never resented this before. I resented it now, and the resentment felt new and strange in my mouth, like a spice from a country I'd never visited.

"You've been to Vault Nine," he said.

Not a question. I kept my face still. Twenty years of keeping ledgers teaches you a certain stillness; grief and guilt both move the face, and I had spent two decades learning to file both away before they reached the surface. "I conduct inventory in all the vaults."

"In Vault Nine you lingered." He came around the desk and sat, folding his hands. He was an old man built like a door — broad, flat, closed — except for those eyes, which were young and wet and quick as a thief's. "Eleven breaths, by the ward-count. Every night this week. You stand in front of Shelf Three, Tier Two, and you breathe eleven times, and then you leave without touching anything." He tilted his head. "It's very disciplined. I almost admire it."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree, the way a ship tilts when it leaves harbor. The ward-count. Of course there was a ward-count. I had counted the wards myself once, years ago, cataloguing the security of the vaults as meticulously as I catalogued everything else, and it had never once occurred to me that the wards were counting back.

"You knew," I said. "When you ordered the inventory. You knew what I'd find."

"I hoped," Corvane said gently. "Knowing is such a blunt instrument. Hope lets a person reveal themselves." He opened a drawer — I watched his hands do it, old hands, ink-stained at the cuticles despite everything money could buy — and set something on the desk between us.

A key. Small, brass, unremarkable. The kind sold in any locksmith's shop in the low city for three coppers.

"The jar is warded," he said. "You know that. Standard preservation warding — nothing you couldn't break, given an hour and the right tools, and you have both, because I gave them to you myself over the years, though perhaps you thought I wasn't paying attention." He pushed the key an inch toward me. "But a warded jar, broken, loses perhaps a tenth of its contents. Spillage. Decay at the seal. Seven years of childhood, minus ten percent." He shook his head slowly, a mourner at a funeral he himself had arranged. "That's no way to get yourself back, August. Half-remembered summers. A mother's voice with holes in it. You'd spend the rest of your life reaching for the missing pieces and hating me for the gaps."

"And the alternative?" My voice came out level. I was proud of that, later. I am proud of it now.

"The alternative is this." From inside his coat he drew a second object and laid it beside the key, and the room changed.

It was a jar. Not mine — mine was seven years of amber afternoons. This one was older, and darker, and it held not memory but something memory floats in: a liquid dark as the space between stars, in which a single pale shape turned slowly, like a fish, like a moon, like a face seen underwater. I knew what it was. Every keeper of the guild knew what it was, the way children know what death is — abstractly, completely, and without ever expecting to see it on a desk in front of them.

A first memory. The oldest kind we traded. The kind that could not be made, only found, because every person had exactly one, and most people guarded theirs without knowing they were doing it.

"This belonged to a woman in the Salt Quarter," Corvane said. "Died last winter. No heirs. Her first memory — her mother lifting her toward a window, snow outside, the whole world white and new. Do you understand what I'm offering?"

"No," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because I understood perfectly and I hated him for making me say it aloud anyway.

"I'm offering you a trade that costs you nothing." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he used for closing sales, warm and reasonable and utterly without mercy. "Your childhood, returned whole — not spilled through a broken ward but decanted properly, by my own hand, every hour intact. And in exchange—" he tapped the desk twice, softly, "—the ledgers."

The word fell into the room and lay there.

"The ledgers are guild property," I said.

"The ledgers are *your blood*, August. Written in it, page by page, year by year. Don't look surprised — you've never asked why I required blood-ink for the master ledger and nothing else. You told yourself it was tradition. Sentiment. Old Corvane and his rituals." He smiled. "Blood remembers what it has recorded. Every entry you've ever made lives in your veins as much as in the pages. Which means the ledgers can be completed by no one else, and bound by no one else, and—" he spread his hands, generous, magnanimous, insufferable, "—given freely by no one else but you."

"Bound," I said. "Bound to what?"

"To the future." He rose and went to the window again, and when he spoke again it was with the cadence of a man reciting scripture he had written himself. "The gods are waking, August. Under Vhalen, under the drowned chapels, under every place we erased and told ourselves erasure would hold. They're waking hungry, because hunger is all that's left when a country forgets its gods and the gods forget their people. And hunger, unshaped, makes monsters." He turned. "But hunger, *fed*, makes instruments. A god that has tasted human longing can be taught which longings to favor. A god that has been fed memories — chosen memories, curated memories, memories selected by someone with your eye — becomes something the world hasn't seen since the first age. Obedient. Useful. *Ours.*"

"And the ledgers bind them."

"The ledgers *name* them. Nothing binds a god like being named correctly. Ask any priestess." Something flickered across his face at the word — distaste, or appetite, I couldn't tell which. "Every soul whose memory we've ever bought or sold or bottled is named in your blood. Hundreds of thousands of names, each one attached to a longing, a loss, a love. Fed to the right god in the right order, that's not a meal. That's a *leash*."

I looked at the jar on the desk. At the pale shape turning in the dark. At the small brass key beside it, worth more to me than everything else in the building.

And I will be honest here, because honesty is the one discipline I never abandoned, even at the end — especially at the end: for a long moment, I wanted it.

I wanted it the way the drowning want air. I saw it all: the jar opened gently, the amber light spilling up over my hands, my mother's voice arriving in my ears after twenty years of silence — *August, come inside, the garden's getting dark* — the smell of that garden, the name of our street, the face of the child I had been before a clerk with neat handwriting labeled my life *excellent condition*. Seven years. Whole. Mine. All I had to do was hand him the only things in the world written in my blood, and let him feed a starving god half a nation's stolen hearts until it learned to heel.

He watched me want it. That was the cruelest part. He sat there behind his low chair and his high window and he watched me want it, and he let me see him watching, because he believed — truly, sincerely, with the full confidence of a man who has never once been refused — that wanting was enough. That everyone had a price and his genius was simply knowing where to look.

"Take your time," he said softly.

"I don't need it."

I stood. My legs held. Somewhere below us, in the vaults, hundreds of jars glowed their small private colors, and I thought of every entry I had ever made in my neat, meticulous, blood-written hand, and the anger came up through me like water through cracked stone — not the hot anger of the moment but the cold kind, the kind that keeps, the kind you can store for years in a cool dark place and it only gets stronger.

Because here is what I understood, sitting in that too-low chair while he dangled my own childhood like bait on a hook: he had done this to me twice. Once when I was four, or six, or nine — some age I couldn't even grieve properly because I couldn't remember grieving it. My parents had sold me, yes, but the Guild had *bought*, and Corvane had signed the purchase, and then he had handed the boy he bought a pen and said *keep the books*, and for twenty years I had called it employment. Called it purpose. Called it, God help me, home.

And now, when I finally found the wound, he offered to sell me back to myself. At a price. Framed as mercy. As if returning what he stole were a gift, as if giving me back my own mother's voice were a favor I ought to be grateful for, as if the crime were erased by the transaction rather than completed by it.

"You think this is generosity," I said.

"I think it's *practicality*," Corvane said. "You're the best ledger-keeper I've ever had. I'd rather not lose you, and I'd rather not take the ledgers by force — blood resists coercion, it curdles, the entries spoil. They must be given." He smiled again, patient as erosion. "So I'm asking. Nicely. With a gift attached. That's more courtesy than this world usually shows anyone, August. More than it showed you."

"No," I said.

The word came out quiet, and it surprised us both — him because he had clearly never imagined hearing it, me because of how little it cost. I had rehearsed refusals my whole life, in the safe theater of my head, and in rehearsal they had always been hard, dramatic, expensive. This one was easy. It cost nothing. It was just true.

"Think about—"

"No." Louder now, and the anger was in it, and I let it be, because I wanted him to hear exactly what kind of no it was. "You don't get to sell me my childhood and call it mercy. You don't get to starve a god on stolen grief and call it stewardship. And you don't get my blood — not the pages, not the veins, not one drop beyond what you already wrung out of me for twenty years." I looked at the jar on his desk, at the pale turning shape. "Keep your dead woman's snow. Keep your key. And keep away from Vault Nine, because if you touch that jar, if so much as breathe on it, I will burn every ledger in the archive before your hunters cross the city — and we'll both find out how useful your obedient god is with no names to feed it."

The silence afterward had texture. Dust in the lamplight. The city waking faintly below the window.

Corvane's expression did not change, which was itself a kind of answer. Men who expect refusal prepare for it; men who cannot conceive of it simply go still, like machines encountering an input they were never built to parse. When he moved again, it was only to slide the key and the jar back into his coat, unhurried, a shopkeeper closing for the day.

"That's a pity," he said, and meant it, and that was the worst thing in the whole terrible conversation — that he genuinely, warmly, regretted it, the way one regrets weather.

I walked to the door. My hand was on the handle when he spoke again, and his voice had shed its warmth entirely, leaving something smooth and cold underneath, like stone under moss.

"A priestess," he said. "Disgraced. Verre's order, unless I miss my guess — and I never miss. She crossed into Vhalen three nights ago, following voices only she can hear. And a certain ledger-keeper has been requesting maps of roads that appear on no map I own." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "I haven't stopped you yet, August. Consider what that patience is costing me. Consider what it will stop costing."

I left without answering. It was the only answer left that he hadn't already priced.

* * *

The stairs up from Corvane's office were long, and I climbed them slowly, and by the top my hands had started to shake — delayed, the way my body always insisted on feeling things a beat after my mind had finished with them. I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone of the wall and breathed, and thought about jars, and keys, and blood.

He knew about Zephyrine. Of course he did. He had known about Vault Nine, about the ward-count, about my eleven breaths a night. I had spent twenty years believing myself the guild's invisible man, the meticulous ghost behind the meticulous books, and I had been a specimen in a jar the entire time — observed, labeled, shelved.

*GALLOWAY, AUGUST. MISC. CONTENTS.*

Well. Let him watch. Let him count my breaths.

Because I had made him a promise in that office, and I intended to keep it, and there was one thing he had forgotten in all his elegant arithmetic of prices and leverage: the ledgers were written in my blood, yes. Every page. Every entry. Every name.

Which meant that when the time came, I would not need to steal them.

I would only need to decide what my blood was for.

I went down to the archive, unlocked the inner door that only I could open, and stood in the dark among the shelves where twenty years of my own veins lay stacked and waiting — and far above me, faint through miles of stone and city, I heard the first bell of morning, and beneath it, thinner than memory, something that was not a bell at all.

Something singing.
