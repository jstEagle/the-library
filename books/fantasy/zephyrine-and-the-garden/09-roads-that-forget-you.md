## 9. Roads That Forget You

*Zephyrine*

The road out of the flooded market-town was gone by the time we needed it.

I don't mean washed away, though there was water everywhere in Vhalen, standing black and patient in the ditches. I mean *gone*. The cart track that had carried us in from the east — rutted, honest, smelling of wet clay — simply ended at the last fence post, dissolving into a meadow of pale grass that had no business growing where a road should be. Behind us, hoofbeats. Ahead, grass.

"They're rearranging," August said, in the flat tone they used for cataloguing disasters.

"Roads don't rearrange."

"These do." They crouched, pressed two fingers to the ground as if taking its pulse — an accountant's gesture, I would learn, everything measured, everything entered. "The country doesn't recognize us. We're not on any list it keeps, because there are no lists anymore. So it's... correcting us. Editing us out of its geography."

I looked back down the vanished track. The hoofbeats were louder now, and there was something wrong about their rhythm, too fast, too many, like a heartbeat with extra chambers.

"So what does it do," I asked, "with people it doesn't recognize?"

August stood and brushed off their fingers with terrible care.

"Nothing violent," they said. "That's the worst of it. You just stop arriving anywhere. You walk forever, and every road is new, and eventually you forget you were ever going somewhere." A pause. "Guild hunters have ledgers. The country has none. We can outrun one. Not the other."

That was when I saw it — or almost saw it — through the grass, half-sunk into the hillside: a squat stone pillar, no taller than a man, carved over and over with hands. Open hands, cupped hands, hands letting go.

A wayshrine. My order had burned the last of them in the lowlands eighty years ago, but here was one, waiting, its bowl-shaped hollow filled with rainwater and one dry flower petal.

"August," I said. "I think I know how you make a country remember you."

* * *

*August*

The wayshrine wanted a memory. Of course it did. Everything in Vhalen ran on the same currency my guild had cornered in the capital — only here, nobody had thought to put it in jars first. Here you paid honestly, from your own hand to the stone's.

Zephyrine explained it the way she explained everything: quickly, with her chin up, as if daring the information to embarrass her. Her old order had kept the rites. The shrines were older than her order, older than the war nobody named, older maybe than the gods themselves. You knelt. You cupped your hands in the basin. You gave up something small and true — not your most precious memory, nothing the heart would bleed over, but something *real*, something with weight and texture, freely surrendered. In exchange, the land took note of you. Wrote you into itself.

"A receipt," I said.

"If you like."

"I've spent twenty years issuing receipts for other people's memories. I'd rather not become one."

"You'd rather vanish instead?" She was already kneeling, sleeves pushed back, water dripping from her cupped hands into the basin. "Then walk on, ledger-keeper. Enjoy the infinite meadow."

I watched her bow her head and murmur something too low to catch. When she rose, she swayed slightly, caught herself on the shrine's carved edge.

"What did it take?" I hated how much I wanted to know.

"The smell of my grandmother's kitchen," she said. Her voice was steady; her eyes weren't. "Bread and lamp oil and something burning slightly. I kept the fact of her. I kept that she was kind. I just—" She flexed her fingers, as if the memory had been held there. "I can't smell it anymore. It's like a room in my mind where the door is painted shut."

"That's theft."

"No," she said quietly. "That's *trade*. There's a difference, August. Your masters never learned it."

The hoofbeats reached the meadow's edge while I was still kneeling. I hadn't meant to kneel, quite — my knees had decided on their own, which I suppose is what devotion feels like to people who have it. I cupped the cold water. I searched myself for something small and true and found, to my surprise, that I was spoiled for choice, because a life spent recording other people's feelings leaves very little room to make your own.

In the end I gave it the taste of ink. Twenty years of it, the particular bitterness of iron-gall ink on the tongue when a tired clerk licks his thumb to turn a page. I gave the shrine ten thousand pages' worth of that taste, and felt it lift out of me like a splinter drawn clean, and I understood suddenly why Zephyrine's eyes had been wet.

The hillside shivered. And through the pale grass, unmistakable, a road unrolled itself westward — worn stones, leaning fences, a crooked signpost bearing three names in a script I had only ever seen in the oldest sealed folios.

Vhalen had written us down. We existed, provisionally, for another day.

Behind us, the hunters reined in at the meadow's edge and did not follow, because the meadow no longer contained us. One of them — I did not look back, but I heard — screamed in frustration, and the sound came to us warped, delayed, like a shout across a valley that wasn't there.

"Move," Zephyrine said. "While the ink holds. While *you* hold."

We moved.

* * *

*Zephyrine*

Three shrines the first day. Five the second. The country tested us constantly, the way a tide tests a sandcastle — roads folding shut behind our heels if we lingered, bridges turning shy and wandering off downstream, whole villages sliding politely out of our path like boats parting around a stone. By the third day I had given away my grandmother's kitchen, the exact blue of the chapel tiles at Saint Verre, the sound my teacher made clearing her throat before scripture, and the name of the boy who'd shared his plums with me at my ordination. I kept the plum. I lost the boy.

It was strange, being robbed so gently. Strange, how much of me was made of things that could be taken without breaking anything.

August paid at every shrine like a man settling debts, grimly, precisely, always giving away clerk-things: the ache of sitting too long, the specific gray of guildhouse mornings, the ritual of winding a clockwork calendar. But at the fifth shrine, on a ridge above a town whose church bell rang notes in the wrong order, August hesitated a long time with wet hands.

"Give it something cheap," I advised. "A habit. A color."

"It's asking for something true," August said. "*Only the true takes root.* That's what the inscription says. Cheap won't hold the road."

So I watched them close their eyes, and whatever they fed the stone, it cost them. When they stood, they pressed a hand flat against their sternum like a person checking whether all their furniture was still upright.

"What was it?" I asked, because fairness demanded they get to ask me back.

"The sound of my own name," August said. "Said aloud, by someone glad to see me. I gave it the whole sound of it." They tried a smile; it didn't quite fit their face yet, like shoes not broken in. "I still know the word. I just can't hear anyone saying it anymore. It'll be silence wearing a shape."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing useful to say, and Vhalen had already taught us both that empty comfort is just a memory you're pre-selling to the shrines. Instead I walked beside them down toward the bell-tones, close enough that our shoulders touched once on the steep part, and neither of us stepped away.

* * *

*August*

The seventh night we made camp in the shell of a waystation that had clearly given up on being a waystation — roof half-open to a sky full of restless stars, walls furred with moss, a hearth where someone long forgotten had left a flint wrapped in oilcloth as a kindness to strangers. Zephyrine built the fire with a priestess's economy, no wasted motion, and I fed it pages.

Not my blood-ledgers. Those stayed against my ribs, where Corvane wanted them and where I had begun, uncomfortably, to want them too — twenty years of my own veins, warm as a second heartbeat. No. These were surplus entries, duplicates, the accounting chaff I'd smuggled out of the vaults: minor transactions, bottled trivialities. A merchant's Tuesday. A child's fear of cellar stairs. Paper burns beautifully when the paper is full of someone.

Zephyrine watched the flames take a description of a wedding nobody remembered attending. "Does it hurt them? The people in the entries?"

"They don't know they've lost it. That's the design. You can't grieve what you never knew you owned." I turned another page. "Though sometimes the jar knows. Sometimes they whisper."

"And this is what you did. For twenty years."

"This is what I did *well*. There's a difference I clung to." The fire popped; a stored Sunday went up in sparks the color of wine. "I told myself the guild didn't make thieves of us. It made librarians. Caretakers. Someone has to keep stolen things safe until—" I stopped. I had never finished that sentence before, in twenty years of saying it to myself, and I couldn't finish it now either.

"Until?" she said softly.

"Until someone worse came for them, I think. That was the whole theology of it. Be the gentle hand inside the machine." I looked at her across the fire — firelight on her face, moss and ruin behind her, this disgraced woman who had heard gods and run from them and then, impossibly, turned around and walked toward them again. "You think I'm complicit."

"I think you're here," she said. "Which is more interesting."

We traded after that. I don't know who started it — I think she offered a memory to me the way she'd offered one to the shrine, freely, cupped in her hands. The boy with the plums; she still had the fruit but not the boy, so she gave me the story entire, reconstructed from its edges, and I received it carefully and found I could hold it without it weighing anything at all. I gave her the layout of the vaults, which was not a memory so much as a map, but she took it anyway because she said maps could be love too, in the right mouth.

Somewhere past midnight she gave me the blue of the chapel tiles back — no. She gave me a different blue. Her mother's blue, a dye lot gone slightly wrong, the color of a dress worn on a homecoming. And I realized, watching her talk with her hands, that I had started doing something I had trained out of myself two decades ago.

I was collecting. For myself. Freely.

"Ask me something true," I said, before I could stop myself.

She considered me a long time, firelight moving in her eyes.

"Why did you open the jar," she said. "Your jar. Corvane told you not to. Every instinct of survival said leave it sealed. Why didn't you?"

Because I had been asking the question my whole life without knowing it, I wanted to say. Because every morning I woke and inventoried myself — pulse, appetite, competence — and found the inventory short. Because a ledger that doesn't balance will keep a clerk awake in ways no ghost ever manages.

"Because I checked," I said. "Against the master index. And my entry had a buyer's signature I recognized." My voice came out strange, flattened, the voice I used for impossible figures. "Mine."

The fire settled. Somewhere out in the dark, faint as a coin dropped in another room, a wayshrine hummed to itself, keeping books.

"Tomorrow," Zephyrine said finally, "we cross the Sorrow Bridge, if the country lets it exist by morning. After that, the high roads. After that—"

"After that, the catacombs. The choir below."

"After that." She lay back against her pack, eyes still open, watching stars that Vhalen might redraw by dawn. "August. If we make the land remember us enough — if we pay and pay and pay until it knows us — what happens when it remembers us *completely*?"

I had no answer. But in the grey hour before sleep took me, I dreamed of the master index, and my own name in it, and beneath the name a word I had never let myself read aloud:

*Pending.*

* * *

*Zephyrine*

The Sorrow Bridge existed at sunrise, thank every listening thing, but it had changed overnight in some mood of the country's — longer now, arcing over a gorge that hadn't been in the gorge's place yesterday, and halfway across, waiting in the middle like a toll-taker, stood a wayshrine larger than any we'd seen. Not carved with hands. Carved with mouths.

And behind us, at the bridge's near end, riders. Not many. Three. Guild hunters travel light because their ledgers travel heavy, and the leader wore Corvane's seal on a chain, and under the seal, on a cord against her breast, hung a small dark jar that whispered even at this distance.

It whispered my name.

"Zephyrine," August said beside me, very calm, the calm of a person doing arithmetic under pressure. "The shrine wants payment. The hunters want us. The bridge wants—"

"The bridge wants us to be real," I said. "So let's be unforgettable."

I knelt at the mouth-carved stone, and reached for something to give, and found — for the first time since Saint Verre — that I knew exactly what it would be. Not a smell. Not a color. Something true, and costly, and mine: the memory of the night my order disbanded, the night I signed the renunciation, the exact feeling of the pen going through my vows like a knife through wet vellum. The country should have that. Let it remember what erasure costs from the inside.

The stone drank. The bridge solidified beneath our feet, every stone singing a low grounded note, and Vhalen wrote us deeper into itself — I felt the pen-stroke of it go through me like weather.

Then I stood, and turned to face the hunters, and the nearest wayshrine in the hills behind them began — softly, patiently, with the hunger of a living thing —

—to sing back.
