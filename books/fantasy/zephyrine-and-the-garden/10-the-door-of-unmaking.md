## 10. The Door of Unmaking

The hermit's hut sat at the bottom of a valley that had no name on any map, because the mapmakers had never thought to look, and the road that led down to it had forgotten itself twice on the way. Zephyrine felt it happen both times — a soft lurch behind her eyes, like a stair that wasn't there — and both times August caught her elbow without comment, as if steadying her had become simply something their hands did.

Three days out from the flooded market-town, and they still hadn't spoken about it. About the god. About what it had done to the hunters, how completely it had eaten them — not their bodies, which lay folded and breathing in the shallows like sleeping men, but everything else. Their names, their mothers' voices, the small private reasons they'd gotten up each morning. Zephyrine had watched a starving god take violence out of five people and leave them innocent as infants, and she still didn't know whether to call it salvation or slaughter.

August hadn't asked her why she'd done it. That was its own kind of mercy, and she was learning that August dealt in mercies the way other people dealt in coins — quietly, precisely, and always with an exact count.

"There," she said, when the hut appeared below them: a squat shape of fieldstone furred with moss, chimney smoking, a garden gone gloriously to weed around it. Smoke meant life. In Vhalen, smoke was practically a miracle.

"You're certain he'll talk to us?" August asked.

"He was my teacher." The words came out strange, unused. "Before the disbanding. Brother Anselm. They declared him dead thirty years ago."

"Was he?"

Zephyrine looked at the smoke rising thin and crooked into a sky the color of pewter. "In this country? Probably not. Dead men here have a way of continuing."

* * *

He opened the door before she knocked. Of course he did. Anselm had always heard prayers before they were finished being prayed, and Zephyrine suspected that in the forty years since his order dissolved, he had worn his listening down to nothing but habit and bone.

He was smaller than she remembered. Everyone was smaller than she remembered; that was one of Vhalen's cruelties, that it shrank your past every time you came back to it. But his eyes were the same — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had spent decades watching a door that everyone insisted was a wall.

"Sister Zephyrine," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like a bell rung in an empty church. "You've been listening."

"I haven't had a choice."

"No one ever does. Come in before the road changes its mind about you. It does that, lately. The land is restless." He stepped aside, then paused, looking at August with an expression she couldn't read — recognition, or its opposite. "And you've brought a guest the country doesn't know."

"They're with me," Zephyrine said.

"Oh, I can see that." Anselm's voice was mild, but something under it was not. "That's rather what worries me."

* * *

The inside of the hut was one room, and most of it was books — real ones, vellum and rag paper, stacked in columns that leaned against each other like tired pilgrims. A fire tickled at the hearth. Anselm put water on for tea and moved through his small kingdom with the economy of long illness, and only up close did Zephyrine see how bad it was: the gray of his lips, the rattle beneath his breath, the way his hand trembled pouring the water so that he let her do it instead, which for Anselm was a surrender more complete than any the disbanding had forced on him.

"How long?" she asked.

"A while." He smiled, settling onto a bench by the fire like a man folding a letter he'd finished writing. "Long enough. Sit. Both of you. You came down that road following something, and things that are followed deserve an answer."

She told him. It poured out of her faster than she intended — the drowned chapel, the voice like roots splitting stone, the choir beneath the capital, the starving god at the market-town that had answered her prayer and kept answering until there was nothing left of the hunters to save. She told him about Corvane, about memories sold by the jarful, about gods being fed human longing like livestock being fattened. She did not look at August while she said it, but she felt them go very still beside her, the particular stillness of someone hearing their own complicity spoken aloud in front of a witness.

Anselm listened the way stone listens: completely, without interruption, giving back nothing until he chose to.

When she finished, he was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the weed-garden with a sound almost like speech.

"I taught you the old catechism once," he said finally. "Do you remember the first question?"

"*Where do the gods come from?*" She'd been nine. She could still feel the cold chapel floor under her knees.

"And the answer?"

"*They come from outside. Through the Door.*"

"The Door of Unmaking." Anselm nodded slowly, as if she'd passed an examination he'd already decided she would fail. "Your order spent three hundred years teaching children that answer and then telling them it was a metaphor. A story. Something to make the mystery feel tidy." His laugh was dry and cost him something. "It is not a metaphor. There is a wound in the world, child, and it has a location, and the gods came through it the way water comes through a crack in a dam — all at once, and forever after, and never quite fitting the shape of what received them."

Zephyrine's mouth had gone dry. "Where?"

"Underneath." He spread one trembling hand toward the floor, toward the valley, toward the whole silent weight of Vhalen. "All of it. This country sits over the Door like a bandage over a wound that never closed. That is why it was erased, girl. Not because it lost a war. Because it won one, once, badly — because it stood between the world and what comes through, and the price of standing there was to be forgotten by everything except itself." His pale eyes found hers. "You asked me once why God allowed suffering. Do you recall my answer?"

"You said you didn't believe in a God who allowed anything. You believed in doors."

"I did say that." He coughed, wet and long, and Zephyrine rose to steady him, but he waved her down. "Listen now, because I don't have the breath for saying things twice, and this is the part your whole order died refusing to learn."

The fire seemed to lean closer. Even August — who had spent the visit cataloguing the room the way they catalogued everything, shelves and spines and exits — went motionless, attention narrowed to a point.

"The Door cannot be destroyed," Anselm said. "Believe me, better men than Corvane tried, in the years before the erasure, and the attempt is what killed this country's memory in the first place. But a door has two natures, doesn't it? It lets things through. And it closes."

"It can be shut," Zephyrine whispered.

"It can be unmade — which is different, and worse, and better. Unmade from within, by a hand passing through it as it closes. And it will admit exactly two kinds of person, together or not at all." He held up two fingers, gnarled as roots. "One who is wholly remembered. Every street, every shrine, every half-forgotten grandmother's story in this country must hold some piece of them, must recognize them down to the marrow — because the Door is made of memory, and it opens to what it knows. And one who is wholly forgotten. Erased. With nothing left of themselves for the Door to hold onto, so that it cannot close around them, cannot keep them, cannot make them part of its keeping."

Silence. Somewhere in the stacks, a book settled with a sigh.

"That's why you flinched at me," August said quietly. It was the first thing they'd said since entering. "At the door. You knew what I was."

Anselm turned his gaze on them, and Zephyrine saw him really look — past the guild-plain clothes, past the careful blankness August wore like a ledger cover, down to whatever was underneath. What he found made his face soften with something close to grief.

"You're not forgotten yet," he said. "But you're well on the road. Someone sold the country's claim on you, didn't they? Piece by piece, year by year, until what's left is a person shaped like an absence." He said it without cruelty, the way a doctor names a disease. "I'm sorry. I know what that costs. But understand what you are, child. You are the second key. Whether you wish to be or not."

"And the first?" Zephyrine's voice came out thin. "Who's remembered by all of Vhalen?"

Anselm looked at her.

Not at August. At her. And the look lasted one heartbeat too long, and in that heartbeat Zephyrine understood, with the sick clarity of a prayer answered too literally, exactly who had spent her whole life being known — by parishes, by congregations, by a disbanded order that had scattered her name across a hundred towns like seed. She had prayed in every chapel in this country. She had buried its dead and blessed its births. She was stitched into Vhalen the way thread is stitched into cloth, invisible everywhere and holding everywhere.

"No," she said.

"You asked where the Door was," Anselm said gently. "I should have warned you that the Door asks questions back."

"It wants one of us to walk through and be erased." Her voice cracked on the word. "That's the prophecy? That's the great hope? One of us goes into the wound and stops existing, and the other stands there and watches, and that's supposed to be—" She stopped, because the word that wanted to come out was *love*, and saying it in front of Anselm, in front of August, would make it a fact, and facts could be taken away, and she had lost enough facts.

"Hope is always priced," Anselm said. "That's what makes it holy instead of comfortable. Your order forgot that too. We taught comfort for centuries and called it faith, and when the truth arrived wearing its true face we called it heresy and ran." He drew a slow breath that whistled faintly. "I won't dress it up. The Door of Unmaking takes a remembered soul and an erased one, together, and it unmakes the boundary — not just the Door but the *kind* of thing it is, the seam in the world that let gods be owned at all. Corvane wants to widen that seam and build a throne in it. You would close it. Forever. No more waking gods. No more feeding. No more power for anyone, including the two of you."

"And whoever passes through?" August asked. Their voice was very level, which Zephyrine had learned meant it was costing them enormously.

"What happens to a hand when the glove burns?" Anselm shrugged, small and terrible. "Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everything. No one has ever come back to write it down, and the Door does not keep records." A ghost of humor touched his mouth. "Unlike some professions I could name."

* * *

Night came down over the valley like a lid. Anselm gave them tea and bread and a corner of the room apiece, and then, because dying men sleep in daylight and wake at dark, he sat up by the fire and talked — not of prophecies now, but of small things. The taste of pears. A student he'd had fifty years ago who whistled off-key during silent prayer. The way snow used to fall on the seminary roof before anyone had thought to erase anything.

Zephyrine lay awake and listened, hoarding the sound of his voice the way August hoarded inventory, knowing it was finite.

Across the room, August's breathing had not changed. She could tell they weren't asleep either. She could tell the way you can tell, after weeks on shared roads, that someone's silence has a pulse.

"August," she murmured.

A pause. "Yes."

"You heard what he said. What it needs."

"I heard."

"One remembered. One forgotten." She stared at the ceiling beams, black against the ember-light. "There are exactly two of us."

"I did that arithmetic too." A longer pause. The fire ticked. "I'm not going to stand in your garden someday and watch you walk into a hole in the world, Zephyrine."

"And I'm not going to let you erase yourself because it's the closest thing to penance you know how to buy." She sat up. Across the room, August's eyes caught the embers, wide awake, guarded, familiar. "We'll find another way."

"There isn't one."

"Then we'll make one. I spent ten years praying to gods who weren't listening, August. I got very good at asking impossible things." Her voice shook and she let it. "Don't you dare decide for both of us. Not tonight. Tonight we just — stay. Both of us. Here. Alive. That's all I'm asking the night for."

Something moved through August's face that she had no word for — not surrender, not agreement, but the place where those things might eventually grow. "All right," they said softly. "Tonight."

* * *

Near dawn, Anselm woke her.

He was sitting upright on his bench with his hands folded, and he looked, suddenly, like a man who had set down an enormous weight and was surprised by the lightness. "Come here, child. Just you. Let your friend sleep — they'll need it more than either of us knows."

She knelt by him. Up close, his breath smelled of herbs and ending.

"There's a last thing," he whispered. "About the Door. About what waits on the other side of the prophecy." His fingers found hers, light as paper. "Everyone assumes the forgotten one is lost. But listen — the Door doesn't destroy what passes through. It *unwrites* it. And unwritten is not the same as ended. Ink can be lifted from a page. A garden can grow again from soil that remembers it." His grip tightened, sudden strength flooding a failing body. "When the time comes — and it will come soon, girl, days, perhaps less — whatever you give up, give it freely, and whatever you lose, lose it forward. Do you understand me? Don't mourn in advance. Mourning in advance is just fear wearing love's clothing, and it will make you grasp when you should open your hand—"

He stopped. His gaze slid past her shoulder to where August slept, curled like a comma, one hand near the satchel that held the blood-ledgers and — though neither of them had said it aloud in the hut, though it lived in the bag like a second heartbeat — the jar labeled in Corvane's neat script with a childhood twenty years sold.

"Ah," Anselm breathed. "There it is. There's the third key nobody prophesied." His eyes came back to hers, bright and urgent and fading even as they burned. "Tell them to open it, Zephyrine. Before the Door. Tell them a person cannot be unwritten properly while half of them is still locked in glass — the Door will know, it will refuse, and Corvane will win by default. Whatever's in that jar belongs to your friend, and the country cannot forget what hasn't been returned." A cough took him, deep and rattling, and when it passed he was smiling. "Open the jar. Open the grief with it. That's the toll. That's always been the toll."

"Brother Anselm—"

"Shh." He laid a finger against her lips, and his skin was cool as candle wax. "I've been listening my whole life, child. It's a mercy, at the end, to be the one who speaks."

He died an hour later, between one breath and the next, so gently that the fire didn't even flicker. Zephyrine closed his eyes and covered his face with his own oldest book, and stood a long time in the gray light while outside the valley the weeds of his garden bowed and straightened in a wind that sounded, unmistakably now, like many voices singing far below the ground.

When she turned, August was awake, watching her across the dark room with the ledgers' satchel already in their lap.

"We need to talk about the jar," Zephyrine said.
