## 11. August, Missing Years

The jar had been sitting in the bottom of August's satchel for three days, and in all that time they had not once touched it directly.

They carried it the way one carries a wound — aware of its weight, careful of the angle, unwilling to look at the thing itself. It rode against their hip through the shifting roads of Vhalen while Zephyrine sang to wayshrines and traded small true memories like coins, and every mile of it, August felt the glass breathing against them. Cool. Patient. Waiting the way only a sealed thing can wait.

It was labeled in Corvane's neat hand: *GALLOWAY, A. — ACQUISITION LOT 114 — MISCELLANEOUS CHILDHOOD, ASSORTED.*

Miscellaneous. As if a life could be sorted into lots. As if a mother's voice were a category of goods.

They made camp that night in the ribs of a drowned granary, its stone silos leaning together like conspirators, the water below them black and still as poured ink. Zephyrine fell asleep almost at once — she was spending herself recklessly on the road, feeding memories to shrines until her hands shook, and August had stopped telling her to ration herself because they both knew she wouldn't. She slept the way soldiers sleep, or penitents: fully, gratefully, with her back against the cold stone and her palms open.

August sat across the fire from her and took out the jar.

Up close, the memory inside moved like weather. Not the flat amber of most stored years — those settled into stillness within hours of bottling, going cloudy and inert, good only for trade. This one shifted. Light gathered in it and broke apart. Something green turned slowly at the center, the way leaves turn toward a window.

August's own hands were shaking. They noticed this with the detached professionalism they brought to everything — *subject exhibits tremor, pulse elevated, judgment questionable* — and found, for the first time in twenty years of ledger-keeping, that the notation gave no comfort at all.

"You're being foolish," they said aloud, quietly, so as not to wake her.

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark water, something answered — a low sound, less a voice than the memory of one, the way Vhalen answered everything now that the ledgers had burned and the country was remembering itself in fits and starts. August had heard freed memories drifting through the air for days: laughter with no owner, a name called from an empty field, the smell of bread arriving from nowhere. The land was full of ghosts that had finally been let out of their bottles, and they were everywhere, and they were not malicious. They were just glad.

August uncorked the jar.

* * *

The first fragment came up like a diver breaking surface.

— A kitchen. Not a grand one. A kitchen with a crooked window and a copper pot gone dull with use, and steam, and someone humming. The humming resolved into a voice, low and unhurried, singing half a song and abandoning it for words:

*"August. August, love, you'll burn your fingers."*

And there were small hands — *their* hands, smaller than they had any right to remember, flour-dusted, reaching — and a woman's face above them, brown-eyed, laughing, a smear of flour on her own cheekbone that she never seemed to notice and everyone else had learned not to mention. Her hair was coming loose from its braid. There was a scar on her thumb. August stared at the scar with an ache that had no name, because here was proof of a body, a whole particular body, that had held them and fed them and wiped their face, and August had spent twenty years not knowing it had ever existed.

"Mother," said the child who had been August, and the word was easy, worn smooth with use.

August, watching from the outside of their own memory, felt the word break something loose in their chest.

The fragment ended. Memory jars did that — they held only what was bottled, and whoever had harvested this one had taken moments, not continuities. The kitchen cut off mid-breath, and August sat alone by the fire again with wet eyes and no idea how long the second gap would last before the next piece surfaced.

It didn't take long. The jar was nearly full.

* * *

A garden.

Of course a garden. They had known since the burned ledgers sent them down beneath Vhalen, since Zephyrine first spoke of the Door and the place where the gods first tasted human feeling — some part of August had understood what they were walking toward. But knowing was nothing beside this.

Sun-warmed tomatoes. A fence made of odd laths painted different colors because paint had been free, someone had said, from the shipyard scrapheap. A child kneeling in dirt with worms held cupped in both hands like treasure, presenting them solemnly upward:

*"Look. Look what I found."*

And the mother's voice again, delighted rather than disgusted, which told August everything about her in a single inflection: *"Oh, well done. Put them in the compost, love, they'll work for us there."*

Work for us. A whole philosophy of living, folded into six words and offered to a five-year-old over a handful of worms. *We keep things alive. We put things back where they can grow.* August had built an entire career on the opposite principle — acquisition, extraction, cataloguing what was taken — and had prided themselves on their neutrality, their clean hands, their refusal to steal for themselves. And all along, underneath the sold-away years, there had been this: a child taught that living things belonged in gardens, not jars.

The person they might have been stood in that garden with dirt under their fingernails, and looked up through twenty intervening years, and did not accuse August at all.

That was the worst of it. August had braced for anger. They had rehearsed defenses in the long walking hours — *I didn't know, I couldn't have known, Corvane, the guild, the poverty that makes families sell* — a whole ledger of mitigating entries. But the child in the garden only smiled at the worms and then at the sun, entirely untroubled, and it was that untroubledness that accused August more thoroughly than any rage could have. The child didn't know what they'd lost. The child hadn't been stolen yet. The child simply was, in the brief window the jar preserved, complete and happy and headed toward a day when men in gray coats would come with papers and a fair price and a promise that the memory would be "kept safe."

Kept safe. In Lot 114. On a shelf. For twenty years.

* * *

Zephyrine stirred once in her sleep and murmured something — a god's name, maybe, or a prayer, or just a sound — and August pulled the blanket higher over her without thinking about it, and then thought about it, and understood that this too was evidence. That some part of them had survived the theft intact. That the hands remembered tucking even when the mind had forgotten being tucked.

The third fragment rose.

* * *

The day the guild bought them.

August had always assumed they didn't remember it because they'd been too young. Now they knew better. They remembered it perfectly; it had simply been sold out from under them, filed away, stamped and shelved, and August had walked past that shelf for two decades without a flicker of recognition.

Gray coats. A parlor that smelled of lamp oil. Their mother sitting very straight in the best chair, the one with the good cushion, her hands folded so tightly the knuckles showed white, and her voice steady — steady, steady, a voice holding itself level by main force —

*"You said kept safe. You said he could visit. You said—"*

And a guild assessor, kind in the way that only truly ruthless people manage, gentle as a surgeon: *"Madam, the arrangement is generous precisely because it is permanent. Sentimental visitation degrades the product. You understand. You signed."*

Product.

August watched their mother sign. Watched her not look at the child standing in the doorway with a wooden horse clutched to its chest. Watched her look at everything else in the room instead — the wallpaper, the lamp, the assessor's polished buttons — because looking at the child would have undone her, and she needed, desperately, to finish being strong for eleven more seconds.

She never looked. The fragment ended before she did. August would never know whether she wept after they left, whether she ran to the door, whether she lived another year or forty, whether she went on gardening, whether she put the grief somewhere it could grow or bottled it herself, privately, the way people do when no guild exists to take it from them.

There was no entry for her in any ledger August had ever written. He had catalogued ten thousand griefs and never once asked where they came from, whose door the gray coats had knocked on, what chair with a good cushion a stranger's mother had sat in while she signed away a piece of her world. August had recorded the merchandise and never the mining.

"I'm sorry," August said to the fire. To her. To the woman with the scar on her thumb, wherever the erasure of a country had scattered her grave. "I'm sorry. I counted it all and I never saw."

* * *

There was one more fragment. August could feel it turning at the bottom of the jar, denser than the rest, older-feeling though it must be later — and they understood suddenly what it was, and their hands closed around the glass hard enough to hurt.

Because there is a moment in every harvest when the harvested knows.

August had seen it, once, early in their career — an old man selling his war years, and at the edge of the extraction, a half-second where the man's eyes cleared and he looked directly at the collector and knew exactly what was happening and chose it anyway, because the hunger money would buy was worse than the loss. August had filed the moment away as technique. *Subjects experience lucidity at threshold. Do not pause. Pausing invites renegotiation.*

Now they were on the other side of the threshold, and the child in the last fragment was lucid too.

Small. Standing in a guild corridor much later — weeks later, months, old enough to have learned the building's rhythms — watching the gray-coated men carry jars, understanding with the terrible clarity of children what the jars were, pressing a face to the glass of one and seeing a stranger's birthday party swirl inside. And the child looked up — looked straight out of the memory, straight at August across twenty years and a campfire — and did not cry, and did not scream, and asked, in a small reasonable voice that destroyed August completely:

*"Did I get to be somebody good?"*

The fire cracked. The water lay still. Zephyrine breathed slow and deep three feet away, and August Galloway, keeper of the Vellum ledgers, meticulous, detached, proud of never stealing for themselves, put their head in their hands and wept.

Not gracefully. Not the single elegant tear of the redeemed. They wept like a child, ugly and gasping, twenty years of it coming up all at once — every grief they'd catalogued and never claimed, every widow's sorrow and soldier's courage they'd handled with gloves on, all of it suddenly, unmistakably, *theirs*. They had read ten thousand lives in blood and ink and felt nothing, because feeling nothing was the job, and now the job was finished and the bill had come due in a single question from a child in a corridor.

Did I get to be somebody good?

"No," August said aloud, when they could speak. "No, I got to be somebody careful. I got to be somebody useful." The words tasted of ash. "But I'm trying now. Do you hear me? Whoever you were — whatever I was supposed to be — I'm trying."

The jar sat empty between their feet. Inside it, faintly, the green light had already begun to fade, released memories dissolving back where they belonged. But for a moment — August would swear this later, to Zephyrine, who would believe them — the light gathered itself, turned, and held, warm as sun on a garden fence, warm as a hand on a floured cheek.

Then it was gone, and August was only themselves: hollowed, lighter, grieving, and somehow more real than they had been that morning.

Across the dead water, far off in the direction of the capital, the sky had begun to glow. Not dawn — it was hours yet for dawn. This was redder, and wrong, and moving, a stain spreading along the horizon like ink dropped in clear water, and beneath it August felt a vibration through the stones, deep and rhythmic, the heartbeat of something vast waking up hungry.

Zephyrine sat bolt upright, awake without transition, her eyes already fixed on the red horizon.

"That's not the gods remembering," she said. "That's something being *fed*."

August looked down at the empty jar in their hands, and then at the satchel where the blood-ledgers' ashes still dusted every fold, and understood, with the clarity of the newly broken-open, exactly what Corvane had wanted the ledgers for — and how much of them he might not have needed after all.
