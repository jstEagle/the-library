## 12. Hunters of the Vellum

*August*

The market-town of Sallow's Rest had drowned on purpose.

That was what Zephyrine told him as they waded its main street, if a street could still be called that when water came to August's thighs and the shopfronts stood open-mouthed beneath the surface, their doors swinging in a current too slow to see. The people of Vhalen, she said, had flooded this place a generation ago rather than let the guild's surveyors map it. Whole families had walked their belongings up into the hills and then opened the sluice gates behind them, drowning their own livelihoods so that no ledger could hold them.

"They thought if they couldn't be counted," she said, "they couldn't be owned."

August, who had spent thirty years counting things professionally, said nothing. The water carried a fine grey silt that had settled over everything like ash — the awnings, the scales hanging outside a grocer's, a child's wooden horse lying on its side in a doorway. Fish moved through the chapel of some forgotten saint. Their shadows crossed the stained glass above, and light fell through them into the flooded nave in pieces: red, blue, gold, broken into coins on the water.

It was beautiful. That was the thing about Vhalen that August could not reconcile — everything here was ruined, and everything ruined was beautiful, because ruin here meant refusal.

"We should not stop long," Zephyrine said. She had been listening sideways all day, he'd noticed. Her head tilted whenever the wind changed, as though the country were speaking just below the threshold of hearing and she was trying to lean into it. "Even unmapped places have edges. And the hunters—"

"I know." August shifted the pack on his shoulder. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, rode what remained of the blood-ledgers — three volumes now, the others burned on the road two nights ago, an act that still hummed in his hands like a struck bell. He had expected to feel cleansed. He felt instead like a man who had amputated something healthy to keep it from being used as a weapon. "They'll follow the ledgers' scent. Blood remembers."

"Then let's not be here when it tells them where we are."

They waded toward the far side of town, where the hills rose green and unmapped, and that was when August saw the water go still.

Not calm — *still*. The whole flooded street stopped moving at once, every ripple arrested mid-shape, and in that terrible flat mirror August saw their reflections multiply: his own and Zephyrine's, and around them four others, standing where no one stood, dark figures reflected on a surface that held no sky for them.

"Zeph," he said quietly. "Don't turn around yet."

She turned around anyway, of course. He would learn that about her over and over: she turned around.

Four men in grey oilskin coats stood on the rooftops along both sides of the street — no. Not on the rooftops. *Above* them, standing on air as easily as tile, because the Vellum Guild's hunters were trained in more than violence; they were trained in the small impossibilities that made people stop arguing. Their faces were smooth and pleasant and utterly unmemorable, faces you would forget before you finished describing them, which was precisely the point. One of them unrolled a sheet of vellum and read from it in a voice like a clerk announcing deliveries.

"August Galloway. Keeper of Ledgers, Third Chair, in breach of oath and contract. Surrender the remaining volumes and the Guild will restore your position, your pension, and your good name." A pause, courteous as a knife laid flat on a table. "Or we take the volumes and leave what we don't need of you."

"And me?" Zephyrine called up.

The hunter consulted his vellum. "You are not on the inventory."

"No," she said softly. "I didn't think I would be."

The water shivered. August felt it through his legs, a tremor rising from somewhere far below the drowned street, below the foundations, below anything with a name — and he realized, with the particular clarity that arrives only in the last moments before disaster, that the stillness hadn't been the hunters' doing at all.

Something down there had been listening. Something down there was very, very hungry.

* * *

*Zephyrine*

She had heard it for an hour before the hunters came and had said nothing, because saying it aloud made it real.

It was under the town. She could feel it the way you feel a train through the floor of a station — a vast slow attention moving beneath Sallow's Rest, circling, circling. It had been starving a long time; she could tell by the texture of its presence, thin and rasping, like breath through cracked lips. Most of Vhalen's gods had starved after the erasure, cut off from worship, from offering, from the ten thousand small prayers of a nation that had been deleted from the world. This one had stayed. It had curled around the drowned town like a hand around a coin it refused to spend, waiting.

And now four hunters stood on the air above its grave, and their violence was the first substantial meal to pass this way in decades.

*Don't,* Zephyrine told herself. *You don't know what it will cost. You don't know what it will become.*

The hunter with the vellum made a small gesture. Two of the others dropped — not fell, *dropped*, straight down like stones that had reconsidered gravity at the last moment — and landed in the water without a splash, wading toward them with knives already open. Their faces stayed pleasant. That was the horror of the guild's hunters; they never looked cruel. They looked like men doing arithmetic.

August stepped in front of her. It was absurd — he was a bookkeeper, narrow-shouldered, ink-stained, holding nothing but a satchel of contraband memory — but he stepped in front of her anyway, and something in Zephyrine's chest broke loose and went tumbling.

"No," she said. "August. Get back."

"There are better ways to settle debts—"

"*Get back.*"

Because she had already opened the door inside herself. She had spent her whole life praying — as a novice, as a priestess, as a woman whose order had been disbanded for the crime of being right — and prayer, she understood now, was not asking. Prayer was *listening with your mouth open*. And she opened it wide, and spoke downward, into the water, into the dark:

*I know you're hungry. I have nothing to give you. But those men carry violence like fat on their bones, and if you save us, it's yours. All of it. Every drop.*

For one heartbeat the world held its breath. The fish stopped swimming. The stained-glass light stopped trembling on the water.

Then the god answered *yes*, and Zephyrine learned what she had done.

It did not rise dramatically. There was no leviathan shape, no wave, no roar. The water simply darkened from beneath, as if night had a basement, and the hunger came up through the flooded street like cold coming off ice — and the two wading hunters stopped, and their knives dropped from fingers gone suddenly slack, and their eyes—

Their eyes emptied.

Not closed. Not rolled. *Emptied*, the way a house is emptied, room by room, the furniture carried out, the walls left standing. The men swayed in the water, mouths open, wearing expressions of mild and permanent surprise, and the god fed.

Zephyrine felt it feed. That was the part no one had warned her about — that she would feel it, that having invited it she was joined to it, a vein between her ribs and its vast thin mouth. It drank the hunters' violence first, and oh, it *loved* that, she felt its pleasure like heat in her own throat, the pure distilled vintage of twenty years of trained cruelty going down sweet —

and then it kept drinking.

Violence ran out fast. Violence was only the topsoil. Beneath it was everything else: the men's mothers, their childhood fevers, the names of their dogs, the taste of the food they loved, their ability to do arithmetic, their ability to want anything at all. The god did not distinguish. Hunger does not read menus. It took the cruelty and then it took the *capacity* for cruelty and then it took whatever else was nearest, because the hunters had stood above its grave carrying loaded minds, and to a starving god a mind is a mind is a mind.

"Stop," Zephyrine whispered. "*Stop.*"

It paused — a vast, wounded pause, the pause of a dog struck while eating — and she felt its attention turn on her, enormous and puzzled and faintly resentful, and in that moment of contact she saw herself as it saw her: a door. A way up. The first person in fifty years who had spoken to it kindly and therefore the first person it might follow anywhere.

*More,* it did not say, in no language, in the pressure behind her eyes. *There is more up there. There is always more up there.*

"Yes," Zephyrine said aloud, and her voice shook. "And that's exactly why you must never listen to me again."

She shut the door inside herself with every scrap of training she had ever resented, every discipline of her disbanded order, and slammed it, and barred it, and the god withdrew — slowly, grudging, circling once more beneath the town like a patient debt — and the silence it left behind was worse than the feeding.

The two emptied hunters stood in the water, breathing. Alive. Their hearts worked. Their lungs worked. They would walk if someone guided them, eat if someone fed them. Everything the god had taken was gone; everything it had not taken remained; and the sum of what remained was a body with the lights on and nobody home.

Above them, on the air, the other two hunters had gone very still. For the first time, their pleasant forgettable faces showed something memorable.

They fled. They simply left, upward and away, and the vellum with August's name fluttered down and lay flat on the water like a dead leaf.

* * *

*August*

August had watched men die before. The guild saw to that early; sentiment was bad for inventory. But he had never watched men be *unmade* while standing upright, and he found that his hands — hands that had recorded a thousand thefts in neat blood-ink without trembling — were shaking so hard the satchel rattled.

Zephyrine stood waist-deep in the flooded street with her arms wrapped around herself, staring at the two hollow men. They had begun to drift, gently, bumping against a submerged cart. One of them smiled. There was nothing behind the smile. It was just a face doing what faces do when the owner has left.

"You called it *us*," August said. His voice came out wrong. "Back on the road. You said the gods were ours, that they were made out of us." He swallowed. "Is that what we're made out of? That?"

"No." She said it fast, too fast. Then slower: "No. That's what we're *made of*, maybe — all of it, the cruelty and the dog's names together, all mixed up, inseparable. That's what the guild never understood. That's what Corvane doesn't understand." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid, and August realized none of the fear was for herself. "You can't drink the poison out of a person and leave the person. There is no poison. There's just the whole of them, and either you take the whole or you take nothing."

"The god didn't take nothing."

"The god was starving." She waded past him, toward dry ground, not looking where she was going because she couldn't look at anything. "I asked it to save us. It saved us. It answered *too well*, and it will always answer too well, that is what gods *are* — and Corvane wants to teach people to command that. To aim it." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, hollowed out. "Do you know what I felt when it fed? Pleasure. Its pleasure, in my throat, like honey, and some part of me — some part of me *liked* it, August. Some priestess-shaped part of me thought: this is what power feels like. No wonder they want it."

August reached out and took her hand. It was shaking. So was his. Together they shook, and somehow that was steadier than either of them alone.

"You stopped it," he said.

"After."

"Most people don't stop it after." He thought of Corvane's office, the gentle voice, the jars arranged like a library of selves. *He never stops. That's the whole of him.* "I've served a man who would have let it finish and asked for seconds. You heard it enjoy itself and you were horrified. Zeph — that's the entire difference. That may be the only moral distinction there is."

She looked at their hands. Then at the two drifting hunters, whom someone, someday, would have to feed and wash and lead about like children, because she had saved August Galloway's life at the price of two strangers' inner worlds, and the arithmetic of that would not close. He could see her trying to make it close. He knew better than anyone alive that some columns refuse to balance.

"It followed you," he said, because it needed saying. "When it withdrew. I felt it — it circled you. It marked you."

"I know." Her voice was very quiet. "It thinks I'm its door."

The water shivered once, far out, beyond the drowned chapel — a single ring spreading from nothing, from somewhere deep, like something vast turning over in its sleep and settling again to wait.

"It can wait," Zephyrine said, to it or to herself. "So can I."

They climbed out of Sallow's Rest on the western side, soaked to the bone, and the hills rose ahead of them green and pathless, and behind them the flooded town lay flat and silent under the afternoon sun. August did not look back at the two men drifting among the fish. He suspected he would see them anyway, every night, for the rest of his life — and he filed that away too, grimly, the keeper of ledgers even now: *one more debt that cannot be repaid, entered under losses.*

They had made the first ridge when Zephyrine stopped walking.

She stood rigid, head tilted, listening to the wind — and August watched the color leave her face as she heard whatever the country was telling her, and she said, in a voice stripped of everything but certainty:

"He's offered to teach me. Corvane. Just now — a voice out of nowhere, out of the wind itself. He says he'll show me how to command them, all of them, every god that wakes." Her hand found August's sleeve and gripped it hard. "All he wants in return is your ledgers. And August — he says he knows exactly where we're going. He says the Door has been waiting for us to bring the books to *it*."
