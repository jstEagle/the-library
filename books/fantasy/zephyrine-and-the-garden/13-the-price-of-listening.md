## 13. The Price of Listening

The god followed her out of the flooded market-town the way a stray dog follows the hand that fed it.

Zephyrine felt it before she saw it — or before she saw anything that could be *seen*, which was nothing. The god had no body to see. It was more like weather, more like a change in pressure behind her breastbone, a low hum in the marrow of her that said: *here, here, I am here.* Three days out from the market-town, wading through fields where the wheat grew gray and forgetful, she stopped on a rutted road and spoke aloud to the empty afternoon.

"You can't come with me."

The air shivered. The wheat bowed in a circle around her, though there was no wind. And then the voice — the voice she had pulled up out of starvation like a fisherman hauling a drowning thing from black water — poured itself into her ear from somewhere beneath the soil.

*You saved me.*

"I freed you," Zephyrine corrected. "There's a difference. Freed things go."

*Freed things starve,* the god said, with no bitterness at all. It said it the way a child recites a lesson learned by heart. *I was starving for nine hundred years under that town. Then you knelt down in the floodwater and gave me your fear, and your anger, and the memory of your mother's hands kneading bread, and I ate them, and for one moment I remembered what it was to be full.* A pause, long as a held breath. The whole field leaned toward her. *I do not want to be empty again.*

"Then find another way to fill yourself. That's what gods used to do — they found people who *wanted* to give. That was the whole covenant. You gave meaning; we gave feeling. Freely. Nobody chained anybody."

*Nobody chained us,* the god agreed. *Someone fed us instead. Fed us until we forgot what free hunger felt like. His name—*

"Don't." Her voice cracked out sharper than she intended. "Don't say his name. If you say his name, he can find you, and if he can find you, he can find me."

But even as she said it, she knew it was already too late, because Corvane did not need a god's name to find her. He needed only a ledger entry, and August Galloway had written her into their books weeks ago — *Zephyrine of Saint Verre, disgraced, useful* — in ink that never fully dried.

She walked on. The god came anyway.

It kept its distance, mostly, hovering at the edge of her awareness like a headache waiting to happen, but every few hours it would drift close and whisper something sweet into the hollow places of her. *I could make the roads hold still for you. I could show you where the hunters sleep. I could give you back the years your order took when they disbanded you — I know where they are stored, I have eaten beside them in the dark.* Every offer arrived gift-wrapped in her own longing, and that was how she knew it was dangerous. Real gifts did not know so precisely what shape your loneliness was.

On the fifth night she made camp in the shell of a wayshrine, one of the little roadside altars Vhalen's forgotten faithful had built before the erasure, its stone saint worn faceless by rain. She built a small fire, fed it pages torn from a hymnal she'd carried since the disbanding — the last of it now, twenty years of scripture going up in sparks that smelled like incense and guilt — and she prayed.

Not to the god that followed her. Not yet. Out of habit, the way she'd prayed in the drowned chapel of Saint Verre, the night everything changed. She prayed the old evening office, the one carved into her before she was old enough to refuse it, and halfway through the second versicle she heard the god settle down beside her fire like a traveler warming its hands, listening.

"Why?" she asked it, when the prayer was done. "Why follow me? There are thousands of people between here and the capital. Some of them must believe again. Believers taste better than doubters — that's what the old texts say."

The silence went on so long she thought it had gone. Then:

*Because you were kind to me when I was hungry, and I have not been treated kindly in a very long time. Because Corvane feeds his gods like a man fattening cattle, and I have seen what happens to the fattened ones. They stop being gods. They become tools, and tools get used up, and then they get melted down.* The firelight bent toward it, curiously, the way all light bent toward gods now that the old laws were loosening. *You did not feed me to use me. You fed me because I was suffering. I remember the flavor of that.*

"That's not a reason to follow someone across a country."

*It is the only reason anyone follows anyone,* the god said, and Zephyrine had no answer for that, because she was following August Galloway across a country for very nearly the same one.

She banked the fire and lay down with her back to the broken saint and tried to sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn, when the border between worlds wears thin as wet paper, Corvane found her.

* * *

He didn't come as a man. He came as a voice inside the voice, sliding into her dream wearing the vestments of her old abbot, and it wasn't until she was already deep in conversation with him — arguing about some trifling point of doctrine, the kind of argument she'd loved once, back when faith was a living thing and not an open wound — that the dream peeled away and she saw the dreamer, and woke inside the dream screaming at herself for being fooled twice.

"There she is," Corvane said, and the abbot's face folded away like wet vellum, and there was only him: a neat, gray, reasonable man, standing in the ruins of her sleeping mind as if he owned the deed. Around them the dream assembled Vhalen out of her memories — the drowned chapel, the shifting roads, the flooded market-town — and she understood he was reading her even now, taking notes, filing her away.

"You're trespassing," she said.

"I'm making an offer." He clasped his hands behind his back, a lecturer before a promising student. "Do you know what I admire about you, Priestess? No — don't scowl, it isn't flattery, it's inventory. You hear the gods clearly. Clearer than any vessel I've cultivated in thirty years of trying. My other listeners have to be hollowed out first — emptied like those jars in my vaults — before a god's voice fits inside them. But you're full to the brim and they speak to you anyway. Do you know why?"

"Because I listen," she said. "Not command. Listen. There's a difference."

"Yes," he said, softly. "That's exactly the difference. And that is exactly the problem."

The dream-Vhalen rippled around them. Somewhere beneath the dreamed soil, the choir of gods she'd heard in the catacombs of the capital was singing still, discordant, starving and glutted, and she felt Corvane's attention turn toward that music the way a man's attention turns toward a strongbox.

"The old covenant failed," he said. "You know this. Your order knew this, which is why they buried it. Gods and humans, trading freely — meaning for feeling, wonder for worship — it sounds beautiful, doesn't it? It was also a catastrophe. Free gods are weather, Priestess. Free gods are floods and famines and cities swallowed on a whim. Your ancestors didn't kill the gods because they hated them. They killed them because love without leash is indistinguishable from disaster."

"They didn't kill them," Zephyrine said. "They starved them and lied about it. And your guild has been feeding them stolen feelings for decades to fatten them for the yoke. I've heard what you've made of them. I've *met* one of your creations." She thought of the hunters in the flooded market-town, kneeling in the waist-deep water with their eyes gone white and empty, the god she'd called devouring their violence along with everything else that made them men. She had not slept well since. "You're not leashing the weather, Corvane. You're breeding a storm that will eat your hand the moment it learns it has teeth."

"A risk I've accounted for." He said it lightly, but she caught it — the flicker, the hairline crack of doubt — and filed it away in her turn. Even a man like Corvane was only human, and humans leaked. "Which brings me to my offer, and I'll ask you to consider it honestly, because it is the most honest offer anyone has ever made you. August Galloway carries ledgers written in their own blood. Those ledgers are the keystone of the binding — every theft catalogued, every sale signed, a complete grammar of human feeling. With them, I finish the ritual, and the gods of this world become instruments: obedient, precise, *safe*. Without them—" he spread his hands, and the dream-Vhalen spread with them, vast and crumbling and forgetting itself at the edges "—the waking continues unmanaged. Starving gods roam a dissolving country, and everyone in it, including your thief, including you, drowns slowly in the erasure. I am offering you a third road. Deliver the ledgers to me, and I will teach you what no priestess has known for a thousand years: how to *command*. Not to be a vessel. To be a voice the gods obey. You could heal them, Zephyrine. You could feed the starving ones properly, unmake the corrupted ones gently. You could rebuild your order — not the frightened little church that disowned you, but the real thing, the thing it was always supposed to be. A clergy with actual authority over heaven."

"And all it costs," Zephyrine said, "is August."

"All it costs," Corvane agreed, without shame, "is one bookkeeper."

The word hung between them. She watched him watch her, patient as erosion, and she understood suddenly that he had already run this conversation a hundred times in a hundred minds, that he knew every objection before she raised it, that he had chosen this offer precisely because it was tailored to the shape of her — duty-shaped, vow-shaped, the shape of a woman who had spent her whole life being told that her heart was a resource to be managed for the greater good.

And that was when it crystallized. Not as a decision — decisions come slow, they argue themselves into being — but as a seeing, sudden and total, the way you finally see the answer to a riddle that has tormented you for years.

Obedience was always the corruption.

Not hunger. Not the gods' terrible appetite, not their floods and famines, not even Corvane's greed. The rot had entered the world the first time someone holy said *you must* to something vast and free, the first time devotion was reorganized into compliance, the first time a priestess like her knelt and was told that kneeling was the highest thing she would ever do. The disbanding of her order, the starving of the gods, the erasure of a whole nation from every map — none of it was hunger's work. All of it was obedience's work, obedience dressed up as duty, duty dressed up as love, until nobody could tell the difference anymore and everyone went hungry together, gods and mortals alike, in a country that couldn't remember its own name.

"No," Zephyrine said.

Corvane sighed, and the dream began to dim at its edges. "You'll reconsider. When the starving ones find you — and they will, they're finding everyone now — and you feel what it costs to be listened *to* rather than obeyed—"

"You've made one error in all your accounting," she said. "You think love is a currency. You think everything is a ledger." She stepped toward him through the failing dream, and she saw — briefly, wonderfully — the gray man take half a step back. "August taught me that mistake's name. The gods taught me its price. Get out of my head, Master Corvane. You can't afford me."

The dream shattered like ice on a warm stone.

* * *

She woke with dawn coming up gray over the wayshrine and the fire dead and frost on her eyelashes, and the god was crouched at the edge of the shrine, closer than it had ever come, watching her with an attention that pressed on her skin like deep water.

"You heard all of that," she said.

*I hear everything near you now,* it admitted. *It is one of the offers you refused. I gave it anyway.*

Of course it had. Gifts without consent; that was what gods did when they'd been starved too long to remember manners. She sat up, stiff and cold, and looked at the thing that loved her badly and meant it anyway.

"He'll send hunters," she said. "If not guild hunters, then worse — bound ones, gods on chains. And he knows where we're going. He read it out of my head while I slept. The Door of Unmaking. The garden." Her hands were shaking, and she let them. "I just sold our route to the enemy."

The god was quiet. Frost melted off the faceless saint behind her, running down the worn stone like tears it had been saving for a thousand years.

*Then walk faster,* it said. *And Priestess — the one you follow, the bookkeeper. When you reach them, tell them to burn the ledgers now. Today. Before the hunters come. Blood-ledgers burn strangely; the country listens when they burn; but only if they burn freely. If Corvane takes them first, everything your thief is — everything they might have been — becomes his forever.*

"How do you know that?"

The god drew back a little, into the shadows of the ruined shrine, and for the first time since it had begun following her, it sounded afraid.

*Because I was there,* it said, *when the first ledger was written. And I have been trying to forget it ever since.*

Zephyrine stood, kicked dirt over the cold ashes, and turned east, toward the hills where August waited with a satchel full of blood and secrets. Behind her the god rose from the shrine like heat off summer stone and fell into step, and ahead of her the roads of Vhalen lay tangled and shifting in the morning mist, already beginning to rearrange themselves around the boots of men who were coming for them.

She ran.
