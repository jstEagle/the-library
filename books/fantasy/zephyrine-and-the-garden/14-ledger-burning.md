## 14. Ledger-Burning

The ledgers weighed more than paper should.

August had known this for twenty years, of course — had felt it every morning lifting them from the iron cabinet in their cell, had calibrated their shoulders to it the way other people calibrated to grief. But tonight, carrying all thirteen volumes up out of the guild archive at once, strapped against their chest with cord stolen from the binding room, they understood that the weight had never been paper at all.

It was the weight of everything the pages held. Every name sold. Every year decanted. Every childhood poured out of a screaming child and stoppered in glass while a clerk with neat hands wrote *received in good order* and moved on to the next line.

Their own neat hands.

The safehouse Zephyrine had found them was an old wayshrine on the shoulder of a hill outside Ostmere, one of those half-forgotten chapels Vhalen grew like moss. The roof had fallen in decades ago; the altar remained, and so did something in the walls that hummed faintly when you touched it, like a struck bell remembering its note. Zephyrine was asleep against the altar steps, her coat bunched under her head, her rosary wound so tightly around her fist that the beads left marks. Even sleeping she looked like someone arguing with God.

August stood in the broken doorway and watched the roads.

That was the thing about Vhalen — you could watch it lie to itself. The road below the hill unspooled toward Ostmere, and as August looked, it hesitated. There was no better word for it. The track seemed to lose conviction about where it was going, drifted ten feet west through the gorse, thought better of it, and snapped back, the way a sleeper twitches. The country couldn't remember its own roads anymore. Couldn't remember its own names. Three days ago, in a market town whose sign read only *MARKET TOWN* because no one living recalled what it had been called, August had watched an old woman stand in the square for an hour, mouthing syllables, trying to find the word for the river she'd lived beside all her life.

And August knew why. August had done the arithmetic themselves, back when the numbers were just numbers: eleven thousand entries in the blood-ledgers, each one a memory drawn out of a person of Vhalen or descended from Vhalen, because the erased country was the guild's richest quarry. You could take anything from people who had been officially subtracted from the world. Who would even notice? The maps said there was nothing there to rob.

Corvane had backup copies. August knew that too — had transcribed them personally, in ordinary ink, in Corvane's own script, years ago, before they understood what they were copying. Burning the originals would not destroy the memories. The jars still sat in the vaults below the guild, patient as seeds.

But the ledgers were the *bindings*. Written in August's blood across twenty years, each entry not merely a record but a tether — the ink-ritual that kept each stored memory owned, indexed, *held*. As long as the ledgers existed, every stolen year in the guild's vaults belonged to somebody. Burn them, and ownership dissolved. The memories would not be destroyed.

They would be *free*. Loose in the air of the country they'd been taken from, the way water freed from a broken dam is loose in a valley.

Zephyrine had said it plainly, three nights ago, over a fire of gorse roots: "You can't steal them back, August. Eleven thousand jars. You'd need a hundred years and an army."

"No," August had agreed. "But I've been the army's quartermaster my whole life. I know exactly where everything is kept. And I know what happens when a ledger burns."

They hadn't told her yet that tonight was the night. They weren't sure they were going to. Some part of August wanted to do this the way they'd done everything else in life — alone, meticulous, without witnesses to the mess they made of themselves.

Another part, a newer part, rougher, kept looking at the shape of her sleeping against the altar and understanding that this was exactly how Corvane had gotten hold of them in the first place. Alone is how you get taken.

August crossed the chapel and put one hand on her shoulder.

She came awake the way soldiers and priests come awake — instantly, silently, hand closing around the rosary like a weapon hilt. Then her eyes found August's face in the moonlight and softened, though her voice didn't. "You didn't sleep."

"I made a list instead."

"You always make lists."

"This one's short." August sat down on the step beside her. Above them, through the broken roof, the stars of Vhalen burned — unfamiliar constellations, the sky of a country that wasn't supposed to have a sky. "One item. It's been on it for twenty years and I keep writing it at the bottom and crossing it off and rewriting it."

"Tell me anyway."

"I'm going to burn the ledgers."

Zephyrine went very still. Outside, the wind moved through the gorse with a sound like turning pages. "Corvane needs those ledgers to bind the gods. You're going to hand him a reason to hunt us to the edge of the world."

"He already has that reason. He's just been merciful about it because he thought he might still persuade me." August laughed, and heard how thin it came out. "He offered me my childhood back, Zeph. Wrapped it up like a gift. All he wanted in exchange was the books."

"What did you say?"

"That's the part I'm ashamed of. I didn't say no because it was wrong. I said no because I was angry." August turned the cord of the book-bundle around their fingers, watching it bite the skin. "I've had two weeks to become a better person since then. It turns out anger converts reasonably well."

"And the memories? If you cut the tethers, where do they go?"

"Home. Wherever home still is." August looked out at the drifting road. "Some of them will land in living people — descendants, the old woman in the square who's forgotten her river. Some will just... soak into the country. Vhalen is starving, Zeph. It's been fed on nothing but erasure for a hundred years. Tonight it eats."

For a long moment Zephyrine said nothing. Then she reached out and took August's hand — not gently; firmly, the way you grab someone standing at a ledge. "You understand what you lose. The moment the bindings break, your blood isn't in those pages anymore. Your blood is in *you*, and Corvane needs it *in the pages*. If you burn them before he can take what he needs—"

"Then the ritual dies unfinished. Yes. He'll want the blood directly from the source after that." August smiled without any humor in it. "I've read enough ledgers to know what happens to assets that become inconvenient."

"Then we run. Tonight, now, before you—"

"No." And here it was, the thing August had circled for twenty years without ever once writing down: *I am tired.* "We ran through the shifting roads for six days. We traded memories to wayshrines like beggars feeding birds. And it bought us days, not safety. Corvane doesn't sleep either, Zeph. He has hunters who don't need roads. There is no version of this where we outrun him to some door at the end of the world and live quietly after. So if I'm going to be spent either way—" They lifted the bundle of ledgers into their lap, all thirteen volumes, black covers gone soft with handling, edges furred with two decades of thumbing. "—I would rather be spent on this."

Zephyrine stared at them. In the starlight her eyes were enormous and wet. "You sound like a prayer," she said finally, almost accusingly. "I used to hear prayers like that, back when the gods still answered. People who'd decided. It terrified me then too."

"I'm not terrifying. I'm a bookkeeper."

"You're a person who keeps accounts," she said. "There's a difference, and tonight it matters." She rose, brushed the dust from her coat, and held out both hands. "Give me four of them. My arms are longer."

* * *

They built the fire in the apse of the ruined chapel, where the altar stone formed a natural hearth, and August supposed — with the distant, clinical amusement that had served them all their life instead of courage — that there was a certain symmetry in burning the ledgers on an altar. Everything the guild did was liturgy with the serial numbers filed off. Corvane called the vaults reliquaries. He called the decanting rite communion. He had never once, in twenty years, called what he did theft, and August understood now that this was the whole trick: never name the thing you're doing, and no one can be accused of it.

Well. August had spent their life naming things. It was the one skill they intended to use.

Zephyrine stood back at the doorway, keeping watch on the road, though they both knew watch was useless now. What was coming would not come up the road.

"On three?" she called softly.

"There's no ritual in this," August said. "That's the point. No rite, no incantation, no three." They knelt, opened the first volume to its first page — *Entry the First: a first kiss, taken from a boy in Lower Marrow, received in good order* — and held the corner of the page into the flame of the single candle they'd allowed themselves.

Paper caught slowly. Blood-written ink resisted longer, hissing, spitting small violet sparks, and for one absurd heartbeat August thought *of course, even the ink fights* — and then the page bloomed into light and the entry was gone and something happened that August had not prepared for.

The air rang.

Not the paper. Not the fire. The *air* — the whole hollow of the ruined chapel, every broken stone, hummed like the inside of a bell, and out of the ringing came a sensation so specific August gasped: the taste of someone else's first kiss, warm and nervous and tasting faintly of apples, arriving in the room like a guest, pausing as if confused, and then pouring out through the gaps in the walls, south, toward wherever a boy from Lower Marrow had grown into an old man or a gravestone.

"Oh," Zephyrine whispered from the doorway, hand pressed flat to the doorframe. "Oh, it *works*."

August fed it the second volume. Then the third.

By the fifth, the night had come apart.

Memories rose off the burning pages the way heat rises off summer stone — invisible, but felt on the whole body — and each one passed through the chapel like a traveler, trailing its cargo. A widow's grief went through August like cold water and left them weeping without knowing whose tears they were. A soldier's courage paused beside them long enough that they felt their spine straighten with borrowed certainty. Someone's mother's voice singing — gone, south, fast, hungry for whoever had sung it first. A wedding. A funeral. A dog named something, the name arriving whole and absurd — *Barnaby* — and then gone. A hundred summers of swimming in a river that August suddenly, briefly, knew the name of: *the Lune, the river is called the Lune,* and then the knowledge tore free and flew.

Outside, the world was waking up.

August saw it between armloads of pages: the road below the hill stopped drifting. Lay still in the moonlight, sure of itself for the first time in a century, and then — this was the impossible part — began, very slightly, to *improve*, ruts filling, stones settling, as if the country remembered what the road was for. Down in Ostmere a bell began to ring, and it was not the alarm bell; it was the old pattern, the one nobody had played in living memory, rung by hands that had just remembered why. Voices rose in the town, first one, then dozens — not screams. Names. People calling out names. Windows lighting one by one like a constellation being redrawn.

"The river," Zephyrine called from the doorway, and her voice broke clean in half. "August, the river has a name again, I can hear them saying it, they're all saying it—"

"The Lune," August said, feeding the ninth volume to the fire. "It's called the Lune." Their own voice was wrecked. They found they didn't care. Twenty years of *received in good order*, twenty years of being the instrument of all this hunger, and it ended here, in a roofless chapel, and the exaltation of it was unlike anything August had ever let themselves feel — this was what the gods must taste, they thought wildly, this flood of human everything, no wonder they woke, no wonder they were starving, we have been hoarding this in the dark—

The twelfth volume burned. The thirteenth.

August held the last book a moment longer than the others. Their own blood was in these pages most densely of all — twenty years of monthly tithe, the small cuts Corvane had called *your contribution*, the ink mixed fresh each time. This book knew them. It lay heavy in their hands the way a sleeping animal lies heavy, trusting.

"Goodbye," August told it, and meant more than the book, and put it in the fire.

The thirteenth volume went up in a column of white flame that stood ten feet tall and made no smoke, and out of it burst not one memory but a storm of them — August's own tithe-work, the memories *August had personally catalogued*, hundreds of them, released in a single chord. The chapel filled to overflowing with other lives. August fell to their knees in the wash of it and let it happen, let strangers' birthdays and betrayals and first snows pour through them and out, and if some of it stuck — if they'd carry a fisherman's knowledge of tides, a girl's terror of cellars, someone's inexplicable love of the smell of tar — then that was fine. That was penance. They would live as a ledger of everyone they'd helped rob, and count it fair.

The flames sank. The last page curled, flared, went grey.

Silence, except the bell in Ostmere, still ringing its recovered pattern, and the wind, and Zephyrine crying quietly in the doorway without any attempt to hide it.

August knelt in the ash. The fire had burned down to a red eye. They were aware, distantly, of exhaustion arriving all at once, of their hands shaking, of the extraordinary lightness of their chest where thirteen books used to hang.

"It's done," they said. Their voice sounded scraped. "Eleven thousand entries. No more owned memories in Vhalen. Whatever Corvane does now, he starts from zero."

"August." Zephyrine's voice had changed. Not grief anymore. Something tighter.

August looked up.

She was staring past them, past the altar, at the eastern horizon — and following her eyes, August felt the last of the exaltation drain out of them like heat from the dead fire, because the horizon was *wrong*. Along the whole line of the hills, above the towns whose bells were ringing and whose windows were lit with remembered names, a slow red light was kindling, one point after another, orderly as lamps being lit along an avenue. And above each point the clouds were pulling apart, peeling back, opening a corridor of clear black sky that ran straight and deliberate as a drawn line —

Straight toward the chapel. Straight toward them.

Every freed memory rising off the burning books had been a beacon. Every entry cut loose had cried out its own location as it fled — and Corvane, wherever he sat, had spent the last hour reading the trail backward through the night like a man following sparks upstream to their fire.

"He knows," August said. The ash under their knees was still warm. "He knows exactly where we are."

Down in Ostmere, the bell faltered mid-pattern, and stopped.

Zephyrine stepped away from the doorway, and August watched her draw herself upright, watched her close her hand around her rosary, and understood that whatever came up that corridor of torn-open sky would be here before dawn — and that neither of them had the strength left to run.

Above the eastern hills, the first of the red lights began to move.
