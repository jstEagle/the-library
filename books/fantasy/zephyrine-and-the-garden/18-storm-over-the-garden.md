## 18. Storm Over the Garden

**August**

The first sign was the bees.

They rose from the garden's wild rows all at once, a dark ribbon unspooling into the grey morning, and Zephyrine — kneeling at the Door with her palms flat against its stone — lifted her head like a deer catching wind.

"He's coming," she said. "The god is singing."

August could not hear it. August had never been able to hear it, and there were hours now when they envied her and hours when they pitied her, and this morning they could not tell which it was. They only saw what she heard made of the world: the flowers turning their faces east before the light did, the soil shivering in fine ripples, the freed memories drifting up from the ground like pollen disturbed by a footfall.

Above Vhalen, the sky had begun to bruise.

"Then we do it now," August said, and knelt beside her at the Door.

It did not look like much. That had been the strangest discovery of the last two days — that the wound through which gods had entered the world was a doorframe of black stone standing alone among tomato vines gone feral and apple trees no one had pruned in twenty years. No wall around it. No hinges. Just an absence shaped like a doorway, and through it not darkness but a kind of *not-yet*, a place where nothing had ever been written down.

August had spent their life among ledgers. They understood, better than anyone living, how much power lived in what was never recorded.

"Say it again," Zephyrine said. "The plan. I want to hear you say it."

"You want to hear me be afraid out loud."

"Yes," she said simply. "It helps me not to be."

August looked at her — this woman who had lost her faith twice, once when her order disbanded her and once when her god ate men alive on her behalf — and said: "Corvane comes with a bound god and backup ledgers he copied from my blood. We cannot kill him. We probably cannot even hurt him. So we don't fight him with force. We fight him with everything the country remembers." They reached into the satchel between them, where the last of the freed memories swam like minnows in glass. "Every jar we broke in the ledger-burning went into Vhalen's air. The country is full of returned joy and returned grief, and it is *listening* now. We open the rest here. We give the land something to defend."

"And if it isn't enough?"

"Then I step through the Door and close it from the inside, because the forgotten can, and you hold the frame until I'm done, because the remembered must." August shrugged, and hated how light the gesture felt, how false. "You know the ending. You've known it since the hermit told us."

"I hate the ending."

"So do I," August said. "I've read a thousand endings. This is the first one I've been inside of. They're always better on the page."

Zephyrine laughed — one short, broken sound — and then the laughter died, because the bruise in the sky opened.

He came down out of it like weather given ambition. Corvane stood upon a cloud of ink-dark feathers that were not feathers, borne by the hungriest god, the one the guild had fed longest, and it wore him the way a hand wears a glove: visible beneath his skin, moving under his face, its hunger articulated through his borrowed body. Behind him descended the guild's hunters in their grey coats, dozens of them, empty-eyed, their minds long since paid over as tithes.

And behind *them*, worst of all, came her old god — Zephyrine's god, the Listener of Verre, the one whose chapel had drowned — walking on legs of stained-glass light, its face a window with someone's silhouette pressed against the other side.

"Oh," Zephyrine whispered. "*Oh.*"

"Steady," August said, and pulled the stopper from the first jar.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The memory left the bottle and became a morning.

That was the only way Zephyrine could describe it. One heartbeat the garden held only rain-heavy air and fear; the next, it held a summer kitchen somewhere, bread coming out of an oven, a woman humming off-key, a child's whole universe contained in the smell of it. The memory unfurled across the hillside like a banner, solid as anything, and when the first hunter walked into it he stopped mid-stride and began to weep, because he had been that child once, or someone had, and for a moment he remembered being loved without condition and could not lift his hand against anything in a world that contained ovens and humming.

One hunter. Stopped. Not forever — but stopped.

"Again," August breathed, and it was not triumph in their voice; it was something closer to prayer. "Zephyrine — again."

She threw the second jar herself. A funeral, this time: a widow's grief, catalogued years ago in the vaults below the guild, released now into the open air where it spread like fog and every hunter who entered it felt the exact weight of every person they had ever failed. Two fell to their knees. One turned around and walked back into the storm rather than feel it.

"It's working," August said.

"It's slowing him down," Zephyrine said, watching Corvane descend untouched through a battlefield of other people's childhoods. He passed through a memory of a wedding and did not smile. He passed through a memory of drowning and did not flinch. The hungry god carried him above it all, and she understood with a lurching horror why: *you cannot be wounded by a feeling you have eaten until there is nothing left of you to feel with.*

"The ledgers," she called. "He's got your blood in those copies. He's proof against it."

"Then we stop aiming at him." August was already digging, sleeves rolled, hauling jars from the satchel and setting them in a ring around the Door. "We aim at what he's standing on."

Zephyrine did not understand. Then she did — and she turned to face the garden, the whole wild ruined garden, and raised her voice the way she had once raised it in chapels, the way she had sworn never to raise it again.

"*Vhalen!*" she called, in the old tongue, the tongue of a country erased. "You are awake now. You remember your names. These are your dead they are marching over. These are your children's stolen mornings. *Wake up and take them back.*"

For a moment, nothing. Rain. The beat of impossible wings.

Then the ground answered.

* * *

**August**

Later — if there was a later, if any of them got a later — August would try to write down what happened, and fail, and be glad of failing, because some things deserved to stay unwritten.

The country rose.

Not an army. Nothing so organized, so obedient, so *guild-shaped*. It was more like a field after a long drought finally breaking — water everywhere at once, finding every crack. From the soil of the garden came hands, and then arms, and then figures made not of flesh but of *memory itself*: the farmer who had worked this hillside for forty years and been forgotten by everyone except the hillside. The schoolteacher whose name had been struck from rolls that no longer existed. Children. Soldiers from the war no one would name. Every soul Vhalen had ever misplaced rose out of its kept earth, translucent and luminous and utterly unafraid, and they did not march so much as *arrive*, everywhere at once, the way grief arrives, the way dawn arrives.

The hunters broke.

What do you do, when the ground you walk on stands up and knows your grandmother's maiden name? What do you do when a translucent girl of maybe nine takes your hand and asks, gently, why you gave away your courage to a man in a grey coat? August watched the guild's ranks dissolve not into battle but into *reunion* — hunters falling into the arms of remembered mothers, remembered sweethearts, remembered versions of themselves — and understood that no weapon forged in a vault could have done in an hour what a single act of honest bookkeeping had done: burn the ledgers, free the memories, let a country remember its own name.

But Corvane still descended.

He had stopped pretending to be human somewhere above the tree line. The hungry god filled him now entirely, and where he passed, the risen dead dimmed like candles in a draft. He did not fight the armies of memory. He *ate* them. Each translucent figure he touched unraveled into him, and August saw the man's face change with each theft — fuller, smoother, more terrible — a face approaching the perfection of a blank page.

"He'll reach the Door," Zephyrine shouted across the wind of it. She stood at the frame with both hands pressed to the black stone, holding it — holding it *closed*, August realized, holding it shut the way you'd hold a wound closed, and her knuckles were white as bone. "August, he'll reach it, and if he binds it instead of closing it—"

"I know."

There was one jar left. August had saved it without planning to, the way you save the last match, the last coin, the last word of an argument you intend to win. Through the glass swam a small golden fragment of light, and August knew what it was before reading the faded label, because some part of them had known since the vault, since the whisper of their own name:

*A garden. A woman's voice. A child sitting in the dirt, happier than they would ever be again.*

Their childhood. The last unbroken piece of it.

Corvane landed in the garden's heart, ten paces from the Door, and the apples fell from the trees around him as if ashamed. His voice, when it came, had harmonics in it that no throat should produce.

"Ledger-keeper." Almost fond. "You've made such a mess of my inventory."

"It was never yours," August said. "It was never inventory. That was always the lie, wasn't it? You needed us to believe people were entries so we wouldn't notice we were burying them."

"I believed it because it is *convenient*," Corvane said, and stepped forward, and the ground flinched away from his foot. "Convenience is the only truth that survives. Give me the ledgers' place in the ritual — give me the final binding — and I will let you keep the priestess. I will let you keep your little door. I am not cruel, August. I am simply *larger* than cruelty now."

Behind him, Zephyrine's god stood motionless, its stained-glass face tilted toward her hands on the stone, and August saw — no one else could have seen it, but August had spent thirty years reading small marks — that the god was *hesitating*.

August looked at the last jar. At the Door. At Zephyrine, straining, luminous with effort, holding shut the wound of the world with nothing but love and stubbornness.

They had always been proud of never stealing for themselves.

They unstoppered the jar and poured their own childhood over Corvane's head like consecrated water.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

She felt it arrive before she understood it — the memory spreading through the garden, and it was *his*, unmistakably his, and it broke her heart in the half-second she had to spare for a broken heart: a small dark-haired child in a garden almost exactly this one, dirt under their nails, a mother singing, safety like a warm coat. August's earliest happiness. The thing sold to buy their survival. Given away now, freely, as a weapon — no. Not a weapon.

As testimony.

Because Corvane, mid-stride, stepped *into* it, and for one moment the hungry god inside him met something it had never once been fed in all its centuries of stolen feeling: love that was given away rather than hoarded. Love spent like a candle spends itself. And the god — the great devouring engine of appetite that Corvane had built himself into — *stumbled*.

Only a stumble. Only a heartbeat. But in that heartbeat the risen country closed around him like a hand.

Vhalen did not strike him. That was the terrible, beautiful thing. The remembered dead simply *held* him — farmers and schoolteachers and nameless soldiers pressing in from every side, translucent arms around the man who had erased them, embracing him with the full weight of everything he had unmade — and each embrace was an accounting, each touch a line item returned, and Corvane screamed as his stolen fullness began to be *repossessed*.

"NOW," Zephyrine roared, to the god at her shoulder, to her god, to the stained-glass silhouette she had prayed to for twenty years. "Whatever you remember being — remember it NOW."

And the Listener of Verre, glutted and enslaved and hollowed, looked at the garden — at a child's memory of being loved, still hanging golden in the air — and its window-face flickered, and behind the glass the silhouette raised one hand, and it was not obedience that moved it then but *longing*, old and simple and unbought.

The god turned on its master.

The world went white.

Zephyrine held the Doorframe and did not look away, not when the light swallowed the garden, not when the hungry god's scream became two screams, not when the black stone under her palms began to warm like something waking — and then a voice beside her, August's voice, very quiet, very steady:

"It has to be me. You know it has to be me."

The light was fading. The Door stood open at last — truly open, its threshold spilling not-yet into the garden like water. And August Galloway, keeper of ledgers, thief of nothing, stood with one foot already in the unwritten, looking back at her with a smile she would spend the rest of her life trying to deserve.

"Wait for me in the garden," they said. "Plant something."

And they stepped through.
