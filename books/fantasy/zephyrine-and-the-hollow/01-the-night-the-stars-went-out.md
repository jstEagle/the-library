## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The charm was a lie, and it was also true, and Casper Ashgrove had spent three years learning to live inside that contradiction without letting it show on his face.

"Here." He pressed the little brass disc into the clerk's palm — a coin-sized thing stamped with a knotwork pattern that looked, to any inspector who might ever glance at it, like ordinary decorative filigree. "Wear it under your shirt. Don't polish it. The tarnish is part of the working."

Tarnish was not part of the working. There was barely a working at all. But the boy's hand was shaking, and his eyes kept darting toward the mouth of the alley as though the magistrate's men might materialize there in their grey coats with their grey irons, and Casper had learned long ago that what frightened people bought from a charm was never really the charm.

"You're certain it works?" The clerk — Pell, he'd said his name was; Tobin Pell, junior records-keeper, third bell of the Chancery — closed his fingers around the brass like it might fly away. "My mother swears by the shrine candles. But I thought— I mean, they say nothing real survives in Grayhallow anymore."

"They say a lot of things," Casper said. He kept his voice light, market-day bored, the voice of a man who sold trinkets and nothing better. Around them, Lanternmarket went on doing what Lanternmarket did on a fourth-bell evening: fishwives crying the last of the day's catch, a knife-grinder's wheel singing against a cleaver, children threading between the stalls with stolen plums in their fists. Lamps bloomed one by one along the colonnade, orange and steady. Above it all, the evening sky was doing what evening skies did — deepening from slate to violet, and pricking out its first stars along the eastern rim over the Belltower.

Casper did not look at those stars longer than anyone else would. He had practiced that. Three years of practice.

"It works," he told the boy. "It works the way small things work. It won't stop a carriage. It won't make your landlord kind. But when the odds are even, it tips them. When the ladder has a rotten rung, your foot finds the sound one. That's all luck ever is — the world listening for half a second."

Pell's throat moved. "And if they catch me with it?"

*Then you'll hang,* said the law of Grayhallow, written in the Magistrate's own hand on every corner post: *NO CHARM, NO SPELL, NO TOUCH OF THE OLD ART. THE CITY IS CLEAN.* Casper had read that placard so many times the letters had worn smooth in his mind, and every time he read it he felt the same small cold thing slide down his spine, because the law was right about him in every way that mattered and wrong about him in every way that counted.

"Then eat it," Casper said instead. "Swallow it whole before they reach you. Brass passes through. Evidence doesn't."

That startled a laugh out of the boy — high, cracked, but a laugh — and he paid over four coppers and a five-percent piece, which was robbery, and tucked the charm into his shirt, and Casper watched him go with the peculiar ache he always felt after these transactions. Four coppers for a placebo. Four coppers for the feeling that somewhere in the universe there was still something watching out for a nervous young man with ink-stained cuffs.

Except.

Except that the charm was not entirely a placebo, and this was the part Casper could never tell anyone, the part he barely let himself think while awake: that he *did* put something into each one. A thread of himself. A hair's breadth of the power he was supposed to be spending on other things entirely. It was such a small siphon. A drop from an ocean. He told himself that constantly, the way a drunkard tells himself the bottle on the shelf is for guests.

Because the ocean — the thing he was supposed to be guarding — sat behind a gate beneath the Hollow Hill, sealed by wards older than the city, older than the law, older than the word *law*, and Casper Ashgrove had been its keeper for three years and had done precisely nothing about it except grow very good at pretending he was only a charm-seller.

He began closing up the stall as the market thinned. This too was ritual, and he took comfort in ritual: shutters latched, trays stacked, the honest trash swept into a pile, the dishonest inventory — two luck-charms left, a fever-knot, a salt-and-iron hanger for doorways disguised as a coat hook — folded into a false bottom under the till. His hands moved through it all without needing him. His mind was on the sky.

It always was, lately. He'd taken to checking the stars the way sailors check weather, or the way a debtor checks the street for bailiffs. Every night since midwinter he'd noticed it — a faintness here and there, a star that seemed to burn at half its usual wick, as though someone far off were turning lamps down one by one. He'd told himself it was his eyes. Told himself it was smoke from the southern kilns. He was, by now, extremely well-practiced in telling himself things.

Tonight the sky looked normal. He made himself believe it. First stars out over the Belltower, bright and hard, the Sower's Fork hanging above the western hills where it had hung since before his grandmother's grandmother. Ordinary. Everything ordinary.

A woman selling roasted chestnuts was raking her coals. A dog was worrying something in the gutter outside the chandler's. Somewhere above, a nightjar made its one rasping call.

Casper latched the last shutter and straightened, and that was when the ring on his finger woke up.

* * *

It was a plain band, iron-dark, worn smooth, and most days he could almost forget it was there — almost, in the way a man can almost forget a splinter lodged against bone. It had belonged to Maren Callows, who had owned this stall before him, who had taught him knots and wards and the seventeen ways of looking harmless, and who had died in this very room three years ago with her hand clamped over his and her last breath spent on words he had done his best ever since to unhear.

*Two voices, Casper. The covenant wants two voices. She'll sing until she's hoarse, and you'll listen, do you understand me? You'll—*

He'd understood. He'd run anyway. And the ring had gone quiet on his finger for three years, cold as any dead woman's metal, and he had come to believe — had needed to believe — that whatever it had wanted from him, it had given up asking.

Now it burned.

Not hot. Cold. A cold like the heart of winter reaching up through his glove, biting down to the knuckle, and Casper hissed and grabbed his own wrist as if he could pin the thing still, and around him Lanternmarket kept laughing and haggling and raking coals, oblivious, and the ring pulsed again — once, twice — like a fist knocking on a door from the inside.

"No," Casper whispered. "No, no, don't you—"

The stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. Not hidden behind smoke. They went out the way a lamp goes out when someone snuffs it — all of them, everywhere, first-stars and late-stars and the whole spilled handful of the Milky Way, snuffed in a single instant, so completely and so suddenly that the darkness overhead seemed less like absence and more like an event, a *thing that happened*, a vast black lid pressing down onto the rim of the world.

The chestnut woman stopped raking. The dog stopped worrying its gutter-treasure. Even the knife-grinder's wheel fell silent mid-song, and in that ringing hush Casper heard the only sound that mattered: a high, crystalline note from beyond the hills, thin as a wire drawn taut — the note of the border wards, the great invisible lattice that had stood between Grayhallow and the Hollow since before there were names for either.

He had heard that note hum quietly all his life, the way you hear a bell that never stops ringing, so constant you forget it. Everyone in Grayharrow heard it; that was the city's secret, that its peace had never been peace at all but a held note, a sustained chord, magic so old and so evenly spun that the magistrates could outlaw the art and call the city clean.

The note broke.

It broke like glass breaks — one crack, then a hundred, then a shivering cascade, a sound with no proper direction, coming from everywhere at once and nowhere, and Casper felt it physically, felt it pass through the soles of his boots and up through his teeth, and he knew — with the terrible, total knowledge of a man watching his own secret come apart in public — that every ward on the border had just shattered like a thrown window, and that whatever had been held back by three centuries of woven light was now free to walk through the gap.

Around him, the market was waking to its own confusion. People were pointing upward. The chestnut woman had dropped her rake. Someone said the fog had come in strange; someone said it was an eclipse; someone laughed, uncertain, waiting for an adult explanation.

"The stars," Pell said. Casper hadn't seen the boy come back, but there he was, standing pale among the stalls with his hand pressed flat over the charm beneath his shirt. "Mister Ashgrove — the stars are *gone*. Where did they go? What is that?"

"I don't know," Casper said, and it was almost true. He knew exactly what it was. He knew it the way you know the meaning of a debt collector's knock. What he didn't know — what was flooding up through him now, cold where the ring was colder — was how bad it was, how far the shattering ran, whether it was one ward failing or all of them, whether the gate itself still held, whether—

*She'll sing until she's hoarse.*

Three years. Three years of silence from behind the stone, and Maren's voice in his memory saying *two voices* as if reciting the terms of a contract he'd signed in blood and then misplaced.

Overhead, where the stars should have been, the darkness was beginning to move.

It was very slight. He would spend the rest of his life being sure he hadn't imagined it: a slow swirl in the black, like ink stirred into ink, like water finding a drain. The city gasped as one animal. Somewhere a child started to cry. Down by the harbor a temple bell began tolling — wrong, frantic, off-rhythm, the alarm-peal that no one under forty had ever heard rung in earnest.

Casper's hands were shaking. He shoved them into his pockets and found the ring burning its winter-cold through the lining, patient now, almost smug, as if to say: *three years we've waited for you to stop pretending.*

He should go to the hill. He knew that with a clarity that felt like falling. He should go tonight, now, this hour, up through the Warrens to the old keeper's path with its buried markers only he could see, down to the gate, and kneel in the dark and find out what was left to save. That was what Maren would have done within a heartbeat of the first star dying. Maren, who had carried the post for fifty years like a soldier carries a standard, and who had died begging him not to let it fall into hiding hands.

But his feet had already turned the other way. Toward his rooms above the stall. Toward the packed bag hidden under the floorboards that he had sworn he'd never actually need — the bag with the false papers and the road-coins and the second, blanker name. Three years of keeping one foot out the door, and now the door was opening and every instinct he had honed since her death was screaming *run, hide, survive, you're nobody, you're just a charm-seller, let the city fend for itself.*

The ring bit his finger hard enough to draw blood. He felt the bead of it, warm against all that cold.

"I'm not her," he said aloud, to no one, to the ring, to the moving dark above the hills. "You chose wrong. She was the keeper. I'm just the man who watched her die."

The ring answered with silence, and the silence was worse than burning, because in it — faint, so faint he could have called it imagination, except that he had spent three years refusing to call it anything at all — Casper heard, threading up through the stones of the city, through the soles of his boots and the bones of his feet, a voice singing.

It was not Maren's voice.

It was a woman's voice, high and clear and frayed to a whisper at the edges, singing something wordless and repeating, the way you sing to keep from screaming, the way you sing into a well hoping the shape of the song will carry farther than the sound. It came from the direction of the Hollow Hill. It came from underneath the world. And it was growing thinner with every bar, like a lamp running out of oil, like a star that had already begun to go out.

Above him, the black lid of the sky churned slowly, and the first of the city's watch-bells joined the temple bell, and Lanternmarket broke into a stampede of dropped baskets and shouted prayers.

Casper Ashgrove stood alone in the emptying square with blood on his finger and a packed bag under his floorboards, and understood, finally and completely, that he had exactly two choices left, and that both of them ended everything he had been for three years.

He turned toward the hill.
