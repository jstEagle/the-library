## 2. Charms for a Lawless City

The stars were still gone when Casper Ashgrove woke on his shop floor with a bruised cheek and no memory of lying down.

He lay still for a long moment, staring at the ceiling beams of the little flat above the charm-shop, listening to the silence where night-sounds should have been. No dogs. No watchmen's boots on the cobbles below. Only a low, wrong hum, like a tuning fork struck against bone, coming from everywhere at once — from the floorboards, from his own teeth, from the direction of the river, which was also the direction of the gate.

*The wards*, said the part of his mind he had spent three years teaching to be quiet. *That's what broken wards sound like.*

He got up. He did not light a candle; there was no need. His hands knew how to find things in the dark, and besides, a faint grey luminescence was leaking up through the floor seams — ward-light, dying ward-light, bleeding out of the border-stones like marrow from a snapped bone. It pooled in the corners of his room and made his shadow lie in three directions at once.

"Right," he said aloud, because saying things aloud helped, even foolish things. Especially foolish things. "Packing, then."

* * *

Three years ago — nearly to the night — he had sat on a stool beside a bed in a room that smelled of tallow and wet stone, and held the hand of a dying man who would not stop talking.

"You'll want to write this down," Odric Vale had rasped. "No. Don't write it down. Writing gets people hanged. Listen, then. Listen with the place I'm about to show you."

And he had pressed two fingers to Casper's sternum, and something had poured through them like cold water finding its level: the shape of the Hollow Gate, hidden behind the brickwork of an abandoned customs house by the Graywater; the seven ward-stones ringing the city at the points of an old star-map; the rhythm of the nightly keeping-rite, three words in a language that predated Grayhallow's walls, spoken against the gate's iron seam at dusk.

"Why me?" Casper had asked. "Odric, I sell luck-charms to clerks. I'm not — I was never trained for anything like—"

"You were trained for exactly that." The old man had laughed, which turned into coughing, which went on long enough that Casper thought it might be the end of him right there. "Small charms for small fears. That is the whole trade, boy. A gatekeeper's work is the same work done larger. You keep the fear out. You just have to mean it."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's the qualification." Odric had turned his head on the pillow, and his eyes — milky now, going out like the last coals of a fire — had found Casper's face with terrible precision. "There should be two of you. There were always meant to be two. She'll come back for her half of it. You hold the door until she does."

"She?"

But Odric's hand had already gone slack around the covenant-ring, and when Casper finally pried it loose — the old man's fingers reluctant even in death, or perhaps especially in death — the ring had been so cold it burned, and he had heard, very faintly, from somewhere under the floor, under the foundations, under the world, a woman singing.

He had told himself, in the weeks after the funeral, that the song was grief. He had told himself a great many things.

* * *

Now Casper dragged his travel-bag from under the bed and began throwing his life into it with the efficiency of a man who had rehearsed this flight in his head every night for a thousand nights and never once believed he'd perform it.

Coat. The good coat, with the deep pockets. His papers — forged twice over, first to get him into Grayhallow as a licensed hedge-tinker, second to get him out again as nobody at all. A block of salt-wax. The little brass scale he used to weigh charm-components, because a man fleeing a city without tools was only a refugee, but a man fleeing with tools might someday be a tradesman again.

He took down the luck-charms from their hooks by twos and threes and let them fall into the bag like dead birds. They weren't really luck-charms, most of them. They were confidence-charms, courage-charms, sleep-well-tonight-charms — bits of copper and glass and knotted thread that carried nothing but suggestion and a little of Casper's own steady attention. Harmless. Legal-adjacent, in a city where the Magistrate's men drew the line between *charm* and *trinket* wherever it suited them that week. He had built a whole quiet life out of harmless. He had been proud of it, in the way a man is proud of a wall he has built between himself and a thing he refuses to look at.

Because here was the truth he packed around, heavier than everything else in the bag:

The wards had fallen tonight because they had been failing for three years, and they had been failing for three years because no one was keeping them, and no one was keeping them because Casper Ashgrove had inherited the keeping of them and had chosen, deliberately, consciously, with a coward's thoroughness, to hide instead.

Oh, he had told himself it wasn't so simple. He had told himself the rites were probably symbolic anyway, old superstition dressed up in older words. He had told himself that magic was outlawed in Grayhallow now, that the Magistrate's edicts had starved the ward-stones of public belief, that one man muttering three dead words at a sealed door couldn't matter. He had gone to the customs house exactly four times in three years — once out of duty, once out of guilt, once drunk, and once because he dreamed the gate had called his name and could not bear the uncertainty of it — and each time the bricks had looked solid, the iron seam had looked cold, and the silence had looked like safety.

Tonight the sky had gone dark in a single breath, and every star above Grayhallow had vanished as if snuffed by a thumb and forefinger, and Casper knew — had known since the moment it happened, standing in the emptying market with a clerk's coin still warm in his palm — that the silence had never been safety. It had been a held breath. And tonight something had finally let it out.

* * *

He remembered the night of the blackout differently than everyone else would remember it, and he knew that too would be a kind of evidence against him someday.

They had all been in the market square when it happened — market evenings ran late in the Lantern Quarter, lamplight and fried food and pickpockets — and when the stars went out there had been a strange collective pause, hundreds of faces tipping upward at once, the whole crowd moving like one animal scenting weather. Then the screaming started at the edges, near the river, near the old border-stones, where the ward-light had begun to bleed visibly into the air, and the crowd had become a flood, and Casper had stood in the middle of it holding a wrapped luck-charm and a handful of change and had felt, with a clarity that felt almost holy, that he had done this. Not alone. But him, among others. Him, whose job it was.

A young clerk named Pell — nervous, ink-stained, came every market week for the same charm against his mother's disapproval — had grabbed Casper's sleeve as the crowd broke around them. "Master Ashgrove, what is it? Is it the Hollow? My gran used to say if the stars ever went out it meant the Hollow had swallowed them—"

"It's a cloud layer," Casper had said. "Weather. Go home, Pell. Lock your door."

It was the smoothest lie he had ever told, because it was addressed to someone else but meant for himself, and the clerk had believed it, because people believe what lets them walk home.

Casper had not walked home. He had walked toward the river, against the whole panicking tide of the city, until he reached the customs house and pressed his palm flat against the brickwork, and felt through the mortar the gate beneath it shuddering like a struck bell that would not stop ringing. And he had stood there with three dead words in his mouth and spoken none of them, and then he had gone home and lain down on his own floor like a felled tree, because his legs had stopped taking instruction.

That had been hours ago. The hum hadn't stopped.

* * *

Bag half-packed, he caught sight of his own hand and stopped.

The covenant-ring was on his finger.

He stared at it. He had not worn it in three years — he kept it in a locked box inside a false-bottomed drawer inside a warded cupboard, triple-nested like a secret inside a shame inside a fear, and he had not opened that drawer in eleven months. Yet here it sat, snug on the third finger of his right hand, dull iron worked with a pattern he had never been able to fully trace: two interlocking circles, one whole and one broken, wound together with a line of script too small and too old to read by any light he owned.

And it was burning.

Not hot. Cold — a cold like the heart of winter, like the bottom of a well, like the space between stars, and the wrongness of that registered a half-second before the memory arrived, Odric's voice across three years of silence: *When it burns you, it means it wants you. Iron doesn't ask twice.*

"No," Casper told it. He tried to pull it off. It did not resist; it simply was no longer shaped in a way that would pass his knuckle, which was impossible, rings do not change, and yet he turned his hand in the grey leaking light and the band sat flush against the skin as though grown there, seamless, patient.

The cold pulsed. Once. Twice. In time with the hum from the river.

"All right," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, which was fitting, because everything else was cracking — wards, sky, the tidy shell of his three-year life. "All right. What do you want?"

The ring answered by showing him.

It was not a vision, quite. It was closer to remembering something that had happened to someone else: he was standing in a doorway of white stone under a black sky, and the doorway was open, and through it came a wind that smelled of nothing at all, and someone was holding the far side of the door — someone tall, someone whose hair moved like slow fire, someone whose eyes were full of a darkness that was somehow brighter than the light behind him — and she was saying, in a voice worn thin as communion paper: *"Hold your side. However long it takes. Hold your side and I will hold mine, and the door will stand between us and it, and that will have to be enough."*

Then the vision folded shut like a book, and Casper was on his knees on his own floorboards with frost flowering across the iron band and his breath smoking in a room that should have been mild, and he understood two things with perfect, ruinous certainty.

The first was that the gate had another keeper, and she was on the wrong side of it, and she had been on the wrong side of it for exactly as long as he had been hiding from his half of the bargain.

The second was that whatever had come through the broken wards tonight was looking for the ring. He didn't know how he knew. He knew the way prey knows.

Downstairs, in the shop, something touched the front door. Not knocked. *Touched* — a soft exploratory pressure against the wood, like a blind man learning a face.

Casper did not pack the rest of his things. There was no time for the rest of his things. He stuffed the ring-hand into his pocket as though modesty could hide him, hooked the bag over his shoulder, blew out the lamp he had never lit, and went out through the window above the cooper's yard, dropping into the dark of the city he had spent three years pretending was his.

Behind him, the shop door opened without a sound, and closed the same way, and from the river came the hum of breaking wards, rising now toward something that was almost — though not quite — a voice calling his mentor's name in a tone that had forgotten how names were meant to be kind.
