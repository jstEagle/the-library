## 3. What Fell Through

The first thing Casper Ashgrove did when the stars went out was nothing. That was the trick of surviving in Grayhallow — you learned to do nothing beautifully. He finished wrapping the luck-charm in its twist of blue paper, slid it across the counter to the nervous clerk who had bought it against every law the city had, and said, "Keep it under your tongue during examinations. Works best that way," while behind him, through the shop's grimy front window, the sky continued to be wrong.

The clerk didn't notice. Most people didn't; they were indoors, shuttering their shops against a night that had suddenly lost its oldest lamps, muttering about weather or war or the magistrate's new decrees. But Casper stood with his hand still outstretched from the transaction and felt the wards break the way you feel a bone snap in a distant room — not as sound but as certainty, a wrongness traveling up through the floorboards, through the soles of his boots, into the marrow where he kept everything he refused to look at.

Three years of wards. Three years of quiet maintenance performed at two in the morning, chalk and ash and whispered syllables disguised as coughing fits, all of it strung together like laundry line between the alley gate and the old bell tower. He had told himself they were strong enough. He had told himself a lot of things.

"Sir?" The clerk hovered at the door, clutching his charm. "Is it — is something happening? The stars—"

"Fog," Casper said. "Rolls in thick off the Graywater this time of year." It was a lie so practiced it came out smooth as poured cream, and the clerk nodded, grateful for any explanation that let him keep walking home, and left.

Casper turned the sign to *closed*, threw the bolt, and stood in the dark of his own shop among the harmless charms — headache-knots and traveler's knots and little braided promises that did exactly what they claimed because claiming little meant lying less — and listened to the silence where the wards should have been humming.

They weren't humming. They weren't anything. Somewhere beneath the city, behind a door he had sworn to keep, the Hollow had noticed that its cage had come apart at the seams.

* * *

He had inherited the post the way other men inherit debts, which is to say: unwillingly, at a deathbed, from a hand too weak to refuse.

Maren Ashgrove had not been his mother, whatever the shared surname suggested — she had been his teacher, then his mentor, then the nearest thing to family that a boy abandoned on the Graywater steps ever collected. And she had died badly, in the small back room above this same shop, three winters ago, gripping his wrist with a strength that had no business living in her failing body.

"The ring," she'd said. "It goes to you. Not the shop. The shop is nothing. The *ring*."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's how you know it's yours." A laugh that had become mostly cough. "There's a gate under the alley, boy, and there's a door inside the gate, and behind the door there is a dark that eats light like fire eats paper. The covenant holds it. Two voices, spoken together. Mine and—" and here her eyes had gone somewhere he couldn't follow, somewhere bright and far away, "—hers. But hers is gone now, gone these three years past, and so mine must hold twice, do you understand? Until it doesn't. Until someone comes to sing the second part again."

"Maren—"

"You'll be tempted to run." Her thumb had moved over his knuckles, over the covenant-ring already sliding on, cold as riverbed stone. "You've been running since I met you. Run if you must. But understand what you're running from, at least. Don't do what I nearly did. Don't pretend it isn't real."

She had died before dawn, and Casper had buried her, and then he had done exactly the cowardly arithmetic she'd predicted: the wards were holding. The wards had held for years without anyone singing anything. Perhaps the covenant was a story keepers told themselves to make the vigil feel holy. Perhaps the second keeper was dead, or mythical, or simply never coming, and one man with a ring and a broom could sweep the doorstep forever without ever opening the door.

So he had hidden. He had sold charms with the serial numbers filed off — nothing illegal enough to draw the wardens, nothing powerful enough to draw the magistrates — and every night he walked the alley, checked the gate, and told himself that routine was a form of duty.

Now the routine was ash, and Maren's voice in his memory sounded less like a deathbed than a prophecy.

* * *

He packed the way he'd always planned to pack, in the go-bag he kept stuffed behind the false drawer of the counter: coin wrapped in oilcloth, bread, the flint, a coil of chalk, Maren's book of keeping-rites bound in grey leather that he had not opened once in three years because opening it would mean admitting the post was real. His hands shook. He watched them shake with a kind of clinical disgust. *You knew this night would come. You packed for it. You just hoped you'd be dead first.*

The ring burned on his finger. Not hot — that was the horrible part. It burned *cold*, a white ache radiating down the bone, as though his hand had been plunged into snowmelt and left there. When he flexed his fingers the cold pulsed, rhythmic, almost like a heartbeat. Almost like knocking.

"Quiet," he told it, and felt immediately foolish, and heard in the foolishness Maren's dry voice: *Talk to it. It's only listening.* He hadn't talked to it in three years. He hadn't done anything in three years except *not*.

He killed the last lamp, eased open the back door into the alley, and stopped.

Fog had come in off the Graywater after all — real fog, thick and grey, swallowing the mouths of the alleys, muffling the distant panic of the city into a held breath. And in the middle of the alley, half-hidden by the murk, stood the gate.

Most nights it looked like nothing: an archway of old black stone wedged between a chandler's wall and a collapsed cistern, its hollow filled with rubble and rat-traffic, so ordinary that even the wardens' maps had marked it as architectural debris. Tonight the rubble was gone. Tonight the archway stood clean and empty, and where the passage behind it should have shown the far wall of the dead-end, there was instead a darkness with depth to it — a darkness that receded, that had distance, that went *somewhere*.

And along the arch's inner curve, the old covenant-script — lines he had touched a thousand times and never once seen glow — was burning a faint, starving silver.

"No," Casper said aloud, uselessly, to a stone archway, to a dead woman, to himself.

Something crossed the threshold.

It came without footsteps. The fog didn't part around it so much as *avoid* it, pulling back in ragged haloes, and what remained at the center of that avoidance was roughly the shape of a person and absolutely nothing else a person requires. It was tall. It wore the suggestion of a coat, the suggestion of long hair, the suggestion of a body the way a burnt house wears the suggestion of a home. Where a face should have been there was a smooth pale expanse, and set in that expanse, too deep, like stones at the bottom of a well, two eyes that were not eyes at all but holes — apertures into a dark that made the Hollow behind the gate look shallow.

Casper's body decided before he did. He was three steps backward, heart slamming, when the wraith spoke.

"Ashgrove."

Maren's voice. Not similar to Maren's voice — *identical*, down to the gravel of it, down to the way she had always swallowed the second syllable of his name as if saving room for more. Every hair on his arms rose. His lungs forgot their work.

"It speaks with my voice," said the thing in Maren's voice, and tilted its head with a gesture she had made a thousand times, wry and patient over some lesson he'd botched. "That's kind of it, isn't it? To give me back my voice. I paid for it, after all. I'm still paying."

"You're not her." The words came out cracked, barely air. "She's dead. I buried her. I buried her with my own hands."

"Dead," the wraith agreed, and the holes of its eyes seemed to widen, to drink the fog's dim light. "Dead is a word for the living. Here we say *taken*. Here we say *spent*. Here we say the ring was promised, and the promise is owed, and the debt walks tonight while the debtor hides behind a shop counter selling knots to clerks."

It drifted forward. Not fast. Nothing so merciful as fast. It crossed the cobblestones the way frost crosses glass, and where it passed, the moss between the stones went grey and crumbled, and a moth that had been battering itself against the chandler's lamp simply dropped, mid-flight, and did not move again.

"The ring, keeper." Maren's voice, gentle as a lesson, terrible as a verdict. "Give me the ring, and I will stop being hungry. Give me the ring, and you may go back to your little life, your little lies, your three years of sweeping a doorstep while the door rotted off its hinges. Give me the ring."

And here was the thing Casper would lie awake examining for weeks afterward: for one full breath, he considered it. Because the voice was Maren's voice, and the argument was *his* argument, the exact ledger of guilt he'd been keeping in the dark of himself — *doorstep, rotting hinges, little lies* — and what is temptation, really, but your own self-knowledge worn by something else?

His hand closed over the ring.

The cold flared — not pain exactly, but *presence*, a grip like Maren's dying hand, like the whole weight of three years of neglected vigil suddenly taking hold of him and saying *no*. And in the archway behind the wraith, the covenant-script blazed silver-white, and out of the deep dark beyond the gate came a sound that stopped both Casper and the wraith where they stood.

A hum. Low, vast, resonant — the sound the wards used to make, magnified until the cobblestones buzzed, until his teeth ached in his skull. It rose and rose, and threaded through it, thin as wire, unmistakable, was something else:

Singing. Far away and muffled, as if heard through miles of stone. A woman's voice, singing a melody he did not know and somehow recognized, the way you recognize a word in a language you can't read.

The wraith recoiled. For a heartbeat its borrowed shape shuddered, frayed at the edges, and the smooth face rippled like water disturbed — and beneath the ripple, for an instant, Casper saw what wore Maren's shape: a hollow, a hunger, a *nothing* shaped by what it wanted.

Then it gathered itself, and its voice — still Maren's, always Maren's, and he understood suddenly and sickeningly that this was deliberate cruelty, that the Hollow had reached into his grief and put her on like a glove — came again, colder:

"Another time, keeper. We are many. You are one, and even you are only half." The well-deep eyes fixed on his hand. "We will come for what is owed."

And it poured itself backward through the archway like smoke drawn up a flue, and the deep dark swallowed it, and the singing cut off mid-note so abruptly that the silence left a bruise.

* * *

Casper ran.

Not bravely, not tactically — he fled down the alley, through the chandler's yard, over a fence he'd climbed since boyhood, down the wet steps into the undercity where the old aqueduct district had been condemned and colonized decades ago by everyone Grayhallow wanted to forget. His lungs burned. His legs shook. Twice he slipped on the slick stone and caught himself on walls furred with moss, and the whole time the ring burned colder and colder on his hand until it felt less like an object than a compass needle jammed into his flesh, dragging, insisting, *down, down, toward the gate, toward the dark, toward the debt.*

In the mouth of a culvert, under the drip of old drainage, he finally stopped and bent double and breathed.

The undercity glimmered around him — smugglers' lanterns hooded in colored glass, the murmur of the night market that existed below the law, the smell of tallow and wet stone and frying onions from some cart that never asked names. Above, through grates and shafts, he could hear the city proper losing its mind: bells, whistles, the magistrate's criers announcing emergency curfew. By morning there would be proclamations. There would be scapegoats. Charm-sellers, probably. People like him.

But that was tomorrow's terror, and it could wait its turn. Tonight's terror stood in the alley wearing his dead mentor's voice, and it had called him *keeper*, and it had called him *half*.

Half. He turned the word over, turning the ring instead, and the singing came back to him — that far voice through stone, cut off mid-note. *Mine and hers,* Maren had said, eyes gone somewhere bright. *Two voices, spoken together.*

"There's someone in there," he said to the dripping dark of the culvert, to no one, to the ring. Someone sealed inside the Hollow when the wards fell — or three years ago, or longer — someone who had been singing into the dark while he sold luck-charms and lied to clerks and called his cowardice a life. Someone the wraith had feared.

He unslung the go-bag, sat down hard on the wet stone, and pulled out Maren's book of keeping-rites. The grey leather was cold as the ring. He had not opened it in three years, and his hands remembered why: because opening it was an admission, and admissions had consequences, and consequences were the one commodity Casper Ashgrove had spent his whole existence refusing to stock.

He opened it anyway.

The first page, in Maren's blunt upright hand, read: *To whoever inherits this: I am sorry. Read it twice. Then go to the gate, and listen — because if the wards are down, she will be calling for you, and she has been waiting longer than you could bear to imagine.*

Somewhere above him, the bells began tolling curfew, and the city that outlawed magic started hunting people like him, and Casper Ashgrove, keeper, coward, heir to a debt three years overdue, began — at last, too late, hands shaking — to read.
