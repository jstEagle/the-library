## 4. The Magistrate's Lantern

Corvane Hale had always believed that fear was a currency like any other, and like any currency, it rewarded the man who kept his ledger clean.

He stood at the tall window of the Magistracy's upper chamber and watched Grayhallow come apart beneath a starless sky. Below, the great square seethed with torchlight and shouting, a river of frightened humanity pressing against the iron gates of the compound. Somewhere in the crush a woman had fainted; he could see the crowd passing her overhead like a boat over waves. Somewhere else, no doubt, charm-sellers were already slinking back to their cellars, their illegal little flames guttering in their illegal little hands, wondering if the darkness was their fault.

It was not. It was his.

"Magistrate." His secretary, Pellam, hovered at the door with a sheaf of papers and the pinched expression of a man who had been awake too long. "The wardens report disturbances in all nine districts. The border wards are—well. There is no polite word for what has happened to them."

"Gone," Hale said pleasantly. "The word is *gone*."

"Yes, sir. And the sky—people are saying the sky—"

"People will say anything. It is the one liberty I have never needed to take from them." He turned from the window, adjusting the fall of his grey coat. Grey, tonight, deliberately: grey for gravity, grey for steadiness, grey as the ash of every fire he intended to put out. "Have the criers ready my address. The steps, not the balcony. A magistrate on a balcony looks like a king. A magistrate on the steps looks like a neighbor."

Pellam scribbled. The man was an excellent secretary precisely because he never asked questions, which was another way of saying he had no imagination whatsoever, which was another way of saying Hale trusted him completely.

"One more thing, sir." Pellam hesitated. "The old quarter. The Ashgrove district. The wardens say there was a light there, just before the blackout. A greenish light. Like—" he consulted his notes, though Hale suspected he knew the words by heart— "like glass breaking upward."

Hale kept his face arranged in its customary mildness, the face he wore to dinners and executions alike. Inside, something cold and precise took note, the way a jeweler takes note of a flaw in a stone he had believed perfect.

"Charm-sellers," he said. "There are always charm-sellers in Ashgrove. When the wards fell, their little frauds went up like sparks off a grindstone. Nothing more."

"You're probably right, sir."

"I am certainly right, Pellam. That is why you work for me." He smiled to soften it, and Pellam, reassured by the smile as he had been reassured by three years of identical smiles, bowed and went out.

Alone again, Hale let the pleasant mask drop—not into anger, nothing so wasteful, but into the quiet attention of a man alone with his life's work.

Three years. Three years of petitions to the Conclave for stronger wards, each one denied. Three years of quietly reassigning every warden sympathetic to the old ways, of commissioning studies that proved magic caused the plagues and studies proving it caused the droughts, whichever drought or plague happened to be fashionable. Three years of learning the border-ward lattice better than its own dead architects had, because a man cannot steal a house until he knows where every beam is laid.

And three years, most importantly, of the lantern.

It sat on his desk where a lesser man would have kept a candlestick: a sphere of dark glass banded in silver, no larger than a melon, utterly unremarkable except that it drank light the way sand drinks rain. He had commissioned its shell from a glasswright who now believed he had made a decorative globe for a magistrate's study. The heart of it—the appetite—he had built himself, from diagrams torn out of books that no longer existed anywhere but in his memory.

Hale lifted the lantern and felt it pull gently at him, the way deep water pulls. Empty still, mostly. Hungry. Patient. He had designed it patient, because patience was the whole of the plan.

Tonight, at last, it would be fed.

* * *

The steps of the Magistracy were broad and worn hollow in the middle by two centuries of boots, and Hale stood at the top of them in the flat blackness where stars should have been and let the crowd see him waiting. That was the first trick of governing the frightened: never rush. Rushing admitted there was something to run from. He let them shout themselves hoarse. He let a warden in half-armor take up position beside him, visible, unhurried, hands folded on the halberd like a farmer leaning on a fence.

Then he raised one hand, and the square went quiet the way water goes still when you stop stirring it.

"Citizens of Grayhallow." His voice carried without effort; he had trained it years ago against the day it would need to carry this far. "You have looked up tonight and found the heavens dark, and some of you are afraid, and others of you are ashamed to be afraid, and are therefore more afraid still. I know. I have looked up too."

He paused. Let them murmur. The murmurs sounded like his name.

"Here is the truth, which your magistrates have not always given you, and for which failure I apologize on behalf of my office." Truth, rationed, was the sharpest tool in the kit. "The lights above us were never lamps of heaven. They were the far end of the border wards—the old covenant-work, older than this city, older than the law that outlaws sorcery. Every charm sold in a cellar, every hex whispered over a cradle, drew its breath through those wards, thinning them a thread at a time. Tonight they failed. Not because the heavens abandoned us. Because we spent them."

A sound moved through the crowd like wind through wheat—half gasp, half snarl. Hale let it move. Anger aimed at magicians was anger not aimed at him.

"The law of Grayhallow has forbidden magic for sixty years," he continued, "and I have enforced that law faithfully, and tonight I tell you plainly: enforcement was not enough. A weed cut down but not burned grows back. There remain among us those who deal in charms, who keep charms, who *are* charms—and every one of them helped darken your sky."

Now the wheat-wind became a low sustained growl, and Hale watched faces by torchlight and saw the transformation he wanted: fear curdling into purpose. Frightened men look for someone to blame; give them a name and they will build you a wall out of him.

"So hear my promise. Until the wards are mended and the stars restored, martial order holds in Grayhallow. Curfew at dusk. Wardens in every district, with authority to search premises and seize contraband. Anyone harboring a practitioner, anyone concealing a charm, anyone who has ever *bought* a luck-token or a fever-rune—you will come forward. You will not be punished for honesty. Honesty is the first ward we will rebuild together." He spread his hands, palms open, a man offering his neighbors his empty gloves. "We did not lose the stars in a night, and we will not win them back in one. But we will win them back. I give you my word, and you know me: I do not spend words I cannot pay for."

He remained on the steps afterward, letting them surge close, shaking grimy hands, learning names—a tanner, a widow, a boy with a broken arm—committing each face to memory, because a ruler who remembers faces spends less gold buying loyalty later. By the time the crowd dispersed to spread his sermon through every alley, his voice had gone slightly rough, and he found, examining himself as he climbed the stairs, that he felt almost warm. It was the nearest thing to pleasure his profession allowed.

Then he closed the study door, and let the warmth go out of him like a lamp being capped.

* * *

The harvesting had begun within minutes of the collapse, and it was going beautifully.

Hale worked at the window with the lantern open on its silver stand, its dark mouth turned toward the ruins of the border wards a mile east. To the naked eye the fallen wards were nothing—night air, a few confused birds. But he had spent three years and a considerable fortune teaching himself to see what the ward-lattice looked like when it broke, and it looked like this: rivers. Shattered light running along channels that had carried it for centuries, spilling outward, dispersing, wasted—unless gathered.

The lantern hummed against his palms. Threads of pale radiance came crawling across the rooftops toward it, faint as spider silk, and where they touched the glass they vanished inside, and the sphere's cold weight grew fractionally heavier in his hands. Ward-magic. Covenant-stuff. The same substance the old texts called *the keeping-light*, the same force that had sealed the Hollow Gate and hung the stars on it like fruit on a branch. Loose now. Ownerless.

Every drop of it belonged, by right of chaos, to whoever arrived first with a bucket.

He thought, not for the first time, about how tidy it was. That was what he admired most in the arrangement, more than the power itself: the elegance. The city had outlawed magic because magic frightened it, and in outlawing magic had left the machinery of magic untended, and untended machines fail, and when this one failed it delivered everything it held into the hands of the one man who had studied it, prepared for it, and kept his ledger clean. No crime in it anywhere, if one squinted. The wards had simply fallen. The magistrate had simply responded. If he gathered what fell, was that theft, or thrift?

Of course, he had not left it entirely to chance. The final threads of the lattice had not frayed on their own; he had cut them, three nights of delicate severing-work disguised as inspection tours. And the timing had required assistance, which was why a certain clerk in the Wards Registry had recently received a promotion he had neither sought nor understood, and why certain maintenance records had gone into the furnace. Tidiness required housekeeping. Housekeeping occasionally required mess elsewhere.

The lantern brightened—or rather, darkened, filling with a light so dense it swallowed the room's candles. Hale watched a strand of ward-light slide across the floor toward it like a ribbon drawn through water and thought, with the calm of a man checking figures that balance: enough. Nearly enough already. The full harvest would take days yet, the lattice bleeding out slowly across the whole border, and he must gather nearly all of it, because there was one loose end the tidy plan could not yet account for.

Two keepers. The old covenant demanded two. He had accounted for the gate itself, for the ring, for the ritual calendar, for everything—everything except the question that nagged at him now, watching the light pour into his private sky: *where had the second keeper gone?*

The records were maddeningly incomplete. Three years ago, when the last keeper died and the young man Ashgrove inherited the post and then, conspicuously, did nothing with it, the registry had shown a second signature on the original covenant—one that simply ceased. Vanished the same night the gate sealed. Either the second keeper was dead, in which case Hale's new covenant, once he forged it from harvested light, would bind the gate to him alone, or—

Or the second keeper was inside.

Hale did not believe in fairy stories, but he believed in evidence, and the evidence included a gate that had taken three years' worth of keeping-light to crack, and a silence behind it that his instruments—his lantern, held near the stone on two occasions he had arranged to inspect the wall—had registered as *consumption*. Something beyond the gate was drinking magic. Slowly. Steadily. Like a prisoner drinking from a cup slid under a door.

If it was a woman, and if the songs were true about what she was, then she was the single largest reserve of covenant-power in existence, sealed in a vault whose lock he now possessed. The lantern would open the door. What stood behind it would be dealt with as circumstances advised—recruited, bound, or rendered down. All three options ended the same way: Corvane Hale, keeper of the only working magic in two worlds, standing between a terrified city and the dark, forever. They would make it official within the year. They would beg him to.

He closed the lantern's mouth and locked it in the iron cabinet behind the false back of his wardrobe, and stood a moment in the sudden ordinary darkness listening to the distant, satisfying sound of a city organizing itself into his hands.

One matter remained before bed. He crossed to the desk, drew a sheet of paper, and wrote in a small exact hand:

*Pellam — the Ashgrove light troubles me. Greenish. Upward. Find me everything about the Ashgrove district: property rolls, the late keeper's effects, the heir. Especially the heir. Casper Ashgrove sells charms, I am told. Charming little trinkets. Begin there, and be gentle — a man who has hidden for three years is a man who frightens easily, and frightened men talk. Also: put quiet feelers out among the smuggling folk. Gates have keys, keys have owners, and owners can be bought.*

He signed it, sanded it, and set it on the tray for morning delivery. Outside, Grayhallow lay under its first starless night in living memory, and somewhere out in that darkness the fallen light of an ancient covenant crept street by street toward his cabinet, and somewhere in Ashgrove—though he could not have said how he knew, only that three years of studying the lattice gave a man instincts he had learned to trust like his own pulse—somewhere in Ashgrove a frightened young man was almost certainly packing to run.

Hale blew out his candle.

Run, then, Master Ashgrove, he thought, into the friendly dark. Everything that runs leaves tracks, and everything that hides has a hole, and I have the best hounds in the world: ten thousand citizens, newly promised their stars back, all of them looking for someone to blame.

He slept extremely well.
