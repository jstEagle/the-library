## 5. A Thief Named Wren

Wren Vasska had three rules, and she'd written them herself, which made them law.

One: never sell a charm you haven't tested on yourself. Two: never sleep in the same room as your stock. Three: never — *never* — let anyone learn where the cache is.

So when she came up the crooked stair to her loft above the dye-works, carrying a paper of fried eel and half a mind to count her inventory before dawn, and heard the soft, deliberate scrape of someone going through her things — she stopped on the landing, set down the eel very quietly, and drew the knife from her sleeve.

Not that the intruder was being loud. That was the insult of it. Whoever was in her loft knew what they were doing. They'd bypassed the tripwire on the window, ignored the loose board with the false stash of cheap love-philtres — the decoy she kept for exactly this occasion — and gone straight for the back wall, where the plaster hid the iron box. The gate-keys. Her grandmother's keys, or the ones Wren had managed to buy back from collectors and fools over eleven years of careful, patient thieving. She counted nine of them in her head the way other people said prayers.

She eased the door open with two fingers.

The loft was lit by one shuttered lamp, turned low, and by the gray bleed of light from the sign across the street — *MAGICK IS THEFT FROM YOUR NEIGHBOR: REPORT IT*, Magistrate Hale's new slogan, painted in letters so large they could be read from Bellwether Bridge. In that stingy light stood a man with his back to her, tall, narrow through the shoulders, wearing a coat that had been good once and was now only honest. He had the iron box open on his knee and was lifting the keys out one at a time, holding each up to the light like a jeweler, and his hands were shaking.

Wren took stock of him the way she took stock of everything: coat, worn at the cuffs but clean — not desperate, then, or desperate enough to be tidy about it. Boots resoled twice. No visible weapon, which meant either none or a very good one. And the way he handled the keys — reverent, almost frightened — told her he wasn't here to steal them. He was here because he needed them, and needed them badly, and men who needed things badly were either the most dangerous kind or the most useful.

He picked up the ninth key, the oldest, black iron shaped like a bird's wing, and made a sound low in his throat. Not surprise. Recognition.

"All right," Wren said, leaning against the doorframe with her knife loose and friendly at her side. "I'll bite. What does a man want with dead women's keys?"

He spun, and the box nearly went over, and he caught it — caught it first, before anything else, both hands cupping it against his chest like a falling child. That decided something in her before he'd said a word.

"I wasn't—" He stopped. Started again. "You're the smuggler. Wren. The charm-dealer."

"And you're the man currently committing two felonies in my house. Burglary and possession." She nodded at the keys. "That's the worse one, if you're wondering. Keys are just iron. But a person who knows what they open?" She let him see her smile, thin as wire. "That person is guilty of believing."

* * *

His name was Casper Ashgrove, and he gave it to her too quickly, which told her either that he was a terrible liar or that he wanted her to know it — wanted there to be a name attached, in case things went wrong, so someone would come asking after him. Wren had met that kind before. They were rare and they were usually trouble, but they were also usually true.

"Sit," she said, and pointed him to the stool by the cold stove while she sat on the table where she could watch the door, the window, and him, in descending order of concern. She fished the eel out from the landing and put the paper between them without offering it. He looked at it longingly anyway, and she revised her assessment downward again. A dangerous man would have eaten it without asking.

"You broke the wards," she said.

He flinched as though she'd struck him. "No."

"Someone did. Three nights ago every star over Grayhallow went out like snuffed candles, and the border wards shattered, and since then the Watch has been sweeping the low quarters arresting anyone who ever sold so much as a wart-charm, and you come creeping to me — to *me*, Ashgrove, of all the fences in this city — hunting gate-keys." She leaned forward. "So I'll ask again, politely, with a knife in view. Did you break the wards?"

"No." His voice cracked on it, and she watched his hand go, without his seeming to notice, to the ring on his right hand — an old band of dark metal she hadn't clocked until now, because he wore it turned inward, stone against his palm. "The wards broke *themselves*. Or—" he hesitated, and she saw him decide, visibly, painfully, to tell her something true. "Or they were broken. There's a magistrate who's been harvesting what fell. Corvane Hale. He has a lantern, and the lantern is drinking—"

"Corvane Hale." Wren laughed, one short, ugly note. "The Magistrate. The man whose face is on the proclamation outside."

"The same."

"The same man who promised protection in Bell Square last night while his Watch chained charm-sellers to a cart like fish." She shook her head slowly. It fit. She hated how well it fit — all those years of Hale tightening the laws, not eradicating magic but *funneling* it, always toward himself, always toward the tower. "Say I believe you. Say the lantern's real. Why come to me? There are other smugglers."

"Not with these." He opened the iron box on his knees and tilted it toward her, and the lamplight slid over nine old keys, and Wren felt something twist in her chest that she had spent eleven years convincing herself she didn't have.

"Where did you get that key," she said quietly. Not a question. Her eyes had gone straight past all nine of his prizes to his hand, to the ring turned inward, and she reached out — didn't touch, never touched, that was rule four, unspoken but absolute — and gestured. "Give us your hand."

He hesitated. Then he held it out, palm up, and turned the ring around, and there it was: a stone like a chip of midnight with a star caught inside it, set in dark silver gone soft with centuries of wear.

Wren had seen that ring exactly once, when she was seven years old, on the hand of a woman named Serafime Vasska who came to their kitchen door smelling of rain and woodsmoke, who gripped her grandmother's forearms and spoke to her in a language neither Wren nor her mother understood, and who left before morning and died — so Grandmother said, years later, in the one story she ever told drunk — *holding a door shut with her whole self*. Grandmother had cried that night, the only time Wren ever saw it, and had taken the copper charm from her own neck — a little wheel-and-star that Wren still carried sewn into her sleeve — and had said: *We were keepers once, on my mother's side. We owe a debt to whoever wears that ring. If you ever see it, girl, you pay it.*

Wren was thirty-one years old and had told no living soul that story.

"That's a covenant-ring," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like a road after frost. "My grandmother used to say that word. Covenant. Like it was capital-C."

"Your grandmother." Casper's head came up sharply. "Who? When?"

"Serafime Vasska. Keeper, on the mother's side, whatever that means. Came to our door the night before the last warding, must be — what, sixty years ago? She and my grandmother sat up all night talking in that language, and in the morning Serafime walked into the hills and never came back." Wren found she was gripping her own wrist hard enough to bruise. "Grandmother said the ring eats its wearer. Said the woman who wore it gave everything she had to hold the door. And she made me swear, if I ever met the next fool fool enough to wear it, I'd help them, because we owed a debt that doesn't die with the family line." She bared her teeth at him, which was the closest she could get, just then, to crying. "I thought it was a story. I sold charms off that story for years. *Worn by the keepers of old,* I'd say, and charge double."

To his credit — and Wren was already learning, reluctantly, to keep score of his credits — Casper Ashgrove didn't laugh, and didn't pity her. He just looked at her with those hollowed-out, sleepless eyes and said, "It's not a story. I'm sorry. I wish it were. I've been telling myself it was for three years."

"Three years?"

"Since my mentor died and left me the post. Since I chose to pretend I hadn't." He closed his fist around the ring. "There's more. There's a woman inside the Hollow — a second keeper, sealed behind the gate — and I think the wards breaking trapped her there, and I think Hale's lantern is starving her, and I don't know how to do any of this alone."

The lamp guttered. Out in the street a Watch patrol went by, boots ringing on cobbles, and both of them went silent and still the way only outlaws can — two creatures of the same hunted species, recognizing each other's stillness. When the boots faded, Wren exhaled and got up and fetched the bottle of plum brandy from behind the loose brick, and poured two cups, because some conversations required it and this was going to be one.

"Here's how this works," she said. "Rule five, newly minted. You get the keys. All nine, including the winged one, which I paid a fortune to a collector in Saltmarket and which opens, if my grandmother's stories are worth anything, the under-gate — the one they built the city on top of and hoped everyone would forget. You get the keys because you're the keeper and because I owe a debt older than the magistracy." She pushed a cup at him. "But you don't get them free, and you don't get them alone. Wherever that gate is, whatever's behind it, the Watch will be between here and there by tomorrow night, because Hale's sweeps are getting wider and dumber and greedier, and a man with your face walking through Bell Quarter carrying iron will be arrested before noon. You need someone who knows the undercity. Someone who knows which cellars connect, which walls are bribable, which Watch captains take coin and which take blackmail."

"And what do you get?"

Wren sipped her brandy and considered lying, and decided against it, mostly because she was suddenly very tired of lies, even profitable ones.

"My brother Tammi sells luck-knots on Chandler's Row. He's got no talent, none, he just ties pretty knots and swears they work, and last week a Watchman bought one as a joke and now there's a warrant with his description on it. If Hale's lantern needs feeding, he'll feed it with people like Tammi eventually — people who traffic in belief. So I help you reach your gate and your starving keeper and whatever else is true in that mad story of yours, and maybe along the way we find out what the Magistrate is really doing, and somebody puts a stop to it." She raised the cup. "Shared illegal inheritance. My grandmother would've called it fate. I call it leverage."

Casper drank. His hand had stopped shaking, she noticed, for the first time since she'd come up the stairs.

"There's one thing you should know," he said, setting the cup down. "Before you commit to this. The thing behind the gate — the Hollow — it isn't empty. Something crossed the broken wards two nights ago. It wore my mentor's voice and it wanted the ring, and it wasn't a ghost, Wren. Ghosts don't make the air go cold in a moving line, like something dragging winter behind it." His jaw worked. "If we open the under-gate, we might be opening it to more than one woman."

"Then we'd better be quick," Wren said, "and better armed." She stood, crossed to the back wall, and pressed the brick behind the stove in the pattern her grandmother had taught her before she could read. The wall sighed open on oiled hinges, revealing not just the iron box's niche but the long shelf beneath it: coils of silk cord, vials of greyfire, a short hooked blade, and a folded map drawn on dyers' linen, the undercity rendered in water-stained ink — every tunnel, every sealed well, every door that officially did not exist.

She took down the map. She took down the blade. Then she paused, went to the little cedar chest on the sill where she kept the things that weren't for sale, and unwrapped the copper charm — the wheel-and-star, green with age, warm as a held hand.

"Grandmother's," she said, and hung it around her own neck, tucking it beneath her shirt. "Debt starts accruing interest tonight."

Casper watched her, and something in his face shifted — the look of a man who has been carrying a coffin alone for a very long time and has just been told, tentatively, unbelievingly, that there are other shoulders available.

"Why?" he asked simply. "Really. You could walk away. Turn me in. There's a reward for covenant artifacts; Hale's posted it himself. Enough to buy Tammi out of any trouble ten times over."

"Aye, and enough to buy a lot of silence, too." Wren rolled the map, tied it, slung it across her back. "But my grandmother held a dying woman's hands once and promised her family's name to a door she'd never see. Vasskas pay what they owe. Besides—" she jerked her chin at the window, at Hale's vast painted slogan glowing over the drowned street, *MAGICK IS THEFT FROM YOUR NEIGHBOR* — "any man who needs slogans that big is stealing something. I want to know what."

Then, from the street below, came the sound that emptied her blood: three sharp whistles, the dye-works yard gate banging, and a Watch voice calling up, bored and terrible: *"Light in the upper window. Occupant will open. Sweep order, Bell Quarter annex."*

They looked at each other in the guttering lamplight — the keeper and the thief, strangers bound by a dead woman's promise — and Wren was already moving, kicking the brandy under the stove, sliding the keys into Casper's coat, pulling up the floor hatch to the dye-vats below.

"Under-gate's through the old culvert under Tanner's Run," she said. "Stay behind me, touch nothing, and if we're separated, follow the smell of madder-red to the third arch and wait."

"Wren—"

"And *that*," she said, shoving him toward the hatch as the pounding started on the stair, "is rule six: nobody follows the rules until the rules try to kill them. Go."

Below them, the vats breathed their vinegar-dark breath, and beyond the walls of the loft the city of Grayhallow lay starless and waking, full of locked doors and listening ears — and somewhere beneath it all, under streets and cellars and sixty years of forgetting, a black iron wing of a key waited in Casper Ashgrove's pocket to turn.
