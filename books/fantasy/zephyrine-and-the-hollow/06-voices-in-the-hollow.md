## 6. Voices in the Hollow

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and Casper had learned in three days to love it the way one loves a blanket pulled over one's head — not because it was beautiful, but because it hid him.

Wren's cache was a cellar beneath a shuttered dye-house, reachable only through a door that pretended to be a wall. She had given him a corner, a cot that bowed in the middle like a tired back, and rules delivered in the flat voice of someone who had buried her generosity under several layers of pragmatism: don't touch the shelves, don't open the far door, don't go out after the third bell.

He had broken the last rule twice already.

Tonight would be a third time, but not until the city slept. He sat on the cot with the covenant-ring in his palm and watched it breathe. There was no better word for it. The band of dark silver held a hairline seam of light along its inner curve, and that light swelled and dimmed in a rhythm too slow for a pulse, too patient for a heartbeat — something between a tide and a long-held breath. His mentor's ring. His mentor's burden, handed over in a deathbed room that smelled of camphor and lilies while old Maren Ashgrove — no relation by blood, though she'd claimed him with the fierce grammar of chosen family — pressed it into his hand and said, *Keep it lit, lad. You won't be keeping it alone.*

He hadn't understood that then. He had understood even less when he'd run.

The ring warmed slightly as he thought of her, and he closed his fist around it as if he could smother the feeling. Guilt was an old lodger in him now; it paid no rent and asked no questions, but it kept strange hours, and lately it had been pacing.

*You won't be keeping it alone.*

Three years ago he had taken that as comfort. Tonight, crouched in a smuggler's cellar with wraith-terror still crusted to the backs of his eyes, he took it as an accusation.

* * *

He went up through the coal-chute at midnight, when Grayhallow above wore its curfew like a muzzle. The city had changed since the stars went out. It wasn't just the dark — Casper knew dark, had lived companionably with it his whole hidden life. This was different. This was a dark with weight, pressing down through the streets, pooling in doorways. The constables had hung lanterns on every corner, fat yellow things that buzzed and guttered, and beneath each one stood a man with a truncheon and a list. Hale's men. They stopped anyone whose hands shook wrong, whose shadow fell at an odd angle, who flinched at the word *magic* spoken loudly by a stranger.

Casper moved through it all with his collar up and his charm-seller's calluses hidden inside his gloves, and told himself the route to the Hollow Gate was reconnaissance, caution, prudence. He was lying and knew it. He was being pulled. The ring had been pulling him since the wards broke — a slow, tidal lean toward the north of the city, toward the hill where the gate slept behind its mask of ruin, and every night the pull got less deniable, like a hand closing finger by finger around his wrist.

The last stretch was a stair of broken steps climbing through bramble and collapsed masonry, and at the top of it the world simply stopped pretending.

The Hollow Gate did not look like a gate. That had always been its genius, or its cruelty. To the casual eye it was a sinkhole ringed with ancient stone, a collapsed cellar in a ruined district the city had written off generations ago. But Casper had been raised on the truth of it: a doorway worn into the boundary between realms, older than Grayhallow, older than the laws against magic, sealed by a covenant that took two voices to speak and three years of silence to crack.

He knelt at the rim and looked down into the dark, and the dark looked back with something almost like attention.

The wards were gone. He could feel their absence the way you feel a missing tooth — a raw socket in the air where something used to sit. What remained was the stone itself, and the stone was *singing*. Not aloud. Not in any way ears could catch. But the ring on his hand had gone cold enough to burn, and through that cold he felt vibration rising out of the hollow below, slow and vast, like a hymn hummed through a mountain.

And then, threaded through the stone's deep note, thin as wire:

Someone else was singing.

Casper's whole body went still. Even his breath stopped, embarrassed to intrude.

It was a woman's voice. It came from everywhere and nowhere — from the rock under his knees, from the air above the pit, from somewhere behind the world. It had no words at first, just melody, and the melody was so achingly simple, so patient, that it sounded like something sung not for an audience but to pass the time in a very long loneliness. A counting song. A waiting song. The kind of thing you sing in a cell to prove to yourself your voice still works.

His eyes stung before he understood why.

"Hello?" His voice came out cracked, absurdly loud in the ruin. "Hello — is someone there?"

The singing stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than any wraith's howl. It had texture. It had *hope* in it, raw and unguarded, and then — slowly, carefully, like someone testing whether a dream will dissolve if they move too fast — the voice spoke.

"You can hear me."

Not a question. A woman testing the weight of a miracle.

"Yes." Casper gripped the rim of the pit. "Yes, I can hear you. Who — who are you? Where are you?"

A laugh answered him, small and disbelieving and frayed at the edges. "Where am I," she repeated softly. "Three years I have rehearsed what I would say if anyone ever asked me that. And now I find I only want to say: *here*. I'm here. I'm still here."

The light in the ring flared, and Casper gasped — because suddenly he could *see*, the way you see in a dream, without light at all. The sinkhole opened beneath him into a shaft of seamless black stone, and far down at its bottom, pressed against the inner face of the gate like a moth against glass, was a figure.

She was made of dimness and distant fires. Her skin held the faint grey shimmer of moonlight on water; her hair moved as if underwater, in currents he couldn't feel. Where her eyes should have been there were points of light, small and steady, and both points were fixed on him with an intensity that made his chest ache.

"Zephyrine," he said, and didn't know how he knew the name until the ring pulsed warm against his finger — *the ring remembers*, some buried corner of Maren's teachings supplied, *the ring remembers everything* — "you're the other keeper."

"I was going to introduce myself with considerably more ceremony," she said. Her voice carried up the shaft clear as a bell rung underwater. "I had a speech. Three years is ample time to write a speech." The light of her eyes flickered — a stutter, brief as a blink, and Casper saw with a lurch how much thinner her glow was than a star's should be. How much of her had already burned away. "But yes. Zephyrine. Keeper of the Hollow side. And you are the boy who let the gate starve."

The words landed like a slap. He didn't step back — there was nowhere to step back *to* — but something in him flinched, and she must have seen it, because her voice gentled at once, which somehow made it worse.

"No. Forgive me. That was fear talking, dressed as blame. I have had three years of talking to myself and I've grown careless with my edges." She pressed her hand — a hand of faint fire and shadow — flat against the stone between them. "Tell me your name, keeper."

"Casper. Casper Ashgrove."

"Ashgrove." She said it like she was tasting it. "Maren's line. Then you are hers. Good. Good — she was the best of us." A pause. "Was. I'm sorry. The ring tells me things slowly, like letters arriving from a far country. I felt her go. Two years ago, in the autumn. The gate went very quiet, and then it began to die."

"It didn't die," Casper said hoarsely. "I kept it. I—" He stopped, because the next words were *I kept it alive with charms and tricks and half-measures, selling luck-knots in a market square while the real work rotted*, and saying them aloud to this starving creature felt like a confession he hadn't earned the right to make yet. "I kept it. Badly. I didn't know—"

"You didn't know about me."

"I didn't know there *was* a me-and-you. Maren said I wouldn't be keeping it alone, but I thought — I thought she meant the ring. The tradition. The dead keepers watching over my shoulder." His laugh came out bitter. "I thought I was the whole covenant."

"The covenant has never had one keeper." Her voice firmed, and the dimness around her gathered itself, and for a moment he glimpsed what she must have been before the starving: a creature of terrifying grace, light folded on light. "That is its entire architecture, Casper Ashgrove. Listen to me now, because I have said these words to stone for a thousand nights and I need them to land in living ears. The gate is a hinge between two doors. One voice holds one door. When the wards fell — when *your* side's wards fell, three nights ago — my door lost its counterweight. The seal didn't break. It *unbalanced*. Like a table with one leg sawn off."

"Then we fix it." He was already leaning forward, already reaching for the practical, the doable, the small magic he could trust. "Tell me the rite. Maren taught me fragments — the keeping-rites, the morning verses. If we speak them together—"

"Together," she echoed, and the word came out of her like a wound. "Yes. That's exactly it. That's the whole of it. The covenant requires two voices spoken together — not in echo, not in turn. *Together.* One voice on each side of the stone, speaking the same word at the same instant, so that the seal is woven from both sides and belongs to neither realm alone. That is why they built it this way. That is why it has held for nine hundred years." Her light guttered again, longer this time, and when it steadied she was visibly dimmer, and Casper felt cold slide down his spine. "But there is a problem, keeper. There is always a problem, or you wouldn't have needed three years of my rehearsal to hear me say it."

"Tell me."

"The covenant isn't only words. Words are the loom. Magic is the thread." She lifted her hand, and he saw it clearly now: her fingers were translucent at the tips, fading like the edge of a photograph left in the sun. "Every night since the wards fell, the covenant has drawn sustenance from both sides — a little of my light, a little of the ward-strength from yours. My share I have been giving. It is nearly gone; you can see what that costs. Your share—" She hesitated, and he realized she was choosing mercy over speed, and loved her and resented her for it in the same heartbeat. "Your share is being taken."

"Taken by what? The wraiths?"

"By a lantern."

The word dropped into the night and rang there.

"There is a light burning in your city," Zephyrine said, "that does not burn oil and does not burn flame. It burns *ward-magic*. Can you feel it? Follow the cold in your ring — not toward me. Away from me. Toward the tall tower on the river bend." She paused. "Something up there is drinking the covenant's thread, Casper. Drinking it nightly, great gulps of it, magic that was meant to cross the stone to me and sustain the seal and keep the Hollow's hunger asleep. It drinks, and I starve faster, and the gate thins, and the wraiths leak through, and your magistrate stands on his balcony telling the city he will protect them from the dark." Her eyes-lights narrowed, twin sparks hardening. "He is protecting them from a darkness he is feeding with a spoon."

Casper thought of Corvane Hale on the grand staircase of the Assembly Hall, three nights ago, gold braid immaculate, voice pitched to perfect paternal gravity: *Grayhallow, we have endured a terrible night, but you are safe. I will make you safe.* He thought of the sweeps, the lists, the arrests. He thought of the new lanterns on every corner — and how none of them ever seemed to need refilling.

"I believe you," he heard himself say. "God help me, I believe you instantly. That's the most frightening part."

"Do not frighten. Plan." Zephyrine's voice sharpened into something almost Maren-like, and he wondered absurdly if all keepers were issued the same tone eventually. "Here is what I know, and you must carry it out of this place and act on it. First: the covenant can be rewoven. Two voices, together, with sufficient strength on both sides of the stone. My side has nearly nothing left — but the Hollow itself is not empty, and there is a way, if I can reach the deep chamber, to draw a last reserve. It will hurt. I have stopped pricing things by what they cost. Second: your side needs a surge of clean ward-magic to anchor the weave — more than charms, more than tricks. Third—"

She stopped. Her whole form flickered like a candle in a draft, and when she re-solidified she had drifted closer to the stone, close enough that he could see the fine grain of her, the way her substance was shot through with threads of darkness that hadn't been there moments before. Threads that were *moving*.

"Third," she said, and her voice had gone quiet and very careful, "the thing I came here to contain is waking. The Unlit does not sleep as you sleep. It waits as winter waits. Every night the gate thins, it presses a little harder against the seal, and every night I feel it learning the shape of the crack. Casper — it has begun to wear faces. Last week it wore my mother's. Two nights ago it wore *yours*. It stood at the edge of my dark looking like a frightened young man with ash-brown hair, and it said, *Hello? Is someone there?*" Her light trembled. "It is studying you through me. Or I am dreaming it. In here I can no longer always tell the difference, and that, more than hunger, more than pain, is how I know my time is running short."

"How long?"

"Honestly?"

"Honestly."

"If the lantern keeps drinking — weeks. Perhaps less. And when I fail, keeper, the seal doesn't merely open. It *inverts*. Everything the covenant ever shut out comes through at once, wearing whatever face will open your door fastest." She pressed her fading hand to the stone again, and though no warmth could cross, Casper laid his own palm against the rock opposite hers, matching her gesture, and the ring between them sang a single low note like a struck bell. "So. Weeks. Call it a deadline and be grateful it has a number."

* * *

They talked until the sky behind him paled toward the false dawn, when the curfew patrols changed shift and Wren's third rule would soon matter again. He learned that she had crossed over willingly — that much she gave him, offered like a coin she'd been holding ready for three years — and that the rest of the story was long and she was tired and the telling deserved better than a whisper up a broken stair. He learned that Maren had visited the stone every month for forty years, speaking the morning verses to the dark on the chance that someone might answer, and Zephyrine had heard every single one, and had never once been able to reply, and Casper had to press his sleeve against his mouth for a while after that.

He learned, finally, near the end, why the singing had started three nights ago.

"The wards broke," she said, "and for the first time in three years, there was a *crack* in the silence. Sound carries through a crack. So I sang. Every night since. Not hoping, exactly. Hoping is expensive and I couldn't afford it." The light of her eyes softened. "And then tonight a young man with his mentor's guilt all over him knelt at my door and said *hello* like it was the most foolish word in the world, and it turns out hope was not spent after all. It was only sleeping."

"I'll come back tomorrow," Casper said. "Tomorrow night, and every night after. I'll find the lantern. I'll find who's behind it. I'll—"

"Casper." Gentle. Firm. "Go home. Sleep. Eat something that isn't fear. A starving keeper cannot feed a covenant, and a terrified one cannot think." A pause, and then, quieter, with something underneath it that made his throat tighten: "And thank you. Three years is a long time to sing to no one."

He walked down through the brambles with the ring burning steady against his finger, and the pull he had felt all week was gone — replaced by something with a shape and a name and a face made of dimness and distant fires. Behind him, faint through the stone, he thought he heard humming begin again. A waiting song. But lighter now. Almost a working song.

Almost morning.

It was only when he reached the coal-chute, hauling himself up into the dye-house cellar, that the full arithmetic landed on him at once, the way dread prefers to arrive: a deadline of weeks. A magistrate with a stolen lantern full of ward-light. A wraith wearing his face somewhere in the dark. An ally who ran caches for a living and had told him explicitly, *don't go out after the third bell* — and who was, at this precise moment, sitting in the lamplight at the bottom of the chute, arms crossed, one eyebrow raised, holding his coat.

"Well," Wren said. "Sit down, keeper. You look like a man with a plan and a deadline, and I want to hear both before I decide how much trouble I'm in."
