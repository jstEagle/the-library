## 7. The Price of Small Magic

Wren Ashbrook had survived three years in Grayhallow by knowing exactly which silences to keep.

The silence when a magistrate's clerk looked too long at her stall. The silence when a customer asked, too casually, whether she had anything *stronger* than a luck-charm. The silence of the undercity at dawn, when the smugglers came up through the drainage grates with their coats full of contraband and their eyes full of fear. She kept all these silences like coins in a purse, spending them only when she had to.

But there was one silence she had never managed: the silence where her brother's name should be.

Tam was nineteen and had their mother's laugh and none of her caution. He ran charms for her along the Tanner's Row circuit — nothing dangerous, nothing grand, just warmth-stones for the old women whose joints ached in winter and sweet-air sachets for the fever houses that the city's physicians pretended not to need. Small magic. The kind that had kept Grayhallow breathing since before the Proscription, back when keeping it quiet was a courtesy rather than a crime.

She hadn't heard from him in two days. That alone would have worried her. But now the bells were ringing wrong — not the hour, not the alarm, but the flat, arrhythmic clangor that meant *sweep*.

Wren stood at the mouth of Kettle Lane and watched the Watch move down Copper Street in files of six, lanterns swinging, their nets made of something that shimmered faintly against the morning gray. Ward-nets. Someone had given the Watch ward-nets, and ward-nets cost more than the whole constabulary earned in a season, and that meant someone above the constabulary wanted them used.

They moved door to door. They were polite about it. That was the worst part — the terrible gentleness with which they asked shopkeepers to step aside, the careful way they laid a charm-seller's stock out on the cobbles like evidence at an inquest. A woman Wren knew by face — Marla, who sold weather-blessings to fishermen — stood wringing her hands while a sergeant read from a printed list. Printed lists meant organization. Organization meant orders. Orders meant the Magistrate.

*Corvane Hale*, Wren thought, and spat into the gutter.

Three nights ago the stars had gone out. Three nights ago the sky over Grayhallow had turned into a smooth black lid, and the city had done what cities do: it had panicked quietly, then loudly, then not at all, because panic needs somewhere to go and Hale had given it somewhere. *The magic-touched*, he'd said from the courthouse steps, his voice carrying through every speaking-trumpet in the district. *The lawbreakers who hoarded forbidden arts while honest citizens slept. They have torn the heavens.*

Wren knew it was a lie. She'd known it the moment Casper Ashgrove had tumbled through her cache-door with a covenant-ring burning cold on his finger and a story about gates and hollows and a star-woman singing through stone. She'd believed him because her grandmother had left her the same kind of inheritance — a key-ring, half-melted, and a deathbed warning: *when the stars go dark, find the other keeper, or die in the dark.*

She just hadn't expected the dying to come so quickly for everyone else.

The sweep reached Kettle Lane. Wren melted backward into the doorway of the chandler's shop, heart drumming, and watched a file of Watchmen stop outside Tam's lodgings above the cobbler's.

No. No, no, no —

They went in politely. They came out politely. And between them, walking with his hands loose at his sides because Tam had never learned to look guilty even when he was, came her brother, blinking at the daylight like a man woken too early.

He wasn't fighting. Thank all the nameless gods, he wasn't fighting, because the shimmering nets had a way of turning struggle into scars. He just looked up at the windows of the buildings he passed — at the faces pressed to the glass, watching and not watching — and Wren saw him say something to the sergeant, something that made the sergeant laugh without humor.

She read his lips. *It's only small magic.*

The net went over him anyway.

Wren stayed in the doorway until her knees stopped wanting to buckle. Then she did what she did best: she vanished. Down the lane, through the chandler's yard, over the wall where the mortar had crumbled, into the throat of the undercity — and she didn't let herself think, because thinking would break her, until she was three levels down where the lamps burned whale-oil and the air tasted of rust and river.

Then she thought it, and it broke her anyway, a little.

*I did this.* Not the sweep, not the blackout — those belonged to Hale. But Tam. Tam had run her charms. Every warmth-stone, every sachet had passed through her hands first, and she had known the risk and priced it in, the way you price in spoilage or weather. Her brother had been a line in her ledger.

She sat on a crate in the smugglers' hall — a vaulted cellar the older runners called the Counting House, though nothing had been counted honestly here in forty years — and stared at her hands until Casper found her.

He came in wet from the storm-grates, hair plastered to his forehead, the ring on his hand throwing off a faint cold light that no amount of glove-wearing quite hid. He'd filled out a little since she'd met him, three days of running doing what three years of hiding hadn't. But his eyes still had that quality she'd privately named *rabbit-at-the-hedge*: a creature that knows the hawk is overhead and is tired of pretending otherwise.

"Wren." He stopped when he saw her face. "What happened?"

"They took Tam."

Casper went very still. "During the sweep?"

"During the sweep." Her voice came out flat as slate. "Nineteen years old. Warmth-stones and fever-sachets. He wouldn't have hurt a fly with magic if flies could be hurt by it. They netted him like a poacher and marched him past his own neighbors, and his neighbors *watched*." She stood, and found she was shaking, and decided to be shaking while standing rather than sitting, because it looked more like anger. "Where are they taking them?"

"The Bell Yard," Casper said quietly. "They're processing arrests at the courtyard behind the cathedral. Holding them in the undercroft until wagons come." He hesitated. "Wren, the wagons go to the Penitent's House. People don't—"

"I know what happens at the Penitent's House." Everyone in her trade knew. It was the reason they were a trade at all.

She started for the stairs. Casper caught her arm.

"If you walk into the Bell Yard, you'll be arrested too, and then there'll be nobody—"

"Nobody what? Nobody to hold your hand while you talk to your singing lady in the rock?" She twisted free. "Let go, keeper, or lose the hand."

He let go. But he didn't move, and he didn't raise his voice, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Zephyrine said the covenant needs two voices spoken together. She said the lantern is drinking the magic meant to sustain her — she has *days*, Wren, maybe less. And the original covenant is in the archive under the cathedral bells. If I can read how it was forged, I can forge it again. Maybe unmake what Hale is doing." He paused. "The archive entrance is through the Bell Yard."

For a long moment the only sound was the drip of water somewhere in the dark and the far-off, wrong-sounding bells.

"You planned this," Wren said.

"No."

"You *hoped* it. You rabbit-hearted son of a hen-house, you hoped it would light the same fire in me." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "Well. It did."

"It's not—" Casper rubbed his eyes. "I'm sorry about Tam. I'm sorry in the way that doesn't help. But I need you to understand the arithmetic, because you're better at arithmetic than I am. We can try to pull one boy out of the undercroft tonight, and maybe we succeed, and then the sweeps continue tomorrow and the next day, and Hale keeps drinking the sky dry until there's nothing left to protect anyone *from*. Or we go into the archive, learn what the covenant actually says, and maybe we break the thing that's breaking everything. Including whatever cell they're holding your brother in."

"And if the covenant says the gate stays shut forever? If it says the Hollow eats whoever opens it?" She stepped close enough to see the cold gleam of the ring beneath his glove. "What's your arithmetic then, keeper?"

"Then at least I'll know the price before I pay it," he said. "Instead of guessing at it in the dark. Which is what I've been doing for three years. It hasn't gone well."

Despite everything — despite Tam in a wagon-bound undercroft and the stars gone out and the whole rotten city hunting her kind — something in Wren cracked toward him. He was afraid. He'd been afraid for three years, and he was still walking toward the Bell Yard, and there was a word for that which wasn't bravery exactly, but was adjacent to it.

"Fine," she said. "The archive. But we do it my way, and my way starts with favors."

* * *

The Counting House, for all its ruin, still worked on the oldest currency in Grayhallow: obligation.

Wren spent the afternoon calling in debts she'd been sitting on for years, the way a miser spends gold, wincing at each coin. Old Sefa, who owed her for the charm that had quieted her husband's coughing through his last winter, agreed to watch the Bell Yard's south door and whistle three notes if the Watch doubled its rounds. The Tallow Twins, who owed her considerably more and knew it, produced two sets of vestry robes stolen off a laundry barge two summers back — *never thought they'd be useful, did you* — smelling of candle-fat and incense. A boy called Finch, ten years old and faster than rumor, carried messages up through the grates and came back with the schedule of the night-watch, copied out in a clerk's neat hand by somebody whose cousin owed somebody.

"Whoever this cousin is," Casper said, studying the schedule by lamplight, "remind me never to play cards against their family."

"Hale's building an army of grudges," Wren said, folding the paper away. "Every clerk he's passed over, every guardsman he's fined, every neighbor who's watched a door get kicked in. The undercity's just where the grudges drain to." She pulled on the vestry robe; the hood sat wrong on her, and she fixed it with two pins and a glare. "He thinks fear makes people obedient. Fear just makes them *keep accounts*."

Casper dressed more clumsily, tugging the robe like a man wearing a costume to his own funeral. Beneath it, the ring's cold light leaked faintly through the weave.

"You can't take that in there," Wren said.

"I can't take it *off*," he answered. "Believe me, I've tried. It's not jewelry, it's—" he searched for the word, "—a promise. Promises don't come off when convenient."

"Then keep your hands in your sleeves and pray the archive clerks aren't the praying sort."

They went up through the grates as the light failed, coming out of the underworld into the cathedral close the way sinners have always entered churches: sidelong, hooded, hoping not to be counted among the congregation. Sefa's three whistles followed them across the yard — the south route clear, the Watch thin, the hour good. The Bell Yard itself was barred and lantern-lit, and through its gates Wren could see the shapes of the arrested, packed onto the undercroft steps. She looked for Tam's shoulders, Tam's ridiculous untidy hair, and found him — third row, sitting very straight, talking to the woman beside him with his hands moving the way their mother's hands used to move when she told stories.

Alive. Unharmed. *Tonight.*

"Not yet," Casper murmured, and realized he'd said it aloud only when Wren glanced at him. "I'm sorry. I know—"

"Don't be sorry twice in one day. It wears the word out." She nodded at the cathedral's flank, where a narrow door stood between two buttresses, marked with a bellfinch carved in weathered stone. "There. Clergy's entrance to the tower stair. Sefa says the archivist works evenings and sleeps like the dead, and the door lock is a *charm*-lock." Her smile came out crooked. "Which means it knows my kind."

The lock was old work, older than the Proscription, and when Wren laid her palm against it she felt it wake under her skin like a cat recognizing a footstep. She fed it a thread of the small warm magic she'd hoarded in her knuckles since morning — enough to make a warmth-stone, half a fever-sachet — and the tumblers turned with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, the stair wound up into darkness, and the darkness was full of bells. Even hanging silent, they filled the shaft with a presence, a weight of bronze and patience, and every step rang faintly in Wren's sternum as though her heartbeat had been conscripted into the tower's service.

At the top: the archive. Not the grand scriptorium she'd imagined — just a round room under the great bell itself, shelves radiating from a central table, dust thick enough to write in. And on the table, already open, already waiting, a ledger bound in grey leather with a covenant-seal pressed into its cover.

Casper stopped dead in the doorway.

"That's not possible," he said. "That book hasn't been opened in—"

Someone had been here. Recently. The dust showed boot-prints, two sets, one large and one small, leading from the shelf to the table and back. Beside the ledger lay a sheet of paper with fresh copying ink, lines transcribed in a precise official hand — and at the bottom of the transcription, where a signature should be, a single word repeated like a refrain:

*Hollow. Hollow. Hollow.*

And beneath the table, caught on the chair-back, a scrap of black cloth that Wren recognized instantly, because half the city wore it sewn inside their collars these days.

Watch-issue. Magistrate's own.

"Casper," she whispered. "Whatever this covenant says — I don't think we're the first ones tonight to want to know."
