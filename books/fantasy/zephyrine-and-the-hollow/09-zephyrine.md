## 9. Zephyrine

She had learned to measure time by her own forgetting.

That was the only clock the Hollow kept — not hours, not tides, but the slow erosion of things she had once known without effort. In the first year she could recite every constellation of both realms, the names the star-born gave them and the rougher names she'd learned from Casper's mentor across the gate. In the second year the constellations went soft at their edges; she remembered that she had known them, but not the knowing itself. Now, in the third year, she sometimes lost whole evenings to a gray half-sleep and woke unsure whether she was Zephyrine at all or only the shape her absence left behind.

So when she felt the words move through the stone — keeping-words, old ones, spoken in a voice that cracked like river ice — she did not at first believe them. Belief was expensive here. The Hollow took payment in it.

She pressed her palm flat against the black wall of her cage and listened.

The words were clumsy. Mispronounced. A keeper's grammar learned secondhand and half-abandoned, dragged out of some dusty corner of memory where a boy had hidden it for three years along with everything else he couldn't bear to carry. She knew whose voice it was before he finished the first line of the rite. She had heard him through the gate on a hundred nights during that first terrible year, sitting on the city side with his back against the stone, talking because silence was worse — about the weather in Grayhallow, about the price of candles, about his mentor Aldous and the way the old man had died with an apology caught between his teeth. He had thought no one heard him. He had been wrong about that, and about so much else.

*Casper Ashgrove,* she thought, and the name tasted like light.

Then, because hope in this place came with a hook in it, she made herself stop. Made herself count the ways it might be false. The Hollow was a mimic; it had worn Aldous's voice at the wards, worn her own voice back at her in the early months until she learned to hold her tongue behind her teeth. It would happily wear Casper's too, if wearing him would make her open something that ought to stay shut.

But the Hollow did not stumble over its own keeping-words. And the Hollow did not *read* — she felt that distinctly, the careful halting rhythm of someone working from text, from memory, from a ring's uncertain instruction. The thing in the dark had never needed to learn anything. It only needed to want.

She sank down against the wall, folding her long limbs beneath her, and let herself listen properly for the first time in — how long? Long enough that her starved blood sang at even this thin thread of contact. The words came again, steadier now, and beneath them she felt the covenant stir. Not waking. Not yet. But turning over in its sleep, the way a fire turns over when someone lays one careful stick upon the coals.

*You're alive,* she told him silently, though she knew he couldn't hear thoughts, only rites. *Three years, and you're alive, and you're finally reading the letters I left you.*

Because she had left letters. That was the thing he didn't know. Every night for the first year, before her strength failed, she had pressed words into the gate's inner face — instructions, warnings, the terms of the bargain she'd struck — written in the only ink she had left, which was herself. Starlight, thinned and spent, syllable by syllable. The writing would be nearly invisible now, drained as she was drained, but it was there. It had always been there. He simply had never come close enough, never sung low enough, never been ready.

Perhaps he was ready now.

* * *

To understand what she had done, you had to understand the Unlit, and there was no understanding it. There was only remembering it, which was different, and worse.

It had not come to her realm as an invader comes. Nothing so honest. It had *accumulated* — that was the nearest word her language held, and even that was wrong, the way a cup is wrong for describing the sea. Her people had been star-born: beings of spent radiance, keepers of the slow economy by which light that burned out above was returned below and kindled again. Every fallen star passed through their hands. Every prayer made to a dead light came home to them to be answered or gently refused.

And every refusal, every renunciation, every gift that was given nowhere — those went somewhere too. They pooled. They deepened. For ten thousand years her people had poured the world's abandoned magic down into the space beneath spaces, telling themselves it was a vault, a cellar, a place of safekeeping. They had never asked what a cellar full of hunger becomes if you feed it long enough.

The Unlit was what became. Not a creature. An *accounting*. All the magic her realm had ever thrown away, standing up at last and asking the only question it had: *why was I not wanted?*

She remembered the night it rose. Of course she did; that memory was the last thing the dark would ever take from her, because she guarded it the way misers guard their final coin.

The sky of her realm had gone quiet first — stars dimming not with age but with *swallowing*, their returned light intercepted on its way down. Then the pools beneath the world had begun to drink outright, pulling radiance out of living hands, out of songs mid-verse. Her elders had convened the great concord and produced, after nine days of argument, a plan worthy of ten thousand years of cowardice: seal the Hollow. Wall the accounting away. Let the outer realms keep their light and pretend the ledger balanced.

Zephyrine had stood in that white assembly hall — she could still feel the cold brilliance of it on her skin, the way you can feel sunburn years later — and understood two things at once, with the terrible simultaneity that is the privilege of the young. First, that sealing the Hollow would only pen the Unlit against the wall until it wore through. Second, that no one else in that shining room was going to do the one thing that might actually work, which was to go down and *speak to it*.

Not banish. Not bind. Speak. Ask the question back: *what do you want?*

Her bargain had been simple to state and ruinous to keep. She would descend into the Hollow and remain — not as jailer but as interlocutor, a single point of attention for a thing that had spent ten millennia being ignored. In exchange, the Unlit would consent to be contained, would stop drinking the skies of both realms, would hold still and *wait* while she taught it, slowly, patiently, what wanting something was worth when you couldn't simply take it. The covenant between realms had been rewritten around her in a single night — two keepers, one on either side of the gate, each sustaining the other. Her light feeding the seal from within. The other keeper's magic anchoring it from without.

Aldous Merrick had taken the outward post. He had wept when he accepted it, she remembered that — a big grey-bearded man crying without shame in front of a being he considered a demigod, saying, *I'm too old for the dark, lady, but I'll hold your door.* And then, three years ago, Aldous had died, and the anchor had passed to a boy who never wanted it, and the boy had hidden instead of holding, and the seal had begun, syllable by syllable, to starve.

She did not blame him. That was the strange part, the part she had arrived at slowly, somewhere in the second year, and never managed to unlearn. Fear was honest. Hiding was human. She had chosen her cage with her eyes open and her whole heart engaged, and there were still nights — most nights — when the dark came in through her skin and she screamed soundlessly into the nothing. What could she expect of a candle-maker's apprentice who'd been handed a god's burden by accident?

Only. Only it had cost her three years. Three years of watching the Hollow eat her radiance a crumb at a time while the Unlit circled her, patient beyond all patience, wearing faces it stole from her memory — her mother, her teachers, once, unbearably, Aldous. Not cruelly. That was the horror of it. The Unlit did not know cruelty. It wore faces the way a child wears its mother's shoes, testing, wondering why they didn't fit, asking in every borrowed mouth the same starving question: *is this what wanted looks like?*

*No,* she would answer, every time. *Wanting doesn't take. Wanting asks.*

And then, tonight, through the stone, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards: a boy's clumsy voice, speaking keeping-words, getting them almost right.

* * *

When her strength allowed — when the Hollow's hunger slackened enough to let her think past the next hour — she sang.

Not the great hymns. Those required radiance she no longer possessed. She sang the small songs, the walking songs, the ones her people used for carrying water and settling children, melodies with barely any power in them at all. She sang them into the gate-stone because the gate was the only thing in the Hollow that wasn't hungry. Everything else here took. The gate only *held*, and she loved it the way you love the one honest person in a room of liars.

Tonight, between one verse and the next, she felt it answer.

Not the gate. The *ring*. Aldous's covenant-ring, on the far side of the wall — she felt its signature flare like a struck match, felt it turning toward her song the way a sunflower turns, stupidly, faithfully, toward the last light it can see. Which meant Casper was wearing it. Which meant Casper was near.

Her voice stopped. Her whole body stopped, breath and pulse together, the way a deer stops when the wind changes — because hope, in this place, was a scent that drew predators.

The dark shifted. Far off in the unlight — distance meant nothing here, and everything — the Unlit turned its attention toward the gate the way she had turned hers, drawn by the same warmth. She felt its vast curiosity spread through the Hollow like cold water finding level. It had noticed the boy before, in the early days; it had sent the wraith wearing Aldous's face to the wards, feeling for the crack in the seal the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

*Casper,* the dark said, without words, in the pressure it used instead of words. *Casper who hides. Casper who renounced. He is ours by rights. He threw his gift away just as the rest did. Why should he get to knock?*

"Because knocking is what you do when you want to come *in*," she said aloud, into the blackness, "and not to swallow." Her voice cracked on the stones of it; three years of small songs had worn her throat to wire. "Listen to me. You've had my lessons for three years and you still don't know the difference between hunger and longing. He is going to come to this gate, and when he does, you will let me speak."

*And if I don't?*

"Then the covenant dies, the gate falls, and everything you've waited for burns up in the first hour of the war that follows. Grayhallow will not negotiate. It will burn whatever leaks through, and it will burn generously." She lifted her chin, though there was nothing to see and both of them knew it. "Patience has kept you alive. Don't spend it now on a boy with a ring and a bad singing voice."

The dark considered. It was genuinely capable of consideration; that was the seed of everything she still hoped for it. Slowly, grudgingly, the pressure receded — not gone, never gone, but folded away like a hand withdrawn from a table.

She put her forehead against the gate-stone and shook.

Not fear. She had used up all her fear years ago and found, underneath it, something more stubborn and less useful. This was the shaking of a body remembering warmth. Somewhere on the other side of six inches of stone older than both realms, a frightened young man was reading her letters — or trying to, or failing to, it hardly mattered — and the covenant was stirring, and the arithmetic of her imprisonment had, for the first time in three years, changed.

Hope hurt. She had forgotten how much. It was like light returning to eyes that had adjusted to the dark: necessary, and blinding, and painful in exact proportion to how long it had been absent.

"All right," she whispered to the stone, to the boy, to the covenant sleeping under her palm, to the patient monster in the deep and the God-only-knew what on the city side. "All right. I'm here. I'm still here. Come find me."

And then, because three years of darkness had not managed to take this from her either, she began to sing again — louder this time, reckless, pouring the last of her hoarded radiance into the melody so that it would carry through the stone and into the ring and up through the hand of a boy who had spent three years pretending he was nobody.

Far away, across the wards, across the city, she felt him go very still.

She felt him *listen*.
