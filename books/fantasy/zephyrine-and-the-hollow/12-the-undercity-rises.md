## 12. The Undercity Rises

Wren had always known the undercity the way other people knew their own hands: by touch, in the dark, without needing to look. She knew which of the old cistern tunnels flooded when it rained hard on the eastern hills, which stairwells still had doors at the top that opened into cellar ways and which opened into constable barracks, which of the hundred low rooms beneath Grayhallow smelled of mildew and which smelled only of old smoke and older secrets. For eleven years she had used that knowledge to move contraband — luck-charms before the ban, everything after it. She had never once imagined she would use it to move an army.

An army was a generous word for what gathered that first night in the Cistern of Bells, an abandoned reservoir under the temple district where the dripping water made a sound like a slow, patient applause. There were perhaps sixty of them when the lamps were lit: smugglers from the river gangs, three charm-sellers whose stalls had been burned in the sweeps, a lamplighter with singed eyebrows who claimed he'd once held a spark of true fire in his bare hand and never told a soul, dockhands, beggars, a midwife, two deserters from the city watch with their uniforms turned inside out. They sat on wet stone in a ragged half-circle and looked at her as if she knew what she was doing.

She did not know what she was doing. Her brother Tam was in a magistrate's cell somewhere above her head, and every favor she had ever banked was about to be called in at once, and the man who had promised to fix everything — the soft-spoken keeper with the burned hand and the ring that glowed like a dying coal — was standing behind her, wringing river water out of his coat hem.

"Wren," Casper said quietly. "They're waiting."

"They're waiting for a general," she muttered. "I'm a fence. I sell stolen things back to the people they were stolen from."

"You sold hope for eleven years and nobody caught you." He almost smiled. "That's more than most generals manage."

She wanted to throw something at him. Instead she stepped forward onto the old sluice-platform, and the murmuring died, and sixty faces turned up toward her through the lamplight, and Wren Ashgrove — who had spent her whole life making sure no one ever looked at her too long — began to speak.

* * *

Here is what she told them, though she barely remembered saying it afterward:

She told them the truth, because she had learned long ago that lies were for people who had something to protect, and she had nothing left. She told them that Magistrate Corvane Hale had put out the stars himself, that he had broken the border wards on purpose and was drinking the spilled magic through a lantern he kept locked in his tower like a miser's coin. She told them about the sweeps — how charm-sellers weren't being arrested for punishment but for harvest, how Hale's men took the small magics out of them the way you'd milk a goat, leaving them gray and shaking and released into alleys to be forgotten. She told them her brother was one of the taken. She watched the word *brother* move through the crowd like wind through wheat, because everyone down here had someone taken, or burned out, or simply gone.

Then she told them the part she'd rehearsed least and believed most.

"The law says we're criminals because of what we are," she said. "Fine. I've been a criminal since I was fourteen and I've never once hurt anyone who didn't have it coming. The law up there belongs to Corvane Hale now, and if justice is going to happen in this city, it's going to have to come from underneath it. From us."

Silence. Dripping water. Then the old lamplighter stood up, and raised his scarred hand, and said, "I banked a favor with you over the Kellam bridge job. You want to spend it?"

"I'm spending all of them," Wren said. "Tonight."

And that, absurdly, was how it started — not with oaths or banners but with debts. It turned out the undercity ran on favors the way the upper city ran on coin, and Wren had been shrewd for eleven years, and every kindness she had ever done came home to roost in a single week. The Kellam gang gave her thirty bodies and access to the flood-tunnels that ran under the Lantern Tower itself. Mother Ilsabet, whom Wren had once smuggled a forbidden birthing-charm for at cost, opened her hospice as a safehouse and taught the fugitives which herbs masked the scent of charm-work from hounds. A river pilot named Osk cleared his debt by running messages through the storm-grates; a locksmith paid hers by teaching fifteen people to pick the new ward-locks the watch had started using; a boy of maybe twelve whom nobody could account for turned out to know every echo-point in the undercity and could whistle a warning from one end of Grayhallow to the other in under a minute.

By the third night there were a hundred and forty of them. By the fifth, two hundred, and the Cistern of Bells echoed with the sound of people learning to be brave together, which is louder than you'd think.

The strangest thing — the thing Wren kept turning over in her mind like a coin she suspected was counterfeit — was that they looked to Casper too. More, in a way. She was the one who fed them and organized them and knew the tunnels; he was the one they gathered around afterward, in the lamplight, when the fear got loud.

* * *

Casper taught them the keeping-rites.

He did it badly at first, she thought — he had the manner of a man teaching something he'd only just forgiven himself for knowing. He'd stand in the center of the cistern floor with his sleeves rolled up and the covenant-ring dull on his finger, and he'd say, "This isn't magic, exactly. Magic is what Hale hoards. This is keeping. Anybody can learn it," and the crowd would shift and mutter, because in Grayhallow those words had been illegal for a generation.

The first rite was the simplest: cupping your hands and breathing into them, naming aloud something small you meant to protect. A door. A child. A memory. "The old keepers believed protection has to be specific," Casper explained. "You can't hold a wall if you don't know what it's holding." Wren tried it, feeling foolish, and named Tam — *I mean to bring my brother home* — and felt something settle into her palms like a warm stone. Not power. Weight. Purpose given a place to sit.

The second rite was harder: passing the weight between keepers. He had them form chains, ten people linking hands, and showed them how a burden of watching could travel down the line so no one bore it alone. The third was a wardsong, four notes repeated, pitched so low it was felt more than heard — "the gate's own lullaby," he called it, and when two hundred voices finally found the harmony in that dripping dark, the sound rose through the old reservoir shafts and, for one impossible moment, every lamp in the cavern burned steady and bright, and even the drips seemed to fall in time.

People wept. Wren didn't — she was busy not weeping — but she understood it. Most of these folk had spent their lives hiding the smallest part of themselves, tucking it away like stolen goods, and here was a man telling them that the hiding had been the crime all along, not the gift. That was worth tears. That was worth more than the lantern full of stolen sky, probably, though Wren was pragmatic enough to note that they'd need to deal with the actual lantern eventually too.

She cornered Casper after the fourth night's lesson, while the crowds drained away up the stairwells and the lamps guttered low.

"You're burning yourself," she said. She'd watched him closely during the wardsong — watched the ring drink from him with every verse, watched him go grayer at the edges each night, the way the harvested charm-sellers went gray. "That ring eats what you feed it. Don't tell me otherwise; I've seen hungry things before and I know the look."

Casper flexed his hand. In the lamplight the ring's band showed fine hairline cracks, like ice about to give. "It's how it's made," he said. "Keepers pay in their own coin. Always have." He said it lightly, the way people say the worst things they know, and something cold slid down Wren's spine and settled in her stomach and stayed there for days.

"Then we find another way to pay," she said flatly.

"There isn't one." He met her eyes, and for once the gentle evasiveness was gone; what was underneath was very tired and very kind. "Wren, the covenant takes what it takes. What matters is what happens before the bill comes due. So teach them fast, and organize fast, and get me into that tower while I've still got enough left in me to matter."

She wanted to argue. She was good at arguing; she'd won arguments against customs men, against cutthroats, against her own mother. But there was nothing to argue against except a fact, and facts don't blink. So she said the only useful thing left: "Then don't waste evenings on speeches. Save your coin. I'll handle the speeches."

"That," said Casper Ashgrove, with the ghost of a real smile, "is why I'm glad you're the general."

"I'm not the general."

"You are," he said, already walking toward the stairwell. "You've been one since the night you decided your brother was worth a war. Everything since then is just paperwork."

* * *

On the sixth night, the boy who knew the echoes came sliding down the rope-ladder two rungs at a time, whistling the alarm — three rising notes, the signal for *swept tunnels, hide everything* — and the whole cistern went dark and silent in the space of ten heartbeats. Wren pressed herself against the wet stone with a shuttered lamp against her chest and counted the sounds: boots above, many; the scrape of probe-irons against tunnel mouths; the thin, wrong hum of Hale's harvest-tools waking the residual magic in the walls.

They were searching for them. Not casually — systematically, grid by grid, the way you'd search for rats you intended to make an example of. Someone had talked, or someone had guessed, or Hale's spies in the undercity were better than hers. She lay in the dark listening to her army breathe — two hundred people trying to be silence — and understood with sudden clarity that the clock had run out on patience. Every hour they hid was an hour of Hale harvesting, an hour of Tam in a cell, an hour of the wards decaying over whatever lived beyond them. She had seen what came through when wards failed. Everyone had, by now; the wraiths walked openly on the ring roads, wearing dead voices.

The boots passed. The probe-irons moved on. After an hour the all-clear whistle came whispering down the shafts, and the lamps relit one by one, and Wren stood up on the sluice-platform with her heart still hammering and made the decision she had been circling for six nights.

"Change of plans," she called, and her voice cracked out over the water like a whip, and two hundred faces turned up to her. "We're done hiding. Hale thinks we're rats; let's show him what's been living under his floor. Tomorrow night we take the tower."

The roar that went up should have frightened her. It didn't. It sounded like the wardsong — four notes, repeated, felt more than heard.

Afterward, when the cistern emptied, Casper lingered at the platform's edge, looking up through the great dark shaft toward the distant coin of night sky where no stars showed. The ring on his hand was glowing again, faintly, steadily — and Wren, who knew hungry things, saw that tonight it wasn't drinking from him at all. It was pulling. Tugging toward some direction below and beyond, the way a compass needle tugs, if a compass pointed at grief.

"Casper," she said. "Your ring's doing something."

"I know." His voice had gone strange and quiet. "It's been doing it for an hour. There's a voice coming through the gate — singing. It's the other keeper, Wren. She's alive in there. And she's getting fainter."

Above them, through the shaft, the starless sky hung like a shut door. Below them, through the stone, a woman neither of them had met was running out of light.

"Then we go tomorrow," Wren said. "Tower first. Gate second. Nobody else runs out of time on my watch."

She only hoped the plan survived the night, and the city, and the man upstairs with the lantern — and that whatever was left of Casper Ashgrove when the ring finished its accounting would be enough to answer a song.
