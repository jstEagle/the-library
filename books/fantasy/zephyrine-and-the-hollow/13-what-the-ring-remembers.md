## 13. What the Ring Remembers

The ring had never been warm.

Three years Casper had worn it — first loose on a finger too thin, then snug as his hands filled out with the work of hiding — and in all that time it had kept the temperature of a winter riverbed, a cold that lived in the bone rather than the skin. He had told himself it was only metal. Old metal, covenant-metal, indifferent as a gravestone. He had told himself a great many things about that ring, and most of them had been lies he needed to survive the night.

Now, in the lamplight of Wren's undercity storeroom, with the cracked gate humming somewhere above them like a struck bell that refused to finish its note, the ring began to burn cold in earnest — and it pulled him under.

He had been trying to sleep on a pallet of smuggled wool blankets. The storeroom smelled of lamp oil, dried herbs, and the particular dustiness of things hidden a long time from honest air. Wren was somewhere in the warren of tunnels beyond the door, doing whatever it was she did at midnight — counting favors, he suspected, the way other people counted coins. Casper had turned the ring slowly around his finger the way he did every night, a habit inherited from Aldous along with everything else, and the cold had deepened, and then—

Then he was not in the storeroom at all.

* * *

He stood on a hillside under a sky crowded with stars, so many stars that the dark between them looked bruised and velvet-rich, and a woman was walking up the hill toward him carrying a lantern full of light that was not firelight. Her hair was white as frost on a windowpane. Her face was wrong for the age of her hands — young, unlined, but her eyes held the patience of centuries, and when she smiled at him he understood with a lurch that she was not smiling *at* him at all. She was smiling at someone who would not be born for generations.

"You'll do," she said to that other person, that ghost. "You'll have to."

The hillside dissolved like sugar in water.

A gate — this gate, though whole, though singing, its arch unbroken and its doors shut fast — stood in a meadow, and a man in a shepherd's coat pressed his palm to the stone. His fingers were blackened to the second knuckle, burned by something with no heat, and he was weeping without sound, and he was speaking to the doors as if they could hear him.

"They can," said a voice behind Casper, and he turned to find an old woman with the covenant-ring already on her hand, wearing it the way soldiers wear scars. "That's the whole trouble, keeper. They always can."

* * *

The visions came faster after that, the ring no longer showing him glimpses but *pouring*, as if it had been waiting three patient years for him to stop flinching and finally look.

He saw a fisherman-keeper standing waist-deep in the sea off some coast that wasn't Grayhallow's, holding the gate's twin arch above the waves with both hands while something beneath the surface pulled, patient as tide, hungry as tide. He saw how the man's shadow stretched the wrong way, toward the water, thinning with every hour until at last there was almost nothing of him left on the sand — and still he held on.

He saw a keeper who was a child. Ten years old, maybe, barefoot in a mountain pass, and Casper's throat closed around the unfairness of it until the vision itself seemed to answer: *there is never anyone else.* The child-keeper fed the ward-stones with small magics gathered from travelers' kindnesses — a wish here, a blessing there — and grew old in thirty years what should have taken eighty, and died sitting up against the gate with her chin lifted, listening to the singing until she couldn't hear it anymore.

He saw Aldous.

Not the Aldous of Casper's memory — the coughing, gray-faced man in the narrow bed who had pressed the ring into Casper's palm with hands that shook and words that didn't: *Keep it sealed, boy. Keep it sealed and keep yourself small.* No. This was Aldous young, Aldous upright, Aldous standing before the Hollow Gate with his coat off and his sleeves rolled like a man preparing for honest labor, and beside him —

Beside him stood Zephyrine.

Casper knew her at once, though he had never seen her face. He knew her the way you know a voice heard through a wall. She was starlight given a body: her skin faintly luminous, her eyes reflecting constellations that had no names in any Grayhallow atlas, her presence making the air around her seem thinner, higher, closer to the top of the sky. When she moved, the ward-stones brightened. When she laughed — and she laughed, in this memory, at something Aldous said, a real laugh, human-sized and warm — the whole arch glowed like dawn through fog.

They were friends. That was what undid him. Not colleagues, not two strangers bound by duty. Friends, standing shoulder to shoulder at the gate they kept together, and Casper realized he had spent three years thinking of the keeping as *his* burden, *his* inheritance, when the covenant had never once been meant for a single pair of hands.

*Aldous knew,* he thought. *He knew I'd try to hold it alone.*

The memory shifted. Night fell across the meadow in a rush, stars wheeling, seasons flickering past like pages turned by a impatient hand — and then the night Casper knew. The night three years ago. The wards shattering. And Zephyrine turning toward the gate with her face gone grave and resolute in a way that made her look suddenly ancient, suddenly exactly what she was.

She was arguing with Aldous. The vision gave him their voices as if he stood between them.

"—the Unlit has found the seam," Zephyrine said. "It will come through regardless. The only question is whether it comes through *here*, into the world, or whether it comes through *me*."

"There has to be another way—"

"There isn't. You know the covenant as well as I do, Aldous. It was written in sacrifice because sacrifice is the only coin both realms honor." She touched the arch gently, fondly, the way one touches the door of a home one is leaving. "Someone must go inside and hold the seam closed from within. Someone whose own light can feed the lock. I am made of light, my friend. It would be wasteful to send anyone lesser."

"And if your light runs out?"

Zephyrine smiled, and even now, even in memory, even knowing what he knew, Casper felt that smile land in his chest like a coal.

"Then someone had better open the door," she said.

* * *

He came back to himself gasping, sitting bolt-upright on the pallet, one hand clamped over the ring as if he could smother it. The lamp had burned low. His legs were rigid, his back soaked, and the cold in his finger had climbed to his wrist, his elbow, his shoulder — a slow tide of winter receding now, reluctantly, like something disappointed by how little it had managed to take.

But it *had* taken something. He could feel the absence the way you feel a tooth pulled: a raw socket where fullness used to be. Small, dull, deniable. For now.

"That's the third time you've cried out in your sleep," Wren said from the doorway. She was leaning against the frame with her arms crossed, and she didn't look like she'd just arrived. There were shadows under her eyes deeper than the lamplight could account for. "Want to tell me what the ring's been feeding on?"

"It's not—" Casper stopped. Swallowed. Started again, because lying to Wren had stopped working somewhere around the time she'd shown him her grandmother's keeper-marks, inked in faded blue along her inner wrist. "It shows things. Showed things. Every keeper. All of them, going back — Wren, I don't know how far back. Hundreds of years. A thousand."

"And?"

"And every single one of them died young." He heard his own voice come out flat, which was better than letting it crack. "Burned out. The ring doesn't just remember, it — it *feeds*. On us. On whatever it is we carry that makes the keeping possible. Aldous's cough. The shepherd whose shadow drained into the sea. A child, Wren. There was a *child*."

Wren crossed the room and sat on the edge of the pallet, unhurried, the way she did everything — as if speed were a luxury only frightened people indulged in. She took his hand, the ringed one, and turned it over. The finger beneath the band had gone pale as candle wax.

"My gran wore her marks until the day she died at ninety-one in a bed with her family around her," Wren said. "But she'd already passed the keeping on by then. Passed it to a cousin who lasted eleven years." She looked up at him. "So. Now you know the price. Does it change anything?"

Casper wanted to say yes. Some sensible, self-preserving part of him assembled the argument neatly: *find another way, find anyone else, this is a trap dressed as a duty.* But the visions were still moving behind his eyes, and what he kept returning to — what he suspected he would return to for the rest of his however-many days — was Zephyrine's hand resting on the arch, fond, familiar. Leaving her home. Smiling while she did it.

"No," he said. "It doesn't change anything. I think that's the worst part. I knew, somehow. Aldous told me the ring takes and takes, and I wore it anyway, because the alternative was letting the wards fall and — well." He laughed, short and ugly. "And look how that went."

"The wards fell anyway."

"Because Hale *cut* them. Because I was holding them alone with half a covenant and no idea, and he had three years to plan it." The anger surprised him with its clean heat. Not at himself, for once — at the tidy magistrate with his tidy lantern, drinking the sky. "Every keeper in that ring gave everything they had, and none of them knew they were fighting a man who'd simply decided the light belonged to him."

"Then we take it back." Wren said it simply, the way she said everything that mattered. But her thumb was moving over the back of his hand, and her eyes had gone distant, tallying. He was learning to read those tallies. Somewhere in the tunnels beneath Grayhallow, she was counting smugglers and charm-sellers and grudge-holders, and the number was getting close to enough.

"Not tonight," Casper said, and lay back down. The cold in his finger had settled into something almost companionable, the way pain does when you've agreed to carry it. "Tomorrow I need to see it again. All of it. If the ring remembers how the covenant was first forged, then it remembers how to forge it again. And if forging it burns me out—" He shrugged against the blankets. "At least I'll know the price before I pay it. Nobody in that ring got to haggle."

Wren snorted. "You sound like Gran."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"It wasn't." But she pulled the spare blanket over him anyway, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch. "Casper. The woman. In the visions. The one who went inside."

"Zephyrine."

"If she's been holding the seam for three years on her own light—" Wren didn't finish it. She didn't have to. They both did the arithmetic in silence, and neither of them liked the remainder.

After she left, Casper slid the ring off his finger and set it on the floorboards, where it sat gleaming faintly, coldly, like a piece of the fallen sky. Sleep took a long time finding him. When it did, he dreamed of a door in a dark place, and behind the door a voice singing — thin now, very thin, a wick burning down to its last quarter-inch of oil — and in the dream he was running toward it, and the dream would not let him arrive.

He woke before dawn with the ring already back on his finger. He didn't remember putting it there.

Above, muffled by earth and stone and the weight of a city pretending to be safe, the bell of the archive tower rang the hour — and beneath its bronze note, faint but unmistakable, the Hollow Gate's hum changed pitch. Higher. Thinner. Like a voice calling from very far away, and getting farther.

Casper sat up in the dark and understood, with the calm of a man who has finally finished haggling, that whatever the price was going to be, he had very little time left to pay it.
