## 14. Storming the Lantern Tower

Wren had planned a hundred jobs in her life — cache runs through the bell-ward, ledger thefts from customs houses, one memorable night smuggling a griffin chick out of the menagerie in a flour sack — and every single one of them had gone wrong in some small, survivable way. She had built her whole reputation on surviving the wrongness.

She stood now at the mouth of Lantern Row and understood that nothing she had ever survived would prepare her for this.

The tower rose against a starless sky like a black finger pressed to the city's throat. Hale's engineers had ringed it with lanterns of their own — ordinary oil-flame, lawful and licensed — but above them, at the crown, burned the thing they'd come to take. The stolen ward-light. Three years of Grayhallow's magic, siphoned night by night into a glass heart the size of a wine cask, and it glowed now with a color Wren had no name for. Not gold. Not white. Something older than both, the color the sky must have been before anyone thought to call it anything.

It made her eyes ache. It made her want to weep, which was worse.

"Say it back to me," Casper said beside her. He was pale in the light of the signal-lamp, his coat pockets bulging with chalk, salt, and the covenant-ring, which he kept glancing at the way a man glances at a debt collector.

"North stair's ours," Wren said. "Sable takes the cistern gate. The ash-bringers hold the square so the watch can't reinforce. I go up the service shaft with six knives and no plan worth the name, and I open the cells." She flexed her fingers. "And you go find your light."

"Our light," Casper said quietly.

"Don't make it noble. Noble gets people killed." She said it harsher than she meant to, because Tam's face was in her head — Tam, three days free of the holding cells, insisting on carrying the banner at the front of the ash-bringers because someone had to show the undercity that Thackerays didn't hide anymore. "Just get to the chamber. Break the lantern. Don't die. In that order."

He almost smiled. "You've been rehearsing that."

"I rehearse everything. It's why I'm still alive." She caught his sleeve before he turned away. "Casper. If it comes down to you or the light—"

"It won't."

"If it does," she said, "choose yourself. The sky's been dark three years. It can be dark one more night. You don't get to be a martyr just because it's fashionable."

For a moment he looked at her with an expression she couldn't read — gratitude, maybe, or grief, or the particular loneliness of a man carrying something heavy who has just realized other people can see it. Then the signal-lamp flared green down the row, and there was no more time for anything.

The undercity came up out of the ground like water finding its level.

They poured from cellar doors and storm grates and the mouths of sewers that hadn't been used since the plague years — smugglers with smuggled steel, charm-sellers with charms that hadn't worked in years and worked tonight anyway, dockhands, lamplighters' apprentices, old women with rolling pins, the whole ragged congregation of everything Grayhallow had spent a decade pretending not to see. Wren watched them flow toward the tower and felt something crack open in her chest that she'd have to examine later, if there was a later.

Then the wards failed all the way, and the wraiths came down.

They fell out of the dark like snow falling upward — pale, elongated things with hollows where faces should be, trailing tatters of whatever they'd been before the Hollow got them. The front rank of the ash-bringers raised their brands and the fire caught, and for one glorious moment the square was lit like a festival.

Then the screaming started, and it was not festival-shaped.

* * *

Wren went up the service shaft on hands and knees in the dark, counting rungs, breathing through her teeth. Behind her, Sable's people were holding the shaft door with furniture and fury. Above her, the cells.

The holding levels smelled of lye and despair. She'd expected guards; what she found instead was worse — empty corridors, doors hanging open, and a single warden sitting at his desk with his keys laid out neatly in front of him and his hands folded, waiting.

"They told me to expect you," he said. His voice was flat as slate. "Magistrate's orders. The prisoners are to be released into the surge."

Wren's knife was at his throat before she finished understanding the sentence. "Released *into*—"

"The wraiths need somewhere to go." He didn't flinch. Didn't even blink. "He calls it thinning. There are two hundred people on the floors below us, smuggler. He wants them walked out the front doors while those things are in the streets."

The world went very narrow and very cold. Wren had grown up learning that the law was a wall you climbed around, a lock you picked, a game with rules you could learn better than the rule-makers. She had never once believed in it. But some part of her had apparently still believed there was a floor beneath it, some depth past which even magistrates wouldn't sink.

Hale had found the bottom and started digging.

"Keys," she said.

"The cells are warded against—"

"*Keys.*" She put the knife-edge against skin, just enough to feel it, and discovered she was shaking — not from fear. From something hotter. "Two hundred people. My brother sat in one of your cells for eleven days. So you're going to pick up those keys, and you're going to walk me through every ward on every door, and if one person dies tonight because you hesitated, I will spend the rest of my life making sure yours is very, very long."

He picked up the keys.

What followed was the worst hour of Wren's life, and she had buried a grandmother and a revolution in the same year. They went cell to cell through the dark, cutting wards, hauling people upright — some of them could walk, some of them couldn't, and the ones who couldn't were carried on the backs of the ones who barely could. A charm-seller named Odile whose hands shook too badly to work her own locks. Two boys off a grain barge, fourteen at most, who clung to each other and asked if the stars were really gone. Tam, when she reached his old block, wasn't there — already out, already in the square, already doing something brave and stupid, and she sent a prayer after him shaped like a threat.

By the time the last cell stood empty, the tower shuddered around them. Dust sifted from the ceiling seams. Through the arrow-slits, Wren could see the square burning and the wraiths wheeling through the smoke like thoughts nobody wanted to think, and above it all the stolen light at the tower's crown burning brighter than before — brighter, she realized with a lurch, than it had any right to be.

It was being *drawn*. Fed. Poured somewhere.

Poured into someone.

"Go," she told the freed prisoners, pointing them down the north stair toward the cistern and the tunnels and home. "Don't stop, don't cheer, don't look at the sky. Go."

Then she turned and ran upward, toward the light, because Casper Ashgrove was somewhere above her walking straight into the brightest trap in Grayhallow, and she had promised him an order of operations he was constitutionally incapable of following.

* * *

Casper felt the tower before he saw it — felt it in the ring, which had gone from cold-burning to a steady, singing heat against his finger, like a wire pulled taut between him and the top of the world.

The upper stairs were empty. Of course they were. Hale didn't need guards for what came next; he needed witnesses, and witnesses could wait outside. Casper climbed through silence broken only by the distant roar of the square and a sound he took several flights to identify: a hum, low and vast, coming through the stone itself. The sound the Hollow made. The sound Zephyrine's song answered, three years of nights, through a cracked gate.

The tower was full of the Hollow's voice. And something up top was drinking it.

The lantern chamber doors stood open.

He had imagined this room a hundred times — vaulted, guarded, ringed with wards. Instead it was almost plain: a high round chamber, windows blacked, and in the center the lantern itself on an iron cradle, blazing with that unbearable pre-dawn color, its light running not outward but *inward*, streaming off its surface in visible threads that converged—

—on Magistrate Corvane Hale, standing at the cradle with his sleeves rolled up like a man washing dishes.

Threads of light sank into Hale's hands, his throat, his closed eyes. Where they touched him, he was changing. His skin had taken on a faint translucence, veins mapped in soft radiance; his hair was going silver-white from the roots; and when he opened his eyes and saw Casper in the doorway, they were lit from behind like lamps.

"Keeper Ashgrove," Hale said, and his voice had harmonics in it now, two tones where there should have been one. "You're late. I began without you."

"Hale." Casper's hand found the ring. It was scalding now. "Stop. Whatever you think this is—"

"This is the only honest act in Grayhallow's history." Hale spread his arms, and the light bent toward him lovingly. "Every magistrate before me hoarded in secret and called it order. I am simply finishing the arithmetic. The wards are dead. The Hollow is leaking. Someone must hold the light, or the dark takes the city. I am holding it." He smiled, and the smile was almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing Casper had seen all night, and he had seen wraiths wear his mentor's face. "You of all people understand. You held the gate alone for three years. Alone, and thanklessly, and illegally. Imagine holding *everything*. Imagine never again being the man hiding in a charm shop while the sky rots overhead."

Casper's mouth was dry. Because the terrible thing — the thing he would never tell Wren — was that he did imagine it. For one whole second, he stood in the doorway of the lantern chamber and let himself imagine being full of light, being *enough*, being the keeper the covenant deserved instead of the coward who'd inherited it and hidden.

The ring burned his finger like a rebuke.

"You're not holding it," Casper said. "It's holding *you*. Look at your hands, Corvane. That's not power. That's a hook."

"And if it is?" For just a moment the harmonics dropped away and the voice underneath was tired, human, almost young. "Three years I've watched this city choke its own wonder to death and call the corpse safety. I watched them outlaw a child's luck-charm. I watched keepers hunted until there was one left, hiding in plain sight, selling trinkets." Hale's luminous eyes fixed on him. "I am offering you the only version of justice this world will ever permit, Casper. Not the law. Not the covenant. *Light*, shared between the only two men brave enough to take it. Reforge the wards with me. Rule what we save. Or stand there clutching your little ring while the dark eats everything you love — starting with the smuggler girl on the stairs behind you."

Casper heard her then — boots on stone, two flights down, running. Coming toward the light. Toward the hook.

He stepped into the chamber and closed the door behind him.

"No deal," he said, and drew the ring off his finger, and held it up between himself and the lantern, where it blazed like a captive star. "There's a clause you never read, Corvane. The light doesn't belong to whoever's strongest. It belongs to whoever's willing to give it *back*."

Hale's face changed. The gentleness went out of it, and what remained was vast and hungry and beginning, at last, to be afraid.

"That light," Hale said, "is mine."

"It was never anyone's," Casper said. "That was always the point."

Above them, through the blacked windows, the first thread of true dawn-light — no, not dawn, something else, something rising — began to seep through the cracks in the glass, and the lantern sang, and the ring in Casper's bare hand answered it, and the whole tower leaned toward the moment when everything would break.
