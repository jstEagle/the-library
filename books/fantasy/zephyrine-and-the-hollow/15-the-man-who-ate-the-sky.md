## 15. The Man Who Ate the Sky

The light came up through Hale like water finding its level.

Casper watched it happen from ten feet away, flat on the lantern chamber floor with the taste of copper in his mouth and the ring burning a cold circle into his bare palm. He had seen magic do beautiful things in his life — his mentor's hands folding charms out of candle-smoke, Zephyrine's voice threading through stone — but he had never seen it do this. Hale stood at the heart of the shattered lantern's cage, arms half-raised, and the stolen ward-light poured into him the way rain pours into cracked earth: greedily, endlessly, with a sound like a city sighing.

It was not gentle. That was the thing no one would have guessed, looking at Magistrate Corvane Hale in his gray coats and his tidy offices, hearing him promise protection in that reasonable voice. There was nothing reasonable in what was happening now. The light went into him and he *bent* around it, spine arching, jaw working, and where it touched his skin it left marks — fine silver traceries climbing his throat like frost on a window, spreading across his cheekbones, pooling in the hollows of his eyes until they glowed faintly, steadily, like lamps seen through fog.

Hale opened his mouth and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong — layered, resonant, the sound of a man speaking from inside a bell.

"Do you feel it?" he said. His voice filled the chamber, filled the tower, maybe filled more than the tower. "Three years I held this. Three years of carrying it *carefully*, Casper Ashgrove, of rationing myself like some starving clerk with a single coin. And all it wanted to do was *pour*."

"You're drinking the sky," Casper said. He got a knee under himself. His ribs screamed where a wraith's blow had caught him on the stairs; something in his side might be broken, or worse. "Every star that should be over Grayhallow tonight. You're swallowing them."

"I'm *holding* them." Hale turned, and the movement was too smooth, gliding rather than walking, his boots an inch above the floor. "There's a difference. A keeper holds things. Isn't that what your mentor taught you? Hold the gate. Keep the covenant. Carry the weight." His glowing eyes found the ring, and something like tenderness moved across his transfigured face, which was somehow worse than hunger. "You've been carrying a candle and calling it a burden. I am offering you the sun."

* * *

Casper had imagined this moment, in the small hours when honesty crept in — imagined standing before real power and discovering what he was made of. He had assumed he would be afraid. He was, but not the way he'd expected. Not the animal fear of the wraith on the night the stars went out, not the sick dread of three years of hiding. This was a cleaner terror, almost clarifying: the fear of a man standing at a crossroads who can see, perfectly, down both roads at once.

Because he could feel the lantern's light from here. Even scattered, even pouring into Hale, it pressed against his skin like a crowd pressing against a door. It was warm. It was *his kind of thing* — ward-magic, keeping-magic, the old slow strength of thresholds and boundaries — and every instinct he had inherited from Aldous Vane's dying hands said *take it, use it, you could seal the gate for a century.* And beneath that older instinct, deeper and less honest, whispered: *you could stop hiding. You could walk down any street in Grayhallow and no law, no magistrate, no wraith could touch you. You'd never have to flinch again.*

"Look at you," Hale said softly, drifting closer. The silver traceries had reached his temples now, framing his face like a saint's halo in an old forbidden fresco. "I can see you wanting it. Don't be ashamed. Wanting is honest. It's the only honest thing left in this city." He spread his hands, and between them the last unswallowed knot of lantern-light coiled, dense as a captive sun. "Here is my final offer, keeper. No tricks. No chains. I have Wren's people released within the hour — all of them, every charm-seller in my cells. I reopen the archive. I let the old rites back into the daylight. And you and I hold this together. Two keepers. A shared sky. Grayhallow will never again have to choose between fear and wonder, because we will decide for them, gently, forever."

"*Forever*," Casper repeated. "That's what Aldous thought too. He held the gate alone because the covenant broke, and it broke because—" He stopped. Because the other keeper had gone into the Hollow. Because Zephyrine had walked into the dark to cage something, and everyone on this side had called it abandonment, imprisonment, anything except sacrifice. "It doesn't work alone. That's not my rule or yours. It's the shape of the thing itself. One keeper holding all that power isn't a guardian. It's just a very bright target."

"For the Unlit?" Hale smiled, and his teeth were very white and slightly too many. "I've felt it pushing at the wards. I know what's in the Hollow. Do you know why I never feared it, Casper? Because it is everything magic ever renounced, everything this cowardly city cast out — and I have spent three years *taking it all in*. I am the most welcoming house it has ever seen. When it comes, it won't fight me. It will *come home*."

The words dropped through Casper like stones through still water, and what they displaced was understanding. He had thought Hale was a thief. A hoarder, a murderer of stars, an ambitious man with a lantern. He had not understood until this moment, watching the silver creep down Hale's forearms, that Hale believed himself to be the solution. That the tidy logic ran all the way to the bottom: swallow the outcast light, become so full of the world's rejected magic that even the Hollow would have nowhere to put itself. He wasn't building a kingdom.

He was building an armor, and he didn't care that the whole sky would burn for fuel.

"That's why you blacked out the stars," Casper said. "Not to harvest the wards. To feed yourself fast enough that when the wards finally failed—"

"When they failed," Hale agreed placidly, "*I* would be the ward. Every wraith that crosses will find me in its way. Every scrap of renounced magic will find a hearth already lit." He drifted nearer still, and now Casper could feel the heat of him, the terrible gravitational warmth of a man becoming a celestial body. "Join me, Ashgrove. Or stand there with your little cold ring and watch me do it alone, badly, and tell me afterward which of us loved this city more."

* * *

Somewhere below, faint through floors of stone, Casper heard the assault still raging — Wren's smugglers against Hale's guard, steel and shouting and someone singing a keeping-rite off-key, which meant Tamsin was alive and holding a doorway somewhere. He thought of Wren's face when she'd talked about her brother. He thought of the clerk from the market, three years ago, buying a luck-charm with shaking hands because the world had taught her to be afraid of Thursday.

And he thought of Zephyrine's voice coming through the cracked gate, thread-thin and stubborn: *the covenant requires two voices.*

Hale was offering two voices. That was the horror of it — the offer was almost right. Shared power, open rites, an end to hiding. If you changed one word — if *forever* became *together*, if *decide for them* became *teach them* — it would be the covenant reborn. Hale had taken something true and bent it around his own hunger the way the light was bending him, and that, Casper thought, was the oldest trick power knew.

"No," Casper said.

Hale's glow flickered — surprise, genuine, almost childlike. "No?"

"It's a beautiful offer. It's the best offer anyone's ever made me, and I think you know that, and I think that's why you made it." Casper stood, slowly, holding his broken side, and raised his hand so the covenant-ring caught the lantern-light. "But you're asking me to help you become the sky, and the sky doesn't work that way. The sky gives. It doesn't keep. That's the whole lesson, the one this city spent thirty years outlawing and I spent three years too afraid to learn. Light that's held dies. Light that's given—" His voice cracked, and he thought of a woman behind stone, starving, singing anyway. "Light that's given comes back as stars."

Something in the room shifted then. Perhaps the tower heard him. Perhaps the ring did. The cold in Casper's palm deepened until it ached like frostbite, and he felt, for the first time clearly, what the ring had been trying to tell him since the night the stars went out: it remembered every keeper. It remembered Aldous dying with the post unfinished. It remembered Zephyrine walking into the dark. It knew exactly how much a keeper could give, and it was asking, patiently, whether he was ready to find out.

He wasn't ready. He did it anyway.

* * *

He did not attack Hale. He had learned enough in the last hour to know that attacking a man full of ward-light was like throwing stones at the sea. Instead Casper did the one thing a keeper could do, the thing the ring burned to teach him: he reached past Hale, past the drifting, blazing, half-divine figure with his too-many teeth, and he put both hands — ring hand and bare hand alike — on the shattered frame of the lantern.

The lantern was dead glass and bent silver, mostly. But its purpose remained. A lantern is a shape that holds light; break the shape and the light must go *somewhere*. And ward-magic, true ward-magic, always knows the way home.

"Casper." Hale's voice had lost its music. What was left underneath was flat and quick and human, and that was the most frightening sound yet. "Casper, wait. We can— there are terms, there's always terms, name yours—"

"You named mine three years ago," Casper said. "The night you snuffed the stars. I'm just now reading them."

He pulled, with the ring, with whatever thin borrowed spark still lived in him, with the memory of Aldous's hands over his own teaching him the first keeping-rite — *not to seize, only to open* — and he broke the lantern's shape entirely.

The light did not explode. That was the miracle of it, the thing he would try to describe for the rest of his life and never quite manage. It *released*, the way a breath releases, the way a long-held note releases — three years of stolen sky pouring out of the broken vessel and streaming toward the windows in a river of silver-gold, flowing through the blacked glass, down the tower's flanks, across the wounded city, all of it running home along channels older than Grayhallow itself, running toward the gate, toward the Hollow, toward the woman behind the stone who needed it to live.

And Hale screamed.

It was not a villain's scream of rage. It was the scream of a drowning man feeling the water leave. The silver traceries tore free of his skin in ribbons, dragged after the departing light like banners in a gale, and Hale grabbed for them — actually grabbed, hands clawing at the air, at his own arms, at the brightness peeling away from him — and Casper saw, in the last second, that the man had poured so much of himself into the stolen power that taking it back took *him* with it.

"No — no, you don't understand, I *am* holding it, if it goes I go—"

"Then hold something else," Casper said, and meant it as mercy, and watched it fail to land.

The last of the light left Hale like a tide leaving a sandcastle. What collapsed onto the lantern chamber floor was small and gray and human-shaped, breathing shallowly, silver veins fading to ash-lines, eyes ordinary and terrified. Alive. Barely. Damned, perhaps, to wake as nothing in a city about to learn what he had done.

Casper would have knelt. He meant to. He remembered intending to, some scrap of keeper's duty about checking a fallen man's breath.

Then the tower shook, and the floor tilted, and the ring on his hand went from cold to *catastrophic* — a cold like being gripped by winter itself — and Casper understood, dimly, through the roaring in his ears, that breaking the lantern had not been free. The ring had spent him to do it. The keeping-rite had drawn its price from the nearest well, and the nearest well was him.

He fell. The stone came up kind, at least. Through the leaning, groaning ruin of the windows he could see it now, unmistakable: the light he had freed rising in the east like a false dawn, bending toward the Hollow Gate, and beneath it the wards' last fragments flaring — failing — tearing wide.

Something was going to come through. Something had been waiting three years for exactly this width of an opening.

Wren's voice reached him from far below, raw with smoke: *"Casper! The gate — it's opening — CASPER!"*

He tried to answer. His mouth wouldn't shape her name. The ring burned colder, and colder, and through the ringing in his ears came another sound — thin, distant, impossibly dear — a woman's voice, singing through stone, growing stronger with every drop of light that reached her.

*Come,* the song said. *Keeper. Come and finish it.*

The floor gave way beneath Casper Ashgrove, and the dark came up to meet him.
