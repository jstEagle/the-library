## 16. Into the Hollow

The gate did not look like a gate anymore.

It had never looked like much — that was the joke Casper had told himself for three years, standing in the cellar beneath the ruined charm-shop with a lantern in one hand and a lie in the other: *all this dread, all this duty, for a door the size of a garden arch.* The stone was old Grayhallow limestone, sweat-dark, carved with keeping-rites worn nearly smooth by hands older than the city's name. It had been cracked since the night the stars went out, a seam of lightless black running from crown to threshold like a scar on a face.

Now it was open a hand's width, and what came through the gap was not light.

It was dark that moved. Dark with texture, like velvet held against the eye. It pooled at the threshold and breathed outward in slow tides, and where it touched the cellar floor, the dust did not scatter — it simply stopped being dust, or stopped mattering that it was.

Casper leaned against the wall and tried to decide whether he could stand.

The answer came back promptly, in the language his body had learned to speak over the past hour: *no.* The ring-burn had gone past pain into something quieter and far more honest. His right hand, the one wearing Aldous's covenant-ring, was a map drawn in blackened lines — the veins of his wrist, the back of his hand, the web between his fingers, all of them traced in charcolor, as if the magic in his blood had been set alight and burned down to the wick. When he flexed his fingers, the skin crackled faintly, like paper near a flame.

He could smell himself burning. Underneath the smoke there was something else, something almost sweet — the scent of a candle guttering out after years of faithful service.

"Say it again," Wren said.

She was crouched at the threshold with her back to him, which was how he knew she couldn't bear to look at him just now. She had a coil of chalk-line in her hands and was winding it, unwinding it, winding it again — the fidget of a woman whose hands needed work because her heart refused it. Beyond her shoulder, through the gap in the stone, the Hollow breathed its slow dark tide.

"You heard me."

"I heard you say something insane. Say it again so I can be sure I'm not concussed from that wall coming down."

Casper laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough halfway up his throat, and the cough tasted of cinders. He slid down the wall until he was sitting, which was less a decision than a surrender.

"I'm going through," he said. "You're staying here. You hold the city side. If the gate starts to close — if anything comes through that isn't her — you chalk the containment circle and you run the rites I taught you, and you don't stop for anything. Not for voices. Not for faces." He paused. "Not even mine."

Wren's hands went still around the chalk-line.

"That's the whole plan," she said. "That's the entire plan. Walk into the murder-dimension, alone, while dying, to find a woman who may not even—"

"She's alive." His voice came out flat and certain, and it surprised him, how certain. He had felt her through the ring since the tower fell — a faint pull behind his sternum, like a fisherman feeling the line go taut. Zephyrine, drawing breath somewhere on the other side of the dark. Starving, but breathing. "I'd know if she wasn't. The ring knows her. It knew her before it ever knew me."

"The ring is also eating you alive, Casper."

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

There was time, while Wren argued, to take inventory. Dying men are given that courtesy, he supposed — a last quiet ledger to keep.

Three years ago, Aldous Merrick had died in this same cellar, coughing blood onto the same flagstones, gripping Casper's wrist with the same desperate strength Casper now couldn't muster. *The gate needs two keepers,* the old man had rasped, pressing the ring onto Casper's finger. *Two voices. Remember — two voices, spoken together. There is a second keeper. Her name begins with Z.* And then he had died before he could say more, and Casper — twenty-four years old, terrified, outlawed for what he was — had done the cowardly arithmetic and decided that one keeper was enough, that silence was safer than seeking, that a man who sold luck-charms to nervous clerks need never find out what waited behind the arch.

Guilt was a country he had emigrated to; he knew all its provinces. But he had never known, until tonight, that guilt could also be a map. Every wrong turn he'd taken led here. Every year of hiding had been a year of Zephyrine alone in the dark, her starlight siphoned away to feed Hale's lantern, and the covenant fraying thread by thread because its two halves had never once spoken each other's names aloud.

Well. He was speaking hers now, silently, over and over, the way you'd hold a rope.

*Zephyrine. Zephyrine. Zephyrine.*

The ring pulled gently toward the gap, like a compass needle finding north. He would follow it until the needle broke.

"Casper." Wren had turned around. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious, which was Wren's way of grieving — she fought everything, including sorrow, on principle. "Listen to me. You can barely walk. You're going to step into a place made of *nothing* and hope your dead mentor's girlfriend finds you before whatever else lives in there does. That is not a plan. That is a suicide note with extra steps."

"It's a rescue," Casper said. "The distinction matters to me."

"And what happens when the ring finishes burning you out? Because that's hours, Casper, maybe less. You've poured everything through it — the tower, the lantern-light, the wards on the square. There's nothing left in you but kindling, and the ring is *hungry*—"

"Then I'd better be quick."

She stared at him. Outside — far above them, streets up — the city was still screaming. The wraith-surge Hale had loosed was loose still, pouring through broken wards into neighborhoods that had never asked to know such things existed. Bells were ringing out of order. Somewhere a building burned with a fire the color of bruises. Wren's brother was somewhere in all of that, freed an hour ago from the magistrate's cells, and every instinct in her was tearing toward him like a dog against a leash — and she was here anyway. She had stayed. That was the thing about Wren Thistle-down, the thing Casper had learned in archive shadows and undercity tunnels: she would complain about every single step of a sacrifice, loudly and at length, and then make it anyway.

"Fine," she said, in the voice of someone signing a terrible contract. "Fine. But we do this my way. Chalk first."

* * *

They drew the containment circle together, or rather, Wren drew it and Casper supervised from the floor like an invalid king, correcting her line-work with the pedantry of a man clinging to normalcy by his fingernails. Nine feet across, centered on the threshold. Three concentric rings. The keeping-rites at the cardinal points, their syllables written in Aldous's shorthand — a language of hooks and circles that looked like nothing and meant everything.

"If the gate tries to close," Casper said, "the circle holds the frame. It won't hold forever — chalk is chalk — but it'll buy you time to reinforce with iron filings. There's a jar under the—"

"I know where the jar is. I've robbed this cellar twice."

"Once. The second time I let you."

"The second time you *thought* you let me." She finished the outer ring and sat back on her heels, surveying her work with a smuggler's squint. Then, quietly, without looking at him: "How long do I wait? If you don't come back."

Casper considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty.

"Don't," he said. "Don't wait. Hold the gate, yes — that's the job, that's the covenant, two sides and a door between them. But don't wait for *me*, Wren. If I'm not back by the time the sun rises, seal the frame with iron and go find Tam. Drink something. Yell at some magistrates. Build the city you kept talking about, the fair one." He tried to smile. "You don't need me for that part. You never did."

"Shut up," Wren said thickly. Then she crossed the chalk, took his burned hand — carefully, so carefully, the way you handle something already broken — and pressed the covenant-ring once, hard, as if she could push courage into it through sheer contrariness. "Zeph will kick your ashes if you die on her doorstep. She seems like the type."

"She is absolutely the type."

"Good." She released him and stood, jaw set, eyes shining and defiant about it. "Go, then. Before I talk myself into tackling you."

* * *

Getting to his feet was the hardest physical act of Casper Ashgrove's life, and he had once carried a grand piano up four flights of stairs for reasons involving a bet and a woman named Odile.

He used the wall. He used the shelf bracket. He used, finally, the cold stone lip of the gate itself, and the moment his palm met the arch, the dark beyond it seemed to lean toward him — attentive, curious, almost tender. The Unlit knew he was coming. Of course it did. It had been wearing the faces of everyone he'd failed since the hour it learned his name.

"Wren," he said. "Two voices. Spoken together. Whatever happens on the other side — however bad it looks, however long the silence runs — remember that's the shape of the answer. It was always meant to be answered from both sides of the door."

"Go," Wren said, and her voice cracked only slightly, which for Wren was practically a eulogy.

Casper stepped through the gap in the stone.

* * *

Falling, but not downward. That was the first thing. The Hollow had directions the way a dream has furniture — present, insistent, and arranged according to no architecture any mason had approved. He fell sideways, or inward, through a dark that received him the way deep water receives a stone: without comment, closing over the place he'd been.

Then ground, of a kind. A plain of black glass stretching to no horizon, and above it — where a sky should be — a slow drift of shapes like drowned constellations, dim outlines of stars that had once been lit and longed to be again. The air was cool and tasted of snow that hadn't fallen yet. It was, and he hated himself for the thought, beautiful. This was the trick of the place, he understood immediately. The Hollow didn't hide its loveliness. It led with it.

Somewhere in that vast soft dark, something began to sing.

Not Zephyrine. He knew her voice now — three nights listening at the cracked gate had engraved it in him, a sound like struck crystal remembering sunlight. This was older and hungrier. It sang in a voice he had heard every day for eleven years, gravel-warm and patient, and it sang Aldous's favorite working song, the one about the miller's daughter, and Casper's whole body went rigid with grief before his mind caught up and named the trap.

"Not him," he said aloud, and walked on.

The dark obliged. The song changed — became his mother's voice, singing the lullaby from before; became the nervous young clerk from the market, three years and a lifetime ago, saying *thank you, thank you, sir, you're the only one who was kind* right before the wardens found her charm and took her away in a wagon with no windows. Casper had watched from his stall and done nothing, hidden behind his harmless face, and here was the price of that doing-nothing, walking toward him across black glass with the clerk's small apologetic smile.

"You could have run," the clerk's face said, in a voice that wasn't hers, that was wind through an empty house. "You could have shouted. You chose the stall. You chose the coin."

"I know," Casper said. His voice came out steady, which astonished him. Perhaps dying simplifies things; perhaps there isn't room in a burning man for both fear and honesty, so honesty wins by default. "I know exactly what I chose. I'm not arguing with you. I'm not here for me."

The figure tilted its head — the clerk's head, wearing an expression the clerk had never made, a hunger looking out through borrowed features with something almost like curiosity. Behind it, more figures were assembling out of the dark, stepping forward as if from a crowd that had always been there: Aldous, mid-cough. Odile, from the piano, whom he'd abandoned in a foreign city with a forged apology. His mother, young, disappointed in ways she'd never once said aloud. Every face was a ledger entry. Every face was a debt he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for bone.

"We could give them back to you," the Unlit said, with all of their voices braided into one. "We hold everything that was renounced. Every spell unspoken, every gift outlawed, every keeper's power poured away. We are made of the given-up things, little gatekeeper. We could fill your empty hands. We could put the fire back in you — more fire than you ever had — and you could walk out of here whole, and burn bright, and no one in Grayhallow would ever again tell you what you're not allowed to be."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Casper would carry out of this dark or not at all: for one full heartbeat, he wanted it. The offer fit the hole in him perfectly. Three years of playing small, of selling trinkets and swallowing his own nature, of watching Zephyrine starve through a crack in a wall while he counted coins — of course some starving animal in him leapt at the promise of never being small again.

But he had seen Hale. He had seen what a man looked like after drinking stolen sky — half-transfigured, magnificent, and hollow-eyed with appetite, offering godhood with one hand and holding chains in the other. Hale had swallowed a lantern's worth of light and was still hungry. Hunger like that doesn't fill. It only grows a bigger mouth.

"No," Casper said. "Thank you. Sincerely — that was well made, all of it. But I've met your best customer already."

The assembled faces flickered. Something vast shifted in the deep dark, the way a whale shifts beneath a boat — not anger, exactly. Reassessment.

"Then you have come to banish us," the Unlit said, and now the voices flattened, all the warmth draining out of the braid. "As they did. As the covenant did. Everything unwanted, thrown through the door and barred behind it. We were patient. We are very good at patience. But we remember being thrown."

"No," Casper said again, softer. "I've come to change the terms."

* * *

He didn't know if that was true. He knew only that it had become true the moment he said it — the way a vow becomes real in the speaking, the way the ring on his hand warmed at the word *terms* like a hound hearing its name. He walked on across the black glass, past the fading faces of his failures, following the tug behind his sternum, following the thin crystal thread of a song that was growing louder and nearer and brighter in a place where brightness shouldn't live.

The ring-burn climbed his arm as he walked. It reached his elbow, then his shoulder, a slow black ivy of spent fire, and with every step he felt pieces of himself going quiet — the memory of a particular summer, the ability to do sums quickly, the taste of pears. Small magics, all of them; a life is mostly small magic. He let them go. There was a strange liberty in it, once the terror crested: he was a candle burning down to nothing, and nothing is a perfectly clean fuel.

The dark ahead began to thin — not to light, but to a different quality of dark. Warmer. Attended. And at its center, suspended in the velvet nothing like a lamp in a deep well, was a woman made of dusk and ember, her hair a spill of cooling sparks, her bare feet a hand-span above the glass. She was thinner than the visions the ring had shown him. Dimmer. Three years of starvation had drawn her down to her essential lines, the way a sketch artist works. But her eyes, when they found him across the distance —

Her eyes were still lit. Two small stubborn stars refusing the general outage.

She looked at him, and Casper Ashgrove, who had walked calmly past every face of his own failure, felt his knees finally give — not from the ring-burn at all, but from the simple unbearable weight of being recognized.

"You came," Zephyrine said. Her voice was the song and the song was her voice, and it cracked on the second word like ice on a pond in spring. "All this time, I told myself the gate-keeper on the other side was a story I'd invented to stay sane. A kind fiction. And you—" She drifted closer, close enough that he could feel her warmth, faint and banked, like a hearth in a far room. Close enough to see that she was crying, and that her tears were tiny points of light that fell upward. "You listened. For three nights you put your hand on the stone and *listened*, and I felt it like weather."

"I'm sorry it took three years."

"I'm sorry it took you three nights," she said, and something in her smile suggested she meant: *I forgive you all of it, the hiding, the fear, the years — provided you never do it again.*

Behind her, far off in the deep of the Hollow, the Unlit gathered itself — vast, patient, reconsidering — and Casper felt the ring give one final pulse against his ruined finger, like a heart deciding whether to beat again.

Zephyrine saw it too. Her gaze dropped to his blackened hand, and all the light in her went suddenly, terribly still.

"What have you spent," she whispered. "Keeper. What have you *spent*?"

"Everything," Casper said, smiling, swaying, falling toward her arms and the dark together. "But I brought good news. The terms are negotiable."
