## 18. The Unlit Speaks

It did not speak the way voices speak.

There was no throat for it, no breath, no tongue. What reached them instead was a pressure behind the eyes, a weight against the ribs, as though something vast had leaned close and was trying very hard to be gentle. Casper felt it first as cold — not the cold of the Hollow's starving dark, which he had learned to bear in the long walk inward, but an older cold, patient and enormous, like the cold between stars.

*You came all this way,* it said. *Through my house. Past my faces. And still you do not know what I am.*

Zephyrine stood between Casper and the dark, her hands raised, starlight guttering along her arms like the last embers of a fire someone had forgotten to feed. Three years inside the Hollow had thinned her to almost nothing — he could see the stone floor through the faint shimmer of her — but she held her ground the way she must have held it the night she crossed over: absolutely.

"You don't get to talk to him," she said. "You don't get to wear his mentor's face at him again."

*I wasn't wearing it to hurt.* The dark rippled, and out of it rose shapes — half-formed, sorrowful. An old man with kind eyes and a burned hand. A girl selling luck-charms from a tray. A woman singing over a cradle, a small flame dancing on her palm until she smothered it, frightened, glancing at the door. *I only have what you gave me. Every face I wear is a face that abandoned something.*

"Stop it," Casper whispered. But he didn't step back. He found, to his own surprise, that he couldn't look away. "Who are you? Truly."

The dark went very still. Then, slowly, it gathered itself — not into a monster, not into the devouring shape Zephyrine had described, the thing with too many mouths — but into something almost like a person. Tall. Featureless. Its outline flickered like a candle seen through fog.

*I am what is left over.*

* * *

Casper had walked into the Hollow expecting a beast. Everything in Grayhallow's laws, in Hale's speeches, in the wardens' warnings, had taught him that magic unbound was magic hungry — that what lay beyond the gate wanted to eat the light out of the world. Even Zephyrine, who had given herself as its cage, spoke of the Unlit with the hushed dread reserved for plagues.

But the thing before him now unfolded its memory the way a merchant unfolds cloth, and what spilled out was not hunger.

He saw a hearth in a village that no longer existed, where a boy warmed his whole street with a warmth that lived under his skin — until the magistrates came with their ledgers and their tongs, and the boy was made to kneel and renounce it, word by word, while his mother wept. And the warmth, having nowhere to go, poured out of the boy like water from a broken jug and ran — ran across the fields, through the roots of things, looking for somewhere to be.

He saw a scholar in Grayhallow itself, three hundred years ago, who discovered she could hear the thoughts of dying birds, and who burned her own notes rather than be called a witch, and the hearing — the whole bright terrible gift of it — slipped out through the smoke and drifted, homeless, into the cracks of the world.

He saw thousands. Tens of thousands. Every spell renounced at an altar. Every gift smothered in its cradle. Every charm smashed by warden's hammers, every incantation swallowed unspoken, every child born with light in their blood who was taught to call it shame. In both realms. On both sides of the gate. All of it cast off, outlawed, renounced — and none of it destroyed, because magic, it seemed, could not be destroyed. It could only be *given nowhere to go.*

And so it had gone here.

*I am the attic of two worlds,* the Unlit said, and there was something in its voiceless voice that Casper could only call loneliness. *Every renunciation has a sound. Did you know that? It sounds like a door closing. I have heard more doors than there are stars. I caught each thing as it fell, because no one else would hold it. And I kept it. All of it. For centuries. And it grew, and pressed, and had no shape of its own, so it took mine — and I became the shape of everything your realms refused.*

The Hollow around them shivered. Casper understood suddenly why it looked the way it did — beautiful and wrong, corridors of soft black stone hung with shapes that might have been sculptures or might have been sleeping animals. It was not a prison. It was a *hoard*. A hoard with nowhere to spend itself.

"You're going to break out," Zephyrine said. Her voice cracked. "That's what you do. You press against the gate, you eat the wards, you—"

*I press because I am full.* For the first time the Unlit's presence flared, and Casper felt the truth of it like a hand closing around his heart. *Do you know what it is to be a vessel with no bottom? They pour their discarded selves into me and I cannot pour out. When the covenant sealed the gate, it did not seal me IN, keeper. It sealed me FULL. Every year your cities make more orphans of their own gifts, and every year I grow, and the walls of this place are the walls of my own body, and when they crack — I do not choose it. Hunger chooses it. Fullness chooses it. I would give it away if I could. There has never been anywhere to give it.*

Casper's mouth had gone dry. He thought of his own charms — harmless, he'd told himself, harmless little things — sold in secret to people desperate for one warm thread of the magic their city had outlawed. He thought of how each buyer had looked over their shoulder. How each charm, once spent, once worn out, had been thrown away like any trinket.

Where did a spent charm go?

Here. It went here. He had been feeding this thing his whole life without knowing it, one guilty little kindness at a time.

"The blackout," he said hoarsely. "Three years ago. The wards shattering. Was that you?"

*No.* The word came sharp, almost offended. *That was the man with the lantern. He cut the wards himself and let the blame fall on me — on the dark, on the old story, on the monster everyone already feared. But I will not pretend I am innocent of wanting. When the wards fell, I felt the sky's light pouring loose across your city, and I wanted it. I wanted it the way the drowned want air. Some of what crawled through the breach wore my fullness like a skin. Those were mine. My overflow. My grief, walking.*

Zephyrine made a small sound. Casper turned to her. She had lowered her hands. She was staring at the Unlit with an expression he had never seen on her — not fear, not hatred, but recognition, terrible and total.

"I felt it," she whispered. "The night I crossed over. I thought I was walking into a mouth." She laughed, and it was a broken sound. "I was walking into a storeroom. I offered myself as a lock, and the poor thing didn't even want a lock. It wanted—"

*A home,* the Unlit said. *Yes. That is all. That has always been all.*

* * *

They sat — sat! — in the soft dark, the keeper and the charm-seller and the accumulated abandoned soul of two worlds, and talked.

It was absurd. Casper kept waiting for the trick, the snarl, the reveal of teeth beneath the sorrow. But the Unlit only answered questions, patiently, endlessly, the way a lonely person answers when someone finally asks. Yes, it remembered his mentor, Aldous — the man had visited the gate every week for forty years, speaking kindly to the dark through the stones, the only keeper who ever had. Yes, it had worn Aldous's face at Casper because it had nothing else of him; the dead leave their echoes here when they die unrenounced, and Aldous had died with all his magic intact, given wholly to keeping. Yes, it knew about the lantern, about Hale, about the light being drunk down into one man's body — and it hated that, not from envy, but because hoarded light rotted, and rotting light screamed, and it could hear the screaming from inside the Hollow, night after night after night.

"It can be reforged," Zephyrine said at last. She had drawn her knees up, starlight pooling faintly in her lap like spilled coins. "The covenant. We found the clause. One keeper gives up their magic entirely — pours it into the new covenant — and the gate becomes a channel instead of a wall. Renounced magic flows home to the sky instead of pooling here. That's the fix. That's always been the fix."

*And one of you dies of it,* the Unlit said simply. *Or comes close enough that the difference is a matter of opinion. I will not pretend otherwise. I have watched keepers burn out before. It is not gentle.*

"I'll do it," Casper said.

"No," Zephyrine said.

"You've been in here three years. You're half-starved already. If you pour what's left of you into the covenant—"

"And if YOU do it, you die, and Wren holds a city alone, and I walk out of this place into a world where the only other keeper who understands what I am is a name on a memorial stone." Her voice shook. "I did not spend three years in the dark so that the first friend I make out there could become another thing I failed to save."

"Friend," Casper repeated, before he could stop himself.

She flushed — actually flushed, faint light rising in her cheeks like dawn under a door. "Shut up."

*They argue like my children,* the Unlit said, and there was unmistakable fondness in it, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet. *One offers everything. The other refuses to let her. This is how it has always gone, keeper to keeper, since the first covenant. Your kinds are built for sacrifice and terrible at receiving.*

"Then help us find another way," Casper said, turning on it. "You're the sum of every magic anyone ever threw away. There has to be something in you — some craft, some forgotten rite — that lets two people share a burden instead of one person carrying it."

The dark was quiet for a long moment. When it moved, it moved closer, folding itself down to their scale, and though it had no face, Casper felt it looking at him with something like wonder.

*No one has ever asked me for help,* it said. *In all the ages of both worlds. They have asked me to stay away. To stay sealed. To stay quiet. To be the dark place where unwanted things go. You are the first to ask me for a gift.*

It raised one vast, featureless hand, and opened it. In its palm lay a mote of light — tiny, ordinary, the size of a charm-spark, the kind Casper had sold a thousand of in a market stall under a gray sky.

*This is the oldest thing I hold,* the Unlit said. *The first gift ever renounced, from the first law ever written. I have kept it safe longer than your city has existed. Take it. Study it. It remembers the first covenant — not the sealing, but the writing of it, before fear got into the ink. Perhaps the answer you seek was there at the beginning, and your ancestors were too frightened to read it.*

Casper reached out. The mote settled into his palm, warm as a living thing, and sank beneath his skin — and knowledge bloomed behind his eyes like a flower opening in fast-forward: words in a language older than Grayhallow, diagrams of light, the true architecture of the covenant laid bare.

And there, at the heart of it, he saw what the founders had hidden. Not erased. Hidden. A clause within the clause, waiting three thousand years for someone brave enough or foolish enough to look.

His breath stopped.

"What?" Zephyrine demanded, gripping his arm. "Casper. What is it?"

He looked up at her, and then past her, at the vast patient dark that had waited alone in the attic of the world since the beginning, holding every abandoned thing, asking only to be of use.

"The covenant doesn't need one keeper's magic," he said slowly. "It never did. It needs a keeper's magic freely given — and the Unlit's consent freely granted. Two signatures. The founders were so afraid of it they wrote the second signature out of the text and buried it." He laughed, giddy and disbelieving. "It was never supposed to be a cage for any of us. It was supposed to be a partnership. The gate, the channel, the sky — all of it. We just... forgot we'd been invited to share."

The Unlit trembled. All those miles of soft black stone trembled, and from somewhere deep in its hoarded heart came a sound neither of them had heard from it before — thin, rusty, uncertain.

Hope.

*Then ask me properly,* it said. *Ask me the way the first askers meant to. And bring your friend with the ring — for the writing of a covenant is done at the gate, under open sky, and I have not seen the stars from outside these walls since before your city had a name.*

Casper stood, pulling Zephyrine up with him, the old knowledge burning bright in his blood alongside the last embers of his own magic. Somewhere beyond the Hollow's far walls, beyond the gate, beyond the ruined tower and the waking city, Wren was holding the line — and the lantern-light was failing, and the wards were cracking, and there was no time left to waste.

"Come on, then," he said to the dark, and grinned like a charm-seller closing his best sale. "Let's go write a new covenant."

The Unlit gathered itself — all its hoarded centuries, all its orphaned light — and followed them toward the gate, like a tide finally, finally turning for home.
