## 19. A New Covenant

The gate looked different from this side.

Casper had known it for three years as a thing of Grayhallow make — mortared stone, iron bands, wards laid in his mentor's careful hand, all of it built like a wall against something dreaded. From within the Hollow, it was none of those things. It was a doorway without a door, a tall arch of pale nothing standing in a dark that moved around it like water around a ship's hull, and every crack in its surface glowed faintly with the last dregs of the covenant's failing light.

The Unlit followed them, as it had promised — or threatened; Casper had stopped trying to translate the difference. It came across the Hollow's dark plains not as the devouring shape it had worn in his visions but as something almost humble: a slow accumulation, a tide of gathered shadow carrying within it points of light like fish beneath night water. Every renounced spell. Every outlawed gift. Three centuries of Grayhallow's fear, given nowhere to go.

*It wants a home,* Zephyrine had said. *It has never wanted anything else.*

"Tell me again," Casper said, because his hands were shaking and speech was the only thing he knew to do with fear. He had sold charms in a city that hated magic long enough to know that a lie repeated calmly starts to feel true. "Tell me the old covenant fails tonight."

"It is already failing." Zephyrine stood before the arch with her back to him, and even starved as she was — hollowed by three years in this place, her starlight down to embers — she filled the dark the way a bell fills a silence. "Look at it. The words were written to hold the Unlit *in*. A lock on one side only. But the Unlit was never made to be kept, Casper. It was made to be carried. Renounced magic must go somewhere, or it goes mad. We gave it a prison and called it mercy."

"And the new covenant?"

She turned. Her eyes had once held constellations; he remembered that from the first time she'd sung through the stone. Now they held only two small stubborn fires, and both of them were fixed on him.

"The new covenant opens the gate," she said. "Not wide — controlled. A channel, a tide-gate. Everything the realms refuse flows through, and we give it back to the sky. The Unlit becomes the stars again. That is all it ever was, before we taught it to hunger."

"That's all," Casper said. His laugh came out cracked. "You say it like rewriting a three-hundred-year-old binding between realms is a Tuesday errand."

"It requires two keepers." She crossed to him, and her cold hand found his ring-hand, closed over the covenant-ring burning there. "Two voices, spoken together. That part never changes. The words change. The words are ours to write."

He looked down at their joined hands. Hers, pale and dimming. His, human and ordinary except for the ring, which pulsed with a light he now understood too well — because he'd seen what it fed on. Not ward-light. Not the Hollow's dark. Himself. Every keeper before him had poured their own magic into the covenant until there was nothing left, and the ring had drunk it down and asked for more.

*Using it will burn you out,* the visions had said. He had accepted that price on the far side of the tower, dying already, and it had seemed simple then. It seemed less simple now, with Zephyrine's fingers laced through his and her hope resting on him like a physical weight.

"There's another way," he said. "There has to be. Hale poured a whole lantern into himself—"

"Hale stole power that was never his. It is tearing him apart from the inside, and it will kill him, and he will deserve it." No softness in her voice. Three years in a cage buys a person the right to be ungentle about certain things. "But you would not survive stealing either, and worse — it wouldn't work. The covenant doesn't take borrowed strength. It takes everything, freely given. That's why the old binding holds at all. Aldous didn't seal it with skill, Casper. He sealed it with himself."

His mentor's name landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He thought of Aldous Ashgrove dying in a rented room with the smell of lamp-oil and blood, pressing the ring into Casper's palm, saying *keep it shut, keep it shut, keep it shut* — and Casper had heard a duty and fled from it, spent three years selling luck-charms and pretending the burning on his finger was only old scar tissue.

Aldous hadn't meant *keep it shut forever.* Casper understood that now. He'd meant *keep it shut until you understand what you're keeping.*

"I know," Zephyrine said quietly. She always knew, lately, what he was thinking. Listening to someone for three years through a wall does that; being listened to does the rest. "I watched him do it. I was already inside when the gate closed. I sang the sealing-words with him while the Unlit pressed against the stone, and I felt him pour out, and I swore I would find whoever came after and never ask the same of them." Her thumb traced the ring's band. "And here I am, asking."

"You're not asking."

"I'm telling you the truth and letting you choose. It's harder."

* * *

They wrote the covenant in the space before the arch, because words spoken in the Hollow did not need ink — they needed intent, and grief, and names.

Zephyrine taught him the grammar of it as the Unlit drew near enough that its presence became weather, a pressure on the skin, a taste of cold iron and old rain. The old tongue of keepers had no word for *banish*, she said, because the people who first spoke it understood that nothing truly leaves; it only changes where it lives. There were words for *open*, and *channel*, and *return*, and a word that took Casper an hour of practice to shape properly, a word that meant roughly *what was refused comes home forgiven.*

"Say it again," she said.

"*Vael-durath en kelleth.*"

"Better. You're strangling the second syllable. It's not a punishment, it's an invitation."

"Easy for you to say. Your mouth was literally forged in heaven."

"My mouth," she said, with the ghost of the dry humor that had survived three years of imprisonment the way a seed survives winter, "was forged in the same place yours was, whatever your city likes to believe about star-born. We only sound different because no one has outlawed our language yet."

He loved her then, suddenly and completely, in the plain unglamorous way he had been falling into for weeks — over shared jokes in the dark, over her fierce mind, over the fact that imprisonment had bent her and not broken her and she had come out of the cage still capable of teasing a dying charm-seller about his pronunciation. He didn't say it. There would be time, if there was a afterward. If there wasn't, saying it would only be a weapon she'd have to carry alone.

The Unlit arrived.

It did not loom, though it could have swallowed the arch and both of them a thousand times over. It settled. It pooled around the doorway like a congregation, and up close Casper could finally see what he had refused to see in his visions: the lights inside it weren't random. They were shapes. Spells, half-finished, abandoned mid-cast by frightened children in Grayhallow's purges. Blessings whispered over doorways and then scrubbed away. A healing that someone had refused out of shame. A hundred years of small magics, outlawed and unmade and given nowhere to go, circling and circling in the dark looking for a sky.

One of the lights drifted close and hung before his face, and it resolved into something he recognized — a luck-charm, the exact weave of it, one of his own, sold to some clerk or washerwoman and then renounced in terror when the sweeps began. His own work, orphaned. It pulsed at him like a question.

"Yeah," Casper said softly. "I know. I'm sorry. Let's get you home."

"Casper." Zephyrine's voice had gone very steady, the way voices do at cliff-edges. "It's time. Do you understand what you're about to do? Truly? Not the shape of it — the substance?"

"I give you my magic. All of it. What's left."

"All of it. And it will not come back. You'll be as any other man in Grayhallow. Worse — you'll feel its absence every hour of your life, because you'll remember what it was to have it." Her hands gripped his. "I can do this alone. I am already half-spent; the covenant will simply take the rest. One keeper giving everything is the same as two. You could walk out of here whole. You could have a life."

"That's the argument you've been rehearsing since the tower," he said. "I heard you practicing under your breath."

"It is a good argument."

"It's a coward's argument wearing a martyr's coat, and we both know it, because I could say the exact same thing back to you and you'd refuse faster than a magistrate refusing a bribe." He turned his hand over so the ring faced up between them, blazing now, answering the proximity of the ritual like a struck tuning fork. "Two voices, spoken together. That part never changes. You told me."

"If you die—"

"Then you'll bring me back to life out of sheer spite. I've seen how you are about losing arguments."

For a moment — one raw, suspended moment — she laughed, and the sound went up through the Hollow's dark like a crack through ice, and everywhere it passed, the trapped lights in the Unlit flickered brighter, as if laughter itself were a kind of starlight.

Then she drew herself up, and he drew himself up, and they faced the arch together with the tide of the refused gathered all around them, and began.

* * *

The old covenant had taken Aldous a single night and a single scream. The new one took everything Casper had, and it took it slowly.

The words were simple — that was the terrible elegance of it. *What was refused comes home forgiven. The gate stands open; the channel runs clear; the sky receives its own.* Simple enough that a child could speak them. But each phrase had to be carried on a keeper's own light, fed into the stones of the arch like wicks fed into oil, and the ring drank from him with every syllable, and what it drank it poured into the doorway, and the doorway grew bright, and Casper grew dim.

He felt it leave in layers, the way warmth leaves a body.

First went the small things — the trickles he'd never valued, the little knacks that had let him warm a charm with a touch, sweeten a bad bargain, sense a ward's health like one senses weather. Then the deeper veins: the gatekeeper's strength Aldous had seeded in him, the quiet hum of power he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for personality. Each layer torn away left something colder underneath, and he kept speaking, and Zephyrine spoke with him, her voice threading his like a second current in a river, and where their voices crossed, the arch blazed.

The Unlit came to the threshold.

It rose up on the far side of the opening gate like a wave deciding, and Casper saw every face it had ever worn in his visions — his mentor's, Wren's brother's, his own — dissolve as it passed through the channel, because those faces had only ever been questions, and now it had its answer. It flowed through the arch the way light flows through glass, and as it crossed the boundary the covenant caught it, and turned it, and gave it upward.

The first star ignited above Grayhallow before the ritual was half done. Casper felt it happen through the stones — a distant *strike* like a flint meeting steel, somewhere impossibly high — and then another, and another, the returned magic pouring up through the opened channel and catching fire in the high dark, and the Hollow itself began to glow at its edges like the rim of a world at dawn.

"Keep speaking," Zephyrine said. Her voice was fraying now too; the covenant was drinking them both. "Almost — the last clause —"

*The keepers give freely,* the final words went, *and holding nothing, hold the door.*

Holding nothing. He understood the clause exactly one heartbeat before it took effect, and he had one heartbeat to be afraid, and then he chose not to be, and spoke it anyway.

The ring flared white.

It didn't hurt, precisely. It was more thorough than pain. It reached into every place his magic had ever lived and folded them flat, the way a map folds, the way a tent falls when the pole is drawn. Three years of hiding, three years of denial, the inheritance he'd fled and the duty he'd run from and the gift he'd never asked for — all of it drawn out through the ring in a single long exhalation, given to the door, given to the sky, given freely, and for one vertiginous moment Casper Ashgrove was nothing at all, a man-shaped absence standing in a blazing archway, empty as an unfired lamp.

And then Zephyrine caught him.

Her arms came around him as he fell, and she lowered them both to the threshold stones, and she was crying and laughing at once, and he was too spent to do either, so he just breathed, and the breath was astonishing — air, plain air, the most miraculous thing he had ever tasted.

Above them, through the open gate, the sky over Grayhallow was filling with stars.

Not the old stars. These were new — hundreds of them, thousands, the returned and forgiven light of a century of renounced spells finding their places in the dark, arranging themselves into constellations no astronomer had ever named. And among them, brightest of all, a figure of light stood at the channel's crown: Zephyrine's stolen radiance, the divinity the lantern had drained and the covenant had reclaimed, pouring itself upward into the sky she had been carved from.

She watched it go without reaching after it. When it finished — when the last thread of her star-nature rose through the gate and hung there, blazing, among its recovered kin — she was only a woman kneeling on the stones, dark-haired, breathing hard, thinner than memory and mortal as bread.

"Oh," she said, very small, staring at her own hands. "Oh, it's *quiet*."

"Is it bad?" Casper managed. "The quiet?"

She considered this with the gravity of someone cataloguing an entirely new element. Then she lay down beside him on the threshold, shoulder to shoulder, and looked up through the gate at the sky she had reforged, and said:

"No. It's the first silence I've had in three hundred years that isn't lonely." She found his hand — both of them empty now, both of them ringless, the covenant-ring lying spent and gray as ash between them like a snake's discarded skin. "Thank you. For refusing to walk out whole."

"Thank you for refusing to let me do it alone." He turned his head to look at her. Up close, in starlight instead of starlight, she was just a tired, beautiful, impossible person, and he grinned at her the way he'd grinned at the dark an hour and a lifetime ago. "So. Two keepers. One powerless charm-seller, one former goddess. Any thoughts on how we explain this to the city?"

Zephyrine opened her mouth to answer—

—and the arch shuddered.

Not from within. From beyond. From the Grayhallow side, where the wards should have been settling into their new channel, where Wren was holding the line — came a sound Casper knew from his worst nights at the market stalls, a sound like the sky cracking along a seam: the deep, rolling concussion of the tower lantern detonating, followed by a rising murmur of ten thousand voices, and threaded through it, thin and high and unmistakable, a woman's voice screaming his name through the open gate.

"WREN—" 

He tried to stand and his legs folded like wet paper; he had nothing left, less than nothing, a man with no magic and barely a body. Zephyrine was already on her feet, mortal and furious and lit by ten thousand new stars, staring through the arch at the smoke beginning to boil over the ruined skyline of Grayhallow.

"Casper," she said, and hauled him up with both hands, and braced his arm across her shoulders. "Tell me everything about your city. Fast. Because unless I miss my guess — we're about to walk into it."
