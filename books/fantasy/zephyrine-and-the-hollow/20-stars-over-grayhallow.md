## 20. Stars Over Grayhallow

Wren had counted the bells her whole life. Bells meant curfew, bells meant sweeps, bells meant the knock on the door that came at the hour honest people pretended to be asleep. So when the great cracked bell of Hale's tower — the one that had rung out arrests and edicts for three years — gave a single broken toll and then split down its seam with a sound like a struck tooth, she felt something in her own chest crack open in sympathy.

She was crouched behind a barricade of overturned market carts two streets from the Lantern Tower, and she was holding her breath.

Around her, the undercity held its breath too.

They'd come up out of the drains and cellars in their hundreds — charm-sellers with singed sleeves, smugglers still wearing the grime of the tunnels, washerwomen who'd hidden a cousin's hex-bag under a floorboard, dockhands, lamplighters' orphans, an old woman who sold ward-ribbons off a tray like they were ribbons and nothing more. Wren had spent three weeks calling them together, spending every favor she'd ever banked, trading secrets she'd sworn to keep, and now all of it — all of them — stood in the smoke-choked dark of Grayhallow's richest quarter, looking up at a tower that was coming apart at the seams with light.

Not lantern-light. Not the sickly amber of Hale's hoard, which had always looked to Wren like butter scraped over rot.

This light was *moving*. It poured from the windows of the tower in slow rivers, silver-blue and alive, and where it touched the barricades, the cobblestones, the faces of the crowd, it left a kind of hush behind — the feeling of snowfall, of held notes, of her grandmother humming over a half-finished charm.

"Stars," said Tam, beside her. Her brother. Three weeks in a magistrate's cell, one week free, still thin as a winter branch, his voice gone rusty. "Wren. Look at the sky."

She looked.

The stars were going out again — no. No, she corrected herself even as her stomach dropped. They were going *in*. One by one, in the east first, above the rooftops where the Hollow Gate lay buried beneath the old shrine district, points of light bloomed like ink dropped into water spreading backward. A star where there had been nothing but black since the night the wards shattered. Another. Another. They didn't scatter across the sky the way stars used to; they gathered, thickening into rivers, into constellations Wren half-remembered from her grandmother's stories and had never once seen with her own eyes — the Lantern-Bearer, the Two Sisters, the long pale sweep the old keepers called the Zephyr's Wake.

"They're coming back," someone said behind her, and the words went through the crowd like current through wire.

*The stars are coming back.*

Wren didn't cheer. She couldn't. Because she knew what the light was, and she knew who was paying for it, and Casper Ashgrove — stubborn, guilt-ridden, infuriatingly gentle Casper, who had taught a room full of outlaws the keeping-rites with chalk on a cellar floor — had walked through a gate into the dark three hours ago and not come back out.

* * *

It had started badly and gotten worse, which was how most nights went now.

The assault on the tower had been Wren's plan, which meant that when it went wrong, it went wrong in ways only she could fully blame herself for. The ladders against the eastern wall had burned. The wraiths had come through the failing wards in numbers nobody had predicted — dozens, scores, hollow-eyed things wearing borrowed voices, and half her people had frozen at the sound of dead loved ones calling their names from inside those empty faces. Wren had watched a dockhand named Sorrel walk straight toward a wraith because it spoke in his drowned daughter's voice, and she'd had to tackle him bodily to stop him, and by the time she'd gotten him behind cover the tower door had been sealed with light and there was nothing to do but trust Casper to do what he'd promised.

*I'll reach the lantern,* he'd said, in the cellar, in the chalk-light, with the ring burning cold on his hand. *You get the prisoners out. That's your half. Don't come after me.*

*You don't get to give me orders,* she'd told him.

*I know.* And he'd smiled — the small, tired smile of a man who had already decided how he was going to die and found it a relief — *but I'm asking anyway.*

So she'd done her half. She'd picked Hale's prison locks with her eyes streaming from the ward-smoke, and she'd led forty-one people out of cells that smelled of rust and fear, and she'd heard the moment the lantern broke — everyone in the city must have heard it, a soundless detonation, a pressure change, like the whole world inhaling — and she'd seen Hale fall.

That was the part the songs would get wrong later, Wren thought. The songs would say the undercity brought him down, and maybe in the ledger of things that mattered, they had. But what actually happened was smaller and stranger: Corvane Hale had climbed to the top of his own tower to drink the sky, and the sky had declined to be drunk. When Casper shattered the lantern, all that stolen ward-light — three years of harvested magic, the wards themselves, the border-spells, the small bright workings of every charm-seller Hale's men had ever confiscated and burned — all of it had tried to go home. And Hale had been standing in its way, full to bursting, a cup trying to hold a river.

He'd come apart slowly, at first. From the street, through the tower's blown-open doors, Wren had seen him silhouetted against the flood — a tall figure in magistrate grey, arms spread, mouth moving, though no one would ever know if he was cursing or praying or reciting the law. The light moved *through* him instead of around him. His shadow burned away first. Then the grey of his coat turned translucent, then gold, then nothing, and Corvane Hale — who had outlawed magic in order to monopolize it, who had put out the stars so he could be the only light left — dissolved upward into the very thing he'd tried to own, one last contribution to a sky he'd never intended to share.

His body hit the tower steps a moment later. Just a man. Just bones and ambition and an empty coat.

Nobody cheered then, either. There was a silence over the whole quarter, deep as well-water, and in it Wren heard — faintly, impossibly, from the direction of the old shrine district — singing.

A woman's voice, high and clear and starved-sounding even at this distance, rising up through the stones like water finding a crack.

And then the stars began to come back.

* * *

Now the crowd was growing. That was the thing that made Wren's hands shake, watching from the barricade: not the stars, not the falling magistrate, but the *people*.

They came out of houses that had been shut tight for three years. They came down from the hill districts where the law lived, in their nightclothes, holding candles they hadn't dared light indoors. They came from the temple rows and the clerk quarters and — this was the part that undid her — from the watch itself. Watchmen in grey, the same grey as Hale's coat, walking without weapons among the crowd, looking up at the sky with the naked wonder of children. One of them — Wren recognized him, Vess, the one who'd arrested her brother, the one whose face she'd memorized for reasons she'd told herself were practical — stood ten feet from her stall-side manner and her knife-honed tongue and said, quietly, to no one:

"I didn't know it could look like that."

"You did," Wren said, before she could stop herself.

He turned. He saw her — the smuggler, the criminal, the charm-dealer whose face had been on the proclamations since the sweeps began — and Wren braced for it, the shout, the grab, the old arithmetic of the city where her kind were worth coin dead or chained.

Vess looked at her for a long moment. Then he took off his watch-cap, which was as close to kneeling as a Grayhallow watchman got, and said, "Is it true? Did you do this?"

"We did," Wren said. "All of us. Even you, probably, in some way I haven't figured out how to forgive yet."

He nodded like she'd given him a sentence rather than an absolution, and put the cap back on, and turned to help an old woman over the barricade rubble. It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't justice. But it was a door opening a crack, and Wren had built her whole life on knowing which cracks a person could fit through.

By the time the eastern sky was full — truly *full*, rivering with light, the Hollow Gate's district glowing like dawn that had arrived from underneath — the crowd had stopped being a crowd of spectators. Charm-sellers who'd been hunted three weeks ago were showing nervous clerks how a luck-charm sat warm in the palm. The ribbon-seller was doing brisk trade in ward-ribbons, openly, on a main street, laughing. Someone had started singing one of the forbidden keeping-hymns — the one about the two voices — and instead of arresting the singer, half the crowd was singing along, badly, tearfully, word-garbled, and the song rose up toward the new stars like smoke from a chimney that finally had a fire behind it.

Tam leaned against her shoulder. "Gran would have hated missing this."

"She wouldn't have missed it." Wren nodded at the sky, at the gate-district glow. "She'd have been down in that tunnel with a crowbar. She'd have been insufferable about it forever."

"She'd have said she told you so."

"Every day for the rest of my life." Wren laughed, and it came out wet, and she let it. "Yeah."

But the laugh died in her, the way laughter does when it brushes up against the thing you've been not-thinking-about all night. She looked east. The singing had stopped some minutes ago. The light still poured, steady, generous — the covenant reforged, Casper had explained it once in his patient scholar's way, the renounced magic going home through an open channel — but the voice that had sung through the stone, the star-born keeper, Zephyrine, had gone quiet, and Casper Ashgrove had walked into the Hollow dying of ring-burn, and no one had come out.

The gate had been quiet for an hour. Too quiet. Wren knew gates the way she knew locks, and a lock that has just been opened doesn't sit silent unless either everything is fine or everything is finished.

"We should go down there," Tam said, following her gaze. "The shrine district. In case—"

"In case we're needed to carry somebody," Wren said. "Yeah. Get the stretchers. Get Sorrel and the Marlow sisters — they've got strong backs and level heads. Everyone else stays and keeps the crowd calm, because if the watch sees the undercity mobilizing tonight, even tonight, even *now*, we're back to square one by morning."

She was giving the orders because that was what she did now — reluctant general, banked-favor spender, the woman whose name got whispered in the tunnels like a password — and she was halfway through turning to give another one when the crowd nearest the barricade made a sound.

Not a gasp. Something softer. A collective intake, like wind through wheat.

Wren turned back toward the east, toward the gate, and saw why.

Two figures were coming up out of the old shrine district, walking slowly through the ruined streets, and the light of the returned stars was moving with them — not following them, exactly, but leaning toward them, the way flowers lean at a window. One was tall and moved like someone relearning her own body step by step, barefoot on the cold stones, hair full of a light that had never in recorded history existed inside Grayhallow's borders. The other—

The other was Casper Ashgrove, leaning on her arm, grey-faced and swaying, and *walking*, and *alive*, and dressed in clothes that looked like they'd been woven out of midnight and then gently set on fire and put out again, and he was laughing at something the star-woman said, actually laughing, the exhausted incredulous laugh of a man who has paid absolutely everything he had and received back, against all odds and all sense, the only thing he actually wanted.

"He's alive," Tam said.

"He's alive," Wren repeated, because saying it twice made it true, and saying it three times might make her stop shaking.

Then the crowd between the barricade and the pair began to do something Wren had never once seen a Grayhallow crowd do to a charm-touched person in her entire life.

It opened. Made way. And *cheered.*

* * *

Later — much later, when the stretchers had been sent home unused, when the watch had quietly handed the keys of the archive back to the archivists, when someone had started a bonfire in the tower ruins' courtyard and the undercity and the uppercity were drinking from the same barrels for the first time in living memory — Wren found Casper sitting on a fallen block of masonry at the edge of the firelight, wrapped in someone's donated coat, looking like a man made entirely of bruises and wonder.

Zephyrine was a little apart from him, at the edge of the courtyard, staring up. She'd been staring up for hours, Wren had noticed. Drinking the sky. Every few minutes a passerby would stop and stare at *her* instead — at the starlight still caught in her hair, at the way the new constellations seemed subtly, politely brighter wherever she happened to be looking — and she'd notice, and blush, apparently a habit she'd picked up sometime in the last hour, and look down, and then look right back up.

Wren dropped onto the masonry next to Casper and handed him a mug of the terrible spiced wine somebody's aunt had brought down from the hill district. He drank it like it was a sacrament.

"So," she said. "You were supposed to die."

"I was," he agreed mildly. "There was a clause."

"There's always a clause with you covenant people."

"The covenant can only be reforged by a keeper giving up their magic entirely. I assumed that meant—" He flexed his fingers. They were ordinary fingers now, chapped from cold, scarred from three years of charm-work. Nothing in them. Wren could see it, the absence, the way you could see where a tooth had been pulled. "I gave it up. All of it. The borrowed power, the ring's strength, whatever was left of my own spark. And it turns out that when a keeper gives up everything, the covenant counts that as payment enough — and gives him back the only thing that was ever really his."

"Which is?"

"A life." He said it simply, without drama, and that simplicity was the loudest thing Wren had heard all night. "Just the life. Which is more than I expected, and less than I deserve, and exactly enough."

Wren looked into the fire for a while. "Hale had all the power in the city," she said. "More than the city's ever seen in one man. And it ate him alive the second he stopped being able to hold it."

"Yes."

"And you gave yours away before anyone could take it, and it made you *free*."

"That's roughly the sermon version, yes."

"Don't get smug, you nearly died twice." She bumped his shoulder with hers, careful of the bruises. "I'm just saying. There's a lesson in there for this city, and I intend to make sure it learns it, because I've got a shop to legitimize and a trade association to found and about forty laws to get repealed, and I'll need the man who broke the blackout to testify."

Casper looked at her sideways. "You want me to *testify*."

"I want you to stand in front of the provisional council in your ruined coat and tell them exactly what magic is for. You're the only man in Grayhallow who's seen both sides of the gate and lived. That makes you worth more than any charm you ever sold." She drained her wine. "Congratulations. You're respectable now. Worst thing that's ever happened to you."

He laughed — really laughed — and then winced and pressed a hand to his ribs, and Zephyrine crossed the courtyard in six unhurried steps like she'd felt it through the stone, and crouched in front of him with her hands hovering over his ribs and a frown of intense professional concentration, and Wren realized with a start that the star-born woman was trying to heal him with instinct alone, having given up, apparently, whatever divine toolkit she'd once had — and that it wasn't working, and that she was doing it anyway, gently, uselessly, because that was what keepers did.

"It won't work without power," Casper said softly. "You don't have to—"

"I know what I have," Zephyrine said. Her voice was low and accented, like music learned in another country. "I have hands. Hands are not nothing." And she kept her hands there, and whether it was the touch or the company or simply time, some of the tension went out of Casper's shoulders, and Wren looked away to give them privacy and found the whole courtyard looking at them anyway, and decided that some things weren't private anymore, couldn't be, belonged to everybody now — the way the sky did.

An old woman — the ribbon-seller — shuffled over to Zephyrine and pressed something into her palm: a plain ward-ribbon, the cheap kind, the kind that had never once worked in three years of outlawry. Zephyrine closed her fingers around it and looked at the old woman with an expression of such startled gratitude that the old woman patted her arm and said, gruffly, "First sale of the new era. On the house."

Wren watched the star-born keeper hold a five-copper charm like it was a crown, and she thought: *we're going to be alright.* Not tonight, not cleanly, not without trials and reprisals and a hundred hard mornings — but the law had bent tonight, visibly, in front of thousands of witnesses, and laws that bend in public don't bend back all the way.

Then Tam came running across the courtyard, skidding on ash, and the look on his face flipped Wren's chest cold.

"Wren. The gate."

"What about it?"

"It's—" He caught his breath. "You need to see it. It's not closed. It's not *sealed*. It's standing open, Wren — wide open, both doors, and there's light coming *out* of it, and—" he swallowed, "—and something on the other side is humming. Happy. Like it's *home*. And there are people already gathering there, touching it, and nobody — nobody knows what happens if it stays like that."

Wren was on her feet before he finished, wine-mug forgotten, and she caught Casper rising too, and Zephyrine, and around them the courtyard was beginning to turn, question by question, face by face, toward the east — toward the gate that two keepers had opened, and had not yet, it seemed, closed.
