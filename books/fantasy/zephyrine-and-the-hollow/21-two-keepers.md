## 21. Two Keepers

### Casper

The last thing the Hollow gave him was a hand to hold.

He had expected, in the fog of his dying, that crossing back would feel like drowning in reverse — some great gasp of light, a trumpet-blast of restored stars. Instead it was this: Zephyrine's fingers laced through his, thin and cool and astonishingly human, and the two of them stepping through the gate together like travelers coming home on the late ferry. The threshold passed over them the way water passes over a stone. One moment the starving dark; the next, the smell of rain-wet stone and woodsmoke and the particular green breath of the gully where the gate had stood, hidden, all these years.

Casper's knees folded the instant they were through.

He didn't fall so much as arrive at the ground, the way a puppet arrives when its strings are cut. The ring — his mentor's ring, the covenant-ring, the thing he had worn so long he'd forgotten the shape of his own knuckle beneath it — was gone. Not lost. *Spent.* He could feel the absence of it the way an amputee feels a hand: a phantom warmth where three years of borrowed power had lived, now nothing but morning air against bare skin.

"Casper." Zephyrine crouched beside him, and her voice was different. That was the first thing he truly noticed, past the roaring in his ears. Three years he had listened to her singing through the gate-stone, and her voice had been *vast* then — a sound like winter constellations turning, like something that had no need of lungs. Now it was only a woman's voice, roughened, uncertain of its own volume. "You're bleeding."

"Am I?" He looked down at himself with mild interest. His hands were scored with fine white lines, ring-burn written into the skin from palm to wrist, and one of the lines had opened. Blood, ordinary and red and absurdly welcome. A body doing what bodies do. "Good. That means I'm still — "

"Don't say it."

"— still me."

She sat back on her heels in the wet grass, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all, because above the gully, between the black silhouettes of the hills, the sky was doing something it had not done in three years.

The stars were coming back.

Not all at once. They returned the way memory returns — one bright point, then a scatter, then a slow constellation assembling itself out of nothing, as if the sky were remembering where it had put things. Somewhere beyond the ridge, faint and impossible, Grayhallow was cheering. Casper could hear it even here: a sound like the whole city exhaling at once, three years of held breath released into the dark.

Zephyrine did not watch the stars. She watched him.

"I can't feel them," she said quietly. It was not a question and not quite a complaint. It was the voice of someone reporting damage, taking inventory of a house after a flood. "The stars. I spent my whole existence — I don't even have a word for how long — being *of* them. When one went out at the far edge of the sky, I felt it like a splinter. And now—" She lifted her hand, turned it over, studied the palm as though expecting light to leak from it. "Now there's just night. Beautiful, ordinary night. And I'm just a woman looking at it."

Casper pushed himself up onto his elbows. Everything hurt, and the hurt was honest, and he found he was grateful for it. "Is it enough?" he asked. "Being just a woman?"

She was quiet for a long time.

Above them, another star kindled. Then three more, in a little cluster he almost recognized — and then he did recognize it, because he had sold charms under that cluster for years, had told nervous young clerks it meant safe travel, and the lie had been small and kind and mostly true.

"No," Zephyrine said at last. "But it's *mine*. That's more than I've ever been able to say about anything."

* * *

### Zephyrine

Walking was a revelation she had not prepared for.

She had crossed realms before — the night of her descent, the willing cage, the bargain struck in the dark behind the sky — but that had been transformation, translation, a star becoming something else the way ice becomes water. This was different. This was legs, one in front of the other, on ground that insisted on being uneven. Her feet inside the Hollow had been an idea of feet. These feet had opinions. By the time they reached the top of the gully, she had learned three separate ways that hillsides are unfair, and Casper had caught her elbow twice, and both times his hand had been warm in a way that no borrowed divinity had ever managed to be.

"You're laughing," he observed.

"I'm *cursing*."

"It sounds like laughing."

"That is because my curse vocabulary is apparently very limited." She stopped at the crest of the hill and made herself look back.

The gate stood open.

That was the sentence she kept turning over in the hours that followed, the way the tongue returns to a sore tooth. *The gate stood open.* For three years it had been sealed, cracked, leaking, a wound in the world; for centuries before that it had been locked, and before *that*, in the deep beginning, it had simply been a door, tended. Now it was tended again — but open. Through its arch she could see the Hollow, no longer starving but breathing, the slow silver tide of renounced magic flowing along the channel they had made of it, up and out, pouring itself into a sky that received it gratefully and answered with light. And beyond the arch, faint as breath on glass, something vast and patient hummed contentment. The Unlit, home at last in the only home it had ever really asked for: a place where magic could go when it was given up, instead of curdling in the dark with nowhere to be.

"It's beautiful," Casper said behind her.

"The gate?"

"The sky. But yes, also the gate." He came to stand beside her, and she noticed he was careful about it — careful of his own body, testing each movement, a man learning the limits of a purse he'd emptied. "We should go down. Wren will have the whole undercity camped around the gate by dawn if we don't show our faces. She'll assume we died heroically and start charging admission to the ruins."

"She sounds terrible."

"She's the best person I know."

They walked down toward the city as the stars finished arriving, and Zephyrine catalogued everything, because cataloguing was all she had now — that, and her hands, and the man beside her. The weight of her own hair. The way cold felt like information instead of indifference. The shocking specificity of smells: loam, smoke, someone's distant bread. She had spent eternity knowing everything in the abstract and nothing in particular, and now the world arrived one detail at a time, demanding to be noticed individually, refusing to be generalized. It was exhausting. It was the richest she had ever been.

At the outskirts, where the first houses of Grayhallow leaned together like old conspirators, people saw them. She braced for reverence. She had been worshipped once, in the manner of stars — distantly, ignorantly, by farmers who named her and knew nothing of her.

Instead a woman carrying a lantern simply stepped aside and said, "Evening. They're letting folk up the tower road tomorrow, if you're going for the celebrations."

Just that. Evening. As if a former star walking out of the dark were an ordinary thing to pass on a road.

Zephyrine found she was gripping Casper's hand very hard.

"Yes," she managed. "We are. We're going that way."

* * *

### Casper

Wren punched him.

It was, in fairness, a restrained punch — a fist against his shoulder, delivered at the door of her brother's lodgings with the full force of three weeks' accumulated terror — but it nearly put him on the grass, because there was very little of Casper left to absorb a blow. Behind her, Tam Wren-who-was-no-longer-lost leaned in the doorway with his wrists still ringed by rope-marks, and grinned like a man who intended never to stop.

"You're alive," Wren said.

"Barely."

"You're *powerless*."

"Completely."

"And you brought her." She looked past him at Zephyrine, who stood in the lamplight wearing Casper's spare coat and examining a doorknob with the concentration of a scholar confronting an unfamiliar text. Wren's face did something complicated — suspicion, awe, grief for her grandmother, all fighting for the same few inches of skin — and settled finally on a crooked smile. "Well. Someone's got to teach her which end of a lockpick is which. Come in before the whole street figures out who she is and starts leaving offerings on your step. You'll never be rid of the smell of candles."

They came in. And this — Casper thought later, sitting at Wren's table while Tam argued enthusiastically with Zephyrine about whether bread could legally be enchanted — this was the strangest miracle of all: how quickly the enormous became domestic. The woman who had contained the Unlit was asking how much butter bread required. The keeper who had shattered a magistrate's stolen godhood was trying to remember whether he owned a second shirt. Hale was in chains somewhere below the bell-tower, awaiting whatever justice the city decided it could stomach, and the world had not ended, and dinner was happening anyway.

Later, on the narrow roof, Wren found him alone.

"They'll want to give you the tower," she said, without preamble. "The archive, the wards, all of it. Keys and titles and a chair with your name on it. Corvane's chair, essentially, just with better intentions."

"I know."

"And you're going to refuse."

Casper watched the sky. Every star was back now; he had counted them twice, superstitiously, like a man checking his pockets. "I don't have anything left to hold power *with*, Wren. That's rather the point of everything we did. But—" he turned the thought over, examining it, "—I keep thinking about my mentor. Old Aldous. He kept the gate alone his whole life and it killed him alone, in a locked room, and nobody found him for days. The covenant needed two voices and he only ever had one, and he spent himself into the grave pretending that was fine. I inherited his loneliness and called it duty. I'm not doing that again. Whatever comes next, it isn't a man in a room with a ring."

Wren nodded slowly. "And her?"

"She's not going back to the sky. She says there's nothing up there anymore that's *her* — the stars are themselves again, not pieces of her. She wants to learn..." he laughed, a little helplessly, "...bread. Doors. Weather. All of it, from the bottom."

"Then build the thing properly this time." Wren knocked her shoulder against his. "Two keepers. Open door. Tended. And smugglers to keep honest, obviously, because someone has to make sure the new guild exams aren't completely corrupt, and I volunteer."

"Obviously," Casper said, and for the first time since the stars went out, the word ahead of him sounded like a future instead of a sentence.

* * *

### Zephyrine

The first snow fell a month after the gate reopened, and Zephyrine stood out in it until her hands went numb, because cold was still new and she refused to master it too quickly.

She had taken to keeping a ledger — an actual book, paper, purchased from a stationer who did not know what she was — in which she wrote down everything the world required of a person that eternity had never taught her. *Haggling: permitted, even expected. Do not apologize to doors.* The entries were ridiculous and she treasured every one. That morning's read: *Rain is not a message. Sometimes weather is only weather.* She had spent a hundred years reading intention into every flicker of the heavens, and this new sky demanded nothing of her at all. It simply went on above her, self-sufficient, indifferent, gorgeous. Being ignored by the cosmos, she was discovering, was a kind of freedom she had never once imagined.

Behind the cottage — their cottage, built into the old gatehouse ruin with their own four hands and Wren's suspiciously well-organized crew of smugglers-cum-carpenters — the gate stood open and tended.

Tending, it turned out, was mostly chores.

Someone swept the arch each morning, because leaves gathered against the threshold and the channel ran truer uncluttered. Someone walked the ward-line weekly, listening with palms flat to the stones, hearing the healthy hum of a boundary that guarded without imprisoning. Renounced magic came up through the gate in its silver thread — every spell given up, every outlawed gift surrendered freely now that Grayhallow had laws for it instead of chains — and rose harmlessly into the sky, and sometimes, on clear nights, a thread of it would flare briefly into being a brand-new star, which struck Zephyrine as the single finest joke the universe had ever told.

Apprentices came on Thursdays. Four of them now: Tam, two defrocked charm-sellers, and a bell-ringer's daughter who could hear the wards before she could see them. Casper taught them the keeping-rites slowly, patiently, with none of the secrecy that had rotted the old covenant. Zephyrine taught them what she could still do without divinity — which was, she was surprised to learn, quite a lot. Listening, mostly. Sitting with the gate the way you sit with a frightened animal. The Unlit liked listeners.

In the evenings, Casper came out to the threshold, and they sat together on either side of the open arch — one of them always on the city side, one always facing the Hollow, the old symmetry kept but softened, two chairs instead of a wall.

"Do you regret it?" she asked him one such evening. Snowmelt dripped from the eaves; inside, supper waited; the gate hummed its low content note between them. "The spark you gave. Some nights you look at the apprentices working their little cantrips, and your hands move."

He looked at his hands — the ring-burn scars had faded to pale silver lines, barely visible — and he flexed them honestly, the way she had learned he did when he was determined not to lie.

"Sometimes I reach for it," he admitted. "Like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth was. But here's what I notice, when I reach: I don't miss the *power*. I miss feeling useful in that specific way. And then I remember—" he nodded at the gate, at the silver thread climbing invisibly toward the stars, "—that the most useful thing I ever did was give it up. So no. Not regret. Just... phantom weather, in an amputated country."

Zephyrine considered this. Then she took the ledger from her pocket, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote, in her careful, newly learned hand:

*Phantom weather, in an amputated country. Still worth it.*

"What are you writing?" Casper asked.

"The only scripture anyone needs," she said. "My own."

He laughed, and the sound of it carried through the open gate and into the Hollow beyond, where something vast and lonely and finally, finally home hummed it back like a chord.

Above them, unhurried, the stars came out — not hers, not his, belonging to no keeper at all — and the door between the worlds stood open behind them, tended, warm-lit, and waiting for whoever came next.
