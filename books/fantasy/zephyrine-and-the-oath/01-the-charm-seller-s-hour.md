## 1. The Charm-Seller's Hour

The bell above the shop door had a crack in it, so it rang in two notes — one bright, one mournful — and Noor Zhou had long ago decided this was the most honest thing about the establishment.

"Good morning!" they called out, before the customer had even fully entered, because the first rule of selling charms in a city that outlawed magic was to sound delighted about it. Delighted people were harmless. Delighted people sold trinkets.

The morning was gray the way all Corvane mornings were gray, the fog pressing against the windows like a polite but persistent creditor. Outside, the lamplighters were snuffing their flames, and the fishmongers on Maren Street were shouting about herring as though the herring had personally wronged them. Inside, Noor's little shop smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and, faintly, of nothing at all — which took work. Real magic had a smell, if you knew how to sniff for it: cold iron and river-stone and something older underneath, like the memory of rain. Noor spent a full hour every morning making sure the shop smelled of lavender instead.

The customer was a young woman with flour on her sleeves, a baker's apprentice by the look of her, wringing her cap in both hands.

"It's my grandmother," she said. "She can't sleep. She says there's something under the floorboards whispering."

"There is," said Noor gravely. "It's called a house settling. Very common. Very natural. But—" and here they reached beneath the counter with the flourish of a conjurer producing a dove, "—this helps."

What they produced was a small disc of painted wood, a moon on one side and a sleeping cat on the other, strung on a leather cord. It cost three coppers. It did absolutely nothing, except that grandmothers who wore it tended to sleep better, because grandmothers who believed someone had thought of them tended to sleep better, and that, Noor had come to believe over seven years, was not nothing. That was maybe the whole point.

"Three coppers," Noor said, "and I'll throw in the advice free: check under the floorboards anyway. Houses whisper less when you've looked."

The baker's apprentice paid, beamed, and left, setting off the cracked bell's two-note hymn. Noor exhaled.

Seven years. Seven years of three-copper charms and lavender oil and smiling at inspectors. Seven years since they had last stood at the door beneath the shuttered house on Vell Street and spoken the words that kept it shut. Seven years of being no one, which was the safest thing a person could be in Corvane, and also — some mornings, when the fog came in thick enough to erase the rooftops — the loneliest.

They were re-inking a price sign when the second bell-sound came, and this time it wasn't the door.

It was boots. Two sets, coming up Maren Street with the particular unhurried rhythm of people who wanted you to hear them coming. Oathkeepers didn't sneak. Sneaking implied doubt, and the Oath had never doubted anything in its life.

Noor set down the ink pen, wiped their hands on the apron, and arranged their face into the expression they'd practiced in every reflective surface in the city: pleasant, slightly dim, the face of a person whose biggest worry was inventory.

* * *

Oathkeeper Sarel came in first. She always came in first; the other one, Voss, liked to linger at the doorway where he could watch the street, which Noor privately considered the smarter habit and had never once told him so.

Sarel was not what people expected from an Oathkeeper. People expected bulk, braid, a voice like a slammed door. Sarel was small and precise, with gray threading into her dark hair and eyes that went through a shop the way an auditor goes through ledgers — left to right, top to bottom, missing nothing, forgiving less.

"Charm-seller." She said it the way she always did, a title rather than a greeting, as though checking whether the word still fit.

"Morning, Keeper." Noor spread their hands. "Come to arrest me for the crime of painted cats?"

"The census," Sarel said, ignoring the joke as she always did, "is due next week. Your registration is current. Your stock list, however—" she pulled a folded paper from her coat, "—lists forty-one items. You have forty-three charms on display."

Noor blinked at her. Genuinely, they hoped. "Do I?"

"You do. The new ones are on the shelf by the window. A blue bird and a green fish. Neither appears on your list."

"Well." Noor scratched their head, letting a beat of confusion play out — not too long, never too long, confusion that lasted too long started looking like performance. "My nephew made those. He visits sometimes, does a bit of carving, leaves his mess everywhere. I keep telling him everything needs to go on the list or the Keepers will have my hide, and he keeps not listening. Children." They sighed with feeling. "You know how it is."

Sarel did not say whether she knew how it was. She crossed to the window shelf and picked up the blue bird, turning it over in her fingers. Her fingers were careful. Everything about Sarel was careful, and Noor had learned to fear careful far more than cruel.

"No maker's mark," she observed.

"He's fourteen. His mark is that he forgets."

"Hm." She set the bird down exactly where it had been, rotated so its grain ran the same direction, which Noor noted with a small internal chill: she memorized rooms. She would remember that bird's angle next visit, and if it moved, she'd know someone had touched it, and if someone had touched it—

Stop, Noor told themselves. You're doing the thing again. The thing where a painted bird becomes a gallows.

"New regulation," Sarel said, producing a second paper, this one official-looking, stamped with the seal of the Oath — a closed eye inside a circle, which Noor had always found a bit on-the-nose. "All charm-sellers within the Old Quarter are required to submit to quarterly premises inspection. Effective this solstice."

"This solstice," Noor repeated. "That's... soon."

"Nine days." Sarel's eyes came up from the paper and settled on Noor, and for a moment — half a moment, less — something moved behind them. Not suspicion. Something wearier than suspicion. Something that looked almost like a question she'd been carrying a long time without asking. Then it was gone, filed away wherever she kept such things. "You'll receive notice. Have your stock list current. All items accounted for."

"Every cat, bird, and fish," Noor promised solemnly. "You'll find me the most boring man in Corvane."

"I don't doubt it," said Sarel, and there it was again, that flicker, that almost-question, before she turned on her precise heel and left, Voss peeling away from the doorframe behind her like a shadow remembering it was attached to something.

The cracked bell sang its two notes. Bright, then mournful.

Noor stood very still in the middle of the shop for a long time after they'd gone.

* * *

In the back room, behind a rack of dried herbs that were exactly as mundane as advertised, there was a trapdoor under a rug under a trunk under twenty years of deliberate clutter. Noor did not go down there. That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of their life: a door they did not open, maintained by not opening it, guarded by absence, which was either the cleverest ward ever devised or the most cowardly, depending on the day and how much lavender Noor had been breathing.

But today they lifted the rug. Just the rug. Just to look at the trapdoor's iron ring, cold even in summer, patient even now.

Because here was the thing that had been sitting in Noor's chest all morning like a swallowed stone: nine days. The inspection was set for the solstice. And Noor had not chosen the solstice. Sarel had not chosen the solstice. The Oath's calendar chose it, the way tides choose things, the way old magic chose things — and old magic had a memory longer than any ledger.

Seven years ago, Noor had walked away from the gate on the longest night of the year. Had sealed it with words that were supposed to hold until they returned, and then had not returned, because returning meant facing what waited on the other side of the iron ring, and Noor had discovered they were better at selling painted cats than at courage.

Words held by oaths didn't care about shame. But words held by *time* — words tied to solstices, to thresholds, to the turning of the light — those had a way of coming due.

Noor pressed their palm flat against the trapdoor. Beneath the wood, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something answered. Not knocking. Nothing so dramatic. Just a warmth, brief and deep, like coals remembered from childhood — and gone again before they could be sure.

The lavender smell in the shop had faded by afternoon. Noor noticed, and did not reapply it, and could not have said why.

* * *

They closed early, which they never did, and walked home the long way, past the shuttered house on Vell Street. Its windows were boarded. Its garden had gone to seed in the aggressive, self-sufficient way of gardens nobody loves. Neighbors assumed it was empty. Neighbors assumed a great many useful things.

Noor stood across the street in the fog, hands in pockets, and watched the house watch them back.

And that was when they saw it — small, at first, easy to blame on the fog. A light in the gap between two boards over the upstairs window. Not lamplight. Lamplight flickered yellow and died. This light was steady and gold and *alive*, the way fire is alive, the way nothing else is, and it pulsed — once, twice — slow, like breathing. Like the breathing of something very large, very old, and running out of air.

Noor's mouth went dry. Seven years of practiced dullness fell away in a single heartbeat, and underneath it they were twenty-eight again, standing at an iron door, holding words they hadn't yet learned would break them.

The light in the boarded window pulsed once more.

Then, distinctly, from inside the empty house, in a language that predated kingdoms and had no business in any living throat:

a child's voice, calling a name.

Noor's name. The true one. The keeper's one.

The one they had buried under lavender and painted cats, seven years ago, on the longest night of the year.
