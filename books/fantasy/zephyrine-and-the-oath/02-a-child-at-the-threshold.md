## 2. A Child at the Threshold

The trouble with burying your own name, Noor had learned, was that you never got to choose who dug it up.

They stood in the street for a full ten seconds, which in Noor's professional estimation was nine seconds too long for a mere charm-seller standing outside an empty house. Charm-sellers did not freeze. Charm-sellers muttered about the cold and fumbled with their keys like everyone else. So Noor muttered — something about the draftiness of Corvane's north quarter, which had the advantage of being true — and fumbled out their keys, and let themselves into the house they had spent seven years pretending was only a house.

Inside smelled of dust and dried lavender and, faintly, of hot stone. That last one was new. That last one was very bad.

"Hello?" Noor called, in the flat, unbothered voice of a person who had checked under every piece of furniture in this building at least four hundred times. "If you've broken in, I should warn you the floorboards are rotten and the previous owner died of stubbornness. It lingers."

The voice came again from below the floor.

*"Vesseth-ir, Noorane. Vesseth-ir, o kestrelan."*

Noor's knees decided independently that sitting down sounded excellent, and deposited them on the third stair from the bottom before their dignity could file an objection. The words rang through the boards and up through the soles of their boots, and seven years of careful dullness cracked like river ice in spring.

*Vesseth-ir.* Gate-of-the-country. *Kestrelan* — keeper. Not keeper as the Oathkeepers used it, all iron and ledger-books. The old word meant something closer to *the one who holds the door handle from the inside*. The old tongue had always been precise about doors. It had never been kind about what waited behind them.

And it was coming from beneath the house. From the direction of the gate.

"No," Noor told the floor conversationally. "No, no. You are sealed. I sealed you. I have the paperwork."

The floor, being a floor, declined to argue, but the light said otherwise. Through the gap beneath the cellar door bled a glow the color of coals just breathed upon — not the steady amber of Noor's ward-seals, which burned like well-behaved candles, but something older, flickering, alive. It pulsed. It pulsed again. And each pulse landed somewhere behind Noor's sternum with a small, terrible familiarity, the way an old song lands, or the smell of a grandmother's kitchen in a stranger's house.

Seven years. Seven years of lavender sachets and painted cat figurines and telling the neighbors their warts were best treated with patience. Seven years, and the past had not merely come looking. It had come *speaking*.

Noor got up, because if the gate was answering someone, then someone was at the gate, and whatever else they had become — shopkeeper, coward, seller of charms that cured nothing but worry — they were still, in some buried and inconvenient part of themselves, the one who held the door handle from the inside.

They went down the stairs two at a time, which their back would make them regret by morning.

* * *

The cellar looked exactly as it had for seven years: low-ceilinged, smelling of earth, its far wall an ordinary fieldstone face with an ordinary iron door set into it — except that tonight every seam of that wall glowed faintly, hairline rivers of ember-light tracing the mortar like veins in a waking arm. Noor's ward-seals hung in the air before it in their usual constellation, forty-one knots of pale gold light, patient and neat.

Thirty-nine of them were still pale gold.

Two had turned the color of sunrise.

And between them and the door, small enough that Noor's heart clenched before their mind caught up, stood a child.

Perhaps eight years old. Thin in the way of children who have learned to eat slowly. A coat too big, mended badly, sleet still melting in their dark hair. They had turned when Noor came down the stairs, and they did not look frightened, which frightened Noor more than screaming would have. Children who don't startle at strangers in cellars have seen worse things than strangers.

"Hello," Noor said carefully, in Corvanian, and then, because the child only tilted their head, tried it again in three other tongues, including one Noor hadn't spoken aloud since the last night of Vessia.

At the fourth language the child answered.

*"Ith-kessa veth? Ith-kessa o maranel?"*

And there it was. The language before kingdoms. The tongue the dragons had taught the first stone-masons of Vessia, the tongue in which the word for *hello* and the word for *forgive me* shared a root, because the dragons had assumed anyone arriving at your door was probably sorry about something. Noor hadn't heard it spoken by a living throat in seven years, and hearing it now was like hearing rain after a long drought — joyous, and wrong, because droughts end and this one wasn't supposed to.

"Yes," Noor heard themself answer, in the old tongue, helpless as a man returning a sneeze. "Yes, I'm the keeper. Who — who are you?"

The child considered them with grave dark eyes.

Then the firelight in the cellar guttered, drew breath — and the child's eyes caught it and threw it back.

Not reflected light. Not lamplight doing strange things. Fire, banked and living, flickering behind the irises the way lamplight moves behind horn windows, gold shot through with red, pulsing once, twice, in time with the breathing of the door. In time, Noor realized with a lurch, with Noor's own heartbeat, as if the child and the gate and Noor's treacherous chest had all agreed on a rhythm without consulting anyone.

Dragon-fire. In a child's eyes. In a city where magic was illegal and dragons were a category of map-decoration.

"Oh," said Noor, with tremendous calm. "Oh, no."

The child, showing an instinct for Corvane that suggested hard recent experience, nodded solemnly. *"Oo,"* they agreed. *"Oo, no."*

Despite everything — despite the dread climbing Noor's spine like frost — a laugh escaped them, short and cracked. Seven years since anyone had agreed with them in the old tongue. Seven years since anyone had been *funny* in it.

"What's your name?" Noor asked, and crouched down to the child's level, keeping their hands visible, the way you do for animals and children and anyone else with good reasons to distrust adults.

"Ash," the child said — Corvanian, accented strangely, the word chosen rather than inherited. Then, softer, in the other tongue, the name underneath: *"Essa-veth."* Little ember. Little coal-that-carries-fire-through-the-night.

Behind them, the two ward-seals flared sunrise-bright, and a third began to change.

Noor stood up sharply. "Stop that."

Ash blinked. "I didn't do anything."

"I know," Noor said grimly, watching a fourth seal blush gold-to-red. "That's what worries me." The seals weren't breaking. That was the horrible elegance of it. They were *answering* — recognizing a voice they'd been built to recognize, unwinding politely, a lock greeting the right key after deciding the key had taken long enough. Noor had woven those wards themself, seven years ago, with hands that shook, and had built into them one fatal courtesy: *open for the blood of Vessia, open for the fire, open for whoever comes home.*

Noor had assumed nobody would ever come home. Dead countries don't send children.

"How did you find me?" Noor asked. "This city, this house, me — how?"

Ash's small hands curled around something at their collarbone, something Noor couldn't see but suddenly, horribly could *feel*, a warmth in the room's arithmetic that didn't add up. The child looked at the iron door, then at Noor, and said, in the careful Corvanian of the self-taught:

"She's dying. The last one. She sent me through the deep roads before the snows. She said—" Ash frowned, translating, and the fire in their eyes dimmed with effort. "She said, *find the keeper who ran away. Tell the keeper the door is a door from both sides. Tell the keeper she is sorry about the oath.*"

The cellar went very quiet. Even the ember-light seemed to lean in.

"She is *sorry*," Noor repeated, "about the *oath*." Their voice came out thin. Seven years they had carried that oath like a stone sewn into their coat, and here was a child relaying, from a dragon, across the ruins of a drowned kingdom, the news that the stone had been somebody else's idea all along. "Sorry how? Sorry it was asked of me, or sorry—"

But Ash wasn't listening anymore. The child had gone rigid, head cocked toward the ceiling, toward the street above, and the fire in their eyes pulled tight and narrow as a held breath.

Above them, muffled by floorboards and distance and the general Sunday-evening hush of Corvane, a bell rang. Once. Twice. Three times.

The Oathkeepers' bell. Not the patrol bell, which rang twice for routine and was ignored by everyone including the patrols. Three strokes. Sweep-and-search. Someone had found something in the census ledgers, or felt something through the wards of the city itself, and whatever it was, it lived in this cellar.

Ash looked up at Noor, and did the thing that undid Noor completely: the child stepped *backward*, putting themself between Noor's gate and the stairs. Eight years old, half Noor's weight, guarding a door they barely understood with nothing but their small body and their borrowed fire.

"They came to my country first," Ash said quietly. "Before it drowned. Men in gray coats, with ledgers." A pause, and then, with the terrible economy of children: "Is it your turn now, or mine?"

Somewhere overhead, boots on cobblestones. Many of them. Keeping step.

Noor looked at the iron door, glowing like a hearth at midwinter. At the seals surrendering one by one, sunrise spreading through the constellation like dawn through a window. At the child standing guard. At the stairs going up, which were the sensible option, the survivable option, the option of lavender and painted cats and seven more gray years.

Then Noor took off their shopkeeper's apron, folded it, and set it on a crate.

"Neither," they said. "It's mine. Both times."

They put their hand on the iron door — warm now, warm as a living thing — and felt it lean toward their palm like a horse toward a known hand, and far away, on the other side of everything, something vast and ancient and failing exhaled a plume of gratitude that Noor felt as heat along every scar they owned.

Above them, the front door of the house splintered inward.

"Hold onto me," Noor told Ash. "Whatever happens, whatever you see — hold on, and breathe when I breathe."

The child's small arms locked around their waist. The last of the ward-seals turned the color of morning.

The door swung open onto fire, and Noor — charm-seller, coward, keeper, liar, citizen of nowhere — stepped through with the future of a dead country clinging to their coat.
