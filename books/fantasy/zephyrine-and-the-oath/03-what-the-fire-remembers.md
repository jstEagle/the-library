## 3. What the Fire Remembers

Ash had never seen so many lamps in one place.

Corvane burned light the way Vessia's people had once burned cedar on feast nights — wastefully, joyfully, as if there were no end to anything. Lamps in windows, lamps over doorways, a lamp hanging from the crooked arm of a sign shaped like a fish that said FORTUNE TOLD in letters Ash couldn't read but could almost taste: the old words underneath, worn smooth by time, like bones showing through skin.

They sat at Noor's kitchen table with a cup of something warm in front of them — *cinnamon milk*, Noor had called it, and Ash had repeated it twice because it was the first Corvani phrase they'd learned that wasn't *stop* or *thief* or *no magic here*. The milk was sweet. Their hands were still shaking anyway.

The fire in their chest had settled since they'd crossed the threshold of the charm-shop. Not gone. Never gone. But quieter, like a bird tucking its head under its wing. It liked this house. That frightened Ash more than anything else, if they were honest, which they were trying very hard not to be.

Noor sat across from them, elbows on the table, chin in their hands, watching Ash the way you watch a pot that might boil over or might just be doing what pots do.

"You're not going to ask me questions," Ash said. "You keep not asking."

"I'm very good at not asking," Noor said. "Seven years of practice."

"Then I'll tell you instead." Ash took a breath, and the breath came out wrong, came out in the old tongue, the way everything important did. *"Zha vethruun. Kessa il."* — *It was burning. Everything.*

Noor went very still. Then, softly, they answered in kind. *"Kessa il,"* they agreed. "Everything. Tell me slowly."

* * *

Here is what Ash told them:

Vessia did not fall the way countries are supposed to fall. There were no armies. There were no trumpets. There was only a morning when the maps changed.

Ash had been small — smaller than now, though they didn't remember exactly how much smaller, because the years after the erasure ran together like wet ink. They remembered the harbor city of Sennet-Vel, where their grandmother sold rope and their grandfather argued with gulls. They remembered the dragon.

Everyone remembered the dragon. Zephyrine. The last one, though last implied there had been others recently enough for anyone to miss them, and the truth was Zephyrine was older than the kingdom itself, older than the word *kingdom*, a creature so old that her name wasn't really a name at all but the closest sound human throats could make to the sound wind makes when it decides to stay.

She slept beneath the palace, Ash's grandmother said, and her breathing kept the seasons honest.

Then came the year the Oathkeepers came across the water with their gray cloaks and their ledger-books, and they did not fight anyone. That was the horror of it. They simply *wrote*. They wrote in a book with pages that hurt to look at, and as they wrote, things stopped being true. Roads unmade themselves. Names slid off gravestones. Ships that had sailed out of Vessia's harbors for four hundred years found no harbor to sail back to — only open sea where a country used to be, and behind them, faintly, the smell of smoke.

"They didn't destroy us," Ash said, and their voice cracked, and the ember flared so hot behind their ribs that they gasped. Noor pushed the cinnamon milk closer without a word. "They *forgot* us. On purpose. You can't kill a country with swords, everyone knows that. So they killed it with a ledger."

*"Ssa-veln,"* Noor whispered. *The Unwriting.* And then, in Corvani, barely audible: "I knew. I knew that's what they'd done, but hearing it—" They stopped. Pressed their lips together. Started again. "Go on."

So Ash went on.

* * *

When the Unwriting began, some of the people of Vessia had done the sensible thing and run. Others stayed, because you cannot outrun being forgotten; wherever you go, the forgetting comes with you, tucked into your mouth like a stone. Grandmother stayed. Grandfather stayed. The rope shop stayed, until one morning it was a ruin that had been a ruin for centuries and no one alive remembered why it mattered.

Ash's mother had not stayed. Ash's mother had carried Ash down into the dark beneath the palace, past doors that opened for her blood, down to where the air glowed like the inside of an ember, and she had knelt before the dragon.

Ash remembered her voice. Not her face — faces were the first thing the forgetting ate — but her voice, steady even while shaking. *"Zhessa-mira, kessa il, zhessa-mira"* — *Great-grandmother of fire, everything is lost, great-grandmother of fire.*

And Zephyrine had opened one eye, gold as harvest, ancient as regret, and looked at a child clutching their mother's sleeve, and made a sound that Ash felt in their teeth rather than heard.

"She spoke to me," Ash said. "Not out loud. In here." They touched their sternum, where the warmth lived. "She said — the words are hard. Old words inside old words. She said the country was dying but dying isn't the same as dead, and she was too old to carry it herself anymore, too old to hold the door against the forgetting alone." Ash swallowed. "So she put a piece of herself somewhere it would be safe. Somewhere the Oathkeepers' book couldn't reach, because you can't unwriting something that's already written into a living heart."

Noor's cup had stopped halfway to their mouth. It stayed there.

"Your mother gave you to a dragon," Noor said slowly, "and the dragon gave herself to you."

"Not all of her. An ember. A seed of fire." Ash stared into the milk. "She told me to run. She said run until the forgetting can't follow, find the keeper of the door, say the words. She gave me the words." Ash closed their eyes and recited them, the ones that had come out of their mouth at Noor's doorstep two nights ago and made every hidden seal in the house sing like struck glass: *"Vel-zhessa mirath, vel-zhessa kaan. Door of my great-fire, door of my keeping."*

When they opened their eyes, Noor was crying.

Not dramatically. Not the way mourners cry in stories, with wailing and torn clothes. Just tears running quietly down their face while they sat perfectly still, as if they'd been crying for years and had simply gotten good at doing it silently, and had forgotten how to stop.

"Keeper of the door," Noor repeated. Their voice came out sanded thin. "That's me. That was supposed to be me."

"I know," Ash said. "The fire knows you. When I walked in, it went — " they patted their chest — "*happy.* Like a dog seeing its person. It's been seven years, it said. Seven years, and where have you been."

"Ah." Noor laughed, wet and awful. "Even the dragon thinks I'm a coward. Wonderful."

"It didn't say coward."

"No. But you thought it loudly enough for both of us."

* * *

They sat with that for a while. The lamp above the table buzzed faintly, fat moth bumping the glass. Outside, Corvane went about its business of not knowing anything, cart wheels and drunk singing and somewhere a dog with strong opinions about the moon.

"Why seven years?" Ash asked finally. It was the question the ember kept asking, hot and insistent, every time Noor came near. "The fire remembers you taking the oath. It remembers you standing at the gate. Then it remembers nothing, and then it remembers a charm-shop that smells like dust and dishonest copper bracelets."

"Dishonest— they're perfectly honest bracelets. Honestly useless." Noor sighed, and rubbed their eyes with the heel of one hand, and for a moment looked older than any person Ash had ever seen, older even than the memory of the dragon. "I took the oath to guard the door. To keep it shut. To keep everything inside it — safe, sealed, sleeping — until such time as the Oath was lifted. And then I stood in front of that door for one whole night, listening to a country breathe on the other side, and I got scared. Not of what was behind it. Of what would happen to *me* if I ever had to choose between my promise and my conscience. So I left. I walked away from the only post I was ever given and I sold charms to people who didn't need them and I told myself I was guarding it from a distance. Watching. Vigilant." They shook their head. "I wasn't vigilant. I was hiding with excellent posture."

Ash considered this. The ember considered it too; it pulsed once, slow, judgmental, grandmotherly.

"That's very stupid," Ash said.

"Yes."

"But you came back when I knocked."

Noor looked up sharply. Something moved across their face — surprise, mostly, and under it, dawning, fragile, something like relief.

"The seals answered me because of the fire," Ash went on, warming to it, the way you warm to a thing when you realize it might be true. "But the door *opened* because you let me in. You could have pretended you weren't home. Charm-sellers pretend they aren't home all the time. I've been chased out of six doorways in this city." They held up fingers. "Six."

"The sixth man threw a boot."

"He threw a boot at a child."

"He was a bad man with poor aim. The point stands." Noor smiled — a real smile, crooked, exhausted, the first one Ash had seen from them that reached all the way up. "Yes. I came back. Late, but back."

"Then the fire forgives you a little," Ash said, with the gravity of someone relaying an official decree. "It says: late, but back. It also says your house wards are sloppy on the north side and you should be ashamed."

"My wards are *fine*—"

The ember flared, playful now, and Ash giggled despite everything, despite the ruined country folded up small inside their ribs, despite the grandmother-shaped hole and the harbor-shaped hole and the enormous sky-shaped hole where a dragon's voice used to live. For three full seconds, sitting in a lamplight kitchen in a city that had erased them both, Ash was just a kid laughing at a flustered adult.

Then the ember turned over.

The laughter died in Ash's throat. The warmth went strange — thinner, stuttering, like a candle in a doorway draft — and pain lanced through their chest, quick and bright, and they grabbed the edge of the table hard enough to whiten their knuckles.

"Ash?" Noor was around the table in a heartbeat, hands hovering, not touching. "Ash, what is it, what's happening?"

"She's tired," Ash whispered. The old tongue came back and wouldn't leave. *"Zhessa-mira... kessa il..."* Great-grandmother of fire, everything is lost. "She's been holding the whole country with her own breath for seven years, and she gave away the piece that was strongest, and now she's — Noor, she's *flickering.* And when she flickers—" Ash pressed their palm flat against their sternum, feeling the ember gutter like a hearth in a dying house — "when she flickers, I feel it. Like I'm the wick."

Noor's face went white as chalk. And then, oddly, their expression shifted — fear folding itself away, making room for something steadier, older, familiar. The look of a person remembering what they were for.

"Show me," Noor said, pulling a chair close, taking Ash's hand, thumb pressing gently to the pulse at the wrist the way their grandmother used to check for fever. "Everything the fire shows you. Every word, even the broken ones. Especially those."

From the street below, faint through the shuttered window, came the ring of boots on cobblestone — many boots, marching in step, passing and then fading toward the river district.

Neither of them mentioned it.

"Vel-zhessa mirath," Ash began, and the ember leaned toward Noor like a story finally finding its listener.
