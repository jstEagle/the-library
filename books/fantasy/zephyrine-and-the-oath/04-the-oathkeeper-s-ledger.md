## 4. The Oathkeeper's Ledger

The census ledgers of Corvane smelled of lamp oil, mildew, and other people's fear.

Sarel Voss had learned to read them the way sailors read weather. Seven years at the Office of Containment had taught her that magic always left a residue in ink — a faint tremor in the ledger-lines where a licensed charm-seller's quarterly declaration had been made under oath-glass. Honest entries sat flat on the page. Liars' entries shivered.

Most of them shivered. She'd stopped finding that troubling around her third year.

Tonight, though, there was something else. She sat alone in the records room with her boots off — a small rebellion nobody saw and therefore nobody could report — and turned page after page of the solstice-quarter census by lamplight, looking for the thing that had been itching at the back of her skull for two weeks like a splinter under a fingernail.

There it was again. Charm-district, ward nine. A charm-seller named *Zhou* — N. Zhou, licensed for minor household charms, luck-braids, fever-knots, nothing above a third-rank working — whose declarations were not merely honest. They were *silent*. Flat as slate. Seven years of them.

"Nobody's that clean," she murmured to the empty room.

She pulled the older volumes, the pre-census years, and found the name change: before *N. Zhou*, charm-seller, there was no entry at all. No prior license. No apprenticeship record. No birth registration. It was as if the person had walked out of the fog on the Vessia coast one morning and decided to sell luck-braids.

Which, she thought, was exactly what someone who didn't exist would do.

She should have flagged it years ago. Anyone should have. But the file had been marked with a small blue seal — a *patronage* seal — which meant some superior had taken an interest, and in the Office of Containment, curiosity about patroned files was a career-shortening habit. Sarel had survived seven years precisely by not asking questions with obvious teeth.

Except tonight the lamplight was low and the records room was warm and she was tired in the particular way that made careful people careless.

She pressed her thumb to the ledger-page and whispered the audit-phrase, the one they taught first-year clerks and never expected anyone to actually use: *"By ink and oath, show what was."*

The page shivered.

Not the entry. The *page beneath* the entry — layers of it, like ripples spreading from something sunk deep underwater. The audit-charm wasn't designed for this. It was meant to catch shopkeepers selling love-philtres out the back door. Whatever was buried under N. Zhou's seven spotless declarations made the whole ledger groan, and for half a heartbeat Sarel saw, ghosted through the paper, a single word in an alphabet she did not recognize — angular, old, wrong somehow, like a footprint from a foot that wasn't shaped like feet.

Then the page went flat and innocent and silent again.

Sarel sat back. Her heart was going like a rabbit's.

"Well," she said quietly. "Aren't you interesting."

* * *

Her mother had a saying: *the Oath is the wall between the wolves and the sheep, and you don't ask the wall its opinion.* Sarel had grown up on that saying the way other children grew up on lullabies. When the Oathkeepers came to recruit — they came to every school, measured every child's resonance, and took the ones who rang true — her mother had wept with pride and terror in equal measure, and Sarel, eleven years old and ringing like a struck bell, had promised to be a very good wall.

She had been, mostly. Twenty-two years a wall. She knew all the stories by heart now, told them herself to new recruits around the training fires: how Vessia had drowned itself in dragon-fire rather than submit; how the survivors had been mad with grief and older, hungrier magics; how the Oath had sealed the last gate between Corvane and that drowned kingdom so the madness could never spill out into the world. How the Oathkeepers stood watch so that no child would ever wake to find their street burning blue.

It was a good story. Good stories were the load-bearing kind.

The trouble was Danya.

Danya Ferro, her partner, eleven years ago now. They'd been assigned together to the coastal watch — the closest any living Oathkeeper got to the gate itself, which lay somewhere beneath the city in a location that existed on no map Sarel had ever cleared to see. Gate duty was ceremonial, mostly. Stand at the marked door in the sea-wall district, feel the cold come through it, log the temperature of your own dread, go home.

Until the night the cold stopped coming.

"It's *warm*," Danya had said, laughing, hand flat against the marked door. "Sarel, it's warm. Eleven years I've stood here freezing and tonight it's warm as a hearth. What do you think that means?"

What it had meant, according to the official finding, was resonance backlash. Danya had been standing too close during an instability event. The door had taken her. Not killed her — *taken* her, the report said, in language chosen carefully to avoid questions, because questions led to places like *what is behind the door, actually?* and *has anyone checked?*

Sarel had requested access to the full incident file seventeen times over eleven years. Denied sixteen times. On the seventeenth request, three years ago, the reply came back stamped with a blue patronage seal.

The same seal as N. Zhou's file.

She hadn't thought about that connection until tonight, sitting in the warm records room with her boots off, and when she thought it now it landed in her stomach like a dropped stone.

Someone very high up had wanted the gate incident buried. Someone very high up was protecting a charm-seller who did not exist. And Sarel had spent eleven years grieving a woman taken by a door that nobody would let her look behind.

"Careful," she said aloud, to herself, in her mother's voice. "Careful, girl."

But careful people never found anything out.

* * *

In the morning she did things in the proper order, the boring order, the order that wouldn't trip any alarms: a routine resonance sweep of the charm district, ward nine, scheduled months ago, nothing to see here.

The charm district on market day was all awnings and noise and the smell of fried dough, and it offended something in her that a place so cheerful could house whatever lived under N. Zhou's ledger. She walked the rows with her resonance-glass in her pocket, taking readings she could have faked in her sleep, and let her feet carry her past the corner stall with the hand-painted sign — *CHARMS, HONESTLY PRICED* — where a lean, gray-streaked figure was wrapping a fever-knot for a grandmother while explaining, with visible patience, that no, madam, it would not make her son write home more often, that was a different kind of knot entirely and also witchcraft, and the Oathkeepers hanged people for witchcraft, so perhaps a letter instead.

Noor Zhou. In the flesh, finally. Smaller than Sarel had imagined. Tired-looking. Hands quick and gentle with the ribbon.

And underneath all of it — under the smile, under the harmless chatter, under the third-rank license that read like a lie written by someone very good at lying — Sarel felt it. Faint as heat from a shut oven. Something vast, patient, and *old*, sitting beneath the floorboards of the little stall like a whale under a rowboat.

Zhou looked up. Their eyes met.

For a moment — one suspended, absurd moment — Sarel had the distinct impression of being recognized. Not identified, not assessed. *Recognized*, the way you recognize someone you once stood beside at a funeral.

Then Zhou smiled the smile of every charm-seller on every corner in Corvane, held up a luck-braid, and said, "Oathkeeper! First one's free. It works better if you pretend to believe in it."

Against her will, against twenty-two years of being a wall, Sarel almost smiled back.

"I don't need luck," she said.

"Everyone needs luck," said Zhou mildly, tying off the braid. "Especially people who go around making sure other people don't have any."

The words were nothing. The words were market-stall patter. Sarel logged them anyway, that evening, in her private notebook, under the case heading she was no longer able to pretend she hadn't opened: *ZHOU, N. — ANOMALY.*

Below it she wrote: *Patronage seal matches Ferro incident file. Requested Danya's full report again today. Denied again.*

Her pen hovered. Then, in smaller letters, the thing she'd been circling for eleven years without landing:

*Danya said the door was warm.*

She looked at that sentence a long time. Walls were supposed to keep things out. That was the whole story — the Oath kept the madness of drowned Vessia out of the bright world. But doors that burned people away in resonance backlash were not warm the way walls were cold. Doors that *took* people —

Doors that took people might, she thought, in some terrible arithmetic, be doors that *kept* them.

And if the Oath was built to keep something in — not madness, but people, survivors, someone's daughter standing on the far side pressing her warm hand to a cold door — then everything Sarel had been for twenty-two years was not a wall between wolves and sheep.

It was a lock on the inside of a prison, and she was the shape of the keyhole.

The bell of the Office tower rang the hour — and then rang again, off-pattern, a sequence she'd only ever heard described in training manuals she'd assumed were historical.

Instability alarm. Sea-wall district. Gate watch reporting the marked door running *hot*.

Sarel closed the notebook, put on her coat, and went to hunt down the source of the anomaly — knowing, with a certainty that felt like grief arriving early, that every road from this morning led back to a corner stall with an honestly priced sign, and to whatever warm, vast, patient thing was breathing beneath it.
