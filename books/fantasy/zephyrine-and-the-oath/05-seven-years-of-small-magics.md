## 5. Seven Years of Small Magics

There was a version of Noor Zhou who had never sold a charm in their life.

That version stood, seven years ago, on the last stair of a house that no longer existed — not demolished, not burned, merely *unmade*, folded out of Corvane's memory by the same hands that had built it. The house had been called the Threshold, and it had smelled of beeswax and cold iron and, underneath everything, faintly of sea-salt from a country that could not be reached by any road.

Noor remembered the oath as if it were yesterday, which was the problem. Memory, for a keeper, did not soften. It sat in the chest like a swallowed stone.

*Say it,* Sarel's predecessor had said. Not unkindly. Her name had been Maren Voss, and she had held the Ledger of Oaths in both hands as though it might wriggle free. *Say it plainly, child. The words do their work whether you mean them or not, but they work better when you do.*

The candlelight had been the color of weak tea. Below them, through a floor of black glass that was not glass at all but compressed silence, the gate had turned slowly — a ring of pale fire, patient as a held breath. Through it, if you leaned far enough and dared, you could see water. Not water like rivers or rain. Water with *streets* in it. Towers standing in green twilight, bells still hanging in drowned steeples, doors that opened onto rooms where lamps burned without oil.

Vessia. The kingdom that had gone under and refused, stubbornly, to finish going under.

*The door stays shut,* Maren had said. *You stay beside it. That is all an oath is, in the end: a person agreeing to be a wall.*

And Noor — twenty-three years old, certain, hungry to matter — had said yes. Had said it *plainly*. Had felt the words sink into their bones like ink into paper, permanent, indelible, theirs.

They had meant every syllable.

That was the cruelest part, Noor would think later, years into a life of selling luck-knots and traveler's blessings to people who needed neither. They hadn't lied. They hadn't faltered. They had meant it completely, right up until the moment meaning became too heavy to carry, and then they had set it down quietly, in the dark, and told themselves it was still being carried.

* * *

The first year at the gate had been almost sweet.

The duty was simple in shape, if not in weight. Wake before dawn. Walk the black-glass floor and listen. The gate spoke — not in words, exactly, but in pressures, in tides. Some mornings it hummed like a wineglass rubbed at the rim. Some mornings it *ached*, a low groan through the floorboards of the world, and those mornings Noor would press both palms flat against the silence and pour what little strength they had into the seal, the way you'd feed a fire one log at a time.

The dragon beyond the door was already old then. Older than kingdoms, older than the drowning. Its name, in the pre-kingdom tongue, was a sound like a wave deciding to turn back — unpronounceable, so the keepers called it Ember-Mother, though it was neither mother nor particularly fiery anymore. It was more ember than dragon now. A coal wrapped in scales, breathing slow enough that one breath lasted a season.

Every breath kept Vessia's echo alive. Every breath fed the gate. And the gate, in turn, was the only thing keeping the echo from spilling out into Corvane — or so the Oath said. Keep the door shut, and the drowned kingdom stayed a dream; open it, and who knew what would come through. Ghosts. Floods. Grief with teeth.

It had sounded reasonable. It had sounded, in that candlelit room, like the most reasonable thing Noor had ever heard.

What no one had said — what perhaps Maren herself had not known, or had known and chosen not to say — was what the door looked like from the *other* side.

* * *

In the fourth year, something knocked.

Not on the gate. On the seal. From inside. A sound like a small fist against a window, polite, repeated, patient. Noor lay awake half the night trying to decide if they'd imagined it, and finally went down before dawn and pressed their ear to the black glass, and there it was again: knock, knock, knock. Soft. Almost shy.

Through the ring of pale fire, down in the green water-light, someone was standing. A woman. Small, gray-robed, holding a lantern that burned underwater without argument. She was looking up at the gate the way you look at a ceiling from inside a locked room.

She raised her lantern toward Noor. She mouthed something. The old tongue, the pre-kingdom tongue, the language Noor had been taught only enough of to read warning-stones: *we are still here.*

Noor pressed their hands against the glass and did not answer.

That was the night the stone in their chest began to grow.

Because here is what the Ledger did not say, what the Oathkeepers' histories had sanded smooth: Vessia had not emptied when it drowned. There had been survivors — hundreds, maybe thousands — who had not fled across the mountains, who had climbed into the high towers as the water rose, who had learned to breathe the way the dragon breathed, slow and sparse and stubborn. They were still there. Tending gardens of pale coral. Ringing the bells at solstice, because some things you do even if no one outside can hear. Waiting.

The Oath was not keeping a monster in. The Oath was keeping *people* out of reach of the sky.

Noor had gone up the stairs that morning and sat in the unmade kitchen of the Threshold and thought about walls. About how Maren had said *a person agreeing to be a wall*, and how she had failed to mention which side of the wall the prisoners were on.

They had not quit. Quitting required courage, and besides, the words were in their bones now; they didn't know if quitting was even possible. Instead they began, very quietly, to cheat. Small magics. A charm slipped through the seal-crack during the low tide of the gate's humming — a dried fish, a vial of clean water, a child's wooden bird. Things that fit through the space between one breath of the dragon and the next. The woman with the lantern — her name, Noor eventually learned, was Ileni — began leaving things in return. Sea-glass polished into lenses. Songs written on strips of kelp. Once, unbearably, a drawing: a stick figure standing at a door, waving.

Noor kept the drawing pinned above their cot for two years and could not look at it directly for either of them.

* * *

Then came the night of the census.

An Oathkeeper named Dannel Roth — young, ambitious, the kind of man who sharpened his suspicion daily like a knife — arrived at the Threshold with instruments that measured leakage. Magic left traces, he explained cheerfully; magic was a smell, and dogs of the proper training could follow it anywhere. His instruments found what Noor's small kindnesses had left behind: a residue, faint but directional, flowing *outward* through the seal.

He did not find the dried fish. He did not find the wooden bird. He found the pattern.

Maren Voss was summoned. Noor watched her walk into the Oathkeepers' hall carrying the Ledger, and watched her come out three hours later with her face like weather about to break. What passed between her and the high keepers, Noor never learned in full. But she came to the Threshold that evening, alone, and stood on the black glass looking down at the turning ring of fire, and said one thing:

*I took this oath when I was nineteen,* she said. *I have kept it for forty years. Tell me honestly, keeper — what have you been sending through my door?*

And Noor, who had lied beautifully to inspectors and tax collectors and their own reflection, found they could not lie to her. They told her everything. The knocking. Ileni. The lantern. The survivors. The truth about what the gate held.

Maren was quiet for a long time. Then she laughed — a terrible sound, a laugh like a hinge rusting — and said, *Forty years. Forty years I have guarded a prison and called it mercy.* And she took the Ledger off her own neck, the one she'd worn since before Noor was born, and set it on the floor of black glass, and walked away into the dark of the city, and Noor never saw her again.

Some say she went north. Some say she went through the gate. Some say the Oathkeepers found her. Noor stopped asking after the third rumor, because every version ended the same way: with a woman choosing, at last, a side, and paying for it.

* * *

Here is what Noor did, and Noor has never once forgiven themselves for it, and has also, in seven years of honest effort, failed to stop doing the arithmetic of cowardice:

They ran.

Not dramatically. Nothing so clean. They simply let the Threshold be unmade. There was a procedure for it — a keeper could dissolve the house around the gate, fold the door into the city's blind spots, render it addressable by no map and no memory. It was meant as a defense, a way to hide the gate from conquerors. Noor used it as an exit. They folded the house away, sealed the gate into its deepest sleep, and walked into Corvane with nothing but a satchel of charm-craft and a new name, and told themselves — this is the part that curdles, this is the sentence they have turned over ten thousand times like a worry-stone — they told themselves:

*I haven't abandoned it. I'm guarding it from afar. No one will look for a gate no one remembers. My disappearance IS the vigilance.*

Seven years. Seven years of luck-knots and love-charms that worked only because belief does half of everything, seven years of learning to haggle warmly and lie gently and flinch, every single solstice, at the sound of bells. Seven years of telling themselves the gate slept better unattended, the way you tell yourself a letter you cannot bear to write is better unsent.

But a gate is not a hearth-fire that banks itself politely. A gate is a wound that breathes. And the dragon beyond it was dying — had been dying all along, slower than a mountain erodes but just as surely — and every year of Noor's absence was a year the seal drank from the ember instead of feeding it. Their small magics had been a trickle of kindness through a crack. Their absence was a drought.

They had not guarded the door.

They had abandoned it, dressed the abandonment in the vocabulary of duty, and moved three districts away to sell charms to people whose problems could be solved with string and optimism.

Noor knew this the way you know a room is messy even with your eyes closed — not as a thought but as a pressure, always, at the edge of every quiet moment. And they had gotten very good at quiet moments. At filling the silences with customers and coin-counting and the pleasant small theater of a shop well-run.

Until tonight.

Until a child had stood in their doorway speaking the language of the drowned, eyes lit with a fire Noor recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting — and the ward-seals beneath the city, the ones Noor had folded into sleep seven years ago, had *answered the child's voice*, waking like old dogs hearing a whistle they'd been bred to.

Which meant the gate was awake. Which meant the seal was thin. Which meant the drought was ending, one way or another, and Noor — keeper, deserter, charm-seller, liar, the person who had agreed to be a wall and then walked away from the wall and called it strategy — was going to have to go back down those stairs.

All of them. All the way to the black glass.

To stand in front of the door they had abandoned, and the people behind it, and the dying coal of a dragon older than sin, and say something. *Anything.* Even if the only honest word available was the one they'd spent seven years avoiding:

*I'm sorry. I'm late.*

The shop bell over the door gave its little tin shiver. Ash was asleep on the cot upstairs, fists curled around a strip of kelp covered in old-tongue writing, breathing in the slow, sparse rhythm of someone learning to breathe the way dragons breathe.

And somewhere beneath the city, in the folded dark where a house used to be, the gate was humming.

Not aching. Not groaning.

Humming — the way a wineglass hums when a hand it knows finally touches the rim.
