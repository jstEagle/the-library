## 6. The Ember Fails

Ash woke because the fire in their chest forgot how to breathe.

That was the only way to describe it. The ember — the dragon's ember, the one that had carried them across a drowned country and seven borders and one very rude ferryman — had a rhythm to it. In and out, like lungs made of light. Ash had learned to sleep inside that rhythm the way other children slept inside the sound of rain.

Tonight it stuttered.

*In. Out. In—*

And then nothing. A gap. A held breath that wasn't Ash's own.

They sat up on the cot in the dark of Noor's back room, hand pressed flat against their sternum, and waited for the ember to remember itself. When it finally did — *out*, slow and grudging, like a bellows with an opinion — the relief was so large it came out as a hiccup.

"You're awake," said Noor from the doorway, which was where Noor always was when anything happened, as if they were stitched to the frame. They were still dressed. There was ink on one wrist and what looked like dried soup on the other, and their eyes did the thing they always did around Ash: went bright with worry and then pretended very hard to be lamps instead.

"The fire stopped," Ash said. "For a moment."

Noor crossed the room in three steps and knelt, and their hands hovered over Ash's chest without touching, the way you'd hover over a candle flame you weren't sure you were allowed to cup.

"For a moment," Noor repeated. Their voice did not pretend to be lamps. "Tell me exactly how it felt."

"Warm. Then warm-er." Ash searched for it. "Like when the hearth goes down to coals and you think it's out but it isn't, quite. Except tonight it thought about being out. It *wanted* to be out for a second."

Noor's face did something complicated and quiet. Then they stood, pulled their coat off the hook, and said, in the cheerful voice that meant the opposite of cheerful, "Well. I suppose it's time you learned what I keep in all those locked drawers."

* * *

The answer, it turned out, was everything illegal in Corvane, sorted by guilt.

The bottom drawer held charms of finding, which were merely frowned upon. The middle drawer held charms of mending, which were prosecutable. And the drawer beneath the loose floorboard under the rug under the cot held a small wooden box containing three objects that made the ember in Ash's chest sit up and pay attention: a vial of dawnlight caught somewhere green and far away, a knot of red thread tied into a shape Ash's mouth knew before their mind did — the old-tongue called it *the river that remembers* — and a sliver of scale, dull as an old coin.

"Restorative work," Noor said, laying them out on the table like a confession. "Banned twice over. Once by the Oathkeepers, once — before that — by my own teachers, who said a charm that pours life from one vessel into another always takes more than it gives." They looked at Ash over the lamplight. "I'm going to do it anyway. I want you to know that, so you can be properly scandalized later."

Ash considered this. "I don't know what scandalized means, but Vessia would have liked you."

"Vessia," said Noor, and had to clear their throat and start again. "Vessia had questionable standards."

They began.

Ash watched from the cot, knees hugged to chest, as Noor's hands moved through shapes the air seemed embarrassed to allow. The dawnlight vial unstoppered and stood upright on its own, glowing like a patient egg. The red thread untied itself in Noor's fingers and became a thin bright line running from Noor's left hand to Ash's chest, humming faintly, the way the gate below the city hummed — the way it had hummed the first night, when Ash spoke old-tongue at the door and the whole hidden house answered like a dog hearing its name.

"There you are," Noor murmured, to the thread or the ember or maybe to both. "There you are, old friend. Hold on. I've got you."

Warmth moved down the thread.

It didn't fix things. Ash felt that immediately, the way you feel a splinter still in after the poking stops. The ember took the offered light and banked it, fed it into its rhythm — *in, out, in* — steadier now, but shallower. Like breathing through a straw. Like a fire burning smaller so it can burn longer.

"It's rationing itself," Ash said.

Noor's hands stopped. "What?"

"It's being careful." Ash pressed their palm to their sternum. "It knows. It's been carrying more than me this whole time, hasn't it? Not just my pilot light. It's been keeping the gate lit. Keeping—" the word arrived with its weight already attached, "—keeping *home* lit."

The lamp guttered. Not the ember. Just the lamp. But outside, faint through the shutters, Ash heard something else flicker too: the streetlamps of Corvane, all along Charm Row, dimming together for one long breath before recovering, confused.

And underneath everything — so deep Ash felt it in their teeth, in the cot's frame, in the soup-dry floorboards — the gate's hum changed key.

Down a note. Down another. A wineglass losing its water.

"Every failure dims it," Noor whispered. They weren't talking to Ash anymore. They were talking to the floor, to the house, to seven years of pretending not to listen. "I knew that. Of course I knew that. I just told myself the door could hold without me. Without anyone." They laughed, one short ugly syllable. "I told myself a lot of things in a shop full of love-knots that don't work."

"They worked a little," Ash offered loyally. "Mistress Parren bought six."

"Mistress Parren is in love with the idea of being in love and would have managed it with string." But Noor smiled, and the smile cracked something open, and then the smile was gone entirely, because that was the moment the bells started.

* * *

Not shop bells. Watch bells. Three of them, in the pattern Ash had learned in one week on Charm Row the way you learn the sound of a dog you must avoid: three-and-stop, three-and-stop. Raid.

"Oathkeepers," Noor said. Already moving. The dawnlight went back in its vial; the vial vanished somewhere in their coat; the red thread snapped back into a knot and leapt into Ash's cupped hands, warm as a small animal. "Ash. Listen. This part matters. If they come in, you are a refugee child I took in out of charity, you speak nothing but boat-talk, and you have never once seen me do anything stranger than misfile a receipt."

"I'm good at pretending not to understand," Ash said. "I did it for two years in Ferris Cross."

"That's my girl. Or boy. Or sovereign dragon-heir, we can discuss pronouns when we're not about to be arrested."

"Ash works," Ash said. "Ash always works."

Boots in the street. Many boots, with the particular heavy rhythm of people enjoying their jobs. Lantern-light crawled up the shutters in bars.

Noor blew out the lamp — the last thing in the room that could plausibly be called innocent — and stood between Ash and the stairs, and Ash watched something settle over them like a coat: the shopkeeper's stoop, the harmless hands, the whole gray costume of seven years. Only their eyes stayed un-gray, fixed on Ash's chest, on the small glow leaking now visibly through the weave of Ash's shirt, gold as a struck match behind cloth.

"Noor," Ash whispered. "It's showing."

"I know."

"If they see it—"

"I know." Noor's jaw worked. Then, quietly, in old-tongue, so old it predated kingdoms and laws and possibly shame: *"Little flame. If it comes to choosing, hide yourself. Don't wait for me."*

*"You waited seven years,"* Ash answered, in the same tongue, and watched Noor flinch as if the words had hands.

The knocking, when it came, did not wait for an answer. The door shook on its hinges. Voices outside — and one of them, cutting through the rest, cold and level and somehow familiar, giving orders that were not *break it down* but something worse, something careful: *"Gently. Gently, if you please. We're not barbarians. Yet."*

Lantern-bars swung across the wall. The boots came around the side of the shop, toward the back stairs, toward the room where a banned charm-seller stood in front of a burning child.

And Ash felt the ember do the thing they dreaded most and understood best: it flared. Not fear. Invitation. Warmth pouring upward through their ribs, toward the floor, toward the deep folded dark beneath the city, calling to the gate the way one fire calls to another across a cold country — *here, here, I am here, come and see.*

Below Corvane, in the house that wasn't a house, the dying door heard it.

The hum climbed back up a note.

Then another.

And every Oathkeeper on Charm Row stopped mid-step, every lantern in every hand dipping toward the cobblestones, as the ground under the gray city glowed — briefly, unmistakably, for the first time in seven years — gold.
