## 7. Sarel at the Door

*Noor*

The trouble with hiding a door beneath your floorboards for seven years, Noor thought, was that eventually you had to stand on it.

They stood on it now. The shuttered house below the charm-shop smelled of dust and old salt — the salt of a sea that existed on no chart — and Ash sat cross-legged against the sealed archway, chin propped in both hands, watching Noor pace the length of the cellar like a cat deciding which shelf to knock things from.

"You're doing the walking," Ash said, in the old tongue first, then, catching themselves, in Corvane's trade-speech. They were getting better at it with alarming speed. Children absorbed languages the way sand absorbed water. "The walking where you pretend you are not terrified."

"I am not terrified," Noor said. "I am *strategically pacing.*"

"You are terrified," Ash agreed pleasantly, and the fire in their eyes guttered — just for a heartbeat, just enough to make Noor's stomach drop — before flaring again. That was the thing Noor could not stop noticing. Each hour the ember burned lower. Each hour Ash's smile got a little more effortful, a little more like something worn rather than felt.

Noor knelt by the gate. Beneath seven years of dust and one very determined spider, the seal still glimmered faintly — the shape of a hand pressed into stone, waiting for a hand that had sworn never to press it again.

"I'm going to say something," Noor told Ash, "and you must not repeat it to anyone, including the door."

"The door already knows everything," Ash said. "Doors are very nosy."

"Then I'll say it anyway." Noor exhaled. "I think we have until dawn. Maybe less. Sarel found the census thread; she'll pull it until it unravels, and when it unravels it will unravel us. Which means tonight I do what I swore I would never do. I open this gate while there is still fire left to open it for."

Ash didn't cheer. Ash did something worse — reached out and put one small, soot-grimed hand over Noor's wrist, the way an adult comforts a child.

"Good," they said simply. "It was a bad oath. My grandmother says—" and here they slipped back into the old tongue, words that tasted of cedar smoke and tide-pools, "*—a promise made to fear is a cage with your own name on it.*"

Noor was still turning that over — grandmothers in Vessia apparently had no business being that wise — when every ward in the cellar lit up at once, white and cold as a struck bell.

Someone was coming down the stairs.

And Noor knew, before the footsteps even resolved into weight and rhythm, exactly who. Seven years, and they still knew her step.

"Sarel," they said to the dark. "You always did ignore the sign that says *closed.*"

* * *

*Sarel*

Sarel Ondrat, Oathkeeper Third Rank, holder of the Ledger, keeper of the city's magic-census and therefore keeper of its lies — because she was increasingly certain the ledger contained at least one — came down the stairs with her lantern high and her hand nowhere near her blade, which was itself a kind of statement.

"Noor Reyes," she said. "Charm-seller. Purveyor of lucky buttons. Fraud to the elderly of three districts."

"Fraud implies my buttons don't work," Noor said from the shadows beside the far wall. "My buttons work beautifully. Ask Widow Marsh."

"Your buttons work because you're pouring a thimbleful of real magic through them, once a week, small enough to slip under the census." Sarel lifted the lantern. There. Against the wall, arms crossed, wearing the same insufferable calm she remembered from a lifetime ago — from before the gate, before the night the sky over Corvane turned the color of a healing bruise, before Merrin. "Seven years, Reyes. Seven years I audited your shop's ledgers and found nothing, and do you know why?"

"Impeccable bookkeeping?"

"Because I *wanted* nothing," Sarel said. "I wanted you to be exactly what you appeared. Because if you weren't — if the person who survived that night was standing behind a counter selling lucky buttons while I filed reports — then I'd have to ask why you ran. And I wasn't ready to know."

She hadn't planned to say any of that. It came out anyway, the way truth does when you've been carrying it down too many stairs.

Noor's face did something complicated. "Sarel—"

"There's a child behind you," Sarel said, because it was easier than the rest. She stepped past Noor, lantern raised, and looked down at the small figure seated against the sealed arch. Small figure whose eyes were — Sarel stopped. Her breath stopped with her.

Fire. Not reflected fire. Fire *from within*, banked low, the way coals look through ash, but unmistakably, impossibly alive.

"That's not possible," she heard herself say. "Dragon-fire died out. It's in the histories. Appendix C."

"Appendix C is wrong about several things," said the child, in flawless Corvane trade-speech with an accent from nowhere on any map. "Would you like me to tell you what else it's wrong about? It's wrong about the Oath."

* * *

*Noor*

"Don't," Noor said quickly. "Ash, love, this is not the moment for history lessons—"

But it was, of course, exactly the moment. Sarel had them cornered in a cellar with a forbidden gate underfoot; there would never be a gentler moment, and Noor had spent seven years learning that gentle moments don't exist, you only get moments and what you dare to do inside them.

"She's Oathkeeper Third Rank," Noor said instead, to Ash. "Her order bound the gate. Bound *me*. If she reports what she sees here—"

"If she reports what she sees here," Ash said, with the terrible patience of the very young, "she reports a lie she has already begun to doubt. Look at her hands, keeper-of-the-door. She holds the lantern like a question."

Noor looked. Sarel's hands, wrapped white-knuckled around the lantern handle, were trembling — not with fear. With the particular shaking of someone holding something they are afraid they will have to set down.

"Ash," Noor said, quieter. "What are you going to do?"

"What the fire remembers," said Ash, and closed their eyes, and opened them wide.

* * *

*Sarel*

Later — much later, in reports Sarel would never file, in confessions she would only half-make — she would try to describe what the child showed her, and fail, because it was not a picture. It was a memory with the temperature of blood.

She saw the founding of the Oath. Not as the histories told it — brave wards thrown up to contain a wild kingdom's dangerous magic — but from the other side of the door. She saw Vessia whole: towers like reeds, streets washed twice daily by a courteous tide, dragons overhead in slow wheeling constellations, and people, ordinary people, bakers and boat-menders and one girl who kept bees on a roof. A country.

Then she saw the war Corvane did not call a war, because you cannot go to war with a country you have decided to erase from the maps. She saw the survivors driven through the last living doorway. She saw the Oath forged — and here her stomach turned over, because she recognized the signatures. Her order's founders. Her own oath-words, older than she knew, spoken in a cellar by men who wrote, in the margin of the binding-scroll, in a clerk's tidy hand: *Let none pass out. Let them keep the door shut from within. Let the country forget it is a country.*

Not protection. Containment. The Oath had never been a wall around danger. It was a lid on a pot of survivors, and it had been clamped down while the fire underneath slowly starved.

And she saw Merrin. Gods, she saw Merrin — young, laughing, standing at this exact gate seven years ago, saying *someone should open it, Sare, someone should just open it* — and then the counter-force arriving, and the flash, and the long gray years after, during which Sarel had told herself Merrin died defending Corvane from whatever was behind the door.

Merrin had died trying to open it. Trying to save these people. And Sarel had spent seven years sharpening herself into the instrument of the ones who killed her.

The lantern slipped from her fingers. Noor caught it — of course Noor caught it, quick hands, seven years of pretending to be slow.

"Ash," Sarel said. Her voice came out in pieces. "How old are you?"

"Nine," said Ash.

"And the dragon. The one dying."

"My grandmother," Ash said. "Sort of. Dragons are everyone's grandmother, if you let them be."

Sarel looked at Noor. Noor looked back, wary as a stray that had been kicked too many times, and something in Sarel's chest — something that had been clenched since a cellar seven years ago — finally, painfully, let go.

"Merrin asked you to open it," Sarel said. "That night. Didn't they."

Noor's jaw worked. "...Every day for a year. And I said no, every day, because I'd sworn."

"So did I," Sarel said. "Sworn, I mean. To the same lie." She straightened. She picked up the lantern herself, deliberately, and turned its light away from the children and toward the stairs she'd come down. "Then here is what happens now. In approximately two hours, my fellow Oathkeepers will arrive in force — I bought you those hours by filing a deliberately confusing report about a smuggling ring in the fish-market, which they will investigate with great enthusiasm, because fish smuggling is the only thing that makes them happy. After that, we are out of confusion."

"*We,*" Noor repeated carefully.

"Do not make me say it twice, Reyes, I am having an extremely difficult evening." Sarel unclasped the Ledger from her belt — the true one, the census of every spark of magic in Corvane — and held it over the lantern flame until the corners caught. It burned green, then gold, then gone. "There. As far as the Order is concerned, I am now a traitor with no evidence and a very suspicious fish-market report."

The cellar was silent except for the soft crackle of ash drifting down.

"Well," said Noor at last, and their voice cracked in the middle of it, warm and wrecked, "the widow Marsh is going to be heartbroken about the buttons."

"She'll survive." Sarel crouched down to Ash's level, and her voice gentled into something she barely recognized as her own. "Young person. You said the door is dying with the dragon."

Ash nodded.

"Then we shouldn't keep it waiting," Sarel said, and stood, and set her shoulder against the darkness beyond the seal like a woman preparing to hold a line — only this time, she understood at last which side of the line she stood on.

Above them, faintly, through seven feet of stone and earth, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Sooner than two hours, then. Someone had read between her lines.

Noor pressed their palm flat above the old handprint in the stone, eyes closed, and whispered, "Merrin, watch this."

The seal began, slowly, wonderfully, to glow.
