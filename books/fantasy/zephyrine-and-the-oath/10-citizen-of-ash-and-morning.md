## 10. Citizen of Ash and Morning

The first thing I learned about mornings in a living country is that they are loud.

Vessia's dawn came up gold the day after the oath broke, and it came with birdsong — real birdsong, not the echo-birds that had sung the same three notes for seven years. Somewhere below the dragon's terrace, someone was laughing. Actually laughing, not the hollow rehearsal of laughter that ghosts do when they remember being people. I lay on warm stone with the ember in my chest doing something new — not guttering, not burning, just *humming*, like a kettle content to sit — and I listened to a whole country clear its throat.

Noor found me there. They looked different. Smaller, somehow, and larger at the same time. Mortality will do that, I suppose. When you can no longer wait out a problem for a hundred years, your face starts taking the present seriously.

"You're up early," Noor said.

"The sun was up first. I didn't want it to feel unaccompanied."

Noor sat down beside me with the careful grunt of a person whose knees have recently become mortal news. Below us, Vessia was busy relearning its own name. Streets that had been gray suggestion were now cobblestone you could stub a toe on. The harbor — which had been a painting of a harbor, more or less, painted by a tired dragon — had filled overnight with actual water, actual fish, and one extremely confused fisherman who had been mid-cast when the world ended around him seven years ago and had simply never finished the motion until this morning.

"Look at them," Noor said softly.

I looked. Survivors coming up from the cellars where they'd hidden when the Oathkeepers sealed the door. Ghosts fading at the edges but lingering, stubborn as stains, unwilling to leave before they saw how it ended. And everywhere, small flames — the dragon's gift, passed hand to hand like bread — flickering in the palms of bakers and weavers and one elderly woman who held hers at arm's length and demanded to know if it could also toast cheese.

"It's real," I said. "It's all real again."

"Mm." Noor was quiet a moment. "The dragon asked me something, before it slept."

"I know. You answered."

"I answered *wrong*, by every measure I was raised on." They said it like a man reporting the weather. "And it was the best answer I've ever given."

* * *

We stayed in Vessia eleven days. Long enough for the ghosts to finish their goodbyes — most of them went into the flame willingly, folding themselves into it like letters into envelopes — and long enough for me to learn that a rekindled country smells like wet stone and woodsmoke and somebody, somewhere, always baking.

The dragon itself had gone down into the mountain's heart to sleep, its fire distributed among the living like a hearth divided among a family. Before it went, it pressed its great gray brow against my chest, right over the ember, and spoke in the old tongue that I had carried across two worlds without understanding a word of it until now.

*"Little lantern,"* it said. *"You carried me well. Now put me down. A flame is not meant to be a burden; it is meant to be a table everyone sits around."*

Sarel stayed three days, then went back through the gate to Corvane, because — as she put it, with the grim precision of a woman rewriting her own ledger — "someone has to explain to the Oath what it actually was, and I am unfortunately the only person alive with both the rank and the grudges necessary to be believed." She left her Oathkeeper's pin on the gatehouse lintel. Then she picked it up again, considered it, and dropped it in the harbor instead, where an actual fish ate it, or at least swallowed it briefly before spitting it out, which Sarel declared symbolic and refused to discuss further.

Before she crossed, she stopped in front of Noor. For a moment neither of them spoke — seven years of hunting and hiding standing in a doorway together, awkward as dance partners who'd forgotten the steps.

"Zhou," she said finally. "Your shop is going to be seized as evidence."

"I assumed."

"Don't assume. It's going to be seized, and then it's going to be quietly returned to a charm-seller named Zhou, whose census records I am going to lose in a very tragic filing accident." She paused. "You still owe me for the inspection I failed to fail you on. Seven years of interest."

"Come to dinner sometime," Noor said. "I'll cook. You'll complain. We'll call it even."

"That," said Sarel, "is the first honest bargain anyone from Vessia has ever offered me." And she walked through the gate with her back straight, carrying nothing but her own doubt, which I suspect weighed less than she thought.

* * *

The gate itself changed on the twelfth day.

Doors are funny things. Sealed, they're walls with ambitions. But nobody sealed ours anymore — the unbinding had taken care of that — and a door that stands open stops being a barrier and starts being a threshold, and a threshold that stays open long enough becomes a road.

It happened gradually. First traders' rumors drifted through: talk in Corvane's markets of a shuttered house on a gray street where the door sometimes stood ajar and the air inside smelled of salt and baking bread. Then the rumor grew legs. Then legs brought boots. Then, one gray morning, a Corvane fishwife marched through holding a basket of onions and demanding to trade them for "whatever impossible thing you people smoke over here."

She left with a jar of ghost-salt — harmless now, mostly flavorful — and came back the next week with her sister. By spring there was a market on the Vessian side, stalls and haggling and a child from Corvane who fell in the harbor and was fished out by the same fisherman who'd finally finished his cast, and who told his parents afterward that the water had *tasted like stories*.

The old tongue came back the way roads fill with travelers: slowly, then all at once. Children picked it up fastest. Children always do. Words that had lived only in my mouth — my mouth, and the dragon's, and the ember's — suddenly had somewhere to go. That's all language ever wanted, I think. To be handed on.

Which brings me to the garden.

* * *

They gave Noor back the lot where the charm-shop stood, exactly as promised, tragic filing accident and all. The shop itself was gone — burned in the raid, or perhaps it had simply decided, in the way of things connected to oaths, that it had been pretending long enough. Just a square of ash-gray ground between standing walls, in a city that was slowly remembering color.

Noor stood in the ruins for a long time the first morning. I watched from the doorway, holding two cups of tea going cold, waiting to see what a person does with the place where they buried their own life.

What Noor did was kneel down, crumble the ash between mortal fingers, and say, "Well. Excellent soil, at least."

We planted in March. Well — Noor planted. I was assigned quality control, which meant eating strawberries prematurely and pronouncing judgment. Noor planted things with patient hands and muttered instructions in the old tongue, because it turned out that words carry better when you speak them to what you love, and Noor loved the garden the way reformed liars love the truth: fiercely, and slightly embarrassed about it.

"What are you saying to them?" I asked once, crouched beside a row of seedlings.

"Telling them where the sun comes up. Where the water pools after rain. Their names." Noor patted the soil. "Everything I wish someone had told me when I arrived somewhere new."

The garden became other things. First a place, then a habit, then — inevitably — a school. Not officially. Officially it remained a garden where a former gatekeeper grew unreasonable quantities of mint. Unofficially, children came.

They came the way children come anywhere: drawn by the smell of baking, the promise of a story, the suspicion that something interesting was happening without them. Corvane children and Vessian children, dragon-marked and plain, climbing the fence (there was a fence for exactly nine days) to hear Noor tell the story of the door — the true version, oathless, with all the cowardice and courage left in it, because Noor refused to edit themselves into heroism.

"I abandoned my post," they'd say, and forty children would gasp on cue. "For seven years I sold charms that did nothing, and told myself I was guarding the door by staying near it. Do you know what that's called?"

"Lying!" the children would chorus, delighted.

"Hypocrisy!" someone would add, having learned it from an older sibling.

"Both," Noor would agree solemnly. "And then a child knocked on my door speaking a language older than kingdoms, with fire in her eyes, and I had to decide whether I was a keeper of doors or a keeper of people."

"And what are you?" some small voice would ask. Every time. It was ritual by then.

"A citizen," Noor would say. "Same as you. Now — who can count to ten in the old tongue? Ash, demonstrate. Try not to show off."

I always showed off.

* * *

This evening, years later or next week — the days here are generous, and I've stopped counting — I sit on the garden wall while the last light goes amber over Corvane's gray rooftops, which are grayer than ever and somehow no longer sad about it. The lesson has ended. The children have gone home trailing mint and new words. Noor is watering the far bed, arguing quietly with a tomato plant that has opinions about its trellis.

My chest is warm. Not burning. Never just burning, anymore. The ember spread itself thin years ago — into the harbor, the hearths, the mouths of children learning to sing counting songs in a dead language that forgot to stay dead — but a little of it stayed home in me, the way a kitchen stays warm after everyone's eaten.

A girl lingers at the fence. New face — Corvane side, river district, by the mud on her shoes. She looks at Noor, then at me, then at the space between worlds that isn't a wound anymore, just an open door on an ordinary street.

"Is it true," she calls up to me, "that you used to be the last one?"

I consider it. The last speaker. The last lantern. The last of a country that died and couldn't stop being born.

"No," I say, and hop down to open the gate. "I'm the first one. Come in — dinner's nearly ready, and you're just in time to learn the word for *welcome*."
