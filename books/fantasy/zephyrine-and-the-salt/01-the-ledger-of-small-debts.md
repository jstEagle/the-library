## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Salt Cathedral kept its accounts the way it kept its god: in white stone, under lock, and never quite explained.

Ulyana Nystrom had copied its ledgers for six years, and he knew to the copper what he cost.

He sat in the scriptorium's eastern gallery, where the morning light came through salt-glass windows and fell pale as skimmed milk across forty desks, thirty-one of them occupied. Scribes worked in the particular silence of people being watched, quills making small wet sounds like mice in a wall. Ulyana's own page was a tithe-roll for the parish of Lower Merrow: so many bushels of barley, so many fleeces, so many candles rendered in kind because the parish was poor and the cathedral was patient. Patience, he had learned, was just interest with better manners.

In the margin of his own ledger—page 411, the indenture record—he had once calculated the arithmetic of his life. His father had owed the cathedral eleven marks, four shillings, and tuppence for a winter of borrowed grain. The debt had passed to Ulyana at fourteen along with his father's name and his father's cough. Room, board, ink, candle-wax, the wool for one winter cloak, the mending of that cloak, the mending of the mend: each entry in a fair round hand, each one pushing the date of his freedom further down the page like a horizon you walked toward without ever arriving.

By his current tally he owed nine marks, seventeen shillings, and change. He had entered payments totaling nearly three times his father's original debt. He had stopped showing this arithmetic to the bursar years ago, when he understood that the bursar already knew and considered the observation impertinent. A debt, at Thornfell, was not meant to end. It was meant to be tended.

*Copied and checked,* he wrote at the foot of the Merrow roll, and blotted it, and reached for the next document in his stack, and that was when Brother Callow appeared at the end of the gallery and said, "Nystrom. Infirmary duty. Bring your kit."

Ulyana looked up. "I'm not an infirmarer."

"You're not anything yet. You're a scribe with steady hands who can write down what dying men say, which is worth more than what they say unrecorded. Move."

He moved. You learned not to argue in the cathedral the way water learns not to argue with a channel.

The infirmary occupied the cathedral's cold northern arm, where the salt in the walls drew moisture out of the air and out of everything else, including the sick. They called the stone merciful. Ulyana had seen enough dried-out patients to think mercy was doing a lot of work in that sentence. The dying pilgrim was in the last alcove, behind a curtain the color of old teeth, and he had been found two days ago at the mountain gate with frostbitten feet and no name anyone could read off him, because what he'd been muttering wasn't in any language the brothers recognized.

Ulyana recognized it.

He didn't tell Brother Callow that. He set up his desk-board beside the cot, dipped his pen, and let the old man talk while he wrote down *sounds approximating* in the margin and felt his heart going strangely fast for work this sedentary.

The pilgrim was perhaps seventy, gaunt, with a face weathered down to the geometry of bone. His lips were cracked white from the salt air. He talked in loops—the same phrases surfacing again and again, like drowned things bobbing up—and slowly, over an hour, the loops tightened and the story came clear in fragments.

A village. A valley. Names for things that matched nothing on any map Ulyana had ever copied, and he had copied most of them. A road that went somewhere the maps stopped pretending. And a word, recurring, worn smooth by repetition: *Zephyrine.*

There was no Zephyrine. Every child in Thornfell knew the little rhyme—*asked the way to Zephyrine, and the mountain swallowed him*—which was how children learned that some questions were dangerous. Three hundred years ago, according to the cathedral's own histories, which Ulyana had copied twice, there had been a country beyond the Thornfell massif, and there had been gods in it, and then there had been a treaty, and then there had been nothing but mountains and the honest empty map. The old gods had gone silent. That was the whole of the catechism: the false gods starved, the true God remained, the border stood, be grateful.

"I carried it," the pilgrim said suddenly, clearly, in ordinary trade-tongue, and his hand came out from under the blanket and caught Ulyana's wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong. "Carried it thirty year. Nobody left to carry it."

His other hand pressed something into Ulyana's palm and folded Ulyana's fingers shut over it.

It was small, hard-edged, cold. Crusted in salt like a pebble pulled from a drying pan.

"Report it," Brother Callow said from the foot of the cot, not unkindly. "Whatever he gave you, whatever he said—it goes to the archivist. That's procedure, scribe."

"Yes, brother," Ulyana said.

"Say the name back to me," the pilgrim whispered. "So it isn't only me saying it. A name needs two mouths or it dies."

Ulyana hesitated. Then he bent close, and said the name the old man had been circling all morning—not the country, the other one, the personal one, a name like a door swinging—and the pilgrim smiled with cracked lips and died between one breath and the next, quietly, as if a debt had finally been paid in full.

* * *

Here is what Ulyana did not do, in the hours that followed:

He did not report the token. He did not report that the mutterings were recognizably Old Zephyrine, which he should not have known himself except that his grandmother had sung lullabies in it before the cathedral's correction officers had explained to her, gently, over several years, that those songs were unhealthy. He did not report that the name the pilgrim made him speak had tasted familiar on his tongue, like a word from a dream he'd had the courtesy to forget.

Instead he finished his shift, ate his supper of barley and salt fish, returned to his cell, barred the door, and opened his fist by lamplight.

The token was carved from something that wasn't quite stone and wasn't quite bone, white as the cathedral walls, worn smooth on one side. On the smooth side, under the crust of salt, which he flaked away with his thumbnail, there was a sigil: a spiral folded through itself, a line that crossed its own border and kept going. Moss-green tarnish filled the grooves.

Ulyana knew that mark. He had copied it exactly once, six years ago, in his first year, from a heresy-suppuration list—a document cataloguing forbidden signs to be expunged wherever found. The archivist had watched him copy that list personally, which he had assumed was standard supervision. Now he wondered if it had been standard, or if someone had already known something about the boy Nystrom and wanted to see whether the sigil would mean anything to him.

It meant something. It meant his hands were shaking, which annoyed him, because he prided himself on steady hands. Steady hands were his entire market value.

He turned the token over. On the reverse, scratched thin and recent, in a hand that shook: coordinates. Not map-coordinates. Mountain-man coordinates—the kind smugglers used, bearings against peaks and water-courses, the private grammar of people who moved cargo where the cathedral preferred gaps. Ulyana had run that grammar for two years before his debt recalled him to the scriptorium, carrying untaxed brandy and unsanctioned books over passes the wardens didn't watch. He read the scratchings the way other men read scripture.

They pointed into the interior of the Thornfell range. Not over it. Into it—into the blank white space at the center of every map, where cartographers wrote *elevations unknown, travel forbidden* and, in older maps that Ulyana had occasionally found and occasionally failed to surrender, wrote nothing at all, leaving the emptiness honest and bare.

*A name needs two mouths or it dies.*

He said the name again, alone, quietly. The lamplight didn't gutter. Nothing happened. It was only a dead man's obsession in his palm, heavy as a coin and considerably less spendable.

All the same, he did not sleep. He lay in his narrow cell listening to the cathedral settle around him—that vast mineral creak the building made at night, as if the salt itself were thinking—and he thought about procedure. Procedure said: report. Procedure said the archivist would take the token, enter it in the suppuration ledger, and dissolve it in brine, and the name would have one fewer mouth holding it, and Ulyana would receive a commendation worth perhaps a shilling against his debt, which was to say, nothing at all, forever.

Or he could do the small disobedient thing. The smallest one. Keep a dead man's keepsake one night longer than permitted. Tell himself he'd surrender it tomorrow, next week, when it was convenient. He knew this rationalization intimately; he used it every time he kept back one coin from a debt-collection run, every time he took the long road past a window he liked to look through. Small debts, small thefts. He was an honest man in every way that was counted, and a thief in every way that wasn't.

Toward dawn he wrapped the token in a scrap of oilcloth and sewed it into the lining of his cloak, beneath the patch he'd mended twice, and then he slept like a man who has committed a crime so small that even his conscience couldn't be bothered to press charges.

* * *

Three days later, the summons came.

Not to the archivist—to the inquisitorial offices, which occupied a tower nobody entered cheerfully. Ulyana climbed the spiral stair with his ledger under his arm like a shield, rehearsing innocence. He hadn't been seen with the token. The curtain had been drawn; Brother Callow had been at the far end of the ward. There was no witness, no record, no thread leading from the dead pilgrim to the scribe Nystrom.

The office at the top of the stair belonged to Inquisitor Vey.

She was younger than her reputation, which was considerable, and she did not look up when he entered. She was writing. He stood on the designated floor-mark, a white square inlaid in the dark wood, and waited, and counted his heartbeats, and got to two hundred before she finished her sentence and glanced up with eyes the pale gray of the salt cliffs.

"Scribe Nystrom." Her voice was pleasant. That was the worst part; everyone said so. "You attended the death of an unidentified pilgrim, eight days ago. You recorded his utterances."

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"Read me the name he kept repeating."

And there it was, laid out flat on her desk between them: the trap, or maybe not a trap, maybe just a question, and no way to know which until after he answered. If he claimed he couldn't render it, she would ask why a man who spoke three languages and half-read a fourth had failed. If he gave the name, he confirmed he'd understood the language of a heretic. Either way—

"The utterances were not transcribable," he said. "I recorded them phonetically. Approximation only."

"Mm." She turned a page. "The cathedral is aware," she said, in the tone of a woman reading a laundry list, "that prayers are being answered again."

The words went into Ulyana like a knife going into snow. He kept his face still. Six years of copying other people's confessions taught you stillness.

"That is not doctrine, Inquisitor."

"No. Doctrine says otherwise, and doctrine is written by the winners, and I have read the originals." She set down her pen at last and looked at him fully, and Ulyana understood that this conversation had begun long before he entered the room, and that he had already lost some portion of it whose size he could not yet calculate. "Thank you for your time, scribe. Your transcription of the pilgrim's effects inventory was very thorough. Almost thorough enough."

She let him go. He descended the spiral stair with his legs working on habit alone, and it was only in the shadow of the lower archive, among the shelves of tithe-rolls and confession-records that smelled of dust and cold stone, that he let himself understand what she had told him, and what she had not.

She had not asked about the token. She had not searched him. Which meant either she didn't know it existed—or she knew exactly where it was, sewn under a twice-mended patch, and was letting him keep walking, curious little fish on a line, to see what he would lead them to.

That night, in his cell, Ulyana took out the token and held it under the lamp, and for the first time he noticed what exhaustion had hidden from him before: deep in the spiral's fold, where the green tarnish pooled, the sigil was faintly warm. Warmer than his hand. Warm as a living thing held cupped.

Somewhere in the mountains, in the blank space at the center of the maps, something that had been silent for three hundred years had heard an old man die whispering a name, and had begun, very slowly, to listen back.

Ulyana sewed the token deeper into his cloak and told himself, in the fair round hand of his own internal ledger, that this changed nothing.

The entry did not balance.
