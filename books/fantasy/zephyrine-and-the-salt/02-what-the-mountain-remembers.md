## 2. What the Mountain Remembers

The ledger said the village of Harrowmere owed the Salt Cathedral eleven marks, four shillings, and a quantity of tallow that Ulyana sincerely hoped he would not have to carry back down the mountain himself.

He read the entry again by the grey light of dawn, standing in the doorway of the cathedral's counting-house with his breath fogging. Eleven marks. Four shillings. Tallow. Somewhere in the fine hand of some other scribe, in some other year, those numbers had been a sentence: *Ulyana Nystrom owes the Cathedral of Thornfell everything he is worth and several things he is not.* His own debt was longer than this one, but it was made of the same arithmetic. That was the trick of indenture. It did not feel like chains. It felt like columns.

He folded the ledger page into his coat, shouldered his pack, and left before the bells.

There were two roads to Harrowmere. The pilgrim road went east around the shoulder of the fell, wide enough for carts, dotted with wayshrines where the devout could leave copper coins they could not afford. The other road went up through Blackgill, a smuggler's cut that shaved half a day off the journey and asked no questions about what you carried or why you preferred not to be seen carrying it.

Ulyana had learned the second road at fourteen, running debts for a man named Corvane who had bought his contract from the cathedral on a seasonal arrangement the cathedral never asked too closely about. Corvane was dead now — drowned, or married, depending which story in lowtown you believed — but his roads remained, worn into the mountain by generations of men who moved goods the cathedral's ledgers said did not exist. Salt that hadn't been tithed. Wool that hadn't been weighed. Once, memorably, a bride.

Ulyana took Blackgill.

It wasn't only the time. He told himself it was the time, and the weather, and the state of his boots, and all of it was true, and none of it was the whole truth. The whole truth was that he had not been able to sleep since the pilgrim died in the scriptorium six days ago, since the salt-crusted token had sat in his palm like a coal that refused to go out, since the old man's cracked lips had shaped a word that Ulyana had written ten thousand times in transcription and never once heard spoken aloud.

*Zephyrine.*

He had checked the archives afterward, quietly, the way you check a lock you already know is broken. The name appeared in exactly three documents in the cathedral's collection: a treaty fragment, an index card with a burn across it, and a hymnal from which an entire psalm had been razored out so cleanly the page still smelled of blade-oil. In every case the word had been struck through or excised. Not forgotten. Removed. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

So he climbed through Blackgill instead of around it, because the high paths were where the mountain kept its oldest things, and he wanted — God help him, he wanted — to see whether anything up here still remembered how to say the name.

* * *

Blackgill in autumn was a throat of grey stone with a stream running down its floor, and the path picked its way along the stream's edge on stones the smugglers had laid and maintained without ever once admitting to it. Ulyana knew the markers: the split boulder shaped like a tooth, the cairn with the iron ring set in its top for roping loads, the birch growing sideways out of the cliff with a horseshoe nailed to its trunk that no one would ever admit to nailing there. The horseshoe was rusted to lace. It had been nailed facing upward, which meant luck running out rather than in, and Corvane had laughed for a full minute when fourteen-year-old Ulyana pointed that out.

*Then we'll be careful, boy. Careful is cheaper than lucky.*

By midday he was above the treeline, and the wind came off the high fells with nothing between it and the northern sea, and the light did that thing it does up there where every shadow looks carved. He ate hard bread and harder cheese and let himself imagine, just briefly, being a man who walked this road because he chose to. A free man with sore boots is still a free man. Then he wrapped the rest of the bread, stood, and stopped.

Someone had built a cairn ahead of him. New. The stones were bright where hands had turned them, unweathered, stacked with the particular care of people who are not building for function. And tied to a wand driven into its top fluttered strips of cloth — seven of them, he counted, each knotted, each faded a different degree. Prayers, in the old highland fashion. The cathedral tolerated them grudgingly; Vey's office called them *residual folk practice* in its reports, which was the kind of phrase that meant *we have decided not to burn anyone over this yet.*

Ulyana should have walked past. He was behind schedule and the weather was turning and a debt-collector loitering at prayer-cairns was a debt-collector with something to report, if anyone saw him.

He looked at the knots anyway. And on the third strip of cloth, brown with age, he saw the mark stitched in thread gone almost the color of the wool itself: a vertical line crossed by three descending horizontals, the lowest one hooked like a fishing barb.

He knew it. Not from the archives — the archives had been scrubbed. From somewhere older and less explicable. From the shape his own hand made when he doodled in margins and then scratched out. From the geometry of the razoned-out psalm's surviving first letters, if you knew how to read the ghosts of words. A Zephyrine sigil, stitched by some grandmother's grandmother's grandmother, kept alive in cloth the way seeds keep alive in the walls of old houses.

The wind moved. The strip of cloth turned on its wand, and for a moment Ulyana would have sworn the hooked line caught the light and held it.

He walked on faster than dignity allowed.

* * *

Harrowmere came down on him in the late afternoon: twelve houses, a sheep-fold, a mill with a wheel turning in a trickle of water, and above it all on a shelf of rock, a shrine stone.

He had collected here twice before, always in bad weather, always greeted by the headman Aldric with the flat courtesy of a man paying money he'd rather spend on his people. The shrine stone had always been there — a grey upright slab taller than a man, furred with moss, unremarkable. Highland villages were full of such stones. Nobody prayed at them. The cathedral's surveyors had catalogued it forty years ago as *standing stone, pre-settlement, no cultic significance*, which was the driest sentence Ulyana had ever copied, and he had copied ledgers for a living.

Today there were people at it. Two dozen at least, from the village and the outlying farms, standing in the wet grass with their hats off, and the sound reaching him up the path was not prayer in any cadence the cathedral taught. It was simpler. It was people saying thank you, over and over, in the voices of those who don't quite believe their own luck.

Aldric found him at the village's edge. The headman was sixty, built like the doorframe of a byre, and today his eyes were red-rimmed and shining.

"Nystrom." He said the name like a bill arriving. Then, ruinously, he smiled. "You've come for the tithe."

"I've come for the tithe," Ulyana agreed.

"You're a week early."

"The roads were good."

Aldric looked past him at the sky, which was doing something complicated in the west — great bruised stacks of cloud building over the fells when the morning had been clear. "Roads were good," he repeated, softly, as if the words belonged to a different language. "Come and see the stone before you take my money. I'd show you for nothing, and I'd count it cheap."

Everything in Ulyana said: collect the debt, eat, sleep, leave at dawn, do not stand in crowds of praying peasants with your ledger in your pocket like an accusation. He had survived six years of indenture by being precisely as curious as his work required and not one measure more.

"Yes," he heard himself say. "Show me."

* * *

The crowd parted for Aldric's shoulders and closed again behind them, and Ulyana got his first clear look at the stone and felt the bottom drop out of the afternoon.

The moss was gone. Someone — many someones — had scrubbed the eastern face of the slab with river sand and lye until the grey skin of centuries came away, and beneath it the stone was not stone-colored at all. It was white, veined faintly with quartz, and carved from crown to base in lines of script that spiraled inward like water finding a drain.

He knew the script. He had never seen it before in his life and he knew it the way you know your mother's voice under other voices. His eyes went along the spirals and something behind his breastbone translated without asking permission — not words, exactly, more like the shapes meanings make before they become words. *Bound. Kept. Given back in season.* A promise, or a threat, or both, written by hands that had considered those two things nearly synonymous.

"Three weeks ago," Aldric said beside him, "that face was green moss to the top. My grandfather's grandfather never saw it bare, nor his. Then Tam's girl — she's eight, her mother died last spring of the lung-rot—" Aldric's voice snagged on itself and restarted. "She went up to the stone one night because she's a child and children talk to rocks, and she asked it for rain. We'd had none since midsummer. The wells were down to mud."

"And?" Ulyana said, though the bruised clouds over the western fells were already answering for the stone.

"It rained, lad. Not showered. *Rained.* For a day and a night, steady as a mill-wheel, filled every well and every cistern we have, and stopped the hour the cisterns brimmed like it had counted them." Aldric turned to look at him, and his face was terrible with hope. "And the moss wept off the stone that same night. Brine, Nystrom. Salt water, running down the face of it, and where it ran, the writing showed. The old ones' writing. My grandmother used to whisper there'd been another country once, before Thornfell, swallowed whole for our sins, and that its god slept under the hills till the world forgot him." He lowered his voice as though the stone might mind. "Seems the world has forgotten enough."

As if summoned, the first drops began. Fat, cold, unhurried. Around the stone, the villagers didn't scatter — they lifted their faces.

Ulyana stood very still and did the arithmetic he was trained to do, because arithmetic was the raft he had and he needed it badly.

Prayers answered. Precisely answered. Measured answers, rain to the brim and no further, like a merchant filling an order. Dormant for three hundred years, Aldric said, and waking now — and the dying pilgrim in the scriptorium had crawled halfway across Thornfell to put a token in the hand of a stranger, whispering the forbidden name, and the archive pages had not rotted away but been *removed*, and the cloth on the cairn in Blackgill bore sigils his own unconscious hand reproduced in margins—

Something old is waking, and it is not waking gently, and it is not waking randomly. It is waking the way a debtor wakes when the term of the loan comes due.

"Your tithe," he said. His voice came out level, and he was proud of that, and ashamed of the pride. "Eleven marks, four shillings. The tallow I'm waiving. It doesn't travel."

Aldric blinked at him, the spell cracking. "Waiving?"

"I'll write 'delivered in kind, consumed en route.'" Ulyana took out the ledger page and made the note against his knee, and his handwriting was as neat as ever, and the neatness felt like lying. "Congratulations on your rain."

"Nystrom." Aldric caught his sleeve as he turned. "You're a cathedral man. You know what they'll say when word of this travels. It's already traveling — folk from two valleys over came to look, and one of them will talk to a priest eventually, they always do." His grip tightened. "What do we tell them? What do we tell the inquisitors when they come?"

The honest answer was *I don't know*, and the fuller honest answer was *I am afraid I am going to find out from the inside,* and Ulyana gave neither. He looked at the white stone instead, at the spiral script drinking the rain, and one line of it near the base caught his eye in a way the others had not. It was smaller, cruder, cut later — an addition, a signature, the way a mapmaker signs the corner of his work. Three descending horizontals crossed by a vertical, the lowest stroke hooked like a barb.

The same mark. The same mark as the cloth, as the margin-doodles, as the ghost-letters of the mutilated psalm.

And beneath the signature, in characters he could not read but whose *shape* he understood the way you understand a door by its handle, two short words. His blood supplied them before any scholarship could, warm and certain and awful, in a tongue no living congregation spoke:

*Warden's line.*

Rain ran off the stone's white face and dripped from the hooked stroke of the sigil, regular as a pulse.

"Tell them the truth," Ulyana said at last. "You wanted rain. You got rain. Miracles are only crimes if somebody decides they are." He pulled his sleeve free, gently. "Keep your children away from the stone until I've settled accounts in Thornfell. All right?"

Aldric searched his face, found nothing useful there — debt collectors learn to have nothing useful in their faces — and nodded slowly.

* * *

Ulyana left Harrowmere at dusk, though the sensible thing was to stay the night. He told himself the inn was full, which was a lie; that the road was safe, which was a lie; that the exercise would do him good, which was the lie of a man running.

The storm followed him west, building over the fells with slow grandeur, and by full dark it broke — but not on Harrowmere. He looked back once from the high shoulder of the road and saw the rain falling in a single vast silver column, precise as a drawn line, onto the valley of the stone and nowhere else for twenty miles. Measured. Counted. Filled to the brim and no further.

Somewhere under these mountains, something was keeping its promises. Keeping them the way a lender keeps accounts.

Ulyana walked on into the dark with the ledger against his ribs and the pilgrim's salt token in his pocket, and it was not glowing — he would have sworn it was not glowing — but through the weave of his glove, faint as body heat, it had begun to feel like the warmest thing on the whole cold mountain.
