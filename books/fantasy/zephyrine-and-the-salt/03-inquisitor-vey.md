## 3. Inquisitor Vey

They came for him at the third bell of morning, when the scriptorium smelled of ink and cold tallow and his eyes had gone grainy from copying the tithe rolls.

Two brothers of the Watch, gray-robed, silent, standing on either side of his desk like a pair of shutters closing. Ulyana set down his pen with great care. He had learned long ago that the speed of your hands told the inquisitors everything they wanted to know before you said a word. A guilty man snatched his hands away. An innocent man finished his line.

He finished his line. Blotted it. Then he stood, folded his own ledger closed, and followed them out through the archive stacks, past the reliquary hall where the salt saints stood in their glass cases, down stairs that grew narrower as they went, until the stone around them stopped being architecture and started being a throat.

*The Undercroft.* Scribes did not go to the Undercroft. Scribes were brought there once, briefly, and afterward they were either promoted or not spoken about.

The room they put him in was small and dry — that was the first thing he noticed, the dryness, because everything in Thornfell's cathedral was faintly damp with brine except this, and dryness here was expensive. A table. Two chairs. A lamp with a good wick. On the table, arranged with the neatness of a place setting: a dead pilgrim's prayer token, salt-crusted, its cord snapped.

His stomach performed a slow, quiet revolution.

"Ink on your fingers still," said a voice behind him. "You didn't wash. Good. That tells me you were working when they came for you."

Inquisitor Vey was not what the novices imagined when they whispered her name. They imagined something tall and iron and terrible. She was small, perhaps forty, with a scribe's stoop beginning at her shoulders and ink stains of her own on the right cuff of her habit. Her face was unremarkable in the way of people who have decided to be unremarkable, which is its own kind of armor. Only her eyes moved quickly, cataloguing, and only her hands were perfectly still.

"Sit, Brother Nystrom." She said *brother* the way a customs officer says *sir*: correctly, and meaning nothing by it.

"I'm not ordained," Ulyana said. "Just indentured."

"Sit anyway."

He sat. The chair across from her was positioned so that he would have to look at the token, and he made himself look at it, because flinching away was also an answer.

"You collected a debt in Hallowmere two days ago," Vey said. "Fourteen coppers and one goose, per the village's schedule. You took the high road back because the low road floods. You passed through the pilgrim camp at the Cinder Ford on the evening of the second day. Tell me about the camp."

"It was a camp," Ulyana said. "People sleeping. Fires. The usual smell."

"Tell me about the man who died."

"I heard someone died. I didn't see it. I was passing through."

Vey nodded, unhurried, and turned the token over with one finger. It rotated on the tabletop with a soft grinding sound, like a shell turning in sand. "A man named Aldous Ferren, wool-factor, sixty-one years old, dying of the lung-rot. He walked three hundred miles to this cathedral to pray to the Salt Mother for his granddaughter's fever. He died at the Cinder Ford before he ever saw our doors. And in his last hour, a stranger sat with him. A stranger who was passing through." Her eyes came up. "Describe him."

"I never spoke to any dying man."

"No," Vey agreed pleasantly. "But you're going to describe him to me anyway, and we're both going to pretend that's strange, so let's skip ahead." She leaned forward slightly, elbows on the table, and for a moment the bureaucrat fell away and Ulyana saw the thing underneath — patient, precise, and very tired in the way of someone who has been awake for months watching a fire she cannot name spread through a country she loves. "Ulyana Nystrom. Indentured since age nine. Debt outstanding: eleven marks, six shillings, four coppers. You know the number exactly, don't you? You copy ledgers all day. You've done the arithmetic on your own soul more times than anyone in this building."

"Eleven marks, four shillings, seven coppers," Ulyana said. "It changed last quarter."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite approval. Recognition, maybe — one numbers person acknowledging another. "At your wage, that's nine more years. You'll be thirty-four when they cut your chain. If the lungs hold. Scriptors die young; the dust gets into everything." She tapped the token once. "I'm prepared to discuss that arithmetic with you. But first I want you to tell me why a debt-scribe carrying nothing but a receipt book came back from Hallowmere with salt under his bootnails from a road that doesn't touch salt anywhere along the high route."

The silence stretched. Ulyana counted his breaths, four in, six out, the old trick for steadying hands before a signature.

"He pressed it into my hand," Ulyana said finally. "The dying man. It was warm. Like it had been held too long. And he said a word I didn't know, and then he was dead, and I—" He stopped. Lied or didn't lie; every path here ended somewhere bad. "I should have reported it. I'm reporting it now."

"Yes," Vey said. "Nine hours late. What was the word?"

"I don't remember."

"You're a scribe. Your entire trade is remembering words."

"*Zephyrine,*" Ulyana said, and the word came out of him like a splinter coming out of skin, relieving and awful at once. The room seemed to lean in. Even the lamp flame straightened. "He said Zephyrine. And then he asked me if the Door still stood, and I said I didn't know what door, and he smiled like I'd lied to him, and then he died."

Vey sat back. She looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she found in his face seemed to settle some question she'd been carrying, because her shoulders dropped half an inch.

"Do you know what we do with heretics in Thornfell?" she asked.

"Burn them. Or worse, audit them."

"We used to burn them." She said it flatly, without pride. "Now we mostly file them. Three centuries of peace will do that to an institution — we got fat and administrative. Do you know how many reports of answered prayers crossed my desk last year?"

"I don't—"

"One hundred and forty," she said. "One hundred and forty claims. Shrine stones weeping brine. Cripples walking after touching moss. Rain called down on a barley field in Duncarrow by a child who couldn't have named a god if you paid her. One hundred and forty, and my predecessors filed ninety percent of them under *folk enthusiasm* and burned the interesting ones quietly." She drew a thin folio from inside her habit and laid it beside the token. "This year, we are at two hundred and sixty. And the year is half over."

Ulyana's mouth had gone dry. "That could be—"

"Coincidence? Fraud? Yes. I spent four months hoping so. I interviewed frauds. Most miracles are frauds; that's the oldest truth of the trade." She opened the folio and turned it toward him. Inside were pages in several hands — reports, sketches, transcripts. A sketch of a shrine stone with sigils scratched beneath drawn moss, and even in a clerk's rough copy Ulyana recognized the spirals-and-currents of the old country, the same marks he'd seen two days ago on a living stone with water running down its face. "Then I stopped hoping, because hope is just laziness wearing a nicer coat. Something is answering prayers, Nystrom. Something that has been asleep for three hundred years is waking up, and it is answering *selectively*, which means it has preferences, which means it has a mind, which means it has intentions. The cathedral's official position is that these are tremors of nothing. My position is that I am paid to know things, and I do not know what this is, and that frightens me more than heresy ever has."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because of the word." Vey closed the folio. "*Zephyrine*. That name hasn't appeared in a cathedral document since the Treaty of Unmaking. It appears nowhere in our liturgy, our histories, our maps — we redacted a nation, Nystrom. We didn't defeat Zephyrine; we erased it, and the erasure was so thorough that most of us can't even say what we erased. And now a dying pilgrim whispers it into the hand of a debt-scribe, and that debt-scribe carries the evidence home in his pocket like a keepsake instead of turning it in." She tilted her head. "Why did you keep it?"

And there it was, the real question under all the others, and Ulyana found he didn't have a clever answer, only the true one, which was somehow worse.

"Because no one had said it in three hundred years," he said slowly, "and it felt like — like finding a coin from a country that doesn't exist. It shouldn't be mine. But throwing it away felt like killing him twice."

Vey studied him. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, genuine, exhausted sound. "Do you know how many people I've interrogated who answer honestly when honesty incriminates them? Perhaps six. In nineteen years." She pushed the token across the table toward him. "Take it back."

He didn't move. "Is that a test?"

"Everything is a test. Take it."

He picked it up. It was cool now, inert, ordinary salt-crusted bone. But his palm remembered the warmth of it, and something under his sternum remembered too, a faint pull like a fisherman feeling a line go tight far below the boat.

"Here is my offer," Vey said, and her voice changed — became brisker, transactional, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Your indenture stands at eleven marks and change. The cathedral holds that paper. I can hold the pen that cancels it. Nine years of your life, Nystrom, signed away this afternoon, effective immediately. You walk out of here a free man with a stipend and a new trade, and all it costs you is the thing you're already suited for."

"Which is?"

"Copying." She smiled without warmth. "Names, dates, places. There are people — we call them resonants — whom the old gods have touched. They pray and things happen. They dream true. They bleed salt. Most of them don't know what they are. Some of them are gathering, whether they understand that yet or not, and some of whatever is waking is gathering *them*, the way a current gathers leaves." She slid a second sheet across: blank, headed with the cathedral's seal. "You move through lowtown. You collect debts from villages. You hear everything, because debt makes people talk. All I ask is that you write down what you hear. That's all. You're not arresting anyone. You're not burning anyone. You're a scribe, doing what scribes do — making a record — and when the storm comes, and it is coming, the record may be the only thing that saves these people from the kind of response this institution is capable of when it panics."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you finish copying tithes for nine years, and I find another informant, and when the storm comes you'll be exactly where you are now, chained to a desk, copying the names of the dead." She said it without menace, which made it worse. "Nystrom. I am not offering you a trap. I am offering you the least bad position on a board where every piece is already lost. Freedom, today, for ink. That is the whole bargain."

Ulyana turned the token over in his fingers. *Freedom.* The word had a taste, and the taste was copper. Nine years, returned to him whole — mornings he could spend however he chose, a door he could walk through in any direction, a life that belonged to himself. He had not let himself want it in so long that wanting it now felt like opening a fist around a hot coal.

And underneath the wanting, cold and clear, the other thing: *resonants.* People touched. People like — and the thought arrived before he could stop it — people like whoever taught that shrine stone in the highlands to weep. People like the child who prayed for rain and got it. If he signed, he would be hunting them with a pen instead of a torch, and the pen would feed the same fire.

"The man who gave me this," Ulyana said. "If I'd written his name on your page, what happens to his memory? His family?"

"Nothing happens to anyone," Vey said. "We watch. We wait. We try to understand what's waking before it wakes fully. That is genuinely all I intend — but I won't insult you by pretending the cathedral shares my patience. Which is exactly why I need people outside the hierarchy writing the record. Men like me are the brake, Nystrom. You'd be the hand on it."

It was a good speech. It was probably even sincere. That was the most dangerous thing about her.

Ulyana looked down at the blank page. The cathedral's seal gleamed dully in the lamplight, wax-red, patient. Somewhere above them, bells marked the quarter hour, and he thought about the highland shrine, the water running silver-bright down old stone while a village wept with joy, and the sigils beneath the moss that no living scribe should have been able to read — and realized, with a small cold shock, that he *had* read them. Instantly. Effortlessly. As though the shapes lived somewhere behind his eyes, filed among the debts.

He had assumed it was the smuggling roads, the old songs, something learned and forgotten. Sitting in the dry room with freedom offered to him in ink, Ulyana understood for the first time that it might not be learning at all.

It might be inheritance.

"I need time," he said.

"You have until tomorrow's third bell." Vey rose, gathered her folio, and paused at the door with her back to him. "One thing, freely given, since you gave me honesty. The token you carry — keep it against your skin tonight and look at it by morning. If it's warm, come to me. If it stays cold..." She glanced over her shoulder, and her quick eyes were almost kind. "Come to me anyway. Because either way, Nystrom, you've already begun to resonate, and neither of us can stop that now. The only question left is who writes you down first."

She left. The lamp burned. Ulyana sat alone with the token in his fist and the unsigned page between his hands, and slowly, so slowly he might have imagined it, the salt crust began, very faintly, to glow.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He tried the dormitory first, lay on his cot staring at the beams, and lasted an hour before the token's warmth through his shirt became unbearable — not painful, but present, the way a hand on your shoulder is present. So he rose, dressed in the dark, and walked.

The cathedral at night was a different animal. By day it was ledgers and queues and the smell of wet wool; by night it breathed. Salt wind moved through the cloisters in long slow exhalations. The reliquary saints caught moonlight in their glass cases and seemed, in the corner of the eye, to shift their weight. Ulyana kept to the scribes' corridors where he had the right to be, and told himself that was why his route took him past the archive stair, and told himself further that what stopped him at the bottom of it was caution rather than the sound.

Voices. Below. Two of them, low.

"...the girl in the washhouse," said one, a brother's murmur. "Vey flagged her. Hands that mend. We take her at dawn, quietly, before the lowtown market opens."

"And the scribe?"

"Vey's handling the scribe herself. Says he's useful." A pause, a dry chuckle. "Useful. You know what happened to the last man Vey called useful."

The footsteps receded up the far stair. Ulyana stood in the dark with his heart slamming and the token burning against his chest like a small sun, and did the arithmetic he was built for.

Dawn. The washhouse. Lowtown.

He had until tomorrow's third bell to give Vey his answer — and a girl with healing hands had until dawn to learn that the question had already been decided for her.

Ulyana turned toward the night door, and the token's glow spilled between his fingers like light through a keyhole, showing him the way out.
