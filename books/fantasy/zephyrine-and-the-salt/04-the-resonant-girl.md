## 4. The Resonant Girl

The name Vey wanted was written nowhere, which was how Ulyana knew it mattered.

She had given him three days. *Find the one the pilgrim spoke of,* she'd said, sliding a purse of coppers across the interrogation table like a woman settling a bar tab. *A name, a face, an address. Do that, and your ledger burns.* She'd even had the courtesy to have a clerk draft the annulment in advance, his debt to the cathedral tallied and struck through in official ink, waiting only for her seal.

Three days. He'd spent two of them telling himself he would do it.

It wasn't even a hard calculation. That was the ugly part. Ulyana Nystrom had spent eleven years learning that sentiment was a luxury item, priced beyond his station. He owed the Salt Cathedral four hundred and sixty-two marks, nine coppers, and change—change being the years of his life the arithmetic didn't bother counting. A name traded for freedom wasn't betrayal; it was bookkeeping. The cathedral itself had taught him that. Every tithe he copied, every widow's mite entered into the great ledgers, came down to the same lesson: everything is owed, and everything can be collected.

So he told himself he'd do it, and then he went down into lowtown to do it, and what he found there was a girl doing laundry.

* * *

The resonant—if that was the right word for her—worked the washing stones behind the tannery row, where the water ran gray and the air tasted of lye and rot. Vey's people had given him more than the pilgrim's dying whisper. They'd given him a list: six names pulled from parish records of inexplicable recoveries. Fevers broken overnight. Wounds closed without scar. The kind of miracles the cathedral officially did not have opinions about, because having opinions would mean admitting the old gods were answering prayers again, and the cathedral had spent three centuries explaining that the old gods were dead.

Ulyana had worked the list like a debt collection route, which was the only kind of work he knew. Five names, five dead ends—or rather, five frightened people who looked at him with the particular terror of the guilty and gave him nothing, because they had nothing to give. The sixth name was Sefa Quill, laundress, nineteen, orphaned twice over by the fever years.

He found her at the stones on the second morning, sleeves rolled past her elbows, hands in water cold enough to ache. And he watched her do the thing.

A boy came down the bank—a tanner's apprentice, maybe eight, hand wrapped in filthy rags, weeping the way children weep when they've been told not to. A hook, Ulyana guessed, or a blade. The kind of wound that rotted. The kind that took fingers, then hands, then arms, in the tannery rows.

Sefa waded out of her tubs, dried her hands on her apron, and knelt. Ulyana saw her lips move—not the formal cadences of cathedral prayer, nothing with the right words in the right order, just something murmured, half-swallowed, the way you'd talk to a scared animal. She took the boy's ruined hand between both of hers.

And the water on her skin began to shine.

Not glow—that was too clean a word for it. It was more like the light you catch in brine at dusk, the faint pearl of salt drying on stone. It moved up her wrists, pooled in the creases of her palms, and where it touched the boy's rags, the rags darkened with fresh blood and then fell away, and underneath—

Underneath there was new skin. Pink, unscarred, obscene in its wholeness. The boy stopped crying. He stared at his hand the way you stare at a door you're sure was locked standing open.

Sefa said something quiet to him, pressed a copper into his good palm—*for the silence*, Ulyana translated from long experience—and sent him off. Then she stood there a moment, alone at the gray water, looking at her own hands as if they belonged to someone she owed money to.

Ulyana knew the feeling.

* * *

He should have gone straight back to Vey. He had a name, a face, an address—the whole inventory, gift-wrapped. Instead he followed the girl home through lanes so narrow the eaves touched overhead, and told himself this was diligence, thoroughness, making sure the report was complete. He was a scribe. Completeness was his trade.

Her lodging was a rented cot in the back of a dyer's shed, behind bolts of cloth that reeked of mordant. She noticed him within a minute of his approach—he wasn't built for stealth, just for endurance—but she didn't run. She picked up a wash paddle instead, and stood in the doorway with it raised, and said, "You're the one who's been asking."

"I'm a debt collector," Ulyana said. It was true enough to pass.

"Then collect." Her voice didn't shake. "I don't have anything."

"You healed a boy yesterday. Tannery apprentice. Hook through the hand."

The paddle lowered an inch. "That was prayer," she said. "Prayer isn't illegal."

"It is if it works."

Something went out of her posture then—not fear exactly, but the tired recognition of someone who has been waiting for this knock a long time. "Who sent you?"

"The Salt Cathedral."

"And what do they pay their collectors?"

"Freedom," Ulyana said. "Mine."

She looked at him properly then, taking in the scriptorium pallor, the ink stains ground so deep into his knuckles they read as dirt, the threadbare coat cut for a man two meals heavier. Whatever she saw made her step aside from the doorway.

"You look like a man collecting a debt he doesn't believe in," she said. "Come in before someone sees you."

* * *

Inside, among the dye racks, she fed him barley tea he didn't want and asked questions he answered honestly, because lying to her felt like falsifying a ledger and he'd been beaten for that once already in his life.

"The pilgrim," she said, when he told her about the man dying against the cathedral wall, pressing the token into Ulyana's hand. "Old man, salt-crusted, mountain accent. What did he say? Exactly."

"He said a name. *Zephyrine.*" Saying it aloud in a room with a window felt transgressive, though the word meant nothing anymore—hadn't meant anything since before his grandfather's grandfather. "Then he said, *it's opening again, tell them it's opening.*"

Sefa sat very still. "Show me the token."

He almost didn't. Eleven years of indenture teaches you that everything you carry can be taken, and whatever you refuse to show becomes the thing they hurt you for. But she had healed a child for free in front of witnesses, and she'd let a stranger into her room knowing what he was, and there was a arithmetic of trust that worked differently from the cathedral's, and he found himself digging the token out of the lining of his coat where he'd sewn it against discovery.

It hadn't changed since the pilgrim died. A disc of gray-white stone, crusted at the edges with something that might have been salt or might have been scale, worn smooth in the center where a thumb had worried it for years. No sigil. No inscription. Worthless-looking, which was why the inquisitors' searchers had passed over it twice.

Sefa held out her hand, and he put it in her palm, and immediately regretted it, because her whole face changed.

"Where did you get this?" she whispered.

"A dying man. Why?"

"Because I've seen its twin." She turned it over, tilted it toward the lamp. "My mother wore one. She called it a *finding-stone*. She said her grandmother brought it out of the mountains when—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "When the country still had a name."

"There's no writing on it."

"There doesn't need to be." She stood, crossed to the window, and held the token up against the last gray light. And Ulyana saw it—saw it and felt stupid for not seeing it before, because he was a cartographer's son by accident of blood he'd never examined, and maps were the first language he'd ever learned to read.

The token was heavy on one side. Not visibly—it looked perfectly balanced—but hold it up and it hung wrong, its weight leaning south and east, toward the Thornfell interior, toward the high white massif the smugglers called the Spine. Toward, Ulyana realized with a coldness spreading through his chest, the exact stretch of mountains where the shrine stone had wept brine and answered a child with rain.

"It points," Sefa said. "It's been pointing my whole life. My mother said all the finding-stones point the same way, and no one remembers why, and the cathedral says they're just rocks." She set it in his hand and closed his fingers over it. "They're not rocks. Something under the Spine is calling them. Calling *us*. And it's getting louder—I can feel it in my hands now, even when I'm not praying. Like a bell rung so far away you feel it in your teeth."

"That's not a reason to go looking," Ulyana said. "That's a reason to run the other direction. The cathedral knows about the resonants. There's an inquisitor—Vey—who's been collecting names for months. Mine was almost on her list. Yours is on it now, because I put it there by asking questions." He heard himself and hated how much he sounded like a ledger entry. "I came here to sell you. I want you to understand that. I'm not warning you out of decency. I'm warning you because—"

"Because you pocketed the token instead of reporting it," Sefa said, "and you've been carrying a piece of Zephyrine against your skin for weeks, and you haven't turned it in, and a man who'd turn me in wouldn't have kept *that*." She nodded at his closed fist. "Whatever you think you are, the evidence says otherwise."

* * *

He stayed until dark, which was the second mistake, or the third—he'd lost count of the accounting. They talked the way conspirators talk, badly and fast, building a structure neither would admit to building. She told him about the others: a ferryman whose drowned passengers woke coughing on his deck, a midwife whose blessings held, a dozen scattered souls across the lowtown who prayed and were answered, all of them hiding it, all of them waiting for the knock Ulyana had come to deliver.

"They're calling us resonants," he said. "That's the word Vey used."

"It's a good word," Sefa said. "It means we answer back."

When he finally rose to leave, she caught his sleeve. "Three days, you said. When does the inquisitor expect her name?"

"Tomorrow. Sundown."

"Then you have tonight to decide what you'll tell her." She said it without accusation, which was worse than accusation. "I won't ask you to lie for me. Lying for strangers gets people like us buried. But I'll ask you to remember what you felt when the token pointed. You're a map-man's son—you know how to read a direction. Ask yourself who drew it, and why it waited three hundred years to start pulling."

Outside, the lanes of lowtown stank of tallow and rain coming. Ulyana walked with the token sewn against his ribs and felt its small wrong weight leaning through the cloth, patient as interest accruing, pointing him south and east toward the Spine, toward whatever slept or starved or sang beneath the salt.

At the mouth of Tanner's Row he stopped, took out his knife, and cut the token free of its hiding place—not to surrender it, but to hold it openly in his fist, the way the pilgrim had.

Behind him, at the top of the lane, a lantern bobbed. Cathedral gray. Someone had followed him down.

Vey's voice carried through the wet air, pleasant as a clerk confirming a figure. "Well, scribe? Sundown's tomorrow. Tell me you've been diligent."

Ulyana closed his hand around the finding-stone and turned to face her, and heard himself say the first sentence of a life that belonged to no ledger yet:

"Inquisitor. We need to talk about what your founders buried under the Spine."
