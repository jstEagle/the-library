## 5. Salt on the Tongue

The sentence hung between them in the fog of Tanner's Row, and Ulyana watched it land the way a coin lands on wet stone—flat, graceless, too loud.

Vey did not move. The lantern at her shoulder threw her shadow long across the puddles, so that she seemed to stand twice, once in gray wool and once in darkness. "That," she said, "is not what diligent sounds like."

"I've been diligent." His fist was still closed around the finding-stone. He could feel its edges through skin gone numb with cold—or he imagined he could, which amounted to the same thing when you were afraid. "Diligent enough to know you're asking me to inform on people for something your own church caused."

"Caused." She tasted the word. "Scribes love causes. Everything has one, in a ledger. Debts have causes. Fires have causes." She stepped closer, boots ringing on cobbles slicked with tannin-water. "Tell me about the pilgrim, Nystrom. The one who died in your scriptorium alcove with his hands full of salt. Tell me what he said, and we can discuss causes like colleagues."

*He said a name.* Ulyana kept his face still, the way he'd learned to keep his face still over eleven years of tallying other men's debts without comment. If he gave her the name, she would have Zephyrine on her desk by morning, filed and cross-referenced and hunted. If he refused her outright, she would have *him*. So he offered her the third thing, the thing smugglers offer when they can't give up the cargo: a road.

"Your founders," he said, "drew a treaty. I found references in the restricted folios—marginalia, water-damaged pages nobody's catalogued since the second cathedral fire. The erasure of Zephyrine wasn't a miracle. It was an administrative act. Paperwork." He let that sit. "You want resonants? They're not appearing, Inquisitor. They're *returning*. Because whatever your founders buried under the Spine is coming loose, and no amount of informing will put it back."

For three heartbeats she said nothing, and the lane filled with the drip of the tanneries, the far complaint of a dog, the ordinary sounds of a city that did not yet know its history was leaking.

"You've been in the restricted folios," Vey said quietly.

"I'm a scribe. I've been in every folio this cathedral owns. That is literally my debt."

Something moved behind her eyes—not anger. Recalculation. He had seen moneylenders wear that expression, right before they decided whether a debtor was worth ruining. Then she smiled, thin as a knife laid flat. "Tomorrow at sundown, then. Bring me names, or bring me these damaged pages. Either will do. You're more useful than I credited, Nystrom, which is either very good for you or very bad." She turned, lantern swinging. "Go home. Sleep. Diligent men sleep well."

He watched her go until the lantern was a smear of gold in the fog, and only then did he open his hand.

The token lay in his palm, salt-crusted, inert, ordinary.

But warm. Still warm, like a stone held too long by someone else.

---

He did not sleep. He sat in his cell above the scriptorium with the shutter cracked and the token wrapped in his spare shirt at the bottom of his debt-chest, and he told himself a story in the careful voice he used for balancing columns: *I bargained. I bought time. I gave her nothing that matters.* The story almost held, the way a dam almost holds, right up until you look at the water line.

Because he had given her something. He had given her the shape of what he knew. Vey now knew that a scribe with smuggler's roads in his past had been reading treaty marginalia, and clever women like Vey did not need names handed to them—they needed only a direction and patience, and they walked the direction until the names fell into their laps like ripe fruit.

And there was exactly one name walking around lowtown wearing that direction on her hands.

Ulyana pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw salt-white blooms. *It isn't your fight.* That was the old arithmetic, the arithmetic that had kept him alive through eleven years of indenture: pay what is owed, touch nothing that isn't yours, walk away from fires on other people's roofs. He had warned Sefa once. Warned her thoroughly. Told her about Vey, about the sundown deadline, about the word *resonant* and what the cathedral did with the word afterward—he'd heard the stories from the older clerks, whispered like dirty jokes: the quiet rooms below the archive, the questions asked in a gentle voice, the way people came out of those rooms agreeable and diminished, agreeing to things in general and to nothing in particular.

She had looked at him with those steady washerwoman's eyes and said, *Then you should stop carrying it, scribe. Things you carry start carrying you.*

As if it were that simple. As if you could set down a compass that pointed at the center of everything.

*It isn't your fight.*

He said it aloud, to the dark, and the dark did not answer, and somewhere below him the cathedral bells counted four hours in their flat Thornfell voices, and he got dressed.

---

Lowtown at dawn was all steam and gray light, laundry lines strung across alleys like bunting for a poor man's festival, the tanners' stink giving way to the soap-fat smell of the washing yards. Ulyana came down the smuggler's way, through the coal arch and along the culvert, because if anyone followed him he wanted to see them at a distance where distance still meant safety.

Sefa's yard occupied the hollow behind the ropewalk, six great copper coppers steaming over wood fires, sheets and shifts hanging in pale rows. She was at the wringer, arms bare to the elbow, feeding white cloth through the rollers with the rhythm of someone whose body had memorized its work. Other women moved among the lines. A child chased gulls off the drying wall.

He stood at the yard gate like a man at a door he'd already knocked on once too often, and hated himself a little for being here, and could not leave.

She saw him on her next pass. Her hands stopped on the cloth. She didn't smile, didn't frown; she simply finished the sheet, set the roller brake, wiped her hands on her apron, and came to the gate.

"You look," she said, "like a man who slept in his boots."

"Sefa." His voice came out lower than he intended. "She knows about this yard. Maybe not your name yet, but the yard. You need to be gone by midmorning. Your aunt's in Millbeck, you said—the one with the goats. Go today. Don't pack, don't settle accounts, just go."

For a moment she studied him, and he felt the look like weather moving over him. Then, instead of answering, she took his wrist—matter-of-factly, the way she'd take a stained cloth—and turned his palm up.

The token was in his fist. He didn't remember taking it out.

In the dawn light it was not pretending anymore. The salt crust had gone translucent, and beneath it something shone—a slow pulse, amber-white, brightening and dimming like breath. Like a lamp seen through a curtain. Like a heartbeat kept in a box.

The yard noise receded. He heard his own blood in his ears.

"That's new," Sefa said.

"It's—" His mouth was dry. "It started warming two nights ago. After the shrine. I thought—friction, pocket heat, some—"

"Scribe." She said it kindly. "Look at it."

He looked. And understood, in the way you understand a debt called in, that the light answered him. When his pulse rose, it rose. When he made himself calm, it dimmed—almost obedient, almost eager. It wasn't glowing at the mountain under the Spine. Not only. It was glowing at *him*, reporting him, singing him home like a struck tuning fork finds its twin.

"The pilgrim didn't choose you at random," Sefa said softly. "Or maybe he did, and it didn't matter. These things find their own. You're touched, Ulyana Nystrom. You've been touched since before you carried it. Probably since before you were born." Her thumb brushed the edge of his palm, an ordinary human gesture that nearly undid him. "So it isn't my fight either, is what you're saying. It's ours."

"I didn't say—"

"You brought it here." No accusation in it. Just fact, laid out like laundry. "You walked it through half of lowtown in your fist at dawn because you couldn't stand not knowing if I'd run, and now we both know."

He closed his fingers over the light. It shone through the gaps of his knuckles anyway, patient, incandescent. "Sundown," he said. "I promised her pages or names. If I don't show, she comes looking, and she'll look here first, because I led her here by being stupid enough to care."

"Then don't be at the yard at sundown." Sefa was already unpinning sheets with quick, practiced hands, dousing the nearest fire's edge with wash-water, and he realized she had decided before he'd finished speaking—that she had perhaps decided years ago, alone in this yard, healing strangers' cracked hands and waiting for the cathedral to notice. "There's a culvert under the ropewalk. Where does it come out?"

"The salt-flats road. Why—"

"Because if I'm running," she said, "I'd rather run toward something than away from it. Your compass. Which way does it pull?"

He hadn't let himself ask. He'd kept it wrapped, boxed, denied. Now, because she asked, because refusing her felt like one more small theft from a life already mortgaged, he opened his fist and let the token swing loose on its cord.

It swung. Stopped. Settled, insistent, pointing east-northeast—away from the cathedral, away from the lowlands, straight at the blue mass of the Spine where the mountains went white.

Toward the Door of Salt, though he had no name for it yet.

"There," he said. "Into the highlands."

"Good," said Sefa. "I hate boats."

And that was when the bell began.

---

Not the hour bell. The *alarm*—the deep cracked note from the cathedral's south tower that Ulyana had heard exactly twice in eleven years, both times for fires. Except this ringing had a different cadence, faster, ragged, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable across the morning air, came the sound of whistles. Cathedral whistles. Gray-coat whistles.

The women in the yard froze among their sheets. Somewhere beyond the ropewalk, doors slammed, a dog burst into furious barking, a cart horse screamed.

"No," Ulyana said. It came out absurdly, conversationally, as if the world might reconsider out of politeness. "No, she doesn't move until sundown, she *said* sundown, she gave me until—"

"She gave you until sundown," Sefa said, already moving, gathering the child by the collar with one hand and a dry cloak with the other, pressing the cloak into a fellow laundress's grip with instructions Ulyana barely heard. "She never gave *me* anything."

They went through the culvert at a run, bent double in the dripping dark, the alarm-bell chasing them even down there, arriving muffled and enormous through the earth itself. Ulyana's lungs burned. His mind worked the problem the way it worked columns, fast and cold, and every column summed the same: *they didn't wait for you, which means you were never the point, which means she had another thread to pull, which means someone talked, which means—*

Which meant it didn't matter how carefully he'd kept the name. Vey had months of reports, patrols, a network. He had been a convenience, not a linchpin. The hunt had never needed his permission.

They surfaced behind the salt-flats sheds into hard white daylight, and Ulyana turned back—one look, one look only—toward the sound.

Across the flats, at the mouth of Soapwife's Lane, he saw them. Four gray coats and a fifth figure walking between them, wrists bound in front with cord, head high, hair coming loose from its knot in the wind off the flats. Even at that distance he knew the set of her shoulders. She was not struggling. That was somehow worse. She walked the way people walk to an appointment they intend to keep on their own terms.

One of the gray coats carried a lantern, unlit, swinging—and hanging inside it, visible when the wind turned the frame, a finding-stone of his own kind, glowing faint amber.

Pointing. Reporting. Singing.

Beside the patrol, unhurried, her hands folded at her back, walked Inquisitor Vey. At the corner she paused. Turned. Looked across four hundred paces of empty salt-flat directly at Ulyana, as if the fog and distance were courtesy rather than obstacle, and raised one hand—not a wave. A summons. An accounting. *You owe me until sundown, scribe,* the gesture said. *And now you owe me interest.*

Then the lane swallowed them, and the bell went on tolling, and Ulyana stood in the reek of the salt-flats with a glowing stone burning its rhythm into his palm and a laundress waiting at his shoulder who had every reason to run and wasn't running.

"They'll take her to the quiet rooms," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Under the archive. There are rooms under the archive, I've seen the doors in the folio-stacks, they're not on any plan—"

"Then that's where she'll be." Sefa took his sleeve, firm as a wringer. "And you know those stacks better than any gray coat alive."

*It isn't your fight.* The old column, the safe sum. He ran it one last time and watched it fail, watched it fail the way the token glowed—because of him, because it had always been going to glow, because you cannot spend eleven years copying other people's debts without learning that sooner or later every account comes due in full.

The token pulsed against his palm: east, northeast, home, home.

But the cathedral lay west, and Sefa was being dragged west, and Ulyana Nystrom—who had spent his whole life walking away from fires on other people's roofs—turned west and began, for the first time, to run toward one.

Behind him, far up the slopes of the Spine, so faint it might have been wind, something vast and patient answered the alarm bell with a single clear tone, like a bell of salt struck once under the world.

Both of them heard it. Neither spoke. They ran.
