## 6. The God-King's Herald

The man who bought my debt arrived at the cathedral on a Tuesday, which I found insulting. Debts are not cleared on Tuesdays. Debts are cleared on feast days, on deaths, on the rare mercy of a bishop in a good mood—never on a gray Tuesday in the month of Hoarfrost, when the salt on the steps had gone dirty and no one bothered to sweep it.

I was in the scriptorium when the summons came, copying tithes with the pen I'd worn down to a nub. Brother Aldric appeared at my elbow, coughing the way he did when something had upset the ledger of the world.

"Nystrom. The Abbot's parlor. Now."

"Which debt?" I asked, because that was always the question. Everything at Thornfell was a debt.

Aldric's mouth did something strange. "Yours," he said. "All of it."

* * *

The Abbot's parlor smelled of beeswax and old money, and the man sitting by the fire did not belong in it the way a fish does not belong in a tree. Not because he was shabby—he was dressed better than the Abbot, in a coat of dark wool with silver thread at the cuffs—but because he sat like the room was his and everyone else was visiting. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with a face that might have been handsome before something had hollowed it from behind. His eyes were the color of candle flame seen through smoked glass.

"Ulyana Nystrom," he said, rising. He pronounced my name correctly, all four syllables, the way nobody outside the highlands ever managed. "Sit. Please."

The Abbot stood by the window wearing the expression of a man who has just watched a stranger hand him an obscene amount of money for a scribe. Which was, apparently, what had happened.

"There has been an error," the Abbot began.

"No error." The stranger produced a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it on the table between us. "Your own seal. Your own arithmetic. Fourteen years, seven months, eleven days remaining against indenture number nine-forty-one. Purchased in full, plus interest, plus—" he glanced at the paper again, amused, "—the fee for early redemption, which is frankly extortionate. You should be ashamed. Keep the money. Consider it reparations."

The Abbot's jaw worked. "The Cathedral does not sell its obligations to private parties."

"You didn't sell it to me." The stranger smiled, and the smile had too many teeth in it, like a dog's. "You sold it to a factor in Lowmarsh, who sold it to a merchant in Cael Row, who sold it to me. Salt moves downhill, Abbot. So does debt. Nobody checks whose hands it passes through until someone drinks it."

He turned to me. Up close there was something wrong with him that took me a moment to name: he did not blink enough. Not never—a normal man would seem dead—but less. As if blinking were a courtesy he'd learned late in life and practiced only out of habit.

"Ulyana Nystrom," he said again. "Smuggler. Debtor. Scribe. Finder of roads that do not appear on maps. You are exactly the sort of man my master collects."

"Who's your master?"

"My name is Marrec." He said it as if that answered everything, and for a moment, absurdly, I felt I ought to recognize it. "And you are free. Or you will be, once you sign this." Another paper, this one blank except for a line at the bottom. "A formality. Your debt transfers to me, then dies. No one will ever collect on it again. Walk out of this cathedral a free man, or stay and copy tithes for fourteen years. Your choice."

Here is what they don't tell you about freedom dangled in front of an indebted man: it stops your heart first and your thinking second. I looked at that blank line and saw every copper I'd tallied against myself since I was nineteen—the ledger where my life was written in somebody else's arithmetic, page after page, year after year, and at the bottom a number that never got smaller fast enough.

"What does it cost?" I asked, because smugglers learn early that free things cost the most.

Marrec's smile widened fractionally. "Conversation," he said. "That's all. Sign, walk, talk. If you dislike what I have to say, leave, and we part as strangers. But I think you won't dislike it."

The Abbot made one last sound about impropriety and protocol. Marrec slid a second purse onto the table—it clinked like a dropped chandelier—and the sound of the impropriety stopped mid-word.

I signed.

My hand shook doing it, which annoyed me. It wasn't fear. It was the strange grief of watching the last fourteen years of arithmetic become worthless in a single stroke—all those careful columns, all those nights counting down to nothing.

The ink dried. Marrec folded the paper away.

"There," he said softly. "Free." And he looked at me with such open, unguarded pleasure that I almost believed he'd done it out of kindness, right up until I remembered that men who buy things want things.

* * *

He took me to dinner at the Gilded Ewe, which is the most expensive inn in Thornfell and the only one I'd ever been thrown out of. He ordered for both of us without consulting the menu, ordered wine older than my grandmother, and tipped the serving girl enough to make her drop the tray.

"Eat," he said. "You've been eating cathedral food for fourteen years. This will taste like a memory of food."

It did. That irritated me too.

"So," I said, around the best bite of lamb of my life. "Conversation."

"Conversation." Marrec set down his fork. "Tell me what you know about Zephyrine."

I put my fork down too.

There is a rule in Thornfell, taught to children before they learn their letters: *don't say the name*. Three hundred years ago there was a country over the mountains called Zephyrine, and then there wasn't—not destroyed, not conquered, *erased*. The treaty-magic took its fields, its cities, its kings, and its gods, and folded them into silence, and in exchange the borders knew peace forever. Every map printed since shows only mountains where Zephyrine was. Every prayer offered to its old gods hits a wall of salt. Even the word itself survives mostly in legal documents and swear words.

"It doesn't exist," I said.

"Wrong," Marrec said, pleasantly. "Try again."

"It hasn't existed for three hundred years."

"Better. Still wrong." He leaned back. "It exists, Ulyana. It has always existed. It was not destroyed—it was *put away*, the way you put away a knife you intend to use later. And now the hand is reaching for the drawer."

I thought of the pilgrim in the scriptorium, the salt-crusted token pressed into my palm, the dying whisper of a name I'd been taught never to say. I thought of the shrine stone in the highlands weeping brine, granting a child's wish with rain out of a clear sky. I kept my face still. Smuggler's habit.

"I've heard stories," I said carefully. "Prayers being answered. Resonants."

"You've heard more than stories." Marrec studied me with those under-blinking eyes. "You held a prayer token last month. A dying woman gave it to you, and instead of turning it in to your inquisitors—you kept it. Don't bother denying it; I know the shape of your pockets better than you do."

Cold walked up my spine, one vertebra at a time. "Then you know Inquisitor Vey wants the same information."

"Vey wants to *catalogue* what's waking. My master wants to *lead* it." He said it simply, without apology, and somehow that was worse than menace would have been. "Ossian Vael. God-king of Zephyrine. Three centuries sleeping behind the treaty-salt, and three weeks risen. He is gathering the faithful, Ulyana, and he is gathering something else as well: sons."

"Sons."

"Zephyrine had bloodlines. Cartographer-priests, Door-wardens, the families who wrote the treaties and tended the thresholds. When the country was folded, the bloodlines were left scattered on the outside like seed corn. Yours is one of them." He said it conversationally, the way you'd tell someone their horse had won a race. "Nystrom. Do you know what that name meant, over the mountain? Before?"

"Highland farmers," I said. "Goats. Debt."

"Roads," Marrec said. "Ways. Doors. Your family drew the paths between what is and what isn't. They were the keepers of passage, the ones who could find a road where the world insisted there was none. Does that mean nothing to you? A debtor scribe who knows every smuggler's cut through the Thornfell interior, every goat track, every seam in the mountains that no map admits to? You thought that was just talent? Just luck?"

I hadn't, quite. That was the terrible thing—I hadn't. There had always been a small cold voice in me that noticed how paths opened for the way I moved, how I never got lost even where I should have, how the mountains seemed to rearrange themselves slightly in my favor. I'd spent fourteen years telling myself it was practice.

"The god-king is risen," Marrec went on, quieter now. "He is waking the echoes of the old gods, answering prayers, calling the faithful home. And he is looking for the Door of Salt—the way back into what remains of his kingdom—and he cannot pass through it alone. It was sealed by wardens. It opens for wardens. It opens," he said, and here his voice changed, warmed, became something almost tender, "for *you*."

"That's madness," I said. "I'm a scribe."

"You're a Nystrom." Marrec leaned forward. "Ulyana. Listen to me. I know what Thornfell gave you. Debt at nineteen. A cell with a window too high to see out of. Fourteen years of copying other men's wealth in somebody else's hand while they charged you for the candle wax. I know what it is to be owned by arithmetic." Something flickered across his face—grief, real grief, gone in a blink that came too late. "My master offers you the opposite. A name that isn't a number. A nation that isn't a ledger. A family you didn't know you had, waiting on the other side of the mountains with three hundred years of your inheritance stored up like winter grain. You could walk into Zephyrine and be greeted as a son returned. Honored. *Belonging*, Ulyana—that's what he's offering. Not wages. Not freedom. Belonging."

And here is the part I'm not proud of: for one full breath, I wanted it.

Not the god. Not the Door. Just the word. *Son.* I am an orphan raised by a ledger; I have no family name that means anything but what I owed on it; I have never in my life been anywhere that was glad to see me coming. Marrec saw that in me the way a locksmith sees the shape of a keyhole. He pressed exactly there, gently, and I felt the whole mechanism of me shift.

"Think," he said. "Don't answer tonight. Answer on the road."

"What road?"

"The eastern pass. Seven days from now. There will be others traveling the same way—some mine, some Vey's, some simply faithful. The mountains are waking up, Ulyana. Everyone is walking somewhere." He rose, dropped another absurd tip beside the plates, and pulled on his gloves. At the door he paused.

"One thing more," he said. "The token you carry. The compass, the little salt-crusted disc. Have you wondered why it warms near certain people? Why it pointed, the night you held it, not toward the mountains but toward yourself?"

I hadn't told anyone about the warmth. Not Sefa. Not anyone.

"How—" I started.

"Blood calls to blood," Marrec said. "Ask it the right question someday, and see which way it swings." He tipped his head, half a bow, courteous as a headsman. "Seven days, son of Zephyrine. My master is patient, but the mountains are not."

Then he was gone into the dark street, and I sat alone with the remains of the best meal of my life, tasting salt.

* * *

I didn't go back to the cathedral dormitory. I couldn't have slept there anyway; my body had already left, some animal part of me pacing the walls like a dog smelling smoke. Instead I walked lowtown with my collar up, past shuttered stalls and guttered lamps, and tried to make the night hold still long enough to think.

Free. I was free. I turned the fact over like a coin found on the ground, checking both faces. On one side: no more ledgers, no more bells, no more kneeling to arithmetic. Fourteen years of my life handed back to me in a single signature. On the other side: I belonged to something now that didn't write its debts in ink.

Marrec hadn't threatened me once. That was what kept circling back. Vey bought obedience with fear; the cathedral bought it with debt. This man had bought it with *hunger*—with the oldest hunger there is, the one for somewhere to stand. He hadn't lied to me either, I was nearly sure of that. Liars flatter; Marrec had been precise. Precise the way a surgeon is precise about where to cut.

And underneath his precision, that flicker of grief. When he'd spoken of being owned by arithmetic, something in his face had cracked for half a heartbeat—like a man describing his own prison from memory. Like someone who had signed a similar line once, a very long time ago, and never found the eraser.

In my pocket, through the wool, the prayer token was warm. Warmer than usual. I took it out and held it up to the lamplight: the little disc of salt-crusted stone, the sigil worn nearly smooth, the faint pull in the palm like a fish on a line.

Pointing east. Toward the mountains.

Toward the Door.

"Ask it the right question someday," Marrec had said, "and see which way it swings."

So I asked it. Quietly, standing in a dark street in a city that had owned me for fourteen years, I asked the only question that mattered:

"Which way is home?"

The token turned in my hand—not drifting, not wavering, but *turning*, deliberate as a compass needle finding north—and settled pointing east, steady and hot as a coal.

East. Over the mountains. Into whatever was waking up there, wearing my family's name.

Seven days, Marrec had said. But I had watched his face when he spoke of belonging, and I had heard the grief under the salesmanship, and I knew one thing he clearly did not want me to know: a herald is just the first kind of sacrifice a god demands.

I went east that same night. Alone, by smuggler's paths, ahead of everyone.

Because if the Door of Salt was going to open for a Nystrom, I intended to reach it before I found out what Marrec's master actually wanted with the key—or what the key was worth to a god-king who had waited three hundred years, and had just discovered he was missing one small, expendable piece of himself.

Behind me, somewhere in the dark of Thornfell, a bell began to ring.

It was not the cathedral bell.
