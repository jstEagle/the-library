## 8. Flight Through Thornfell

The back stairs of the Salt Cathedral were built for people who did not want to be remembered passing through them, and Ulyana had spent six years learning every one of them. He took them two at a time now, past the tallow-stained alcoves where junior clerks stole kisses and cigarettes, past the disused chapel where the salt had eaten through the altar stone so that it wept perpetually onto the floor. Nobody stopped him. That was the trouble with being a scribe: you became furniture. Furniture was invisible until someone needed to burn it.

He came out through the laundry yard, where the great copper kettles steamed into the gray morning air, and let himself be one more stooped figure in a borrowed apron until he reached the gate. The gate-warden, old Pellas, was asleep in his chair with a mug of chicory going cold beside him. Ulyana lifted the bar himself, easing it out of its brackets so it wouldn't scrape, and slipped through into Saltrow like a thread pulled from a hem.

Then he ran.

Not the panicked sprint of a man who has just been caught — he was too good at this for panic — but the flat, economical lope of a boy who had carried contraband over these mountains since he was eleven years old. Debt-running, they'd called it, back when his mother was alive: carrying a ledger's worth of other people's obligations up into the highlands and a ledger's worth of coin back down, trusting no road twice, trusting no face fully. He had hated every mile of it then. Now those miles rose up in his memory like an old friend showing him exactly which doors were unlocked.

*Left at the chandler's. Down through the tannery. Don't take the Pilgrim Road — the Pilgrim Road has eyes.*

Saltrow woke around him as he moved through it: fishwives arguing over brine-cured herring, a knife-grinder setting up his wheel, children chasing a dog with half a loaf. Ordinary life, ticking on, indifferent to the fact that somewhere beneath all of it a bell was ringing wrong and a god-king three centuries dead had decided to come home. Ulyana envied them. He bought a strip of dried mutton and a heel of black bread from a stall without breaking stride, and by the time the vendor turned around to make change, he was already gone.

* * *

By noon he was above the treeline.

Thornfell's mountains didn't climb so much as lean — vast gray shoulders of rock pressing down on the valleys, wearing their snow like a debt they couldn't pay. There were four ways across the range that appeared on cathedral maps, and Ulyana was taking none of them. Instead he followed a smuggler's cut called the Shepherd's Regret: a goat track barely wide enough for a man, threading between scree fields and frozen waterfalls, invisible unless you already knew it was there. His mother had taught it to him when he was small enough to ride her pack-frame, pointing out the cairns she'd built herself, each one a little promise: *this way lies not-death.*

The prayer token rode in his breast pocket against his heart, and he could feel it. Not glowing anymore — he'd checked a dozen times, wrapped it in wool, buried it in the bottom of his pack — but *warm*. Like a coal that refused to die. Like something patient.

"Whatever you are," he muttered to it, "you picked a terrible time."

The wind answered with nothing useful.

He made camp that first night in a hollow beneath an overhang, no fire, chewing the mutton slowly and watching the valley lights below. From this height, Saltrow was a smear of amber, and the cathedral stood over it like a held breath. Somewhere down there, Inquisitor Vey would have discovered his empty desk by now. She would not send soldiers. Soldiers were loud, and Vey despised loud things. She would send quiet men with long memories and longer knives, and she would send them with a message: *come home, scribe, and all is forgiven.* Forgiveness being the cheapest currency the church ever minted.

And Marrec — Marrec would come differently. Marrec would come smiling.

Ulyana slept in snatches, hand on the knife he'd stolen from the cathedral kitchens, and dreamed of a door made of white stone humming like a struck bell, and woke with frost in his beard and the token warm enough now that he could feel it through two layers of wool.

* * *

The second day, the weather turned.

It came down off the peaks like something with intent — not snow at first but a pressure, a silence, the particular hush the mountains wore before they shrugged. Ulyana had crossed in worse. He told himself that twice, and then the sky opened and told him he was lying.

Snow fell thick and sideways, erasing the cairns, erasing the track, erasing the difference between sky and slope until the world was one white roar. He roped himself along the cliff-face section from memory, fingers numb, counting handholds his mother had taught him to count twenty years ago: *one for the ledger, two for the debt, three for the man who pays too late.* Superstition. He clung to it anyway.

He was halfway across the shoulder of the mountain — the place the smugglers called the Widow's Shelf, though nobody remembered whose widow — when the mountain spoke.

It wasn't a sound so much as a decision. A deep, subterranean groan, felt in the teeth before the ears, and then the whole slope above him let go.

Ulyana had seen avalanches before, from a safe distance, from the comfortable side of a valley. This one he saw from underneath. A white ocean peeling off the mountainside, silent in the way enormous things are silent, beautiful the way enormous things are beautiful, and absolutely indifferent to whether he lived or died. He threw himself sideways toward a rock overhang he'd clocked an hour earlier and hadn't let himself hope about, and the world hit him.

Not the full weight — the overhang split the slide around him like a stone in a river — but enough. Snow hammered him, filled his hood, packed against his chest, dragged him. For one endless moment he was tumbling inside the mountain's fist, up and down meaningless, breath gone, and he thought with absurd clarity: *the ledger will show me delinquent.*

Then the slide passed over him and dumped him, bruised and half-buried, into the throat of a ravine below the shelf, and went on without him into the dark.

He lay there a long time, listening to his own heart bang against his ribs, tasting blood where he'd bitten his tongue. The token, he realized distantly, had gone cold for the first time in days. Even it, apparently, had been frightened.

He dug himself free. Every joint reported complaints. His left ankle had swollen inside its boot to the size of a fist, and when he put weight on it, the mountain offered him a bright white opinion on the matter.

He couldn't go up. He couldn't go back — the slide had erased the Widow's Shelf behind him, and even if it hadn't, Vey's quiet men or Marrec's smiling ones would be working the passes by tomorrow. He could only go down, into the ravine, limping, one-handed, following the smell of woodsmoke.

Because there was woodsmoke. Thin, bitter, wrong-smelling — burning something that wasn't quite pine — rising from a fold in the rock below where a cave mouth gaped like a missing tooth.

A man who trusted no face fully should have kept walking. But the mountain had voted, and Ulyana's ankle had seconded the motion, and night was coming down with the temperature still falling. He limped toward the smoke.

* * *

The cave was deeper than it looked, and warm, and lit by a fire of dried peat that burned slow and sour. Drying herbs hung from the ceiling in bundles — yarrow, wormwood, and one dark-leaved thing Ulyana didn't recognize that made his teeth ache when he looked at it. Shelves had been cut into the living rock, holding clay pots, bundled papers, and a row of small stones each painted with a sigil.

Zephyrine sigils. The same ones he'd seen beneath the moss on the weeping shrine stone, three valleys east and a lifetime ago.

"You're bleeding on my floor," said a voice from the dark.

The man who emerged from the inner passage was old in the way of trees — bent but rooted, gray-bearded, with a heavy knitted coat and eyes the color of wet slate. He carried a walking staff shod in iron, and he looked at Ulyana the way a man looks at weather he has been expecting.

"Avalanche?" the old man said.

"Avalanche," Ulyana agreed.

"Hm. It's been restless lately." The old man crouched, took Ulyana's ankle without asking, probed it with thumbs hard as tent-pegs, and grunted. "Not broken. You'll limp for a week and thank me for it later. Name?"

"Ulyana."

"Just that? People who give one name are usually lying about the other one." But there was no malice in it; it sounded almost like ritual. "Brannoc. Sit. Drink." He pressed a clay cup into Ulyana's hands. It smelled of pine resin and something bitter underneath. "It's not poison. If I wanted you dead, the avalanche and I have an arrangement."

Ulyana drank. It tasted awful and spread heat through him like a rumor. "You knew the avalanche was coming?"

"I know this mountain better than it knows itself, which is a full-time occupation." Brannoc settled onto a stool across the fire, staff across his knees, and studied him. "You came down the Widow's Shelf. Alone. Off-season. Running, by the look of you — nobody walks that fast uphill unless something's walking faster behind." His slate eyes flicked to Ulyana's breast pocket. "And carrying something that hums."

Ulyana's hand closed over his coat before he could stop himself.

Brannoc smiled, thin and dry as old paper. "I spent forty years serving gods that most people think are dead, boy. Do you imagine I can't feel one sitting in your pocket, sulking because you won't talk to it?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere far above, the mountain settled with a sound like a sigh.

"They're calling them resonants now," Ulyana said carefully. "People touched by the old gods. The cathedral pays well for names."

"I know what they pay. I know what they pay very precisely." Something moved behind Brannoc's eyes — old anger banked down to coals. "I was a priest of the Zephyrine rite, before the last of my order was burned or scattered or quietly absorbed into the cathedral rolls. I sealed what needed sealing and buried what needed burying and I have watched this mountain for thirty years waiting for it to start answering prayers again." He leaned forward. "So. Which are you, scribe-with-one-name? Hunter? Hunted? Or messenger?"

Ulyana looked at the fire. At the sigil-painted stones on the shelves. At the bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth that sat, conspicuously, within reach of Brannoc's hand — maps, or scripture, or both.

"Hunted," he said finally. "By both sides."

"Both sides." Brannoc turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "Inquisitors on one side. And on the other?"

"A man named Marrec. He says he serves Ossian Vael."

The change in the old man was small and total. Nothing moved except everything: the set of his shoulders, the depth of his breathing, the way the firelight seemed suddenly afraid to settle on his face.

"Say that name again," Brannoc said quietly.

"Ossian Vael. The god-king of—"

"I know whose king he is." Brannoc rose, slow, joints cracking like green wood, and walked to the shelf of painted stones. He stood before them a long moment, then took one down and turned it in his fingers — a sigil Ulyana recognized now, having copied its cousins a thousand times in the scriptorium without knowing what they meant: the mark of the Door-wardens. "Three hundred years I assumed the treaty would hold because it was supposed to hold forever. We wrote forever into it. Forever is a long word for men to use." He looked at Ulyana, and there was something in his face that was not kindness and not pity but a grim, exhausted recognition. "And now it's failing, and the first thing to crawl out from under it sends its herald hunting a boy with a warden's token in his pocket."

"It's not—" Ulyana started. "I don't know what it is. I'm a scribe. I copy tithes. I owe the cathedral four hundred and twelve marks and nine coppers, and until five days ago the largest thing in my life was a miscounted shipment of candle wax."

Brannoc laughed — one short bark, entirely without humor. "Yes. That's how it always starts. With the miscounted candle wax." He dropped the stone back on the shelf and pointed the iron-shod staff at Ulyana's chest, not quite touching. "Listen to me, boy, because the storm will keep you here three days at least, and we will spend them talking whether you like it or not. You are going to tell me everything. About the pilgrim. About the token. About Marrec and whatever map he showed you. And when you're finished, I am going to tell you something about your ancestors that the cathedral never wrote down and Marrec certainly hasn't."

"And why would I trust you?"

"You shouldn't," Brannoc said. "Trusting people is how priests end up exiled in holes in mountains. Don't trust me. Listen to me, verify everything, watch my hands when I speak. But know this—" he nodded at Ulyana's pocket, at the warmth neither of them pretended wasn't there — "that thing chose you before either of us got a vote. And the avalanche that put you on my floor didn't fall by accident. This mountain has been keeping my door for thirty years. Tonight it delivered."

Outside, the wind screamed down the ravine, and the snow came down like the sky was trying to bury the evidence.

Ulyana looked at the fire, at the old priest, at the sigil stones lined up on their shelf like a congregation that had waited three hundred years for a sermon.

"All right," he said. "But I start with the candle wax."

Brannoc grinned, and for a moment looked thirty years younger and considerably more dangerous. "Boy," he said, "everything worth telling starts with a miscount."
