## 9. Brannoc's Confession

The storm spent itself against the mountain all night, and I lay awake listening to it the way you listen to an old debt collector at the door—knowing he'll come in eventually.

I had not spoken to another living soul in eleven years. Not properly. I spoke to goats, to the fire, to a stone I kept on my shelf that may or may not have been listening. Eleven years is long enough to forget the weight of being seen. Then the avalanche brought me a boy with salt-crusted hands and a face like every map I'd ever burned, and by morning there were three of them in my hut: the boy Ulyana, shivering and too proud to admit it; a laundress girl named Sefa whose fingers left faint light on everything she touched; and a cathedral inquisitor who had arrived with her sword drawn and her certainty already cracking.

Vey sat nearest the door. Of course she did. Inquisitors sit near doors even when they're allies, especially then.

"You knew," Ulyana said. It wasn't a question. He'd been building toward it since dawn, turning his cup of goat's milk without drinking, and now he set it down with a click that sounded louder than it was. "When you pulled me out of the snow. You looked at my hands and you *knew* what I was."

"I looked at your hands," I said, "and I saw a Warden's blood. Yes."

"Then say the rest." His voice had the flatness of someone who has stopped hoping for good news and only wants the shape of the bad. "You said you helped seal the Door. Three hundred years ago. Men don't live three hundred years, so either you're lying, or—"

"Or I'm something worse than a liar." I fed a split log to the stove because it was easier than meeting his eyes. The fire took it greedily. Fire always does. "Sit down, boy. All of you. What I have to tell will take until the storm breaks, and none of you will like any part of it."

Sefa sat cross-legged on the floor without hesitation, the way the young do, as if the story were a gift already. Vey remained standing for a full minute—a deliberate minute, I could see her counting it—before she lowered herself onto the bench and laid her sword across her knees. Not sheathed. I respected that. Trust should be rationed like grain in a bad winter.

* * *

My name was not always Brannoc. It was Brannoc Halev, and I was third acolyte of the Door-wardens, which meant that when the great work came, I carried things. Rope. Salt. Buckets of my own blood, drawn morning and evening into copper basins, because the sealing of a country cannot be done with anything less than what you are.

Three hundred years. I have counted them the way a prisoner counts bars. I did not choose this life; it was given to me the way a sentence is given, and I have made my peace with it the way prisoners do—badly, in pieces, over decades.

"You're saying you're immortal," Vey said. Her voice was careful, the voice of a woman cataloguing evidence against herself.

"I'm saying I haven't died yet. There's a difference, and the difference has teeth." I touched the scar at my throat, the one shaped like a closed eye. "The Door takes its toll from those who work it. Mine was time. Every year I served, a year was taken back out of me—not returned, *taken out*, the way you'd draw poison from a wound. I aged backward through my service and then simply... stopped. A man suspended. Do you know what it is to watch everyone you buried be forgotten by everyone else? To outlive even your own griefs until they're just words?"

No one answered. Sefa's hand had drifted to the prayer token at Ulyana's belt, and I noticed it glowed faintly in the dim hut, like a coal remembering fire.

"The treaty," Ulyana said. "Tell me about the treaty."

So I told him. And here is where I must be honest, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I intend to spend all of it before they leave my mountain.

* * *

We did not seal Zephyrine to destroy it. Remember that, whatever else you take from this room. We sealed it to cage a king.

Ossian Vael was not always a god. He was a man first—I knew him, I poured his wine at the solstice feasts when I was young and stupid and honored to do it—and he was a good king for perhaps twenty years. That's the part the histories get wrong, if histories survive at all. He was beloved. He ended a famine. He forgave the border lords their rebellions. When the first prayers rose to him, he answered them, and it seemed like grace.

It was appetite.

"A god who answers prayers must eat," I said. "Do you understand? Prayer is not free. Nothing is free. Every miracle Ossian worked drew its cost from somewhere, and at first the somewhere was himself—he thinned, he dimmed, we watched our radiant king grow translucent as sea glass. So he learned to spend others. A prayer answered here, a life shortened there. Never obviously. A farmer blessed with rain would lose his mother two winters early. A soldier healed of a spear wound would find his children born sickly. The ledger balanced, always, but the entries were written in other people's blood."

"And people prayed anyway," Sefa said quietly. "Because it worked."

"People prayed *harder*," I said. "That's the horror of it. The more it cost, the more miraculous it seemed, and the more miraculous it seemed, the more they gave. Whole villages competing in devotion. Mothers offering years of their own lives for their children's fevers to break. Ossian didn't invent cruelty. He invented a system where cruelty felt like love."

Ulyana had gone very still. I recognized the stillness—it was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. "The valley," he said. "South of here. The hollowed village."

"You've seen it."

"We walked through it yesterday. Before the avalanche." His jaw worked. "Everyone old. Or gone. Children with old men's hands. I thought it was sickness."

"It's feeding," I said. "He's awake again, and he's hungry, and the treaty's decay has opened cracks wide enough for him to drink through. Those aren't miracles happening out there, boy. They're *meals*. The gods answering prayers across Thornfell—most of them are echoes, fragments, the shed shadows of Zephyrine's pantheon, starving and half-mad and granting small wishes for small prices because that's all they can afford. But Ossian is fully awake. Fully himself. And he remembers exactly how to build an altar out of gratitude."

Vey leaned forward. "The cathedral teaches that Zephyrine was erased for its sins. That the old gods were demons wearing kindness."

"The cathedral teaches what its founders needed it to teach." I let that sit. She was quick—I could see her arriving at the implication before I said it, and flinching from it. "Your church did not destroy Zephyrine, Inquisitor. Your church *helped build the cage*. The founders of the Salt Cathedral were signatories to the treaty. Half the salt in your walls was quarried from the Door itself. You serve, whether you know it or not, the descendants of jailers."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "The erasure. An entire country. Millions of people folded away like a letter. If the goal was only to imprison one man—"

"What would you have done?" I asked, and I asked it gently, which somehow made it crueler. "He could not be killed. We tried. Nine times, with nine different weapons, including ones I will not describe in mixed company. A god who is fed by belief cannot die while anyone believes in him, and Ossian had made certain that believing in him was indistinguishable from loving your family. Kill the faith, kill the god—but the faith was a nation. So we folded the nation. All of it. The guilty and the innocent, the priests and the children, the whole bright country, tucked behind a seam of salt where no prayer could reach out and no hunger could reach in."

Silence. The storm pressed against the shutters like something eavesdropping.

"We told ourselves it was mercy," I went on, because the worst of it deserved saying aloud. "The people inside wouldn't starve—the fold preserves. They'd sleep, in a manner of speaking, until wiser hands found a better answer. We wrote into the treaty a provision for renewal: every generation, a Warden of the Door would renew the terms with his own blood, adjusting them, easing them, working generation by generation toward a true unbinding that harmed no one. That was the design. That was the promise."

"But?" Ulyana said.

"But the Wardens died, boy. Or were killed. Your ancestors—the cartographer-priests, the Door-wardens—were hunted down within four generations. By whom, do you think? Who benefits from a treaty no one can renew and no one can read?" I spread my hands. "The cathedral wanted the past buried. Certain families in Thornfell had grown rich on Zephyrine's vanished trade routes. And something behind the salt was whispering through the seams, finding the frightened and the greedy, teaching them exactly which bloodlines to end. The renewal clause required a willing hand of Warden blood. No Wardens, no renewal. The treaty fossilized. The fold became a tomb. And I—" my voice caught, and I hated it, three hundred years and it still catches— "I was left outside, holding the rope, with no one left to pass it to."

* * *

Sefa spoke first. "The people inside. Are they asleep?"

"No." I had been dreading this question most of all, and it deserved a straight answer. "The fold preserves the body but not the waiting. They are awake, girl. Awake and praying. For three hundred years they have prayed to be delivered, and their prayers have nowhere to go—they pool against the inside of the Door like rain against glass. Some of them have turned prayer into an art. Some have turned it into a weapon against each other. And some—" I looked at the glowing token at Ulyana's belt— "some of their pooled faith is what's leaking through the cracks now. The echoes answering prayers across Thornfell? Most of that power is Zephyrine's trapped faithful, spending themselves blind, trying to buy a rescue with the only coin they have left."

"They're paying for our miracles," Sefa whispered. "The hollowed village—that wasn't Ossian eating. Or—it was, but the coin was theirs."

"Now you understand why I live alone on a mountain with goats." I managed something that was almost a smile. "Every answered prayer I hear about is a theft I helped make possible. I sealed the vault and lost the key, and the people inside have been paying interest ever since."

Ulyana stood. He crossed to the window, pushed the shutter open despite the cold, and looked out at the white waste of the high pass. When he turned around, his face had settled into something I recognized from mirrors three centuries dead: the look of a Warden receiving bad terms and reading them twice.

"My blood renews the treaty," he said. "Marrec told me that much, dressed up as an honor. You're telling me it's worse than that. My blood *rewrites* it. Opens it, closes it, changes the terms entirely."

"If you reach the Door willingly, yes. Which is why Ossian needs you alive and willing, and why the cathedral needs you dead or bound—because a rewritten treaty could go either way. Open the Door wide, and you loose a starving god and three hundred years of desperate faith onto Thornfell. Keep it shut forever, and you condemn a nation to pray itself to nothing behind the salt." I met his eyes. "There is a third term, boy. There was always a third term. My colleagues and I wrote it into the original text knowing no one might ever be brave enough to speak it. But I'm old, and tired, and I have hoarded this truth like a miser hoards bread in a famine, and I am done hoarding."

"Then tell me," Ulyana said.

Before I could answer, Sefa made a sound—small, sharp, involuntary. She was staring past us all, through the open shutter, down the white throat of the pass.

"Smoke," she said. "Down-valley. Someone's coming up the smuggler's road. A lot of someones."

Vey was on her feet with her sword drawn before the word finished, and I moved to the shelf where I kept the stone that might be listening, and put my hand on it, and felt, for the first time in eleven years, it listening back.

"They found the hut," I said. "Storm broke. Someone broke with it."
