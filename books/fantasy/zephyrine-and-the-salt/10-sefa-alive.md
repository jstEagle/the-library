## 10. Sefa, Alive

The cathedral does not have dungeons. That is the first thing they teach you, in the way they teach you everything—by never saying it. The Salt Cathedral of Thornfell has *retreat cells*, quiet rooms where the spiritually afflicted are given time to reflect, and warm meals, and no doors on the inside, and windows of salt glass thick enough to swallow a scream before it reaches the street. I spent four days in one after they took me from the laundry line, and I will tell you what I learned there: you can get used to anything except the sound of your own prayers coming back to you wrong.

They questioned me twice a day. Inquisitor Vey did not come herself; she sent men with soft voices and ledgers, and they asked me who had shown me the token's use, and I said nobody, it was my grandmother's, it was nothing, and they wrote *nobody* and *grandmother's* and *nothing* in their books as if words were cages and ink the bars. On the fourth day they stopped asking questions and started drawing blood instead, three vials each morning, and an old brother with milky eyes carried the vials away murmuring something that might have been a hymn.

That was when I understood they were not deciding whether I was resonant. They were measuring how much.

I had been a laundress long enough to know what happens to linen that comes back from the mending room changed. You do not ask where the stain went. You fold it and you sell it and you do not think about the girl in the bleach-yard whose hands have stopped healing, because thinking is a luxury for people whose hands still work.

So I prayed. Not the way they taught us in chapel—not the tidy kneeling prayer with its approved cadences. I prayed the old way, the way my grandmother showed me over a basin of wash water: palms open, salt on the tongue, and the words not asked but *offered*, like setting a table for someone you love who may or may not come. And on the fifth night, when the retreat cell went dark, the lock on my door wept brine. It ran down the iron in slow white lines and the mechanism inside turned with a groan like something waking, and the door stood open onto an empty corridor, and I understood that somewhere under Thornfell, in whatever thin place the old gods had been sleeping, something had heard me and decided I was worth a miracle.

I did not waste it. A miracle that costs nothing means nothing; this one had cost me four days and most of my certainty about the shape of the world, and I meant to spend every drop.

* * *

You have to understand the geometry of the Salt Cathedral to steal from it. Everyone thinks the treasure is up—in the reliquary spires, the gilded choir, the archive towers where the inquisitors keep their ledgers of debt. But linen gets everywhere, and linen sees everything. I had washed documents for six years. I knew which brothers burned their drafts and which kept them, which cells had braziers and which relied on body heat, which corridors the night shift walked and at what hour the walking thinned to nothing.

The original treaty was not in the archive towers. It was under them. There is a stair behind the reliquary of Saint Adelheid—you have to move the saint herself, and she is heavier than grief—that drops into rooms the cathedral pretends it does not own. The founders' rooms. The shame rooms. Everything Thornfell has ever done and then decided never happened lives down there, folded in oilcloth, waiting for an honesty that has been three hundred years coming.

I took the treaty pages because they were the oldest thing down there and because my grandmother's name for the old gods had been written on their margin in a hand I recognized—the same slanted letters she scratched into our doorframe every spring equinox. I did not know what they said yet. I knew only that they were mine the way a foundling's face is hers: proof of belonging to something that was taken before I could consent to it.

Getting out was harder than getting down. The storm had broken over Thornfell two days before, and the cathedral gates were sealed against drift, and the inquisitors were mustering in the lower yard with lanterns and rope and the particular grim efficiency of people who have been given permission to be afraid. I heard a name pass between two brothers in the cloister, low and urgent, and the name was Ulyana Nystrom, and the word attached to it was *flight*, and the other word was *mountains*.

He had run. Good. Better than good—I felt something in my chest turn over, some fierce unchristian gladness, because the scribe with the smuggler's maps and the guilty careful eyes had looked at me in the slums like a man deciding, and apparently he had decided.

I went out through the laundry flume, which runs beneath the east wall and empties into the millrace, and the water took my warmth and gave me nothing back, and by the time I dragged myself onto the shingle a mile downstream I understood that the storm was going to try to kill me before anyone else got the chance.

The mountains did not care that I had a message. The mountains are honest that way. It is the gods and the churches that dress their appetites in ceremony.

* * *

I followed the road until the road stopped being sensible, and then I stopped following roads. My grandmother came from the highlands, and her stories were full of the rules of snow: travel in the blue cold before dawn when the crust holds, sleep at noon in the white blindness, watch for the places where the wind combs the slope into ribs—those hold your weight—and avoid the smooth pillows, which are hollows filled with your own grave. I broke every rule at least once. I fell into a drift to my shoulders outside a ruined shrine and lay there for a long moment considering how easy it would be to stay, and it was the thought of those pages going unread that got me out. Paper rots. Truths rot fastest of all when only one person carries them.

On the third day I saw the hut—or rather I saw smoke rising from a fold of the mountain where no honest shepherd would build, and I thought: smuggler's roof, or priest's, or thief's, and all three might feed me and only one might mean me harm, and I was too frozen to be choosy about the odds.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and Brannoc—he is Brannoc, I know now, the exiled priest with the hands of a stonemason and the voice of a man doing penance aloud—Brannoc will tell you in his own chapter how the smoke deceived him too, how the trackers came up his valley with their dogs and their salt-blessed chains, and how he doused his fire and led Ulyana and Vey out the back and down into the white maze of the high fells while the hut burned behind them. He will tell it better than I could, because he was there, and because guilt makes wonderful prose.

I met them on the fourth morning, and here is what happened, as plainly as I can set it down, because everything after depends on it.

* * *

I smelled their fire before I saw it—a real fire, reckless with fuel, hidden in a hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite where the wind made a doorway of itself and nothing else could enter. I stood at the edge of the light with my hood down and my hands open, palms forward, the old way, and I called out the greeting my grandmother taught me, the one that means *I bring more than I take.*

The silence went on so long I began to laugh, a stupid cracked sound, and then Ulyana's voice came out of the dark, hoarse with disbelief: "Sefa?"

"Half of me," I said. "The rest froze off around midnight."

He came into the firelight looking worse than I felt—snow-crusted, shadowed, a knife of pale stone tucked in his belt like something he hadn't decided to trust yet—and behind him stood the big exiled priest and, of all the impossible things in a week of impossible things, Inquisitor Vey, sword sheathed, frost in her close-cropped hair, watching me with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hates.

"You're dead," Ulyana said. "Vey said—they took you down into the—"

"They took me," I agreed, and let him help me to the fire, because pride is a warm coat but frostbite doesn't care. "And then the old gods unlocked my door. Which tells you something about whose side the locks are on now." I pulled the oilcloth packet from inside my shirt, where it had ridden against my skin through three days of white hell, stiff and frozen and safe. "And then I robbed the founders' vault. Because if I was going to die in the snow, I wanted it to be for something better than a rumor."

Vey's hand went to her sword hilt—not drawing, just resting, the way you touch a wound. "Those pages are cathedral property. Sealed property."

"Then the cathedral can press charges," I said, "once we're somewhere warmer. Or you can read them. Your founders wrote them, Inquisitor. I'd have thought you'd want to know what your own house is built on."

She held my eyes for a long moment. Then, to my lasting surprise, she sat down on the stone beside the fire and said, "Read them."

Ulyana unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a man who has handled rare manuscripts all his life—which, I suppose, he has, though always other people's confessions, other people's debts. The pages were drawn on salt-cured hide, dense with Zephyrine script, and beneath the script ran diagrams: lines and seals and a shape I knew without knowing how, because I had dreamed it since childhood—a door, drawn in section, like a carpenter's plan, except that the door was drawn *folded*, the way you'd draw a map that had been creased into itself.

"Read it aloud," Brannoc said quietly. He had gone very still. "Some things need air."

Ulyana read. His voice was a scribe's voice, even and clear, and it was only watching his hands that I saw what the words were doing to him. The treaty was not a border document. That was the first blow. Every child in Thornfell learns that three hundred years ago the treaty drew the frontier and erased Zephyrine beyond it—dissolved a nation like sugar in water. The founders' own hand said otherwise. The treaty was a *containment*. The signatories had not erased Zephyrine from the world. They had folded it inward, sealed it behind the Door of Salt, whole and intact and starving—a country turned into a prison cell, and every soul in it locked inside with the thing the founders had truly come to bury.

"The Door doesn't lead out of the country," Ulyana said, and his voice lost its scribe's evenness on the last word. "It never did. The country leads into the Door. We've been standing outside a sealed room for three centuries, telling ourselves the room was never built."

"There's more," I said, because there was, and because hope should have teeth or it isn't hope. "Look at the provisioning clauses. Read them."

He read them. His lips moved through the archaic formulas twice before he trusted himself. "Annual passage of sustenance through the threshold. Grain, salt, medicine, seed-stock. To be renewed—" He stopped.

"To be renewed every generation," I finished for him, "by the Warden's hand. It's all there. The founders didn't just seal Zephyrine away and throw away the key. They promised to feed it. Promised the people inside that the seal was temporary, that the Door would be tended, that someone would come." I spread my blistered hands over the fire. "Three hundred years ago somebody stopped coming. And everyone inside has been praying into the salt ever since, waiting for a rescue that was cancelled without their knowledge."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere above us the wind worked at the mountain like a patient locksmith.

It was Brannoc who broke, because Brannoc had been alive in some sense of the word for all three hundred years and knew which promises he had kept and which he had buried. "We told ourselves it was mercy," he said, to the fire more than to us. "Seal the god-king in with his people rather than let him loose on ours. And then, when the sealing was done and the cost became visible—we told ourselves the fold was painless. A country asleep. Not a country starving." His big hands opened and closed. "I helped write the first clause. I never read the last one."

"The last one's the important one," I said, not gently. Some things deserve a hard edge. "Because it means they're alive. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I grew up on the residue of those prayers. My grandmother's gods, my healing hands—all of it leaking through the seam of the Door like light under a shut door. They're in there, right now, real people, farmers and children and probably a few very annoyed cartographers, praying themselves empty, and the answer to every single prayer has been silence." I looked around the fire—at the exile, at the inquisitor, at the last son of the Door-wardens with his ancestor's map and his ancestor's face. "Until now. Until the treaty started failing. Until the prayers started getting *out*."

"And Ossian Vael wakes with them," Vey said flatly. "The god-king, fed by the faith of his own imprisoned people. Every answered prayer in the highlands—your rain, your healed hands—is him reaching through the crack."

"Maybe," I said. "Maybe some of it is. But hear the other half, Inquisitor, because I've been on the receiving end of the church's habit of lumping people together, and I won't do it back. Those aren't his people in there. They're *hostages*. Three hundred years of his prisoners' prayers, and he's been drinking them at the keyhole. If we open the Door the way he wants it opened—wide, forever, all at once—then yes, we hand him a nation and a hunger and a grudge." I leaned forward and put my palm flat on the treaty pages, on the diagram of the folded door. "But if we don't open it at all—if we mend the seal, if we choose safety again the way the founders chose it—we murder them. All of them. Slowly, politely, with excellent documentation. Those are the choices. There is no clean one. There never was."

Vey stared at the fire. Ulyana stared at the pages. Brannoc stared at his hands, and I would have given a great deal to know the arithmetic running behind his eyes.

It was Ulyana who spoke at last, and when he did, he was not talking to any of us. He had turned the final page and gone white to the lips, and his finger rested on a signature block at the foot of the renewal clause—a place left blank for the next willing hand, with a space for blood and a space for a name, and above the space, in the founder's careful script, a single instruction:

*Let the Warden's heir renew, or release, but never let the Door decide.*

"My blood opens it," he said softly. "Their blood wrote the terms. And the terms expired three hundred years ago." He looked up, and there was something in his face I had seen only once before, in a slum alley, in the moment before he chose to warn me instead of sell me: a man arriving at a decision from the far side, already past arguing with. "Everyone in that country has been paying my family's debt."

* * *

That was the moment, if you want to know it—that exact moment, firelight and frostbite and an exiled priest's silence and an inquisitor's unsheathed doubt—when I stopped being afraid of what had been done to me in the cathedral and started being afraid of what we were about to do to the world. Hope is not a warm thing. Hope is a hook. Once it's in you, everything pulls.

We broke camp within the hour. Brannoc knew a route toward the Door of Salt that the storms hadn't erased; Ulyana knew the mountain ways that weren't on any map, including Brannoc's; and Vey walked with us, armed and unforgiven and necessary, because she had seen the founders' handwriting and could not unknow it.

We were halfway down the first slope when Brannoc stopped and raised a hand, and we all heard it at once, threading up the valley through the windless cold: bells. Many bells, high and thin and wrong, ringing not from any tower but from the pass below—the sound of salt-glass choir-chimes, the sound the cathedral's hunting parties carry to drown out old gods' whispers.

Except I had been inside the cathedral's depths, and I knew those chimes, and these were not theirs. These rang older. These rang hungrier.

"Sefa," Ulyana said quietly, "how many days ahead of us is the Door?"

"Two. Maybe less, if the weather breaks."

Behind us, down the black throat of the valley, the bells rang on, closer than they had any right to be, and under their ringing—so faint I might have dreamed it, so faint that only my hands, my grandmother's hands, my healing useless resonant hands, felt sure of it—came the sound of a great many voices praying in unison. And they were not praying to the god-king.

They were praying for us to hurry.
