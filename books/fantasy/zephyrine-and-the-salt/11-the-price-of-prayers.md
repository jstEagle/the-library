## 11. The Price of Prayers

They smelled the valley before they saw it.

It was not a bad smell, which was the first wrong thing. It was sweet—bread baking, woodsmoke, the green rot of a well-tended garden in high summer—but it came at them across two miles of frozen scree like a voice calling through a shut door, too loud, too warm, pitched to be heard by something specific.

"That's not right," Sefa said. She had stopped walking without appearing to decide to stop. Her breath hung around her face in little clouds. "Bread doesn't smell like that in winter. Nothing bakes in a village this small in winter."

"Everything about this road is wrong," Brannoc said. He had walked with a staff since the avalanche pass, favoring a knee that had never quite forgiven him for seventy years of kneeling, and he used it now to point down the slope ahead. "Look at the smoke. Count the chimneys."

Ulyana counted. Six chimneys, six threads of smoke rising straight up into a windless sky—and that was the second wrong thing, because there was no such thing as a windless sky at this altitude in late autumn, and every one of those smoke threads rose perfectly vertical, like painted lines on a stage flat.

"A village that size should have twelve or fifteen hearths going," he said. "I've run debts through valleys half this size. You can read a settlement by its smoke."

"You can read this one," Brannoc said grimly, "by what it isn't burning."

They went down anyway. That was the thing Ulyana would turn over later, in the dark, when sleep wouldn't come: they knew the valley was wrong and they went down into it anyway, because the token in his pocket had begun to pull—not toward the Door anymore, but toward the village itself, tugging at his hip like a dog on a lead, and none of them were willing to say out loud what that meant.

The road descended through pine shadow and then opened all at once onto the valley floor, and Ulyana stopped so abruptly that Sefa walked into his back.

The village of Hallowmere—it took him a moment to find the name; he'd collected a tithe here once, years ago, eleven copper owed by a widow named Grett who'd paid it in buttons—was beautiful. That was the only word for it. Roofs newly mended. Fences straight and freshly whitewashed. Gardens still green in November, beans climbing their poles, cabbages fat as fists in rows. A well with a carved stone arch over it, moss scrubbed away, and beneath the moss—

"There," Sefa breathed. "The sigils."

Zephyrine script, cut deep into the arch, older than the cathedral, older than the treaty. Ulyana knew the letters from his ancestor's map: *ask and be answered.*

And the people. The people were the third wrong thing, and the worst.

There were perhaps forty of them visible, working the gardens, carrying water, laughing. They looked *well*. Rosy-cheeked, clear-eyed, dressed in good wool with no patches. Children ran between the bean rows chasing a dog. An old man sat on a bench whittling, shavings curling at his feet, smiling at nothing.

None of them looked at the strangers on the road.

Not a glance. Not a pause. Forty people, and not one head turned.

"They're happy," Sefa whispered. "Why does happy look like *that*?"

"It doesn't," Brannoc said. "That's the point. Look closer, girl. Look at their hands."

Ulyana looked. The old man's hands, moving over the knife, were translucent at the fingertips. Not wounded—*thin*, like paper held up to light, the bones faintly visible beneath skin that had been worn down to almost nothing. The woman at the well had the same fading at her wrists. A boy chasing the dog stumbled, laughed, got up—and where his knee pressed against the ground, the grass showed *through* him, briefly, like a coin under cloth.

"Oh," Sefa said softly. "Oh, no."

"They've prayed themselves thin," Brannoc said. "Or someone has." His voice had gone very quiet, the voice he used for scripture. "Every prayer answered takes its price from somewhere. I told you the gods waking are hungry echoes. This is what hunger looks like when it learns manners."

***

They walked in slowly, and still nobody looked at them.

A woman passed within arm's reach of Ulyana carrying a basket of bread so fresh it steamed, and her eyes went through him the way light goes through water. He said, "Good day," and she smiled—a lovely, absent smile, aimed at the middle distance—and kept walking.

"Grett," he tried, at the house he half-remembered. "Grett? Widow Grett, you owed the cathedral eleven copper, you paid me in buttons, blue ones, off your dead husband's coat. Do you remember me?"

She was sweeping her step. She turned her head toward his voice with that same gentle smile, and for a moment—one terrible moment—her eyes focused, and Ulyana saw what was behind the smile, and he wished to every nameless god that he hadn't looked.

Grief. Vast, drowned, patient grief, looking out from behind the happiness like a prisoner looking through ice.

Her mouth moved. No sound came. Then the smile closed over her face again like water closing over a stone, and she went back to sweeping a step that was already spotless.

"Sefa," Ulyana said. His voice came out wrong. "Sefa, touch her. You heal things. See if—"

But Sefa had already reached out, and when her fingers brushed Grett's wrist the widow flinched—the first real movement any of them had made—and made a sound like a hinge in an empty house, and then the smile came back and she swept on, and Sefa stood there holding her own hand as if she'd touched a stove.

"She's not sick," Sefa said. Her voice shook. "There's nothing to heal. She's just... *gone*. Something's wearing her."

"Not something," Brannoc said. He had walked to the well. He stood beneath the carved arch with his head bowed, reading the old letters by touch, and when he spoke again it was barely above a whisper. "*Ask and be answered.* The valley gods of Zephyrine were small gods. Kind ones. A prayer for rain, a prayer for a safe birth, and the god gave it gladly, because giving was how they stayed alive. Faith was food, yes—but freely offered food. A meal shared. What's happened here—" he laid his palm flat on the arch, and the stone was warm, warm as a body, and he snatched his hand back—"what's happened here is a meal taken. Forced. These people didn't pray themselves empty. Something reached into them and started drinking."

"Ossian," Ulyana said.

"Ossian." Brannoc nodded once, heavily. "Three centuries sealed behind salt, and the first thing he learns to do is *farm*. Why hunt the faithful when you can pen them and let them pray?"

***

They found the ledger at dusk, and that was when Ulyana understood, fully, in the place where understanding becomes sickness.

It was in the meeting-house, on a lectern, written in a fine hand on good paper: a record of prayers asked and prayers granted, kept in Zephyrine script by some unseen clerk. Page after page. Ulyana read it by the light of a lamp that burned without oil—he noted that distantly, catalogued it, filed the horror of it away for later, because the ledger demanded all of him.

*Brannoc Hale asks for his wife's fever to break.* Granted. *Price borne by:* a name Ulyana didn't know, *one year.*

*Marta asks for a son.* Granted. *Price borne by:* another name, *three years.*

*Tomas asks for the blight to spare his fields.* Granted. *Price borne by:* four names, *two years each.*

The prices were always borne by someone else. Always. He flipped forward through pages and backward through pages, faster now, and the pattern held like a heartbeat: wish granted, stranger pays. Wish granted, neighbor pays. And the names of the payers grew familiar as the ledger went on—the same names recurring, year after year, decade after decade, each entry shaving them thinner, until the entries began to say *price borne by: remainder of account,* and after that simply *account closed,* and after that the names stopped appearing at all, and Ulyana realized he was reading a list of the translucent-handed dead-who-still-walked, and that the whole village was a larder, and that the ledger was a menu.

The last page was recent. The ink still gleamed.

*A stranger passes through asking nothing. Let him ask. Let him ask anything.*

Below it, in different ink, older and darker, as if the paper itself had sweated it:

*He carries the blood. Be generous. Be patient. What is given freely weighs more than what is taken.*

Ulyana set the book down very carefully, the way you set down a snake.

"Sefa," he said. "We need to leave tonight."

"He knows we're here." She had come up beside him and read it too; her face in the lamp-light was gray. "He wrote it knowing we'd read it."

"He wants me to pray." Ulyana heard his own voice as if from a long way off. "That's what this valley is. It's not a farm. It's bait and a lesson both. Look what faith buys, says the ledger. Look how happy they are. All you have to do is ask. Ask for your freedom, scribe. Ask for your debt to vanish. Ask for your mother's grave to give her back. He'll grant it. Oh, he'll grant it beautifully, and he'll charge it to these people, to everyone in this valley, maybe to everyone outside it, and I'll walk out of here owing him the way Marrec owes him. That's how he caught Marrec. That's how he catches everyone. He doesn't take. He *gives*, and lets the giving do the taking."

Brannoc appeared in the doorway, silhouetted against the last violet light. "Then we leave," he said, "and you don't open your mouth in this valley, not even to curse. Not even to say grace. Understand? Your voice is a key and this whole place is a lock."

But it was already too late for silence, and Ulyana knew it, because the moment they stepped out of the meeting-house the village had changed.

The forty were standing in the street. All of them. Facing the meeting-house. Facing him. In the blue dark their translucent hands glowed faintly at the edges, and their smiles were wide and identical and wrong, and somewhere behind the smiles, behind the ice, forty drowned faces were screaming.

The old man from the bench spoke first, in a voice like many voices speaking together through one dry throat:

"Won't you stay for supper, Warden's heir? We have everything. We asked for everything. Everything was given."

"We're leaving," Ulyana said, and hated how small it sounded.

"But you haven't *prayed*." The woman with the basket tilted her head, sweetly, terribly. "Everyone prays here. It's such a comfort. Ask us for something. Anything at all. Ask, and watch how kindly the world says *yes*."

Behind them, at the far end of the street, the well-arch began to glow, the old sigils lighting up letter by letter—*ask and be answered*—and the air pressure changed the way it changes before a storm, and Brannoc grabbed Ulyana's sleeve and Sefa seized his other arm and the three of them ran, down the street, past the smiling dead-happy villagers who did not follow, who only watched, who only called after him in that layered voice, gently, over and over, like a lullaby:

"Ask. Ask. Ask."

They ran past the last house, past the fences, up the trail into the pines, and the sweetness of the valley followed them for a mile before the cold finally ate it, and when Ulyana finally dared look back there was nothing below but six chimneys' worth of smoke rising perfectly straight into a sky that had begun, very quietly, to snow upward instead of down.

And on the ridge above them, black against the last light, someone was waiting. A tall figure in cathedral gray, hands folded, utterly still, watching them climb with the patience of someone who has been sent rather than arrived.

Inquisitor Vey.

"Brannoc," Ulyana managed. "Tell me that's not who I think it is."

Brannoc squinted, and swore in a language three hundred years dead.
