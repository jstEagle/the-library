## 12. Vey's Offer

Inquisitor Vey had followed liars for thirty years. She knew the geography of a lie the way other women knew the roads to market: where it forked, where it thinned, where it doubled back on itself out of panic. So when she stood at the mouth of the high valley with three dead horses behind her and a tracker who would not meet her eyes, she did not need divination to know that Ulyana Nystrom had come this way.

She had been given the hunt because no one else wanted it. That was the arithmetic of the cathedral now. Six months ago, reports of answered prayers had been heresy, and heresy was career-making work. Now they were catastrophe, and catastrophe was something you handed to the person whose judgment you trusted least, so that if it went wrong you could say her name instead of yours.

"The trail goes up," said Corvan, the tracker. He was young, pious, and frightened in the specific way of men who had prayed to Saint Aldric that morning and were not entirely sure the saint had heard. "But Inquisitor—the stories from this valley—"

"I've read the reports."

"They say wishes come true here."

"They say worse than that." She tightened the strap of her satchel and started walking.

The valley of Hollowmere opened below them like a cupped hand. From the ridgeline it should have looked like any other highland settlement: stone byres, turf roofs, a river stitching silver down the middle, smoke standing up straight in the cold air. It almost did look like that. It took Vey half a minute of looking before she understood what was wrong, and another full minute before she could name it.

Nothing moved.

No dogs. No children. No smoke rising, because no fires burned. The fields were full of wheat gone gold and unharvested, which in these mountains meant famine waiting for winter, unless—

"Saints," Corvan whispered. "The harvest's ripe."

"Wheat doesn't ripen itself," Vey said, and went down into the village.

* * *

The first house stood open. Not broken open—opened, the door folded back against the wall as if someone expected guests. Inside, a table set for six. Bread on the board, hard as slate but whole. Six chairs, five occupied.

Vey had seen bodies before. She had made bodies herself; she was not going to pretend otherwise, not even in the privacy of her own skull. But she had never seen anything like the people at the table. They had not died of violence or hunger. They sat upright, hands folded, faces calm, and they had died of *nothing*—as if life had simply been called in, the way a lender calls in a debt, quietly and without malice, and they had paid without protest.

On each chest, over the heart, the skin bore a pale mark like a watermark: a small circle with a seam through it.

Corvan crossed himself four times and then, absurdly, began to pray aloud, and Vey let him, because the alternative was to listen to the silence, and the silence had texture. It pressed. It felt attentive.

The second house held eleven people in a single room, kneeling. The third held one old woman in a bed, and beside her bed a ledger.

Vey should have left it. Everything in her training said leave it, catalogue it, burn what cannot be catalogued. Instead she picked it up, because the old woman had kept a ledger, and ledgers were Vey's mother tongue.

It was a debt book. Not of coin. Column after column in a shaking hand: *Wished my knees well again — paid.* *Wished rain for Marta's wedding — paid, heavy, sorry.* *Wished the fever out of the Hewett boy — paid, oh, paid.*

And in the final pages, the arithmetic that explained everything:

*Asked who pays. It is us. It has always been us. Every wish takes a year from someone, and lately it does not wait for us to wish. It has learned our wants. Last night I dreamed of my daughter's face and woke younger in the mirror and my neighbor's boy did not wake at all. God help us. God forgive us. We wished too much.*

The last entry was dated nine days ago. Below it, in a different ink, steadier, newer, someone had written a single line—not in Thornfell script at all but in angular sigils that Vey recognized from confiscated heresy fragments, from the sealed archives, from the oldest stones of the cathedral's foundation:

*He is awake. He thanks you for your faith.*

Vey closed the ledger and stood very still in the dead woman's room while Corvan prayed and the wheat rustled outside in a wind that touched nothing else.

She had known, in the way an official knows things—abstractly, in reports, in numbers filed under *anomalies*. Standing in Hollowmere, she knew it in her body. The prayers being answered across the mountains were not mercy. They were feeding.

* * *

She caught them two days later, and it went wrong immediately, which was to say: it went according to nothing she had planned.

They came down a smuggler's path at dusk—Nystrom in front, moving like a man who knew every stone personally; the resonant girl Sefa behind him; and supporting both of them, leaning heavily on a staff, an old man Vey did not know and recognized anyway from thirty years of files. Brannoc. Excommunicated before she was born. A name from the cathedral's most embarrassing drawer.

Her men were six hours behind her; she had gone ahead on foot, alone, because she had wanted to see the trail before she trusted anyone else with it. Alone, then. Alone against three, one of them a resonant whose hands healed wounds, one of them the last of the Door-wardens, one of them whatever Brannoc was.

"Inquisitor." Nystrom stopped ten paces off. His hand didn't go to a weapon. His face did something more dangerous—it went tired. "You're a long way from your choir."

"Sefa." Vey said the girl's name deliberately, watched her flinch. "Ulyana Nystrom. And the heretic Brannoc, whom the cathedral has wanted for three centuries, though I confess I thought he'd be taller." She let her hand rest on the seal at her belt. It was habit, theater, and threat all at once, and she needed all three, because her legs ached and her head was full of a table set for six. "By authority of the Salt Cathedral of Thornfell, you are—"

"Hollowmere," Sefa said.

Vey stopped.

"We came through it yesterday," the girl said. Her voice was flat the way voices go flat when they are carrying too much weight to bend. "We buried who we could. The ones at the tables we couldn't—we couldn't move them, there were so many. Did you see the ledger? The old woman kept a ledger."

"I have the ledger," Vey said.

"Then you know." Sefa stepped forward, and Brannoc's hand rose to stop her and she ignored him. "You know what's granting the prayers. You've known for months—that's why they sent you. And you're going to stand there and arrest us for trying to stop it?"

"I am going to do my office," Vey said, and heard how it sounded. She had rehearsed those words in forty interrogations. They had never sounded like a man reciting lines in a burning house before.

"You saw them," Nystrom said quietly. "At the table. Sitting up. Didn't you."

"Yes."

"And you still think the cathedral's answer is the right one. Erase it harder. Pray louder to saints who—" He stopped himself, but not fast enough.

"Who what?" Vey asked. "Finish it. You've been finishing it in your head since the pilgrim died in your scriptorium. Say it to my face."

"Who aren't answering," Nystrom said, "because there's no one on the line."

The wind came down the path and moved through the dry grass, and nobody spoke, and Vey found that her hand had fallen away from her seal.

Brannoc laughed. It was not a kind laugh, but it was not cruel either; it was the laugh of a man watching a long fall finally end. "Thirty years I've imagined meeting one of you," he said. "I always gave myself better arguments."

"You'll get no argument from me tonight," Vey said. "Sit down. All of you. Keep your hands where I can see them, and we'll talk instead of bleed, because I have two men a half day behind me who will kill first and report second, and I would rather settle this before they arrive."

They sat. Warily, in a loose arc around her, the way people sit around a fire they don't trust. Sefa never took her eyes off the seal at Vey's belt. Nystrom sat closest, arms around his knees, watching her with the terrible patience of a debtor—which, she remembered, he was. Indentured. Property of the cathedral until the coppers ran out. She had read his file. Everyone's file ended the same way: *obedient.*

"So talk," Nystrom said. "Why did they really send you?"

* * *

Here is what Vey had carried up the mountain besides her satchel: a folio stolen from her own archive, three nights of insomnia, and a confession that had been growing in her like a tumor for eleven months, ever since the first prayer was answered and she had gone looking, as a good inquisitor does, for precedent.

She laid it out the only way she knew how, which was as evidence.

"In the founding years of the cathedral, three hundred and twelve years ago, Thornfell was losing a war it had already lost. Not a war of armies—a war of gods. Zephyrine's gods were alive and ours were memory, and memory loses every time. The founders were not saints. They were scholars and survivors and, mostly, frightened men with one asset." She paused. "A treaty framework. Old border-magic, older than either nation, designed to settle territorial claims between divine courts. It had never been used at this scale. They used it anyway. They wrote a treaty that defined Zephyrine out of existence—folded the whole country, land and people and pantheon, into a sealed space behind what the documents call the Door of Salt—and they signed it themselves, in secret, and then they built a cathedral on top of the secret and spent three centuries teaching everyone that Zephyrine had never existed at all."

Sefa had gone very white. "The people inside—"

"Are still there. As far as I can determine, alive, diminished, praying." Vey heard her own voice go dry and clinical and hated it, and continued anyway, because clinical was the only voice that could carry this. "Every prayer a Zephyrine sent upward for three hundred years hit the inside of the treaty like a moth against glass. The founders' design required renewal—reaffirmation by a willing hand of Warden blood, once a generation. Somewhere along the way, probably within fifty years, the cathedral stopped performing the renewals. Either they forgot, or—" she thought of the archives, the missing folios, the burn marks in the margins of the founding charter, "—or someone decided a permanent prison served the church better than a temporary treaty. The magic has been failing for decades. It failed completely this spring. And the first thing to wake up behind the salt was not a kindly harvest god."

"Ossian Vael," Brannoc said.

"Ossian Vael," Vey agreed. "Whom your generation imprisoned with the rest, and who has had three hundred years to grow patient and one spring to grow hungry. He answers prayers because answering prayers is how he eats. Hollowmere is not an anomaly. I have four other valleys with similar reports. The cathedral's response plan is annexation of the affected regions, suspension of all unsanctioned worship, and accelerated construction of a new suppression engine beneath the cathedral—a bigger treaty, cruder, folding not just Zephyrine but every resonant in Thornfell behind the salt with it." She looked at Sefa. "That includes you. All of you. Anyone the old gods have touched. The drafters call the list the Roll of Resonants. There are eleven thousand names on it so far."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them on the ridge, a stone loosened and clicked down the slope, and everyone flinched except Brannoc, who was watching Vey with an expression she couldn't read and didn't enjoy.

"You stole these documents," he said. "From your own archive."

"I copied them and put the copies somewhere the cathedral will never find them, and I brought the originals, yes."

"Why?" Nystrom asked. It wasn't hostile. It was genuinely puzzled, and that puzzled tone did more damage than hostility would have. "You believe in the cathedral. Thirty years. You wouldn't be good at your job if you didn't."

And there it was: the question she had climbed a mountain to avoid, sitting in the firelight between them like a third party.

"When I was a novice," Vey said, "they taught us that the founders were martyrs. That erasure was mercy—that a country allowed to die naturally would have taken Thornfell down with it, and the founders chose damnation for their own souls to save millions. I built my life on top of that story the way the cathedral built its nave on top of the treaty. And then I read the founding charter in the original hand." She reached into her coat and drew out the folio, old leather gone soft as cloth, and did not open it yet. "There is a marginal note. Founders' hand, verified twice. It says: *Let none after us mistake necessity for innocence.* They knew. They knew it was a sin while they committed it, and they left a note saying so, and the church locked the note in a box for three hundred years and canonized them anyway."

She opened the folio and turned it around so the firelight fell across the pages: the treaty text, the renewal clause, the Warden lineage, the signatures.

"My institution's foundation is a crime its own architects confessed to," Vey said. "Everything I have done in thirty years was done in that building's name. I am not asking you to forgive that. I am telling you that the current plan makes Hollowmere permanent and multiplies it by eleven thousand, and that the man who wrote the suppression engine's specifications attended my novitiate classes and smiled at me in corridors. I have run out of ways to call that necessary." She looked at Nystrom. "You want to reach the Door of Salt before Ossian Vael does. So do I, though for different reasons, and I don't care anymore whose reasons are cleaner. My men are a half day behind me carrying orders to take you alive. Behind them is the cathedral. Ahead of you is Ossian. I am offering you the thing in between: me. My authority, my knowledge of the treaty, and my willingness to stand in a doorway and slow down everyone I have worked with for thirty years."

Nobody spoke. Sefa looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at the folio with the expression of a man reading his own death warrant and finding the handwriting familiar.

It was Nystrom who asked the question, and he asked it gently, which was somehow worse.

"What happens to you, if we say yes? When your men catch up and find you sitting at our fire?"

Vey considered lying. It was her profession, and she was very good at it, and she found she had no appetite left for it tonight.

"Excommunication at minimum. The suppression engine folds resonants behind the salt. There is a shorter list, drafted more recently, for collaborators." She held his eyes. "My name is on it. I checked before I left. I assume they added me when I stopped filing reports."

The wind moved through the grass again. Sefa was crying silently, angrily, wiping her face with the heel of her hand as if the tears were someone else's fault. Brannoc murmured something in Old Zephyrine that Vey didn't understand and didn't need to.

Nystrom looked into the fire for a long time. Then he said: "Show me the renewal clause. All of it. If I'm going to break my ancestors' seal, I want to know exactly what my blood is worth—and I want it from the document, not from you, no offense."

"None taken," Vey said. "It's the first sensible request anyone's made of me in months." She passed the folio across the fire, and watched the last Warden of Zephyrine begin to read, and did not tell him the other thing—the thing she had saved for last, the thing she had told no one, because saying it aloud would make it real.

Two days ago, in the archive, checking her copies one final time, she had found the renewal clause annotated in a modern hand. Recent ink. Someone in the cathedral already knew the Door's mechanism, already knew about Warden blood—and had written, beside the clause requiring a willing hand, four words of cold institutional shorthand:

*Subject acquired. Proceed.*

They weren't hunting Ulyana Nystrom to stop him.

They were hunting him to use him.
