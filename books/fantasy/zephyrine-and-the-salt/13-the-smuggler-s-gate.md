## 13. The Smuggler's Gate

They walked the last three days in silence, because the mountains had begun to listen.

Ulyana had learned this as a boy, running debts for the Salt Cathedral's collection office—learned that sound carried wrong above the treeline, that a cough could travel a valley and come back to you wearing someone else's cough, that the smart thing was to keep your mouth shut and your feet on the stone. He had been eleven the first time they'd sent him up alone, a child with a ledger and a satchel of collection notices, because no one suspected a child and no one missed one if the mountain took him. He had climbed these switchbacks with other people's debts in his hands and his own debt growing in the cathedral's books below, copper by copper, and he had learned every crack in the Thornfell interior the way other children learned prayers.

Now he was leading an inquisitor, a healer, and a three-hundred-year-old priest through the same cracks, and the mountain was listening to all of them.

"Stop humming," he said over his shoulder. It was the fourth time.

Brannoc, who had been humming something low and tuneless for the better part of an hour, stopped. "It's a road-chant. Old custom. Travelers sang it so the passes knew they came in peace."

"The passes don't want to know anything right now." Ulyana crouched at the edge of the scree and touched the stone. It was warm. Not sun-warm—the sun had been behind the ridge since noon—but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, a heat that seemed to come from somewhere underneath the world. He pulled his hand back and wiped it on his coat without quite deciding to.

Sefa noticed. Of course she noticed. She had spent six days watching him pretend not to notice things. "It's getting stronger," she said quietly.

"It's a warm rock."

"Ulyana."

He didn't answer. He stood and kept walking, and behind him he heard Vey murmur to Brannoc, "How long has he known where he's going?" and Brannoc's answer, soft and amused: "Longer than he's been willing to say."

That was true, and he hated it. He had known since the second night, when they'd camped in a hollow below a ridge he'd last seen at fourteen, carrying a collection notice to a widow who had nothing left to collect, and he had sat up most of the night doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. The token in his pocket—the dead pilgrim's gift, the salt-crusted compass Sefa had taught him to read—had been pointing not at the Door of Salt in any direction he could have guessed. It had been pointing at a route. A smuggler's route. A child's route, worn into his memory by years of other people's debts.

The Door had always been on his map. He just hadn't known what the map was for.

* * *

On the fourth day they came to the place the maps didn't go.

Every map Ulyana had ever drawn—and he had drawn hundreds, in the scriptorium, in taverns, in the margins of ledgers—had a blank space here, a white patch in the high interior of the Thornfell range where the surveyors' lines simply stopped. The cathedral's cartographers called it the Unclaimed. Smugglers called it the Quiet. Ulyana, at twelve, had called it nothing at all, because he had been told never to speak of it, and he had obeyed, because at twelve he still obeyed.

The entrance was a crease in the mountain's flank, barely wider than a man's shoulders, hidden behind a fall of grey stone that looked like every other fall of grey stone for a mile in either direction. You found it by counting. Eleven steps past the split birch, three hand-spans above the lichen line, push where the stone sounds hollow. His father's voice had taught him that—no. Not his father. The debt-collector who had raised him, a hard, silent man named Osk who had died owing the cathedral more than Ulyana's whole indenture, and who had taught him the route with the flat patience of a man passing on something he could not explain why he carried.

*Push where the stone sounds hollow.* Osk had never said why he knew. Osk had never said anything. And Ulyana, standing before the crease with his palm raised, understood for the first time in his life that Osk had been a doorkeeper's son, or a doorkeeper's grandson, passing the key down the way you pass down a debt you can't pay—silently, generation to generation, until someone finally came to collect.

He knocked his knuckles against the stone. Hollow. He pushed.

The rock swung inward without a sound, and cold air breathed out of the mountain, and it smelled of salt.

Vey's hand went to her knife. Sefa made a small sound, half prayer, half gasp. Brannoc, behind them, whispered in a language Ulyana didn't know and then, translating himself, in one he did: "Three hundred years, and it still remembers to breathe."

"Stay close," Ulyana said, and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The passage ran through the mountain's heart for most of a day.

It was a smuggler's road and then it wasn't. The first hours were what he remembered from the stories Osk had never quite told—narrow galleries, worn steps, the marks of old lantern-brackets cut into the stone. But as they went deeper the workmanship changed. The walls smoothed. The steps became a stair, and the stair became a ramp, and the ramp became a corridor wide enough for six abreast, and the corridor's walls began to bear marks that made Vey stop and raise her lamp and go very still.

"These are treaty-marks," she said. Her voice had the flat, careful tone she used when she was handling evidence. "I've seen fragments. In the cathedral vaults. We were told they were decorative."

"They're not decorative," Brannoc said. "They're the treaty. Or they were. Written into the stone of the Door itself, so that it couldn't be amended by any hand smaller than the mountain's." He ran his fingers along the carvings without touching them, an old man's tenderness. "I helped cut the first of these. I was younger than you, Ulyana. I thought we were saving the world."

"And were you?"

Brannoc didn't answer, and the corridor carried them onward, and the marks on the walls grew older and stranger, and somewhere below them—Ulyana felt it in his teeth before he heard it with his ears—something began to hum.

It was faint at first. A vibration in the soles of his boots, so slight he could have called it his own pulse. But Sefa heard it too; he saw her tilt her head, the way she did when a prayer was being answered somewhere near. And Vey heard it, because her lamp-hand had gone steady in the way of someone forcing steadiness. And Brannoc heard it, and wept, quietly, without changing his pace, tears running into his grey beard while he walked.

"It knows you're coming," the old priest said. "It has always known. It was only waiting for the blood to come home."

"Stop saying things like that."

"It's true, boy."

"Stop saying true things like that, then."

The hum deepened. It wasn't sound, exactly—it was more like the memory of sound, the way a struck bell keeps ringing in the body after the ear has let it go. Ulyana found himself walking faster without deciding to, drawn down the ramp by something in his own chest that answered the hum, note for note, the way a second bell answers the first. His hand, he realized with a distant, floating horror, was already in his pocket. Already holding the token. The salt crust on it had worn away days ago; what remained was a disc of something white and dense and warm, and it was vibrating against his palm like a living thing, like a heart, like a key turning in a lock from the inside.

"Ulyana." Sefa caught his sleeve. "Your hand. Let me see your hand."

He let her. There was nothing else to do. She turned his palm up to the lamplight, and they all looked at it, and no one said anything for a long moment, because there was nothing to say. The lines of his palm had darkened. Not with dirt—with salt. Fine white crystals, tracing the creases of his hand the way water traces a riverbed, mapping him from the inside. The same pattern, Brannoc said softly, that was carved on the walls. The same pattern that was in the blood-salt signature on the ancestral map. The same pattern his hand had been making, in ink, on every ledger page he had ever copied, in a script the cathedral had told him was just his own cramped handwriting and had never once, in all his years of service, corrected.

"They knew," Ulyana said. His voice came out very small in the great corridor. "The cathedral knew what I was. They indentured me anyway. They put me in the scriptorium on purpose. They—" He stopped. The arithmetic he'd been refusing all week finished itself, cold and complete. "I wasn't a scribe. I was a lock they were keeping oiled."

"Or a lamp they were keeping lit," Sefa said. "It depends what they meant to do with you. And we're going to decide that before they do."

* * *

The corridor ended at noon, though there was no noon down there—only a light that came from nowhere, a pale diffusion in the air, as if the stone itself remembered daylight and was reproducing it from memory.

They stepped out of the passage and into a cavern that was not a cavern.

It was a hall. Vaulted, vast, its ceiling lost in the pale glow above, its floor a single sheet of white stone so smooth it held their reflections like still water. And across the far side of it—seamless, floor to vanished ceiling, curving away into the dark on either side until the eye gave up—stood the Door.

It was not a door. It was a seam. A single line of darkness, hair-thin, running through the white stone like a crack in porcelain, and from that seam came the hum—no, the ringing, there was no other word for it, a struck-bell note that had been sounding for three hundred years without dying, and Ulyana understood suddenly that it had not been ringing in the mountain at all. It had been ringing in the blood of his family, passed down father to child to child, a note held in trust, and every Warden who had ever lived had carried it without knowing, and now it had come home to the last of them and it wanted to be released.

"Ulyana." Vey's voice, sharp, from a long way off. "Your hand is bleeding."

He looked down. His palm had opened along the salt-lines, a clean split, painless, beading red. The blood ran down his wrist and dripped onto the white floor, and where it fell the stone drank it, and the seam of darkness brightened.

"No," Ulyana said. "No, no—" He pressed his hand against his coat, against his thigh, trying to stop it, and the blood soaked through and kept coming, and with every drop the seam widened, a hair, a finger's width, and through the widening gap came air that smelled of rain on warm earth, of harvest, of woodsmoke and bread, of a country that was not dead, that had never been dead, that had been folded shut like a letter waiting three centuries for someone with the right blood to break the seal.

"Ulyana, you have to want it shut," Vey said, and she had her knife out now, and Ulyana saw with a strange clarity that she was pointing it at him, and that she was crying. "The treaty—whatever your ancestors wrote, whatever the terms were, you can't open it blind, you don't know what's on the other side, you don't know what comes through—"

"I'm not opening it." His voice cracked. He was backing away from the Door, from the seam, from the smell of that other air, and the seam widened anyway, following him, answering him, because it was not answering his choices. It was answering his blood. "I'm not doing anything. It's doing it. It's me, it's—Brannoc, how do I stop it, you sealed it, tell me how you sealed it—"

"We didn't seal it." Brannoc had not moved. He stood in the pale light with his face lifted toward the seam like a man looking at a sunrise he had been promised all his life, and his voice was wrecked and gentle. "We only shut it, boy. Shutting and sealing aren't the same thing. It was never meant to stay shut. It was meant to be *answered*. Every generation. A willing hand, a willing wound, a renewal—" He stopped. He looked at Ulyana's bleeding palm, at the salt-lines mapping him, at the seam drinking his blood, and the old priest's face went through wonder and arrived at grief. "Oh. Oh, they didn't tell you. They didn't tell you what you are."

The seam was a hand's width wide now. Through it Ulyana could see—light. A different light than the cavern's memory-glow: golden, low, evening light, falling across something green. A hillside. Terraces. The terraces were overgrown and the light was dim, dimming even as he watched, like a lamp running out of oil, but it was a hillside, a real one, in a real country, and there were people on it. Far away, small as grains, figures standing in the failing gold, and every one of them turned, all at once, toward the seam. Toward him.

The hum became a chord. The chord became a voice—not words, not yet, but the shape of words, the way a crowd's murmur holds the shape of the sentence it's about to become. And underneath it, threaded through it, one voice, older and deeper and patient as stone, that Ulyana had never heard before and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a name you've been signing your whole life.

*Warden,* it said. *Little key. Come home.*

Ulyana's knees hit the white floor. His blood ran freely now, and the Door drank, and the seam stood open a full arm's width, and the golden country breathed toward him through the gap, and behind him Sefa was shouting his name and Vey was shouting something else and Brannoc was saying, over and over, in the old tongue and the new, *the renewal, the renewal, it was always meant to be renewed*—

And from somewhere on the far side of the Door, from the dimming gold, from the crowd of small upturned faces, a single bell began to ring.

It rang the note his blood had been carrying for three hundred years.

And the Door, unbidden, began to open.
