## 14. Inside the Folded Country

Sefa went through the Door third, because Ulyana grabbed her sleeve and said *with me*, and because Vey had her sword out and Brannoc was still chanting, and someone had to be the one who wasn't shouting.

The passage was short. That was the first wrong thing about it. From outside, the Door of Salt had looked like a seam in a cliff face, tall as a cathedral gate, and she'd expected—she didn't know what. A long dark throat. A crossing measured in hours. Instead it was six steps of white stone, warm underfoot like a sun-baked road, and then the light changed from grey to gold and there was no more mountain around them at all.

She stopped walking. The others piled up behind her, which meant she got the first full look at Zephyrine, and for a moment Sefa understood why people had fought wars over lesser things.

It was a valley. No—it was valleys folded inside valleys, terraced down and down in golden steps, orchards hanging heavy with fruit that glowed faintly, like the trees were remembering sunlight rather than receiving it. Rivers ran silver without quite sparkling. The sky above was the color of late afternoon and did not move. Clouds hung where clouds had hung three hundred years ago, painted into place, patient. Everything was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful: accurate, loving, and still.

"It's dying," Brannoc said quietly, behind her. "Look at the edges."

And once he said it, she saw it. The gold thinned toward the horizon the way light thins underwater. The farthest orchards were grey as ash. A river in the middle distance ran to a certain point and simply stopped, its silver ending in a soft blur, like ink giving out on a page. Even the air tasted thin. Not stale—thin, as if the country had been rationing breath.

"Three hundred years behind salt," Brannoc said. "No sun but what they brought with them. No rain but memory."

"Then how is any of this alive?" Vey asked. Her sword was still drawn; she hadn't noticed, or hadn't cared.

"The prayers," Sefa said.

They all looked at her. She hadn't meant to say it aloud, but it sat in her chest with the weight of certainty, the same way the token used to sit in her palm and pull.

"That's what answered us back home. That's why the gods woke up. They've been praying through the Door for three centuries. All of it—the shrines weeping brine, the child's rain, my hands." She flexed her fingers. They still tingled sometimes, the healing warmth, though she hadn't prayed since the cathedral cells. "It leaks. It's been leaking the whole time."

"Prayer doesn't cross stone," Vey said.

"Nothing else here does," Sefa said. "So it must."

* * *

They came down the terraces on roads that had been swept within an inch of their lives. That was the second wrong thing, and it took Sefa longer to name it: everything was too tended. Orchards pruned. Paths raked. Doorways washed white. A country kept clean the way a sickroom is kept clean—not for living in, but for waiting in.

The people came out as they descended. First a few, standing in doorways with that particular stillness of the very old or the very careful. Then dozens. Then, by the time they reached the lowest terrace—a market square of pale stone, fountains dry but gleaming—there were hundreds, flowing in from every street with a silence that raised the hair on Sefa's arms. No shouts. No questions. Just faces, hundreds of upturned faces, all of them looking past her, past Vey's blade, past Brannoc's ruined vestments—

—at Ulyana.

He had gone white as the Door. His cut hand was wrapped in a strip of his own shirt, but the blood had soaked through, and every eye tracked the stain like a compass needle.

An old woman pushed through the crowd. She walked badly, leaning on a staff of salt-white wood, and when she reached him she did not kneel or weep. She took his bandaged hand in both of hers, turned it palm-up, gentle as a midwife, and read the half-healed cut the way Sefa might read a ledger.

"Warden's blood," she said. Her voice carried impossibly far across the square, or perhaps everyone had simply leaned in to hear. "After three hundred years."

"I don't—" Ulyana started.

"You are his heir. The Cartographer's line. The last of the Door-wardens." She said it not with reverence but with relief, the tone of someone reading good news off a bill of lading. "We felt you come. We have felt you coming for months—every time one of your tokens warmed, every time a prayer landed on the far side. You opened the Door." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, sweet and starved at once. "Now open it wide."

The crowd breathed. All of them, together, one long inhale, and Sefa realized with a lurch that they had done this before—that breathing together was something you learned, in a folded country, the way sailors learn to sleep in storms.

"We can't," Brannoc said, stepping forward. "You don't understand what—"

"We understand perfectly." A man this time, younger, with the burn-scarred hands of a kiln worker. He didn't raise his voice either. None of them raised their voices. It was worse than shouting. "We've counted the years, priest. We invented counting them."

"There's a god-king in here who eats faith," Vey said flatly. "There's a treaty that holds him. Open the Door wide and you let him loose on two countries instead of one."

"And how many of us has he eaten?" the kiln worker asked. "Go on. Guess. You clearly have a number ready."

Silence.

"Eleven thousand four hundred," the old woman said, releasing Ulyana's hand. "Give or take. We keep the tally ourselves. We are not children, inquisitor—we know exactly what our rescue costs. And we are telling you the price is worth paying." Her eyes moved to Sefa, and softened, and that was somehow the most frightening thing yet. "You're one of ours, aren't you? Or near enough. Lowtown blood, resonant hands. You know what it is to pray and be refused."

"I know what it is to pray," Sefa said carefully.

"Then tell them." The woman gestured at Ulyana, at Vey, at the whole armed, unwilling delegation of the outside world. "Tell them what it's like to beg for three hundred years and be told the lock is for your own good."

* * *

They were given lodging. Of course they were—refusal wasn't a language this place spoke anymore. A house on the fourth terrace, whitewashed, stocked with bread and fruit and water that tasted faintly of tears. The door did not lock from the inside. Nobody mentioned it.

Sefa found Ulyana on the rooftop terrace at dusk, if dusk could be said to exist here; the golden sky had simply dimmed a degree, obedient to some ancient schedule. He was looking out over the valley with his bandaged hand cradled against his chest, and the token—the pilgrim's token, the compass that had led them all here—lay on the parapet beside him, glowing softly, pointing at nothing. There was nothing left to point at. They had arrived.

"Do you think they're wrong?" she asked.

He didn't startle. He'd gotten better at hearing her come, these last weeks. "About what?"

"Any of it. Opening it wide. Letting him out. Paying the price."

Ulyana was quiet for a while. Below them, lamplight bloomed terrace by terrace, orderly as a litany.

"When I ran debts for Corvas," he said finally, "I collected from a weaver in the highlands. Owed eleven coppers. Eleven. She had seven children and a loom and no food, and I stood in her house with the ledger and she begged, and I wrote it down anyway, because the debt was real and the rules were real and I needed the work." His jaw tightened. "I told myself the same thing they're telling us. *I know exactly what it costs. I'm paying it.* And the arithmetic was even true. But you know what I never asked?"

"What?"

"Who set the price." He turned to look at her, and in the dimming light his eyes had the flat shine of a man holding something down. "Ossian Vael set this price. Three hundred years ago, sealed in here with them, eating their prayers while they starved. And now they're so hungry they'll call the famine mercy if it opens the door. That's not freedom talking. That's hunger wearing freedom's clothes."

"They've suffered," Sefa said. It wasn't disagreement. She needed it said plainly, between the two of them, before anyone else could use it.

"They have. More than we ever will. And that's exactly what makes them dangerous." He picked up the token, closed his fist around it. "Brannoc says Ossian needs a Warden's voice to open the Door fully. Mine. Every person in that square knows it now. They aren't going to hurt me—they can't afford to. So they'll do something worse."

"What's worse than hurting you?"

"Convince me." He almost laughed. "Sefa, they spent ten minutes making me feel like the villain of my own family's story, and it worked. I *am* the villain, from where they stand. My ancestors locked the box. I'm the key. And they're going to spend every hour until Ossian gets to me showing me starving children and singing the old songs and being so unbearably, reasonably desperate—and one night I'm going to be tired, and grieving, and I'm going to say the words just to make the suffering stop." He looked back at the valley. "That's the trap. Not force. Pity."

Sefa thought of her mother's hands in the wash-water, cracked and bleeding every winter. Thought of praying over them and feeling the warmth answer, and how for one shining minute she had believed the world owed her something after all.

Compassion had teeth. She'd always known that. She'd just never considered whose throat they'd end up at.

"Then we leave soon," she said. "Tonight, even. Before—"

"Bell," Ulyana said.

She heard it then. Far below, in the market square, a single bell ringing the deep note they'd heard through the Door—the note of his blood, the note of summons. One bell, then another, then a dozen, spreading across the terraces like fire through dry grass, and with them the sound of feet, hundreds of feet, all moving in one direction.

Toward the palace of salt glass on the far ridge, its towers catching the unmoving golden light.

"He's called an assembly," Ulyana said, standing. "Or he's called us. I genuinely don't know which anymore."

Down in the streets, the people of Zephyrine flowed toward their god-king's hall with the terrible patience of those who have waited three hundred years and can wait one evening more—but will not wait two.

Sefa took Ulyana's unwounded hand.

"Whatever he offers you in there," she said, "remember the weaver. Remember who set the price."

He nodded. But as they followed the crowd down through the dimming gold, Sefa watched his bandaged hand, and saw that beneath the linen, the cut had begun to glow.

Like a wick. Like something waiting to be lit.
