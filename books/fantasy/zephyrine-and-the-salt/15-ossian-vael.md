## 15. Ossian Vael

The guards did not take their weapons, because Ulyana and the others had none worth taking. They took names instead. A woman with salt-gray braids spoke each of theirs aloud before the doors opened, and the hall repeated it back to her—*Ulyana Nystrom. Sefa of the lowtown wash-houses. Inquisitor Vey of the Salt Cathedral.* The echo came down strange, as if the hall were tasting them.

Then the doors opened, and Ulyana stopped thinking about echoes.

The hall of salt glass was not built so much as grown—a cavern of translucent columns rising out of white stone, each one veined with faint light like a candle seen through a closed fist. The ceiling arched high enough to hold weather. Somewhere above, clouds moved inside the glass, slow and gray, and a fine grain of salt fell from them continuously, drifting down through the colored light like snow that had forgotten which way was up.

"It's beautiful," Sefa whispered.

It was. That was the trap of it. Everything here would be beautiful first and terrible second, and by the time you noticed the second thing you'd already knelt.

Because the columns were not empty. Ulyana saw that as they walked the long white floor. Shapes hung inside some of them—people, or what had been people, suspended mid-gesture, faces calm, eyes closed. A man with his hands cupped as if in offering. A girl turned as if dancing. Their lips moved, all of them, all together, though no sound reached the floor.

"They're praying," Vey said. Her voice had gone flat, the way it did when she was frightened and refused to be. "Those are resonants. Taken."

"Taken," said a voice from the far end of the hall, warm as a hand on the shoulder, "*kept*. There is a difference, and you will come to feel it before we are done speaking."

Ossian Vael sat on no throne. He stood among the columns with his sleeves rolled to the forearm, hands stained to the wrist with something dark and glittering, working at a frame of salt glass with the concentration of a carpenter. He looked up as if he'd been expecting a delivery rather than enemies, and smiled.

He looked ordinary. That was the worst of it. Three centuries of worship and horror stories, and the god-king of Zephyrine was a broad, gray-eyed man in his fifties with laugh lines and ink on his fingers, dressed plainly, as if for a day of honest work.

"Don't kneel," he said pleasantly, as Brannoc's knee began to bend. "Please. I've had three hundred years of kneeling. It curdles. Walk with me instead."

* * *

He led them down an avenue of columns, and Ulyana made himself look at the praying figures until the wonder wore off and he could see the arithmetic underneath.

"You've been collecting them," Vey said.

"I've been *answering*," Ossian said. "Answering is expensive. You know this now—I'm told you walked through Harrowmere Vale." At Ulyana's flinch, he inclined his head, courteous, unashamed. "Yes. I'm sorry about the vale. Truly. It was never my intent that faith should run downhill into the innocent. But my people have been locked behind the salt for three hundred years, Warden's heir, praying into a sealed room. Where do you imagine all those prayers went? They pooled. And when I woke, I woke thirsty."

"You ate a village," Brannoc said.

"I drank a debt," Ossian corrected, gently. "Every year of life taken in that valley was a year my people spent buried alive. I am owed, Brannoc. You of all people know how carefully I kept accounts, back when you still called me king." He stopped before a column and rested his palm against it, and inside, the dancing girl slowed her turn to look at him through the glass with eyes full of perfect devotion. "You see? No fear. No hunger in their faces. Ask any of them if they'd take it back."

"The ones who could answer," Sefa said quietly, "aren't in the glass."

Ossian laughed—a real laugh, delighted, and for one obscene moment he sounded like someone's uncle. "Oh, Marrec said you had spine. He undersold you." His gaze slid to Ulyana, and the delight sharpened into attention, and Ulyana felt it physically, like sunlight through a lens. "But then, Marrec undersells everyone these days. Grief does that."

"He told us what he is," Ulyana said.

"He told you a version. He always does." Ossian resumed walking, and they followed, because there was nothing else to do in his hall. "Let me tell you mine, since we're being honest, and I intend to be honest with you, Ulyana Nystrom, because you are the first honest thing to walk into this room in three centuries."

* * *

He stopped at the center of the hall, where the floor was a single pane of salt glass, clear as ice, and beneath it—far below, dim, like a city under lake water—the lights of Zephyrine burned.

Ulyana went to his knees after all. Not in reverence. His legs simply quit.

Streets. Actual streets, laid in a star pattern around a central plaza, lamps lit in windows, tiny figures moving in the market square. Fields beyond the walls, terraced up hillsides. A river catching the light. It was small—folded small, compressed into the space beneath the hall—but it was whole, and inhabited, and it was starving. He could see it even from here: fields too thin, streets too crowded, the lamplight feeble.

"Three hundred years ago," Ossian said, above him, conversational, "your ancestor Aldric Nystrom stood where you're kneeling and helped me seal the Door—not to save Thornfell, whatever the cathedral's founders wrote in their tidy treaty. We sealed it because Zephyrine was losing a war, and I chose imprisonment over extinction, and Aldric chose to make sure I couldn't change my mind later. The Door was meant to be reopened when the war was over. Then your cathedral's founders got hold of the treaty, rewrote its terms, and buried the renewal clause so deep that only a Warden's blood could find it—and killed the Wardens' line down to children and bastards, of which, it turns out, one survived." He crouched, bringing himself level with Ulyana. Up close his eyes were the exact gray of the salt glass. "They hunted your family for three hundred years, boy, while mine starved underground. And you copy tithes for them at three coppers a day."

Ulyana's mouth was dry. "I know what I'm worth."

"Do you?" Ossian straightened. "You're worth a nation. Speak four words and I will give you a name with lands attached, a purpose with a history attached, a people with a country attached. Every Zephyrine alive will know your family name as the name that opened the sky. Your debts—" he smiled, "-are not annulled. They are revealed to have been an insult. You will never count coppers again. You will count years. Centuries. Your bloodline will keep the Door for a thousand years in honor, the way it was always meant to, before the cathedral perverted the office into a prison wardenship."

"And the cost," Vey said.

"The cost," Ossian agreed, turning to her with terrible patience, "is that the Door opens *wide*, Inquisitor. All the way. My people walk out into the sun, and the land that was stolen from them is returned, and yes—" his voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse, "-some of Thornfell's valleys change hands. Some of Thornfell's prayers stop being answered by your salt god, whose silence you've all grown so comfortably deaf around. There may be fighting. There has been three hundred years of it, one-sided, fought with starvation against a locked door. I'd call the ledger balanced before the first spear is raised."

"You'd call it balanced," Ulyana said, still on the floor, looking up through the glass at the market square. A tiny figure there had stopped moving. Was looking up. Looking at him, maybe, if such a thing were possible—up through three hundred years of folded stone, at the shape of a boy kneeling on the lid of their sky.

"Look at me," Ossian said, and Ulyana looked, because refusing would have taken strength he wanted to save. "I know what you think you see in me. You've heard Brannoc's confession—you've heard the old priest tell you I ruled on sacrifice. Ask him what the sacrifices bought. Ask him who the altars fed before I ever touched one." He glanced at Brannoc, and something old and cold passed between them. "Your priest sealed the Door to cage *me*, that part is true. But the founders rewrote the treaty to cage *them*. I am the excuse. The people are the crime."

"That doesn't make you the cure," Sefa said.

"No," Ossian said. "It makes me the physician you don't like the smell of." He extended his hand down to Ulyana—an ordinary hand, ink-stained, warm-looking. "Four words, Warden's heir. *Open the Door wide.* That's all. The rest follows: your name, your nation, your purpose. Or refuse, climb back out through the snow, and spend the rest of your indenture copying tithes over a starving country, listening every night to whether the prayers under the floor have finally stopped."

* * *

Ulyana did not take the hand. He stood on his own, which took longer, and Ossian waited without impatience, because he had three hundred years of it.

And in the waiting, Ulyana did the thing he knew how to do. He tallied.

It was the scriptorium habit, older than his debts: break the overwhelming total into entries, audit each line. *Name:* Ossian offered his family's true history—which Vey had partly confirmed, which Brannoc had partly confessed, which was probably mostly true, and truth was the most dangerous currency in the room. *Nation:* Zephyrine, real, visible, starving under the glass—he had seen it; no one could unsee it now. *Purpose:* warden of the Door, honored, hereditary—everything the cathedral had ground him down for, restored at a word.

Every entry audited clean. That was what terrified him.

Because the ledger had a final page, and Ossian had shown it to him without showing it: the Door opening wide meant not exodus but flood. A nation three centuries hungry pouring into valleys full of people who had never wronged it, led by a king who drank villages and called the drinking arithmetic, who kept his faithful in glass and asked them if they'd take it back, knowing they couldn't answer. Restoration meant war—not because Zephyrines were wicked, but because hunger of that depth does not distinguish between debtors and bystanders, and Ossian Vael had just demonstrated, courteously, at length, that he considered everything outside the glass to be an account receivable.

And beneath all of it, the smallest entry, the one Ulyana almost missed because it was written so faintly: *the prayers under the floor.* If he refused and walked away, they would go on praying into the sealed dark. If he accepted, they would go on being owned. Either way, Ossian held the pen.

Unless the treaty could be rewritten differently. Unless there was a third line in the ledger.

Aldric Nystrom had stood here too. Had helped build the lock—and, if the cathedral's founders had feared something deep enough to murder children over it, had left something behind besides a lock. A renewal clause, Ossian had said. Rewritten terms. Buried deep.

*Only a Warden's blood could find it.*

"You're auditing me," Ossian said, amused.

"Occupational habit."

"Good. Auditors find errors." The god-king stepped back, spread his ink-stained hands, and the whole luminous hall seemed to lean in, columns brightening, the falling salt hanging suspended between them like held breath. "So. Have you found one?"

"Yes," Ulyana said. "Yours is the only signature on the offer."

For a heartbeat the hall went very quiet, and Ossian's smile did not move, but everything behind it did—the gray eyes going flat and bottomless as the glass floor, three centuries of patience revealing itself as three centuries of appetite.

"There," he said softly, "is the error you shouldn't have made."

Behind Ulyana, the doors of the hall closed without being touched, and from the colonnade shadows, the guards began to advance—not running, walking, unhurried, in step with the rhythm of the praying dead—and somewhere above the clouds in the ceiling, thunder rolled through the salt.

Marrec stepped out of the colonnade. He would not meet Ulyana's eyes.
