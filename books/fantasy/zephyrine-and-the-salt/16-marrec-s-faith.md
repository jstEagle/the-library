## 16. Marrec's Faith

Marrec had died once, and he remembered it the way other men remember their wedding day.

He was nine years old. His father had lifted him onto the salt altar in the great hall of Vael, and the choir had been singing, and the god-king—his father, his father before he was a god—had kissed his forehead and said, *You will be the first stone of the new country.* Then the knives came, all of them at once, and Marrec had felt himself become something else: not a boy anymore but a hinge, a nail driven into the architecture of a nation's founding.

That was three hundred and eleven years ago.

He had not aged since. He did not sleep, or if he slept it was only to stand again inside that memory, the choir singing, the knives descending, his father's face above him lit with a love so absolute it had no room for mercy. He woke every morning into the same devotion he had died inside, and he had long ago stopped asking whether that was faith or sentence. The question assumed there was a difference.

Now he stood in the antechamber of the hall of salt glass, watching through the translucent wall as Ossian Vael received Ulyana Nystrom, and Marrec's hands would not stop trembling.

Not with fear. With hope. Hope was the oldest pain he owned.

* *

He had found the scribe in Thornfell's lowtown, in a room that smelled of ink and mildew, and had bought his debt for less than Marrec paid for boots. It had felt like theft. It had felt like grace. Both, probably; everything worth having arrived wearing both faces.

He had shown Ulyana the map—the blood-salt cartography of the Door-wardens, the signature in the corner that matched the name on the indenture papers. *Nystrom. Warden-line. You were born to this,* he had said, and watched the man come apart quietly, the way stone comes apart, grain by grain, no drama, just a slow surrender of shape.

Marrec had thought that would be enough. Inheritance was a lever; you only needed to find where a man's ancestors gripped him.

But Ulyana had run. Of course he had run. The Wardens always ran—that was in the blood too, along with the key-work. And now here they all were, inside the folded country at last, standing in the dimming light of Zephyrine while its last starving thousands prayed themselves hollow behind them, and still the Warden's heir hesitated at the Door as if he had a choice.

Marrec pressed his palm flat against the salt glass. Through it, distorted like a figure seen underwater, he could see his father on the throne of fused crystal, and beyond him, far down the corridor of white stone, the Door itself—a seam of light that hummed even through walls, even through three hundred years of silence.

*Soon,* Marrec told it. *Soon.*

* *

They gave him an hour alone with Ulyana. His father gave him an hour, which was either trust or a test, and after three centuries Marrec had learned not to distinguish.

The chamber they chose was small, round, floored with salt tiles worn smooth by generations of kneeling. Someone had left water and bread on a wooden tray—an old courtesy, hospitality for guests who might soon be sacrifices. Ulyana sat on the bench against the curved wall with his bandaged hand in his lap, and Sefa stood beside him like a drawn knife, and Brannoc—Brannoc, traitor, seal-brother, the priest whose hands had helped close the Door—sat apart with his eyes closed, praying, or pretending to.

"You look tired," Ulyana said.

"I don't sleep." Marrec took the chair opposite. "I want you to understand what you're refusing. Not the throne, not the restoration—I'll get to those. I want you to understand me, because everyone in this country will tell you what I am and none of them will be right."

"Then tell me."

"My father killed me when I was nine." He said it plainly, because he had rehearsed plainness for three hundred years and it was the only register left to him. "First of the sacrifices. There were nine hundred more after me before the Door closed. My blood is in the foundation stones of this whole nation—you're literally sitting on me. And when the Door sealed, the sacrifice stopped working the way it was meant to. The dead were supposed to feed the land. Instead—" he spread his hands "—the dead fed the faithful. Every prayer spoken over my grave, every candle, every mother teaching her child my name as a blessing—it kept me stitched together. I exist because people loved my father enough to grieve me forever."

Sefa's voice came sharp: "So you're a ghost."

"I'm a congregation," Marrec said. "I'm what worship does to a body when it won't let go. And I have watched my father starve for three centuries, Ulyana. Slowly. Patiently. The echoes take scraps—answered prayers, small hungers fed. But he is a king, and a king cannot live on scraps. Do you know what he was, before? Do you know what Zephyrine was?"

"A country," Ulyana said carefully. "A stolen one."

"A garden." Marrec leaned forward. "Yes, there were altars. Yes, there was blood. I know how Brannoc tells it—he's been telling it to himself for three hundred years to make his betrayal bearable. But I was there. I remember the harvests. I remember a country where no child went hungry, where the winters bent around the villages instead of through them. My father asked for very little and gave everything, and when the cathedral's founders couldn't abide a god who wouldn't kneel to them, they didn't defeat him—they *erased* him. They folded a living nation into a box of salt and called it peace. Your church has been singing about its own crime for three centuries, and calling the song scripture."

Brannoc opened his eyes. "The sacrifices were children, Marrec."

"The sacrifices were *willing*." Marrec heard his own voice crack on it, and hated that, and continued. "I was willing. Ask me now if I regret it. Ask me."

"I'm asking," Brannoc said softly. "Three hundred years of being kept alive by the thing that ate you. Are you willing still?"

The silence went on too long. Marrec made himself fill it.

"Yes," he said. "That's what none of you can comprehend. You think love should be reasonable. You think devotion should pay out fairly, like wages. Mine doesn't. Mine is a wound that sings. And I would rather be his wound than your free man. Now—" he turned back to Ulyana, and made his voice gentle, because gentleness was the last tool in the kit "—do you understand what happens if you walk away?"

"The Door stays shut."

"The Door stays shut and my father finishes starving. It will take another fifty years, maybe sixty. He'll grow thin and strange, and the echoes will grow bold, and the last of these people—" Marrec gestured at the wall, beyond which the dimming country lay "—will pray until there's nothing left of them but the praying. And then the box opens on its own, rotten, empty, and everything your refusal protected will be ash anyway. You can't keep the treaty frozen, Warden. It was written to be renewed. Renewal is the only mercy in it. Open the Door *wide*, speak the full words, and Zephyrine steps back into the world with its king alive. No one else has to die. Not one person outside this fold. That is the offer. That has always been the offer."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. His bandaged hand flexed.

"And the war?" he said. "Your herald promised us a restored nation. Nations don't come back from erasure peacefully. Thornfell took our valleys, our names, our graves. You think the returning faithful will light candles about it?"

"They will light candles," Marrec said, "and weep with joy, and go home."

"And if Thornfell objects?"

"Thornfell erased us first." He kept his voice level. "But no. My father doesn't want war. War feeds other gods. He wants what he always wanted: a country that loves him back. Give him that and he will be gentle. I promise you gentleness on my—"

He stopped. On my life, he almost said, and the joke of it sat in his mouth like a stone.

"On what?" Sefa asked, merciless.

Marrec looked at her, this laundress with prayer in her hands, and saw that she understood him completely, and pitied him completely, and that both of those things were worse than hatred.

"You've never been free either," she said. "Have you. All this time. You talk about devotion like it saved you, but you can't leave him. Even if you wanted to. Can you?"

"No," Marrec said. It cost him nothing to admit; it had cost him everything decades ago, and the account was long settled. "No. I can't. That's why I need you to open the Door, Ulyana. Because I can't ever open anything. I can only stand at hinges." He smiled, and knew it was the smile of a dead boy. "You're the last free hand in this story. Use it."

Ulyana stood. Slowly, joints stiff, a tired scribe in a borrowed coat, and Marrec felt the terrible vertigo of watching three centuries of hope balance on that unremarkable body.

"No," Ulyana said.

* *

"No," he said again, when Marrec rose too. "I've read my ancestor's testament. The Door was never meant to be opened wide. It was meant to be *renewed*—rewritten each generation by a willing hand, so the living could pass and the hungry thing behind the veil could stay hungry. Your father built the first version of that treaty, Marrec. Did you know that? He wrote his own cage, because even he knew what he'd become if the sacrifices never stopped. Even *he* drew the line past himself."

"Liar." Marrec's voice came out of him like something torn. "My father would never—"

"He was a man once. Men draw lines." Ulyana's eyes were wet and steady. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry—for you most of all. But I won't loose him on two countries to save one ghost's feelings. There are people in Zephyrine who deserve to walk out into the sun. He isn't one of them. He gave that up. Maybe he knew he was giving it up. Maybe that was his last act as a father—writing the cage before the hunger finished him. Think about that, Marrec. Please. Think about what he was protecting you from."

And that—*that*—was the unforgivable thing. Not the refusal. Marrec had planned for refusal; refusal was simply the next scene in a play he had watched rehearsal after rehearsal across centuries of imagining this meeting. What he had not planned for was Ulyana handing him a kinder story than his own and making it fit. A father's final mercy. A cage built as a gift.

Because if that was true—if Ossian Vael had chosen the sealing, chosen the slow starvation, chosen to bind himself for the sake of the son he had already killed—then everything Marrec was, every year of his stitched-together existence, every prayer he had whispered into the dark asking his father to wake, had been a cruelty performed on a sleeping man by a loving child. He had spent three centuries keeping alive the one being who wanted most to stay dead.

He felt the floor tilt. Salt tiles, worn smooth by knees. His own knees found them without his permission.

"Marrec." Ulyana's voice, careful. "Marrec, sit down—"

"*Get away from me.*"

He stood. He was aware, distantly, of Sefa moving between him and Ulyana, of Brannoc rising with an old man's caution, of the water on the tray trembling in rings. Inside his chest, the congregation stirred—all those centuries of grief-love, all those candles, waking to the sound of their keeper breaking—and Marrec held them down the way you hold a dog by the collar, and understood, with perfect clarity, that he could not hold them much longer.

There was only one way this ended now. There had only ever been one way; he had simply refused to read the script.

"I'm sorry," Marrec said, and meant it, and that was the worst part, that he meant it. "You should have lied to me. You should have told me my father was a tyrant and I was a fool. I could have carried that. I've carried worse." He stepped back toward the door, toward the guard-post beyond it, toward the horns that would summon the salt-guard. "But you told me the truth, and the truth means the Door closes with him awake and screaming behind it, and I will not hear that. I can't. Forgive me. Forgive me. *Guard!* GUARD!"

The door burst open on white armor and drawn blades, and Marrec stood aside to let his father's soldiers pour past him toward the people he had begged to save him, and he did not watch them take Ulyana's arms, because he was looking at the floor, at his own reflection in the polished salt, a nine-year-old boy's face on a three-hundred-year-old ruin, and the reflection was smiling, because the congregation demanded it, because faith was the one door Marrec Nystrom's son had never once been allowed to close.

They dragged the Warden heir toward the vaults. Toward his ancestors' testament, and the knife of door-stone hidden in Sefa's sleeve, and whatever the three of them had already begun planning in the language of glances while Marrec made his speech.

He knew. Some part of him, deep under the devotion, knew exactly what he had just set in motion.

He let the guards march them down anyway, and followed, and prayed.
