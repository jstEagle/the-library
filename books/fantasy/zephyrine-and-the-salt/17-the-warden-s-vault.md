## 17. The Warden's Vault

They took his boots first.

Ulyana didn't understand why until the guards marched him down the spiral stair behind the salt-glass hall—down past the level where the torches gave out, past the level where the light came from the walls themselves, a pale luminescence like moonlight trapped in ice—and he felt the stone change under his bare feet. Warmer. Almost soft. The Door-stone recognized him before the guards did.

The vault was a round chamber at the bottom of everything, and it was not a prison. That was the first cruelty of it. It was beautiful. Shelves of white stone curved along the walls, holding jars of salt that glowed faintly amber, each one labeled in a hand so old the letters had gone round with age. A desk stood beneath a skylight of salt glass, and through it he could see the dim sky of Zephyrine, the folded country's permanent dusk, stars that were probably not stars at all but the underside of Thornfell's mountains seen from within.

A bed. A chair. A lamp that burned without oil.

"They kept Warden's heirs here," Marrec had said, before the guards took him away. His voice had been gentle, which was worse than cruel. "Generations of them. You'll want for nothing."

*You'll want for nothing.* The same sentence as the cathedral's indenture contract, dressed in better cloth.

The door closed behind him with a sound like a struck bell fading, and Ulyana stood in the middle of his inheritance and did not move for a long time.

He catalogued, because cataloguing was what he knew how to do. It was how he'd survived nine years in the scriptorium: when the world became unbearable, reduce it to entries. *One chamber, circular, approximately eight paces across. One desk, one chair, one bed, one prisoner. Provision unknown. Term of sentence: unknown. Debt owed: everything.*

His palm still throbbed where Ossian Vael's guard had taken blood—not much, just enough to confirm what the Door already knew. The cut had been closed with a salve that smelled of the same amber salt as the jars, and it had healed in an hour, which should have frightened him more than it did. He was beginning to accustom himself to being impossible.

He sat at the desk because sitting was better than standing, and because the desk faced the skylight, and because if this was to be his cell then he would at least choose his angle on it.

That was when he saw the drawer.

It was flush with the desk's side, seamless, invisible unless you were looking for the seam—and he was always looking for seams. Smuggler's habit. Debts-runner's habit. Every lock in Thornfell was a promise that something valuable was behind it, and every hidden thing was hidden by someone who wanted it found by the right person. He ran his thumb along the wood and felt the faintest ridge, pressed, and the drawer slid open without sound.

Inside lay a book.

Not a grand thing. No gilding, no salt-glass cover. Just a codex bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked from centuries of opening and closing—or perhaps from never being opened at all. On the first page, in ink faded to the color of weak tea, was a signature.

*Nystrom.*

His own name. Not a name like it—the exact name, spelled exactly as his father had spelled it, as his grandmother had spelled it, in the cramped leftward slant that Ulyana had inherited along with his bad knees and his talent for finding mountain passes that didn't officially exist.

He did not open the book immediately. He sat with his hand flat on the cover and listened to the vault breathe—that faint hum in the walls, the Door's pulse, slow as a sleeping heart—and he thought about all the people who had sat in this chair before him. All the Nystroms. All the wardens.

Then he opened it.

*To the child who finds this,* the first page began, *who will be my blood or no one's, because the Door does not call strangers.*

*I am Aldric Nystrom, last warden of the Door of Salt, and I am writing this in the year after the Sealing, which everyone around me is calling a victory, and which I know to be a mistake of historic proportion.*

Ulyana read.

* *

*You have been told, whoever you are, that the treaty was written to save Thornfell from Zephyrine. This is a lie told by men who needed their sin to look like virtue. I helped write the treaty. I helped seal the Door. Hear me now, while my hands can still hold a pen: we did not seal a country away to protect another country. We sealed a god away, and we used a country as the lid.*

*Ossian Vael was starving. Do you understand? Gods starve, though no priest will say it aloud. He had eaten three generations of Zephyrine's faith—willingly given at first, then less willingly, then taken. The sacrifices you have heard rumors of, the ones the cathedral calls Zephyrine's barbarism: they began as prayers and ended as harvests. He grew stronger on devotion the way a fire grows on timber, and there was not enough timber left. So he turned to other fuel. Blood works. Years work. Children work best of all, gods forgive us, because children believe so completely.*

*We could not kill him. Understand that too. We tried. My grandmother tried; her testament is in the third jar on the eastern shelf, if the salt has preserved it. What cannot be killed can be contained, and containment requires a boundary, and a boundary requires a country to draw it around. So we drew the treaty. We folded Zephyrine inside the Door—not destroyed, not erased, only set aside, like a letter sealed for later reading.*

*For later reading. That is the part they burned out of every copy. That is the part I am writing here in blood-salt so that no archivist, no inquisitor, no well-meaning saint can ever remove it again.*

*The treaty was never meant to be permanent.*

*A seal that endures becomes a tomb. A tomb breeds resentment, and resentment in a folded country curdles toward the very god we locked in with them. Every generation that passes with the Door shut feeds Ossian twice—once with the despair of the faithful who feel abandoned, and once with the anger of those who remember why. We handed our descendants a debt we intended them to pay forever, and debts forever are not debts. They are ownership.*

*So hear the terms as they were actually written, in the version only the wardens kept:*

*The Door shall be renewed each generation by a willing hand of the warden's line. Renewed—not merely maintained. Rewritten. Each heir comes to the threshold, reads the treaty anew, and amends it according to the needs of their age. Perhaps in one generation the amendment is to widen the passage for trade. In another, to let the hungry god thin further. In another, perhaps, to end the sealing entirely, if Zephyrine has healed and Ossian has starved into something harmless.*

*The sealing was meant to be a conversation between generations. Instead it became a silence. Three hundred years of silence.*

*My son refused the renewal. I write this with shame I will carry past death: my own son, terrified of the Door after watching what it cost me, fled to Thornfell proper and let the treaty lapse unamended. And a lapsed treaty does not vanish—it hardens. The words calcified into law. The cathedral took up the keeping of it, and the cathedral keeps things the way a tomb keeps things, and so the Door has stood unopened and unrenewed for three centuries while a god grows patient inside it and a people grow desperate around him.*

*If you are reading this, then the Door has called you home, which means the calcification is cracking, which means time has run out in ways I cannot predict. I can only tell you what you are.*

*You are not a key. Keys turn once. You are not a lock. Locks only refuse. You are a scribe.*

*Do you understand me, child? Whoever you are, whatever they have made of your life—you come from a line of scribes. Cartographer-priests. Our gift was never power. It was literacy. We could read the treaty, and therefore we could amend it, and the Door obeys its text above any king, any god, any cathedral. Ossian wants you to speak the opening words because he believes the words are his. They are not. They never were. The words belong to whoever holds the pen with a willing hand.*

*Willing. Mark that word. It appears eleven times in the original treaty and it is the whole of the law. Blood given under compulsion seals nothing; blood offered rewrites everything. That is why he cannot simply cut you. That is why he needs your consent, dressed up however he likes—as honor, as belonging, as nationhood. He will make the cage beautiful. He will make the cage feel like home. He has had three hundred years to practice.*

*And when you understand what must be done—if you understand it—know that you need not do it alone, and know also that you may do it alone, and both are permitted. The treaty asks for a willing hand. It does not ask for a willing martyr.*

*I have written all I can. The salt takes the rest. Forgive us, all of us, for mistaking endurance for duty. The sealing was meant to end. Everything meant to end. That is what makes it bearable.*

*—Aldric Nystrom, warden, in the year after the Sealing, in the vault that was meant to be a study, and became a cell, and waits to be a study again.*

* *

Ulyana set the book down.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, the way he'd once watched a ledger balance to the copper after hours of error-hunting—the small astonishment of a thing finally coming true.

*You are a scribe.*

Nine years copying tithes. Nine years tallying his own indenture down to the copper, learning that every number was a sentence someone had written and someone else had agreed to serve. He had thought the skill was his cage. It had been his inheritance all along, waiting in a drawer.

He laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and echoed off the salt-glass skylight, and the walls hummed a little louder, as if the Door itself had leaned closer to listen.

Because here was the thing—the terrible, liberating thing—that the dead man's testament made plain: Ossian Vael had made a catastrophic error. The god-king had bought Ulyana's debt, carried him over the mountains, installed him in a vault full of his ancestors' writing, and left him alone with a lamp and time.

Left a scribe alone with the original documents.

Marrec would be back. Or the guards would come to fetch him for whatever ceremony Ossian was preparing—the hall of salt glass had been built for acoustics, Ulyana realized now, for amplifying a single voice into a choir, and a god about to be freed would want his key presented properly, bathed and robed and willing-looking. There would be a performance. There would be an audience of the faithful, starving and hopeful, and Ossian would ask for the words gently, publicly, so that refusal looked like betrayal of Zephyrine itself.

But between now and then—

Ulyana pulled the drawer fully open and found, beneath the testament, what he had been praying against hope to find: more books. A shelf of them, hidden in the desk's deeper hollow. The warden's working library. Treaties. Amendments. Marginalia in six different hands across three hundred years of lapsed renewals—no, not lapsed, he corrected himself, *suspended*, because the difference mattered, because in law and in salt the difference was everything. The final entry was Aldric's own draft amendment, unfinished, its last lines trailing into blank space:

*Let it be recorded that the Door opens—*

Opens what? Opens outward? Opens for whom? The old man had died—or been stopped—before finishing the sentence, and three centuries later the sentence was still waiting, and the pen was in Ulyana's family, and the pen was in Ulyana's hand.

He got up and went to the shelves of amber jars. Aldric had said his grandmother's testament was in the third jar on the eastern shelf. He found it—a scroll rolled tight, preserved in salt, the handwriting fierce even through the fog of age. He did not read it yet. He stacked it with the others. He was building a ledger. An evidence file. A case.

Because that was the other thing the testament had given him, sharper than any knife: *blood offered rewrites everything.* Ossian could not force the words. Which meant the god-king's entire strategy rested on making Ulyana *want* to speak them—which meant every offer, every kindness, every beautiful cage was a weapon aimed at his consent, and a weapon you can see is a weapon you can survive.

And Sefa was out there somewhere. Vey too, unless the inquisitor was dead, unless the resonant network had been crushed. If either of them was alive, if either of them remembered what he'd told them about the salt-glass hall—*it amplifies him, it's a bell, break the bell and you break his voice*—then there might be a moment, one narrow loud moment of chaos, when a scribe could walk out of a study that had been mislabeled a cell.

He sat back down at the desk. He took Aldric's unfinished draft and laid it flat. From the tray beside the lamp—he allowed himself a thin smile at that; his ancestors had equipped this prison with an inkwell, because of course they had, because they were Nystroms—he took the stylus, and a vial of salt-ink that gleamed like dawn.

He did not write yet. Writing was commitment, and commitment required knowing the terms, and he was done signing things he hadn't read. But he laid the stylus across the page, aligned with the margin, ready.

Above him, muffled through three floors of folded stone, the salt-glass hall began to sing.

It started as a hum, one voice, deep and patient—Ossian's voice, or the choir tuned to it—and then other voices joined, dozens, hundreds, the faithful of Zephyrine gathered to pray their god back into the world. The vibration came down through the vault floor and up through Ulyana's bare feet and settled in his chest like a second heartbeat.

They were gathering the congregation. The ceremony would be soon.

Ulyana Nystrom, indentured scribe, debtor, smuggler, heir of wardens, put his palm flat on the unfinished page and waited for the bell to crack.
