## 18. Breaking the Bell

Vey had spent nineteen years learning how cathedrals lie, and she had never once believed she would spend the twentieth unlearning it.

She stood in the shadow of a buttress on Zephyrine's side of the Door, watching the salt-glass hall from a distance that felt safe and was not. The hall rose out of the folded country like a wound in the landscape — a cathedral of translucent panes, each one taller than three men, each one singing. Not metaphorically. The glass hummed, a low sustained note that Vey felt in her back teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the scar tissue of her left hand where an inquisitor's brand had healed crooked. Every pane was tuned to a different voice, and together they made a chord that Ossian Vael breathed like air.

The choir. Marrec had called it that, back when he'd been showing Ulyana the map with pride instead of betrayal. *His power is gathered there,* he'd said. *Three hundred years of prayer, held in glass.* He had said it as a boast. He had not understood he was giving them the blueprint for his own grief.

Sefa stood beside her in the dark, hands wrapped in strips of wool to hide the light they gave off when she prayed. Even muffled, they glowed faintly at the knuckles, like coals banked under ash.

"You're certain," Vey said. It wasn't a question. She asked it anyway, because asking was the last ritual she had left that still meant anything.

"I can hear them." Sefa's voice was flat with effort. "The resonants. There are eleven of them in the hall right now. They think they're being honored. Ossian has them standing between the panes, singing the responses, and every note they give him, he takes more than they know."

"Eleven." Vey did the arithmetic she hated. Eleven people who would be standing inside a structure built to concentrate divine force when it came apart. "And you can reach all of them?"

"They reached *me* first." Sefa turned her bandaged hands over. "That's what resonance is, Inquisitor. It isn't a gift the old gods hand down. It's a frequency. When one of us prays near another, we hear it. I've been listening since they brought me through the Door. They're frightened. They were told this hall would let them speak directly to their god, and now some of them suspect what the speaking costs, and none of them can leave because the guards outside answer to Marrec."

"And the ones who don't suspect?"

Sefa was quiet a moment. "They'll learn," she said. "Or they won't. I can't save everyone from their own faith, Inquisitor. Neither could you."

That landed where it was meant to. Vey looked away, toward the vault entrance cut into the mountain's flank — the dark mouth where Ulyana Nystrom had been carried two days ago, bound, silent, refusing even then to look afraid. She had put him in those chains herself, or rather she had failed to stop the men who did, and the distinction felt thinner every hour.

The plan was simple, which was the only kind of plan that survived contact with a god. Sefa would pray. Not quietly, not privately — she would open herself the way she had never dared open herself in Thornfell, and every resonant in the hall would feel her arrive like a shout in a quiet room. Eleven frightened people, suddenly aware they were not alone, suddenly aware that someone on the outside knew what was happening to them. Some would panic. Some would run. And if even three of them broke formation, if even three voices dropped out of the chord —

"The glass is tuned," Vey said. "If the harmony breaks—"

"It doesn't shatter like window glass." Sefa flexed her fingers inside the wool. "It shatters like a bell. All at once, everywhere, from the inside. That's why we have to be far enough away. And why he has to already be moving."

Ulyana. Everything came back to Ulyana. The vault was guarded by six of Marrec's faithful, men and women who had surrendered their names years ago and answered now only to titles. Vey had watched them change watch twice. Disciplined. Devout. Unbribable, probably.

But not unkillable, and that was the part of the plan she had not said aloud, the part sitting against her spine like a cold coin: someone might have to die tonight, and it would not be a god, and it would not be clean, and the cathedral's doctrine had no absolution for killing in service of a heresy that happened to be true.

"Say it again," Sefa said. "The part about why you're doing this."

Vey almost laughed. "You want my confession before a battle?"

"I want to know what holds you together if it goes wrong."

Vey considered lying, found she didn't have the energy, and told the truth instead. "I interrogated a laundress," she said. "In the cathedral depths. For nine hours. I was good at it. I've always been good at it. And somewhere around the eighth hour she stopped answering me and started praying, and her hands began to heal the burns I'd given her, and I stood there holding my instruments and realized I was torturing a miracle to protect an institution that had erased a country." She paused. "Then the founders' letters confirmed it, and I discovered my whole career had been janitorial work for a crime. So. That's what holds me together. Spite, mostly. And arithmetic."

Sefa studied her for a long moment. Then she unwound the wool from her hands.

* * *

They climbed to the ridge above the hall while the folded country's false dusk settled — that dim amber half-light that never fully darkened here, as though the land itself were too tired to finish the day. From the ridge they could see everything: the singing hall, the guard fires at the vault mouth, the thin thread of smoke rising from the god-king's tower where something vast and patient was feeding.

Sefa knelt on bare stone and pulled off her gloves.

"Whatever happens," she said, "when the glass goes, run for the vault. Don't wait for me."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are, actually. Because if you stay, you'll try to fight, and you're one woman with a stolen sword, and I need you alive to get him out." She looked up, and her eyes had begun to glow at the edges, faint gold bleeding into the irises. "Besides. I'm not alone either. Not anymore."

Vey wanted to argue. Instead she drew her sword — cathedral issue, the blade etched with the treaty-script she now knew was a lie — and took up position behind a shelf of rock with a clear line down to the vault guards.

Below, Sefa closed her eyes and prayed.

Vey had expected words. What came instead was silence that pressed outward, a stillness like the moment before lightning, and then the ridge itself seemed to inhale. Light moved across the valley — not from Sefa but through her, gold running along the ground in branching lines like frost forming in reverse, reaching toward the hall, toward the eleven small flames of the resonants standing among the panes.

Inside the hall, the chord faltered.

It was subtle at first — a wavering in the hum, a pane whose note bent sharp. One of the figures between the glass raised her head. Vey saw her mouth move, saw her turn to the resonant beside her, saw the question pass between them: *did you hear that?*

They had heard it. Someone was praying for *them*. In three hundred years inside the fold, no one had prayed for them; they had only ever been the currency.

A second voice dropped out of the chord. A third. The hum warped, went sour, and high above the valley the panes began to ring against each other, dissonant, quarreling.

Guards spilled from the hall. Marrec's faithful, running toward the ridge with torches, trying to find the source of the interference. Vey counted four breaking away from the vault to join them. Two remained at the vault mouth.

Two she could manage.

She came down the scree fast and low, and the first guard heard her too late — her pommel caught him behind the ear and he folded without a sound. The second turned, saw the cathedral sword, and hesitated for exactly the length of time it takes a devout man to reconcile the symbol with the act. Vey did not hesitate. She struck the sword from his grip, swept his legs, and put her knee on his chest with the blade at his throat.

"Inquisitor," he gasped. "You're — she said you were coming. The herald said—"

"Where is the key to his chains?"

He stared at her. Above them, the hall screamed — a sound like a thousand wineglasses dying — and light burst from every pane at once, white and total, and the chord that had held three hundred years of prayer tore down the middle.

The glass did not fall. It *detonated inward*, collapsing toward its own center, and the shockwave flattened the guard fires and threw Vey sideways across the scree. Her ears filled with ringing. Through it, dimly, she heard the second guard scrambling away down the slope, fleeing, faith broken at last by simple physics.

Vey got up. Her sword was gone. It didn't matter.

The vault door hung open, torn from its hinges by the blast, and beyond it stairs descended into dark, and somewhere down there a scribe with smuggler's hands was waiting to find out whether anyone had come for him.

She went down into the dark at a run.

* * *

Ulyana heard the bell break from three levels underground, and it was the most beautiful sound he had ever heard.

The vault was a long chamber carved from white stone, lined with niches, and every niche held a dead Warden's effects — maps, seals, bones, the accumulated guilt of his bloodline arranged like a museum of sins. He had spent two days among them with his wrists chained, reading what he could reach, and on the second night he had found the testament: a slim codex wrapped in oilcloth, hidden behind his great-grandmother's funerary seal, written in a hand that trembled with age and certainty both.

*The Door is not a wall,* the testament said. *It is a covenant, and covenants expire. We sealed nothing forever. We wrote a treaty meant to be renewed by a willing hand each generation, and our cowardice let it lapse into a prison. Renewal, not opening. Renewal, not erasure.*

He had read it eleven times. He had wept once, briefly, and hated himself for the luxury.

Now the ceiling cracked, dust sifted down, and somewhere far above the choir's sustained note ended — not faded, *ended*, severed — and in the silence that followed he heard footsteps on the stairs, fast and uneven, and a voice he had last heard across an interrogation table.

"Nystrom!" Vey emerged into the niche-light with soot on her face and no weapon, which somehow made her more frightening, not less. She had a keyring wrenched from some unconscious guard. "Tell me you found it."

He lifted the testament. "Found it."

"Can you do what it says?"

"I don't know. I'm going to find out." He stood, chains clacking. "The knife, though. Tell me you brought a knife."

Vey stared at him. "Why would I bring you a knife?"

"Because door-stone answers door-stone, and the Door is thirty miles north, and I refuse to walk into a negotiation with a god unarmed." He rattled his chains. "Also, these need to come off, and I'd rather not owe Marrec's people the favor."

For a moment — one strange, suspended moment — Vey smiled. It transformed her face entirely, made her look like someone who had once been young in a country that still existed. Then she was working the lock, and the chains fell, and Ulyana rubbed his wrists and took stock: testament, chains, a dead Warden's knife rack ten feet away.

Except the knives in the rack were ceremonial, silver-hilted, useless. He was turning away from them when he saw it — set apart in its own niche, wrapped in leather gone stiff with age. A blade of cloudy white stone, veined with gold, humming faintly when his fingers touched it. His blood recognized it before his mind did. Door-stone. Cut from the threshold itself by some ancestor who had understood, even then, that a key should sometimes look like a weapon.

"There," he said. "That one."

Vey looked at the knife, then at him. "Above us," she said, "a building full of imprisoned prayers just collapsed, a god just lost his amplifier, and in approximately no time at all he is going to be very interested in this vault. So whatever you're going to say next, say it while running."

They ran.

Behind them, deep in the mountain, something that had been patient for three hundred years stopped being patient. The stone itself seemed to flinch. And ahead, up the stairs, through the ruined hall, across the starving country — thirty miles north, the Door of Salt waited, humming, recognizing, ready to be opened or rewritten, depending entirely on what a scribe chose to say when he finally stood in front of it.

Ulyana took the stairs two at a time, the testament against his chest and the knife warm in his fist, and for the first time since the dying pilgrim had pressed a token into his hand, he was not running *from* anything.

He was running *toward*.
