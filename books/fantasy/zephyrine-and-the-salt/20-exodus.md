## 20. Exodus

Marrec had spent three hundred years learning how to kneel, and in the end it was the only skill that mattered.

He knelt now on the salt-glass floor of the shattered choir, among the ruins of his father's voice. The great panes that had caught Ossian Vael's prayers and multiplied them lay in glittering drifts, kicked apart by Sefa's resonants, ground under the boots of fleeing guards. Where the choir had sung, there was only a ringing silence — and inside that silence, Marrec could feel his father diminishing. Not dying. Gods didn't die. But thinning, like light through a closing door.

The Door of Salt was open behind him. He could hear it: the crowd, the weeping, the first footsteps of Zephyrine crossing into Thornfell after three hundred years of folded dark.

His father's hand closed on his shoulder. It had never once felt like flesh. It felt like a debt.

"You will stay," Ossian said. Not a question. A king's arithmetic.

"I will stay," Marrec agreed.

* * *

He had been nine years old when his father first fed him to the god.

That was not how the priests of the old court had phrased it, afterward, when they wrote the histories that no one would ever read. They called it *consecration*. They called it *the first fruit of the restored throne*. Marrec remembered it differently: the cold of the vault, the taste of salt on his tongue, and his father's face above him — young still, beautiful in the way mountains are beautiful, indifferent to what they crush.

Ossian Vael had been rising even then. The treaty was thirty years old by then; the cartographer-priests had folded the country into the Door and salted the seams, and the people of Zephyrine had learned to pray quietly, because loud prayers were answered, and answered prayers cost. The god-king needed faith like a furnace needs coal, and he had taken it from his own household first. From his son most of all.

Marrec should have died. Every other consecrated child had died — the vaults beneath the palace were stacked with their small bones, and Ossian had walked past them every morning for centuries without slowing. But Marrec had loved his father with the whole stupid totality of a nine-year-old heart, and love, it turned out, was a fuel more potent than fear. His devotion burned so clean and so long that the god-king kept him lit. Kept him alive past his own death, past the death of everyone who remembered his name, past the death of the country itself. A candle that refused to go out because someone kept cupping their hands around it.

Three hundred years. And in all that time, his father had never once asked him a question about himself.

Until tonight.

"Ulyana Nystrom spoke the renewal," Ossian said. He stood at the threshold of the ruined choir, looking out through the open Door at the grey light of Thornfell's morning, and his voice was the voice from before the fall — vast, patient, certain. "Not the opening. The little scribe chose the smaller word."

"He chose the word that costs him nothing," Marrec said. "You taught me that much, at least. Read the ledger before you sign."

His father laughed. It was not a kind sound, but it was not unkind either; it was the sound of a thing too large to bother being cruel. "The people leave. My people. Three hundred years I have starved in the dark keeping them alive, and they walk out my door without a glance back, and you tell me this is wisdom?"

"They're glancing back." Marrec nodded toward the threshold. "Look."

* * *

They were pouring through in families, in clusters, in single file — farmers with seed-sacks over their shoulders, children carrying birds in wicker cages, old men walking with the straight-backed dignity of people who have decided not to limp in front of witnesses. The Zephyrine light came through with them, that dim amber glow of a country running low on sun, and where it touched Thornfell's grass the grass seemed briefly astonished.

And some of them did look back. An old woman stopped at the seam of white stone, set down her bundle, and bowed — not to Ulyana, not to Sefa or Vey or Brannoc standing watch on the Thornfell side, but inward, toward the dark, toward the fading presence at the heart of the folded land. She pressed her palm flat against the Door-stone and left it there a moment, the way you touch the forehead of a dying parent.

Then she picked up her bundle and walked on.

"They will make songs about today," Ossian said. "In the songs, I will be the monster. That is the tax on defeat." He turned from the threshold. For the first time in three hundred years, something in his posture had come loose — not weakness exactly, but the absence of the thing that had held him upright, which had been hunger, which had been certainty. "The veil thins. In a generation, perhaps two, this fold will flatten entirely, and I will be nothing but weather. You understand what you are choosing, Marrec. You have always understood arithmetic better than faith."

"No," Marrec said. "I've always understood faith better than arithmetic. That was always our disagreement."

* * *

Sefa found him at the edge of the choir ruins, an hour later, when the exodus had slowed to a trickle and the Door hummed its struck-bell note in the background like a held breath.

She looked terrible. Everyone did — Vey's coat scorched along one sleeve, Brannoc leaning on a staff he'd clearly cut himself within the last day, Ulyana with his bandaged hand held close to his chest like a small animal he was protecting. But Sefa stopped in front of Marrec alone, and she studied him with the frank, unsentimental gaze of a laundress assessing a stain.

"You could come through," she said. "Vey's already arguing with her own cathedral about amnesty. One more name on the list won't tip it."

"And do what? Pray to him from across the border?" Marrec smiled. It hurt his face; he hadn't used it much. "I'm not a man, Sefa. I'm a wick. Take the wick away from the candle and see what's left."

"There are other things to burn for."

"Not for me. There never were." He glanced back at the deepening dark beyond the choir, where his father waited, already thinner than he'd been an hour ago. "Do you know what he said to me tonight? First question anyone's asked me in three hundred years. He asked if I understood what I was choosing." Marrec shook his head slowly. "He thinks it was a choice between him and freedom. As if I'd know what to do with freedom. As if it would fit."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then she reached out and took his hand — the one that had held doors open for his father for three centuries, the one that had dragged Ulyana before the guard, the one that had begged. Her grip was warm and ordinary and it undid him more than any blade could have.

"When he's gone," she said, "come find us anyway. There's work in the valleys. Nobody there will care what you were."

"When he's gone," Marrec said, "there won't be enough of me left to find."

She let go. She didn't argue, and he was grateful for that; argument was a currency he couldn't spend anymore. She rejoined the others at the threshold, and Ulyana Nystrom looked back at him last — the Warden's heir, the little scribe with the knife of door-stone and the rewritten treaty drying on his palm — and gave him a nod. Not forgiveness. Recognition. One ledger-keeper acknowledging another.

Then they crossed, and the Door began to close behind them, and Marrec turned to walk back into his country.

* * *

It took four days for the last of the Zephyrines to pass through, and Marrec spent them doing the only honest work of his long life: counting.

He stood at the threshold with a tally-stick, as his father's court had once demanded of everyone, and counted his people out of bondage. Nine thousand and change. Farmers, weavers, a whole guild of bell-founders who wept as they carried their molds through. Children born inside the fold who had never seen an unfurrowed horizon and kept stopping to stare at the sky, which went on forever here, which had no ceiling of salt.

On the fifth morning, the last family came through — a grandmother, her daughter, a boy of six who planted himself in front of Marrec and frowned up at him.

"You're the one in the stories," the boy said. "The bad one."

"Yes," Marrec said.

"The god-king's son."

"That too."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My gran says you stayed 'cause you love him."

"My gran," Marrec said, "would have said the same."

The boy nodded, satisfied, and followed his family out into Thornfell. Marrec watched until they were small, then turned and walked back through the folding light, and the Door sealed behind him with a sound like a book closing gently, on purpose, by someone who meant to reopen it someday.

* * *

The palace was very quiet.

Ossian Vael sat on his throne in the hall of salt glass — cracked now, dimmed, the panes holding only a faint amber memory of amplification — and he was thinner. Not in body; gods don't waste in body. In *presence*. The air around him had less weight than it used to. When he turned his head, the movement cost him something visible.

"You counted them out," his father said. "All of them."

"All but me."

"A sentimentality." But there was no edge on it. The edge required strength. "Come here, Marrec."

Marrec climbed the steps of the dais and knelt, as he had knelt for three hundred years, and found that the posture fit differently now. Looser. Like a coat he'd finally grown into, or out of.

"What happens now?" he asked.

"Now?" His father looked out over the empty hall, the empty country, the fields going untended beyond the windows, the sky inside the fold dimming by degrees as the last prayers drained out of it. "Now I get smaller. Now the echoes finish waking and find nothing to eat. Now the fold flattens, and I become what your friend the inquisitor always said I was: a story. A bad one." He paused. "You could still leave. The Warden's heir wrote the Door generous. It opens outward for the living, and technically, Marrec, you are the living. Barely. Technically."

"Do you want me to go?"

The silence lasted a long time. Long enough for Marrec to count his own heartbeats, the slow ones, the ones that had beaten maybe ten thousand times in three hundred years because his blood barely bothered circulating anymore.

"No," Ossian Vael said at last. "A king does not say such things. But no."

"Then I'm staying," Marrec said. "Not as your son. Not as your herald. As the last man in Zephyrine who remembers your face when it was kind. Someone has to. Otherwise the stories win, and the stories are worse than you were."

His father regarded him for a long moment, and then — the first and last time in three hundred years — Ossian Vael put his hand on his son's head, and it almost felt like flesh.

"Sentimentality," he said again, softer.

"Faith," Marrec corrected. "You never did learn the difference."

* * *

Outside, the last light of the folded country was failing, and somewhere beyond the salt, in the green valleys of Thornfell, nine thousand people were learning the names of new rivers. Marrec sat on the palace steps with his back to his fading father and watched the dim sky, and did the arithmetic he'd always been good at.

A generation, perhaps two. Then the fold flattened, and the story ended.

But tonight, in the refugee camps below Thornfell's walls, a woman was telling her grandson about the god-king's son who stayed behind out of love, and the boy was listening the way children listen — wholly, without mercy — and a story was being born that Marrec would never hear, and it was kinder than he deserved.

He found he didn't mind. Faith, it turned out, could survive its object. His always had.

He settled in to wait for the dark, and the dark came slowly, and it was not cruel, and it was not alone.
