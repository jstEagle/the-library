## 22. A Map With No Borders

The archive room they gave Ulyana had once been a cell.

He knew the dimensions by heart. He'd spent six years in one like it, back when he was property, copying tithes by tallow-light and tallying his own debt down to the copper. This room had the same proportions—eight feet by ten, one window, one desk—but the door had been removed entirely, and the desk was oak instead of salt-bleached pine, and the ink was good ink, and no one checked on him at night except to bring tea.

Vey had offered him a room in the reformed cathedral proper. He'd declined. He wanted the cell, or something shaped like it. He wanted to sit where he'd been kept and do the one thing no one had ever let him do as a scribe: draw.

The blood-salt original lay on the desk under a sheet of glass. His ancestor's testament beside it. He had spent four months with them, and four months was enough.

The new map took him eleven days.

He worked from memory and from the surveys the refugee Zephyrines had helped the Thornfell cartographers compile—valley by valley, the two nations' names set side by side where they overlapped. The Thornfellers called it the Greywash; the Zephyrines called it the Weeping Steppe, because the brine had run there for a century before the sealing. The Thornfellers called the high pass the Widow's Tooth; the Zephyrines had a name for it that meant *where the mothers wait*, and Ulyana wrote both, one above the other, neither struck through.

He drew no border.

That was the whole argument of the map, and he made it the way he'd been trained to make arguments in the scriptorium: by omission. Every other map in the archive drew the line somewhere—a river, a ridge, a treaty-mark in red. His map drew the roads instead, and the roads crossed everywhere, and where they crossed he wrote the names of the villages that now had two languages on their signs. There were more of them every month. There would be more still.

On the last day he sat looking at the finished sheet and realized his hands were shaking, and that it wasn't fear.

It was the absence of it.

* * *

He burned the original at dawn, on the flat rock behind Brannoc's hermitage, where the old priest still kept a fire going most mornings out of habit older than doctrine.

The blood-salt map did not burn the way paper burns. It burned the way salt burns—slowly, with a low white light, and a smell like the sea at the end of summer, and for a few minutes Ulyana could have sworn he heard singing, very far off, the way you hear a bell from a village you're leaving.

Brannoc stood beside him with his hands folded into his sleeves, watching.

"You don't have to," the old man said. "The archive would have kept it."

"The archive keeps everything. That's the problem." Ulyana watched the last of the border-lines go white, then grey, then nothing. "Three hundred years ago, my family drew a country into a box and called it mercy. I'm not going to spend the rest of my life being the box."

"And the blank page?"

He had it with him—a single sheet, cut to the same dimensions, and where Zephyrine had been there was nothing. Clean paper. He'd thought about writing something on it. *Here lived a people.* *Here the prayers were answered, and it cost too much.* But every sentence he tried was either an apology or an epitaph, and the Zephyrines were alive, and they were not asking for either.

"Blank," he said. "It stays blank. Anyone who wants to know what was there can ask someone who was there. That's what people are for."

Brannoc made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Your ancestor would have hated that."

"My ancestor," Ulyana said, "sealed his own children behind a door and told them it was for their protection. I'm not taking his advice."

The white light guttered. The singing stopped—or he stopped hearing it, which came to the same thing. Ash lifted on the morning wind and drifted east, toward the mountains, toward the thin place where the Door had been, where now there was only a seam of ordinary stone that the shepherds had already given a new, boring, wonderful name: *the Salt Scar*, and no one prayed at it, and nothing answered, and Ulyana found that he loved it for exactly that.

* * *

Sefa arrived at midday with bread and news, in that order.

"Thornhollow's voted," she said, tearing the loaf. "Refugees get plots in the lower fields. Full title after five years."

"That's fast."

"That's Brannoc's fault. He went down there and gave a sermon." She grinned. "It was forty minutes long and half of it was in Old Zephyrine and nobody understood a word, and they voted yes anyway. Something about the way he said *neighbor*."

Ulyana took his share of the bread. Six months ago Sefa had been a laundress in the lowtown slums whose hands healed when she prayed, and she had been hunted for it, and he had chosen—without admitting he was choosing—to warn her instead of selling her. Now her hands were calloused from farm work instead of lye water, and the healing had faded to almost nothing, and she said she didn't miss it. "Prayers used to go somewhere," she'd told him once. "Now they just go up. It's better. Up is where they belong."

"Vey's asking for you again," she said, around the bread. "The archive. She says the treaty codex is ready for the final scribe's mark."

"She has forty scribes."

"She has forty scribes and one man who wrote his blood into the original. She wants the continuity on record. Her word." Sefa made a face. "She's very proud of her word."

He would go. Not today. Today the light was good and the road was open and there was a thing he had been putting off for four months because he hadn't known how to say it, and he found, watching Sefa brush crumbs from her sleeves, that he knew how now.

"I'm leaving," he said. "For a while. Maybe for good."

She didn't look surprised. He suspected she'd known before he did.

"Where?"

"I don't know. That's rather the point." He laughed, and it came out strange, unpracticed. "I've been indentured since I was nine. Debt, then duty, then inheritance, then treaty. There's always been a ledger with my name in it. I burned the last page this morning. I'd like to find out what a day looks like when nothing in it is owed."

Sefa considered him for a long moment. Then she stood, brushed off her skirt, and said, "I'm coming."

"You have a farm."

"I have a farm and a sister who's dying to run it. Ulyana." She said his name the way she'd said it in the slums, that first night, when she'd shown him what the prayer token really was—not a compass to a god, just a compass. "You walked me out of a hunt. I'm walking you onto a road. That's not a debt. Don't you dare put it in a ledger."

* * *

Brannoc came too, which surprised everyone, including Brannoc.

"I'm old," he said, packing almost nothing, "and I have spent three hundred years—" he caught himself, "—a very long time being a man who knows things. I'd like to spend a season being a man who doesn't. You two can know things. I'll carry the bread."

They went in the last week of the thaw, when the mountain roads were mud and meltwater and the world smelled like it was starting over. They walked without a route. That was Ulyana's one rule for himself, and it was harder than any fast he'd ever kept: no map. The smuggler's paths he knew by heart he avoided on principle, and took the open roads instead, the slow ones, the ones with tolls and taverns and other people's carts.

It was on one of those roads, four days out, in the hill country above the Greywash, that the stranger found them.

* * *

She was young, maybe nineteen, mud to the knees, leading a mule that had gone lame an hour back. She saw them from a distance and Ulyana watched her do the arithmetic every traveler does—three strangers, empty road—and decide, with the particular recklessness of someone out of options, to flag them down anyway.

"Please," she said. "Is there a farrier in the next village? Or anyone who'd know about a thrown shoe? I've got goods for the market at Thornhollow and if I miss the market day I lose the stall, and if I lose the stall—"

She stopped herself. Ulyana recognized the gesture. It was the gesture of a person who had learned that finishing the sentence made people walk faster.

He knelt by the mule. The shoe was loose, not thrown; the near hind, nails worked free in the mud. He'd shod mules before, in his debt-running years, badly but adequately, and Brannoc had shod everything that walked during his century of exile, and between the three of them they had a hammer, four nails, and Sefa's unnervingly calm way with frightened animals.

It took half an hour. The girl talked the whole time—about the market, about her brother, about a prayer she'd made at a roadside stone two days ago that *you never know*, maybe it worked, because look, here were three people with a hammer. She said it lightly, the way people say things they half-mean, and Ulyana felt the old reflex rise in him—the scribe's reflex, the warden's reflex, the reflex of a man who had watched prayers get answered at the price of other people's years.

*Tell her. Tell her what answered prayers cost. Tell her to want less.*

He said nothing. He finished the last nail, patted the mule's neck, and stood.

"Farrier's in the village," he said. "Past the mill, left at the well. Tell him Ulyana's road-menders sent you and he'll only charge you half, because he owes me a drink he's never going to buy."

The girl laughed, thanked them twice, and led the mule on toward the village, and the market, and the stall she would not lose.

They stood in the road and watched her go.

"That was it," Sefa said quietly. "That was the whole miracle. Directions."

"Directions," Ulyana agreed.

Brannoc was looking at him with an expression the old man probably thought was neutral. "Three hundred years ago," he said, "a traveler on this road would have gotten a shrine. A token. A god in the stone, listening, and taking notes."

"And a bill," Ulyana said. "There was always a bill." He looked down the road, where the girl and her mule had become a small moving speck against the green. "She'll make the market. Her brother will be fine or he won't, and it won't be because anyone prayed correctly. It'll just be the world, being the world. I spent my whole life thinking that was the smaller thing."

He picked up his pack. The blank page was in it, wrapped in oilcloth, going wherever he went. He still didn't know what it was for. Maybe it wasn't for anything. Maybe that was what a page was, when you finally stopped being afraid of it: not a record, not a treaty, not a debt. Just room.

They walked on. The road bent north, toward country none of them had names for, and the afternoon came down gold and ordinary across the valleys, and somewhere behind them a village bell rang the hour with no god in it at all, and it was the most beautiful sound Ulyana Nystrom had ever heard.
