## 1. Charms for the Godless

The luck-knot was the color of wet sand and about as beautiful, which was exactly how Xanthe Sandoval liked to make them. Beauty invited questions. Ugliness invited only haggling.

"Three coppers," he said, "and I'll throw in a blessing."

"You're not a priest."

"I'm not. It's a discount blessing. Non-denominational. Works for any god you've got, or none at all — hence the price."

The dockworker, whose name was Brannig and whose hands were the size of dinner plates, laughed through his nose and counted out three coppers onto the counter of the little shop that smelled of dust and dried lavender and nothing else, certainly not magic, because there was no such thing as magic, and anyone who said otherwise could be hanged for it.

Brannig wound the knot around his wrist without ceremony. He'd bought one every spring for six years. His ship came home. His wife's cough cleared. His youngest son, born feet-first and blue as a herring, had grown into a boy who could outrun the harbor watch in bare feet. Whether any of this was the knot's doing, Brannig had never once asked, because asking would ruin everything, and Brannig was a man who understood that some bargains were best kept unexamined.

"Same time next spring, Sandoval?"

"Gods willing," Xanthe said. "Though they'd have to be willing quite specifically."

Brannig grinned, tapped two fingers to his brow in the loose salute sailors used for luck, and went out into the gray morning of Vellamare, where the gulls were already screaming over the fish market and the tide was dragging its slow silver tongue up the channel.

Xanthe watched him go. Then he turned the little sign on his door from OPEN to CLOSED, which took four seconds, and stood in the middle of his shop doing absolutely nothing, which took considerably longer.

This was the part nobody warned you about, hiding in plain sight. Everyone imagined the danger would come at knifepoint, or in the night, or from a witch-hunter's boot in the door. Nobody told you it would instead arrive as silence — the long, unremarkable, airless silence of a life lived entirely in the conditional. *If* anyone asked. *Should* it ever matter. *In case.* Twelve years of tucking himself smaller and smaller, like a letter folded too many times, until the creases were part of the paper.

He put the kettle on.

* * *

The shop was called Sandoval's Curiosities, which in Vellamare meant it sold charms to people who were certain charms didn't work. This was the great joke of Xanthe's existence, the one he could never tell anyone: half the city wore his work and called it superstition, the way you might wear a coat against a cold you refused to believe in.

There was the fever-knot Mrs. Pell hung above her granddaughter's bed and credited to Saint Osmine. There were the salt-charms sewn into fishing nets by men who spat twice and muttered that it couldn't hurt. There was old Widow Quennel, who came in every month for a "headache ribbon" and paid in honey-cakes, and who had once looked at Xanthe for slightly too long and said, "My grandmother knew a hedge-witch on the coast road. You have her hands." And Xanthe had said, "It's a common hands," and sold her the ribbon, and not slept properly for three nights afterward.

Because that was the other thing about hiding in plain sight. It worked right up until it didn't, and you never got to know which day was which.

He drank his tea standing at the window. Across the lane, Tamsin the baker's boy was hauling trays of morning rolls, and the smell drifted over, warm and ordinary, and Xanthe thought — as he did most mornings, in the small gray hour between opening the shutters and finding a reason to be busy — *twelve years.*

Twelve years since Zephyrine had stood in her doorway on the coast road, arms crossed, looking at him the way she looked at a brew that wasn't going to take.

"Vellamare," she'd said. "Of all the foolish—"

"It's big," he'd said. "Big enough."

"Big enough to drown in, more like."

"I'll write."

He hadn't written. Writing was a thread, and threads could be followed, and Zephyrine — practical, unsentimental Zephyrine, who buried her spellbooks in the garden and rotated them like root vegetables — would have been the first to say so. He'd loved her anyway. He'd loved her the way you love the person who taught your hands their first true thing: the way knotweed curls, the way salt answers water, the way a charm holds if — and only if — you mean it.

She'd let him go. She'd packed him bread and a jar of pickled ramsons and said one strange thing at the gate, which he had turned over perhaps ten thousand times since.

"Keep your gift small, lad. Small enough to hide under your tongue." Then, softer, almost to herself: "And if ever anything comes looking for me — anything at all — don't come after. Promise me the foolish thing, since you're going to do it regardless."

He'd promised. He'd meant it the way you mean things at nineteen, which is to say completely and for about a week.

That had been twelve years ago, and nothing had come looking, and every year the coast road grew longer in his memory, and every spring he made Brannig a new luck-knot and told himself that careful was the same as happy, if you squinted.

* * *

Midmorning brought customers.

First, a young woman wanting a "love token" for a soldier shipping out on the evening tide. Xanthe made her a plain braided cord — no craft in it at all, just patience — and charged her two coppers and did not tell her that love-magic was real but never worked the way anyone wanted, being essentially a very insistent form of weather. What the soldier needed was a safe-keeping knot, and what she needed was to believe she'd done something, and both of those Xanthe could honestly provide.

"He'll come back?" she asked.

"That depends on him," Xanthe said. "But he'll know someone's waiting, and knowing is half of most things."

She left lighter than she'd come in, which was the entire business model.

Then a fidgeting clerk who wanted something "for luck at cards" and was deeply offended when Xanthe declined to guarantee outcomes. "Then what am I paying for?"

"Hope with a string attached," Xanthe said. "Cheaper than the alternative."

The clerk bought it anyway. They always did. Vellamare was a godless city on paper — the temples had been converted to customs houses a generation back — but godless people are the most superstitious creatures alive, having emptied heaven out and then gone looking for somewhere to put all that needing.

At noon he ate bread and cheese on the back step among his pots of herbs, which any herbalist might keep, and which were exactly as innocent as they appeared — except the feverfew in the blue pot, which he'd sung to as a seedling, and which now bloomed out of season and cured headaches twice as fast as it should. He gave it a reproachful look.

"Small," he reminded it. "We agreed. Small enough to hide under the tongue."

The feverfew, being a plant, ignored him, and being a charmed plant, did it smugly.

* * *

The trouble with a double life is that it is still only one life, and loneliness does not care how many names you answer to.

Xanthe felt this most acutely in the afternoons, in the hours when the shop was empty and the light went slantwise through the jars of dried flowers and the whole place looked like what it pretended to be: the cluttered little kingdom of a harmless man. He would catch himself composing sentences to say to no one. *You should have seen the size of Brannig's hands.* Or: *Do you think it's going to rain, or just threaten?* Or the dangerous ones, the ones that started *I miss* and ended nowhere he could afford to go aloud.

There was no one to tell that he'd gotten better at knots. No one who would understand that a luck-knot made today was finer than one made five years ago, tighter-woven, truer, the way a song improves in the mouth of someone who has finally stopped performing it. Mastery kept secret is a kind of wealth no one can confiscate, he told himself, and also a kind of poverty no one can see.

Once — maybe eight years ago — he'd almost slipped. A girl named Merrit, an apprentice chandler, sharp-eyed and funnier than the whole harbor put together, had spent a season coming into the shop for no reason either of them named. One evening she'd picked up a half-finished knot off the counter and said, quietly, not joking at all: "This is real, isn't it."

And Xanthe had felt the whole folded-up letter of himself tremble on the edge of unfolding — and he had laughed, easy as breathing, and said, "Real enough that you're holding my stock. Put it down before you jostle the profit out of it."

She'd smiled. Something in her eyes had closed, gently, like a door. She stopped coming by in the autumn. He heard later she'd married a cooper in Saltmarket. He was glad for her, he told himself, and it was even mostly true, and the rest of it was the loneliness, which sat where it always sat, under the ribs, patient as a stone.

Small enough to hide under the tongue. Well. He'd swallowed larger.

* * *

Toward closing, the light changed.

It was nothing dramatic — Vellamare's weather rarely bothered with drama, preferring a long gray insistence — but Xanthe noticed, because noticing was half his trade. The gulls went quiet all at once. The wind swung round and came off the land instead of the sea, carrying coal smoke and horse and something underneath, something like a struck flint. The feverfew in the blue pot turned its leaves as if listening.

"Hush," Xanthe told it, without much hope. Plants were terrible gossips.

He was trimming the wick on the lamp when the shouting started — distant, rhythmic, the cadence of official voices rather than quarrels. A crier, probably. Vellamare had criers for everything: fish prices, tide tables, the Prince Regent's birthdays. He finished the wick. He straightened the counter. He performed, for no audience, the complete pantomime of a man who has heard nothing unusual.

Then he unlocked the door and stepped out into the lane like a fool, like a curious citizen, like exactly the sort of man who had survived twelve years precisely by staying behind doors.

Down by the Customs House square, a figure in the crown's colors stood on the steps with a scroll, and the crowd around him wore the particular stillness of people listening hard while pretending to merely stand there. The words came across the rooftops thin and broken, snatched by the wind:

"—by order of the Crown — registry of persons gifted — pardon for those who come forward —"

The wind shifted. Xanthe lost the rest.

He stood in his doorway with his hand on the frame, and found that his heart had begun to beat in a way he hadn't allowed it to in twelve years — quickly, loudly, like something waking up in a cellar and starting for the stairs.

Across the lane, Tamsin the baker's boy paused with his empty trays and caught Xanthe's eye, and grinned, and called something cheerful about the weather, and Xanthe heard himself answer in a normal voice about rain, and watched the boy laugh and go inside, and understood that he was still standing in the doorway, and that the doorway had suddenly become the most dangerous place in Vellamare.

*Registry of persons gifted.*

Twelve years, he thought. Twelve years of small. And the crown, with perfect timing, had decided to go looking for exactly what he was.

He closed the door. He turned the sign. He stood in the middle of his harmless little shop, in the smell of lavender and dust and definitely-nothing-else, and listened to his heart climbing the stairs.

Somewhere on the coast road, he thought, there was a cottage, and a woman with crossed arms, and a gate she'd once said a strange thing at.

He began, very calmly, to count what he would need to burn.
