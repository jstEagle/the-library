## 2. The Proclamation

The morning the criers came to Saltrow Lane, Xanthe Sandoval was doing what he did every morning: lying to a woman about thread.

"Lucky red," he said, holding up the spool so it caught the light from his shop window. "Dyed at midsummer, madam, when the luck runs thickest. Sailors swear by it."

The sailors swore by nothing of the kind. The dye had come off a barge from Corveth two months ago, ordinary as rainwater. But Mrs. Pellam bought three spools for her daughter's wedding hem, paid without haggling, and left the shop believing — just a little — that the world might be kind to her girl.

That was the trick of it, Xanthe had learned. People didn't want magic. They wanted the *feeling* of magic, which was cheaper, legal, and came in any color they liked. So he sold them knots and spools and little tin fish for luck, and if he sometimes whispered over the stock at closing time — not a charm, never a charm, just a habit, an old man's superstition inherited from a dead woman's teaching — well. The gods were deaf anyway. Everyone said so.

He was counting out change for the next customer when the bells started.

Not the harbor bells, which rang in fives for fog. Not the temple bell, which rang once at dawn and once at dusk like a bored employee clocking in. This was the citadel bell, and it rang fast and flat, over and over, the sound of official business, and every head on Saltrow Lane turned toward it the way wheat turns from the sun.

"Ah," said his customer, a rope-maker named Doss with forearms like mooring posts. "They're at it again."

"At what?" Xanthe asked, though his hands had already gone still on the counter. He'd learned long ago that his body knew things before his mind agreed to know them.

"The registry." Doss leaned on the counter, comfortable, a man settling in for gossip. "My wife heard it from her sister in Harborside. Criers all through the lower city this morning. Some new edict."

Through the window, Xanthe saw them coming up the lane: three figures in citadel gray, the front one carrying a scroll tube and a voice built for shipyards. A crier. Behind him, two soldiers with the bored, watchful posture of men who had done this in six other neighborhoods already that morning.

"By proclamation of His Majesty Aldric the Third, of that name, King of Vellamare and its waters—" The crier unrolled his scroll with a flourish that suggested he enjoyed the job far too much. "—all persons possessing the gift of sorcery, hedge-craft, charm-working, or any art forbidden under the Statute of Ashes are hereby called upon to present themselves for registration."

Xanthe kept his eyes on Doss. Kept his face pleasant. Kept breathing.

"Those who come forward within thirty days shall receive full pardon for past offenses," the crier boomed, "a crown stipend, and placement in lawful service. Those who conceal themselves—" here the crier paused, savoring it, "—shall be subject to seizure, forfeiture of goods, and such penalties as the Inquisition shall determine."

"Well," said Doss cheerfully. "That'll shake a few rats out."

"Mm," said Xanthe.

*Pardon,* the word sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Pardon implied forgiveness, and forgiveness implied a crime, and the crime was simply *being what you were* — something he hadn't chosen, couldn't unchoose, had spent twelve years folding up small enough to hide inside a shopkeeper's smile. They were offering to forgive him for existing, provided he signed his own name onto a list. And lists, Zephyrine had told him once, stirring some foul-smelling tea on her cottage hearth, lists are how hunts begin. First they ask where the mages are. Then they ask where the mages' *friends* are. Then they stop asking.

He was twelve years old when she told him that. He'd thought she was being dramatic. She'd been being accurate.

Outside, the crier moved along, and the soldiers with him, and Saltrow Lane exhaled and went back to its business. But Xanthe noticed — because noticing was the one gift he'd never managed to smother — that people walked home a little differently after. Quicker. Doors closed a half-beat sooner. Old Widow Marsh, who sold pies, looked up and down the street before going inside, and she wasn't even gifted; she was just old enough to remember the last time the citadel bell rang like that.

Doss paid for his rope-tar and left. Xanthe locked the shop door behind him, stood in the sudden quiet, and looked at his shelves.

Knots and spools and tin fish. Twelve years of harmless trinkets, each one a small lie he told the world about himself. *Just a shopkeeper. Just a man who sells luck he doesn't believe in.* He had gotten so good at the lie that some mornings he believed it, and those mornings were the closest thing to peace he'd found since the night he'd run from Graywater with blistered feet and a master's warning ringing in his ears.

Now the king wanted a list. And somewhere in the city, Xanthe knew, there would be people desperate enough to go on it — hungry people, frightened people, people who'd take a stipend and a pardon over another winter of hiding. And why shouldn't they? What loyalty did the hidden owe the hidden? He'd never organized anything. Never protected anyone. He'd spent twelve years being safe, and safety, he was beginning to understand, was just complicity wearing a comfortable coat.

He made himself tea he didn't drink and waited for the day to get worse.

It obliged.

* * *

The Ferrows lived two doors down, in the narrow blue house with the crooked shutters. Tam Ferrow was a net-mender with salt-cracked hands and a laugh that carried; his wife Lissa sold mussels off a cart; their daughter Pip was four years old and had been sick with fever for the better part of a week.

Xanthe had watched the fever from his window with his hands pressed flat against his thighs to keep them still. He knew what a child's fever could do. He knew the charm for it too — a simple thing, breath and salt-water and a word in the old tongue, the first working Zephyrine had ever taught him, back when learning it felt like being handed the sun. He could have crossed two doors' distance and ended it in a minute.

He had not crossed the distance. He had stood in his shop and told himself: *too risky, too visible, someone always watching, you'd ruin everything.* And then he'd told himself the Ferrows wouldn't want a witch's help anyway, wouldn't thank him, might even report him — and maybe that was true, but he suspected it was mostly a story he told so he could keep liking himself.

On the fourth evening of the fever, Lissa Ferrow had done what generations of frightened mothers had done before her. She'd taken a ribbon, tied three knots in it over the child's bed, and whispered the words her own grandmother used to whisper — words she probably didn't fully believe in, words she'd have sworn were just tradition, just comfort, nothing more.

It worked. Of course it worked. Half the old charms in Vellamare worked, which was precisely why they were illegal. By morning, Pip's fever broke, and Lissa wept with relief, and the blue house filled again with the sound of a four-year-old demanding breakfast.

And someone had seen. Someone always saw. A neighbor at the window. A cousin with a grudge. Perhaps someone who'd already decided, this very morning, that the crown's thirty days of pardon sounded safer than silence.

Xanthe heard the boots before he understood them — many boots, moving with purpose, stopping at the blue house. He was at his door before he'd decided to be, which was unlike him, which was the first crack in twelve years of careful habits.

They brought Tam Ferrow out first. Two witch-hunters in ash-gray cloaks, the sigil of the Inquisition stitched at the shoulder — a circle broken by a vertical line, the mark of the statute that had burned the last hunt's names into history. Tam wasn't fighting. That was the worst part. He was looking back at his own doorway with an expression Xanthe recognized from mirrors: the look of a man recalculating his entire life in a single breath.

"He didn't do it!" Lissa was screaming from the step, holding Pip against her hip. The girl looked pale but upright, fever-bright eyes wide at the crowd gathering. "It was me — I tied the knots, it's just an old custom, my gran taught me, it's *nothing*, it's—"

"Madam." The lead hunter's voice was almost gentle, which somehow made it obscene. "The working was performed over the child. The child is recovered. These facts will be weighed."

"She's *four*. She had a fever. I'm her mother—"

"And the law," the hunter said, "makes no exception for mothers."

They took Tam by the arms — not roughly, not kindly, with the terrible neutral efficiency of men doing paperwork with bodies — and walked him down Saltrow Lane while the whole street watched. Nobody shouted. Nobody stepped forward. Doss was there, and Widow Marsh, and the chandler's boys, and a dozen others who'd shared bread and borrowed tools with the Ferrows for years, and every one of them studied their shoes or their windows or the interesting sky.

Xanthe stood among them and did the same.

He told himself: *if I speak, I join him, and then Pip has no one, and Lissa has no one, and nothing changes except that two households break instead of one.* It was even true. It was the same arithmetic he'd been running for twelve years, the arithmetic that had let him sleep at night, and standing there in the crowd with Tam Ferrow's eyes passing over him — passing over, not pausing, because why would they pause, Xanthe Sandoval had never given anyone a reason to expect rescue — the arithmetic finally tasted like what it was.

Ashes. The statute had named itself honestly after all.

At the corner, Tam stumbled, and the hunters steadied him, and he turned his head once to look back at the blue house, at his wife and daughter, and Xanthe saw his mouth move. No sound reached the crowd. But Xanthe read lips the way he read weather, an old dockside skill, and the shape of the words was unmistakable.

*I'm sorry.*

Tam Ferrow was apologizing. For a fever breaking. For his daughter living. He was being dragged away and apologizing for the crime of it, and the street let him go, and the boots faded, and the blue door closed, and Saltrow Lane began — immediately, hungrily — to pretend none of it had happened.

* * *

That night Xanthe didn't light his lamp. He sat behind the dark glass of his shopfront with a cup of cold tea and watched the lane empty, and did the thing he did perhaps twice a year: took out the memory instead of locking it away.

Graywater. The cottage with the leaning chimney. Zephyrine in her chair by the hearth, small and sharp-eyed, her hands knotted with age and work. *They'll come again someday,* she'd said, apropos of nothing, stirring her terrible tea. *They always come again. Fear is a tide, boy, and tides don't negotiate.* And he'd laughed, because he was young, because she was always saying doomful things, because doom had never once arrived on schedule.

Then it had arrived early, one night in autumn, and she'd woken him with a hand over his mouth and shoved a bundle into his arms and said, in a voice he'd never heard her use — flat, fast, absolutely certain — *"South road. Don't pack. Don't write. Don't come back. Whatever you hear about me, whatever they say I confessed to, you keep walking."*

He'd kept walking. To Vellamare, where nobody knew his face. He'd built the shop, the lies, the small invisible life, brick by careful brick. And every year the bricks had gotten heavier, and every year he'd told himself the weight was the price of safety, and tonight, sitting in the dark while a child slept safely two doors down because of a knot her mother would hang for the rest of her life and never dare explain — tonight the weight finally had a name.

It wasn't safety. It was debt. Twelve years of it, accruing interest, and today the collectors had come to Saltrow Lane and taken a net-mender as the first payment.

*Thirty days,* the crier had said. Thirty days of pardon, and then the seizures would begin in earnest. They'd start with the desperate and the stupid, the ones who registered, and the ones who didn't would be rooted out neighborhood by neighborhood, informant by informant. And Xanthe's name — his real name, the one he'd buried — was already written somewhere in the Inquisition's files, because Zephyrine had been seized at last, he was sure of it now, sure in the way you're sure of weather. They would have asked her who she'd taught. She would have given them riddles. But riddles only delay; they don't erase. Somewhere in a gray file, a runaway apprentice existed as a question mark, and question marks were exactly the sort of thing an Inquisitor loved to resolve.

He got up, lit a single candle, and pulled his strongbox from beneath the floorboards. Inside: eleven years of savings, a forged bill of sale, and a lock of his sister's hair he hadn't looked at since the night he left. Enough to buy passage south. Enough to start again, somewhere the criers hadn't reached yet.

*Run,* Zephyrine had said. *Keep walking.*

He set the candle on the counter and began, methodically, to make a list of everything he'd need to burn.

He was halfway through the inventory — the herb drawer first, definitely, and the grimoire pages sewn into the seat cushion, and gods, the *knots*, half of them were genuinely charmed, he'd grown careless, he'd grown *comfortable* — when exhaustion took him where he sat, chin on chest, candle guttering.

And the dream came.

Not his dream. He knew that within a heartbeat, the way you know a stranger's footsteps on your stairs. He stood in a round stone room he had never seen, high up, wind worrying at a barred slit of window, and a woman sat against the wall with her wrists chained and her hair gone white as gull-feather. Her face was gaunt. Her eyes were not diminished at all.

She looked directly at him — at *him*, across whatever impossible distance this was — and smiled the way his master used to smile when he'd finally gotten a lesson right.

"There you are," she said, in a voice worn thin as paper. "Took you long enough, love. Listen to me now, and listen close—"

But the dream folded, the way dreams do, and the last thing that followed him down into sleep was not her voice but the mark on the wall above her head: a sigil burned into the stone, coiled and patient, like something waiting to be remembered.

Xanthe woke at dawn with the candle drowned in its own wax, the burning-list unfinished in his hand, and the absolute certainty that his past had not waited politely in Graywater for him to outrun it.

It had found him first.
