## 3. Twelve Years Gone

The fire took the luck-knots first.

Xanthe had expected them to go reluctantly — they were, after all, made for holding on — but they caught with a soft, almost grateful crackle, the knotted threads flaring green for a heartbeat before curling into ash. Twelve years of work, burning in a washbasin in a rented room that smelled of tallow and river damp.

"Sentimental," he told himself, feeding the flames a bundle of fever-charms. "That's what this is. Sentiment."

It was not sentiment. It was arithmetic.

The proclamation had gone up on the market posts two days ago, and already the criers had moved from *pardon for the registered* to *ruin for the concealed*, and already the tone of the city had changed in that particular way Xanthe knew from stories his mother told — the way a room changes when a stranger walks in and everyone decides, silently and all at once, who they will point at if asked. Vellamare was deciding. And Xanthe Sandoval, seller of trinkets, keeper of a very small and very illegal flame, had done the arithmetic and found that staying cost more than going.

He worked methodically, because method was the one inheritance from Zephyrine that could never hang him. Charms by category, weakest first. Love-tokens — he winced, apologized aloud to no one, and burned them. The weather-bells, which had never once worked and which he had sold honestly as *decorative*. The knot-work. The salt-charms for fishermen's boots. A jar of bottled courage he'd been meaning to dispose of for six years because courage, bottled, tended to curdle.

"You were a bad idea," he told the jar, and dropped it in.

The fire in the basin was not a proper fire. He'd coaxed it small and hungry, the way Zephyrine had taught him — *a flame that eats its own smoke, boy, or don't bother* — so that the room stayed dark and the chimney told no tales. It was the last magic he intended to do in Vellamare, and there was something almost tender in it, this little obedient fire cleaning up after him like a dog that knew it was being left behind.

Outside, the criers were still at it, their voices bouncing off the close-packed houses of the Tanner's Quarter. *By order of the Crown, all persons possessed of the unsanctioned gift shall present themselves for registry—*

Xanthe had heard the word *gift* so many times in the last two days that it had begun to sound like a threat. Which, he supposed, it was. A gift you could not refuse, from a crown you could not refuse, registered in a book that would outlive you.

He fed the last of the stock to the flames — a single knot he'd been keeping back, the one he'd made the morning after he arrived in Vellamare, twelve years and one week ago, a boy of fourteen with burned hands and no plan, tying his fear into string because it was the only thing he had to tie it into. He'd never sold it. He'd never been able to.

"Right," he said, and put it in the fire, and watched it go, and did not feel lighter, which was annoying, because the whole point of the exercise had been feeling lighter.

The basin of ash he carried down four flights and scattered in the rain barrel, where no one would ever look, because no one ever looked in rain barrels. It was one of the small mercies of city life. People would pour anything into a rain barrel — ash, blood, secrets — and the rain forgave it all by morning.

Back in his room, he packed. It did not take long. He had spent twelve years learning to own almost nothing: one good coat, one bad coat, a knife, a spoon he liked, three shirts, and a false bottom in his satchel that held a bundle of letters tied in oilcloth. The letters were the only thing in the room that mattered, and he had read them so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth.

*Dear Xanthe. The plums came in early. The cat is fat and stupid and I have taught it to hate the tax man. Write when you can, and mean it when you write.*

His sister, Wren. Every letter the same warm, bossy, unbothered voice, as if the world could not possibly touch a person who kept plums and a fat cat. He had never written back about anything that mattered. He had written about the weather. He had written about a fictional employer, a fictional landlady, a fictional life so aggressively dull that Wren had once replied, *You are the only man I know who can make living in a city sound like watching paint dry, and I love you for it, but also I worry.*

He tucked the letters against his ribs, where the satchel's strap would hold them, and blew out the candle.

The plan was simple, because plans made in fear should be simple. South road at first light, before the criers woke. Walk to Saltmere, three days if the weather was kind. Take ship from Saltmere — any ship, a fishing boat, a barge, he was not proud. Somewhere the registry was a rumor and not a drumbeat. Somewhere a man could be dull in peace.

He lay down on the narrow bed in his coat, because a man fleeing at dawn should sleep, and sleep, as everyone knows, is a thing you can simply decide to do.

* * *

The tower was not his.

He knew that immediately, the way you know a dream is not your own — by the architecture of it. Xanthe's dreams were small, damp, municipal. They featured missed ferries, unpaid rent, teeth. They did not feature towers.

This tower was old and round and its stones sweated cold, and he stood inside it the way you stand inside someone else's memory of a room: seeing it, but from slightly wrong angles, as though his eyes had been borrowed. There was a slit of window, and through it a gray light that might have been dawn or might have been the color the sky turned when it had simply given up. There was a stool. There was a straw pallet. There was a woman.

She was old. Not frail-old — old the way a ship's mast is old, still bearing weight, still doing its one job, but groaning about it. Her hair had gone white in patches, as if the color had fled in stages rather than all at once, and her hands, folded in her lap, were mapped with veins and old burns. She wore a plain gray shift, prisoner's cloth, and she sat very straight, which in a cell is either dignity or stubbornness, and in her case was clearly both.

And Xanthe knew her.

Not from the dream. From before. From a cottage with a crooked chimney and a garden that grew what it was told, from a voice that said *again, boy* and meant *I believe you can*. From twelve years ago and a road and a goodbye he had never quite managed to make into words.

"Zephyrine," he said, and the dream had the decency to give him a voice, thin and strange as it sounded in the tower's cold air.

She didn't look up. That was the worst of it, at first — she was looking at the wall, or through it, and her lips moved without sound, counting something, or praying, or both. He took a step toward her and the dream resisted, the way water resists, and he understood with a lurch that he was not *in* this room. He was *in her*. Seeing from behind her eyes or beside them, a passenger.

*Oh no,* he thought. *Oh no, no, no—*

Her lips moved again, and this time the sound came, small and cracked and unmistakably hers, the voice that had once made him scrub a floor with sand for a week because he'd enchanted the dirt out of it instead of out of the broom.

"Xanthe."

Just his name. His childhood name — not *Xan*, which he'd been in Vellamare for twelve years, not *master Sandoval*, which he'd invented for his own stall sign, but *Xanthe*, said the way she'd always said it, with the *th* soft, almost a lisp of it, a name made to be said kindly.

She said it again. And again. Not calling him. He realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the tower, that she wasn't calling anyone. She was *practicing*. Saying his name over and over to herself in a cell, the way you hold a coin in your mouth against thirst, the way you repeat a thing to keep it from being taken.

"They'll ask me names," she murmured, to the wall, to the gray light, to no one. "They always ask names. And I will give them riddles, and they will give me the rack or the question or whatever they're calling it this decade, and I will still give them riddles, because I am a stubborn old woman and stubbornness is the one charm they can't burn out of you." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "But a name, Xanthe. A name is a door. And I have left so many doors open behind me. Sloppy. Sloppy of me. I always told you—"

She stopped. Her head turned — slowly, painfully, the way old necks turn — and for one impossible moment her eyes found his, or seemed to, gray eyes gone bright in the cell's dimness, and the dream went taut as wire.

"Run," she said.

And this time she was not murmuring to a wall. This time the word had an edge on it, a blade's edge, and it was aimed — he felt it land, felt it in his chest like a thrown knife that chose, at the last moment, to be a word.

"Run, boy. Not tomorrow. Not when it's convenient. Not when you've finished packing, because you will pack too much, you always pack too much—" her voice cracked and steadied itself, mast in wind, "—*now*. Because they have my name and my name is a road, and every road I ever walked leads somewhere I loved, and I loved so *many* places, Xanthe, I was careless with my loving—"

The tower shuddered. Not the dream-shudder of waking, but something else, something that felt like the dream itself being *pulled*, like a fish on a line, like a page turning. The gray light guttered. The woman's face began to blur at the edges, and she leaned forward as far as her chains — and he saw them now, iron at her wrists, and saw, with a sickness that would outlast the dream, that they were not plain iron. There were marks on them. Sigils. Work he recognized, work he'd been taught the theory of and never hoped to see.

"Xanthe," she said, one last time, and the name was different now — not a coin held against thirst but a key, or a summons, or a hand reaching down a well. "You were the best thing I made. Better than the garden. Better than the knots. And I am *so sorry* for what I've—"

The dream tore.

* * *

He woke on the floor.

He did not remember falling off the bed, but the floor was where he was, on his side, one arm dead beneath him, the candle he'd blown out guttering again in its dish as though it had never gone out — and he looked at it for a long, stupid moment before admitting to himself that it had gone out, and that it was lit now, and that he had not lit it, and that this was a problem he would think about later, or possibly never, if he could arrange it.

Gray light at the window. Dawn. His dawn, his escape dawn, the dawn around which the entire simple plan had been built, and he had spent it on the floor of a rented room with a dead arm and a lit candle.

"Right," he said again, because apparently it was the only word he owned.

He got up. The arm woke with a swarm of pins and he shook it, grimacing, and in the window's gray reflection his own face looked back at him — thirty years old now, ordinary, forgettable by design, the face of a man who sells trinkets — and behind it, faint as a watermark, the tower. The stones. The slit of light.

It had not felt like a dream. That was the thing he kept circling and could not land. Dreams had that soft, forgiving blur, that sense of everything being slightly made of the dreamer. This had been *load-bearing*. The cold of the stones had been in his shoulders. The smell — straw, iron, and something faintly herbal, yarrow, she'd always kept yarrow — had been in his nose. And her voice had been in his ears with the grain and crackle of a real voice, a voice currently existing somewhere in the world, in a tower, saying his name.

*They'll ask me names.*

The proclamation. The registry. The criers. *Pardon for names, ruin for silence.* He had assumed the hunt was a net dragged through the city at random, catching fever-charmers and hedge-healers, the small fish, the careless. He had not — he realized this now with the particular horror of a man finding a hole in a boat he'd been standing in for days — he had not let himself ask what the hunt was actually *for*. Hunts this loud, this organized, this *funded*, wanted something. Crowns did not spend gold on small fish.

They wanted a name. And Zephyrine, stubborn old woman, mast of a woman, was in a tower giving them riddles, and riddles, as she had taught him herself, were only locks, and locks only held until someone brought the right key, and keys could be made from anything — from time, from pain, from patience.

And every road she'd ever walked led somewhere she loved.

The cottage. Graywater. Twelve years of his own footprints between here and there, and he had spent the night packing a bag and planning a road south, and the whole time the road that mattered had been lying behind him, north, quiet, patient as a trap.

He stood in the middle of his rented room with his packed satchel and his lit-by-no-one candle and did the arithmetic again, the real arithmetic this time, the kind that doesn't flatter you.

Option one: south, as planned. Saltmere, a ship, a dull life in a dull place, and the knowledge — carried like a stone in the chest — that somewhere behind him, men with marked iron were working a riddle loose, and that when it came loose, the name at the bottom of it would be his, and the door it opened would be Wren's kitchen, Wren's plums, Wren's fat stupid cat. Running would not save him. Running would just decide *where* they found the people who loved him.

Option two.

He didn't want to think about option two. Option two was north. Option two was the cottage and whatever the hunters had left of it, and a woman in a tower who had said *run* with a blade's edge and then said his name like a key in the same breath, which was exactly the sort of contradiction Zephyrine had spent twelve years teaching him to distrust.

*Run,* she'd said. And then: *Xanthe.* As if the word itself were the opposite of running. As if his name were a rope thrown down a well.

He pinched out the candle with his fingers — a habit, and also, he admitted, a small private comfort, the one tiny domestic magic that no inquisitor could hang a man for — and shouldered the satchel, letters against his ribs.

"South," he told the empty room, firmly, in the voice he used for customers who haggled. "I'm going south. That's decided. That's final."

The room, being empty, said nothing, which he chose to interpret as agreement.

He was three steps down the stair and four streets from the Tanner's Quarter, moving fast in the gray crowd of early carts and early fishermen, before he admitted to himself which way his feet had been pointing the entire time.

North. Toward Graywater. Toward the cottage with the crooked chimney and the garden that grew what it was told.

Toward whatever was left of the woman who was saying his name in a tower, over and over, like a coin held against thirst — or like a bell being rung.

And somewhere behind him, faint but unmistakable, he could have sworn he heard it still.
