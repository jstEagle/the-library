## 4. The Tower at Graywater

The north road out of Vellamare was, in Xanthe's long experience of bad ideas, the worst idea he had ever walked down.

He had done the arithmetic three times at the city gate and it came out wrong every time. A wanted runaway traveling toward the one place the witch-hunters would certainly be searching — this was not cunning. This was not even courage, properly speaking. This was a man walking into a burning house because he'd heard a voice inside it, and the voice happened to know his name.

"You could turn around," he said to himself, somewhere past the third milestone. "You could be a coward very efficiently. You've had twelve years of practice."

But his feet had apparently unionized sometime overnight and were no longer taking instructions from the management.

The road climbed away from the sea in long switchbacks, through olive terraces gone wild and villages that watched him pass with the flat, assessing stares of places that had already given up their names to the criers. In one of them, a woman was scrubbing a chalk circle off her own doorstep while a neighbor stood guard with a broom, both of them working fast, saying nothing. The registry had reached this far, then. The pardon for names, the ruin for silence. Xanthe kept walking. It was the thing he did best.

He had planned, insofar as planning was involved, to be someone on this road. A tinker, perhaps. Tinkers were allowed to look shabby and directionless; it was practically the job description. He had bought a roll of copper wire in the Tanner's Quarter before dawn and a battered kettle from a rag-and-bone man who had tried to charge him double for the dents. He carried the kettle hanging from his pack by its handle, where it swung and clanked with every step like an enthusiastic idiot companion.

"You," Xanthe told it around midday, "are going to get us killed."

The kettle declined to comment. He was beginning to notice a pattern in his conversational partners.

* * *

He camped that night in a shepherd's hut with no sheep, a roof that leaked with great precision directly onto his bedroll, and a view of the lowlands where Vellamare's lamps made a smear of gold against the dark, like something spilled that no one had bothered to wipe up.

He dreamed.

Not the tower dream — or not only. The dream began in the tower, gray stone, gray light, a woman's hands folded on a table worn smooth by other women's hands. Zephyrine. Older than he remembered her, thinner, her hair gone entirely white at the temples in a way that looked less like age and more like weather. She was speaking his childhood name — not *Xanthe*, which was a name he had invented for himself at fourteen along with a whole new person to hang it on, but the name she'd used when he was nine and had just set the kitchen fire alight by accident and she had laughed instead of scolding, laughed until she had to sit down, and said, *well, that's one way to toast bread* —

She said that name in the dream, and the dream tilted.

Suddenly he was not in the tower. He was standing in the cottage garden at Graywater, but the garden as it had been — rosemary hedges clipped into submission, the bean poles Zephyrine insisted were "decorative" though they grew actual beans, the crooked chimney breathing smoke into a blue morning. Everything sharp with the terrible clarity of memory. He could smell it. Thyme crushed underfoot. Woodsmoke and rain.

*Look where I taught you,* the Zephyrine in the tower said, from impossibly far away, her voice coming through the garden like light through water. *When you're ready. Not before. You'll know when —*

And then, because dreams are under no obligation to be kind, the garden caught fire.

Xanthe woke before dawn with the kettle clanking against his pack in the wind and his heart going like a rabbit's and the words still hanging in the hut's damp air: *look where I taught you.*

"I don't want to know when," he told the ceiling. The ceiling dripped on him thoughtfully. "I have never in my life wanted to know when. Knowing when is how people end up in towers."

He broke camp anyway. He was walking before the sun cleared the hills, and if he walked faster than a tinker strictly needed to, well — tinkers were eccentric. Everyone knew this.

* * *

Graywater announced itself the way it always had: first by sound, then by smell. The sound was the weir — a long low constant rushing, water combed through the stones of the old fish-locks, a noise so unbroken that the village seemed to exist inside it rather than beside it. The smell was the river itself, cold mud and clean stone and, underneath, faintly, the mineral tang of the springs that gave the place its name.

Twelve years, and the village had changed almost nothing, which was either comforting or sinister depending on your view of time. The same crooked sign over the ferryman's porch. The same dog — or the dog's grandson, presumably — asleep in the middle of the one street, radiating the confidence of an animal that has never once been wrong about anything.

What had changed was the silence.

Graywater had never been a chatty village. But there is a difference between the quiet of people minding their own business and the quiet of people minding each other's, and Xanthe felt the difference the moment he passed the first house. Curtains shifted. A man splitting kindling stopped splitting it and simply stood, axe in hand, watching Xanthe come up the road with his clanking kettle and his shabby coat and his face, which Xanthe suddenly feared had *Zephyrine's apprentice* written across it in letters visible from the weir.

*They know,* he thought. *Someone talked. Someone always talks.*

He made himself stop at the well, because a tinker would, and asked after work in the flat market cadence of the lowlands. An old woman drawing water looked him over with eyes like wet slate.

"No work here," she said. "No charms neither, if that's what's in the kettle."

"Just a kettle."

"Mm." She hauled the bucket up, hand over hand, unhurried. Then, without looking at him, in a voice pitched exactly for the space between the two of them and no wider: "You'll want to go up before dark. And you'll want not to be seen doing it." She set the full pail on the well's lip and straightened, groaning theatrically, loud enough for the street. "And take some bread, tinker, you look half-starved, all you traveling folk do."

She pushed a wrapped loaf into his hands. Her fingers pressed his once, briefly — and Xanthe felt it, the tiny static-shock flicker of a warding gesture, the kind hedge-wives did without thinking, grandmothers' magic, bread-and-salt magic, the kind that had never been worth a pyre and had survived every hunt precisely by being invisible.

"Thank you," he said, meaning six different things.

"Don't thank me," she said, loudly, for the street. "It's stale."

* * *

The path up to the cottage hadn't changed either. It wound through birch and then pine, threading between the trees the way paths do when they were made by feet rather than surveyors, and Xanthe's body remembered every turn of it before his mind caught up. Here was the stump where he'd sat at ten years old and been sick after his first lesson in grounding, Zephyrine holding his head and saying, cheerfully, *everyone vomits the first time they touch the current, it's the body filing a complaint.* Here was the fallen log he'd used as a desk for his letters. Here was the bend in the path where, at thirteen, he had first seen—

He stopped.

The bend opened onto the clearing, and the clearing opened onto the ruin, and Xanthe stood on the path for a long moment doing nothing at all, because there is a kind of sight the mind refuses on first presentation, like a bill too large to pay.

The cottage was sacked.

That was the only word. Sacked — a word from ballads, from histories, a word that belonged to castles and coastal towns and had no business being applied to a cottage with a crooked chimney. The door hung off one hinge. Every window was broken inward, not knocked but broken, methodically, the glass ground under heel afterward; he could see the little crushed constellations of it glittering on the floorboards from twenty paces off. The herb beds were torn up — not trampled, *excavated*, turned over spadeful by spadeful, the way you dig when you're looking for something buried or proving there's nothing buried. The bean poles lay stacked neatly against the wall by someone with a soldier's habit of order.

And the chimney. The crooked chimney, which had leaned fifteen years out of true and smoked sideways in any wind and which Zephyrine had refused on principle to fix, calling it — Xanthe heard her voice say it, heard it in his own skull in her exact intonation — *character* — the chimney had been pulled down onto the hearth.

Onto the hearthstone specifically.

Xanthe crossed the clearing without deciding to. His legs carried him the way they'd carried him north, mutinous, and he registered the details one at a time, the way you drink something scalding in sips. The door, when he touched it, fell inward with a sigh. Inside: wreckage, but selective wreckage. Nothing stolen — her copper pots were still there, her good quilt still on the bed frame, her books (harmless ones, the almanacs and the herbal) still shelved. They hadn't come for valuables. They had come for evidence, and where they found none they had made some. The table was split lengthwise. The floor near the hearth was scorched in a wide fan.

And the hearthstone itself — the great flat slab that had warmed the backs of two generations of apprentices — was split down the middle. Not cracked. Split, cleanly, with the particular violence of things done deliberately and reported upward afterward.

Zephyrine had kept everything important under that stone. Twelve years, Xanthe had assumed it was empty now, that whatever she'd hidden there she'd moved or burned before he ever fled south. But she had taught him to look there — *the hearth is the heart, boy, and hearts are where you keep things, everyone knows this, even kings, especially kings* — and she had taught it to him with the elaborate casualness she reserved for the most serious lessons, and last night a woman in a tower had said *look where I taught you*, and Xanthe's whole body had known, before his mind caught up, exactly what those words meant and where they pointed.

He knelt at the split stone.

The ash inside was deep. Someone had burned a fire in the ruined hearth after the chimney fell — a hot one, fed with something beyond firewood; he could see the curled, carbonized ghosts of paper among it, and one corner of vellum that had survived just enough to show three lines of Zephyrine's cramped, left-slanting hand before dissolving into brown lace. They had burned her papers. They had burned her books' missing sisters, her letters, her records, whatever a witch accumulates in thirty years of quiet practice, and then they had left, and the ash had sat in the split heart of the house through however many days of rain the sky had spent since.

Xanthe put his hands into the ash.

It was cold. That was the detail that undid him, more than anything else — the sheer, ordinary coldness of it. His master had been dragged out of this room — he could see where the table had been shoved aside, could reconstruct it, the boots, the noise, her voice, had her voice been raised? Had she fought? No. No, she wouldn't have. She'd have gone quietly, because going quietly was the whole craft of her life, the entire art — and all of it had cooled into this gray powder that got under his fingernails and smelled of nothing, absolutely nothing, not smoke anymore, not herbs, not her.

"Grief makes idiots of us," Zephyrine said in his memory, some rainy lesson, decades ago by feel. "Grief is the current with nowhere to go. Ground it, boy, or it grounds you."

Xanthe knelt in the wrecked house and cried like a nine-year-old who had set the kitchen on fire, and there was no one there to laugh and make him sit down.

* * *

It was full dark when he noticed the mark.

He had grounded himself eventually, in the manner she'd taught — palms flat on living wood, breath counted, the grief allowed to move *through* and not settle — sitting against the old pear tree she'd grafted herself, which had, absurdly, impossibly, survived the sack with its branches full of ripening fruit, as if the soldiers hadn't considered pears to be evidence. He had eaten two of those pears and buried the seeds, and that small act — burying seeds in torn earth, the oldest and least outlawed magic there is — was what finally stilled his hands.

Then he'd come back inside with a shutter-scrap for a shovel and moved the ash from the split hearthstone, because *look where I taught you* was a bell that would not stop ringing, and because hope, in Xanthe's experience, was best handled the way you handled anything else that might explode: at arm's length, with your eyes half shut.

Under the ash, in the hollow between the split halves of the stone, the floor was scored.

At first he took it for the work of the pry-bar they'd used on the stone — until he saw that the lines were too fine, too deliberate, and that they described a shape he knew. He fetched the stub of candle from his pack and held it low, and the light filled the scored lines with shadow, and the mark leapt out at him the way faces leap out of wallpaper.

A circle. Within it, interlocked, seven lines crossing at the center and passing through seven points on the rim — and at each point, a small glyph he could not read and yet *recognized*, the way you recognize a face in a crowd twenty years on. It was drawn not carved, he realized, touching it; the lines were fused into the stone itself, vitrified, smooth as glass under his fingertip. Burned in. From below. As if the stone had been merely the surface the mark happened to surface through, the way a bruise surfaces through skin.

He had seen it before. He had seen it last night, and the night before, and every night for three nights, rendered in the precise, patient strokes of a dream — because the woman in the tower drew it on air, over and over, with one finger, teaching it to him across whatever distance the dream had crossed, making sure he could not mistake it.

"It's yours," Xanthe said aloud to the empty house, and his voice cracked on the second word. "It was always yours. What did you do?"

The house, being a ruin, said nothing.

But the mark answered.

The moment his finger traced the final point of the seventh line — completing, without meaning to, without knowing he meant to, the circuit the dream had taught him — heat bloomed under the stone. Not fire-heat. The other kind, the current, the deep warm pulse he had felt once at ten years old and then spent twelve years pretending he had never felt at all, rising through the split heart of the house like sap through a cut stem. The ash in the hearth stirred, all of it, every grain, lifting in a slow spiral with no wind to lift it.

And in the center of the burned mark, something small and bright and patient, something that had waited under the stone through fire and pry-bars and twelve years of his absence, began — unmistakably, gladly, with what he could only describe as relief —

to rise.
