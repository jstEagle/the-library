## 5. What the Sigil Wants

Xanthe had always assumed that when his past finally caught up with him, it would arrive wearing a uniform. It would kick the door in. It would have forms to fill out.

Instead it arrived as ash.

He knelt in the ruins of Zephyrine's cottage with his sleeves rolled to the elbow — an old habit; you couldn't feel a charm's hum through wool — and sifted his fingers through the cold hearthstone rubble for the fourth time since dawn. The cottage had been sacked with the thoroughness of people who were paid by the item. Her herb bundles hung in sad gray tassels from the rafters. Her copper kettle lay in the garden bed as if it had tried to escape by growing legs and hadn't quite managed the second part.

But the sigil was not here. Of course it wasn't. Corvane's men had taken it, along with her grimoires, her crystals, her collection of scandalously labeled tea tins (one read *For Nerves*, another, ominously, *For Other People's Nerves*).

Except.

Xanthe sat back on his heels and stared at the mark burned into the split hearthstone. He'd taken it at first for the witch-hunters' brand, their seal of confiscation, but no hunter's sigil looked like this. This was a circle of interlocking hands, drawn in a single unbroken line, small enough to cover with his thumb. And he knew it. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

Zephyrine had taught him where she hid things on his very first day of apprenticeship, twelve years ago, when he'd been all elbows and terror and a name he couldn't say aloud in public anymore.

"Everything important," she'd said, tapping him on the sternum with one bony finger, "is hidden either inside something worthless or somewhere embarrassing. Never anywhere clever. Clever hiding places are for people who want to be found."

The hearthstone was neither worthless nor embarrassing. But the ash pit beneath it —

"Oh," Xanthe said aloud, to nobody. "Oh, you *wretched* woman."

*Somewhere embarrassing.* The night soil trench behind the cottage. Which he had dug for her every autumn for six years, and which she had always insisted he line with river clay "for drainage," and which he now understood had been lined for considerably more than drainage.

* * *

The trench was exactly as unpleasant as memory suggested, and Xanthe — a grown man, a survivor of Vellamare's dockside taverns, a person who had once eaten a pickled egg from a barrel of questionable provenance and lived — gagged twice before his fingers struck something that was neither clay nor worse than clay. A wax-sealed jar, wrapped in oilcloth, buried beneath a flat stone.

He washed it in the rain barrel until his hands stung, then sat on the overturned washtub in what remained of Zephyrine's garden and held the thing up to the gray morning light.

Inside the jar, floating in oil gone amber with age, was a disc of some material he could not name. Not metal. Not stone. Not horn or bone or shell. It was the color of storm-light over deep water, and it moved when he wasn't looking at it directly — not floating, precisely, but *settling*, the way a cat settles into a new lap: deliberately, with intent.

The sigil. Or a copy of it. A decoy? His heart did something complicated. If this was a decoy, then the real one truly was in a hunter's strongbox, and everything he'd walked three days through drizzle to do had been pointless. If it wasn't a decoy —

It turned over. By itself. To face him.

"Right," Xanthe said. "Absolutely not."

He set the jar down on the washtub and stood up and took four steps away from it, because he was a sensible man who had survived twelve years in Vellamare by knowing exactly what things wanted from him and never, ever letting them have it. The luck-knots wanted to be noticed. The fever-charms wanted to be thanked. And this thing, this impossible disc that should have been in a strongbox in Graywater Tower, wanted —

It didn't even wait for him to finish the thought. There was a sound like a struck bell played backward, the wax seal cracked like ice on a spring pond, and the sigil came through the glass without troubling to break it, which felt frankly rude, and pressed itself flat against his bare palm.

The world went white.

* * *

Later — hours later, though he had no honest way of knowing — Xanthe came back to himself lying in the garden mud with rain on his face and a scar on his palm shaped like interlocking hands, and the first thing he thought was: *I am standing in a kitchen I have never stood in.*

Which was a problem, because he was also lying in mud, and both things were true at once, and neither cancelled the other out.

In the memory-kitchen, bread was rising under a cloth. In the actual world, rain fell into his open mouth. In the memory-kitchen, a woman with silver-streaked hair and forearms like a dockworker's was humming — and Xanthe knew the tune, had *always* known the tune, except he'd only heard it yesterday from a fishwife on the road, and the fishwife had learned it from her grandmother, and Zephyrine had learned it from —

*Stop,* he told himself. *Stop, stop.*

She was younger in the memories. That was the first shock: not the content of them but the *texture*. He had known Zephyrine as old, dry, sharp as a paring knife, a woman who salted her tea and called thunderstorms "weather showing off." The woman in the sigil's inheritance was fifty and laughing at someone he couldn't see, flour on her nose, saying:

"— and I told him, Aldric, if the crown wants my charms registered, they may register each individual knot, and I will charge them a registration fee per knot, and we'll see whose ledger bleeds first—"

Aldric. The name arrived with its own freight: a husband. Broad hands. A laugh like a cart going over cobbles. Dead eleven years before her arrest, of a winter fever, and Xanthe grieved for him with an immediacy that made his throat close, because the sigil did not deliver grief secondhand. It delivered it *firsthand*. It was not a story about a man named Aldric. It was the specific weight of Aldric's head on her shoulder in front of a fire, the smell of pine resin and lamp oil in his hair, the exact way he said her name with the *ph* softened like a local, *Zeff-reen* —

"I never called you that," Xanthe said to the rain. "I called you Mistress Ish— I called you *Zephyrine*, formal as a tax notice, and you *hated* it, and I know now you hated it, and I'm sorry."

The sigil pulmed warmly against his palm, entirely unsympathetic, and gave him more.

* * *

Her arrest came last, and it came whole.

He saw it through her eyes, which was the worst part — not watching it happen but *being* the body it happened to. Boots in the garden at dusk. Six of them plus one man in gray who did not introduce himself because men like him believed introductions were a courtesy owed downward, and she was already below the courtesy line. Corvane. Even his thoughts smelled like ink and cold iron.

They had come for the sigil, and she had given them the cottage, the books, the teas, forty years of harmless trinkets, and watched them catalog her life into evidence sacks with the expression of a woman watching children rearrange her furniture.

"The sigil," Corvane had said. Not a question.

"It's a story," Zephyrine said. "Old witches tell it to frighten young ones. *Behave, or the sigil will find you.*"

"Where is it?"

"In the story, or in fact?"

"*Where.*"

And here Xanthe felt — actually felt, in his own chest, in his own ribs — what it was to be Zephyrine Sandoval-who-was-not-Ishikawa, ninety-one years old, standing in her ruined garden with her life in bags around her, deciding to be funny about it.

"In fact," she said pleasantly, "it is hidden somewhere embarrassing."

The blow, when it came, she took without staggering. Xanthe felt his own knees buckle in sympathy, mud against his cheek, rain in his ear, while the memory carried him through the rest: the wagon, the tower, the cell, and — threaded through all of it, quiet as a third heartbeat — her thinking. Not panic. *Planning.* She had known they would come for years. She had buried the sigil in filth because filth was the one place hunters' gloves wouldn't dig, and she had left the mark burned in the hearthstone because —

Because it wasn't a confession. It was a *signpost*. Only one person alive would recognize that mark and know what it meant and know where to look next, because only one person alive had spent six autumns digging that trench while she stood over him complaining about his technique.

*Run,* she had written in burning lines across the ash of her own hearth, in the language of the mark itself, which Xanthe now understood the way he understood his mother tongue, which was to say he could not remember learning it and could not imagine not knowing it.

*Run, and don't stop running until you understand what you're carrying.*

*Carrying?* he'd wanted to shout back across twelve years. *Carrying WHERE? Carrying WHAT? You've put it on my HAND, you catastrophic old woman, there's no un-carrying a thing that's ON MY HAND—*

But the memory had already moved on, and the sigil, having delivered its message, went still against his palm. Still, and warm, and faintly pleased with itself, like a cat that has deposited a dead bird on the doorstep and considers the matter closed.

* * *

Xanthe lay in the garden until the rain stopped, mostly because moving required deciding which direction to move in, and every direction led through what he now knew.

That was the cruelty of it, he realized. He had braced himself for the arrest — he'd seen proclamations before, seen neighbors vanish before. What he had not braced for was the ordinary stuff. Forty years of mornings. The way she'd talked to her hens ("they're better company than most aldermen and half as corrupt"). Aldric teaching himself to bake bread badly for eleven years and getting it right the winter he died, so that her last taste of it was perfect. The little economy of her love — never said, only *done*: his boots resoled, his name kept, his trench dug beside hers every autumn after he was gone, alone, so the hiding place would stay sound for the apprentice she was not allowed to write to.

"You could have just sent a letter," Xanthe said hoarsely. "One letter. 'Dear Xanthe, flee, also there's a haunted heirloom in the latrine.'"

The sigil warmed reproachfully, and he got — not words, exactly, but the shape of her reasoning, the way you catch the shape of a thought from a friend's raised eyebrow: *Letters are read. Trenches are not. And you would have burned it, you dreadful boy. You'd have burned it within a day of knowing what it was, and then forty lifetimes would be gone, and I would rather hang twice than let that happen.*

"Well," Xanthe admitted to the clouds, "you're not wrong. I did consider throwing it in the sea just now. Briefly."

It pulsed again, smug.

And there it was — the terrible arithmetic she'd handed him without asking permission. The sigil was not a trinket. It was not a tool. It was a grave with forty people in it, still talking. Every mage who had borne it had poured themselves into it at the end — he could feel them now, stacked like rings in a tree, the oldest ones so deep and strange that touching their edges was like pressing a hand against ice-floe water — and Zephyrine had spent her last free years steering this mass of the dead toward the one living person she trusted to hold it.

Him. Who could not hold a luck-knot stall together without inventing three different cousins to explain his absences.

He sat up. His palm throbbed in time with his pulse, and when he looked at it, the mark had sunk below the skin, visible only when he tilted his hand against the light, like a watermark.

*Things I know now that I did not know yesterday,* he thought, and began the list grimly, because lists were how he stayed a person: *One. The bread song. Two. How to cure a cough with pine and honey, which I already knew, except now I know it in a dialect that's been dead eighty years. Three. What it feels like to bury a husband. Four. That Zephyrine's name was not originally Zephyrine. Five. That she chose me. Six. That being chosen by Zephyrine is not the same as being safe.*

Seven. Somewhere between items six and seven, the dread shifted key. Because underneath the terror — underneath the grief for Aldric and the ache of her bruised ribs and the horror of the cell door closing — there was something else, and it took him a long moment to name it, and when he named it he was almost ashamed of it.

Wonder.

Forty lifetimes. Forty people's worth of songs and spells and stubbornness, and it was in his hand, and some vast drowned part of him wanted to dive into it and keep diving.

"That," he told the sigil severely, as he gathered his pack and pulled his hood up against the returning drizzle, "is exactly how it gets you. That is the first thing they teach you and the first thing you forget. Wanting more is the trap."

The sigil said nothing, but it settled deeper under his skin, comfortable as an old coin in a pocket, and Xanthe suspected — with the particular clarity of a man who has just been warned and is already ignoring it — that it had heard that speech before. From her. Word for word.

He looked once more at the ruined cottage, at the hearthstone with its signpost of hands, and bowed to it, low, the way he'd never once dared bow in life.

Then he ran.

He made two miles before the feeling caught up with him — not the fear, he'd packed that already, worn soft from use. It was the other thing. The certainty, arriving cold between his shoulder blades like a hand, that Zephyrine had not merely chosen a vessel.

She had chosen a messenger. And messengers, in her stories, always had someone behind them.

He did not look back. But the sigil flared once against his palm, hot as a fresh coal, pointing south — and somewhere behind him, at the edge of hearing, a horse was coming up the Graywater road.
