## 6. Inquisitor Corvane

The cells beneath Graywater Keep were carved for better purposes than the ones they now served. Corvane had made a study of the place during his first week — as he made a study of everything — and had concluded from the mason's marks that the lowest corridor had once been a wine cellar belonging to an abbey whose monks had believed, with the gentle confidence of men who never expected to be proven wrong, that no one would ever need chains in a house of God.

The crown had inherited the abbey, then the chains. Corvane had inherited both, plus a ledger, four assistants, a warrant sealed in green wax, and a prisoner who would not stop smiling at him.

"You're doing it again," Zephyrine said.

He looked up from his notes. She sat on the plank bed with her back against the wall and her cuffed hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for tea, silver-haired, small-boned, her wrists ringed with iron that should have made magic impossible and — by every test he ran each morning — did. She was sixties, perhaps seventy. Her eyes were brown and quick and terribly, unbearably kind.

"Doing what?" he asked.

"Writing my name at the top of the page before you've asked me anything. You do it every time. *Zephyrine of the Ashwood.* As though writing it often enough will make me confess to being her."

"You are her."

"I'm someone your clerks decided to call that." She shrugged. "Names are coats, Inquisitor. I've worn several."

"Then tell me which one fits."

"The gray one, this morning. It itches."

Corvane set down his pen with great care. He had learned, over nineteen years of this work, that patience was not merely a virtue but an instrument, and that a man who lost his temper in an interrogation cell had effectively handed the prisoner the keys. He recited this to himself now, silently, twice, because Zephyrine of the Ashwood had a talent for loosening screws in him that he had not known existed.

"You burned a warding circle into your own hearthstone," he said. "You kept forty-one prohibited texts in a false floor beneath your loom. You taught. We found the wax tablets — children's handwriting, copying sigils."

"Letters," she agreed. "Lovely letters. The little Ferane girl has beautiful loops. Terrible spelling."

"They weren't learning their letters. They were learning charms."

"Well." Zephyrine tilted her head, considering this the way another woman might consider a menu. "I did teach them that *b* comes after *a*, so I can't claim I taught them nothing useful."

He wrote: *Deflects with humor. Consistently. Possible genuine indifference to outcome — or long preparation for capture.* His hand paused on the last word. He crossed out *capture* and wrote *arrest*, which was the correct term, and then noticed that he had crossed out the correct term in favor of the euphemism, and disliked himself briefly, efficiently, and moved on. Nineteen years of practice.

"The sigil," he said. "Where is it?"

And there it was again — the smile. Not smug; that was what unsettled him. He knew smug. Smug he could work with, could pry open, could peel like fruit skin. This was something warmer. This was the smile of a grandmother watching a grandchild lose at cards while holding a winning hand herself.

"It's where I put it," she said.

"Which is?"

"With the person who needs it."

"That is not an answer, madam. That is a riddle."

"Everything true is a riddle the first time you hear it," said Zephyrine. "The second time it's a memory. The third time it's just weather."

He wrote that down too, before he could stop himself. Then he underlined it. Then, irritated at his own hand, he drew a line through the underline, leaving a strange hybrid mark that meant nothing, and turned the page.

* * *

Inquisitor Aldous Corvane was, by the admission of everyone who worked under him and several who worked above him, the most methodical witch-hunter the Office of Purity had produced in a generation. He did not believe in intuition. He did not believe in hunches, omens, dreams, or the efficacy of prayer — though he attended chapel quarterly for form's sake and gave the chaplain no cause for complaint. He believed in ledgers. He believed that the world, properly recorded, explained itself, and that evil — by which he meant magic, by which the crown meant magic, the two words having been legally synonymous since before his grandfather's birth — left tracks like anything else that walked.

His private catalog occupied eleven volumes and was written in his own shorthand, which no one else living could read. It contained every gifted soul the Office had ever registered, suspected, arrested, pardoned, executed, or merely heard rumors of, cross-referenced three ways. Other men collected sins; Corvane collected facts about sinners, and he slept very well.

That night, after the day's session ended, he sat in his office above the armory with a single candle and volume nine, and read the entry he had been avoiding since the arrest.

**ASHWOOD COTTAGE — associated persons.**

There were only two names. The first was Zephyrine, subject of record, taken four days ago without resistance — which troubled him, though he had filed the trouble rather than examine it. Witches fought. Even the old ones, even the tired ones. They threw light, they threw fire, they threw themselves down stairs. Zephyrine had opened the door before the knock finished, looked at his warrant, and said, "Ah. Sooner than I expected. Come in, then, you'll catch your death on the step." His men had searched the cottage twice afterward for hidden apprentices, escape tunnels, bound guardians. They found a cat, who had scratched Sergeant Vell, and a loom, and a false floor.

The second name had been entered twelve years ago, in ink that had faded to the color of old tea.

**SAN DOVAL, XANTHE. Apprentice, age est. 9–11 at time of flight. Mother deceased (fever); sister unaccounted. Fled Ashwood the night of the Hallowmas raid. No sighting since. Status: OPEN.**

Twelve years open. Corvane had closed files on men who had been swallowed by the sea. He had closed files on women walled into convents, on children given new names by well-meaning aunts in provinces so remote the maps apologized for them. Files stayed open because someone, somewhere, might still surface. Twelve years was longer than most griefs.

But the entry bothered him for a different reason, and tonight, finally, he let himself look at it directly.

It was the date. He had fled — the boy had fled — the night of the Hallowmas raid. Which meant the boy had run *before* anything happened. Before the raid reached the cottage. Before Zephyrine was questioned, before the roadblocks went up, before there was any reason in the world for an apprentice to be afraid. Corvane had noted this discrepancy twelve years ago and resolved it with the obvious explanation: the boy had been warned. Some hedge-cunning, some scrying trick, some whisper down the mages' invisible post. Guilty knowledge, fleeing ahead of justice. He had filed it under *Confirmed: foreknowledge = guilt* and thought no more of it.

Except that Zephyrine, in four days of questioning, had told him forty-one times — he had counted, because counting was soothing — that she had no apprentice. Had never had an apprentice. "Apprentices are for mages," she'd said this morning, pleasantly. "I keep children the way villages keep goats. They eat things, they wander off, and mostly what they produce is noise."

She was lying, obviously. The wax tablets had a dozen hands on them, and among them, in the corner of the oldest, a child's drawing of a cat with too many legs, labeled — phonetically, badly — **XAN**.

So she lied to protect him. That was natural; that was even forgivable; mothers lied, masters lied. What Corvane could not make sit flat in his ledger was simpler and smaller and had been rattling around his skull all evening like a coin in a jar:

*If she wanted to protect the boy, why did she leave evidence?*

A woman clever enough to hide forty-one books under a floor for twelve years does not accidentally keep a tablet with a name on it. A woman who says *run* to no one, who opens her door to her hunters with her hands empty and her eyes knowing — that woman leaves nothing behind she doesn't choose to leave.

Which meant either the boy's file was wrong —

— and Corvane's files were never wrong; this was not pride, it was bookkeeping —

— or Zephyrine of the Ashwood wanted him to look for Xanthe Sandoval. Wanted it badly enough to hang a name in her own cottage like a lantern over a door.

He sat back. The candle guttered and steadied.

"Why," he asked the empty office, in the tone he reserved for prisoners who were also, inconveniently, telling the truth, "would you want me to find him?"

The office declined to speculate. Offices generally did.

* * *

The next morning's session began the way all its predecessors had: he brought the tray, she commented on the tea, and he asked his questions in the order the manual prescribed, because the manual was written by men who had never met anyone like Zephyrine and he followed it anyway, out of spite toward her if nobody else.

"Name," he said.

"A good question. I'll let you know when I settle on one."

"Place of residence."

"A cottage. You've seen it. You knocked the mantel down carrying it out, which I'll thank you to pay for."

"Your profession."

"Widow. Weaver. Occasional midwife. Once, memorably, a pie judge at the Harvestwake fair, though that was a dark chapter for everyone involved."

"Madam." He laid his hands flat on the table between them, a gesture of openness the manual recommended and which had never once worked. "Four days ago you told me the sigil was with the person who needed it. Yesterday you told my sergeant the same thing when he asked about your supper preferences. I have begun to suspect you would say it if I asked you the time."

"I might," Zephyrine agreed. "Time's short enough that it seems worth saying."

"The sigil is a relic of the Purge. It is crown property. Its recovery is a matter of state." He heard his own voice go dry and official and hated it a little. "Every mage who bore it is dead. Whatever it contains belongs in the vaults at Vellamare."

"Does it," said Zephyrine.

It was not a question. That was the trouble with her — half her sentences wore questions' clothes and none of them behaved like questions. He waited. Waiting was an instrument. He was very good at waiting.

She leaned forward as far as the chains allowed, and for a moment the humor drained out of her face entirely, and what was underneath was so old and so calm that Corvane felt, absurdly, like the child in the room.

"Inquisitor," she said, "what do your books say happened at Karst Field?"

He blinked. "The battle. Three hundred years ago. The last stand of the mage-lords. It's in every schoolbook."

"Mm. And how many died?"

"The histories say the rebel host was destroyed. Some thousands. The numbers aren't —"

"Trust me," said Zephyrine gently. "They aren't."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them a gull complained — Graywater was close enough to the sea that the cells smelled of salt at high tide, which Corvane privately considered a design flaw in his suffering.

"What's in the sigil," she said, "isn't power. Power is cheap. Any hedge-witch with a copper kettle and a grudge can raise power. What's in the sigil is *everyone*. Everyone they killed that day, and everyone after, and every one of us since who carried it and added our little handful to the pile. It isn't a weapon, Aldous." She used his Christian name without asking permission, the way she had since the first hour, and he had stopped correcting her because correction implied she cared. "It's a grave that walks. And graves," she added, settling back against the wall, "should never belong to any one person. That's how you get hauntings."

"The crown believes —"

"The crown," said Zephyrine, "believes whatever fits in a strongbox. That's what crowns are *for*."

He gathered his papers. He stood. At the door — and he would examine this later, the way he examined all anomalies, though he already suspected this one would refuse classification — he paused.

"You said sooner than expected," he said. "The morning we took you. You said we came sooner than you expected."

Her eyebrows rose. For the first time in five days, she looked genuinely surprised, and Corvane understood with a cold, sliding feeling that he had just witnessed something rare: Zephyrine of the Ashwood, caught off guard.

"Did I say that," she murmured.

"You did. What were you expecting?"

She smiled again — the warm one, the terrible one — and turned her face toward the tiny barred window and the gray light coming through it.

"Someone," she said, "coming to ask me exactly that question."

* * *

He worked until the candles were pools, and then he did the thing he had not done in nineteen years of service.

He broke protocol.

Not loudly. Corvane did nothing loudly. He simply took down volume nine, turned to the Ashwood entry, and beneath the faded line — *SAN DOVAL, XANTHE... Status: OPEN* — he added a new line in his careful shorthand:

*Suspicion, unfiled elsewhere: subject did not flee FROM danger. Subject may have been sent FROM safety. Query: by whom, and toward what. Reassign priority: HIGH.*

He looked at the words for a long moment. Then, because he was honest with his ledgers if nothing else, he added one more line, smaller, almost apologetic in its neatness:

*Query the second: if the old woman sent her boy away carrying what I think she sent him carrying — then I am not hunting a fugitive.*

*I am hunting the heir to everything my office buried.*

Down in the cells, faint through three floors of stone, he could have sworn he heard an old woman laughing.

He snuffed the candle, and in the dark he began, quietly, to plan a journey south — toward Vellamare, toward the docks, toward twelve years of a life spent invisible.

Somewhere in that city, a boy who had once drawn a cat with too many legs was keeping a secret.

Corvane intended to introduce himself.
