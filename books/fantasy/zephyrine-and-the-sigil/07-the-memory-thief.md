## 7. The Memory-Thief

The road south of Graywater was the kind of road that made you talk to yourself.

Xanthe had resisted for two days. On the third, somewhere between a leaning mile-stone and a crooked oak that looked like it was eavesdropping, he gave in.

"She said *run*," he reminded the oak. "She didn't say run where."

The oak offered no opinion. The road did, though — it forked, and one branch bent south toward the coast and Vellamare and everything he'd fled, and the other bent southeast toward hills he only knew from Zephyrine's stories: the Lantern Fells, where hedge-witches had once kept midsummer fires going on the peaks like a promise that nobody up there was hiding anything.

He took the fells road. Then, because the sigil under his palm-cloth bandage gave a slow, satisfied pulse — like a cat settling into a lap it had chosen — he immediately regretted it.

"Don't agree with me," he muttered. "One of us should be nervous."

The trouble was that the sigil never got nervous. It had opinions the way a river has currents: constant, patient, and entirely uninterested in what Xanthe wanted. Since Graywater it had been pushing images at him in the quiet moments — not the full flood of that first terrible minute in the ruined cottage, but glimpses. A green door. A kettle with a dented spout. Zephyrine's hands, younger than he'd ever known them, folding a charm of river-clay and speaking words he almost understood.

He didn't know if the sigil was showing him memories or making requests. That was the worst part. It felt less like carrying a treasure and more like carrying a passenger who kept pointing at scenery.

"Green door," he said to the road. "Fine. I'll keep an eye out for green doors. Happy?"

It pulsed once, warm as a coal through the linen wrapping, and Xanthe sighed and walked on, and if anyone had seen him they would have thought exactly what he'd spent twelve years training people not to think: *there goes someone talking to something.*

* * *

He made camp that night in the hollow beneath the crooked oak, mostly because the oak had a face-like knot of bark that seemed disapproving enough to discourage visitors, and partly because his feet had unionized and gone on strike.

He built no fire. Twelve years of habits don't burn off in a week. He ate cold oat-bread and a hard-boiled egg he'd traded a luck-knot for at the last village — the last luck-knot he'd ever make, maybe, or maybe not; the thought of never again tying the seventh knot with his thumb pressed just so made him lonelier than the dark did — and he chewed slowly and listened to the night.

That was when he noticed the taste.

Not in his mouth. Somewhere behind his sternum, where the sigil sat. It was doing something new: a faint, rapid fluttering, like a moth testing a window. Warning-flutter. He'd learned its rhythms by now, three days of involuntary intimacy — the contented pulse, the insistent tug, the homesick ache it gave off whenever they passed a standing stone. This flutter was none of those.

This flutter meant *something is watching.*

Xanthe went very still. He finished his egg. He brushed the crumbs from his knees with exaggerated care, the way a man does who wants the darkness to believe he's an ordinary traveler getting ready to sleep, and then he lay back against the roots with his hands folded on his chest, right hand over the wrapped sigil, breathing slow.

The night breathed back.

There — footsteps, or the memory of footsteps. Not approaching. Circling. Something moved around the edge of his little hollow with the unhurried confidence of an owner inspecting a fence, and Xanthe's heart began doing arithmetic it didn't like.

Then a voice, from directly above him, which was impossible because there had been nobody above him a moment ago:

"You wrap it like it's a wound." A woman's voice. Low, amused, and tasting every word. "Which I suppose is honest of you. Most people would call it a gift."

Xanthe rolled sideways and came up in a crouch, and there she was, sitting on the root beside his bedroll as though she'd been invited. She was small and sharp-faced, somewhere between thirty and ageless, dressed in traveling gray so unremarkable that his eyes kept sliding off her the way they slid off a good concealment charm. Her hair was bound back with what looked suspiciously like a luck-knot done wrong on purpose, seven knots tied backwards, which any hedge-apprentice could tell you wasn't a luck charm at all.

It was an un-luck charm. A bad-luck magnet. Somebody else's misfortune, knotted and worn.

"Oh, don't get up," she said pleasantly.

"Who are you?" His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was a small mercy. His hand was already working at the wrapping of the sigil without his permission — the sigil wanted *out*, the traitor, it was practically wagging.

"Odile." She said it the way other people said their own name when they hadn't used it in so long it had gone stale. "And you're the apprentice. The famous one, if you can be famous to about six people, all of whom want you dead or opened up. Xanthe Sandoval. Or—" her head tilted, birdlike, and she smiled, and her smile had too many corners in it, "—shall I tell you the name you were called before Vellamare? I can taste it. It's still in you. Barely."

The bottom dropped out of his stomach with a soft, sickening lurch.

"Stay out of my head."

"Not in your head." She tapped her own temple, then her tongue, then the air between them, three quick gestures like a woman seasoning a stew. "Tongue. Nose. Whatever this sense is. You reek of it, boy. You walk down a road and the whole countryside knows magic passed by — well. The countryside that has my kind of nose. Which is currently," she added, with the mild satisfaction of someone reporting good weather, "one person. Me."

"You're a witch-hunter's hound, then." He backed up a step, putting the oak's trunk at his shoulder blade. "Corvane's."

Something crossed her face — fast, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "I am nobody's hound," she said quietly, and for just a moment the amusement drained out of her voice and left behind something old and tired and hungry, and Xanthe understood, in the way you understand a cliff-edge is a cliff-edge, that whatever Odile was, *hired* was the least of it.

Then the amusement came back, snapping into place like a shutter. "Though I won't pretend the Inquisitor and I aren't reading the same book. We simply disagree about the ending. He wants to burn the pages. I want to eat them."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be." She stood, unhurried, dusting off her gray skirts. Up close, unremarkable became a kind of artistry: nothing about her was memorable except everything, the way a room feels different after someone's left it. "Let's not dance. I've watched you for two days. You sleep badly, you talk to trees, you make charms out of habit and then apologize to them. And the sigil—" her eyes fixed on his wrapped palm with an intensity that made the skin underneath prickle, "—the sigil is getting louder every hour. Am I right? Little tastes. Little leaks. Dreams that belong to a dead woman."

Xanthe said nothing, which was, of course, an answer, and her smile widened by one more corner.

"I thought so. Here's what will happen, apprentice, and I'll say it kindly because I have a fondness for kindness, it being rare." She stepped closer, and the smell of her reached him — cold iron and dried violets, a smell that made the sigil shiver in a way he couldn't tell was fear or longing. "That mark is ripening in you. Every day it pours a little more of Zephyrine into your skull, and every day you pour a little of yourself out to make room. I've seen it before. I've *been* it." For half a breath her composure slipped again and he saw down into her, down and down, into something vast and crowded and howling softly. "In a month, maybe two, there'll be so little of Xanthe Sandoval left that taking it from you will be a mercy. So I'm going to let you walk, tonight. Carry it. Feed it. Let it grow sweet."

"And then?"

"And then," Odile said, "when it's ripe, I'll take it. From you, or from whatever's wearing your face by then. Whichever complains less."

* * *

He didn't remember deciding to run. His legs decided for him, and by the time his mind caught up he was a hundred yards down the moonlit road with his lungs full of knives and the sigil hammering against his palm like a second heartbeat, and there was no sound behind him at all, which was worse than pursuit.

Because he'd seen her move. Sitting on the root, standing up, stepping close — she'd moved like poured water, like something the world politely rearranged itself around. If she'd wanted to catch him tonight, catching him would have taken no more effort than closing a hand.

She was letting him go. She'd told him so to his face, cheerfully, the way a farmer tells the calf.

Xanthe ran until the road bent and the oak vanished behind him and the fells rose black against the stars, and then he stopped, hands on knees, and laughed — one ragged, horrible bark of a laugh, because some part of his brain had spent the entire chase noticing something absurd and had waited, patiently, for the rest of him to shut up.

She'd complimented his knots. The bad-luck charm in her hair was a luck-knot tied backwards, and she'd worn it *knowing he'd recognize the craft*, the way a pickpocket wears stolen rings to a party. She hadn't just ambushed him. She'd introduced herself.

*She'll take it when it's ripe.* But she also could have taken it tonight, ripe or not, and hadn't. Why warn the prey? Why show the prey the trap?

Unless, said a small cold voice in the back of his skull, unless she wanted him running. Running toward something. Toward the fells, perhaps. Toward whatever waited in the old witch-country, where the sigil was pulling him anyway, tugging at his ribs even now like a dog scenting home.

"Or," Xanthe said aloud, to the empty road, "she's mad, and cruel, and enjoys games."

The sigil pulsed. It felt, outrageously, *amused*.

"Don't you start agreeing with her."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He walked instead, fast, through the gray hours before dawn, and somewhere on a ridge with the sky turning the color of a healing bruise, exhaustion caught up with fear and dragged both of them down into a ditch-side doze, and he dreamed.

Not his dream.

He was kneeling in a garden he had never planted, planting things he couldn't name, and his hands were wrong — broader, older, a scar across the left thumb he'd never earned — and someone behind him said, in a voice like warm bread, *"Zeph, you've planted them upside down again,"* and Zephyrine's body, his body, laughed and turned, and there was a man in the doorway with flour on his sleeves and love all over his face, and Xanthe woke with tears freezing on his cheeks and grief sitting on his chest like a cat that had chosen him.

A husband. Zephyrine had had a husband. Flour-sleeved, warm-voiced, and gone — the dream had that shape too, the shape of a room with a missing chair.

"Things that are mine," Xanthe whispered, to prove to himself he still knew what they were. "My name. My sister's lullaby. The smell of the salt market at dawn. The first knot I ever tied, that Master Zeph undid and retied without laughing at me." He counted them like coins, like prayer-beads. "My hands are mine. These blisters are mine. This fear is mine."

The sigil pulsed gently, comfortingly, and that was somehow the most frightening thing yet — that it was learning to soothe him, that it was becoming *good at him.*

He got up. He walked. The sun climbed, and the fells opened ahead of him, treeless and bright and full of old stones, and by noon he had almost stopped flinching at every crow, every snapped twig, every stranger-shaped shadow.

Almost.

Because near midday, resting on a milestone, he found a small thing tucked into the crack in the stone. A knot. Seven knots, tied perfectly, correctly, in good hemp — a luck-knot, fine workmanship, better than his own.

Except the seventh knot, the sealing knot, the one every hedge-child learns last and never forgets, had been tied left-handed.

Mirror-wise. Backwards. The signature of someone who had watched him tie a hundred knots and remembered every detail, and wanted him to know she could do it too — and undo it.

Xanthe stood on the road in the wide daylight with the little charm in his hand, and the wind came down off the fells smelling of thyme and old stone, and he understood that he had not escaped anything last night. He had merely been given a longer leash.

"Well," he said to the milestone, to the fells, to the patient warm weight in his palm. "At least now I know what I'm walking toward."

He put the knot in his pocket — because throwing it away felt like letting her win twice — and he turned up the fell-road, toward the high country where the witch-fires had once burned openly, and behind him, far enough away to be a trick of hearing, someone began to hum.

His sister's lullaby.

The one he had, just that morning, been unable to recall past the fourth line.
