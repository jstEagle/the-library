## 8. Memories Not Mine

Xanthe woke speaking a language that had been dead for two hundred years.

He knew it was dead because Zephyrine had taught it to him — or rather, she had taught him *about* it, one winter evening when he was fourteen, when she'd been in her cups on plum wine and feeling sentimental. "Old Vellamari," she'd said, tracing letters in spilled wine on the table. "Nobody's spoken it since the Purge of the Coast. Lovely tongue. All hissing sounds and words like river stones." She'd demonstrated a phrase, laughing at herself, and he had repeated it clumsily and forgotten it by morning.

Now he woke with the whole of it sitting behind his teeth like a mouthful of smooth pebbles, and the first thing out of him — before thought, before breath, before his own name — was:

*"Alden, the fire's low."*

He lay still in the hayloft above the cooper's barn outside Millbrook, staring at the rafters, listening to rain.

*Alden.* The name arrived with a face attached: broad shoulders, flour always in his dark hair no matter how many times his wife scolded him about it, a laugh that started in his belly and took over his whole body. A baker. He rose before dawn every day of his life and came home smelling of yeast and woodsmoke, and he would kiss her — *me*? — on the top of the head and say, *"Sera, you let the fire die again,"* and she would say, *"I was reading, love, the book was better than warm,"* and he would —

Xanthe sat up so fast he banged his skull on a rafter.

"Ow," he said, in plain Low Vellamari, his own tongue, and clung to the sound of it. "Ow. Ow. My name is Xanthe Sandoval. I am twenty-six years old. I have never been married. I do not bake. I once burned water."

The last part was true and verifiable and he held onto it like a rope thrown to a drowning man. *I burned water. That happened. I was eleven. Zephyrine laughed until she cried.*

But the memory had a strange doubled quality now, because Zephyrine's laugh in it overlapped with another laugh — Alden's — and for one dizzying half-second both men loved him and both memories were equally warm and equally real and equally not his.

The sigil pulsed under his glove, at the base of his palm where it had pressed itself into his flesh three weeks ago, patient as a seed.

"Don't," he told it. "Whatever you're doing in there, stop it. I liked my memories fine. They were small, but they were mine."

The sigil did not answer, because it was a mark and not a person, though lately Xanthe had his doubts. It warmed slightly against his skin, which could have been an apology or could have been indigestion. There was no way to tell.

---

The trouble — one of the troubles, the list of troubles had recently required its own shelf — was that the grief didn't fade like ordinary dreams did. Ordinary dreams dissolved by breakfast. This one stayed. By noon he'd caught himself reaching for a second cup out of habit, because *Alden* liked two cups and complained all morning if there wasn't. By evening he'd nearly answered to *Sera* when the cooper's wife called for her daughter Sarah.

And at night he lay in whatever barn or ditch or cheap room he'd found, and mourned a man who had been dust for two centuries, with an intimacy that made his chest actually hurt. He knew how Alden took his tea. He knew the scar on his knuckle from the bread knife, and the way he sang badly and only when he thought no one was listening, and the exact weight of his arm across the blanket at night. Two hundred years dead and Xanthe missed him the way you miss an ache when an illness passes — the absence of it felt wrong.

"That's the cruelest thing it does," he said aloud to the loft, to the rain, to nobody. "It doesn't give me strangers' lives. It gives me *loves*. Like it wants me to keep them."

*We keep what must not be lost,* something in him said, in Old Vellamari, in a voice not quite his own — and then it was gone, and he was left holding the shape of the sentence and none of its certainty.

---

Three days later, in a room above a tavern in the river town of Brayford — paid for with the last honest coin he had, which is to say coin earned mending a fisherman's nets with charms disguised as knots, some things about a double life follow you down every road — Xanthe sat at a wobbly table with a stub of pencil and a sheet of paper he'd stolen from the tavern keeper, and began a list.

He titled it carefully, pressing hard enough to leave grooves in the wood beneath:

**Things That Are Mine**

Then he sat for a long time, pencil hovering, discovering that this was a much harder question than it sounded.

His name. Probably his name. Though even that had been shaken loose lately, because sometimes when he said *Xanthe* to himself in the dark it took a heartbeat to feel true, like a coat borrowed from a bigger man.

His mother's face — brown-eyed, quick to smile, gone since he was six. His sister Wren. The lullaby she used to hum, off-key, the one about the fisherman's wife. Zephyrine's cottage, the smell of rosemary and hot iron. The way she said *boy* like it was an insult and an endearment simultaneously. Twelve years of small careful life in Vellamare: the crooked shutters of his rented room, the taste of burnt coffee from the cart on Salt Lane, Mrs. Hobb next door who thought he sold trinkets and told him so kindly, as if it were a failing she forgave him.

He wrote. The pencil moved. And here was the thing that frightened him, the thing he set the pencil down twice to avoid thinking about directly: he could not always tell, mid-sentence, whether a memory was arriving from his own past or the sigil's. They came the same way. With the same textures. Rain on a window he might never have stood behind. A dog's bark in a yard he might never have cut through. His hand wrote *the smell of the harbor at low tide* and he stopped, cold, because Vellamare's harbor smelled that way and so did a hundred harbors in a hundred inherited lifetimes, and there was simply no knowing whose nose had first noticed it.

He kept writing anyway. What else was there to do?

**Things That Are Mine**
1. My name: Xanthe Sandoval. (Verify.)
2. Wren is my sister. She has green eyes and hums flat. (Verify.)
3. Mother died of marsh fever when I was six. I think. (Verify.)
4. Zephyrine taught me everything. Her hands shook when she ground valerian but never when she worked a charm.
5. I burned water, age eleven.
6. I am afraid of deep water. (Am I? Verify.)
7. I like the smell of rain on dust. (Mine? Or hers? Or anyone's?)
8. I have never been married. **This one is certain. Write it again.**

8. I have never been married.

He wrote it a second time and stared at it and felt briefly, absurdly better, as if ink were an anchor.

---

The days stacked up. He moved south along the river road, because south was away from Graywater and away from Vellamare and away, he hoped, from Inquisitor Corvane's tidy gray reach. He worked small jobs under false names — he'd had four names in ten days and was starting to lose track of which stranger owed which innkeeper an apology. He learned to sleep with one ear open for Odile, though he hadn't seen her since the road ambush, and somehow that was worse. *I'll take it when it's ripe,* she'd said, tasting the air near him like a cat deciding whether the mouse was worth the pounce. He dreamed of her twice, and in both dreams she was kind to him, which he chose not to examine.

The inheritance kept coming. Not all at once — the sigil seemed to have manners, or cruelty, of a slow and deliberate sort. It came in weather.

Some mornings he woke as himself. Some mornings he woke as a boy of nine learning to gut fish on the northern shore, hands remembering the knife-angle before his mind caught up. One morning he woke weeping without knowing why and spent an hour discovering why: a woman named Maren, a healer, who had been taken in the third purge, and the grief was old and worn smooth like sea glass, a grief someone else had carried so long they'd shaped it, and now it fit him perfectly too.

One afternoon in a market he picked up an apple and heard himself mutter, in a dead man's accent, *"three coppers, robbery,"* and haggled the fruit-seller down out of pure reflexive habit from a lifetime he had never lived. He got the apple for two coppers. He ate it standing in the square, feeling like a thief of something far worse than fruit.

---

In Brayford, on the ninth night, the list saved him, or he decided afterward that it had, which amounted to the same thing.

He'd woken at midnight as an old man — a bridge-builder, a good long life, children and grandchildren, a deathbed surrounded by family — and the sheer *weight* of it had pressed him into the mattress. So many years. So much love accumulated and stored. He lay there being ninety-one years old, remembering his granddaughter's wedding, and the horror wasn't the intrusion; the horror was how *welcome* it felt. How easy it would be to just sink. To put down Xanthe Sandoval like a heavy pack at the end of a long road, and be someone finished instead of someone still in progress.

He got up. He lit the candle stub. He read the list.

Most of it looked like it belonged to a stranger. But item five was solid: he could feel the truth of *burned water* in his body somewhere, in the specific embarrassment of an eleven-year-old showing a pot of gray boiling nothing to a witch who laughed until she had to sit down. Item four: Zephyrine's shaking and unshaking hands — that was his, bone-deep. Item two, Wren — mostly his. The green eyes were right. The humming was right. There was a thread of doubt wrapped around it now, thin as spider silk, but he could still find the edges of his own memory underneath the doubt, and while he could, he would hold them.

"You don't get me yet," he told the sigil. It pulsed, warm and noncommittal.

He added a line at the bottom, in careful letters:

9. I talk to a mark on my hand. This is mine. Nobody else gets to have it.

And then — because gentle humor was, according to everyone who had ever fled a magical artifact through river towns, essential to survival — he added:

10. The apple was two coppers. The haggling instinct was a dead man's. The apple itself was mine. I ate it. It was decent.

He slept after that, dreamlessly for once, list under his pillow like a saint's medal.

---

On the eleventh day the inheritance changed tactics, and Xanthe finally understood that it was not random. It was *looking* for something.

It gave him a memory of Zephyrine — not the arrest this time, not the tower cell, but something older. A kitchen. Forty years ago. She was young, dark-haired, arguing joyfully with a heavyset woman over a pot of stew, and the sigil offered this memory up gently, almost shyly, the way you'd offer a homesick child a familiar blanket. *Here,* it said. *Someone you love. Look.*

And it worked. God help him, it worked. He sat down in the middle of the road with tears running into his collar, homesick for a kitchen forty years gone, loving a woman he already loved, from an angle he'd never had. It was a gift. It was also, he understood dimly, a demonstration. *Look how good this is,* the sigil said, wordless and warm. *Look how much I carry. You could carry it too. You're already carrying it. Stop fighting.*

"Zephyrine sent you to me," he said to the empty road, wiping his face. "She chose me. Fine. But I want it noted that choosing me doesn't mean I have to *like* it."

The sigil pulsed. If marks could look smug, this one managed it.

---

It was also — he had to admit, alone in his room at night, in the spirit of honest accounting — making him more powerful. That was the seduction nobody warned you about, or rather everybody warned you about and you believed you'd be the exception. Charms that used to take him an hour of careful work now flowed out of his hands in minutes. Knots tied themselves into luck. Wards rose like breath on glass. He fixed a miller's grinding-stone with a touch and a word in a dialect extinct since the Purge, and the miller paid him triple and asked no questions, and Xanthe walked away richer and more dangerous and less himself, and could not honestly say which of the three troubled him most.

Because here was the shoreline arithmetic, which he worked out one evening watching the tide eat the riverbank: the water takes a little every day. A handful of sand. You don't notice a handful. Then one morning you stand further back from the edge than you meant to, and you can't remember walking back, and the willow tree that used to be at the bank's edge is now knee-deep in the river, and everybody tells you it was always like this.

He was losing inches a day. The list was his surveyor's stakes, driven into the sand.

---

Brayford ended the way his stays in towns always ended lately: too quickly, with too many people having noticed him.

He'd overheard it in the tavern's common room, keeping his hood up and his mug full and his ears working, the oldest skill Zephyrine ever taught him. Two drovers, complaining. Crown riders in the town. Asking questions — not the usual registry questions, not *have you seen a witch*, but specific ones. About a young man traveling alone, southbound, who paid in odd coin. Who talked in his sleep, according to one innkeeper, in languages nobody knew.

Talking in his sleep. Of course. Of *course*. He was carrying a library of dead tongues and grieving husbands and forty years of stew-arguing, and it all leaked out of him at night like light under a door, and in a country where magic was treason, a man who spoke in extinct dialects in his sleep might as well carry a bell.

He left before dawn, list in his inner pocket, coin short, options shorter.

South, then. Properly south, past Brayford, past the river country, to the coast — and the thought surfaced from somewhere with the smooth inevitability of something that had been waiting for him to need it: there were smugglers on the southern coast who moved more than brandy and silk. Zephyrine had mentioned them exactly once, in the tone she used for things she expected him to survive needing. *If it ever goes wrong, boy, go to the salt markets and ask for passage on a ship that doesn't exist.*

At the time he'd thought she meant it as a joke. Zephyrine had maybe three jokes and none of them were jokes.

He walked south with his collar up, rehearsing under his breath — his own name, Wren's lullaby, the taste of burnt coffee on Salt Lane, item by item, stake by stake against the tide. Behind him the crown riders fanned out through Brayford asking their precise questions. Ahead of him the road bent toward the sea and toward a ship that didn't exist, and under his glove, patient as a seed, warm as a hearth, the sigil carried its thousand dead onward, and waited to see what he would become.
