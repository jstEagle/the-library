## 9. The Salt Market

The Salt Market did not appear on any map of Vellamare's southern quarter, which was fitting, because it did not officially exist at all. It lived beneath the fish-criers and the tar-smell, down a stair that looked like it led to drowning and actually led to everything else.

Xanthe found it the way you were meant to find it: by being lost, looking lost, and being watched by someone who decided he was the right kind of lost.

"You're leaking," said the woman.

She was mending a net outside a shuttered smokehouse, and she said it the way you'd tell a stranger his shoelace was undone. Xanthe stopped on the wet cobbles and considered denying it. He had gotten very good at denial over twelve years. Denial was practically his trade.

"Leaking what?" he asked, which was, he realized too late, not a denial.

"Magic." She bit her thread and squinted up at him. "Like a bucket with a split stave. You've been carrying something heavy for days, boy, and it's sloshing."

He should have run. Every instinct Zephyrine had ever hammered into him — *small, careful, invisible* — screamed at him to laugh, apologize for the smell of fish, and walk back up the stairs into the honest daylight world where nobody's shoelaces were made of treason.

Instead he stood there, exhausted to the bone, three nights of bad sleep behind him and Odile's parting smile somewhere behind *that*, and heard himself say:

"I'm looking for passage south."

The woman's needle paused. "South for what?"

"For winter," Xanthe said. "I hear it's warmer there."

It was an old phrase. He didn't know how old until her face changed — softened around the eyes, the way a door softens when the bolt is drawn back. She stood, joints cracking like the net-lines, and jerked her chin toward the smokehouse door.

"Then you'd best come in out of the cold," she said. "Name's Brisa. And whatever that thing is in your pocket, it can stop staring at me."

* * *

The Salt Market, it turned out, was less a market than a family reunion where nobody shared blood but everybody shared a sentence once handed down by some magistrate or priest: *guilty of the craft*. There were perhaps forty of them, living in the vaults and cellars beneath the fish-quay — a hedge-witch who cured the fishermen's rheumatism in exchange for herring; a former choir-singer whose voice could still, when pushed, calm a storm, though it cost him two days of silence afterward; twin brothers who read currents the way scholars read scripture and charged smugglers accordingly.

They fed Xanthe stew. They gave him a cot in a room that smelled of brine and dried kelp. And they asked him, very politely and very carefully, nothing at all — because the first rule of the Salt Market, as Brisa explained over a second bowl, was that you never asked another soul what their magic was or how they'd escaped with it.

"We're all of us liars here," she said comfortably. "Best liars in Vellamare. So we don't ask each other's stories. Ruins the craft."

"That seems healthy," Xanthe said.

"It's cowardice," Brisa agreed cheerfully, "but organized."

He laughed for the first time since Graywater. It came out rusty, like a hinge. The sigil, wrapped in its cloth against his chest, went warm against his skin — pleased, he thought, or amused, or possibly hungry. It was getting harder to tell the difference.

That night he slept without dreaming for the first time in a week, which should have been a mercy, and instead woke him at dawn with the cold, specific terror of a man who has been watched all night by something patient.

* * *

Three days he stayed. Three days of small kindnesses that undid him worse than any interrogation could have.

It was the rheumatism witch, a round and grandmotherly man named Fosco, who broke him down first — not with questions but with chamomile tea delivered to his cot each morning and a running commentary on everyone else's business.

"The twins are sulking," Fosco reported on the second morning, settling onto the edge of Xanthe's cot uninvited. "Someone undercut their current-readings with actual weather-magic. Very rude. Very effective. Don't tell anyone I told you."

"Who would I tell?"

"That's the spirit." Fosco sipped his own tea. "You know, you remind me of my third husband."

"I didn't realize you'd had three."

"Two real, one theoretical. He had that look. The look of a man doing arithmetic about how many people he can afford to care about." Fosco patted Xanthe's knee. "The answer's more than you think, generally. But it's your sums to do."

On the morning of the fourth day, Brisa came to fetch him, and her face had that organized-cowardice look turned all the way up.

"There's people want to meet you," she said. "Or—" honesty won, briefly, "—want to meet *it*. The thing you're leaking. Fair warning, boy: I told them you weren't mine to offer. They're going to ask anyway."

The meeting was held in the old salt-store, a low brick room hung with nets gone gray, lit by lamps that burned without oil. Xanthe counted nine people around the table and recognized, with a lurch, at least four faces from Vellamare's ordinary life: the apothecary from Chandler's Row. A customs clerk. A woman who sold pies near the Cathedral gate and had once, memorably, overcharged him for a mutton pasty.

"We call ourselves the Undertow," said the pie-seller, who apparently chaired things. Her name, he learned, was Maren. "Which is sentimental, but we're sentimental people. You'll forgive the theater."

Around the table, hands folded. Lamps guttered attentively. Xanthe became extremely aware that every person in this room could do something illegal, and that he was standing in the middle of them with a stolen relic pressed to his sternum.

"You came looking for passage," Maren said. "We can give you that. South, past the Crown Sound, no manifest, no questions. That's ours to promise and we keep our promises — it's most of what we have left." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat. "But before we book your berth, I'm going to say a word, and I'd like you to listen to the whole word before you run."

"All right," Xanthe said warily.

"Registry."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Everyone let it fall.

"You've heard the criers," Maren went on. "*Pardon for names, ruin for silence.* You've seen what happens to the ones they drag in — the fever-charm woman off Lantern Street, the cooper's boy. And I'd wager—" her eyes flicked, deliberately, to the shape under his coat— "that you know something the rest of us don't about why the crown has suddenly remembered we exist after forty quiet years."

"I don't—"

"They're hunting something," said the choir-singer quietly. "Something specific. We've all felt it, these last weeks. Like weather changing. And you walked into our cellar wearing it around your neck."

Brisa shifted by the door, unhappy. Fosco, to his credit, looked at his tea.

"So here is our proposal," Maren said. "Passage south, yes. Safety, yes. But also — purpose." And there it was, rising in her voice, in the leaning faces around the table, in the lamp-flames themselves: hope, raw and enormous and aimed directly at him. "Forty years we've hidden. Forty years of charms sold as trinkets and gifts buried alive and neighbors dragged away for curing a child, while we sat in cellars being *organized cowards*." She spread her hands. "And now something has come up out of the old world, out of Zephyrine's keeping, and landed in the chest of a runaway apprentice. Do you understand what that is? That's not cargo. That's a *banner*."

"A banner," Xanthe repeated.

"The sigil remembers every mage who bore it," said the customs clerk, with scholarly hunger. "If even half the stories are true, it holds the names, the works, the *proofs* — everything the crown burned and called extinguished. Carried openly, it says: we were never gone. Carried at the head of ten thousand hidden folk—" 

"Ten thousand?" Xanthe said faintly.

"Twelve thousand if you count the islands," said one of the twins.

Maren leaned forward, and her voice gentled into something almost kind. "You've been alone a long time, haven't you? Twelve years small and careful and invisible. We know. All of us, we *know*." Around the table, heads nodded — the apothecary, the pie-woman, the storm-calmer with his ruined voice. "You don't have to be invisible anymore. Stand with us. Let them see it. Let the whole coast see it. One mark, carried in daylight, and every hiding-place in Vellamare becomes an army."

For one dangerous, shameful moment — and he would hate himself for it later, would write it down and cross it out and write it again — Xanthe *wanted* it.

That was the trap of it. Not the danger; he'd had twelve years of practice being afraid, and fear fit him like an old coat. This was worse. This was the apothecary's face and Fosco's tea and Brisa holding the door and forty people who smelled like him, hurt like him, hid like him — and the sudden vertiginous relief of *they exist, I was never the only one, I could stop pretending* — all of it gathered up and pointed like a weapon at the bundle against his heart.

Hope, he thought, dizzily. Hope arrives as another kind of cage. Who taught me that?

The answer arrived unbidden, in a voice not his own: *Zephyrine did. When you were fourteen. You thought she was being grim. She was being accurate.*

"No," he said.

The room went very still.

"Not — " He swallowed. Started again. "I mean to refuse kindly, and I'm grateful for the tea, and Fosco, your third husband sounds lovely. But I've seen what happens to banners. Banners get carried. Banners don't get to say where." His hand rose without permission and flattened against his chest, over the sigil. "There are memories in this thing that aren't mine, and they're eating the ones that are, and every person who ever bore it ended badly, and I will not be a flagpole for a rebellion I might believe in if it asked me instead of my luggage."

Silence. Then Fosco, softly, into his cup: "Told you he'd say no. Nobody bets with me."

Maren's face didn't harden, which somehow made it worse. "And when Corvane finds you?" she asked. "Because he will. He's cataloguing us already — we've lost two runners this month. Alone, you're a courier carrying treasure through a war. With us, you'd have forty pairs of hands between you and the Registry." She shrugged, elegant and terrible. "Refusal noted. Passage stands, as promised. Berth on the *Kestrel's Debt*, tide after next. Think on the rest."

* * *

He thought on it, which was his mistake. He thought on it through a sleepless night and a long grey morning, walking the fish-quay with his collar up, turning the arithmetic over: forty pairs of hands against one Inquisitor. A banner against a grave. Twelve thousand strangers against one apprentice who couldn't remember his sister's lullaby correctly anymore.

He had started keeping the list, in those weeks. *Things That Are Mine.* He took it out now, standing in the spray-shadow of the quay wall, and read it like a lifeline.

*My name is Xanthe Sandoval.*
*I burn charm-stock in a tin basin and blame the chimney.*
*I am left-handed, except when I'm not, which worries me.*
*Fosco's tea is chamomile. That one's new, but I'm keeping it.*
*Zephyrine smells of woodsmoke and burnt sugar.*
*I wanted to stop running. I think that one's still mine.*

The ink swam. Or the quay did. Hard to say, lately.

"Told you," said Brisa's voice at his elbow. She'd followed him out, or been sent; with Brisa it was always both. "Organized cowardice. Works fine till somebody brings hope in off the street." She looked out at the grey water. "Maren means it, mind. She's not lying to you. She just believes so hard it functions like a lie."

"I know," Xanthe said. "That's what frightens me."

"And the other thing?"

"What other thing?"

Brisa's jaw tightened, just slightly. "Runner we lost this month. Name of Pell. Good lad, quick as anything. Came back yesterday." She turned to look at him fully, and her weathered face was very old in the morning light. "Came back wrong, boy. Quiet. Smiling at nothing. Kept asking whether the cargo had decided yet." She held his gaze. "Whatever's hunting the gifted in this city — it doesn't only arrest."

Cold moved through Xanthe that had nothing to do with the sea wind. He thought of Odile on the road, tasting the air. *I'll take it when it's ripe.*

"Cancel my berth," he said.

"Now hang on—"

"*Cancel it.* If they're watching your runners, then the *Kestrel's Debt* isn't an escape, it's a marked crate." He was already moving, back toward the stair, back toward the cellar where Fosco kept tea and forty people kept hope. "And Brisa — get them out. All of them. Tonight."

"You can't warn forty people by yourself—"

"No," Xanthe agreed. His hand had gone hot against his chest, hotter than it had ever been, the sigil thrumming like a struck bell, and he felt — distinctly, unmistakably — the sensation of being *pulled*, downward and inward, toward a memory that was not his own and was insisting on being visited. "But I know someone who's good at impossible things, and she taught me everything I know. She just needs telling."

The salt-store lamps guttered as he passed. Behind his eyes, a tower cell materialized, stone by stone, and a woman's voice — woodsmoke and burnt sugar, wry even now — began to speak.

He sat down on the stairs rather than fall down them, and let her carry him under.
