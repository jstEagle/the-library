## 10. Zephyrine's Cell

The cell had one window, and the window had a view of the sea, and Zephyrine had decided within the first hour that this was either a mercy or a mistake on the part of her captors, and she intended to make it the latter.

She sat on the cot with her hands folded in her lap like a grandmother waiting for tea, which was, in fairness, exactly what she looked like. Sixty years of weather and worry had settled into her face in a way that made strangers offer her their seats on ferries. It had also, over the years, made them underestimate her, which was the single most useful feature of her entire body. Her hands were the second most useful, but those were bound in iron now, and the iron sang a thin, sour note against her skin, dampening everything she might have tried.

Not everything. Never everything. That was the thing about witch-hunters: they built cages for witches as they imagined them, and witches were never what they imagined.

The lock on the door, for instance. She had counted its tumblers by sound on the first night — six, and one of them lazy, the way the third tumbler of any lock made by the Vellamare Guild of Locksmiths was lazy, because old Master Fennick had cut corners on the third tumbler for forty years and no inspector had ever caught him. She could open it in her sleep. She would not open it. That was the part Corvane would never understand, and she was rather looking forward to never explaining it.

The door opened. Of course it did. It always opened at the hour when the light through the window turned the color of weak tea, as if the man kept a schedule even for his cruelties.

"Good evening, Inquisitor," she said pleasantly. "You look tired. Are they feeding you? Because they are certainly not feeding me, and one of us ought to complain to somebody."

Corvane did not answer. He never answered the small talk, which was a shame, because she had a lifetime of small talk stored up and nowhere to spend it. He set the little table between them — they always brought the table, as if the interrogation were a dinner party with terrible hospitality — and laid out his instruments in their neat row. Candles. A bowl of water. The long silver needle he believed could find lies in the blood.

Zephyrine had watched that needle lie to three different inquisitors over the course of her life. It found what the holder expected to find. Corvane, she suspected, expected very little and therefore would be the hardest to fool, and also the most interesting, and she had been starved of interesting company for twelve years.

"Zephyrine of Graywater," he said, in the flat voice of a man reading a ledger. "You are accused of the practice of forbidden arts. The sigil recovered from your possession is under crown seal. You will tell me what it is."

"It's a birthmark," she said. "Family thing. Very embarrassing at the beach."

The needle went into her arm. It came back clean, because she was telling the truth — it *was* a birthmark, in the way that a river is a line of water. She had learned long ago that the only reliable way past a truth-needle was to be relentlessly, tediously, unhelpfully truthful. It exhausted them. It also, if you were patient, let you build a little architecture of honesty with a room hidden inside it.

"The sigil stores memories," Corvane said. Not a question. He had seen it work; she knew he had seen it work, because she had let it work, briefly, in the first hour of her capture, a flash of something — a market, a hanging, a woman's scream — just enough to frighten him. Fear made men careful, and careful men were slow, and slow men gave her time. "Memories of mages. Dead mages. You carried the memories of the dead on your skin."

"I carried *recipes*," Zephyrine said. "Mostly for bread. You'd be amazed how much of magic is bread. The rest is knowing when not to stir."

The needle again. Clean. She watched his jaw tighten by a degree that no one but a grandmother would have noticed, and she catalogued it the way he was no doubt cataloguing her: *subject resists through levity; levity is armor; armor has a weight; find where it tires.*

Let him look. She had been tiring places on purpose since before he was born.

* * *

The nights were the hard part.

She would not have admitted it to Corvane under torture, and he would likely have tried, though she gave him poor odds; the man had the soul of a customs clerk, and customs clerks make terrible torturers because they keep asking the same question in the same order. But alone, in the dark, with the iron cuffs singing their sour note and the sea making its endless patient argument against the rocks below, Zephyrine allowed herself the small dishonesty of grief.

She missed her cottage. She missed the crooked door that stuck in wet weather. She missed the garden where the rosemary had gone feral and staged a slow coup against the thyme. She missed — and here her chest did the thing it did, the tightening, the hand around the heart — she missed the boy.

Twelve years, and not a letter. Not that letters were possible; that was the whole shape of the sacrifice. She had sent him away with a pack, a false name, and the instruction never to come back, and he had obeyed her so thoroughly and so resentfully that she could have wept with pride. *Xanthe Sandoval,* she thought into the dark. *Stubborn as his mother, careful as his father, and twice as clever as either, though he'd die before admitting the cleverness came from me.*

The sigil was not on her skin anymore. That was the piece Corvane hadn't understood yet, the piece he turned over at night in his cold little room, she'd have wagered. He had taken it from her — a band of living mark, cut from her wrist in a procedure she had made as difficult as dignity allowed — and he had it locked in an iron box in an evidence vault, and he believed he had it.

He had an empty glove. The sigil had left her the moment she'd called it, three days before the hunters came, and it had gone where she sent it. It had gone home to the hearthstone, into the split and the ash and the hidden seam of the cottage where she had first taught a frightened eleven-year-old to feel for warmth in a cold flue. *Where I taught you to look,* she had told him once, kneeling at that hearth, his small hands in hers. *When you're lost, look where I taught you to look.*

She had no way of knowing if he'd remember. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Twelve years of silence, and her whole plan resting on whether a boy now grown had kept one sentence the way he'd kept her lullabies, badly, in a voice he thought no one heard.

*And if he doesn't remember,* said the dark, *then the sigil waits in a cold hearth until the cottage falls down, and everything the dead gave to it — every mother's song, every last word, every small bright ordinary hour of every witch they burned — stays in the ground forever.*

"Then I'll have failed," she told the dark, "and I'll have deserved it."

Because that was the cost, and she was honest with herself at night if nowhere else: what she was doing to Xanthe was not a gift. She knew what the sigil did to a bearer. She had watched her own mother drown by degrees in borrowed grief, smiling at breakfast with a dead man's sorrow behind her eyes. She had taken it anyway, because someone had to, and she had held it for forty years by building walls inside herself, room after room, and now she was asking the boy — her boy, her stubborn, careful, *invisible* boy — to hold what she had held, knowing what it would cost him, knowing he might lose himself entirely.

She had dressed it up as destiny. She had arranged the path like a trail of breadcrumbs. And underneath it, plain as bread, it was this: *I could not carry it anymore, and I could not let it die, and I loved him, and love is a thing you can spend other people's coin with if you're careful enough.*

The guards changed at midnight. She heard the new one humming. Off-key. A lullaby — the lullaby every child in the coastal provinces knew, the one about the fisherman's daughter and the moon. Xanthe's sister had sung it. Xanthe had pretended to hate it.

Zephyrine lay back on the cot and began, very softly, to hum along, and if her eyes were wet, there was no one to see, and she had arranged it that way, and she was beginning to hate how much of her life was arrangement.

* * *

Corvane came on the fourth day with a new question, and Zephyrine knew it was new because he asked it badly.

"The sigil is inert," he said. He set the iron box on the table between them, and her heart — her own heart, the one thing in this cell that was entirely hers — went very still. "It does nothing. It is a scar on a strip of skin. You were its keeper for forty years and you gave us a dead thing."

"Do you want your needle fee back?" she asked. "I can recommend a very good lawyer. He's dead, but so is most of the profession these days."

"Where is the living one?"

And there it was. She had wondered when he'd get there. He was not stupid; that was the trouble with him. Stupid men she could have managed in her sleep, and indeed had, several times. But Corvane looked at a thing until it confessed, and the sigil's absence was a thing that could not lie forever.

"Where is it," he said again, and this time it wasn't a question, it was a door being put in a wall.

Zephyrine folded her hands. The iron cuffs sang. She thought about the hearthstone, the split, the seam of ash, the boy who might or might not be walking that road right now — she had timed it as best she could, the arrest, the news spreading down the coast like oil on water, the way fear travels faster than any rider — and she thought: *he will be angry. He will be so angry with me. He will stand in the ruins of my garden and curse my name and every rosemary bush I ever grew, and then he will kneel at the hearth because he is his mother's son before he is anyone's apprentice, and he will look where I taught him to look.*

And then he will hate me more, because the sigil will not ask his permission. It never had. It had never once asked hers.

"You want to know where it is," she said, "and I want to know something too, and I'm older, so I ask first." She leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "When you were a boy — and you *were* a boy once, I refuse to believe you were issued fully grown from a bureau — did anyone ever teach you anything true? Anything warm? Anyone at all?"

Something moved behind his eyes. Something old, and locked, and — she would have staked her garden on it — grieving. He shut the door on it so fast she heard the latch.

"The needle," he said.

"Ask your question with it, then."

He asked. *Do you know where the sigil is?* And Zephyrine, who did know, who knew to the width of a hearthstone, let the needle read her — and thought of the sea. The sea, the sea, the gray patient sea out her window, which was where the sigil was not, and which was true, because she did know where the sigil was in the way that a woman knows the sea: completely, and in a direction that would drown him.

Clean. The needle came back clean. Corvane stared at her for a long moment, and she smiled at him the way she'd smiled at tax collectors and bishops and one memorable bear, and she saw it land: the first hairline crack in the certainty. Not doubt of her. Doubt of his instruments. Doubt, the beginning of the long cold fever that would carry him, in time, to places he could not yet imagine.

"Enjoy the sea," she said to his back as he gathered his instruments. "It's the best view in the building. I recommend it at dawn. Very humbling. Everything should be humbling at dawn; it sets the tone for the day."

The door closed. The lock turned. Six tumblers, one lazy.

Zephyrine sat in the weak-tea light and let herself shake, just for a minute, just the hands, just where no one could see. Then she straightened, and wiped her face with the back of her bound wrists, and began the evening's work: the small, patient sabotage that was the only magic left to her. Tomorrow she would tell Corvane, under the needle, a true thing about the sigil's history — a true, useless, maddening fragment, a breadcrumb that led in a circle. The next day, another. She would feed his certainty until it grew so heavy it cracked the floor beneath him.

And somewhere down the coast, if the boy remembered, if the boy obeyed the oldest instruction she'd ever given him, a hearthstone was about to open its hand.

*Hold on, Xanthe,* she thought at the sea, at the distance, at the boy she had spent like her last coin. *I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. And run — the moment it touches you, whatever I left you, whatever it shows you — run.*

The sea said nothing. It never did. But it kept the window, and she kept the window, and between them they kept the watch.

* * *

On the ninth night, the humming guard was replaced by a silent one, and Zephyrine woke to the sound of boots in the corridor — too many boots, moving too fast for a schedule — and a voice she did not know saying a word she did: *Graywater.*

They had found the cottage. Of course they had found the cottage; she had never believed otherwise, had only gambled on the order of events, on the sigil moving faster than soldiers. She pressed her ear to the cold wall and listened to her own plan come apart or come true, and could not tell which, and understood that she never would, and that this — this not-knowing, this handing of everything she loved into the dark and waiting — this was the cost, and she had known it when she paid, and she had paid anyway.

The boots stopped at her door.

The lock — six tumblers, one lazy — began, very slowly, to turn.
