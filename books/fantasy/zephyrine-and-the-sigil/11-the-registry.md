## 11. The Registry

The Salt Market smelled of brine and secrets in roughly equal measure, and on the morning the world ended — though of course I didn't know it yet — it smelled mostly of fried dough.

I want to be fair to myself here. I want the record to show that I did everything right. I came in through the fish-gut door instead of the front arch. I wore the face of a man buying lamp oil. I kept my hands in my pockets where they couldn't betray me by doing anything clever. Twelve years of hiding teaches you habits so deep they're practically bones, and I had followed every one of them.

And still.

"Xanthe," said Marrow, the smuggler with the beard like a storm cloud that had given up halfway, "you'll take some breakfast."

It wasn't a question. That should have been my first warning. Everything Marrow said that morning was shaped like a question and loaded like a crossbow.

* * *

Perhaps I should explain how I'd come to be sitting in a den of criminals eating what claimed to be a sausage and what was, I suspect, a sausage's apology.

After the salt market had shown me its other face — after Dessa had pulled aside a barrel of pilchards to reveal a wall covered in chalk sigils, real ones, working ones, humming faintly like bees thinking about honey — I had thought, for one mad week, that I had found something. Not safety exactly. Safety was a word I'd retired years ago, like a coat too small for me now. But *company*. People who understood why you flinched at the word *witch*. People who bought their luck-knots knowingly and paid extra for the ones that actually worked.

They were careful people. They were kind people, mostly. There was a woman named Hesper who could coax warmth out of any fire and did so for the street children every winter, and a boy called Finch who could talk to gulls, which sounded charming until you learned that gulls are terrible gossips with no respect for privacy whatsoever.

And they wanted the sigil.

That was the thing. They hadn't said it plainly at first — careful people never do — but it had come up over cards, over stew, over the little rituals by which trust is built out of shared meals. A banner, they called it. Proof. If the gifted ever rose against the registry, they'd need something to rise *around*, and a living sigil full of every witch who ever lived — well. You could build a cathedral around that. You could build an army.

"We don't ask you to give it to us," Dessa had said, laying her hand flat on the table, all her fingers visible, the smuggler's gesture for *no blades*. "We ask you to stop running. Stand with us. Let them see what they've been hunting."

"They don't hunt what they can't see," I'd said. "That's rather been my strategy."

"And how's that going?" asked Finch, who was seventeen and therefore immortal.

I had laughed. That was the trouble. I'd laughed, and laughing is a door left open, and doors left open let things in.

* * *

So there I sat, in the back room above the chandlery, with Marrow pushing a plate at me and three people I had begun, foolishly, to think of as friends arranged around the walls like punctuation.

"Eat," said Marrow. "You look thin."

"I always look thin. It's one of my best features. Where's Dessa?"

"Dessa's busy." He folded his enormous hands on the table. His beard trembled, which I had learned meant he was either amused or ashamed, and his eyes said ashamed. "There's a man upstairs wants a word with you."

The floor went very far away and then came back.

"Marrow," I said quietly. "What man."

"He calls himself an Inquisitor. Grey coat, grey eyes, a voice like a ledger being balanced." Marrow wouldn't look at me. "He came to us two days ago. He knew about the wall. Knew about Hesper's fires, knew about Finch and his blasted birds. He knew everything, Xanthe, and he offered us the one thing we can't buy anywhere else."

"Amnesty."

"The registry opens Thursday. Names given before then walk free. Names not given—" He made a small, awful gesture, the gesture everyone in Vellamare knew, the one that meant *the wagons*.

"You could have warned me," I said. My voice came out steadier than I expected. "You could have just — not said anything, and I'd have been gone by dawn, and you'd have had your amnesty anyway."

"He has your description," Marrow said miserably. "Twelve years old. Apprentice to Zephyrine of Graywater. He's had it written down since before you grew the beard, lad. You were never going to outrun this one. We only chose the hour."

Which was, I suppose, almost a kindness. Smugglers' kindness, weighed and measured and sold at cost.

Above us, a floorboard creaked.

I looked at the plate of food a stranger had pushed toward me, and at the door, and at the window that was too narrow and too high and barred besides, and I thought: *twelve years*. Twelve years of small charms and smaller life, of luck-knots sold to dockworkers who'd have turned me in for a copper and a clear conscience, of burning my stock and running and running and running, and it had all come down to a room with one exit and a man upstairs who balanced ledgers with his voice.

The floorboard creaked again, closer to the stairs.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about terror: it is boring. It flattens everything into a single grey note. But underneath it, that morning, under the boredom and the fear, something in the sigil-scarred palm of my hand began to *warm*, and through the warmth came a feeling that was not mine and was unmistakably Zephyrine's, and the feeling was contempt.

*Oh, child,* said the memory of her, dry as old paper and twice as flammable. *You've been hiding so long you've forgotten what you're hiding.*

The stairs began to complain under a measured weight. Boots. Even his boots sounded organized.

"Come up when you're ready," Marrow said to the ceiling, wretchedly. And then, to me: "I'm sorry. For what it's worth."

"It isn't worth much," I said, "but I'll take it."

I stood. And because I am, at the end of the day, a hedge-witch's apprentice, and because twelve years of hiding had apparently taught me nothing except where to hide, I palmed the luck-knot from my own pocket — the first one I'd ever made that truly worked — and set it on the table in front of Marrow.

"For the road," I said. "You'll want it more than me, I think. Things are about to get complicated for smugglers with consciences."

* * *

Inquisitor Corvane was exactly as advertised, which is somehow worse than if he'd been ten feet tall with horns.

Grey coat. Grey eyes. Hands folded behind his back as though he'd borrowed them from a museum. He stood at the top of the stairs with the patience of a man who had never once needed to hurry, because everything eventually walked toward him. Beside him, two hunters in the crown's livery, and beside *them*, to my genuine surprise, Dessa — not restrained, just standing very straight with her chin up, wearing the expression of someone who has done arithmetic she despises and gotten the right answer.

"Xanthe Sandoval," Corvane said. Not a question either. Everyone that day spoke to me in statements.

"That depends," I said, "on who's asking."

"Inquisitor Corvane, Office of the Registry, by appointment of the Crown." He produced a document. Of course he did. "This certifies the detention of one runaway apprentice, name recorded in the year of the Graywater seizure. You've grown, Master Sandoval. The sketch does you poor service."

"People say that. It's the beard."

"It is not the beard." He studied me the way I imagine a jeweler studies a stone he intends to cut. "Zephyrine of Graywater gave the crown nothing. Twelve days of questioning and nothing but riddles. I confess I wondered whether her apprentice even existed, or whether she invented him to occupy my attention." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "But she did not invent the sigil, and the sigil requires a bearer, and here you stand with her mark scarred into your palm. May I see your hand?"

"No, thank you," I said. "My mother taught me not to give my hand to strangers."

One of the hunters snorted. Corvane did not.

"Master Sandoval. Let us be efficient, since your friends were not." He descended one step, unhurried. "You will come to the citadel. You will be registered, questioned, and — depending on what the sigil shows me — possibly pardoned. The crown values cooperation. Zephyrine chose defiance, and defiance has a cell with her name on it. You may choose differently."

And there it was. The open door. The pardon. Walk quietly, hold out my hand, tell him what he wanted, and live. Live small, but live. Hadn't smallness served me for twelve years? Hadn't I built a whole philosophy on the idea that a charm sold as a trinket harms no one?

Except.

Except Dessa was looking at me, and her eyes weren't apologetic anymore. They were saying *take it, you fool, take it and live.* Except Finch was somewhere in this building and would hang or worse if my interrogation went badly enough. Except Hesper's winter fires had warmed forty children who would grow up learning that warmth gets people taken in wagons. Except Zephyrine was sitting in a cell giving that grey man nothing but riddles, twelve days strong, while I stood here being offered a deal for doing precisely nothing.

Except the sigil in my palm was hot as a hearthstone now, and it was showing me things. Zephyrine's things. Her hands younger than mine, pressing a knot of thread into a frightened girl's palm. Her voice, saying words I suddenly understood had been meant for me all along, saved up across years like coins in a jar:

*Someday, boy, they will offer you the small life. Take it if you must. But know it for what it is — a coin they mint from you, spendable only once.*

"Master Sandoval?" said Corvane. "Your hand."

Do you know what relief feels like? It feels like taking off boots after a long march. It feels like putting down a weight so familiar you'd forgotten it was heavy.

I took off the mask. All of it. In one motion.

"My hand," I agreed pleasantly, "will see you right."

I opened my palm, and the sigil woke.

* * *

I cannot fully describe what happened next, because half of it happened to me and half of it happened *through* me, and the seam between those two things is not a place words fit well.

The sigil had spent weeks flooding me with memories, patient as tide. Now, for the first time, it went the other way. It *gave*. Light poured out of my hand — not the pretty theatrical gold of festival mages, but a hard white light like the inside of a lightning strike, and with it came everything. Every memory the sigil held spilled outward into the room, into the air, into the *world*, uncontained, and I heard myself laugh, high and wild, because it felt —

Oh, it felt *magnificent.*

Every witch-hunter in that room staggered as the inheritance struck them. I felt what hit them, because for a moment I was the hitting: a fisherman dead eighty years remembering the exact blue of a particular morning; a healer in a plague year, her arms full of strangers' children; Zephyrine herself, young, dancing on a seawall in bare feet while a man we will never meet again laughed at her balance. A thousand lives, a thousand griefs, a thousand small joys, all of it loose in the air of a chandlery in Vellamare, touching everyone present whether they had asked or not.

The hunters dropped their weapons — not from pain, exactly. From *fullness*. There is no room in a man for duty when he is suddenly remembering a lullaby in a language that died before his grandfather was born.

Corvane alone stayed standing. Grey to the last, he gripped the banister, jaw clenched, and through the blaze of everything I saw him watching me with those ledger-keeper's eyes, and I saw — this is important — I saw that he was *afraid*, and that he was filing the fear away for later study, and that neither of these facts changed his footing by an inch. Some men are built like that. God help us all.

"Seize him," Corvane said, and his voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, just enough.

But the hunters were weeping. Both of them. One of them was whispering a name over and over, and I don't think it belonged to anyone living.

Dessa grabbed my arm. "Run, you magnificent idiot—"

I ran.

Down the back stair, through the fish-gut door, out into the salt-smelling morning, and behind me the light followed, spilling out the windows, rising off the rooftops like heat-shimmer made visible. Anyone in Vellamare with eyes could see it. Anyone with ears would hear by nightfall: a mage lives. Not rumors, not trinkets, not maybe. *Confirmed.* I had spent twelve years teaching this city that magic was a fairy tale sold in market stalls, and I had just undone all of it in the time it takes to open a hand.

And the terrible thing — the thing I have not told anyone until now — is that I was *happy*. Running through the streets with the wind tearing at me and the sigil singing in my blood, I was happier than I had been in twelve years. The mask was gone. The small life was gone. Whatever came next, I would face it as what I actually was, and there is a freedom in catastrophe that safety can never afford you.

The poisoning took about four streets to arrive.

Because by the fifth street, the bells had started. By the sixth, I could hear the criers. By the seventh, I passed a notice board where men were already hammering up fresh paper, wet ink gleaming, and I read my own fate in a clerk's tidy hand:

*By order of the Crown: the city of Vellamare is closed. Gates sealed. House-to-house search to commence at dawn. Any harboring the mage known as Xanthe Sandoval will answer for his crimes as their own.*

Harboring. As their own. Every door I knocked on now became a gallows for whoever opened it. Every friend I had ever made became evidence against themselves. I had wanted to drop the mask, and I had, and beneath it the city had discovered it was holding hostages, and the hostages were everyone I loved.

Hesper. Finch. Marrow and his miserable conscience. Dessa, who had sold me and then saved me and was now, presumably, being arrested for both.

I stopped in the shadow of a chimney-stack, breathing hard, and the sigil pulsed in my palm — warm still, triumphant even — and I pressed it against the cold brick and felt, for the first time, something underneath its song that might have been the sigil's own version of an apology.

*I know,* I told it. "We'll discuss your timing later."

A shadow detached itself from the alley mouth ahead.

Not a hunter — wrong silhouette, no armor, and hunters announce themselves the way cathedrals do. This figure was slight, wrapped in a traveller's cloak, and when she stepped into the grey light I saw the smile I had seen once before on a dark road, the smile of someone tasting the air.

Odile.

"Well," she said, and her voice was velvet over knives. "You've gone and made yourself *interesting.*"

She looked at the light still bleeding from my palm, and then at my face, and her smile deepened into something almost tender, which from Odile was the most frightening thing yet.

"I did tell you," she said. "I said I'd take it when it was ripe."

Behind her, the bells rang on. Ahead of her, the only gate not yet sealed. And Odile held out her hand — not demanding, not threatening, merely offering, as though we were old friends meeting at a crossroads — and said:

"Run with me, little vessel. We have so much to discuss, and the whole city is about to be very crowded with people who want you far less gently than I do."
