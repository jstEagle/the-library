## 12. Odile's Bargain

The boy slept like a stone that had been thrown into a well.

Odile crouched at the mouth of the ruined chapel and watched him breathe, and felt — as she always did when she watched people sleep — the old hunger sit up in her chest like a dog hearing a bell. His dreams were loud. Dreams always were. They spilled off sleepers the way heat spilled off a hearthstone: unguarded, unpriced, free for anyone with the nerve to reach out and take.

She had taken from sleepers before. She would likely take from this one, eventually. But not tonight.

Tonight she had come to talk.

She built a small fire — not because either of them needed warmth, but because negotiations went better with firelight. People trusted firelight. Firelight said *we are only two travelers sharing a road*, which was a lie, but a comfortable one, and Odile had learned over three hundred years that most bargains were just lies arranged politely around a truth both parties were afraid to touch directly.

Xanthe Sandoval woke the way he seemed to do everything: suddenly, guiltily, as though waking were a crime he'd have to explain.

"Your wards are terrible," Odile said, feeding a stick to the flames. "You salted the doorway, which is charming, very grandmother-taught, but you salted it in a spiral instead of a circle. You basically drew the magic an arrow pointing at your own bed."

He sat up too fast and put his hand behind his back, which was where he'd been sleeping on the sigil. She could see the shape of it through his shirt. She could practically taste it through his shirt, and oh, what a taste it was — like biting into a peach and finding a cathedral.

"How long have you been sitting there?"

"Two hours. You talked in your sleep."

"I don't—"

"You do." She smiled, showing teeth. "Mostly in a dialect that died four centuries ago. Once in High Cantoric. And once, memorably, you wept and apologized to someone named Berengar for burning the bread."

His face did something complicated. She recognized the expression. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic against himself and losing.

"Berengar," he said carefully. "I've never met anyone named Berengar."

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "You haven't. He died in 1189. He was a hedge-witch in the marsh country, married to a woman named Sef who made excellent cheese and terrible decisions. You grieved him last night between midnight and the second cockcrow." She tilted her head. "How does that sit in you? Does it sit like yours? Or does it sit like furniture in a borrowed room?"

* * *

Here is what the songs got wrong about memory-thieves, Odile thought, watching him try to decide whether to be afraid of her: the songs assumed theft was easy. That you crept into a mind and plucked out a pearl and left the shell intact. As if memories were coins in a purse, separate things, each with its own edge.

They weren't. A life was a braid. You pulled one strand and the whole thing came apart in your hands, and then you had to carry it — all of it — because a memory out of context was worse than useless. It was a wound shaped like someone else.

She had been born Odile Marchand, daughter of a dyer, in a town whose name was now a field. She remembered her mother's hands stained blue to the wrist; she remembered them clearly, which was how she knew they had been real, because everything else about her childhood had been worn down to rumor by the sheer traffic of other lives passing through hers.

She had stolen her first memory at nineteen, dying of fever, from a traveling physician who offered it as medicine. *Here,* he'd said, *here is my mother's kitchen, here is being seven years old and safe, hold onto this and live.* And she had lived, and she had never given it back, and something in her had understood even then what she'd become: a house with too many tenants, a purse that could never again hold only its owner's coin.

Since then: a soldier who fought at the Fords of Ash, so she knew the taste of mud and terror and the strange boredom of waiting to die. A queen's taster. A nun. A lighthouse keeper named Ilsa who had loved the sea in a way Odile still couldn't decide whether she envied or resented. A plague-doctor. A child — she'd taken a child's memory once, by accident, and had spent a year crying at bread crusts without knowing why until the grief finally surfaced and introduced itself.

Centuries of it. She was, if you stacked the years end to end, older than the crown itself, and she had been no one in particular for all of it.

And now here sat this boy with a sigil full of every witch the crown had ever burned, holding — without understanding it — the exact thing Odile had spent three lifetimes trying to build alone: continuity. A self that did not dissolve. A thread long enough to hold.

It wasn't fair, really. He'd gotten it by accident. Inheritance, no theft required. The universe had a sense of humor, and it was cruel, and Odile respected that in an institution.

"Say it," Xanthe said. He'd found his voice somewhere in the last minute, and there was steel under the fear now, which she approved of. "Whatever you're circling toward. Say it."

"All right." She folded her hands. "Here is my offer, and I'll only make it once, so listen with both ears."

* * *

"The sigil is eating you," she said. "You know that. Don't make the polite face; I can smell it happening. Every night you go to bed as Xanthe Sandoval and every morning there's slightly less of him answering the roll call. You keep a list — don't look shocked, you mutter the entries in your sleep — *Things That Are Mine.* How many entries have you crossed off lately? How many have you added that you're not sure of?"

His jaw tightened. She pressed on, gently, because gentleness was also a tool and she kept hers sharp.

"A sigil like that isn't a jewel. It's a vessel, and vessels want filling, and right now you are the nearest liquid. Zephyrine poured herself into it deliberately. The others before her — some chose, some didn't. But the mechanism doesn't care about consent. It cares about capacity. Left alone, in a year, maybe two, there will be a person walking around wearing your face who remembers being forty different witches and almost remembers being you. Like a coat with too many previous owners. Eventually you can't tell which patches were original."

"That's a lie," he said. But his hand had drifted up, unconsciously, to rest over the sigil through his shirt, the way a man touches a wound.

"It's a diagnosis," Odile said. "Lies are cheaper. If I wanted to lie to you I'd tell you I could take it away cleanly, painlessly, and you'd be yourself again by spring. I can't. Nobody can. What I *can* do is teach you the discipline I've learned across three hundred years of carrying lives that aren't mine. How to sort. How to label. How to stand in the middle of a thousand borrowed voices and say, *no, this one is me, this voice is mine, I know it by its weight.*" She leaned forward. "Because I am the only person alive who has done it, Xanthe. Everyone else who ever carried more than one life either drowned or became something that used to be a person. I'm still here. Still Odile. Barely, some days — but barely counts."

"And the price," Xanthe said. His eyes had gone flat and careful. "There's always a price."

"There is." She let the silence stretch, because honesty landed harder after silence. "A share. When you learn enough to know what you're holding — and only then, I swear it on the first memory I ever stole, which is my mother's kitchen and the truest thing I own — you give me a portion of the sigil. Not half. Not yet. A share. Enough that when the inheritance comes to me, it comes with your discipline wrapped around it, so I don't drown either. We teach each other to swim, and then we split the sea."

"Why would I ever agree to that?" he asked. "Why would I hand you anything?"

"Because," Odile said, and here she did something she rarely did, something she saved for moments that deserved it: she stopped performing.

She reached into herself — down past Odile-the-smiler, Odile-the-threat, down through the strata — and pulled forward the oldest thing she carried, and let him see it. Not told. *Seen*. It was a thief's trick turned inside out: instead of taking, giving. She opened the door of her own house and let him look in.

He flinched. She felt him feel it — the blue-stained hands, the kitchen with the crooked shutter, a small girl waiting for a mother who was already dead, held perfectly preserved for three centuries because it was the only proof Odile Marchand had ever existed at all.

"Oh," he said quietly.

"Yes," she said. "'Oh.' Now you understand what you're looking at. I am what happens when there's no sigil. When there's no craft, no vessel, no design — just appetite and accident, century after century. You are standing in a chapel holding the most dangerous object in the world, and the choice in front of you isn't whether to trust me. The choice is whether to become *me*, slowly, by trial and error, or to learn from someone who already paid tuition."

She let the vision close. The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, an owl complained about the state of the world, which Odile found relatable.

"You said 'not yet,'" Xanthe said at last. "Not half. Yet."

"You were listening. Good. That's the first lesson, actually — listen to exactly what people say. Half the thieves in the world are defeated by their victims' precision." She shrugged. "Yes. Yet. Because I think — and I have thought this since the moment I tasted you on the road — that this sigil is not finished. That it's ripening toward something. Zephyrine didn't hide it from the hunters, Xanthe. Think about that. She had twelve years and a hundred hiding places, and instead she aimed it. At you. Whatever she meant it for, it isn't sitting still, and I want to be beside it when it arrives, not chasing it."

"You think she planned this."

"I think," Odile said, "that Zephyrine Voss never did a single unplanned thing in her life except love an apprentice who ran away, and even that she probably scheduled." 

Something moved across his face — grief, quick and raw, and beneath it a fierce stubborn tenderness that Odile catalogued immediately, because tenderness was rare and rarer still in people carrying that much death.

"If I say yes," he said, "you teach me first. Weeks of teaching. Before any share, any split, any — anything."

"Weeks," she agreed. "Months, if you're slow."

"And if I learn enough to destroy it instead?"

Odile laughed, genuinely, and the sound surprised even her. "Then you'll destroy it, and I'll have spent weeks teaching a boy to ruin the finest thing I ever wanted, and I will deserve it, because I'll have taught him well." She spread her hands. "That's the bargain. No chains, no oaths written in blood — blood-oaths are for amateurs and leave scars. Just two people dissolving, agreeing to hold each other steady long enough to see what they become."

* * *

He didn't say yes that night. Of course he didn't. He paced the chapel like a cat in a sack, checked his salt spiral twice, glared at her, asked eleven questions (she answered ten honestly and lied beautifully about the eleventh, which was about Zephyrine's cell, because even Odile had limits), and finally lay back down facing the wall with the rigid dignity of a boy pretending to sleep.

But Odile had watched people for three hundred years, and she knew the difference between *no* and *not yet*, and this was *not yet* wearing a very convincing costume.

She sat by the fire and did her nightly accounting, the ritual that kept her Odile. *My name is Odile Marchand. My mother dyed cloth and her hands were blue. I am afraid of deep water and I do not remember why, which means it isn't mine. I like figs. I stole a lighthouse keeper's love of storms and I have decided to keep it.*

Across the chapel, the boy murmured in a dead tongue, then — surfacing briefly — in his own: "*...and the blue door, the blue door is mine...*" Then he was under again, and the sigil drank.

Odile looked at him for a long time.

*Recognition,* she thought, was a strange word for what she felt. It wasn't recognition, exactly. It was more like meeting someone walking the same road from the opposite direction — you pass, you glance, you both know what the journey costs, and neither of you says it aloud because saying it makes it heavier.

Three hundred years she had been the only one.

Now there were two of them, and one of them was asleep, and the thing in his hand was dreaming too, and Odile Marchand — daughter of a dyer, keeper of a kitchen that no longer existed — sat alone by a small dishonest fire and waited for morning, and for an answer, and for whatever Zephyrine Voss had aimed them all toward.

Far to the north, in a tower at Graywater, a bell began to ring.

Odile stood up slowly, listening.

Not a warning bell. Not an alarm. It rang in threes, paused, rang in threes — a pattern she hadn't heard since the last purge, a pattern that meant one thing and one thing only:

*They have found another one.*

She looked at the sleeping boy, and for the first time in three centuries, Odile wished she could give a memory back instead of take one — wished she could leave him innocent a little longer.

"Wake up, Xanthe," she said softly. "We're out of time."
