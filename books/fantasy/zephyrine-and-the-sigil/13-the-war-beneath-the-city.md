## 13. The War Beneath the City

The smugglers' tunnel smelled of salt, tar, and somebody's abandoned dinner, and Xanthe was trying very hard not to think about any of it.

"You'll want to mind your head," said Brannic, the broad-shouldered man leading them down, "though I see that's a lesson you've already learned." He gestured at the fresh bruise blooming along Xanthe's hairline where a low beam had introduced itself an hour ago.

"I like to lead with my forehead," Xanthe said. "It disarms people."

Behind him, Sefa — the smuggler-network's quartermaster, a wiry woman with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like a door hinge — snorted. "Disarms them into what?"

"Confusion. Mostly."

They had been walking for hours beneath Vellamare, through passages older than the city above them, and the deeper they went, the more the sigil in his chest pulled. It wasn't pain, exactly. It was *attention* — the sensation of a dog straining at the end of a leash, except the dog was made of every dead witch who had ever carried the mark, and the leash was threaded through his ribs.

He had stopped sleeping properly three nights ago. The dreams came in fragments now instead of floods: a field of white flowers that no one alive had ever planted; a woman singing in a language with no living speakers; the smell of smoke and wet stone and something underneath both, something old and patient and *waiting*.

"It's pulling you," Sefa said quietly. Not a question.

"Like a fisherman who's decided I'm worth the effort."

Brannic glanced back at him. In the lamplight his face was hard to read. "You could still turn around, hedge-witch. Take the southern passage out. Nobody here would stop you." A pause. "Nobody here would *help* you either, mind."

"And there's the famous smuggler hospitality."

"We're hospitable to paying customers and useful allies," Sefa said cheerfully. "You're currently neither, since you refuse to hand over the sigil and refuse to tell us what it does."

"It stores things," Xanthe said, for perhaps the hundredth time. "Memories."

"Memories of whom?"

"That's rather the question, isn't it."

The tunnel opened ahead of them without warning — one moment cramped stone, the next a cavern so vast their lamp-flames shrank to insects' sparks against it. Xanthe stopped walking. The sigil didn't.

It surged against his ribs like a tide coming in, and the memories came with it, and this time he did not so much remember as *fall*.

* * *

He was standing in a valley that no longer existed.

He knew it was a valley because the mountains were on both sides of him, green and folded, and a river ran silver between them, and thousands upon thousands of people were camped along its banks — mages, all of them, in the days before hiding was invented. He knew they were mages because the air itself was thick with working: wards layered over wards, lights that burned without flame, a child juggling stones that orbited her hands like small obedient moons.

And he knew — with the terrible certainty of memory that is not your own but lives in your body anyway — how it ended.

Fire first. Then the sky going wrong. Then the screaming, which lasted, by his count, four days.

Xanthe stood inside the memory and watched the war that every schoolchild in Vellamare had been taught was fought over territory, over succession, over the pride of kings. He watched mages die in ditches holding hands. He watched a circle of nine elders stand their ground on a hill while the crown's armies broke like grey water around them.

And then he watched what those nine elders did when the last of their people lay dying.

They did not run. They did not surrender. They built a cage.

He felt it happen from the inside — felt the sigil take shape under nine pairs of bleeding hands, felt the intention pour into it like molten metal poured into a mold. *We will not let them be lost.* The memories of the slaughtered, torn free of dying minds before the dying finished, gathered, pressed, sealed. Every voice. Every name. The child with her orbiting stones. The old man who had taught three generations to sing rain out of the sky. All of them, kept.

Not preserved, Xanthe understood, with a horror rising in him like floodwater. *Imprisoned.*

Because a memory held in a vessel is not a memory anymore. It is a prisoner. And the nine elders — weeping, half-dead themselves — had chosen imprisonment over erasure, and called it mercy, and died believing it.

The vision shifted. Centuries compressed. He saw the sigil passed hand to hand down generations of carriers, each one drowning a little more in other people's lives, each one passing it on sooner than the last. He saw carriers go mad. He saw carriers go quiet. He saw one carrier, a hundred years ago, try to destroy it on a riverbank — and watched the sigil defend itself, coldly and completely, and understood why Zephyrine had never once told him what she carried.

She hadn't been protecting herself. She'd been protecting him from ever being tempted.

The memory released him like a hand letting go, and Xanthe found himself on his knees on the cavern floor, lamp guttering, Sefa's hand gripping his shoulder, Brannic saying his name in a voice that had lost all its earlier roughness.

"The war," Xanthe managed. His mouth tasted of ash that was four centuries old. "Everyone thinks the war was about land."

Sefa crouched beside him. Her eyes, when he forced himself to meet them, were wide and hungry and afraid all at once. "What was it about?"

"Us." He laughed, and hated the sound. "It was always about us. They slaughtered us, and our elders couldn't bear to let the dead be forgotten, so they — " His voice failed. He tried again. "This thing in my chest isn't an heirloom. It's a prison. And every mage who carried it was a jailer who slowly became part of the collection."

* * *

They made camp at the cavern's edge, because Brannic refused to go further until dawn and Xanthe's legs refused to carry him anywhere at all.

He sat against the wall with his list — the actual paper list now, worn soft at the folds — and read it by lamplight while Sefa pretended not to watch and Brannic actually watched, having given up pretense somewhere around the second hour.

*Things That Are Mine,* the heading read.

1. My sister's lullaby (three verses, the third one wrong on purpose).
2. The smell of Zephyrine's kitchen: rosemary, burnt sugar.
3. I am left-handed.
4. I hate eel pie. (Verified twice.)
5. My name is Xanthe Sandoval.

Item five had been on the list for six weeks now. That it needed to be written down at all was the kind of joke he would have made if he weren't too frightened to make it.

"You want to know the worst part?" he said, to nobody, to everybody.

"No," said Brannic. "But tell us anyway."

"The elders thought they were saving something. Four hundred years ago, that was probably even true. But the sigil doesn't *keep* memories. It keeps them *fresh*. Every carrier feeds it. Every carrier becomes it." He turned the paper over in his hands. "I've got a husband I never married whose face I know better than my father's. There's a dialect in my mouth that died before Vellamare had walls. And somewhere in there are nine elders who built this thinking they were building a memorial, and what they actually built was — "

"A mass grave," Sefa said softly. "With a lock on the inside."

Xanthe looked up sharply. "That's exactly it. How did — "

"Because I've been carrying something smaller my whole life." She held up her ink-stained fingers. "Names. We all do it, in the network. Every mage we lose to the hunts, we write the name down and hide the page. Forty-one pages now." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I used to think we were doing the same thing your elders did. Keeping them alive. Now I'm wondering if we're just digging a very tidy grave."

"No," Xanthe said, and surprised himself with how fast it came. "A list you can *choose* to read is different. A name on a hidden page can be spoken aloud someday. That's not a prison, Sefa. That's a seed waiting for spring."

"And the sigil?"

The sigil pulsed against his ribs, patient as stone. He realized, with a lurch, that it had been listening. It always listened. That was rather its entire nature.

"The sigil," he said carefully, "is four hundred years of good intentions that curdled. And I don't know yet whether you cure a thing like that or burn it."

* * *

Dawn, such as it was underground, arrived as a faint greying of the distant air, and they went on.

The pull strengthened with every step until Xanthe no longer walked so much as was carried, his feet finding the way while his conscious mind trailed behind like a coat on a hook. Brannic and Sefa followed with the lamps, and neither of them spoke, which told Xanthe everything about how he must look — pale, sweating, murmuring in dead languages under his breath.

The passage ended at a wall.

Not stone. Well — stone, but worked stone, fitted blocks taller than two men, carved over every inch with script that Xanthe's borrowed memories read as easily as a market notice. A warning, in the old tongue:

*HERE LIE THE VOICES OF THE TAKEN. NINE HANDS SEALED THEM. LET NO ONE HAND OPEN THEM.*

Beneath the script, at the exact height of a human heart, was a recess. Empty. Shaped unmistakably like a sigil.

"Oh," Xanthe said. His voice came out very small in the vast dark. "Oh, that's not good."

"What is it?" Sefa's lamp swung toward him. "What does it say?"

"It says this is where the cage was built. And it says — " He touched the empty recess, and the sigil in his chest answered with a pulse so strong his vision whited at the edges. "It says the cage has come home. Or wants to."

Brannic set his lamp down slowly. When he spoke, his voice was careful in the way of a man approaching a spooked horse. "The rebels wanted it as a banner. The crown wants it destroyed or owned. And you're telling me it just wants to go back to its hole in the wall?"

"No." Xanthe pressed his palm flat against the carved stone. Cold bled through his skin, and behind the cold, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something stirred. "It doesn't want to go back. Going back means being alone again. Sealed. It's spent four centuries learning to want the opposite." He swallowed. "It wants an audience. It wants *out*. And it brought me here to show me the door."

He turned to face them, and saw in the lamplight that Sefa had gone rigid — staring past him, at the wall, her lips moving.

"Sefa?"

"Those carvings," she whispered. "The names. There are *names* in it. Thousands of them." She looked at him, and her hunger was gone, burned away, leaving only grief. "Your mass grave has a guest register."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "And last night I dreamed in the voice of a woman named Maren who could sing weather, and this morning I woke up missing her, and I never met her, and I will grieve her for the rest of my life because someone four hundred years ago couldn't let go." He laughed again, that awful laugh. "Do you understand? I'm not haunted. I'm *occupied*."

The sigil pulsed. And this time, for the first time, it pulsed in answer to something outside itself — because far off in the dark, down the passage they had come by, lamps were appearing. Many lamps. Moving fast.

Brannic swore with the fluency of a career smuggler. "That'll be the others. Kestrel's people were supposed to signal if anyone followed you from the Salt Market." He grabbed his lamp. "Somebody talked."

"Somebody always talks," Sefa said grimly, snuffing hers.

Through the sigil, Xanthe felt something else arriving with the footsteps — a familiar wrongness, a taste of magic like copper and old snow, sliding through the dark toward him with the unhurried confidence of a creditor.

Odile.

She had said she would take the sigil when it was ripe. Standing before the cage-wall with his heart hammering and pursuit closing in, Xanthe understood, with terrible clarity, that he was standing in the orchard.

"They're coming," he said. "Both problems. All of them." He put his hand over his own chest, over the thing that wore his heart like a rented room. "And I have maybe an hour to decide what four hundred years of ghosts get to be. A grave, a banner, or a key."

Above them, muffled by forty feet of ancient stone, a bell began to ring in the city of Vellamare.

The sigil, warm against his palm, began — very softly — to hum along.
