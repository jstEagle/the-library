## 14. Things That Are Mine

The list lived in the false bottom of Xanthe's satchel, beneath a layer of dried lavender and three charm-stones he no longer trusted himself to make.

*Things That Are Mine,* it began, in his own handwriting — or what he believed was his own handwriting. That had become the first uncertainty, actually: the slant of his letters. He had sat up one night in the loft above the smugglers' safehouse at the Salt Market, holding two pages side by side, one written last spring and one written only days after the sigil had chosen him, trying to decide whether the hand had changed because grief had changed it or because someone else's wrist was guiding the pen.

He had not solved it. He had added an entry instead: *My handwriting is mine.* Then, after a moment, he had crossed it out.

That was the trouble with lists. They were supposed to be anchors, and instead they kept turning into questions with ink on them.

It was early still — gray light through the loft's one window, gulls arguing over the harbor, the smell of salt fish and tar that had become, over the past weeks, almost like home — when he took the list out and read it again. He did this every morning now. It was the first thing he did before anything else, before tea, before checking that Odile hadn't left some new gift of a dead sparrow on the windowsill (she did this; he had never asked why; he suspected asking would only encourage her). The ritual went: wake, touch the satchel, read the list, confirm that he was still mostly himself, then get up.

Some mornings the confirmation took longer than others.

*Things That Are Mine.*

*1. My name is Xanthe Sandoval.*

That one held. It held every time, and he had learned to feel a small, stubborn pride in it, the way a man might take pride in a fence post that had survived a flood.

*2. I was born in Vellamare, in the Lantern District, third house past the chandler's.*

Held. He could still smell the wax if he tried. Could still hear the bells of the morning tide.

*3. My mother mended nets. My father drowned when I was six.*

Held. Mostly. Though lately when he thought of his father he thought of him standing in a wheat field, and his father had never in his life stood in a wheat field; they were a harbor family, three generations deep in brine. But the image came anyway, warm and golden and wrong, wearing his father's face like a borrowed coat.

*4. I have a sister. Maren. She sang me to sleep with a song about a boat made of moonlight.*

Held. That one had always held. It was the deepest thing he had, the bedrock entry, the one all the others were measured against. When the sigil's memories crowded too close — when he woke weeping for a husband named Tomas whom he had never married, in a dialect that had died with its last speaker two hundred years ago, in a village whose stones had been carried off to build a cathedral — he would lie in the dark and sing Maren's song to himself, quietly, and the strange borrowed griefs would loosen their grip like hands unclenching.

*5. Zephyrine taught me everything worth knowing and half of what was dangerous.*

Held. That one hurt to hold, but he held it anyway.

He worked down the rest. *I am left-handed. I hate eel. I once stole a peach from the godmother of the harbormaster and have felt guilty about it for eleven years.* These were small things, pebble-things, but pebbles held too, and lately he had come to appreciate pebbles. The great sweeping memories of the sigil's inheritance — centuries of them, mage after mage, love after loss after burning — those were oceans. You could not hold an ocean. But a stolen peach. An aversion to eel. A left hand. Those you could carry.

Except.

Halfway down the page, his eye caught on an entry he had been circling for days the way your tongue circles a loose tooth.

*9. I am afraid of deep water.*

He stopped reading. Set the list on his knee. Looked out the window at the harbor without seeing it.

Here was the problem, laid out plain as a dropped knife: Xanthe Sandoval, son of a net-mender, grandson of a net-mender, born three houses from the chandler's in a city built entirely on top of the sea — was afraid of deep water?

Maybe. Probably even. Plenty of harbor children feared deep water; the sea gave back so few of what it took. But he could not remember *being* afraid of it. He could not summon the memory that would explain the fear — no near-drowning, no body pulled up tangled in the nets, nothing. And meanwhile, entry by entry, the sigil had been filling him with people who had excellent reasons to fear deep water. There was a woman named Serafina, four inheritances back, who had watched the king's men sink a boatload of mages with ballast stones. There was a boy whose name Xanthe didn't even know anymore, who had hidden in a flooded cellar while hunters searched overhead, treading water for hours in the dark.

So: was entry nine a fact about Xanthe Sandoval, or was it an echo? Had he written down something true about himself, or had the sigil slipped it into his pen?

And the worse question, the one underneath, the one he had been not-asking since the night he'd started the list: if he couldn't tell which entries were his, how long until he couldn't tell which ones weren't?

"You're doing the face," said a voice from the ladder.

Xanthe flinched hard enough to nearly drop the list. Toma — the smugglers' youngest runner, fourteen years old, all elbows and insolence — hung halfway up through the loft floor, hair like a storm survivor.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're arguing with somebody invisible and losing." Toma hauled himself the rest of the way up, deposited a tray of bread, hard cheese, and tea that smelled faintly of the ship's hold, and eyed the papers scattered around Xanthe's bedroll. "More homework? Auntie Vess says you've been writing since before dawn."

"It's research," Xanthe said, sliding the list under his thigh as casually as a man hiding evidence, which, in a sense, he was.

"Research." Toma pronounced the word as though tasting something suspicious. "You're the only fugitive I ever met who does homework. The hunters come, and you fight them off with footnotes."

"There are worse weapons." Xanthe poured the tea. It tasted of tar. Everything here tasted of tar. He had decided weeks ago that this was one of the things he liked about the Salt Market, and then had spent a full hour wondering whether he'd actually decided that or simply remembered deciding it, and had given himself a headache, and had gone to bed. "Anything moving outside?"

"The usual. Fishermen pretending not to be smugglers, smugglers pretending not to be fishermen. Oh—" Toma paused at the ladder, and something in his voice changed, lost its showman's bounce. "There's a gray-eyed woman asked after you at the chandlery last night. Not the thief. Older. Wore a crown's badge under her shawl, and everyone pretended very hard not to see it."

Xanthe's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, like a stone through harbor water. "Did she give a name?"

"She said she'd come back." Toma shrugged, recovering his bounce, and dropped down through the floor. "Everybody comes back eventually. That's what my mum always says about the sea, anyway."

He meant to spend the day on useful work. He had charms to examine — carefully, now, with the constant low hum of the sigil under his skin making everything he touched want to do *more* than he intended — and a meeting with Auntie Vess about southern passages, and a dozen sensible tasks. Instead he sat in the loft until noon with the list spread before him like a patient on an operating table, and did the thing he had been dreading.

He tested himself against Maren's lullaby.

It seemed simple enough, in theory. The song was his. It predated the sigil by twenty years. Whatever else the inheritance had flooded him with, it could not touch the one true thing — his sister's voice in the dark of their attic room, singing about a boat made of moonlight sailing down the Milky River to fetch the sun home in the morning. He had sung it to himself a hundred times since Graywater. It had never failed him.

But he had begun to notice, in the last few days, that when he sang it, there were *other songs* underneath it. Old ones. Songs in dead tongues, cradle-songs of women four hundred years drowned, all of them doing the same work his sister's song did — soothing a child, promising the night would end — layered under his like sediment under topsoil. And he had begun to wonder whether he could still find the topsoil. Whether he knew where it ended and the sediment began.

So he sat cross-legged on the loft boards with his eyes shut, and he sang it from memory, and he paid attention the way Zephyrine had taught him to pay attention — *not to what you expect, boy; expectations are just memories wearing armor* — and for the first verse, everything was fine.

*A little boat of moonbeams bright,*
*sails down the river of the night...*

Fine. True. His sister's voice was in it, thin and sweet and slightly off-key on *bright*, the way she always was, bless her.

Second verse. Also fine.

Third verse—

Third verse, he sang a line that stopped him cold.

*...and if you wake before the dawn,*
*the ferryman will carry on—*

He opened his eyes.

That wasn't right.

It wasn't that the line was wrong, exactly. It scanned. It rhymed. It fit the melody perfectly, and moreover it *felt* correct, felt worn smooth with use the way only a much-sung line can feel. But Maren's song had never had a ferryman in it. The boat of moonlight sailed alone; that was the whole point of it, that was the thing that had comforted him as a child — the little boat needed no one, feared no dark, would bring the sun home all by itself. There had never been a ferryman.

He sang it again. *The ferryman will carry on.* Again, hunting for the real line underneath, the way you dig for something dropped in sand.

He found it. Or he found *a* it:

*...and if you wake before the dawn,*
*the moonboat waits and won't be gone.*

Was that it? It sounded like it. It *almost* sounded like it. But so had the ferryman, and now both versions sat in his mind with equal weight, equal smoothness, and he understood, sitting there in the dusty noon light of a smuggler's loft, that he had no way to choose between them. No way except Maren, and Maren was in Vellamare, in the Lantern District, and he could not go to Vellamare, and even if he did — even if he stood in her doorway and heard her sing it — what then? What if she sang the ferryman version? What if the sigil's reach was longer than he feared and had touched more than him?

He put his head in his hands.

It was not a dramatic grief. That was the terrible part. Nobody died; nothing burned. He simply sat there on the floor of a loft that smelled of tar, holding the knowledge, letting it settle over him gently as harbor fog: *I got my sister's lullaby wrong.* The one thing. The bedrock. The entry that all other entries were measured against — and it had a ferryman in it that had never been there, or maybe had always been there, and he could not know which, and not-knowing turned out to be its own particular kind of loss, quieter than the others and somehow deeper.

Somewhere below, the gulls went on arguing. Somewhere across the city, presumably, Maren was alive and well and singing the song correctly to children of her own, and had no idea her brother was dissolving one verse at a time.

He thought of Zephyrine. He thought of her often, but he thought of her now with a new and awful specificity — because he finally understood, in his bones, what she must have known when she pressed the sigil toward him from inside her cell. She had known this. She had known exactly what she was handing him: not a treasure, not a weapon, but an ocean, and she had chosen to drown him in it anyway rather than let the Inquisition have it. She had loved him and doomed him in the same motion, and he had spent weeks being angry at her, and now, sitting among his own unreliable memories, he found the anger wouldn't come. Only a vast, tired tenderness, and the thought: *she must have been so tired, at the end. So tired of carrying.*

"I'm still me," he said aloud, to the empty loft. It came out smaller than he intended. "My name is Xanthe Sandoval. I'm left-handed. I hate eel. I stole a peach."

The loft declined to confirm any of it. The loft was not a reliable witness either, these days.

He picked up the pen. Below the last entry, in a hand he chose to believe was his own, he wrote:

*15. The song has no ferryman.*

Then he looked at it for a long time, crossed it out, and wrote instead:

*15. If the song has a ferryman, I will learn to love the ferryman. It can be mine too.*

It was either wisdom or surrender. He genuinely could not tell, and he was beginning to suspect that distinction mattered less than he'd once thought.

He was blowing on the ink when the ladder creaked.

Not Toma — Toma announced himself with the subtlety of a rockslide. This ascent was slow, measured, deliberate, the climb of a man who wanted to be heard coming. Xanthe slid the list into the satchel and stood, heart hammering, one hand already curling with the sigil's heat.

A head appeared through the hatch. Gray eyes. Streaked silver hair. A face he had seen across interrogation-room memories and dreamed about in Zephyrine's cell, drawn and tired and wearing an expression that no witch-hunter should ever wear —

Inquisitor Corvane pulled himself into the loft, alone, unarmed, and said:

"Before you burn me where I stand, apprentice — I need to tell you what I found in Zephyrine's cell. Because it wasn't you she wrote her last letter to. It was me."
