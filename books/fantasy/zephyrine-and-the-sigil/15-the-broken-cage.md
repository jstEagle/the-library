## 15. The Broken Cage

Inquisitor Corvane had conducted one hundred and forty-three interrogations in his nineteen years of service to the crown, and he had a private taxonomy for them, the way other men collected coins or sins.

There were the Weepers, who confessed to everything including crimes that had not yet been invented. There were the Screamers, whose noise he endured with the patience of a man waiting for rain to stop. There were the Stolid ones, who said nothing at all and were, in their way, the most honest. And there was a rare fourth kind — perhaps four or five in his whole career — who treated the interrogation as though *he* were the one being examined, turning his questions over like a jeweler assessing paste stones, and answering only what amused them.

Zephyrine of Graywater was the finest of the fourth kind he had ever encountered.

She sat in her cell in the Saltspire's deepest level — they had moved her down after she'd charmed the bars of the first cell into singing sea shanties until three guards threatened resignation — with her hands folded in her lap and her white hair braided back with what appeared to be actual twine, because even in custody, apparently, the woman could not be persuaded to use proper cord.

"Madam," Corvane said, "for the eleventh time. The sigil. Where is it?"

"Where is *what*?" She smiled at him. She had been smiling at him for six days, and each smile was slightly different, and each one made him feel as though he'd walked into a room mid-conversation.

"The mark. The living sigil that has passed through every mage of your line since before the war."

"Ah." She nodded thoughtfully. "You mean my grandmother's grandmother's trouble. My inheritance. My *guest*." Her eyes, gray as harbor water, fixed on him with terrible gentleness. "You're asking me where I've hidden a hundred lifetimes of memory, Inquisitor. You might as well ask where I've hidden the tide."

"I am asking you where you have hidden evidence of treason."

"And I'm telling you," she said, "that the tide doesn't hide. It simply goes out, and comes back, and drowns whatever was foolish enough to build on the flats."

Corvane wrote this down, because he wrote everything down. It was the discipline that had made his career: the ledger mind, the cold page. Other witch-hunters trusted instinct, informants, the hot certainty of righteous fear. Corvane trusted ink. A thing written down could be checked. A thing written down did not flatter itself.

He wrote: *Subject continues to respond to direct questions with maritime metaphor.*

Underneath, because he was honest with himself if no one else: *Subject is winning.*

* * *

The trouble — and he had identified it on the second day, and it had been growing like mold in a wall ever since — was the question.

Not her questions. His own.

The file on Zephyrine of Graywater was thick as a hymnal, and Corvane had read it the way he read everything: twice, once for content and once for what was missing. What was missing was an apprentice. Twelve years ago, a boy of fifteen had vanished from her cottage the same season the Registry first flagged her. Name stricken from the record by some clerk's efficient quill. Age now twenty-seven. Gift unconfirmed but statistically probable — apprentices did not simply stop being apprentices; magic was not a coat one hung by the door.

Corvane had pulled the old census rolls himself, working late with a lamp and a pot of tea going bitter, and found the gap where the name should have been. Someone had struck it. Someone careful. He had sat looking at that small black line for a long while, feeling something he declined to name stir behind his sternum.

Because here was the question, the one that would not fold itself away into the ledger:

*Why had the boy run?*

Apprentices ran when masters died, or when masters were cruel, or when masters were about to be caught. But the timing was wrong. Zephyrine had not been caught for twelve more years. If the boy had fled her arrest, he'd have fled twelve years too early. If he'd fled her cruelty, why had neighbors' accounts — which Corvane had collected in exhaustive detail — described the two of them arguing cheerfully over herb prices at the Graywater market like an old married couple?

Which left the third option, and Corvane did not like it, because the third option was that the boy had run to protect the sigil, which meant Zephyrine had *sent* him, which meant that twelve years ago a hedge-witch had looked at the shape of the coming hunt more clearly than the entire Office of Inquiry, and had planned around it with the patience of a woman planting an orchard she'd never sit under.

And if she could see that far —

What else had she seen?

* * *

On the ninth day, she stopped eating.

Not dramatically. No hunger strike declared, no speeches. She simply began leaving most of the bread, and thanked the guards so sweetly for bringing it that two of them came to Corvane separately, troubled, to ask if the prisoner was ill.

"She's fine," Corvane said, and then went down to see for himself.

She was sitting by the slit window — a finger's width of gray light, high up, showing nothing but sky — with her hands folded and her face turned toward it, and for a moment, watching her, Corvane felt the strange vertigo of seeing a person become a monument. There are faces built for portraits and faces built for doorways, and hers had always been a doorway face, ordinary and warm, the sort of face you forgot and then missed. Now it was becoming stone, and the stone was smiling.

"You're dying," he said.

"I'm *finishing*," she said. "There's a difference. You of all people should appreciate precision, Inquisitor."

"Food will be administered if necessary."

"Force-fed witches." She clicked her tongue. "Your office has such a gift for making martyrs. Honestly, if you wanted the gifted loved by the commons, you couldn't design a better method than strapping us to tables and pouring gruel down our throats. Half of Vellamare would be casting charms within the year just to qualify."

"Madam—"

"Sit down, Aldous."

The use of his given name went through him like a needle finding a nerve he'd forgotten he owned. He had not told her his given name. He checked — he was certain, he *checked*, replaying six days of transcript in his head — and he had not told her his given name.

"How do you know that?"

"Oh, sit *down*," she said, "and I'll tell you a story, and you can decide afterward whether to write it in your little book. Though I warn you, it won't fit. Some things are bigger than pages. That's rather the whole point of them."

He sat. He told himself it was interrogation technique. He was aware, distantly, that he was lying to himself, and he filed the awareness away to be dealt with never.

"Once," Zephyrine said, "there was a girl in Vellamare who could make bread rise with a song. Not much of a gift. Useful, though. The kind of gift that keeps a family fed through a bad harvest, if nobody asks why your loaves come out perfect every time." Her eyes stayed on the window. "She married a cooper. Had a daughter. Sang to the dough every morning, quiet, with the shutters closed. And one winter the hunt came through the tanners' quarter, and someone — a neighbor, a friend, someone she'd lent flour to — remembered the perfect loaves, and mentioned them, and that was that. Fire and a shallow grave. Standard procedure. You'd know the forms. You sign them."

Corvane's pen had stopped moving.

"The daughter survived," Zephyrine went on. "Married a chandler. Had a son. And that son grew up with his mother's stories about a grandmother who sang to bread, and he grew up angry, and angry boys with clean handwriting get recruited by offices like yours, because the crown loves nothing so much as a hound that hates the scent it's tracking." She finally turned to look at him, and her expression was not triumphant, which was somehow worse. It was sorrowful. It was the look of a woman watching weather come in over a field she'd planted. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, Aldous Corvane?"

"No," he said, and his voice came out wrong.

"Your grandmother's mother sang to bread." She let it land. "You are quarry by blood, Inquisitor. You have spent nineteen years hunting your own family, and doing it *well*, and I am sorry for it, truly, because you seem to be a man who values competence, and there is no competence in that. Only obedience wearing competence's coat."

"That's a lie," Corvane said. "A manipulation. Textbook. You're attempting to destabilize the interrogator through fabricated personal history. It's in the manual. I helped revise the manual."

"Mm," said Zephyrine. "Check the parish records of Saint Ferrel's, tanners' quarter. Burned in the last purge, yes — but the chandler's guild kept duplicates, because chandlers are careful people, wax being what it is. Ask for the marriage entry of Maren Ostrewey, year of the Gray Frost. Then come back and tell me I lied." She settled back against the wall, and the smile returned, small and tired and absolutely certain. "Take your time. I'm not going anywhere. Well. I'm going somewhere. But not quickly, and not anywhere you can follow."

* * *

He checked.

That was the thing about Corvane that his superiors mistook for loyalty and that was actually something colder and more dangerous: he could not leave a question unanswered. It was a sickness. It had cost him sleep, friendships, one engagement. He requisitioned a horse and rode to the chandler's guild hall in the lower city under a pretext so thin he didn't bother making it convincing, and a myopic guild clerk with ink-stained fingers fetched the duplicate register, and there it was, in brown fading ink, in a careful chandler's hand:

*Maren Ostrewey, daughter of Lise the baker, wed to Tomas Corvane, cooper's son.*

*Lise the baker.*

Lise, who had sung to bread. Lise, who had been burned in the winter of the Gray Frost, in the last great hunt, the one the histories called regrettable and necessary in the same breath.

Corvane stood in the guild hall for a long time. The clerk asked twice if he was quite well. Rain started outside, the soft gray rain of Vellamare autumns, and he walked out into it without his hood up, which he had never once done in nineteen years, because a man's hood was part of the uniform and the uniform was the man.

His grandmother. His grandmother's mother. The blood in his veins had been outlawed before he was born, and he had spent his life pouring it out on the ground for the men who'd outlawed it, and calling it service.

He thought of the struck name in the census roll. He thought of the boy, the runaway apprentice, and understood suddenly — with the sick lurch of a floor changing angles — that Zephyrine had not told him this to break him. Or not only. She had told him because she needed him to understand what was coming, and why, and what side of it he was standing on. She had planted him like she planted everything. Like an orchard she'd never sit under.

* * *

She died on the eleventh day, in the morning, between one guard's rounds and the next.

They found her sitting upright, hands folded, facing the little window and its finger of gray light. The physician said her heart had simply stopped, as hearts do, and there was nothing suspicious in it, and Corvane signed the death certificate with a hand that did not shake, because he was a professional, and professionals' hands do not shake, and if anyone had looked closely they might have seen how much effort that took.

She was smiling. Of course she was smiling. Every smile different, and this one — the guards reported it uneasily, crossing themselves — this one looked like a woman who has heard the punchline of a very long joke, and found it good.

It was while overseeing the inventory of her effects — a ritual he performed personally, always, because personal effects were where prisoners hid truths — that he found it.

Her cell had yielded almost nothing: the twine from her hair, a wooden button, a prayer card to a saint the Church had deposed two centuries ago. But sewn into the hem of her spare shift, in stitches so small they must have taken weeks, working by the finger of window-light, was writing.

He unpicked it with a knife, alone, late, lamp low, and spread the strip of cloth flat on his desk, and read.

*To Aldous Corvane, who checks.*

*I knew you would check. It is why I chose you. Not chose to harm — do not flatter yourself, we have met four times before today and you remember none of them, and I remember all of them, and you were kind twice, which for an Inquisitor is practically sainthood.*

*You are Lise's blood. So am I, three lines over — the bakers and the witches of Graywater were always cousins, dear, the whole coast is knotted together like that, which is precisely what the crown fears and why the hunts never end. They cannot kill a family tree by burning branches.*

*I am dying on my own schedule, as is my right, and the sigil has gone where I sent it, and you cannot follow it and neither can your masters, and by the time you finish being furious about that sentence you will already have decided what to do next, because you are a man who cannot leave a question unanswered, and I have left you the largest one of your life:*

*Ask what really happened at the war. Not what the histories say. Go beneath the citadel. Read what the crown buried, and then decide whether your oath serves the crown, or the truth, or the dead — because those are three different masters, Aldous, and you have been serving the wrong one.*

*You are quarry by blood. Be something better by choice.*

*With love, and apology, and no regret whatsoever,*
*Z.*

Corvane sat for a long time. The lamp burned. Somewhere above, the Saltspire bells rang the hour, indifferent.

Then he opened his ledger — the great one, the career one, nineteen years of cold ink — to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in his precise hand:

*Questions the crown does not want answered.*

And below it: *1. The war.*

Below that, after a pause, his pen hovering: *2. The apprentice. Find him first. Protect him.*

He looked at the word *protect* and did not cross it out.

Outside, the rain kept falling on Vellamare, soft and gray, washing the streets the way rain does — carrying things downhill, toward the sea, toward Graywater, toward wherever a runaway apprentice was sleeping badly with a dead woman's memories folded inside him like letters he hadn't yet dared open.

Corvane put on his hat, took his ledger, and went to find him.
