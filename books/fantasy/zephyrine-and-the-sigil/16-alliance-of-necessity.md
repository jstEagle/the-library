## 16. Alliance of Necessity

The lighthouse keeper's cottage at Graywater Point had been empty for nine years, which was why Xanthe had chosen it. Empty cottages asked no questions. This one had a leak in the roof directly over the bed, a stove that smoked like an old man complaining, and a view of the sea that Zephyrine would have called "good for the soul" and Xanthe called "good for spotting witch-hunters coming up the coast road."

He was watching that road when he saw him.

Inquisitor Corvane did not sneak. That was the first thing Xanthe noticed, crouched behind the salt-crusted window with his heart attempting to exit through his ears. The man walked up the cliff path in the gray morning light at the pace of a clerk going to collect a debt — unhurried, inevitable, hat squared against the wind. No soldiers behind him. No chains rattling in a saddlebag. Just Corvane, alone, carrying a ledger under one arm like a schoolmaster's primer.

Xanthe considered the back door. He considered the window. He considered, briefly, whether the sigil could teach him to fly, and got back a memory of a woman named Aldith who had tried it off a tower in 311 and whose opinion on the matter was, understandably, unavailable.

So he did the only sensible thing. He put the kettle on.

If a man was going to arrest him, let it happen while the tea was brewing. Zephyrine had taught him that too, though she'd meant it as a joke about dignity. *Never be taken looking frightened, boy. Be taken looking annoyed. It confuses them.*

He'd just set out two cups before he realized what he'd done and stood there glaring at them, betrayed by his own hospitality, when the knock came.

It was, absurdly, a polite knock. Three measured raps, the kind you'd give a neighbor about a fence.

Xanthe opened the door.

"Inquisitor," he said.

"Apprentice." Corvane looked past him into the cottage, cataloguing everything — the stove, the cups, the list pinned to the wall that Xanthe snatched down half a heartbeat too late. Something flickered across the man's face. Not triumph. Curiosity, maybe, or something wearing curiosity's coat. "You've made tea."

"It seemed rude not to. You walked so far."

"I did." Corvane stepped inside without being invited, because men like him never waited for invitations; invitations were for people who might say no. He hung his dripping hat on the peg by the door — hung it *carefully*, straightening the brim, and Xanthe hated that he noticed — and sat down at the table like a man settling in for a long winter. "Your master kept a cottage like this. Smaller. Worse tea."

"You've been inside her cottage?"

"I searched it. After." Corvane's voice didn't change, but something behind it did, the way ice shifts over a pond before it breaks. "The hearthstone was split. She'd hidden things there for thirty years. I found receipts, letters, a child's drawing of a cat with wings." He paused. "The cat had wings."

"She drew all her cats with wings. Said cats were wasted without them."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The kettle began its slow climb toward whistling. Rain ticked against the glass. And Xanthe stood in his own kitchen, hands flat on the table, facing the man who had caged Zephyrine — who had put her in a cell at the citadel where she was, right now, this very hour, sitting in the dark giving him nothing but riddles while her memories bled slowly into a runaway's hands.

He should hate him. He did hate him. But hatred, he was learning, was a poor thing to steer by. It kept wanting to look at details: how tired Corvane's eyes were. How the ledger under his arm had swollen with rain. How a witch-hunter came alone, unarmed, to a known mage's hideout, which was either arrogance or desperation, and Corvane did not look like an arrogant man. He looked like a man who had been awake for several nights doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Say whatever you came to say," Xanthe said. "Then leave."

"I came to bargain."

"You don't bargain. You arrest."

"Today I bargain." Corvane laid the ledger on the table between them, and did not open it. His hand stayed flat on the cover the way a man keeps a hand on a dog that might bite. "Before I tell you what it is, I want you to understand that I have spent eleven days verifying every word of it. I do not deal in guesses. Guesses are how innocents burn."

"How thoughtful of you."

"Yes," said Corvane, with no irony whatsoever. "It is my entire profession."

* * *

Here is what Corvane told him, while the tea went cold in its cups:

That he had been a witch-hunter for nineteen years. That he had taken the office believing, sincerely, completely, in the story every child in Vellamare learned at their mother's knee: that three hundred years ago, mages had turned their gifts on the crown, that the War of the Sigil had been fought to stop them, that the registry and the hunts existed so that such a war could never come again. That order required vigilance. That vigilance required him.

"And then your master began talking to me," he said. "Riddles, mostly. I told you that when I took her."

"She does love riddles."

"One of them has been sitting in me for eleven days like a swallowed stone." Corvane opened the ledger at last, to a page near the back, dense with his small precise handwriting. "She said: *Ask the archive what happened at Vaelmoor Ford.* So I did. Quietly. Through channels that will not report back to the chancery." He turned the ledger around.

Xanthe read. And the sigil, folded asleep beneath his skin, woke and *read with him*, and he felt the memories stir like birds in a hedgerow — because Vaelmoor Ford was in here, in the inheritance, in the mass grave of stored lives, and the sigil knew the ford the way a scar knows the knife.

What the crown's official history said, in the Annals, in the schoolroom charts, in the painted frieze in the Hall of Concord: at Vaelmoor Ford, the rebel mages made their last stand and were destroyed, and from their ruin the wise king ordered built the great seal that would bind wild magic forever.

What Corvane had found, in a quartermaster's dispatch, in a paymaster's tally, in a soldier's letter home that someone had preserved by accident among grain inventories: at Vaelmoor Ford, the mages were not destroyed. They were *surrendering*. They had laid down their charms and knelt. And the king's order — the sigil — was not a binding of wild magic at all.

It was a prison for *them*. For their minds, their memories, everything they were. Built not to end magic, but to own it. To keep the dead from being dead, so their knowledge could be drawn upon, hoarded, controlled. Every mage slaughtered in that war and every mage purged since — the sigil drank them. The crown had been farming its enemies' souls for three hundred years.

"The sigil they took from Zephyrine," Xanthe said quietly. "The one they're holding in the citadel vault."

"A copy. A shell. She'd already passed the true one on — to you, I believe. The shell sits in the vault glowing prettily while the chancery congratulates itself." Corvane's mouth did something that was almost, almost a smile, and had clearly not practiced lately. "Which brings us to the bargain."

Xanthe sat back. His hands were not entirely steady. In the inheritance, a woman named Sera — no, that was Sera's memory of her grandmother — no, it went back further, it always went back further — someone who had *knelt at that ford*, someone whose knees remembered wet grass and the smell of the river, was weeping in a language three hundred years dead. He pressed his palms to the table until the wood bit.

"Why?" he said. "Why do you care? Your whole life is that lie. If it falls, you fall with it."

"Yes." Corvane said it plainly, the way other men say *it's raining*. "When I verified the dispatches, I sat in my office until the candles burned out. Then I wrote my resignation. Then I burned it. Then I thought about my grandmother."

The rain filled the silence.

"Her name was Maren Corvane," he said. "She died when I was six. My mother never spoke of her. Last week I requested the registry archives of the purge of 289, the one before mine, the one they call the Quiet Correction." His jaw tightened. "Maren Corvane is listed. Page forty-one. A hedge-healer in the Fenmark. Her crime was a charm to keep milk sweet. There is a note beside her name in the registrar's hand." He recited it without looking down, and Xanthe understood he had read it a thousand times: *'Assets transferred to the seal.'*

"They fed her to it," Xanthe whispered.

"They fed her to it," Corvane agreed. "And I have spent nineteen years pulling people toward that same door, and calling it justice." He closed the ledger. "So. The bargain."

* * *

The bargain was this:

Corvane could not expose the lie himself. An inquisitor's testimony, however damning, could be buried — he had buried enough testimony to know exactly how deep the hole went. What it needed was proof no chancery clerk could dismiss. Proof that moved. Proof that spoke.

"The sigil," Corvane said, "contains them. Every soul the crown ever poured into it. If that could be shown — if the memories themselves could be witnessed —"

"Witnessed by whom? The court? The court *built* it."

"Not the court." Corvane leaned forward, and for the first time Xanthe saw the crack running through all that certainty, hairline-thin, running the whole length of the man. "Soldiers. Clerks. Dockworkers. The people whose grandmothers are in there. You cannot imprison the truth once ten thousand people have watched it walk past them in the street. I know how power protects itself, apprentice. I have been its fist. It protects itself with secrecy and with smallness — one witness here, one document there, each deniable. So we take away both. All at once. Publicly."

Xanthe laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "You're asking me to break the most guarded secret in the kingdom in front of a crowd. You're asking me —" he spread his hands, and the sigil stirred under his skin, restless, *interested*, and he hated that too — "you're asking *this* to stand in a public square and open itself like a book."

"I'm asking you to help me choose the moment and the means. I am not asking you to do it tomorrow."

"And in exchange?"

"The hunt ends." Corvane said it simply. "Not pardons — pardons can be revoked, I've revoked them. An ending. The registry dissolved. The hunts recalled. The sigil's contents released to everyone or to no one — that choice, I think, is yours and no one else's. In exchange, you help me bring the lie down in daylight, where it can't quietly grow back."

"That's it? You'd throw away nineteen years?"

"I threw them away eleven days ago," said Corvane. "This is just the paperwork."

Xanthe looked at him for a long time. The kettle had gone cold. Outside, the gulls were arguing about something over the gray water, and the light through the salt-fogged window was the color of old pewter, and somewhere south of here a woman named Odile was probably also thinking about the sigil, and somewhere north a woman named Zephyrine was sitting in a cell smiling at riddles, and every path led through this tired, dangerous man at his table.

"If this is a trap," Xanthe said slowly, "it's a good one. I'll grant you that. Walk in unarmed, trade your whole career for my trust — very elegant."

"Do you think I enjoy this?"

"No. That's the problem. If you enjoyed it I'd know what to do."

Corvane almost smiled again. It suited him poorly, like a borrowed coat. "Your master said something similar. When I arrested her, she looked at me and said, *You'll be along after him eventually, Inquisitor. They always send the ones who ask questions.*" He paused. "I didn't understand her then."

"And now?"

"Now I think she arranged all of this from inside a cell, and I resent it deeply, and it changes nothing."

Despite everything — despite the grief and the fear and the dead woman's tears still pooling somewhere under his ribs — Xanthe felt something in his chest loosen, just slightly. Not forgiveness. Forgiveness was a country he wasn't ready to emigrate to. But maybe a shared border. Maybe a bridge under guard.

"Conditions," he said. "One: you never touch the sigil. You don't hold it, study it, catalog it. It's not evidence. It's *people*."

"Agreed."

"Two: Zephyrine walks free before anything else happens. Before the square, before the announcement, before any of it. First."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — regret, or its cousin. "I can petition for her transfer to softer custody within the week. Full release requires the case resolved. If I release her now, the chancery replaces me with someone who doesn't ask questions, and we both hang."

Xanthe wanted to shout. Wanted to put his fist through the pewter light. Instead he heard Zephyrine in his head, dry as chalk: *Take the half-loaf, boy. Half a loaf feeds you today, and tomorrow you steal the baker's cart.*

"Softer custody," he said at last, grinding it out. "With visitors. Me specifically."

"Done. I'll escort you myself."

"Three." He hesitated. This one was harder to say, because it meant admitting it aloud. "There's a woman. Odile. She steals memories — she's been following me since Graywater, and she wants the sigil, and when it's — when she thinks it's ripe, she'll take it. If your hunt ends and hers doesn't, I'll be trading one cage for another. Find out what she is. Who holds *her* leash."

Corvane wrote it in the ledger without question, and Xanthe watched the pen move and marveled, distantly, that he was sitting here making terms with the fist of the crown like a merchant haggling over cloth.

"Four," said Xanthe. "When this is done — when it's all done — you stand in front of whoever remains of the court, and you say her name. Maren Corvane. Out loud. On the record."

The pen stopped.

"I know what I found in those archives, apprentice."

"I know you do. Say it anyway. In public. Where it counts." Xanthe held his gaze. "That's my price for trusting you. Not the sigil's price. Mine."

For a long moment the only sounds were the gulls and the rain. Then Corvane nodded, once, the way he probably signed death warrants — precisely, and meaning every stroke.

"Agreed."

They shook hands. His grip was cool and careful, a man handling something he was afraid of breaking, and Xanthe couldn't decide whether that was better or worse than contempt.

* * *

Corvane left before full dark, walking back down the cliff path with his hat squared against the wind, a lone figure shrinking into the gray. Xanthe stood in the doorway watching until the fog took him, then went inside and relit the stove and pulled the crumpled list from where he'd stuffed it and smoothed it out against the table.

*Things That Are Mine.*

1. My name is Xanthe Sandoval.
2. I burned my stock of charms in the Copper Lane grate. (I remember the smell.)
3. Sister's lullaby. (Unverified. Do not sing it yet.)
4. I hate coriander.
5. Zephyrine drew winged cats.

He stared at it a long while. Then, at the bottom, in fresh ink:

6. Today I made a bargain with a witch-hunter and shook his hand and did not die.
7. I gave him two cups of tea without thinking. Zephyrine says that's dignity. I think it might be hope.
8. Tomorrow we go to the citadel. I am going to see her. Twelve years, and I'm going to see her through bars, and she's going to make some remark about my haircut.

He capped the ink. He blew out the lamp. And as he lay down under the leaking roof with the sea muttering below, the sigil stirred one last time — and for the first time it didn't feel like drowning. It felt like a thousand people turning over in their sleep, murmuring, waiting for morning.

Somewhere far off, down the coast, a light that was not a lighthouse blinked twice and went dark.

Xanthe did not see it. But the sigil did, and it flinched.
