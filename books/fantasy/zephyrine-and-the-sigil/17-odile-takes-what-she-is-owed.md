## 17. Odile Takes What She Is Owed

Odile had stolen many things in her long, borrowed life: a king's signet ring, a saint's fingerbone, three separate identities (two of them still in circulation), and — though she rarely admitted it — a pie cooling on a windowsill in Marrow's Cross, because she'd been young once, in someone else's body, and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with bread.

She had never before stolen from someone sleeping.

It felt, she decided, watching Xanthe breathe in the shallow hollow of the abandoned mill, like theft of an entirely new category. Not quite burglary. Something closer to grave-robbing, except the grave was warm and muttered in its sleep.

"Things that are mine," he'd murmured an hour ago, half-dreaming, one hand curled around the list he kept folded against his heart. She'd read it over his shoulder at the fire two nights past. *My sister's lullaby. The smell of Zephyrine's chimney smoke. My own name.* The handwriting had grown shakier as the list went on, as if even the ink couldn't be sure whose hand it belonged to.

Odile knew that handwriting. She'd seen it in a hundred journals across four centuries, written by people who were mostly themselves on Monday and somebody else by Thursday.

That was the trouble with understanding someone. It made what you intended to do to them feel unbearably personal.

*He'll thank me,* she told herself, settling cross-legged beside him in the dust. *Eventually. Possibly. In a century or two.*

The sigil sat under his skin like a coal wrapped in silk — she could taste it from here, could have tasted it from a mile off. Magic always had a flavor to her. Zephyrine's work had tasted of cedar smoke and stubbornness. Corvane's hunters carried the tang of iron filings and fear. And the sigil itself —

The sigil tasted like a library on fire. Every memory of every mage who had ever borne it, pressed into one living mark, waiting for a vessel strong enough — or foolish enough — to hold it all.

Xanthe Sandoval was not strong enough. She'd known it the moment she ambushed him on the road, when his fear had come off him in waves flavored with salt and loneliness and, beneath all of it, a thread of something so bright and hopeful it had made her physically nauseous. Hope. In this economy. Honestly.

He was drowning in it, the inheritance. She'd watched it happen these last weeks — watched him wake speaking dead dialects, watched him grieve husbands and children he'd never had, watched him test himself against his own childhood and come up wrong. He was becoming a shoreline, and the sigil was the tide, and every day more of Xanthe washed out to sea.

So really, when you thought about it clearly and without sentiment, taking half of it was an act of mercy.

*Half for me,* whispered the part of her that was not merciful at all, the part that had been hungry since before Xanthe's grandmother was born. *Half is owed. Half is fair.*

She flexed her fingers. Her hands remembered how to do this even when the rest of her forgot which hands they were. That was the joke of Odile, the great irony she wore like a bad hat: the memory-thief who could no longer say which memories were originally hers. She had been so many people that *Odile* was less a name than a coatrack, hung about with centuries of other people's winters.

But her hands knew. Her hands had always known.

"Forgive me," she said softly, and then laughed at herself, quiet as breath. "No. I won't lie to you. You've had enough people lying to you. Zephyrine lied to you out of love, which is somehow worse."

Xanthe stirred but didn't wake. Even asleep he looked tired in the bones, tired somewhere sleep couldn't reach.

She laid two fingers against his temple and opened herself.

*

Stealing a memory was nothing like the ballads said. There was no wrenching, no scream, no dramatic flash of green light. It was gentler than that, and therefore worse — it felt like unthreading a stitch. Like lifting a stone from a wall so smoothly the wall didn't even sag. The victim rarely noticed until the whole structure came down.

And the sigil's memories — oh, the sigil's memories came eagerly. That was the horror of it. They *wanted* to be taken. Centuries of the dead, packed shoulder to shoulder in one small burning mark, and here at last was a door, a mouth, a thief willing to carry them.

They poured into her like a river into a cracked jug.

She tasted them as they came: a hedge-witch named Brenna who could coax beans up through frost and sang to her garden in a voice like wet gravel. A soldier-mage who had turned his sword to a plowshare and wept doing it. A girl in a plague year who walked door to door cooling fevers with bare hands until her own burned out. A man who loved another man in a kingdom where saying so was death, who kept his love alive in the sigil because nowhere else would hold it.

Each one arrived complete. Each one settled into her like a guest hanging up a coat.

*Stop,* Odile thought, dizzy. *I only want half. Half. Do you hear me, you greedy dead—*

But the dead were not greedy. The dead were *tired*, and she was warm, and they flowed and flowed and she took and took, and somewhere in the middle of it — she would swear this later, to anyone who asked, though no one ever did — she lost track of which half was leaving and which half was staying, of whether she was emptying the sigil or filling herself, of where Odile ended and the inheritance began.

When she finally pulled back, gasping, the mill was dark and cold and Xanthe lay very still.

Too still.

"No," she said. "No, no, come on, I left you half. I counted." Had she counted? She had meant to count. She pressed her fingers to his throat and found a pulse, slow and steady as a deep well, and let out a breath she'd apparently been holding since the reign of some king whose name now lived in her head alongside his favorite recipe for eel pie.

His face in sleep had gone strangely smooth. Younger. Emptier. A page with most of the writing faded, a few words still legible near the margins.

She found the list where it had slipped from his hand. *Things That Are Mine.* She read it by moonlight through the broken roof, and her throat went tight, because fully a third of the entries had gone grey — not crossed out, not erased, just *faded*, like ink remembering it was never really there.

*My sister's lullaby* — still dark, still his.
*The name my master called me* — grey.
*The taste of burnt honey cakes* — grey. Oh, that one hurt. She'd eaten those cakes with him at Zephyrine's table, back when she'd been casing him instead of caring about him. Burnt on purpose, he'd said. Zephyrine liked them that way. Said perfection was for people with no imagination.

"I left you half," she whispered again, uselessly, and folded the list back against his heart.

Here was the thing Odile could not explain to anyone, least of all herself: she had not taken more than her share because she was greedy. She had taken more because the sigil's grief was *enormous*, and grief that enormous does not fit neatly into halves. You cannot steal a mass grave by the pound.

And — smaller, meaner truth — some traitorous part of her had thought: *if I take enough, maybe I'll finally be full. Maybe one of these lives will fit me so well I can stop being nobody wearing everybody.*

She stood. Her knees cracked. One of the newly arrived memories informed her that at her age she ought to see a physicker, and she told it, silently and with feeling, to shut up.

"Goodbye, Xanthe Sandoval," she said to the sleeping man. "Or whatever's left of him. I'd say I'm sorry, but you'd know I was lying, and worse — *I'd* know."

Then she ran.

*

The rebels had made camp in the drowned quarry outside Vellamare, torchlight bouncing off flooded stone, and their sentries were exactly as competent as smugglers always were, which is to say Odile walked past three of them while humming.

Maren Salt was waiting by the big fire, arms crossed, jaw set in the expression of a woman who had already decided to be angry and was merely waiting for a reason. Maren had a face like a cliff and a heart like a spring tide, and she had spent three weeks telling Odile, in exhaustive detail, why trusting her was a mistake.

"You're late," Maren said.

"I'm punctual. Your expectations are early."

"You said dawn."

"It's nearly dawn. Somewhere. Probably on a ship."

Maren's eyes narrowed, tracking down to Odile's hands. Smugglers learned to read hands. "You've got it. Part of it."

"Half," Odile said. "Give or take a graveyard."

The word moved through the camp like wind through wheat. Rebels and hidden mages alike turned, and Odile felt their hunger hit her — hope-flavored, desperate, the same bright nauseating flavor she'd tasted on Xanthe's road. They wanted the sigil the way starving people want bread. As a banner. As proof. As *him*, the mage who'd defied the Inquisitor and lived.

"Show us," Maren said quietly.

So Odile opened her hands and let a little of it show: one memory, lifted to the surface like a lantern. Brenna, the bean-witch, singing to her garden in the frost. The rebels saw her — actually *saw* her, a dead woman walking again in borrowed light — and someone began to cry, and then several someones, and Maren Salt's cliff-face crumbled by a full inch.

"The crown says magic died in the war," Odile said, pitching her voice the way she'd learned from a preacher whose body she'd worn for six years. "The crown says the gifted were cleansed, root and branch, and good riddance. But I am carrying the roots. I am carrying the branches. I am carrying every witch they burned, and they are *furious*, and they would like a word with Vellamare."

A roar went up. Somewhere a drum started. Torches multiplied like spilled fire.

*Marek the plowman says you're laying it on thick,* observed one of her new residents, wryly, from inside her skull.

*Hush, Marek, the adults are working.*

*You were a charlatan before you were anything else,* said another voice, older, amused, unmistakably Zephyrine-adjacent — one of the sigil's oldest residents, a woman called Ivette who had died laughing in a cell much like Zephyrine's. *But it works. Keep going.*

That was the thing no one warned you about inheriting the dead: they had opinions. Odile had spent four centuries collecting memories the way a magpie collects buttons — shiny, portable, hers alone. Now she had a thousand houseguests, and they all wanted to steer.

Maren caught her arm as the camp erupted into preparation. "The boy," she said. "The vessel. Where is he?"

"Asleep," Odile said. "Where I left him."

"You took his half too, didn't you." Not a question. Maren had raised four younger siblings; she knew exactly what a person looked like after being robbed by family.

"He was drowning in it," Odile snapped, sharper than she meant. "Another month and there'd have been nothing left of Xanthe Sandoval to save — just a shell wearing a sigil, and you'd have had your banner anyway, except it would have been a corpse holding it up. I gave him a chance to remember his own name. That's more mercy than this world has ever shown either of us."

"And you? Did you leave yourself any?"

The question went in like a splinter. Odile smiled the smile she'd learned from a courtesan in a country that no longer existed. "I'm fine. I'm always fine. I've been fine for four hundred years."

*Ivette says that's a lie,* Marek offered helpfully.

*I know it's a lie, Marek, thank you.*

Maren held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded slowly. "March at first light. If you're leading us to Vellamare, you march at the front where I can watch you." A pause. "And Odile? When this is done — if the boy comes looking for what you took — I won't stand in his way."

"Wouldn't dream of asking you to," Odile said lightly.

But that night, in the noise of the mustering camp, sitting apart from the fires with a thousand dead murmuring in her chest like tenants arguing about the rent, she took out a scrap of paper and wrote, in a hand that kept shifting between three different scripts:

*Things That Are Odile's:*

She stared at the page a long time.

She wrote: *the pie in Marrow's Cross (still sorry).*

She wrote: *a song in a language I don't speak, that I've hummed since before I can remember, that I think — I think — might actually be mine.*

And then, because the dead were quiet for once and the moon was up and no one was watching, she wrote a third line:

*What I owe Xanthe Sandoval.*

Underneath it, after a while: *everything.*

She folded the paper away and looked south, toward the city, toward the citadel, toward the war-site below it where the sigil's remaining half was surely pulling at Xanthe even now — or pulling at whatever remained of him. Toward the reckoning.

"Keep breathing," she murmured to a boy miles away who might no longer remember why. "Hold on to whatever's yours. I'm coming back for the rest of it."

Whether she meant to return what she'd taken, or to finish the job, she honestly could not say.

Neither, she suspected, could the thousand voices inside her — and for the first time in four hundred years, Odile fell asleep afraid of her own dreams.
