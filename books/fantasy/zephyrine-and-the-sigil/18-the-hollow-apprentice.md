## 18. The Hollow Apprentice

I woke on the third morning after Odile and reached for my mother's face before I remembered I no longer had it.

Not her face — I had never had her face, that was rather the point of faces — but the memory of it. For months the sigil had kept a portrait gallery behind my eyes, and my mother's smile had hung there among Zephyrine's tower rooms and a dead man's wedding and a hundred other lives that were not mine. Now half the gallery stood empty. Odile had taken what she took while I slept, and what remained was a house with the furniture dragged out of it: echoes in the halls, pale rectangles on the walls where pictures used to be.

Worse than the emptiness was the suspicion. I could no longer tell which of my own memories had been mine to begin with.

I sat up in the smugglers' loft above the salt-shed — my lodging since the Registry, my lodging since everything — and performed the morning ritual I had developed, which was to say nothing at all for a full minute and listen for strangers in my own skull. A woman named Maren grieved a husband named Tomas. That much I knew, because the knowing came with a flavor, and the sigil's memories all tasted faintly of rosemary and cold iron. My own griefs tasted of nothing. They simply *were*, the way my hands were, unexamined until someone tried to take them.

But my mother's lullaby. The one about the fisherman's daughter and the moon. Was that rosemary-flavored? I turned it over in my mind like a coin I suspected of being shaved, and found I could not say. It had always been there. Things that are always there are exactly the things you cannot vouch for.

"Well," I said aloud, to the rafters, to the gulls, to nobody. "Hello. My name is—"

And here I stopped, because I wanted to check.

*That*, I decided, swinging my legs out of the cot, is intolerable.

* * *

Corvane arrived at midmorning with the air of a man who has been insulted by furniture.

He came alone, which was itself remarkable; twelve days ago he had come through the salt market with six witch-hunters and a writ, and now he walked through a den of illegal mages carrying only his ledger and an expression like a man attending his own trial as both judge and defendant. The smugglers let him in because of me, and only because of me, and they watched him the whole way up the stairs with the tenderness of people deciding where to hide a body.

"You look terrible," he said, by way of greeting.

"You've been talking to my mirror."

He set the ledger on the table between us — the great black registry ledger, the one whose pages I had spent twelve years hoping never to see my name on — and did not open it. He looked, I thought, worse than I did. There is a particular exhaustion that comes from discovering your life's work has been built on a lie you were also the victim of, and Corvane wore it like a coat two sizes too small.

"I'm told," he said, "that you've lost yourself."

"Not all of me." I poured him tea, because my hands needed something to do and because hospitality toward witch-hunters is the sort of small defiance I have always enjoyed. "Half. She took the sigil's half and some of mine tangled up in it. What's left is—" I gestured vaguely at myself. "Draft quality."

"And you can't tell what's real."

"Can you?" I said, before I could stop myself, and had the pleasure of watching him flinch. His grandmother. Zephyrine's last message. Some wounds you do not get to choose; they choose you, usually at the worst possible moment.

But Corvane was Corvane, and he answered the flinch the way he answered everything: with procedure. He opened the ledger.

"Twelve years ago," he said, "a boy disappeared from the registry rolls of Vellamare's Saltquarter. Not arrested. Not flagged. He simply ceased to appear — paid his harbor dues under another name, rented a room from a woman who didn't ask questions, filed a trader's mark with the customs house." He turned the ledger toward me. "The crown believes its records are instruments of control. In truth they are the most complete biography ever written. Every fear of being caught leaves a paper trail."

On the page, in faded clerk's ink: *XANTHE SANDOVAL, b. Wellspring Row, Vellamare. Mother: Lira Sandoval, netmender. Sister: Juna, d. age six, spring fever.* And beneath it, in Corvane's own narrow hand, added recently: *Apprentice of record: none. Suspected: Z. of Graywater. Status: AT LARGE.*

"Your sister," Corvane said, "died in the winter of the great fever, not spring. The clerk made an error, and errors interest me more than truths, because truths don't leave fingerprints." He tapped the line. "You corrected this once. Did you know that? There's a marginal note in a child's hand — you went back to the registry hall when you were eleven and wrote *winter, she loved the ice* beside the entry, and some tired clerk let it stand."

I hadn't known that. I hadn't known it, and reading it, I *felt* it — the shame of a boy sneaking into a government building to argue with a piece of paper about his sister, the desperate pedantry of grief. It rang in me like a struck bell, and no part of the ringing tasted of rosemary.

"Winter," I said. My voice cracked on it. "She loved the ice. She'd skate on the fountain basins when they froze and mother would—"

"—would pretend to be angry," Corvane finished, reading from somewhere — his notes, or his own recovered past. "Yes. It's in the margin."

I put my face in my hands for a moment. When I looked up, the tea had gone cold and Corvane was pretending not to have noticed anything, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in weeks.

"Why?" I asked. "You're not doing this out of the goodness of your heart. You don't have one. I've seen the inventory."

For the first time in our acquaintance, the Inquisitor smiled, and it was almost human, the way a knife is almost a spoon.

"Because you are going to help me tear down the lie about the war, and a man whose memory can be stolen is a man whose testimony cannot be trusted — unless every word of it can be verified against independent sources." He closed the ledger. "So we verify. All of it. You will be the best-documented mage in the history of Vellamare, Sandoval. Your selfhood will rest on customs filings and harbor ledgers and the testimony of persons of dubious morality. It's not poetry. But it cannot be stolen, because it doesn't live in your head. It lives in the world."

"The world," I said, "burns things that live in it."

"Then we'll keep copies."

* * *

It became, over the following weeks, the strangest project of my strange life: the reconstruction of Xanthe Sandoval from outside in.

The smugglers helped, though they pretended it was commerce. Brannoc the harbormaster — a gray whale of a man with contraband charms sewn into his coat lining and a heart he'd deny to his dying day — produced the actual trader's mark I'd filed at nineteen, and read my application aloud in a voice like gravel settling. *"Purpose of trade: luck-knots, hearth-blessings sold as decorative items, general curios. Applicant states no magical aptitude."* He looked up. "You lied on a government form, lad."

"I've missed precisely nothing about my life."

"Best liar I ever employed," he said fondly, which from Brannoc was practically a sonnet.

There were others. The woman I'd bought candle-wax from for ten years remembered that I haggled but always overpaid in the end, because I couldn't bear the look on her face. A retired ferryman recalled the young man who crossed twice a year, always alone, always carrying honey-cakes he never ate — offerings, though the ferryman hadn't known that word, taken down the coast to a grave that wasn't marked. Juna's grave. I had gone every year. I had forgotten going, in the flood of inheritance, and now a stranger handed the memory back to me like a coat left at a door.

Each verification was a small resurrection. Each one terrified me, because I began to see how many of the things I'd called mine had already been replaced — how the sigil's tenants had redecorated while I slept, moving my furniture into their rooms and theirs into mine, so that the house still felt like home and nothing in it matched.

So I kept the list. *Things That Are Mine.* But I changed its rules.

The old list had been written from inside, and inside was enemy territory now. The new list required evidence. An entry earned its place only if something outside my skull confirmed it: a record, a witness, a scar with a story attached that someone else had heard before I lost the telling of it. Beside each item I noted its source, like citations in a scholar's thesis, except the thesis was *I exist* and the reviewers were smugglers.

*My name: Xanthe Sandoval. Source: registry ledger, Corvane's testimony, Brannoc's employment rolls.*
*I am left-handed. Source: ink stains on the right cuff of every coat I've owned since boyhood, per Odessa the rag-seller, who found this hilarious.*
*I burn lavender with my charms because my master did, and she burned it because it reminds her of rain on hot stone. Source: Zephyrine's own daybook, recovered from the cottage ruins. Her hand, her words, her smell-memory. Mine by teaching, not by theft.*

That last one mattered more than I can easily say. Because it turned out — and this is the thing nobody tells you about hollowing — that a self rebuilt from evidence is not smaller than one that was simply given. It is *chosen*. Every entry cost something to confirm, and what you pay for, you keep. The sigil's memories had been a river carrying me; the list was a wall I laid stone by stone, and every stone I set with my own hands.

Some nights Odile's absence sat across the table like a guest. Half my inheritance, gone into a thief's pocket — Zephyrine's arrest, the tower cell, a thousand years of witches, all of it marched off while I dreamed. I should have hated her. Mostly I did. But there were evenings when the loss felt like an amputation of something that had been killing me anyway, and I caught myself breathing easier, and hated myself a little for the relief, and wrote another entry in the list instead. Grief and gratitude, sharing a pot like old roommates.

*My sister's name was Juna and she died in winter and she loved the ice. Sources: too many to count.*

That one I underlined twice.

* * *

On the last night of the month, Corvane brought the final document.

He laid it on the table without ceremony, the way he laid everything, as if papers might bite. It was a testimony sheet — his own, signed and sealed, the first confession the Office of Inquiry had accepted from one of its own inquisitors in living memory. His grandmother's name. The purge of forty years ago. The family the crown erased and the family he had unknowingly spent his career re-erasing.

"Read it," he said. "If you're going to stand in front of soldiers and testify about the war, you'll want to know what my word is worth. And I want it on record somewhere that isn't my head." His jaw tightened. "Memories can be stolen. I intend to be harder to steal than that."

I read it. When I finished, I took out my list, and under the lamp I added a new line:

*Inquisitor Corvane keeps his grandmother's thimble in his left coat pocket and touches it when he lies to himself. Source: observed. Twice.*

"That's slander," he said, reading upside down, with the affronted dignity of a wet cat.

"It's documented," I said. "Everything on this list is documented."

He glowered. Then, in a movement so grudging it was nearly geological, he drew out his pen, took the list from me, and appended at the bottom, in the same narrow clerk's hand that had once recorded my name as a fugitive:

*X. Sandoval laughs at his own jokes before finishing them. Source: three weeks' observation. Confirmed.*

We looked at each other across the table. Somewhere below us, the smugglers were singing badly; the harbor smelled of salt and tar and, faintly, of lavender, because some things persist. My head was half-empty and my list was four pages long and every line of it belonged to me the way a path belongs to the feet that made it.

"Sleep," Corvane said, gathering his papers. "Tomorrow we ride for Vellamare. Brannoc's had word from the capital." He paused at the door, and whatever he saw in my face made him deliver it plainly, without softening, because by then he knew me well enough to know I'd stopped wanting softening.

"Odile has reached the city. She's given the rebels half the sigil's memories as proof of her claim — and they've proclaimed her the heir of the murdered witches. She's marching on the citadel at the head of an army." He settled his hat. "And she's carrying your master's voice in her pocket, Sandoval. Whatever Zephyrine seeded in that sigil — whoever she meant it for — Odile is about to spend it as a crown."

I looked down at my list. Four pages of borrowed proof, of stones laid by hand, of a self chosen rather than given — and beneath it all, faint as rosemary, the pull of the remaining half in my chest, turning toward Vellamare like a compass needle finding north.

"Then we'd better march faster," I said.
