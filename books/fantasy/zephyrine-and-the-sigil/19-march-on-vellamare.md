## 19. March on Vellamare

### Odile

The road to Vellamare was full of people who had never held a sword, which Odile considered the most promising sign of any rebellion she had joined in four hundred years.

She rode at the head of them on a borrowed mule named Turnip, because the horse she'd requisitioned had died of indignation somewhere outside Graywater, and because there was something honest about arriving at the gates of a kingdom on an animal named after a vegetable. Behind her stretched perhaps three hundred souls: smugglers from the Salt Market, hedge-witches with charms braided into their hair like festival ribbons, farmers who had walked away from fields for the first time in their lives, and one extremely committed baker named Perrin who carried a rolling pin and refused all offers of an actual weapon.

"They'll laugh at us," Perrin had said when she suggested it.

"They laughed at me in 1211," Odile told him, "and I was carrying a crossbow at the time. Laughing is not the same as winning."

The truth was that half her army could not fight and the other half did not want to, and this was precisely why she had stolen half of Xanthe's sigil while he slept.

She still felt it inside her — that vast sediment of other people's lives, layered like river silt. It should have made her stronger. Instead it made her strange even to herself. She caught weeping over things that had happened before she was born, or rather before the woman whose body she'd first stolen was born. She dreamed in dialects. This morning she had woken with a craving for a soup from a village that no longer existed, cooked by a husband she had never kissed, and she had stood in the dawn feeling like a house where every room belonged to someone else.

*You took it,* said a voice in her that might have been a dead witch named Maren or might have been simple honesty. *You wanted what they had.*

"I wanted to stop drowning," Odile murmured to Turnip, who flicked one ear in forgiveness or dismissal; mules are famously noncommittal.

Ahead, Vellamare rose out of its bowl of sea-fog, white walls and red roofs, the citadel's spire needling the sky. Somewhere beneath that spire lay the war-site, and beneath the war-site, the thing her stolen memories remembered building: a cage shaped like a coin, a prison shaped like a gift. And somewhere in the city — she could feel him now, the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth — was the boy with the other half.

*Come home,* the sigil-half whispered through her blood, in Zephyrine's voice, in a dozen voices, in his voice. *Come home and be whole.*

"No," she said, and spurred Turnip forward, and three hundred people who believed she knew what she was doing followed her toward the gates.

### Corvane

Inquisitor Corvane stood on the citadel wall and watched the rebels come, and felt nothing so much as embarrassment.

Not fear — though fear was present, sitting quietly in his chest like a well-behaved guest. Embarrassment, because he had spent thirty years perfecting the machinery of the hunt, and here was the result: three hundred unarmed citizens walking up the coast road in broad daylight, led by a memory-thief on a mule, and his own hunters scattered along the borders chasing rumors he himself had planted. He had built a machine for catching witches in cellars and attics, in whispers and hidden charms. No one had designed it for this.

"Shall we loose the ballistae?" asked Captain Verre, young and eager and holding her orders like a fan.

"Against a baker," Corvane said, "with a rolling pin."

"There's a woman behind him with what looks like lightning stored in jars—"

"Yes, I see her." He did see her. He saw all of it, with the terrible new clarity that Zephyrine's last message had burned into him. *Your grandmother's name was Sera Corvane,* her letter had said. *They drowned her in the harbor in the Year of Ashes, and your grandfather helped them do it, because they promised it would end the hunt.* Thirty years of certainty, and it had taken a dying witch five minutes to hollow him out like a gourd.

He had resigned that morning. The resignation sat unsigned in his desk drawer, because resignations require a future, and Corvane no longer trusted himself to have one until today was finished.

"The rebels want the city," Verre pressed. "Or the citadel. Or—"

"They want what's underneath it," Corvane said. "As does the crown, though the crown doesn't know why. As, I suspect, do I." He turned from the wall. "Open the lower galleries. And send word to the Salt Gate — if a young man comes asking, alone, looking rather like a man being dragged by an invisible rope, let him pass."

Verre frowned. "Sandoval? You'd let the fugitive walk in?"

"He's the only person in this story," Corvane said, "who has ever chosen correctly under pressure. I intend to watch how he does it."

### Xanthe

By the time Xanthe reached the outskirts of Vellamare, he had stopped pretending he was walking there.

This was the humiliating truth of the last two days: his legs went where the sigil wanted. He had tried turning south, toward the sea, toward anywhere, and his body had simply — declined. Not violently. Politely. Like a servant who has decided, after long service, that the master's instructions are no longer reasonable. His feet would carry him down the south road for a mile and then, gradually, without his noticing, the road would bend back north again.

"You're a coin," he told the sigil's scar on his palm, somewhere past the second milestone. "And coins always roll downhill. Is that what I am to you? Slope?"

The sigil answered, as it always answered, with memory. A flash: the war-site cavern, torchlight on wet stone, a circle of dying mages pressing their hands together around the first seed of the mark. A voice — old, cracked, familiar in the way a song is familiar when someone else is humming it — saying: *Let it be carried. Let it be carried until carrying it is impossible.*

"Impossible," Xanthe repeated bitterly. "Yes. Congratulations. Mission accomplished."

He was talking to his hand now. Twelve years of hiding, of small careful invisible life, of selling luck-knots to dockworkers and never once saying the true word for what he was — and it had ended with him narrating his own possession on a public highway, arguing with a scar.

There was a time when that thought would have terrified him. Now it mostly made him tired, and a little amused, and he suspected the amusement wasn't entirely his either. Half of him had been stolen by a centuries-old thief; what remained sloshed around in the space like water in a dropped bucket. But the list was still folded against his heart — *Things That Are Mine*, fourteen entries in his own handwriting, verified against Corvane's testimony and the smugglers' records and his sister's lullaby, which he had finally gotten right, finally, after weeping through six wrong versions on a smuggler's floor. Entry one: *My name is Xanthe Sandoval.* Entry nine: *I burn the porridge. Every time. It is mine to burn.*

He clung to entry nine the way sailors cling to driftwood. Small, undignified, verifiable.

Vellamare's walls appeared through the fog, and with them the strangest sight he had ever witnessed: an army approaching from the east and nobody fleeing it. Instead, people were streaming out of the west gate to *watch*. A fruit seller had set up near the road. Children perched on the seawall. Someone was roasting chestnuts, and the smell drifted across the field with a cheerfulness that struck Xanthe as either profound courage or profound denial, possibly both, probably both.

He slipped through the watching crowd toward the gate, hood up, and the sigil pulled harder than ever — down, always down, through the city's bones to whatever waited beneath the citadel.

A guard stopped him at the archway. Xanthe braced himself, hand half-raised, magic prickling unwillingly along his arm—

"Xanthe Sandoval?" the guard said. "Inquisitor's compliments. You're expected. Lower galleries, third door past the cistern. He says—" the guard consulted a scrap of paper with visible reluctance, "—he says, 'Tell him the hearthstone is open.'"

The breath went out of Xanthe all at once. *Zephyrine's phrase.* Her old password, the one she'd used when hiding things from the neighbors, delivered in Corvane's dry clipped words via a bored teenager with a spear.

"All right," Xanthe said faintly. "Lead on, then. Apparently everyone knows I'm coming."

*Everyone,* agreed the sigil, warmly, horribly, like a family reunion.

### Odile

The gates of Vellamare opened before her army arrived, which Odile found more alarming than resistance would have been.

An open gate is either a trap or an admission, and she had survived four centuries by assuming trap first. She halted the column — a process requiring much shouting and several collisions, as three hundred civilians make a worse army than even she had feared — and rode forward alone under a white rag tied to Turnip's tail.

The captain of the gate watched her come with an expression Odile recognized: the look of a soldier who has been given orders she does not understand and has decided to survive anyway. Good. Odile liked survivors. They were the only interesting kind.

"You're expected," the captain said.

"Am I." Odile swung down from the mule. "By whom?"

"Inquisitor Corvane. He asks that you come to the citadel. Alone. He says—" another scrap of paper, gods, the man ran his city on scraps, "—he says the door below is already unlocked, and locking it would only insult everyone involved."

Odile laughed before she could help it. It startled her, the laugh — it came from somewhere shallow and human, some layer of herself that predated the thefts, and it felt like finding an old coin in a coat pocket.

"And my people?"

"May camp on the green. There will be bread."

"Bread," Odile repeated.

"The baker insisted," said the captain, with the first crack of humanity in her voice. "He says armies march on their stomachs and also he would very much like to meet you, he's read about you, he thinks what you did in 1319 was—"

"Tell him 1319 was exaggerated," Odile said quickly. "Tell him most of it was exaggerated." She handed over Turnip's reins, patted the mule's nose, and looked up at the citadel spire needling the sky above. Beneath it, in her stolen blood, the other half of her inheritance called and called, patient as tide.

She had come to take the rest of the sigil. That was the plan, the clean simple plan, the plan that had seemed so clear when Xanthe's sleeping face had been inches from hers and the taking had been as easy as breathing. But standing here, in a city opening its gates to an army of bakers, with bread being arranged on her behalf — the plan had developed a wobble.

*You could give it back,* whispered Maren-the-dead-witch, or honesty, or hunger.

"I could," Odile admitted, and started up the hill.

### Xanthe

The lower galleries smelled of salt and old stone, and the pull of the sigil was so strong now that Xanthe walked bent slightly forward, like a man leaning into wind.

Corvane waited for him at the third door past the cistern, exactly as advertised, holding a lantern and wearing the expression of a man attending his own funeral and disapproving of the flowers.

"You came," Corvane said.

"My feet did. I'm mostly a passenger at this point." Xanthe held up his marked palm. "It wants the war-site. It's been wanting it since Graywater. I assume you didn't summon me here to admire the architecture."

"No." Corvane hesitated — actually hesitated, this man of iron procedure — and then said, with the care of someone defusing something: "The rebels are camped on the green. Their leader is on her way up the hill. The crown's council is in session directly above us, arguing about whether to fire ballistae at civilians. And beneath our feet, according to everything you've shown me, is the grave of every mage this kingdom ever killed, kept alive in a coin of memories that both those parties want to own." He set the lantern down. "I have spent thirty years believing the hunt protected people. I no longer believe it. I believe it fed on them. So I will ask you plainly, apprentice: what happens next?"

Xanthe looked at the door behind the Inquisitor. Old stone, older than the citadel, older than the kingdom. Through his palm he could feel it — the vast slow heartbeat of the thing below, the mass grave wearing the mask of an heirloom, calling its halves home.

"Honestly?" he said. "I think it happens whether we approve or not. But I've got a list of things that are mine, and top of the list is choosing. So." He rolled his shoulders, took a breath that shook only a little, and pushed the door open onto stairs going down into the dark, into the deep, into the place where every dead witch he had ever inherited was waiting.

Behind him, boots on stone — Corvane following, lantern high. Above, faint through the rock, a bell began to ring, and under the bell, closer than his own pulse, the sigil sang:

*Home. Home. Home.*

And from the stairwell above, unhurried, familiar, came the click of heels and a voice he knew too well, sweet as stolen honey: "Don't start without me, darling. I hate missing the ending."
