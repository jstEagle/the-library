## 20. The Sigil Shattered

The war-site beneath the citadel was not what the histories promised. The histories — the few that dared mention it — spoke of a great vaulted hall, a monument to the crown's victory, all banners and marble and solemn triumph. What Xanthe found instead was a cave. A wide, low cavern of black stone beneath the citadel's foundations, ribbed like the inside of some enormous throat, its walls slick with salt water that wept down from the harbor above.

And carved into every inch of it, in spirals and lattices and rivers of script older than Vellamare itself, was the sigil. Thousands of them. Millions. The whole cavern was one vast, patient knotwork of them, dimly glowing like embers under ash.

"It's not a monument," he said aloud, and his voice came back to him strange and doubled off the stone. "It never was. It's a cage. They built the citadel on top of it so no one would ever look."

*We built it,* said a hundred voices inside him, not unkindly. *And then they built their city on our graves and called it peace.*

"Stop that," Xanthe muttered. "I'm trying to have a dramatic moment."

The sigil in his palm pulsed. It had been pulsing since the moment he'd descended the last stair, pulling him forward like a fish on a line, and behind him he could hear — distantly, muffled by stone — the sounds of two armies meeting above. Odile's rebels pouring down from the hills with stolen memories burning in their eyes; the crown's hunters marshaled on the walls with Corvane's writ in their fists. The knot of history tightening, exactly as the sigil had always known it would.

He wondered, briefly, whether Zephyrine had ever stood in this cavern. Whether she had seen this ending from her tower cell at Graywater and set the whole thing moving toward it, the way she used to set her loom moving toward a pattern only she could see. He hoped so. He hoped she'd been smug about it. She would have been. She'd have raised one white eyebrow and said something insufferable like *Well, boy, you did take your time.*

His eyes stung. There wasn't time for grief either. Grief could wait its turn, like everything else.

Boots on the stairs. Two sets, arriving almost together, which told him everything he needed to know about how the battle above was going — badly, for everyone, or strangely well, which amounted to the same thing when Odile was involved.

She came through the cavern mouth first, torchlight and torch in hand, and twelve years of hiding had taught Xanthe to read people quickly, so it took him only a heartbeat to see what the theft had cost her. Half her face was smooth and ancient and knowing as ever. The other half was *empty*. Not young — vacant. Like a house with the furniture gone, where you can still see the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures hung.

"You took half," she said. Not a greeting. An accusation.

"You took half first," Xanthe said.

"That half was owed to me." She advanced into the ember-light of the carvings, and her empty eye reflected them dully, without wonder, which was somehow the saddest thing he had ever seen. Someone who had lived centuries and could no longer be astonished. "I spent lifetimes earning my share of the dead. You were given yours by an old woman's sentiment. Give me the rest, apprentice, and I'll let you walk out of here with whatever's left of yourself. Which, I grant, isn't much."

"You know," Xanthe said, "for someone with three hundred years of borrowed memories, your offers are remarkably repetitive. Every bandit on the coast road makes the same speech. Just once I'd like to hear *please*."

"Xanthe."

The second voice. Corvane came out of the dark behind Odile, and he looked — wrong. Not the cold Inquisitor of the registry proclamations, the man whose seal had dragged Xanthe's neighbor away over a child's fever-charm. His coat was torn. There was ash in his close-cropped hair. And his hand, resting on his sword hilt, was shaking.

Behind them both, filtering into the cavern, came soldiers. First one, then a handful, then dozens — rebels and hunters both, drawn down the stairs not by orders but by the pull of the thing, the way moths find flame and rivers find the sea. They spread along the walls with weapons half-raised, enemies eyeing enemies, and none of them advancing, because the whole cavern glowed now, answering the sigil in Xanthe's palm, and even angry men hesitate before a mass grave that is looking back at them.

"Inquisitor," Xanthe said. "You're supposed to be arresting me. Or bargaining with me. You've done both this month; it's been very educational."

"I'm supposed to do neither." Corvane's voice was flat, but underneath it something moved, tectonic and slow. "Zephyrine left me a message. My grandmother's name was in it. Sera Corvane, midwife, purged in the hunt of forty-one." He swallowed. "Every record of her was destroyed. I checked. I spent eleven days checking. The crown erased her so thoroughly that I grew up believing my own blood had never existed." He drew his sword — and drove it point-down into the black stone, where it stood quivering, abandoned. "I am done being their instrument. But the memories must not go free, Sandoval. They must go somewhere *safe*. Give them to the crown's keeping—"

"The crown's keeping," Xanthe repeated. "Like they kept Zephyrine. Like they kept your grandmother."

Corvane flinched. Good, thought Xanthe, and immediately felt sick about it. There was no room left for cruelty, not here, not with so little time.

"He can't give it to anyone," Odile said softly. She had drifted closer while they talked — of course she had; she had stolen from sleeping men and trusted friends and knew better than anyone how attention could be spent like coin. Her empty eye was fixed on his palm. "That's why he walked past both of us in the night and came *here*, to the place the sigil has been calling him since Graywater. Look at him, Inquisitor. He hasn't come to surrender it or spend it. He came to bury it."

"To free it," Xanthe said.

His heart was going like a hammer, and his hand — his poor ordinary hand, the hand that had knotted luck-charms and gutted fish and held his sister's lullaby wrong against his own memory — his hand was steady. That surprised him. Twelve years of smallness, of careful invisible living, of shrinking himself to fit the space between the crown's law and his own conscience, and now, at the end, his hand did not shake at all.

*Boy,* said the voices inside him, and there were so many of them now, a congregation, a tide: hedge-witches and court mages, midwives and rain-callers, the woman who had taught Zephyrine and the man who had taught her, and further back, name after name after name, back to the ones whose names the war had eaten. *Boy. Are you certain?*

"No," Xanthe said honestly. "I've never been certain of anything in my life. Ask anyone. I once couldn't decide between two kinds of bread for a full hour." A ripple went through the watching soldiers — a laugh strangled at birth, nervous and human. "But I know this. A grave is not a memory. A memory is supposed to *move*. It's supposed to get told wrong at weddings and argued over at funerals and embroidered until it barely resembles the truth, because that's how the living carry the dead. This—" he lifted his palm, and the sigil blazed, and every carving on the cavern walls blazed with it, "—this isn't carrying. This is hoarding. We made a prison and called it love, and everyone who ever bore it got eaten alive by other people's yesterdays. I've lost my sister's face to this thing. I've grieved husbands I never had and spoken in tongues that died before my grandfather was born. And it would keep going. It would find another vessel, and another, forever, because that's what cages do."

"And us?" said a voice from the crowd of soldiers — a rebel woman, mud-splattered, a hunter's arrow still quivering in her shield. "If you break it, what happens to *them*? The ones inside?"

"They stop being property," Xanthe said. "Of the crown. Of thieves. Of the dead themselves. They go out — into the air, into all of you, into everyone. No one owns them ever again. Everyone carries a piece. That's not a cage. That's—" he groped for the word, and found it waiting, planted long ago in a cottage garden while an old witch pretended not to watch him fail at knots. "*That's inheritance.*"

Odile moved.

She was faster than anything human had a right to be, and later Xanthe would understand she had been moving before he finished speaking, that she had heard the word *everyone* and understood in one cold instant what it meant for her — for the woman who had built herself out of stolen centuries precisely so that no one else could ever have what she had taken. Her hand closed on his wrist like a shackle.

"Don't," she said. "Don't you dare make me nothing, boy. Three hundred years. Do you know what I gave up to last this long? Every face I loved, traded away, one at a time, so I would never have to lose—" Her voice cracked down the middle, and out of the crack fell something young and ruined and real. "So I would never have to lose anyone *again*."

"I know," Xanthe said, and meant it, and hated that he meant it. "That's the whole tragedy, Odile. You stopped losing people by making sure you were never really with any of them. I did the smaller version of the same thing for twelve years. Hid so well I nearly disappeared." He turned his wrist in her grip — and the sigil answered him, flaring, and she gasped and let go, because the mark knew its vessel and would not be pickpocketed, not here, not on the threshold of its own grave. "You asked me once if it was ripe. It is. But it was never fruit. It was never anyone's to eat."

He raised his hand and brought it down, edge-on, against the oldest carving — the first knot, the anchor-stone, the seam where the cage had been sewn shut over a slaughtered people.

It did not break like stone. It broke like ice on a spring pond.

A sound went through the cavern — not loud, at first. A crystalline *tick*, like a hearthstone settling, and then the crack raced outward through the black rock in veins of light, and the light was not white and it was not gold; it was every color that has ever been forgotten, and it rose. Out of the shattered lattice it rose, out of ten thousand broken sigils, up through the salt-weeping stone, and it was not light at all. It was voices. It was faces. It was the dead, unbraided at last from the knot that held them, pouring upward past the startled soldiers, past Odile with her hands pressed to her hollowed face, past Corvane standing rigid as a man at his own trial.

They passed through people as they went. Xanthe felt them — oh, he felt them. A rain-caller from the eastern steppes brushed through a crown hunter and the man sat down abruptly on the cavern floor, weeping for a homeland he had never seen. A midwife's ghost slid through a rebel soldier and the woman laughed out loud, sudden and helpless, at a joke four hundred years old. Memory after memory settled into the crowd like snow, like seeds, finding soil everywhere it touched.

And Xanthe felt himself coming back.

Not all at once. It came like the tide returning, over things he had thought were lost forever: the smell of his sister's hair, cheap tallow and riverwater. The exact wrongness of the third note in her lullaby, which he alone in all the world had ever sung correctly. The taste of burned porridge in Zephyrine's kitchen and her voice saying *you'll live, boy, worse scholars than you have survived my cooking.* His name. His own name, in his own mother's voice, and after it, gently, all the things that were his, item by item, the list unfolding in him like a hand opening.

He was crying. He noted it remotely, the way one notes weather. Around him, everyone was. Even Odile — especially Odile — stood in the rising flood of light with tears cutting channels through the dust on her face, and as one of the dead, a young man with a scholar's ink-stained fingers, drifted through her, she said a name. Just one. Softly. As if answering a question she had waited three hundred years to be asked.

Corvane had not moved. The light poured through him and he bore it like a sentence, and when a small fierce woman with a midwife's hands brushed past him, he whispered, "Grandmother," and she paused — she *paused*, one shade among thousands — and laid something weightless on his bowed head before she went.

Then the last of them rose, and the light went with them, up through the stone, up through the citadel's foundations, up into the night above the city, where two armies looked up from their broken lines and saw the sky fill with the departed.

The cavern went dark. Ordinary dark. Salt water dripped somewhere. The sword in the floor hummed faintly and was still.

Xanthe sat down, quite suddenly, on a chunk of broken cage. His palm was bare. For the first time in months it was only a hand, chapped and ordinary and entirely, blessedly his.

"Well," he said, to no one in particular. His voice came out wrecked and wobbling and, he noticed with distant approval, entirely his own accent, his own rhythm, his own self. "That's that sorted."

Above them, muffled by stone, the armies had stopped fighting. In the silence that followed, Xanthe could hear footsteps — many, uncertain, converging on the cavern mouth — and Corvane straightening slowly, drawing breath as if for the hardest speech of his life. And Odile, kneeling in the ruin of everything she had hoarded, began very quietly to laugh, or to weep, or both.

The first soldiers reached the threshold, torches raised, and stared at the shattered grave of the ancient war, at the witch sitting in its wreckage grinning through his tears, at the Inquisitor removing his badge of office and letting it fall into the salt water — and one of them, a rebel with mud on her boots and a stranger's homeland suddenly warm in her chest, asked the question that would remake the world:

"What *was* that?"

Xanthe opened his mouth to answer — and found, to his astonishment and delight, that he had absolutely no idea how to begin.
