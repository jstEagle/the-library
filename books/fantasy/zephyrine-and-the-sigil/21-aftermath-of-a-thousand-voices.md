## 21. Aftermath of a Thousand Voices

The baker's wife, Maren Cotter, was kneading dough when her grandmother died at the Battle of Vellamare.

Her grandmother had actually died forty years ago in bed, peacefully, after a life of complaining about damp weather. But on the morning the sky over the citadel split open like a dropped egg, Maren's hands stopped mid-fold and she was somewhere else entirely — standing on a green hill outside a city that had no walls yet, holding a staff of woven rowan, watching fire come down from the clouds.

She screamed. Then the memory passed through her like weather and she was back in her kitchen, flour to the elbows, weeping into bread that would turn out, despite everything, rather good.

By noon, half of Vellamare had stopped working. Not because they were ordered to. Because a fishwife on the Salt Quay had burst into a language no living throat had spoken in three hundred years, and a customs officer had answered her in it, and the two of them had stood there holding hands like strangers who had just discovered they were siblings, which in some unfathomable way, they now were.

The dead were everywhere. Not ghosts — nothing so tidy. Fragments. A soldier's last thought of home lodged behind a cooper's eyes. A healer's knowledge of poultices surfacing in a boy of nine who had never dressed a wound in his life. An old woman's recipe for plum brandy arrived whole and perfect in the mind of a man who hated plums.

They called it the Settling, though nobody agreed on who had named it. It took eleven days for the worst to pass, and in those eleven days the city of Vellamare did not so much grieve as *grieve out loud*, publicly, in streets and taverns and doorways, ten thousand people mourning people they had never met and somehow now missed.

On the twelfth day, Maren Cotter put a sign in her bakery window that read: **MAGE BREAD — RISES BY ITSELF, DON'T ASK HOW.**

It sold out by eight.

* * *

Odile did not cry easily. She had stolen the habit of crying from someone, centuries ago, and had never cared for how it fit her.

She sat in the wrecked undercroft beneath the citadel, where the war-site lay open to the sky now — the crown's engineers had peeled back the vaulting like the lid off a pot — and she turned her empty hands over and over, looking at them as if they belonged to a stranger.

Which was, she realized, precisely the problem. They didn't belong to a stranger. For the first time in four hundred years, her hands belonged to *no one*.

She had carried thirty-one lives when she came to Vellamare. Borrowed faces, borrowed names, a smuggler's knowledge of tides, a nun's fluency in prayer, a king's memory of betraying his brother — all of it taken, all of it hers, all of it stacked inside her like stolen books in a house with no shelves. She had told Xanthe Sandoval that she wanted a share of the sigil. She had told herself she wanted all of it.

Then he had broken the thing, and every memory in it had gone up like seed on the wind, and the wind had not consulted her.

Half of what she'd stolen was simply gone — dissolved into the general inheritance, scattered among fishwives and bakers and customs men who would wake tomorrow remembering a smuggler's tide-tables without knowing why. The other half had *stayed* only long enough to recognize itself, and then left too, gently, the way a bird leaves an open hand. As if the memories themselves, once freed, had preferred exile to captivity. Even captivity in her.

She understood it. That was the humiliating part. She had spent four centuries telling herself she was a rescuer of sorts — that memories would die otherwise, that someone had to carry them, that carrying was its own kind of love. And then one tired hedge-witch's apprentice with ink-stained fingers had cracked a stone in his fist and every memory she'd ever hoarded had looked at her — she could not describe it any other way — had looked at her and *chosen the air.*

"They didn't want you," she said aloud to the empty undercroft. Her voice echoed off stones that still smelled faintly of old blood. "Four hundred years, and not one of them wanted—"

A boot scraped above. A young woman peered down the stair — one of Odile's rebels, or former rebels; allegiances had gone soft as overcooked greens since the Shattering. "Begging pardon. The others are asking. Are you still — I mean. Are we still doing the revolution?"

Odile looked at her own hands again.

"No," she said, and was surprised to find the word cost her nothing. "Go home. Learn your grandmother's name. All of them, if you can manage it."

The girl hesitated at the top of the stairs. "There's a rumor," she offered, because rumors were the last currency still circulating, "that the witch who broke it — Sandoval — is going to teach. Openly. Children and everything. Down by the harbor."

Something moved in Odile's chest, and she let herself hope, briefly, that it was one of her stolen sorrows finally taking its leave.

"Good," she said. "Someone should write down how it's done before we forget we ever knew."

* * *

Inquisitor Corvane resigned by letter, which was the only way he knew how to do anything — in triplicate, witnessed, filed.

He wrote it at his own desk, in his own office, on the morning after the citadel fell silent, and he found the words came harder than any confession he had ever extracted. *I hereby tender...* No. *Effective immediately...* He crossed it out. In the end he wrote only: *I was wrong about the nature of the thing I was hunting, and I cannot un-know it, and a man should not keep a job he can no longer do honestly.* He signed it, blotted it, and sat looking at his hands, which had caged a woman who loved him by proxy — who had, in her final message, called him *child* and meant it.

His grandmother had been burned in the Last Hunt. He had learned this from a dead woman's riddle, delivered with the precision of a knife laid on clean linen. He had spent six weeks since then not thinking about it, with the tremendous discipline of a man who had made not-thinking a profession.

Now, everywhere he walked in Vellamare, other people's griefs brushed against him like coats in a crowded hall. A guard at the citadel gate had wept on duty — wept in a dead tongue, for a brother lost at a battle Corvane had spent his career insisting had been fought against monsters. A clerk in his own office confessed, red-faced, that she could now light candles with a word and had been doing so secretly for eleven days and was very sorry and also it was lovely.

The registry — his registry, his life's work — listed four thousand names of the gifted. Within a fortnight of the Settling, the honest number of people in Vellamare carrying a fragment of magic was closer to everyone, and the fragments did not sit still. They mingled. They taught each other. A dockworker who remembered siege-craft traded it in a tavern for a midwife's charm against bleeding, and neither of them thought to report it, because there was no longer any such thing as reporting it. You could no more arrest the city than you could arrest the tide.

Corvane had built his life on the principle that magic was a wolf and the crown the fence. He had believed the fence was necessary. What the Shattering had shown him — rudely, at volume, in front of witnesses — was that the crown had built the fence around a graveyard, and charged admission, and called the gravestones wolves.

He submitted his resignation. The Lord Chancellor refused it twice, then accepted it with the expression of a man setting down something heavy near his foot and pretending not to notice.

On his last day, Corvane cleaned out his office and found, at the bottom of a locked drawer he did not remember locking, a folded paper in a hand he recognized — small, wry, unhurried. Zephyrine's hand. He had confiscated her effects months ago; this must have slipped through, or been planted, which with her had always been the same thing.

It said: *You'll know when to stop hunting. You'll know because you'll start grieving instead. Don't fight it. Grief is just love with nowhere left to go — give it somewhere to go.*

Underneath, in the same hand, a postscript: *The boy will need a schoolhouse. You're good at paperwork.*

Corvane sat in his empty office for a long time. Then he laughed — once, harshly, the laugh of a man ambushed by his own tears — and put the note in his breast pocket, and went to see about a schoolhouse.

* * *

Xanthe woke on the first morning of the rest of his life and, before opening his eyes, performed the ritual.

*My name is Xanthe Sandoval. My sister's lullaby goes:* — and here he sang it, under his breath, in the dark of his rented room above the harbor — *and it goes like that, mine, wrong in the second verse the way my sister always sang it wrong, and nobody else's version will do.*

He got it right. He got it right most mornings now. Some mornings he got it right but had to check, and those mornings were harder, but Corvane had assembled — with the terrifying efficiency of a converted inquisitor — a file on Xanthe Sandoval thicker than any heresy case he'd ever worked. His school records. The smugglers' ledger with his purchases noted in a dead man's careful hand. A statement from the dockworker who'd bought his very first luck-knot, twelve years ago, who remembered it because the knot worked and his wife's fever broke that night. Evidence. Outside proof of an inside self. Xanthe read it sometimes the way other men read letters from home.

The city outside his window was loud in a new way. For twelve years Vellamare had been quiet in the specific manner of places where everyone is minding everyone else's business for the authorities. Now it was loud in the manner of a market where the goods were grief and wonder and nobody was charging for either.

He walked to the harbor past the bakery with the sign — MAGE BREAD — and past the fishwife and the customs officer, who now ran a Sunday class in their extinct language and had attracted six students, all strangers to each other, all suddenly cousins. He passed two children arguing over whose memory of the Battle was right, until they discovered they were both right, because there had been two brothers on opposite hills, and the children shook hands with the solemnity of diplomats.

At the old salt shed Corvane had requisitioned — paperwork immaculate, windows newly glazed — Xanthe pushed open the door and found the room already occupied.

Zephyrine's hearthstone sat at the center, salvaged from Graywater, split down the middle and deliberately left so. *Leave the crack,* her memory had instructed him, once, in a tower cell a hundred miles away. *Let them see what breaking looks like. It's the most instructive thing a stone can do.*

Around it, on mismatched chairs, sat the first class: the cooper's boy who could dress wounds. Maren Cotter, on her day off, wanting to understand why her bread rose. A gray-haired smuggler pretending he'd come in out of the rain. And at the back, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man auditing his own redemption, Inquisitor — former Inquisitor — Corvane.

"You're late," Corvane said.

"I'm the teacher."

"Then your students have been discussing pedagogy without you. The boy wants to know if the memories are souls." Corvane's mouth did something complicated, not quite a smile. "I told him it was above my former pay grade."

Xanthe set his satchel down beside the cracked hearthstone and looked at them all — frightened, curious, carrying their strange inheritances like parcels wrapped in somebody else's paper — and felt the loneliness of chapter one of his life rise up in him one more time, so he could see exactly how far away it was.

"They're not souls," he said. "Souls go wherever souls go. These are memories, which is different, and possibly better. Souls you can't argue with. Memories you can. So." He pulled out chalk. "First lesson. How to tell what's yours."

At the back of the room, unnoticed by anyone, a woman with four hundred years of stolen faces behind her eyes stood in the doorway, listening — and when the cooper's boy answered a question in a dead woman's voice, Odile felt something settle in her at last, something with weight, like a debt being paid to the wrong address but paid nonetheless.

She did not come in. Not yet. But she wrote the address of the salt shed on the inside of her wrist, next to the faded ghost of a tattoo she'd stolen from a sailor in another century — and under it, in her own hand, for the first time in four hundred years signing her own name:

*Odile. Things that are mine: one.*
