## 22. A New List

The school had no name yet, which the children considered a scandal and Xanthe considered a mercy.

"A school should have a *name*," insisted Pip Wren, who was nine, gap-toothed, and possessed of the kind of certainty that would one day either build bridges or burn them down. She said this every morning, standing at the door of the old granary on Lantern Row while the other children filed past her with their slates and their apples and their assorted small animals.

"Name it yourself, then," Xanthe said, as he did every morning.

"I named it yesterday. You didn't like it."

"I liked it fine."

"You said 'hmm' in a way that meant no."

Xanthe set his satchel on the teacher's desk — a door laid across two barrels, because Vellamare was still a city of salvaged things — and looked out at the room. Twenty-three children this season, ranging from six to fourteen, from families of fishwives and chandlers and one extremely apologetic tax collector. Eleven of them could do something. Not all the same something, and not much of anything yet: a girl who made candle flames lean toward her like sunflowers; a boy whose knots untied themselves when he lied; twins who could find each other blindfolded across the whole of the Salt Market, which their mother said was less magic than a nuisance.

A year ago, every one of those children would have learned to hide it or died learning. A year ago, Xanthe would have taught them to hide it himself, gently, the way Zephyrine had never taught him — because she had refused, and he had run from that refusal for twelve years.

"Today," he announced, "we're doing lists."

A groan went up, theatrical and collective. Lists were writing practice, and writing practice was work.

"Not *those* lists," he said. "Better ones."

He chalked the title on the board in his careful, unlovely hand:

**THINGS THAT ARE MINE**

"Every day," he told them, "you write one thing. Just one. Something that belongs to you and nobody else. It can be big." He tapped the board. "It can be small." He held up the stub of chalk between two fingers. "It can be strange. Yesterday mine was *the exact sound the harbor bell makes when the fog is coming in*. Nobody else in the world has that one. It's mine."

Pip's hand shot up. "What if someone else writes the same thing?"

"Then you're both wrong," said the boy with the self-untangling knots, whose name was Tam and who had been born knowing more about other people than was strictly polite.

"No," Xanthe said, smiling. "Then you've found each other. That's different."

He let them write. He walked the rows out of habit, though there was nothing to catch anyone at anymore, no charm to confiscate, no gift to smother under a blanket of ordinary. The candle-flame girl, Sefa, had written *my grandmother's laugh, which I can do now* and was practicing it under her breath, badly, wonderfully. One of the twins had written *the left half of everything*, which Xanthe chose not to examine too closely.

At the bottom of his own slate, where they couldn't see, he kept his own list. He'd started it over a year ago, in a hollowed-out tower cell of grief, back when the entries were life rafts: *my name is Xanthe Sandoval. I had a sister who sang me a lullaby about a boat. I burned my stock of charms in a copper kettle.* Back then he'd written them like a man nailing doors shut against a flood.

Now the list lived differently. Now it grew.

*Things that are mine:* the harbor bell in fog. The way Pip Wren says "scandal." Burnt honey on porridge, which he had discovered only after Odile — but that was his now; she couldn't take it back. The particular ache in his left hand on cold mornings, which was the sigil's parting gift and which he had decided, after long argument with himself, to keep. A scar on his palm shaped like nothing at all, where the sigil had been, smooth as river stone.

He still dreamed in borrowed voices sometimes. He suspected he always would. But the dreams came now like letters from distant cousins rather than burglars in the night — memories with addresses attached, returnable if he wished. When he woke grieving a husband with river-grey eyes, he could say: *that was Maren-of-the-Ninth-Generation, and I am sorry for her loss, and it is not mine to carry alone,* and set it down beside his own pillow instead of on top of it.

That was the whole trick, really. The one thing the sigil had never understood, being a cage built by desperate people in a war: memory wasn't meant to be *kept*. It was meant to be shared, passed hand to hand like bread, until no one could say precisely whose it was and everyone could say *ours.*

He'd shattered a thousand-year-old prison to learn that. Pip could probably have told him in an afternoon.

"*Master Sandoval?*" Sefa had her hand up. "Can a thing be yours if you got it from somebody dead?"

Xanthe crouched by her desk. "Best question anyone's asked me all week, and I include the tax collector's."

"But can it?"

He thought of Zephyrine's hands, flour-dusted, folding a luck-knot wrong on purpose so he'd correct her. He thought of the lullaby about the boat, which he now knew had been sung in nine generations of his sister's line before it ever reached him, and which was still, unmistakably, *his.*

"Yes," he said. "But you have to make it yours again. Every time you use it, you add a little bit of you to it. Like — like soup. Everyone who cooks it changes it. It's still the same soup."

"That's disgusting," said Tam.

"It's beautiful," said Sefa.

"It's both," said Xanthe. "Most true things are. Write it down."

* * *

School let out at noon, as it did every sixth day, and Xanthe walked the long way to Graywater, because the long way was his.

Vellamare had changed in a year the way a shoreline changes — not all at once, but undeniably. Charms hung openly in shop windows now, priced honestly, labeled plainly: *fever-ease, fair winds, mending.* Some of them were rubbish. Quite a lot of them were rubbish, sold by enterprising frauds, and the city had responded by developing a robust culture of reviews scrawled on slate boards outside each stall, which Xanthe found funnier than any of the actual charms. *Bought a love-charm here. My husband remains exactly as annoying. Two stars.*

There were schools like his in three districts now, and a hall where adults came in the evenings to learn what their grandmothers had whispered, and a council — an actual elected council, gods help them all — that included two hedge-witches and a very tired former smuggler named Brannic who still greeted Xanthe with the word "*trouble*" in place of hello, but warmly.

And everywhere, woven through the city like gold thread through rough cloth, were the fragments. The released memories of the sigil, settled into ten thousand strangers. The fishwife on the quay who hummed a mourning song from a country that had been underwater for four hundred years. The guard captain who woke some mornings fluent in a dialect no living scholar spoke, and had begun teaching it, delightedly, to anyone who'd listen. People grieved losses that weren't theirs and loved people they'd never met, and slowly, clumsily, they had stopped calling it madness and started calling it inheritance. There were arguments. Of course there were arguments. There were philosophers making fortunes and priests having crises and at least three new festivals invented in a single year, two of which appeared to be the same festival.

But no one hunted. No one registered. No one hid.

Xanthe passed the granary on Lantern Row and touched its doorframe — his doorframe, his school, his scandalously unnamed school — and turned up the hill toward Graywater.

* * *

The cottage had been rebuilt by volunteers, which was to say by people who owed Zephyrine nothing and everything. Brannic had hauled the timber. Sefa's grandmother, who remembered the old woman distantly from market days, had re-thatched the roof and complained about it the entire time, which everyone agreed was the appropriate tribute. Children from Xanthe's school had pressed their handprints into the fresh plaster of the eastern wall, and rather than smooth them away, the builders had left them, so the cottage wore a constellation of small palms like a blessing.

The hearthstone was new. That had taken longest. They'd quarried it from the same seam of gray granite as the original, and Corvane himself — retired Inquisitor Corvane, who wore plain wool now and flinched slightly at his own old title — had carried it up the hill on a handcart, alone, refusing help, sweating, stubborn, in what Xanthe recognized as the man's native language of apology.

It sat in the rebuilt hearth now, whole and unsplit, and carved into its face — done by Corvane, with a chisel, over three days, badly at first and then well — was the mark that had once been a sigil and a cage and a mass grave and a key.

Zephyrine's garden had come back on its own. Nobody had planted it. Rosemary and feverfew and something blue and nameless crowded the doorway, and the bees that lived in the south wall produced honey that tasted faintly of burnt sugar, which Xanthe took as a personal joke from beyond whatever lay beyond.

He sat on the doorstep, as he did every sixth day, and talked to her.

Not prayer. He'd tried prayer once, early on, and it had felt like shouting into a well. This was more like — reporting. A student to a teacher. An apprentice to a master, twelve years late and finally caught up.

"The school still doesn't have a name," he said. "Pip has proposed eleven. Today's was *The House of Very Important Secrets,* which I rejected on the grounds that we don't have any secrets, which is rather the point." He pulled a heel of bread and the honey jar from his satchel. "Tam asked why we bother learning wards at all if nobody's going to attack us. I told him wards are like fences — you build them for the neighbor's goats, not for the wolf. He wrote that down, which frightens me. He'll be running this city by thirty."

The bees droned. Somewhere down the hill, the harbor bell rang, clear, not yet foggy.

"I dreamed about Maren again last week," he went on. "The one with the husband. Only this time I dreamed the *end* of it, the part the sigil cut off when it broke, and she was old, and happy, and her husband grew the worst tomatoes in recorded history and she loved him anyway. I woke up crying, but it was — good crying. If that makes sense. It felt like finishing a book."

He spread the honey on the bread. Burnt sugar and rosemary. His, now. He added it to the list without breaking stride, the way you breathe.

"I tested myself again last month," he said, quieter. "The lullaby. About the boat." For a long time after the shattering, he hadn't dared — not since the day he'd sung it and gotten the third verse wrong and sat on the floor of his rented room understanding that some part of him had gone out with the sigil's light, dissolved into ten thousand strangers, irrecoverable. "I got it right. All seven verses. And then I realized—" he laughed, short and wondering— "I realized it doesn't matter whether I got it right because it's *mine* or because some fragment landed in the right head at last. Sefa asked me if a thing can be yours if you got it from somebody dead. I told her yes, if you make it yours again. I think I finally believed my own lesson."

The wind moved through the feverfew. It didn't answer, obviously. But he'd spent a year learning the difference between silence and absence, and this was silence — companionable, patient, smelling of herbs and old smoke. She was in the stone and the garden and the seven verses and the way he folded a luck-knot correctly, which was itself a kind of inheritance, and none of it needed keeping. It just needed visiting.

He stayed until the light went long and gold across the harbor. Then he stood, brushed off his coat, and did what he did every visit: he took out his slate and added to the list, out loud, in the open air, freely.

"One: the harbor bell in fog. Two: burnt honey. Three: the way the light comes through the granary windows at noon, which looks like the inside of amber. Four: twenty-three students and one scandalized Pip Wren. Five: a scar that aches in cold weather and means I survived something. Six: a master who taught me everything twice — once when I ran, and once when I came back."

He paused, chalk hovering.

"Seven," he said, and smiled at the hearthstone, at the carved mark, at the whole improbable, unfinished, shared world. "Seven: tomorrow. Which isn't mine yet. But I'm making it so."

He tucked the slate away and started down the hill toward Lantern Row, where tomorrow was already waiting, unnamed and full of children.

Behind him, in the rebuilt hearth of the cottage at Graywater, the carved mark caught the last of the sun — and for half a heartbeat, no longer than a bee's wingbeat, it seemed to warm, the way stone does when someone has just been sitting near it.

Xanthe didn't look back.

He didn't need to. Some things you carry by leaving them behind.
