## 1. Charms for the Godless

The night market of Vantrelle opened the way a wound does—slowly, reluctantly, and only after dark. By the time Wendell Delacroix had hung their lanterns along the stall's crooked frame, the lamplighters had finished their rounds and the city had settled into its favorite posture: shoulders hunched, eyes down, pretending very hard that nothing had ever burned.

Seven years since the Edict. Seven years since the temples were gutted and their bells melted down for cannon, since the last priestess was paraded through Ashgate Square with her vestments pinned to her skin. Vantrelle had learned to worship quietly, or not at all, or in the furtive way that people worship anything forbidden—with a hunger they were ashamed of.

Wendell sold them little glass charms.

They sat behind a table scarred by candle-heat and arranged their wares the way they did every night: small orbs of blown glass, each one holding a swirl of color like smoke caught mid-sigh. Memory-charms, though no one in Vantrelle called them that anymore. Officially they were *keepsakes*. Unofficially, every buyer knew what they were getting—a stolen hour from someone else's life, decanted and bottled, harmless as a lullaby if you knew how to ask it to sing.

Wendell had gotten very good at asking.

*Take only what won't be missed,* was their rule, older than the Edict, older than the fear. A baker's pride in a perfect loaf. A child's delight at first snow. The particular peace of a nap taken in afternoon sun. Nothing load-bearing. Nothing structural. They had spent seven years building a reputation as the most boring thief in Vantrelle, and boring, in this city, was a kind of armor.

The widow arrived an hour after moonrise.

Wendell marked her before she'd crossed half the square—the way she moved, like someone following a map drawn on the inside of her eyelids. She was perhaps sixty, wrapped in grey wool gone shiny at the elbows, and she stopped at the edge of the lantern-light as if the light itself might cost her something.

"Help you?" Wendell asked, in the flat, friendly register they'd perfected. *Nothing to see here. Just glass and candle-wax.*

"I'm told—" The widow's voice cracked like river ice. She started again. "I'm told you sell memories."

"I sell keepsakes." Wendell gestured at the table. "Little comforts. A summer afternoon. A wedding morning. The taste of honeycomb straight off the comb."

"I want his laugh."

There it was. Wendell kept their hands still on the tablecloth and let three full seconds pass, because grief deserved at least that much silence.

"Whose laugh?"

"My husband's. Forty-one years we were married. He died in the winter fever, and I find that I—" Her jaw worked. "I can hear everything else. His coughing. His snoring. The way he said my name when he was annoyed. But the laugh is gone. It went first, like it knew it was needed most and so it left me something to carry."

Wendell looked at the woman and thought, with the weariness of long practice: *this is where I tell her no.*

That was the other rule. Never sell anyone their own dead. Not because it couldn't be done—Wendell could lift a memory out of a room the way other people lifted dust, could pull the echo of a man's laugh off the walls of a house he'd lived in for forty years—but because the ones who came asking for their dead never came back to buy again. They came back to sit at the stall until the lanterns burned out, listening to someone else's happiness in a bottle, and Wendell could not afford that kind of haunting. Neither could the business. Neither, frankly, could Wendell.

But the widow stood there in her shabby wool, and the market breathed around them—frying onions and wet stone and the far-off clatter of the tannery district—and Wendell heard themselves say, "I don't have his laugh. But I can go get it. If you give me three nights and the name of his favorite chair."

Her face did something complicated. "It's by the east window. He watched the gulls."

"Gulls," Wendell repeated, and wrote nothing down, because names given over grief were not for writing. "Three nights. Then come back."

She paid the deposit in coins worn smooth as sea-glass, and when her fingers brushed Wendell's palm—

Something *stirred.*

It happened deep, below the sternum, in the place where Wendell kept the oldest thefts locked away behind doors they did not visit. Like a sleeper turning over. Like a coal, banked seven years, deciding on a whim to remember it was fire.

Wendell did not flinch. Flinching was for people who could afford to be noticed. They counted out change with steady hands and wished the widow good evening and watched her go until the dark folded her up and took her home.

Then they sat down very slowly on their stool.

*No,* they told the thing stirring under their ribs. *Not now. Not ever, ideally.* Because Wendell knew what they carried. They carried hundreds of pilfered nothings, harmless hours, but beneath those, sealed in wax and will, lay the memories they had taken during the worst year of their life—the year of the purge, the year of blood in the gutters of the temple quarter, the year Wendell Delacroix survived by any means necessary. And one of those memories had just rolled over in its sleep, and it was heavy, and it was old, and it was waking up.

They poured themselves the dregs of cold chicory and drank it without tasting it, and told themselves it was indigestion.

* *

The prayer came at the dead hour, when even the market's rats had knocked off work.

Wendell stayed late sometimes—not out of diligence but because the stall was quieter than their rooms, which had walls thin enough to gossip through. They were trimming the wicks when they heard footsteps stop beyond the lantern-light. A young man. Dock-worker's hands, ink-stained cuffs. He didn't approach. He stood in the dark like a man testing river ice, and then he knelt, right there in the mud of the market square, and pressed his forehead to the ground.

Wendell should have looked away. Worshippers were dangerous; worshippers drew the Wardens like flies to a wound. But the man was whispering, and whispering carries, and the words drifted across the square on the smell of rain:

*"Grandmother Zephyrine, who sleeps under the mountain, who keeps our names in her keeping—wake, if you can wake. Hear, if you can hear. My sister is sick and the physicians cost more than boats. Wake, Grandmother. We are still yours."*

Old words. Older than the Edict. Words from the liturgy of a god whose name hadn't been spoken aloud in Vantrelle for decades except as curse or joke—Zephyrine of the Spire, the fire-that-listens, the last dragon, the grandmother under the mountain. The temples had taught once that she heard every prayer the way mountains hear rain: patiently, and without answering.

The dock-worker finished. He rose. He wiped his face with his sleeve and walked away into the dark, leaving Wendell alone with the lanterns and the cooling night and a silence that had just, unmistakably, changed shape.

Because the silence answered.

Not in words. In warmth. A breath of heat crossed the square like something enormous turning over in its sleep several miles distant, and every flame in Wendell's stall leaned—*leaned*, all eleven of them, in perfect unison—toward the mountain at the city's edge. The wax guttered. The glass charms rang against each other, a sound like tiny teeth, and in the deepest vault of Wendell's own memory, behind doors sealed with seven years of careful forgetting, something sat up and *recognized its master.*

Wendell gripped the edge of the table hard enough to hurt. Their heart slammed. The air smelled suddenly of sulfur and cedar and a courtroom they had sworn never to think about, and beneath their ribs the waking memory uncoiled like smoke finding a draft, and the first fragment surfaced, whole and terrible:

*A door of black stone. A voice like coins being counted. Their own, younger, terrified voice saying:* take anything else. Take my hands. Take my name. *And the voice answering:* the court accepts souls only, little thief. Yours, or your life. Choose.

"No," Wendell said aloud, to no one, to the flames, to the mountain. "No, that's paid. That's done. That was the price and I *paid it.*"

The lanterns steadied. The night resumed its ordinary hush, as though nothing had happened, as though gods still slept.

But Wendell Delacroix sat in the dark a long time, watching the flames burn true toward the mountain, and understood with cold, absolute clarity that the bargains made in desperate years were coming due—that somewhere beneath Vantrelle, a dragon-fire was guttering, and the courts below were gathering up every soul-note, every debt, every scrap of paper written in blood and terror.

Including theirs.

Three nights until the widow returned. Wendell blew out the lanterns one by one, saving the last flame for last—the one that pointed, patient as a compass needle, home.

Then they pulled their coat tight, and went to find a priestess.
