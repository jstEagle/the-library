## 2. The Debt Beneath

The memory came back the way floodwater comes back to a cellar: slowly at first, a dampness in the corners of the mind, and then all at once, everywhere, rising.

Wendell woke on the third night after the market with salt on their lips and their hands pressed flat against the floorboards as if the room were tilting. The garret above the dye-shop was cold enough that their breath showed, but the cold was not what had woken them. What had woken them was a smell—mountain stone and tallow smoke and something older underneath, something like iron left too long in rain.

They knew that smell. They had spent seven years making sure they would never know it again.

*You will forget this,* they had told themselves, the way you tell yourself anything you cannot afford to remember. *You will forget the stair, the torchlight, the voice that counted your name among its holdings.* And for seven years the forgetting had held, sealed under layers of other people's memories—the widow's wedding day, the fishmonger's first kill, a hundred small joys bought and sold like buttons—until the gods came back and everything old began to stir.

Now it rose.

* * *

Seven years ago, Wendell Delacroix was seven years old and running.

They remembered that much without any god's help: the running. Vantrelle in the Year of the Cull, when the magistrates decided that magic was a rot in the city's teeth and began pulling it out one practitioner at a time. Wendell's mother had been a hedge-witch of no great power—a woman who could coax beans from stubborn soil and fevers out of children—and that had been enough. Enough for the black wagons. Enough for the lists nailed to every door.

Her name had been on the list. Wendell's name had been under hers, in smaller letters, the way a child's name is written when it is an afterthought, an attachment, a thing carried along.

She had pressed them into a culvert behind the tannery row and said, *Go down. Go down and bargain if you must. They respect bargains down there. They are the only ones who ever have.*

And Wendell, seven years old, smelling of tallow smoke from a house already burning behind them, had gone down.

* * *

The mountain court did not look like a court. It looked like a throat.

Stairs spiraled into the dark beneath Vantrelle, cut so long ago that no living mason claimed them, worn smooth in the centers by feet that had been descending for a thousand years. Torches burned without flame—dragon-fire, though Wendell hadn't known the word then, only that the light was warm and red and seemed to breathe. It rose and fell along the walls like the chest of some enormous sleeping animal, and perhaps that was exactly what it was.

At the bottom there had been water. Black water, still as glass, and across it a bridge of pale bone—or something like bone—and beyond the bridge, benches carved from the living rock, and upon the benches, shapes.

Wendell remembered trying not to look at the judges. They remembered looking anyway.

They were not monsters. That was the terrible part, the part the child's mind had refused and the waking memory now delivered whole: the judges looked like people. Tired people. Old people. A woman with ink-stained fingers and a magistrate's chain of office, except the chain was made of small linked vertebrae. A man whose face kept forgetting itself, sliding between expressions like a man dreaming. And above them all, on a bench higher than the rest, something that wore the shape of a tall figure robed in grey, whose hood held no face at all, only a depth, a patience, an attention.

*A soul,* the hooded one had said, and its voice had not come through the air but through the stone, through the water, through the soles of Wendell's shoes. *A soul is the oldest coin. We accept nothing newer.*

*I don't have anything else,* Wendell had said. Seven years old, voice cracking, fists clenched so the nails bit. *Take it. Take my soul. Just let me live.*

Silence, then, long enough that the child had begun to think the bargain refused, that the black water would rise, that the wagons above would find them after all.

*Witnessed,* the hooded one had said at last. *Entered. Sealed until the fire fails or the gods return, whichever comes last.* And the dragon-light along the walls had flared once, bright as a struck match, and dimmed again, and somewhere far above, Wendell had heard the black wagons pass by on the road and keep going.

They had climbed back up into a city that no longer wanted them dead, carrying nothing visible, owing everything invisible. They had learned, in the years after, to steal other people's memories and sell them as charms—not because it was honest work, but because a person with no soul of their own needs something to fill the space, and other people's joy fit the hollow almost perfectly.

Almost.

* * *

Wendell came back to themselves on the floor of the garret with dawn graying the window and their cheeks wet and their jaw aching from clenching. They lay still a long time, listening to the dye-shop below begin its morning noises, vats sloshing, the apprentice singing off-key.

*Sealed until the fire fails or the gods return.*

The gods had returned. Everyone in Vantrelle felt it now, the way you feel weather changing in an old wound. Prayers answered in the streets. Shrines weeping real tears. And the dragon-fire under the mountain—if it guttered, if the courts moved to finalize every outstanding bargain before the old order ended—

Then Wendell's soul belonged to someone else forever. Or worse: it was called due, collected, dragged down those worn stairs while the body it had abandoned went on walking around Vantrelle, selling charms, smiling at widows, empty.

They sat up. Their hands were shaking. Seven years of careful invisibility, of being no one worth noticing, and it had all been built on a debt they couldn't even remember owing.

*I sold myself,* they thought, and the shame of it was worse than fear. Not stolen from. Not robbed. Sold. Volunteered. A seven-year-old's choice, yes—but a choice, made with open eyes and a burning house behind them, and every year since had been lived on the proceeds.

* * *

Sable Ondine knelt in the ruined chapel of Saint Verene with her palms flat on the floor, the way she had knelt every morning for six years, praying to gods who did not answer.

This morning, the floor answered back.

It was subtle—a warmth in the flagstones, a hum below hearing, the candle flames leaning all in one direction though the air was still. Sable lifted her head. Six years ago she had stood before the Synod and declared that the old gods were not dead but sleeping, that their silence was illness and not judgment, and the Synod had stripped her vestments, broken her ring, and put her out of Vantrelle's temples with a heretic's mark seared into her wrist. She had kept praying anyway. Faith, she had learned, was mostly stubbornness wearing better clothes.

But now the stones were warm. Now the candles leaned toward the mountain like flowers toward sun.

"Ah," she said softly, to the empty chapel, to the waking dark. "There you are."

She had seen the signs gathering for weeks—the answered prayer in the night market, the charms going strange, the dreams that everyone in the city swore they weren't having. And she knew what waking gods meant for the courts beneath the mountain, because the Synod's forbidden archives had been her favorite reading before her fall. Bargains sealed against the fire's endurance. Debts called due at the turning of ages.

Somewhere down there, someone was about to lose everything. Possibly several someones. Possibly, if the signs pointed where she thought they pointed, the charm-seller from the night market with the hollow eyes and the stall that smelled faintly of other people's childhoods.

Sable rose, knees cracking, and took down her travel cloak from its hook. It was, she reflected, the first decision she had made in six years that felt like priesthood rather than exile.

* * *

Sable found Wendell at the stall that evening, which surprised neither of them as much as it should have.

"You're the priestess," Wendell said, not looking up from the charms they were pretending to sort. "From the market. You prayed over my table and something answered."

"I did." Sable set a hand on the stall's edge. Up close she smelled of candle smoke and cold stone, and her heretic's mark showed dark on her bare wrist, deliberately unhidden. "And I've come to talk about what answered in you. Last night. Around the hour before dawn."

Wendell's hands went still. "I don't know what you mean."

"You do. You've known for three days. Your charms have gone quiet—they're not yours anymore, are they? They never were. And whatever you buried to survive the Cull is coming up like a body in a thawing river." Sable's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "I know what's under the mountain, Wendell Delacroix. I know what bargains the courts seal, and I know the fire is failing. When it goes out, every debt comes due at once."

"Then I'll pay it," Wendell said, too fast, too loud. A customer nearby glanced over; Wendell dropped their voice. "Whatever it costs. It's mine to pay. My choice. My—" But the word *soul* stuck in their throat like a fishbone.

"It was a child's choice," Sable said. "Made while your mother's house burned. The courts count that as binding. I do not." She leaned closer. "There is a window, and it is closing. While the fire gutters but does not die, the old law holds: debts may be contested. Argued. Staked against. I can take you down the stairs—I am exiled from everything above ground, but the things below remember me differently. Once the fire fails, no argument will matter. Everything owed will simply be taken."

Wendell stared at the charms spread across the cloth—other people's weddings, other people's griefs, seven years of borrowed fullness filling a hollow they had never once let themselves examine.

"And if I go down there," they said quietly, "and argue, and lose?"

"Then you lose what you were always going to lose," Sable said. "But you'll have stood in the court of the old justice and spoken your own name as more than property. There are worse ends than that. I've watched people find most of them."

Far away, beneath the city, beneath the streets, beneath the roots of everything, Wendell felt—or imagined they felt—the dragon-fire shudder, like a great beast drawing a breath it might not finish.

"When do we leave?" they asked.

"Now," said Sable Ondine. "Before it remembers it's dying."
