## 3. Dragon-Fire, Guttering

The mountain did not have a door. It had a *wound*.

Wendell felt it before they saw it—a seam in the rock where the stone had been peeled back like fruit, edges still glistening with something that was not sap and not blood but moved with the same slow reluctance. The air that breathed out of it was warm in the wrong way, the warmth of a mouth, of held breath, of a room where someone has just stopped speaking because you walked in.

Sable Ondine went first. She carried no torch; the priestess's hands were raised, palms out, the way one approaches a frightened animal or an old friend who has reason to hate you. Her exile-marks—cut into her forearms in the old script, the heresy of *the gods belong to no one*—caught the light from below and seemed to drink it.

"Stay behind my shadow," she said, "until we reach the first landing. Then you'll want to be seen. Down here, being unseen is worse than being guilty."

"What's down there?"

"Everything you sold." Sable glanced back at them, and in the dimness her face was all planes and patience. "And everything everyone else sold too. The court doesn't keep souls in jars, Wendell. It keeps them *working*. A soul is collateral, and collateral is put to use."

They descended.

The stairs were cut for taller things than people—each step came to Wendell's knee, and they climbed down them the way one climbs down into a well, hand over hand along a wall that pulsed faintly, faintly, like a throat deciding whether to swallow. And all around them, in the dark beyond the stair, Wendell heard it: the sound of the court at its accounting. A rustle like ledger-pages. A ticking like a thousand small clocks disagreeing. And underneath, so low it might have been imagination, voices—not speaking, but *reciting*. Names. Terms. Interest accrued over years.

Their own name came up once. Twice. A third time, closer, and Wendell's stolen memories stirred against their ribs like birds in a sack.

*Delacroix. Delacroix. Sold in the year of the purge, seven winters past, payment rendered: passage, protection, silence. Outstanding balance: one soul, whole and unencumbered.*

"I can hear it," Wendell whispered. "It's reciting my contract."

"Then it knows you've come." Sable's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Good. Debtors who hide are seized. Debtors who present themselves must be *heard*. That's the oldest law under this mountain, older than the courts themselves: every debt gets its hearing before the fire goes out."

"The fire?"

"You'll see."

* * *

The last dragon-fire hung in the great chamber like a dying star in a bottle.

That was Wendell's first thought—*a star in a bottle*—and their second thought was that the bottle was the mountain itself, and the mountain was a throat, and they had walked down into the belly of something that had been swallowing for a thousand years. The fire did not flicker. It *breathed*. Each exhalation lit the chamber in deep amber, and each inhalation drew the light back into itself, and in the stutter between breaths the whole court went dark as a closed eye.

Around the fire's basin, tier upon tier, sat the judges.

They had been human once. That much remained—the set of shoulders, the gesture of a hand turning a page that was not there. But the courts had worn them long, the way water wears stone, and what sat in those tiers now were shapes of judgment given flesh: faces like sealed contracts, eyes like scales weighing something invisible. They did not look at Wendell as they entered. They looked *through*, the way one looks through a window at weather.

A clerk-thing unfolded from beside the basin. It had too many fingers and each finger held a pen.

"Wendell Delacroix," it said, and its voice was the sound of a stamp coming down on paper. "Presented for final settlement. The courts move to close all outstanding bargains before the fire fails. Your soul is called. Do you contest the debt?"

Wendell's mouth was dry as ash. Seven years of keeping their head down, of being nobody, of selling other people's happiness in little glass vials—and now every sealed face in the tiers was turning toward them, and beneath the blankness of those faces they could feel the older thing, the recognition. *Property does not argue,* the silence said. *Property arrives.*

"I contest the terms," Wendell said.

The clerk's pens stopped. Somewhere above, the dragon-fire inhaled, and the chamber went nearly black, and in that darkness Wendell heard the memory wake fully at last—not the memory of selling, but the memory of *that night*: the purge-torches on the hillside, the knockers in the walls going silent one by one, their mother's hands pushing them toward the mountain's wound saying *run, run, don't look back,* and the voice from the stone offering the only bargain left in the world. *Your soul, child, and you live. Refuse, and you live until morning.*

They had been sixteen. They had said yes. They had never once, in seven years, let themselves remember saying yes.

Now they remembered, and the remembering was like swallowing a coal.

"The terms," Wendell said again, steadier, "were made under duress, by a child, during a purge your courts permitted. I don't deny the debt. I deny that this is justice."

* * *

From her place at the chamber's edge, Sable Ondine watched the boy she'd found in the night market stand alone in the amber dark, and she did the hardest thing a priestess can do: nothing.

She had argued cases here once, long ago, before the exile. She knew the law the way the judges knew it—from the inside, where it was carved into whatever they still were. And she knew what Wendell did not: that the courts were afraid. The old gods were answering prayers again. The fire was guttering. Every bargain ever struck under this mountain would come due or come undone, and the judges' sealed faces hid a hunger to close the books cleanly, finally, while there was still fire enough to seal them by.

Which meant Wendell had one thing the courts wanted more than a soul.

*Assets,* she thought, willing the word across the dark like a prayer. *Show them your assets.*

The clerk-thing was reciting: "Contest noted. Duress is not recognized. Age is not recognized. The purge was lawful. The bargain stands. Settlement is required. What do you offer?"

And Wendell—she saw it happen, saw the moment the thief understood what they were holding—went very still, and then reached into their coat, and began, one by one, to set the charms on the stone floor between themselves and the fire.

Little vials, glowing soft as fireflies. Seven years of theft. A widow's wedding day. A soldier's last sunrise. A child's belief in her father. An old man's first love, sold twice. Hundreds of them, months of the night market, the whole quiet empire of borrowed joy—and each one sang as it touched the stone, a thin bright note, and the judges' heads turned, all of them, all at once, like iron filings finding a magnet.

"These," Wendell said. "Every charm I ever stole. Every memory returned to its owner, freely given back—that's the settlement. Not my soul working in your dark for a thousand years. *Restitution.* You want the books closed? Close them clean. Take the hoard instead of the debtor."

The dragon-fire exhaled. The light swelled gold across the tiers, and in it Sable saw the judges' faces for the first time truly move—cracking along old seams, mouths opening, the sound that came out not speech but the groan of a scale settling after centuries of imbalance.

The clerk's many pens wrote, all together, one line.

"The offer," it said, "is *unprecedented*."

Above them, far above, through a mile of stone, the last dragon-fire guttered—and did not go out. It flared. And in Vantrelle, in a hundred dark houses, prayers that had gone unanswered for decades suddenly felt, for the first time, *listened to*.

The eldest judge leaned forward, and its voice, when it came, was almost kind.

"Then let us weigh what you have brought us, thief. And know this—" the sealed face split into something like a smile, "—some of what you carry was never yours to give. Some of it belongs to *us* already."

The vials on the floor began, one by one, to go dark.
