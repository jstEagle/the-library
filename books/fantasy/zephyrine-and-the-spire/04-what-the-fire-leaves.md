## 4. What the Fire Leaves

The vials went dark one at a time, and each darkness was a small death Wendell had agreed to.

They watched from the floor of the court as their life's work dimmed above them, arranged in rows on the black stone like offerings—which was, they understood now with a clarity that felt like a blade turning, exactly what they had always been. The widow's charm first: seven years of a husband's laughter, surrendered to return to the woman who had bought it without knowing it was stolen goods. Then the ferryman's memory of his mother's face. The baker's wedding morning. The child's—gods, the *child's*—first snow.

Each vial as it went dark made a sound. Not glass breaking. Something softer and worse: a sigh, like a held breath released after years. Somewhere in Vantrelle, in a narrow bed or a cold kitchen, a person was waking up whole and not knowing why the world had suddenly gotten larger.

*Let it hurt,* Wendell told themselves, when the ache came. *You stole this ache. It's yours.*

The judges' pens scratched. The clerk's many hands moved over documents older than the city, older than the mountain, unsealing contracts written in ink that still moved. And in the center of the court, on the anvil of black stone where souls were weighed like coin, Wendell's own soul sat waiting for them—smaller than they had imagined, brighter than they had feared, humming a note they felt in their teeth and their wrists and the soft backs of their eyes.

It looked like nothing at all. It looked like everything.

"Do you understand," the eldest judge said—not asking, never asking—"what you surrender?"

"I do."

"Then say it. Courts run on speech. Gods run on names. Say what you are giving, and say what you were."

Wendell stood. Their knees had gone stiff from kneeling on stone that remembered every bargain ever struck upon it, and standing felt like surfacing from deep water. Around the court, the sealed faces of the judges turned toward them, and Sable Ondine stood at the chamber's edge with her heretic's sigil burned into her palm and her eyes fixed on Wendell with something that was not pity. Pity would have been easier to bear.

"I give," Wendell said, and their voice did not shake, which surprised them, "every memory-charm in my keeping. Every stolen thing returns to its owner. I give the trade itself—the hands, the tricks, the seven years of hiding." They swallowed. "I give Wendell Delacroix, thief of Vantrelle, who never existed."

The pens stopped.

"And who remains?" the eldest judge asked.

Wendell looked at the small bright thing on the anvil. Their soul. Sold at nineteen, in a corridor full of smoke, to buy three minutes of head start from a purge they had not started and could not stop. They had spent seven years telling themselves it was survival. It was survival. It was also a sale, and sales have two ends, and this was the other one.

"Someone," Wendell said. "I don't know yet."

For the first time in the history of the court beneath the mountain—or so Sable would later swear, and she had read the records—a judge laughed. It was not an unkind sound. It sounded like rock settling, like a mountain deciding to be patient a while longer.

"Good," the eldest judge said. "*Good.* That is the only honest answer we have heard in nine hundred years."

The anvil flared white.

* * *

The returning was not gentle, and Wendell had not expected it to be.

Every charm emptied at once meant every memory flew home at once, and the court filled with the passage of them—shadows with weight, warmth with edges, brushing past Wendell like a crowd leaving a burning building. Some of them paused. The widow's husband's laughter circled Wendell twice before going, and if a memory could be grateful, that one was, though Wendell knew better than to take credit for gratitude. You don't thank the thief for opening the cage. You just walk out through the open door and don't look back.

Except Wendell looked back. At all of it. Seven years of charms, seven years of other people's best days sold cheap in a night market, and now every one of them going home. It was the closest thing to restitution the law of gods allowed, and it cost them everything, and standing in the middle of its cost, Wendell found—to their genuine astonishment—that they were not sorry.

The soul came last.

It did not fly to them. It walked, in whatever way light walks, across the black stone, and stopped a hand's breadth away, and waited. Asking permission. Even sold, even collateral for seven years in a court of bartered justice, it had kept the one thing ownership cannot touch: the right to choose its owner back.

"Yes," Wendell said aloud, to a piece of their own self. "Come home."

When it entered them, there was no thunder. There was a smell—the smoke of the purge corridor, and under the smoke, impossibly preserved, bread baking somewhere above, some ordinary morning, some ordinary street. There was the sensation of a coat returned after years in pawn: familiar, slightly wrong in the shoulders, warm anyway. There was grief, theirs, native, unsold, unedited—grief for the nineteen-year-old who had stood in the smoke and made the only terrible choice available—and Wendell let it come, because it was *theirs*, and they had spent seven years with nothing that was theirs, and you do not refuse your own inheritance twice.

"Unowned," the eldest judge pronounced, and the word went down through the stone like a keel touching harbor bottom. "The debt is paid. The bargain is closed. Let the record show—" and here all the pens wrote together, one line, and Wendell felt the sentence settle into the mountain's bones, "—that Wendell Delacroix leaves this court owning nothing, and owned by nothing, which is more than any king in Vantrelle can claim."

"The fire," Sable said quietly, from the edge. "Look at the fire."

High above, through a mile of stone, the last dragon-fire had caught. Wendell felt it before they saw any sign of it—a heat in the air that had nothing to do with flame, an attention, a vast old *noticing*, spreading outward from the mountain like ripples from a stone dropped in still water. The old gods, waking all the way down. Answering prayers all the way up.

"It followed your soul home," Sable said. "Or your soul followed it. Down here, I've stopped pretending those are different things."

* * *

They climbed out of the mountain at dawn, which no longer existed in Vantrelle, because dawn in Vantrelle was now a *choice* the sky made rather than a habit, and it had chosen gold.

Wendell stood at the mountain's mouth with empty pockets and empty hands and a body that hummed like a struck bell, and looked down at the city, and wept without shame for the first time since childhood. Below, in the night-market streets, fires were kindling in doorways—small ones, prayer-fires, the kind that hadn't burned legally or otherwise in thirty years. Old women were singing on stoops. A ferryman stood at the river's edge staring at his own reflection like a man seeing a stranger he'd been told he'd recognize.

Somewhere in a narrow bed, a widow was laughing at a joke she'd forgotten her husband told, and it wasn't a charm anymore. It was just hers. Just hers, forever.

"You'll want work," Sable said beside her, practical as ever, her burned palm open to the gold light. "Powerless people still eat. The temple will take you—I'll vouch, heresy notwithstanding. Someone should teach the godless how to pray, and who better than a thief who knows exactly what a soul is worth?"

"I don't know how to pray," Wendell said.

"No," Sable agreed. "But you know how to listen. It's the same door, entered from the other side."

Below them the city blazed with small fires, and above them the mountain breathed dragon-fire old as the world, and between the two, Wendell Delacroix—who had sold their soul to survive and survived to buy it back—stood empty-handed in the morning light and found that their hands did not feel empty at all. They felt like hands that belonged to someone. Whoever that someone turned out to be, whoever remained when the thief was gone—

—they were finally, entirely, home to stay.

And far below, in the closing dark of the court, the eldest judge wrote one final line in the record, and smiled, and let the mountain sleep:

*"Paid in full. Witnessed by fire."*
