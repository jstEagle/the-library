## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules in the Vellum Guild, and I have broken none of them in eleven years, which is either a triumph of discipline or a eulogy delivered slightly early.

Rule one: never be remembered. Rule two: never be named. Rule three: everything taken must be entered in the ledgers before dawn, in iron-gall ink, in my own cramped hand, because a memory that isn't catalogued is just theft, and the Guild does not traffic in mere theft. We are *archivists*. We are custodians of the impossible. We are, if you believe the guildmaster, the only reason this city's sins stay neatly filed instead of spilling into the streets.

I believe her, mostly. It makes the work easier.

Tonight's work began at the house of Widow Corvane, on Candlemaker's Row, where the lamps burn low because grief is expensive to illuminate. She had hired us herself — that's the part civilians never understand. Nobody breaks into your head uninvited in this trade; there are forms. There was a form. Her late husband had been dead eleven months, and her grief sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed whole, and she wanted it out but could not bear the indignity of weeping in front of a stranger.

So she wept in front of me instead, which is different, because by morning she wouldn't remember I existed.

"It doesn't hurt, does it?" she asked, for the third time. "The taking?"

"Not even a pinch," I said, which was true. What hurts is later, when you reach for the shape of him and find a hole wearing his outline. But I don't say that part. Saying that part would be bad for business, and worse for my conscience, which needs all the help it can get.

I took the grief in pieces, the way you'd dismantle a clock: first the sound of his cough from the next room, then the weight of his coat still hanging by the door, then the terrible arithmetic of waking up and counting to two when there was only one. She sat very still with her hands folded while I worked, and her face did something I've seen a thousand times and never gotten used to — relaxed and collapsed at once, like a building whose scaffolding has been quietly removed.

When it was done, she looked at me with polite blankness and offered me tea, because a strange young person in her parlor at midnight clearly required hospitality, and I thanked her and declined, because rule one applies to tea as well as to memories. Never accept anything you can be remembered over.

In the ledger, back at the counting-house, I wrote:

*Item 4,401. One (1) grief, conjugal, eleven months' standing, taken intact. Vendor: M. Corvane, widow, Candlemaker's Row. Condition: excellent. Purchaser: pending.*

The purchaser line is always my favorite. People imagine the Guild sells stolen memories to rich men who want to know what drowning feels like, or to actors preparing for widow roles, and they're not wrong — there is a market, and it is grotesque, and I help run it. But most of what we take goes into cold storage in the vaults below the counting-house, row upon row of little leaden boxes, all the sorrow the city produces and cannot hold. Sable says we are doing the city a service. The city, being a city, has never once said thank you.

I blotted the page, sanded it, closed the ledger. Somewhere below my feet, four floors of vaults hummed with other people's yesterdays. It was, I reflected, a good night's work, and I went out into the street feeling nothing at all, which is the closest thing to satisfaction our profession offers.

* * *

The general was harder.

General Aldric Marren lives in a fortress of a townhouse on Vantage Hill, behind gates and guards and a paranoia so well-fed it has its own cook. He had not hired us. His *wife* had hired us, through intermediaries, through intermediaries of intermediaries, because the general keeps a memory he should not keep, and it was beginning to leak.

Here is what leaks: twenty-two years ago, as a young officer with a bright future, Aldric Marren stood in a courtyard in the eastern provinces and gave an order he has spent two decades pretending not to remember. The order concerned a country that no longer exists. The order was carried out. The country in question was struck from every map, every treaty, every tongue, so thoroughly that schoolchildren in this very city learn geography without ever noticing the gap where it used to be, the way you stop noticing a tooth once the tongue heals around it.

I know about the gap. That's the thing. Everyone knows about the gap, in the way everyone knows not to look down certain alleys after certain hours. You don't learn it; you inherit the flinch.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The memory itself I never saw entire — we don't watch what we take, mostly, because watching leaves residue, and residue is how thieves become haunted. I took it in the general's own study while he slept badly in the next room, and it came out heavy, heavier than the widow's grief by an order of magnitude, and it came out *hot*. Some memories fight. This one fought like something that had been waiting twenty-two years for exactly one chance.

It took me forty minutes and left my hands shaking. I do not shake. Rule one has a corollary: the thief who trembles gets remembered. I stood in the dark of that study and breathed until my hands were mine again, and then I walked past two guards who looked directly at me and registered, apparently, a deliveryman, a shadow, a chair — whatever the mind supplies when it declines to notice.

Outside, on the hill, the city spread below me: lamplit, sprawling, built on top of something it had agreed collectively to forget. I have lived here my whole life and I have never once been able to say what the country under this one used to be called. I tried, standing there. I reached for the name the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found the smooth healed place where it should have been, and felt — briefly, unwelcome — like a woman reaching for a dead husband.

In the ledger:

*Item 4,402. One (1) memory, military, classified. Vendor: A. Marren, general (via proxy). Condition: volatile. Note: resisted extraction. Purchaser: the Vault. No resale.*

No resale. Some things go in the deep shelves and stay there. Sable's instruction, standing instruction, older than my employment. I've entered perhaps thirty items under those terms in eleven years, and I've stopped wondering what they have in common, because the answer is a shape my mind slides off of, and I have learned to let it slide.

Comfortable loneliness, I've decided, is a skill. You can get good at it. You get a room above the counting-house with a window that looks out on other people's lit windows, and you eat supper alone and call it peace, and when the loneliness comes tapping at the glass you tell it: you're safe here, nobody in this whole city knows your name, isn't that what safety is? And it usually goes away. Usually.

* * *

My third call of the night was routine, or should have been.

A debt collection, technically. A merchant on Salt Lane had bought a memory he couldn't afford — the taste of his mother's bread, if you must know, which is the kind of small sweet thing people sell first and regret forever — and when he defaulted, the Guild repossessed. Repossession is the bottom rung of our work and I am, despite eleven years of flawless service, still on it some nights. Sable says it keeps me honest. Sable says a lot of things in that voice of hers that could mean anything, and I have learned not to ask, because rule two cuts both ways: I am not named, and therefore I am not owed explanations.

The merchant cried. They always cry. I took the memory of the defaulting itself instead — the ledger entry, the shame of the letter, the arithmetic of failure — which sounds merciful and is actually just procedure: leave the man his mother's bread, take the memory of losing it, and the books balance. I wrote it up on Salt Lane's corner by lamplight, my breath fogging, my hand steady again:

*Item 4,403. One (1) memory, financial, minor. Vendor: J. Pell, merchant (default). Condition: ordinary. Purchaser: resale, common shelf.*

Ordinary. That's the word for most of it. Grief, shame, debt, regret — the city produces them like smoke, and I gather them like a chimney sweep, and I go home blackened and clean at once, and no one I pass will ever know my face.

That's the trick of it, if you're wondering how one person does this for eleven years and stays whole. Or stays *shaped*, anyway. You make yourself into a doorway. Things pass through you and you remain, technically, but no one remembers a doorway. No one writes ballads about doorways. You stand very still and let the world's sorrows file past you into the vaults, and at the end of the night you count yourself among the catalogued items: one thief, condition excellent, purchaser pending, no resale.

I was walking home along the canal when I let myself think the thought I don't let myself think, the one about how I cannot remember the last time anyone said my name — partly because no one knows it, and partly because I have sold so many of my own mornings to the vault over the years, little slivers of childhood, the standard tax on Guild orphans, that my own past is mostly shelving. There is a box down there with my seventh birthday in it. I checked, once. I couldn't tell you why I checked. I couldn't tell you what was in it, either; reading your own file is against rule three, and rules are the only family I've got.

The canal smelled like winter coming. Somewhere a barge horn sounded, low and lonesome, and I thought, not for the first time: this is fine. This is a life. Nobody hunts a shadow. Nobody grieves a doorway.

This is fine.

* * *

The counting-house at three in the morning is my favorite place in the world, which says more about me than I'd like. Lamplight on the long tables. The smell of ink and dust and cold stone. Four floors of other people's yesterdays sleeping below, and above, in the scriptorium, the new consignment waiting to be catalogued — a looted library, come in off a smuggler's barge that afternoon, three hundred books the Authority had ordered pulped and someone had ordered saved instead, which is the sort of quiet rebellion the Guild approves of, since rebellion is profitable precisely twice: once when you buy it and once when you sell it.

I sat down with the first crate and began the inventory. Sermons. Almanacs. Histories, all of them officially nonexistent — the Authority burns histories the way other governments build roads, diligently and constantly. I logged each one with the mechanical tenderness I reserve for books, which are the only things in this trade that can't be taken out of a person's head and therefore can't be sold at our usual margins, and which I love accordingly.

Halfway down the second crate, my hand stopped.

The book was small, bound in grey leather gone soft with age, unmarked except for a date pressed into the cover in gold that had mostly flaked away. A century old, at a guess. I opened it to check condition, because condition is my job, and the pages fell open — fell open, I want to be clear, the way a book falls open when it has been opened to that same page so many times the spine has given up arguing —

— to tonight's date.

Not the day it was printed. Not a feast day. Tonight's date. Day, month, year, written in a century-dead hand, beside a paragraph I read once, and then again, and then could not stop reading, because the paragraph was describing a thief, and the thief it was describing was described as remembering nothing and being remembered by no one, and the thief it was describing was me, to the letter, down to details I have never told another living soul.

Somewhere below my feet, four floors of vaults held their breath.

I want to say the floor dropped out of my life at that moment, but that's not honest. The floor dropped out slowly, over the following hour, tile by tile, while I sat under the lamp telling myself it was coincidence, misattribution, a prophecy-shaped coincidence, while the candle burned down and my hands — steady enough for generals — would not stop shaking.

Which is when Sable spoke from the doorway behind me, in that voice that could mean anything, and said, in a tone I had never heard her use:

"Burn the page, Theo."

She had never said my name in eleven years.
