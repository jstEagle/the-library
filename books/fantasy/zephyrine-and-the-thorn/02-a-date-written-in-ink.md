## 2. A Date Written in Ink

There is a particular smell to a looted library, and it is not the romantic one people imagine. People imagine dust and leather and the ghost of candlewax. What you actually get, when the Authority has finished stripping a house and we come in after to strip what they couldn't be bothered with, is mildew, rat, and the sour tang of ink that has been left too long in damp air. Books are mostly water when you get down to it. Water and other people's certainties.

I had been cataloguing since sundown, kneeling on the cold marble of what had once been some magistrate's private collection, and my knees were informing me that I was no longer nineteen.

"Item," I said, writing as I spoke, because saying things aloud helps me remember them, which is ironic given the trade. "One folio, natural philosophy, water-stained. Value: sentimental only. One crate of almanacs, obsolete. Value: none. One—"

The last item was wedged behind a shelf that had been pulled away from the wall, as if someone in the house's final hours had tried to hide it and run out of time. It was smaller than the others. Bound in grey vellum—not our guild's vellum, but real skin, the old kind—and unmarked except for a blind-tooled circle on the cover, worn nearly smooth by thumbs.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the one thing my profession never trades in and therefore the one thing I hoard.

I almost didn't open it.

There is an instinct, well-developed in those of us raised by the Vellum Guild, that recognizes trouble the way a dog recognizes weather. The book was hidden. Hidden books are either worthless or worth more than the house they sit in, and this one had cost somebody a risk. My instinct said: leave it for the rats, log the shelf as empty, go home to your narrow bed above the counting room and sleep the dreamless sleep of the successfully invisible.

But Sable had said *catalogue everything*, and Sable's orders have more weight with me than my own instincts, which is a thing I will regret remembering later. So I opened it.

* * *

The first third was boring, which is how you know a book is dangerous. Nobody hides boring books; nobody burns them either. This one had begun its life as something mundane—a household ledger, actually, dates and purchases and servants' wages, the arithmetic of a life a century dead. Someone had written over it. The older entries showed through like bones under skin, and on top of them, in at least three different hands across maybe sixty years, someone had been keeping a different kind of record.

Prophecies are a genre I hold in low esteem. In my experience they function exactly like memory does in the wrong hands: as a tool for making people do what you want. Half the contraband we move through the Ashmarket is prophecy-shaped. *The child born under the drowned star.* *The king who returns when the river runs backward.* Buyers eat it up, especially buyers who have lost something, because a prophecy tells the grieving that their loss is part of a design, and design is comforting even when the design is cruel.

So I skimmed. I gave each entry the professional courtesy of two seconds and moved on. A famine foretold, arrived late. A fire foretold, prevented, smugly annotated by some long-dead hand: *averted, see margin*. A list of names, seven of them, each with a date beside it and a line or two of description, like a ledger of appointments.

The first six dates were all in the past.

That was the moment I should have closed the book. A prophecy whose dates have all come due is a spent shell, a curiosity, a thing we would sell to a melancholic collector in Cinderside for forty crowns and forget by morning. I turned the page because turning pages is what hands do when the mind has stopped giving orders.

The seventh date was tonight.

Not approximately. Not *in the days when the thorn blooms*, not any of the usual prophetic hedging that lets seers collect payment whether or not anything happens. The entry gave a day, a month, and a year, written in ink that had gone brown with age, and the year was this one, and the month was this one, and the day—

I am going to transcribe it here from memory, which is safe, because I am very good at forgetting and no one can steal what I don't write down. The description read:

*The seventh comes at evening, in the season of ash. A thief who is no one. Kept by the keepers of taken things. Their name is written nowhere and their face is held by no one. Through their hand the door remembers.*

I sat back on my heels on the magistrate's cold floor and listened to the rain start against the high windows, and I did the arithmetic that people in my position always do first, the coward's arithmetic, the *surely not me*.

A thief who is no one. Well. That described half the guild, or aspired to.

Kept by the keepers of taken things. The Vellum Guild keeps taken things. We also, though we use softer words, keep people.

Their name is written nowhere. Mine isn't. That's not poetry; that's policy. Rule one of my existence, taught to me before I could write cursive: *never be remembered, never be named*. I sign nothing. I appear in no register. When clients describe the collector who came for their grief or their shame or their mother's recipe for plum brandy, they describe a shape, a coat, a voice already going out of focus. There are perhaps four people alive who could pick my face from a crowd, and two of them are guild, and one of those is Sable.

And their face is held by no one. I have made sure of that the way other people make sure of their prayers.

I sat there until my knees went fully numb, and then I did the second arithmetic, the one that comes after the coward's, which is: *if not me, then who?* And the honest answer was that I could not think of anyone else in Zephyrine—or whatever we're supposed to call the country now, since apparently it isn't called anything—who fit all four lines. Which meant either the prophecy was a coincidence of such exquisite craftsmanship that I ought to frame it, or it was about me, and both possibilities were bad, and one of them was much worse than bad, because if a hundred-year-old book knew me, then I was not invisible at all. I was *predicted*. Being predicted is the opposite of being no one. You cannot be a ghost with an appointment.

I wrapped the book in my coat. I did not enter it in the catalogue. That, too, was instinct, and this time I listened, and I want it noted somewhere—I suppose nowhere, since I trust nowhere—that for once in my careful life I broke procedure, and it saved my skin for exactly as long as it takes to walk from a magistrate's house to the counting room in the rain.

* * *

Sable keeps her office above the vaults, where the temperature stays constant and the light stays borrowed. She was at her desk doing what she does every night: reading entries aloud in a low voice and striking through the ones she has decided never happened. Guild accounting works in both directions. We record what we take, and we erase what we must.

She didn't look up when I came in. "You're dripping on my floor."

"You don't have a floor. You have a rug that hates people."

"Theo." She still wasn't looking up. "Report."

"Forty-one items logged. Three crates of almanacs worth nothing. A folio with marginalia that might fetch something from the Cinderside collectors." I stood there, dripping, holding a book inside my coat against my ribs like a stolen organ. "And one item unlogged."

Now she looked up.

I have watched Sable's face for twenty years, since I was a child she pulled out of a debtors' cell and made into a ledger-keeper, and I have seen it do maybe five things. Interest. Calculation. The flat gray nothing she wears instead of anger. Amusement, rarely, and usually at someone else's expense. What I saw when I set the grey book on her desk and opened it to the seventh entry was none of these. What I saw was fear, arriving fast, like water finding a crack.

She read it twice. Her finger stayed on the date. She didn't touch the description, as though the words might be hot.

"Where."

"Behind a shelf. Hidden."

"Who else has seen it?"

"The rats."

"Theo." My name in her mouth, and no one else's, because no one else's mouth holds it. "Who else."

"No one. I brought it straight here."

She nodded slowly. Then she reached into her desk drawer—the left one, the one I have never been allowed to inventory—and took out her knife, the little paring knife she uses on vellum, and I thought, absurdly, *she's going to cut the page out and give it back to me as a lesson about ownership.*

What she said was: "Burn it."

"Burn—"

"The page. All of it. The whole book if the page won't part cleanly." She said it the way she says everything, level and dry, but her hand wasn't steady, and in twenty years I have never once seen Sable's hand unsteady. "Tonight. Now. And then you'll tell me exactly what the entry said, word for word, and afterward you'll take a forgetting, a small one, just the evening, just the book, and we'll log it as spoilage."

"A forgetting." I heard my own voice go carefully pleasant, which is what it does when I'm frightened. "For the record, I've never taken one."

"I know."

"It's rule three. Never carry what can be carried out of you."

"And tonight," Sable said, "you're going to break rule two as well, because you're asking questions with your face right now, and I can hear them." She closed the book. She slid it back across the desk to me, which I understood, even then, was a test dressed as a courtesy. "Burn it, Theo. Some pages are debts. This one is a bill coming due, and I am telling you, as the person who has kept you fed and unnamed and alive since you were small enough to fit in a ledger box, that you do not want to pay it."

Here is the thing about being raised by the guild: they teach you that questions are liabilities, and I have been a good student my entire life. I have walked past locked doors for twenty years without wondering. I have catalogued griefs and shames and a general's worst hour and never once asked *why do we want these, why does anyone pay for this, what is the vault for underneath the vault*.

But I looked down at the seventh entry, at the brown ink and the exact date, and the question came up through me like floodwater through floorboards, and for the first time in my careful, comfortable, forgettable life, I let it reach my mouth.

"Who was the sixth?"

Sable went pale.

I don't mean she looked tired, or that the lamplight was unkind. I mean the color left her face the way color leaves a page when the ink is strong and the paper is cheap—drawn out of her, taken. Her hand went to the edge of the desk and stayed there, and she looked, for one unguarded half-second, like a woman reading her own name in a stranger's death notice.

"That entry is a hundred years old," she said quietly.

"So were the other six."

"The sixth—" She stopped. Started again, in her ledger voice, the one she uses for striking things through. "The sixth was handled. Forty years ago. Before your time. It's struck through. Struck-through means concluded."

"Concluded how?"

"Theo."

"Concluded *how*?"

And she looked at me then with something I had never been on the receiving end of, something I had only ever seen her aim at loose ends and unlogged items, and she said, in a voice like a door closing:

"Sit down, take the forgetting, and burn the book. Or stand there and become the seventh line in full. Those are the entries available to you tonight. Choose the one with fewer consequences."

I chose, in the way that people like me choose, which is to say I didn't decide anything at all; my body decided while my mind was still negotiating. I picked up the book. I said, "Of course, Sable. Right away," in my most forgettable voice, and I bowed slightly, and I walked to the door, and I felt her watching me the entire way, and I knew—with the certainty of the professionally unseen, the certainty I have trusted more than any person—that she believed me.

That was the worst part. Not the pale face, not the knife-drawer, not even the sixth entry lying struck-through in forty years of silence. The worst part was realizing that the safety I had been living in all this time, the warm gray comfort of being no one, was not a wall around me at all.

It was a shelf.

And I had just been catalogued.

* * *

I did not burn the book. I want that on the record too, wherever records go when everyone involved agrees to lose them. I stood in the stairwell with the grey vellum against my chest and the rain loud on the roof, and I thought about the sixth entry, struck through, *handled*, and about how the guild retires people the way it retires debts—thoroughly, and without witnesses—and about how Sable had offered me a forgetting so smoothly that she must have offered it before, to someone, with the same level voice and the same unsteady hand.

Rule one: never be remembered. I had obeyed it my whole life, and a dead man's handwriting had found me anyway.

So I did the one thing I have never done. I stole.

From my own guild. From the woman who named the part of me that anyone knows. I went down past the counting room, past the dormitory where the junior keepers slept in their neat anonymous rows, down to the scribes' hall where the working ledgers live, and I found the volume with my own entries in it—my takings, my deliveries, my twenty years of tidy columns—and I cut out the page that bore my hand most plainly. The knife was Sable's kind of knife; I'd learned on one. The vellum parted clean.

Then I walked out the loading door into the Ashmarket rain, past the sleeping gatekeeper who would swear tomorrow that no one had passed, because no one ever does, because I am no one, I am no one, I am—

Well.

A hundred years ago, somebody wrote down exactly who I am, and they got the date right, and they got the evening right, and they knew I'd be kept by keepers of taken things. Which means that somewhere ahead of me, in the wet dark of the market, there are at least three more lines in that book with dates attached, describing people I haven't met yet, waiting for me to find them.

Or waiting for someone to find me first.

The rain came down on the Ashmarket awnings like applause for a play nobody admitted to attending, and I pulled my collar up, and I became no one as thoroughly as I have ever been, and I ran.
