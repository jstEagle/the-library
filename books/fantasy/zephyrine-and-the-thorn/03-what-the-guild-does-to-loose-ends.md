## 3. What the Guild Does to Loose Ends

There is a particular way a house goes quiet when it is deciding what to do about you.

I didn't know that yet. I want to be clear about that, because I've had years to revise the story and every version of me in it insists on being cleverer than she was. That night I thought the quiet was just the quiet—the Vellum Guildhouse settling into its bones the way it always did after midnight, when the clerks went home to beds they couldn't afford and only the ledgers stayed awake with me.

I was doing what I always did: filing.

The Guild's ledger room sits three floors below the street, behind two locks and a door that doesn't so much open as consent. Shelves and shelves of memory, catalogued by year, by seller, by weight of grief. A widow's last afternoon with her husband, sold to pay for his burial—Entry 4,411, Class II (Sorrow, Domestic), condition excellent, buyer anonymous. A general's shame over an order he gave at Kettering Bridge—Entry 4,412, Class I (Guilt, Military), fragile, handle with tongs. Somewhere in those shelves a bride's doubts, a poet's single perfect line, a child's belief that the cellar door opened somewhere better. All of it legal. All of it, technically, ours.

My name isn't in any of them. That's the arrangement. I take memories out of heads and put them into vellum, and in exchange nobody remembers me doing it. Never be remembered. Never be named. The Guild raised me on those two rules like other parents raise children on grace before meals. And like most things taught before you can read, they settled in deeper than scripture.

Rule one kept me alive through the coup's aftermath, though I didn't know it yet. Rule two kept me lonely, which I mistook for safe. There's a difference. It took me another nineteen chapters of my life to learn it, but that night I still had all my mistakes ahead of me.

So: filing. Candle low. The smell of tallow and old skin and the faint copper tang of fresh-cut memory, which some people find unpleasant and which I have always, privately, found like home.

That's when I found the prophecy book again.

* * *

I'd found it two nights earlier, cataloguing the looted Halvern library—a whole life's worth of reading pulled off its shelves because Lord Halvern had made the mistake of asking questions in public. Most of it was dross. Sermons, tax records, a truly alarming quantity of poetry about nightingales. But there was a slim volume bound in grey calfskin, unmarked, that my hands knew before I did. You develop instincts in this trade. Some books are just books. Some books are *inventory*.

It was a century old, give or take. Handwritten. And it wasn't a prophecy in the thunder-and-riddles style; it was worse than that. It was tidy. A list. Dates down the left margin like a clerk had written it—which, believe me, I recognized—and beside each date, a description.

The third of Frostmonth, forty years ago: *the singer who will not sing.* Crossed out. Struck through in ink so hard it tore the page.

The eleventh of Thawmonth, twelve years ago: *the cartographer who draws what is not there.* Also struck through, though more gently, the way you cross out something that disappointed you rather than frightened you.

And then tonight's date. My date. The exact day, written a hundred years ago by someone whose dust had been dust before my grandmother was born:

*the thief who is no one, who keeps the accounts of what was stolen.*

Below it, in the same patient hand: *Through them, the door.*

I should have burned it that first night. Sable told me to. When I brought it up to her office—third floor, north corner, a room where the candles never gutter no matter the draft—she read the page exactly once, and I watched her face do something I had never seen it do in twenty-two years of studying it for approval.

It went white. Not pale. White, like paper. Like a page itself.

"Burn it," she said, and her voice was perfectly level, which with Sable meant the opposite of calm. "Both copies if there are two. The binding too. Then come back and tell me it's ash."

I said yes. Of course I said yes. But I was raised a thief, and a thief knows that an order given that fast is an order given scared, and scared people tell you where the bodies are even when they're trying not to.

So I didn't burn it. I copied it. Word for word, date for date, including the crossed-out names, because a copy is insurance and insurance is the only inheritance the Guild ever gave me. Then I burned the original, watched the calfskin curl and stink, and went back upstairs and lied to the only mother I've ever had.

She thanked me. That's the part that still sits wrong. She looked at me across the desk with those grey eyes and said, "Good girl,"—she used whichever word suited her; I never corrected her—"you were never named in anything. Understand? You are no one. That has always been your great protection."

*You are no one.* Said like a benediction. Said like she was proud of it.

I went back to the ledger room and told myself the shaking in my hands was the cold.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of sleeping, which I no longer expected to do anyway: I researched. It's what I'm for. Give me a shelf and a decade and I'll find you whatever you're afraid of.

The singer. *The singer who will not sing,* dated forty years back, struck through hard enough to scar three pages beneath. If prophecy worked like inventory—and I was beginning to suspect everything worked like inventory—then the Guild would have handled it the way we handled anything valuable and dangerous. There'd be a file. Intake records. A disposition.

Disposition is the Guild's word. We don't kill anyone, officially. We *retire* them. The distinction matters enormously to the people making it and not at all to the person it happens to.

The retirement files live in the deep stacks, Class 0, the shelf nobody files to because nothing filed there ever comes back out. I took the candle and went down.

The file was thin. Thin files frighten me now; I know what thinness means. A name that had been inked over rather than written—I ran my thumb over the ridge of it and got nothing, which is its own kind of answer. An intake date matching the prophecy to the week. A note in Sable's handwriting, younger then, the loops not yet drawn tight:

*Candidate confirmed. Door assessed. Cost prohibitive. Recommend retirement of subject and closure of inquiry.*

And below that, in the same hand, smaller, pressed so lightly it was nearly breath:

*I'm sorry.*

Forty years ago, someone else had been named. Someone else had stood exactly where I stood, in exactly this light, holding exactly this knowledge. And the Guild—the Guild that fed me, taught me my letters and my lockpicks, told me I was family in the way you tell a lamp it's furniture—had retired them. Erased them so thoroughly that even their name had been inked out of the record. Not deleted. Worse. *Corrected.*

I sat down on the floor of the deep stacks among forty years of quiet endings and understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives after it can be of any use, that I had never been family. Family doesn't keep your page in a locked file between intake forms. Family doesn't strike you through.

I had been inventory. Well-kept, well-trained inventory. And I had just handed my owner a reason to process me.

* * *

You want to know the worst part? It wasn't fear. Fear came later, on the stairs, in the street, and it hasn't entirely left since. The worst part was that my first feeling, crouched there in the dark with Sable's young handwriting in front of me, was *hurt*. Twenty-two years old, a professional, a thief of other people's hearts, and my first reaction was the raw stupid hurt of a child left out of a game.

Because I'd believed it. That's the humiliating thing. I'd believed the family story the way you believe in the honesty of the hand that feeds you. Sable teaching me to read a face. Old Merrick in receiving teaching me to weigh a memory by feel. The birthday nobody remembered except me, marking it alone in the ledger room with a candle stub and a stolen hour of summer from the shelf—Entry 2,208, Class IV (Joy, Pastoral), borrowed without leave, returned by dawn. I'd built a whole self out of that house's spare parts and called it belonging.

They had let me build it. That was the part that turned hurt into something colder. Every kindness had been scaffolding around the thing I actually was to them: a tool, useful until named, and disposable the moment a hundred-year-old book wrote my description down.

*Never be remembered,* rule one. I'd always thought it protected me from the world.

It protects the Guild from the world remembering what it does to loose ends.

* * *

I went back up to the ledger room. My hands were steady, which surprised me; the body is loyal in ways the mind isn't, and mine had apparently decided this was just another job. Enter quietly. Take only what won't be missed. Leave nothing behind but the appearance of ordinary.

Except this time what I was stealing was myself.

My ledger page lived in the current-year folio, because of course I had one. Everyone who works for the Guild has a page—it's how Sable kept us, a line apiece: duties, debts, the small transactions of our lives rendered in her crabbed clerk's hand. Mine recorded eleven years of service. It recorded, near the bottom, in fresher ink, an entry added sometime in the last two days while I'd been upstairs burning prophecy books and lying badly:

*Subject confirmed against text. Preparation authorized. Disposition pending.*

Preparation. Authorized. While I slept under her roof, she had already begun.

I cut the page out along the spine with a bone knife, clean, the way I'd been taught—you never tear vellum, tearing damages the memory in it, and this page was the only memory of me that existed anywhere in the world. Then I took two other things, because sentiment is expensive but I've always paid it: Entry 2,208, my stolen hour of summer, and the grey calfskin cover of the burned prophecy book, which I had rescued from the fire and told no one about. Ash clung to it still. I wrapped both in oilcloth and put them inside my shirt, against the skin, where thieves keep the things that matter.

Last, I wrote one line in the folio where my page had been, in Sable's own hand, which I have always been able to counterfeit. It said: *Retired per standard procedure.* Let her find it and wonder whether the Guild's own instrument had learned her tricks. Sentiment, I told myself. Professional courtesy between monsters.

Then I blew out the candle and walked up through the sleeping Guildhouse, past the receiving room where Merrick had weighed my first stolen memory when I was nine, past the kitchen where someone had once—once, I could prove it once—left a honey bun out where I'd find it. Grief walks with you like that, pointing at things. I let it. I figured I'd earned the company.

The street door consented to open. Cold air. The city of Vell, wet slate roofs and gaslight, and beyond the river district, glowing faintly orange like a hearth banked for the night, the Ashmarket—where the Guild sells what it takes and everyone who deals outside the law deals there eventually.

I had never been there as anything but a ghost. Rule two: never be named. In the Ashmarket, unnamed means unprotected. Unnamed means meat.

I went anyway.

* * *

The Ashmarket at four in the morning smells of burnt sugar and secrets. Stalls of bottled weather. A woman selling secondhand courage off a handcart. Somewhere a fiddler playing a tune no one had commissioned, which in the Ashmarket counts as rebellion.

I moved through it with my collar up and my heart going like a press-gang drummer, and I told myself a story I needed badly: that the Guild was slow, that bureaucracies take days to notice a missing page, that I had until dawn at least.

I was halfway down Memory Row, past the stall where a dealer was haggling over a jarred lullaby, when I felt it—that prickle at the neck, the specific sensation of being *looked at* by someone trained to look. Twenty-two years of being invisible, and the first time in my life I was seen, I felt it like a hook going in.

Not Sable's people. Later I'd learn it was worse than that—not the Guild at all, but a knight of the Provisional Authority, a woman with a soldier's braid and eyes that held on to what they saw the way a ledger holds ink. Eyes that, unbeknownst to either of us, would be the end of rule one forever.

But that morning I only knew someone was hunting, and I did what the Guild taught me: I became no one. Shoulders down, face slack, purpose invented—I was a clerk late for a delivery, I was a widow buying courage, I was smoke. I slipped down the alley behind the lullaby stall, over a wall I'd have sworn last month I couldn't climb, and ran.

Somewhere above the market, dawn was coming up the colour of struck-through ink. Somewhere behind me, a grey-eyed woman was reading my description in a hundred-year-old book. And in my shirt, against my hammering heart, an hour of summer and a burned cover and one torn page that said, in Sable's hand, the truest thing the Guild ever wrote about me:

*Disposition pending.*

Well. I thought, ducking into the dark between stalls as the first bell rang. Pending no longer.
