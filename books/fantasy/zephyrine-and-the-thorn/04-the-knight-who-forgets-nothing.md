## 4. The Knight Who Forgets Nothing

The Provisional Authority trains its knights in three disciplines: swordwork, statecraft, and the careful unseeing of things that ought not be seen. I excelled at the first two and was politely asked to stop practicing the third, because it turned out I was constitutionally incapable of it.

My name is Wren Aldous, Knight-Errant of the Fourth Bell, and I remember everything.

Not *remember* the way other people remember—the soft-focus way, where a face blurs into an impression of a face, where a conversation becomes a rumor you once heard about yourself. I mean everything. The exact grain of my mother's voice on the last morning I heard it. The number of steps between the barracks gate and the mess hall (two hundred eleven, two hundred twelve if it rained). Every word of every oath I have ever sworn, including the ones I regret, filed in perfect order, timestamped, unblurred by mercy.

The physicians call it a gift. They said so when they found me at seven years old reciting the names of forty drowned ferrymen off a mass grave marker I'd seen for the span of a breath. *What a gift*, they said, and wrote it down, and the Authority has been writing it down ever since, and using it.

Here is what nobody tells you about a gift: you cannot put it down.

* * *

The morning they assigned me the prophecy-thief, it was raining, which meant the walk to the Marshal's office was two hundred twelve steps, and I counted them, because I count them every time, and if I am going to be cursed with precision I might as well be precise about something harmless.

Marshal Corvane did not look up when I entered. She never does. It is a technique—she told me once, years ago, in a moment of unusual honesty—that makes subordinates feel like evidence being reviewed rather than people being addressed. I had respected her enormously before she explained it. I have spent the six years since trying to decide whether knowing the trick ruins it. Mostly I have concluded that most things are ruined by explanation, and that I will explain them anyway, because an unexplained cruelty sits in my chest like a swallowed coin.

"Knight Aldous," she said, to the paperwork. "You know the Vellum Guild."

"I know of them. They trade in stolen memories. Impossible goods. They don't officially exist, which our own records confirm three separate times, which is how we know they exist." I paused. "That joke is from your own briefing packet, Marshal. Page four. You laughed at it in 914."

"I remember," Corvane said, in a tone that suggested she wished she didn't, which for her was nearly a confession of love. "There was a theft last night. A library, looted decades ago and catalogued by the guild since. One book taken. One page removed and burned, we believe, before the thief fled the premises."

"The guild burns its own inventory?"

"That is the question that kept me up, yes." She slid a folder across the desk. On it, in someone's tidy hand: *prophecy*. "The book is old. A century, perhaps older. It contains predictions, most of them nonsense, several of them uncomfortably accurate. One of them describes tonight's date. And a person."

I opened the folder. Transcription, not the original—the original had already vanished into some vault or some fire, because that is what the Authority does with dangerous texts, and I try not to think about how similar that sentence sounds when applied to people.

The transcription read:

*When the year turns as the ledger turns, the thief who is no one shall stand at the border of nowhere, and the door out of Zephyrine shall open for their hand.*

Below it, a second hand had added: *the thief bears no name that sticks; all who meet them forget.*

"Zephyrine," I said carefully.

"Is a cartographic error," Corvane said, in the flat recitation of a woman who has said it ten thousand times. "There has never been a country by that name. There was an administrative region, dissolved in the Reorganization, whose territories were apportioned to—" and here she gave the modern provincial names, and I recited along silently, because I have memorized the speech, everyone has, we were given it in school like multiplication tables. *There has never been a country by that name.* The strange thing about official erasure is that it requires this much maintenance. Truths sit still. Lies need walking.

"Your assignment," Corvane said, "is the thief. Find them. Bring them to me alive and whole—their memory intact. The Authority wishes to interview them regarding the text."

"And if they resist?"

"Then exercise judgment." She finally looked up, and her eyes were the color of ledgers, gray and ruled and full of entries. "Wren. This one matters. The Guild has operated inside our borders for a century because they were useful and quiet. If their own ledgers are naming thieves in prophecies, they have become neither. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"You're telling me the quiet part is over."

"I'm telling you that whatever you find, you report only to me." She closed the folder against my hands. "And Wren—you'll be good at this, won't you? You'll remember everything you see?"

It was not quite a question. It was never quite a question. That is the arrangement: she points the gift, I fire it, and neither of us names what it would mean to miss on purpose.

"Everything," I said, and went to hunt a person no one could remember.

* * *

Here is the trouble with hunting someone invisible: witnesses are useless.

I worked the library district for two days. I interviewed the night porter, who swore no one had entered; the archivist, who swore he'd catalogued the prophecy book himself just last week and couldn't imagine why anyone would want it; the street sweeper, who remembered nothing unusual, and then, when I showed him a sketch I'd commissioned of the likely thief—a figure of indeterminate build, because that is what "no one" gives you to work with—shook his head and added, helpfully, "Might've seen 'em. Might not. Hard to say. Faces slide off, you know?"

"They slide off," I agreed.

That is the guild's signature. Memory-theft leaves traces in the mind the way pickpockets leave traces in a crowd: a gap shaped like an interaction, a conversation you can't source, a stranger you somehow owe money to. Most people paper over the gaps without noticing. The mind hates a hole. It fills them with weather, with dreams, with plausible strangers.

Mine doesn't. Mine keeps the holes open, raw-edged, dated.

Which means that when I walked the Ashmarket on the third evening and passed a narrow alley mouth where something had happened—I knew it the way you know a missing tooth. There was a hole in the world with my name on it. Someone had been in that alley. Someone had made three people forget a transaction, and the forgetting sat in the air like smoke, and my mind stood in the middle of it refusing to breathe anything but the absence.

I remember standing there in the rain-smell and the fry-oil smell, thinking: *there you are.*

I remember thinking, a half-second later, a thought I did not file or authorize: *what did they take, and did they need it more than the person they took it from?*

I want to be clear that this second thought alarmed me more than the first.

* * *

Let me tell you about my brother, briefly, because he is the reason I am the knight I am, and because I have never once been able to avoid telling this story to myself. It plays nightly. Reserved seating.

His name was Cass. He was four years older than me and worse at everything except asking questions, at which he was the finest I have ever known. When the Reformation curriculum dropped Zephyrine from the maps—not corrected, not renamed, simply lifted out like a splinter—Cass was sixteen and I was twelve, and he noticed. Of course he noticed; he had my memory's hunger without my memory's discipline. He noticed that our grandmother hummed lullabies in a meter that matched no province's folk tradition. He noticed that the word *harbor* appeared in old songs from provinces that had no coastline. He started a list. Cass kept lists the way I keep everything: completely.

He asked his questions politely. That was the horror of it. He petitioned the provincial records office. Twice. He wrote letters with his neat draftsman's hand, citing dates and discrepancies, respectful as a courtier, and the third letter got answered.

Two men came. Not for him—for his papers, his list, his letters, all of it, with a receipt, because the Authority is scrupulous about receipts. And then they offered our parents a choice, delivered in the gentle administrative tone reserved for acts that cannot survive being described plainly: the boy could go to a corrective facility in the eastern provinces, or—

There is no *or*. That is the trick of it. The sentence just stops, and everyone in the room fills in the ending themselves, and the filling-in does the work better than any threat could.

Cass went east. We got one letter, censored into gibberish. Then nothing. My parents aged like fruit left in sun. And I, twelve years old, holding my brother's habit in my head—every date, every discrepancy, every question he'd asked, preserved in amber because that is what my curse does—I made a decision that I have re-made every morning since, because remembering it fresh each day is the closest I come to choosing it.

I decided that if I was going to be forced to keep everything, I would make keeping into a weapon. I would remember so well, so relentlessly, that the Authority would have no choice but to aim me at the people who *unmake*—the guild, the thieves, the ones who leave holes in the world like the hole where my brother used to be.

They gave me a sword and a title and pointed me at gaps. For eleven years I have filled them in. Missing persons. Tampered witnesses. Guild transactions unwound thread by thread from the memories of people who didn't know they'd been robbed until I arrived and asked exactly the right exact questions.

I told myself it was justice. I have eleven years of journal entries saying so, in my own hand, and my own hand does not lie.

But here is the entry problem: justice is supposed to give back. In eleven years I have recovered hundreds of stolen memories and returned every one. I have never once found Cass. And lately—at night, in the dark behind my eyes where everything lives forever—another thought has begun to surface, patient as water: *you are very good at finding what the Authority wants found, Wren. Has it ever occurred to you to wonder why they have never once wanted you to find your brother?*

I do not let that thought finish. I am the only person alive who can't look away from a thought, and I have learned to starve them instead. But they are getting louder. They have been getting louder for years, and I suspect—precisely, measurably, because I measure everything—that the prophecy-thief is going to make them deafening.

* * *

I found the trail on the fourth day, and I want it recorded—I record everything, that is the whole point of me—that the trail was found through competence, not accident.

A bargemaster on the Serpent Reach had sold passage and could not remember to whom, but remembered the price: "a memory of the sea, real one, salt in it." Nobody pays for passage with a memory unless they can't afford coin, and nobody steals a memory of the sea unless they've never had one. So: young. Untrained, or newly trained. Frightened enough to flee, sentimental enough to buy the ocean they'd never seen. I wrote it all down in my field journal, in order, and felt the familiar click of the hunt engaging—the clean machine of it, purpose fitting into purpose like gears.

Then I reached the tollhouse at Marrow Cut, and the machine cracked.

Later, I would go over that hour frame by frame, because that is what I do instead of sleeping. What I retain is this:

The tollhouse keeper, hollow-eyed, mid-forgetting, the transaction still dissolving behind his face. Me stepping past him onto the wet planks. The thief at the far end of the pier with my knife at their throat before either of us had decided that was the plan—my body is well-trained; it handles these moments while I catch up.

And then the strange part. The part I have replayed nine hundred times and will replay nine hundred more.

They didn't run. They held out a book. Old vellum, guild-stamped, and they opened it with shaking hands to a page they had apparently not burned after all—or copied, or salvaged—and they said, in a voice like someone apologizing for existing:

"It has your name in it. Read it. Please. Before you do whatever you're going to do, just—read it."

And I read it. Because of course I did. I am incapable of not reading a thing placed in front of me; ask my tutors, who wept.

*The knight who forgets nothing shall stand opposite the thief at the water's edge, and her choice shall be the hinge.*

I looked at the words. I looked at the thief—thin, rain-wet, eyes the color of someone braced for a blow that has been coming their whole life. I looked at the river. I thought, with terrible clarity: *a century-old book predicted this pier, this hour, my name, my face, my choice,* and beneath that thought, rising like something surfacing from deep water, the thought I have starved for eleven years opened both its eyes:

*If this is true, then the Authority knows. The Authority has always known. Everything erased, everything disappeared, everyone filed east—it was never about correcting errors. It was about this. About a door. About keeping a whole country buried under a lie until whatever was prophesied could be prevented or harvested, and my brother died of a question about a harbor because somewhere above him a marshal read a prophecy and decided the story mattered more than the people in it.*

My hand lowered the knife.

I did not decide to lower it. I want that in the record too, because I am honest even when honesty costs, and the truth is that some choices get made in a body before they reach the person. The knife went down, and the thief went pale, and I heard myself say—the words arriving like a transcription of something written long ago:

"Run. Downstream, not upstream; Corporal Vesh checks the upstream weir at dusk. Don't take the barge you paid for; it's marked. And give me the page."

They gave me the page. Their fingers brushed mine, and I watched them realize, watching my face, that I wasn't going to forget them. That I *couldn't*. Something moved across their expression—fear, then something worse, something almost like relief, like a person handed a heavy package after carrying it alone.

Then they ran, downstream, as instructed, and I stood on the pier with a page of forbidden prophecy in my hand and committed, in the space of ninety seconds, the first treason of my life.

I have committed it again every day since, in the only way I know how: by remembering. Every detail. Their voice. The page. The exact wording of the line about the hinge.

Because here is what occurred to me, standing in the rain while the clean machine of my purpose ground and sparked and failed to close its gears: I have spent eleven years recovering stolen things and returning them to their owners. It is the only justice I have ever actually performed.

And Zephyrine is a stolen thing. The biggest one. Stolen from itself, from its exiles, from my humming grandmother, from Cass and his lists.

The Marshal believes I am hunting the thief. I am. I promise I am. I am hunting them downstream, along the Serpent Reach, toward the border of nowhere, one careful, meticulous, unforgettable step behind.

By tomorrow's dusk, I calculate, I will have caught up with them at the camp of the mapmaker's daughter—if my calculations hold, and they always hold.

I am bringing my sword, my journal, and eleven years of a question I have never dared finish.

I intend to finish it.
