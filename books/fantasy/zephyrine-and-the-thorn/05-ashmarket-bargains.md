## 5. Ashmarket Bargains

There is a particular smell to a place where memories are sold over the counter: something like copper, something like rain on hot stone, and underneath it all the faint sweetness of other people's happiness going stale. The Ashmarket smelled like that at three in the morning, when I arrived with nothing but the clothes I'd stolen off a laundry line and a ledger page pressed flat against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

I had been in the Ashmarket before, of course. Everyone in the Vellum Guild had. We simply hadn't *mattered* while we were there. That was always the trick of it—I could walk through a crowd of dealers and fences and cutpurses and leave no more impression than smoke leaves on glass. Being forgotten was my profession. For eleven years it had also been my entire personality, which I realize now says something depressing about me, but let's not dwell.

The trouble with fleeing the guild is that the guild teaches you everything you need to know to survive being hunted by it. I knew which alleys dead-ended into informants, which taverns kept two ledgers (one for the tax assessor, one for the truth), which faces belonged to Sable's eyes. I knew how to walk so that no one's gaze snagged on me, how to answer questions so the asker would forget having asked them. I had spent a decade becoming nobody, and tonight—for the first time—it felt less like armor and more like amputation.

I bought a cup of something hot and brown from a stall whose owner forgot me before I'd finished paying, which saved me from small talk, and I found a doorway with a good sightline to the memory-dealers' row and tried to think.

Here is what I knew. The prophecy book in the guild archive had listed tonight's date beside a description of me: *the thief who is no one*. Sable had gone the color of old vellum and ordered the page burned. And the last person named in that book—a locksmith named Corvin Ansel, forty years ago—had been *retired*, which is guild-speak for what happens when you stop being an asset and start being a liability. I had stolen my own page out of the ledger before anyone could do the same to me. It sat against my chest now, ink facing inward, like a name tag for someone who wasn't allowed to have a name.

What I needed was to get out of the city. What I needed, specifically, was a smuggler, because the roads out of Zephyrine—the country that officially did not exist, whose borders had been redrawn with the enthusiasm of a child scribbling over a drawing they hated—were watched by the Provisional Authority, and the Authority had recently developed an interest in prophecy thieves.

What I had was the contents of my pockets: three coins of dubious provenance, a lockpick disguised as a pen nib, and access to the single most valuable black-market commodity in the world, which is other people's heads.

* * *

Memory-dealers' row runs along the sunken canal where the tanners used to work, back before the tanneries were closed for reasons no one remembers, possibly because someone sold the remembering. By day it's fishmongers and rope-sellers. By night the stalls change hands without changing much else, and the dealers come out with their velvet trays.

Each dealer has a specialty. There was Madge of the Tow-Rope, who dealt only in childhoods—not whole ones, she wasn't a monster, just the good hours—and there was a fat man called Uncle who sold griefs at a markup to aristocrats who liked to feel things they hadn't earned. And at the far end, half-hidden behind a hanging oil lamp, was the stall I wanted: Odile's. Odile traded in large goods. Journeys. Weather. One memorable summer she'd done brisk business selling the experience of drowning to thrill-seekers, until a client's heart gave out and the market decided near-drowning was safer marketing.

I drifted toward her stall doing my best impression of a customer, which is to say no impression at all.

"Buying or selling?" Odile didn't look up. She was fifty-something, built like a barge herself, with forearms that suggested she'd personally winched every cargo she'd ever moved. A tin lamp guttered between us.

"Selling," I said. "One item. Sea passage."

That got her eyes up. They were quick and grey and entirely unfooled. "That's not inventory, that's freight."

"The memory of a crossing," I said. "Storm off the Dagger Coast. A woman's last voyage before she settled inland. Full sensory. Salt, spray, the works." I had lifted it three nights ago from a retired harbormaster who'd been trading it down gradually to pay a gambling debt; the Guild had brokered the sale, and I had skimmed a copy off the master record the way clerks skim everything. It was, technically, theft from my employers. This no longer weighed on me as heavily as it might have, given that they currently wanted me dead.

Odile turned her lamp up a notch and studied me the way you study a coin you suspect of being shaved. "Guild stock."

"Guild surplus."

"Guild surplus comes with guild paperwork."

"The paperwork burned," I said, which was true, though not in the way she'd hear it.

She was quiet a moment. Somewhere behind us a dealer laughed too loudly at a joke, and a drunk argued with a lamplighter about whether the moon was government property. Then Odile said, in a voice pitched only for me: "You're the one everyone's whispering about. The loose end."

My stomach performed a maneuver I associate with barges in bad weather. "Am I."

"Don't bother," she said. "You've got the look. Nobody walks into my market at this hour with guild stock and no shadow unless the guild itself is the thing they're running from." She spat, expertly, into the canal. "I don't sell to hunters, love. Question is whether you're worth the risk of the alternative."

"I can pay," I said. "Sea memory. Clean copy. You won't find better south of the coast itself."

"For passage, maybe. Not for silence." She leaned on her counter. "Silence costs extra these days. There's Authority money moving through this market like bilge water. Somebody's offering a purse for a person matching no description anyone can hold onto." Her mouth twitched. "Which is clever, I'll grant. Hard to poster a face nobody can remember."

"I wouldn't know."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

And that was when the child bit me.

Not hard. A warning nip on the wrist, quick as a sparrow, from beneath Odile's counter where I had assumed there were only crates. I jerked back and came face to face with a kid—ten, maybe eleven, all elbows and insolence, hair cropped short and dirty-blond, sitting cross-legged on a coil of rope like a small dragon on a hoard.

"They're lying," the kid announced, pointing at me with evident satisfaction. "Both times. 'Clean copy'—lie. 'Wouldn't know'—lie." A grin split the grime. "Tastes like vinegar, your lies. Cheap vinegar."

"Pip," Odile said, in the tone of someone who has said this word ten thousand times, "we do not taste the customers."

"You *said* taste the customers."

"Taste their *goods*. Their goods are business. Their persons are none of ours." But she didn't sound angry; she sounded like a woman recalculating. Her grey eyes went between the two of us—the child who could taste lies, and the thief nobody could remember—and something behind her face began, visibly, to add numbers.

"A lie-taster," I said slowly.

"And a ghost," Odile said. "And me, with a barge leaving on the morning tide." She held out one broad hand. "Show me the sea."

I set the memory vial on the counter. It was a small thing, amber glass no longer than my thumb, the liquid inside moving even when held still, the way the sea does. Odile uncorked it, touched the rim to her tongue, and closed her eyes.

For perhaps four seconds, her weathered face went somewhere else entirely. When she opened her eyes they were wet, and she pretended they weren't, and I pretended I hadn't noticed, because that is the courtesy of the Ashmarket.

"Dagger Coast," she said roughly. "Nineteen years back. There was a boy at the rail who—" She stopped, corked the vial, and set it down with exaggerated care. "Right. Passage for one. Hold cargo. You keep your mouth shut and your hands visible, and if the Authority boards us, you're my deaf nephew and you can't swim."

"That's a lot of conditions."

"That's a lot of tide coming in." She glanced past me, and her voice dropped further. "One more thing, ghost, free of charge. There's a knight asking after you. Not Authority-uniformed, either—plainclothes, working the market since dusk. Woman. Doesn't drink, doesn't haggle, doesn't forget. My people say she looked straight at three different dealers and asked them questions they'll be repeating word for word in their sleep." A pause. "You want to not be here when she works her way down the row."

I was already moving.

* * *

Pip followed me.

I want to be clear that I did not invite this. At no point did I say *yes, small criminal, accompany me onto a smuggling barge at dawn*. What happened was that I paid Odile, stowed myself among crates of contraband maps in the hold of her barge—the *Improbable*, which struck me as either very honest or very ironic naming—and lay awake in the dark smelling of tar and old geography until, an hour before the tide, a hatch creaked and a small body insinuated itself into the space beside mine with the total lack of apology unique to children and cats.

"No," I said.

"Yes," said Pip.

"This is dangerous."

"I know." The grin was audible in the dark. "That's why."

I considered my options, which were: eject the child, negotiate with the child, or accept the child. Ejecting meant noise, and noise meant Odile's deckhands asking questions, and questions are the natural predator of stowaways. Negotiating with Pip was, I sensed, a war of attrition I would lose. Which left acceptance, and I want it noted that I framed this as pragmatism and not as the first act of a man building a family out of strays, because I still had some dignity left that night.

"Why did you bite me?" I asked instead. "Back at the stall. You didn't have to announce I was lying. Most people like liars fine."

Pip was quiet for a moment. In the dark, the kid's face was just a pale smudge and two bright eyes. "Because you lied about the memory," Pip said finally. "You told Odile it was clean copy. It isn't. It's got somebody else stitched in the middle of it—a boy at the rail. He doesn't belong to the harbormaster. Someone grafted him in. Guild work?" I must have made a sound, because Pip nodded, satisfied. "Thought so. And you knew, and you sold it anyway, and you felt rotten about it. Your lies taste like cheap vinegar, but your *rotten* tastes like—" the kid hunted for it— "like green plums. Before they're ripe." A shrug, elaborately casual. "I like plums."

"That is an alarming amount of information to extract from biting a stranger."

"You taste like an open book," Pip said. "A book somebody's trying to tear pages out of."

I didn't answer that. Above us, footsteps crossed the deck; the mooring lines came off one by one; the *Improbable* leaned into the current with the unhurried confidence of a vessel that had never once been anywhere legally. Through the seams of the hatch came a thin line of lantern light from the quay, and in that light, for one moment, I saw a figure walking the row of shuttered stalls. Plainclothes. Unhurried. Asking questions of a shutter, of a sleeping dealer, of the dark itself, patient as tide.

She couldn't have described me. No one could. That had been the whole point of me, for eleven years.

So why did the back of my neck prickle like someone had read my name aloud?

"Ghost," Pip whispered, watching my face. "Who's she?"

"I don't know," I said, and for once it wasn't a lie, and Pip didn't correct me.

The barge slid out into the black water, carrying a lie-taster, a nobody, and a stolen sea, away from the city where a woman who forgets nothing was already, I understood with terrible certainty, coming to find me.
