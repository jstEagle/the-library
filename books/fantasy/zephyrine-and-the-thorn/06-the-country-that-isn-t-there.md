## 6. The Country That Isn't There

Theo

The barge was called the *Merciful Widow*, which told you everything you needed to know about smugglers and their sense of humor.

She sat low in the water, black-hulled, smelling of tar and old fish and the particular staleness of a hold that had once carried something it wasn't supposed to. Odile—captain, owner, and apparently sole crew—had taken my payment without ceremony: a memory of the sea, lifted from a drowned admiral's widow two years back and never spent. She'd held it up to the lantern like a jeweler appraising a stone.

"This is real salt," she'd said. "You don't see this anymore. Everyone deals in secondhand weather." Then she'd spat over the side, which I took as a contract signed.

Now we were three hours downriver from Ashmarket, huddled under an oilcloth in the bow while rain did its patient work on everything above deck. Pip slept against a coil of rope, mouth open, one hand still curled around a stolen brass button they refused to explain. And Odile sat across from me, whittling a piece of wood that was slowly becoming either a bird or a very ambitious spoon.

I should have slept too. Fugitives are supposed to sleep when they can; it's in all the pamphlets. But my ledger page was burning a hole against my ribs where I'd tucked it inside my shirt, and every time the barge rocked I felt the ink shift, like the words were trying to rearrange themselves into a sentence I hadn't agreed to read.

*The thief who is no one.*

"You keep touching your chest," Odile said, not looking up from her carving. "Like you've got a saint's relic under there."

"It's paper."

"Paper's worse. Paper makes claims." She turned the wood, considered it, shaved off a curl. "You're guild-trained. I can tell."

"How?"

"You flinch when people look at you, but you count them anyway. Exits, hands, weight of their coats. Guild children learn arithmetic before they learn names." She grinned at whatever she saw in my face. "Don't worry. I'm not going to remember you. Bad for business, remembering passengers."

"That's the idea," I said. "Remembering is bad for business. It's practically the guild motto."

"Mm." The knife paused. "And yet here you are, paying in sea-memory to get *somewhere*, which means somewhere matters to you, which means you've already broken rule one."

I didn't answer, because she was right, and because Pip chose that moment to sit bolt upright and say, "I'm awake," in the tone of someone who very obviously was not, then immediately: "Are we talking about the country? I want to hear about the country."

"What country?" I asked, because I genuinely didn't know which one they meant. In Ashmarket, *the country* could mean any of six things, four of them illegal.

"The one that isn't there." Pip scooted closer, dragging their blanket. "Odile talks about it sometimes when she thinks I'm asleep. The one the maps got told to forget."

Odile's knife stopped moving entirely.

I felt it then—the particular silence of a person deciding how much truth a stranger can carry. I'd worn that silence myself, behind guild counters, weighing what a client could afford to know. It was strange to be on the other side of the scale.

"They're coming with us or they're not," Pip said, with the merciless logic of children and drunkards. "If they're coming, tell them. If they're not, throw them off at the bend and I'll have their boots."

"Your loyalty is inspiring," I said.

"I'm practical," said Pip, who was nine, maybe ten, and had pickpocketed my last coin within an hour of meeting me. "Boots are practical."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. Rain ticked on the oilcloth. Somewhere behind us, the river kept saying its one word over and over.

"All right," she said finally. "But not because the child threatened your boots. Because you paid in salt, and salt remembers."

Wren

The report wrote itself, which was the problem. Reports that write themselves are reports that lie.

*Subject entered Ashmarket district approximately the eleventh hour. Subject's description unavailable per standard memory-interference protocols. Subject presumed to have acquired river passage southward.*

Presumed. I stared at the word until it stopped looking like a word. In eleven years with the Provisional Authority I had written four thousand reports—I counted; counting was the only comfort my condition allowed—and I had never once used *presumed*. I knew things. That was the entire reason I existed in the machinery of the state: everyone else's recollection could be lifted, traded, edited, sold back to them improved. Mine could not. The memory-thieves had tried on me twice, early on, before they learned. A knight who cannot forget is the only witness the Authority trusts, and the only witness it fears.

So why had I stood in that tollhouse lane—no. Not the tollhouse yet. That was ahead of me, though I already knew it was ahead of me, the way you know a stone is ahead of you when the road turns downhill.

Why had I stood in that alley off Tallow Street and watched the thief walk away?

Because of the page. They'd held it out flat between two fingers, careful as someone offering bread to a strange dog, and I had read it. I could recite it now, verbatim, in the voice of whoever had set it down a century ago, the ink browned to rust:

*The knight who forgets nothing shall meet the thief who is no one at the water, and at the water she will choose, and her choosing is the hinge.*

A prophecy is just a rumor with better posture. That's what Captain Vess would say. That's what I would say, in the report, if I reported it. Which I would not, because reporting it meant handing my own name to men who made citizens into blanks, who had done it to—

To Dorian.

I put his face away. I was getting good at putting his face away, which was its own kind of horror, because nothing about me was supposed to go away. Every other grief in the world could be stolen out of its owner; mine stayed. Eleven years and my brother's absence sat in my chest as fresh as the day the Authority's clerks smiled and said *he asked unauthorized questions, Knight Aldous, he asked about Zephyrine*, as if asking were a wound you caught.

Unauthorized questions. About a country that officially did not exist, had never existed, whose name appeared in no census, no treaty, no schoolbook—except it appeared in my brother's mouth at breakfast, once, when he was nineteen and reckless and had found something in an archive he shouldn't have been able to open.

*Dor,* I'd said. *There's no such place.*

*I know,* he'd said, delighted, terrible. *That's what's so good about it.*

They took him nine days later. I remembered all nine days. I remembered the tenth, when the letter came, unsigned, expressing the Authority's regret for the loss of a citizen whose records had been, quote, administratively consolidated.

Administratively consolidated. My brother, folded into a filing error.

I finished the report without the prophecy in it, sealed it, and did not send it, and told myself the delay was procedural. There was a lot of telling myself, lately. It was becoming a full-time occupation, right alongside hunting a person whose face slid out of my mind like water off glass—which was impossible, which had never happened to me before, which meant the thief was either using something stronger than the guild's ordinary tricks, or was exactly what a dead man's book said they were.

At the water, she will choose.

I saddled my horse and rode south along the river road, toward a tollhouse I hadn't reached yet, carrying a choice I hadn't made yet, and hated every mile for how familiar the shape of it already was.

Theo

"So," Odile said, settling back against the gunwale with her knife finally stowed. "How much geography did the guild give you?"

"Enough to price maps. Not enough to read them."

"Naturally." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Then start here. Look at the water. What do you see?"

I looked. River, rain, dark banks. "Water."

"Look again. South bank, past that crooked willow. What's the ground doing?"

"Rising. There's a ridge."

"There's a *wall*," Odile said. "Or there was. Cut stone, fitted without mortar—that's Zephyrine work, older than anything the Authority builds. They quarried it apart when they drew the new border, shipped the stones upriver to build customs houses. But the foundation courses are too deep to bother with. So the land keeps the shape of what was taken off it, even when nobody's allowed to say so." She tapped the gunwale. "This river used to run through the middle of a country. Now it runs along the edge of a province. Same water. Different sentence."

Pip had gone very still, which for Pip meant total attention.

"The country has a name," Odile said quietly, watching me. "Say it wrong and you'll find out fast who's listening. Say it right and you'll find out even faster. Zephyrine."

The word landed in my chest like a coin dropped down a well. I waited for the splash and heard none. "I've never—" I started, and stopped, because I didn't know how to finish. Never heard it? Never said it? There was a third thing, some door-shaped absence in me, and I hated it.

"Most people haven't," Odile said. "That was the whole point. Forty-one years ago—some say thirty-eight, dates are contraband too—the Verrecourt Compact redrew the eastern provinces. Conquered, partitioned, renamed. On paper, Zephyrine never fought a war, because on paper Zephyrine never was. The maps came out the next spring with new borders drawn straight through villages, through graveyards, through somebody's kitchen, and everyone who objected was invited to stop objecting. Permanently, some of them."

"And everyone else?"

"Went along. You do. You wake up in the Province of Verre-Maren, and your town is called something with fewer syllables, and your grandmother's stories are just stories, and after a while—" she shrugged, and the shrug carried more weight than most confessions— "after a while you can't prove otherwise. Memory's a trade good now. The Authority buys nostalgia by the barrel and sells back contentment. Why do you think the Vellum Guild thrives? Half their inventory is this erasure, bottled."

My hand went to my chest again. The page crackled under my palm like a small fire.

"The old country survives three ways," Odile went on, holding up fingers. "One: contraband. Maps, songs, seeds—yes, seeds, apple varieties that only grow on the old soil, try confiscating an orchard's grandchildren. Two: the exiles' tongues. Kids in exile camps speak Zephyrine to each other so the clerks can't follow. Three—and this is the one that keeps magistrates up at night—the prophecy. A hundred years old, written before the fall, which means it's either fraud or foresight, and both options terrify the government equally." Her eyes settled on me, gray and unhurried. "It lists people, that prophecy. Names them by what they can do rather than what they're called. A thief who is no one. A knight who forgets nothing. A child who tastes lies." She nodded at Pip. "A mapmaker's blood."

The barge rocked. The rain kept on. I became aware that Pip was looking at me with an expression far too old for their face, and that Odile was waiting, and that the honest thing and the suicidal thing were, for once, the same sentence.

"The page named me," I said. "Tonight's date. A century ago, someone wrote tonight's date next to a description of—" I almost said *a stranger*. "Next to me."

"Did they now." Odile turned her carved bird-spoon over in her fingers. "And what did the guild do when you showed them?"

"Ordered it burned."

"And you ran instead. Good instinct. Bad odds." She leaned forward. "Do you understand what you are yet? Because I'll tell you what the Authority thinks you are: a loose thread in a garment they spent forty years weaving. Erasure isn't an event, guild-child. It's maintenance. Countries don't stay forgotten on their own—someone sweeps the floor every day. And a prophecy that comes true is a broom handle sticking out of the dust where anyone can trip on it."

"So they'll kill me."

"They'll *unwrite* you. Worse. Kill a man and you make a martyr; blank a man and you make nothing at all." Odile's voice dropped. "Ask me how I know the difference."

"How do you—"

"My mother," she said, "was born in Zephyrine. City called Aurelane, on the coast, where the light comes off the water so gold the poets wouldn't shut up about it. She remembered it perfectly. Until she sold her memories—one by one—to feed me during the famine winters, and the Authority bought them, because they buy everything true. By the end she couldn't tell me her own street's name. She'd hum a song and cry and not know why." Odile looked out at the black water. "I've been running cargo south ever since. Maps, mostly. Seeds, sometimes. Truth, when I can afford it. Grief, always."

Pip had crept close enough to lean against Odile's arm. She let them.

I thought about the ledgers. Rows and rows of neat entries in my own handwriting: *one grief, widow, aged; one shame, general, mint condition.* All those memories flowing through the guild's vaults, and somewhere in that flood—Odile's mother's golden light. Aurelane. A street name. I had catalogued a homeland and priced it by the ounce and never once wondered where it had come from, because wondering was not in my job description, and being no one meant never having to ask what you were standing inside of.

"I never knew I was missing a country," I said. It came out smaller than I intended.

"Nobody from inside knows," Odile said. "That's the trick of erasure. You don't mourn what you never had—unless someone shows you the shape of the hole." She pointed her chin at my shirt, at the hidden page. "Sounds like a dead writer's been showing you the shape for a while now."

Wren

The tollhouse came into view at dusk, and my stomach did something it had never done in eleven years of service: it dreaded my own competence.

Because I was competent. I had tracked the thief this far on evidence no one else could hold—boot wear in the Ashmarket mud, a bargemaster's ledger entry paid in a memory I could smell on the transaction record, the specific way dockhands avoided looking at a spot where, presumably, someone unforgettable had stood. Any other knight would have lost the trail three times over. I had not lost it. I never lost anything. That was the curse of me.

*At the water, she will choose.*

Vess's orders sat in my saddlebag: capture or kill, effective immediately, no transport required. No transport required. Four words that meant *don't bring back anything that can talk.* I'd written similar orders myself. I knew their grammar intimately.

I dismounted a half-mile out and walked, because the tollhouse keeper owed me a favor and I wanted to arrive quiet. The river moved past, brown and patient, and I thought about Dorian again, unbidden, the ninth day, the letter, the phrase *administratively consolidated*—and underneath that, the thing I never let surface: that I had said *there's no such place*, that I had repeated the Authority's line to my own brother at his own table, that some part of his recklessness that morning might have been him trying to hand me the truth before they took him, and I had declined delivery.

A country that isn't there. A brother who isn't anywhere. And a thief at the water, ahead of me, carrying a page that said my choosing was a hinge—as if I were a door, as if duty and I were separable things.

Ahead, lamplight burned in the tollhouse window. A barge lamp flickered downstream, closing.

I checked my blade out of habit and found my hand steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Shaking would have meant I still had a choice left to lose.

Theo

Past midnight the rain thinned to mist, and Odile pointed the barge toward a stone arch spanning the river—the tollhouse, its lamp bleeding yellow onto the water. Pip had gone back to sleep, or was pretending to with the dedication of a professional. Odile steered with one hand and spoke without turning her head.

"Tollhouse ahead. Authority garrison rotates through—two riders, sometimes three. If you're the person I think you are, they'll be looking for you, and there's only one kind of hunter they'd send."

"A knight," I said. The word arrived with a face attached, and the face arrived with a memory attached, and the memory was a narrow alley and flat gray eyes reading a page I'd held out like bread to a strange dog. Except the eyes hadn't slid away. Everything else in the world forgot me within minutes—clients, guards, Sable's own clerks—but those eyes had held, and I'd run anyway, because holding was worse than forgetting. Holding meant the prophecy's other line was walking around in a uniform.

"The one who forgets nothing," Odile murmured. "Yes. I'd bet the boat." She glanced at me sidelong. "Whatever happens at that tollhouse, guild-child—remember what I told you about the wall under the willow. This river runs along the edge of a country that's still in it. People died keeping the shape alive. Don't make their sweeping pointless."

The tollhouse grew. The lamp burned. And on the near bank, a horse stood tethered, riderless, which meant its rider was already inside, already waiting, already choosing—or about to make me choose.

Odile cut the lantern.

"Get below," she said. "Both of you. And Theo—"

"You know my name?" I said, stupidly, uselessly, because no one was supposed to know my name, including me.

"I paid for a sea-memory once," Odile said, pushing the tiller over. "Salt remembers. Go."
