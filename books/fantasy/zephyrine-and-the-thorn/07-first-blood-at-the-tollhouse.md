## 7. First Blood at the Tollhouse

The Authority teaches you that a fugitive is a problem of arithmetic. Distance divided by speed, multiplied by the stubbornness of the hunted. I had done the sums on the thief who was no one for six days now, and every sum came back wrong.

Six days of asking questions in river towns where people smiled at me with the particular blankness of the recently touched. Six days of innkeepers swearing no one had come through, while their eyes slid toward a door as if something had just gone through it. Six days of watching witnesses forget me mid-sentence — and worse, watching them *want* to forget me, the way you want to stop humming a tune. The thief left no footprints because they left a kind of weather behind them instead: a soft pressure on the mind, an instruction to look elsewhere.

Everyone forgot them. That was the whole architecture of their survival.

Everyone except me.

I don't know why my memory holds what it holds. The Authority's physicians have poked at the question with instruments I'm not permitted to describe in a report, and their conclusion was a shrug dressed in Latin. Some minds are wax; mine appears to be stone. It has never been a gift. Ask me about my brother's face and I can give it to you down to the chipped tooth, eleven years after the Authority took the rest of him, and there are nights when that precision is less like sight and more like a wound that refuses to close because it insists on staying clean.

So when everyone else's recollection of the thief dissolved like sugar in rain, mine stayed. I remembered the figure in the Ashmarket who had brushed past me — slight build, hood, hands too careful for a cutpurse, a smell of ink and candlewax. I remembered the ledger page glimpsed over a dealer's shoulder, columns of stolen griefs entered in a neat hand. And I remembered the prophecy fragment the Archivist had read aloud to me before sealing it away again, his voice going reverent and afraid:

*The thief who is no one shall stand at the door out of Zephyrine, and beside them the knight who forgets nothing.*

Beside. Not against. Beside.

I had reported none of this. I had written in my official log: *Subject unremarkable. Pursuit proceeding.* Which was itself a small treason, though I told myself it was merely tidiness. You do not put prophecy in a report. Reports are for facts, and prophecy is for priests and madmen, and I am neither.

I am, however, very good at arithmetic.

* * *

The tollhouse at Greyford sat where the Liss narrowed enough to be bridged, which meant it sat exactly where a fugitive traveling by water would have to stop, pay, and be seen. Odile's barge had been logged passing three checkpoints downstream — the smuggler either didn't know she carried the most wanted person in the Provisional Authority or didn't care, and I'd met Odile Vane once at a customs hearing and knew perfectly well which it was.

I arrived an hour before the barge would. This is the part of pursuit nobody writes songs about: the waiting. I bought tea from the tollkeeper's wife, paid with coin that wouldn't be traced, and sat on the bridge wall with my cloak doing its best impression of an ordinary traveler's boredom. Rain came sideways off the river. A dog regarded me with professional suspicion and then, deciding I was beneath its standards, lay down.

I thought about my brother during the wait. I try not to — the Authority frowns on sentiment the way it frowns on everything it can't file — but the rain made the river grey the way the canal outside our childhood house had been grey, and memory, being stone in me, does not erode on request. Tomas had asked one question too loudly at the wrong dinner party: *whatever happened to Zephyrine? Just — administratively speaking. Where did it go?* Three weeks later he was administratively speaking nowhere. I joined the Authority eight months after that, and everyone said how brave, how loyal, and what I actually was, though I could not have said it aloud then, was a woman walking into the wolf den to learn the wolves' paperwork. If they took people by procedure, I would master the procedure. I would find the drawer where they kept my brother.

Eleven years. The drawer stays shut. My memory does not.

The barge appeared at midafternoon, low in the water, flying the flag of a grain concern that did not exist. I watched it nose toward the tollhouse and saw, among the cargo figures on deck, a slight shape in a hooded coat standing very still, the way people stand when stillness is a skill rather than a mood.

Ink and candlewax. Careful hands.

I gave them ten minutes to disembark, pay the toll, and start up the cart road — the only road, which is the cruelty of tollhouses — and then I moved.

* * *

Cornering someone is easier than fiction makes it look, if you've chosen the ground. The cart road climbed through a cutting with walls of wet slate, one fork, no cover, and I stepped out of the culvert shadow with my blade drawn and my warrant held high, and I said the sentence I had rehearsed until it had worn smooth:

"Theo Eriksen. By order of the Provisional Authority, you're detained for questioning regarding thefts of—"

They turned around.

Here is what I had not prepared for, despite six days of preparation: being looked at by them. The hood fell back and I saw a face that was young and not young, ordinary in the way of faces built specifically to be forgotten, and eyes that went through me like a clerk auditing a soul and finding the entries wanting. They were not afraid. That unsettled me more than a knife would have. Everyone is afraid of a drawn blade; it is practically a civic duty. Theo Eriksen looked at my sword the way I might look at rain — acknowledged, resented, not feared.

"You're Wren Aldous," they said.

My name in their mouth went through me strangely, because almost no one says it anymore. The Authority numbers its knights; the towns forget them on principle. "You know my name."

"I've known your name since before you did." They reached slowly — correctly slowly, palm visible, the gesture of someone who had been robbed by professionals and had learned the choreography — into their coat, and drew out a book.

"Keep your hands—"

"It's a ledger," they said. "It's the ledger. Mine, from the Guild. And there's a page in it that has your name on it, Knight Aldous, and I think you should see it before you decide what happens in the next thirty seconds, because I promise you the next thirty seconds are not the ones you planned."

Every training instinct said take the book, take the thief, ask questions in a room with bars. Every instinct except one — the stone one, the one that remembers everything, including the Archivist's voice saying *beside them*, and the way his hands shook when he closed the book.

"Slowly," I said.

They opened the ledger to a ribbon-marked page and turned it toward me, holding it out at arm's length like a man offering meat across a kennel fence.

The hand was neat, clerical, beautiful even — the hand of someone who found comfort in columns. But the entry was no inventory. It was a transcription, dated a century past, in ink gone brown:

*On the night of the [date], the door out of Zephyrine may be opened only by the thief who is no one, accompanied by the knight who forgets nothing, bearing the map of the country's true borders, at the price the door itself will name.*

And beneath it, in the same careful modern hand, two lines added recently:

*Theo Eriksen. Wren Aldous.*

My own name. In a hundred-year-old prophecy, transcribed by a guild clerk, dated to this season.

"That date," I said, and my voice came out level, which I counted as the day's single professional achievement, "is three days ago."

"Yes," said the thief.

"This is forged."

"Do I look like a forger?" They spread their arms slightly. "Look at me. Truly look. I'm a bookkeeper. I keep ledgers for people who trade in stolen memories — yes, that crime exists, yes, your Authority buys from us, invoice number whatever, check your own archives if your superiors let you. I was good at being forgotten and I thought that was the same thing as being safe. Then I catalogued a looted library and found my own description in a book older than the Authority, and the Guild tried to burn the page, and I learned what the Guild does to loose ends, and now I'm running, and you—" their voice did something complicated, "—you are the first person in a week who has looked at me for longer than four seconds without forgetting why."

I hadn't lowered my blade. Note that. Whatever else happened at the tollhouse, I want it recorded somewhere other than my own unreliable-for-nothing memory that I did not lower my blade out of sentiment. I lowered it — no. I did not lower it. Let me tell this properly.

"What the Guild does to loose ends," I repeated. "Which is?"

"Ask the last person the prophecy named." Their mouth twisted. "Oh — you can't. She was retired forty years ago. The Guild's word. There's a grave in the Guildyard under a stone that says only *A Faithful Servant*. Sable keeps her ledger page framed in her office. As a warning, or a souvenir. I haven't decided which is worse."

Sable. I knew the name from intelligence files the thickness of bricks. Guildmaster of the Vellum Guild. The Authority's relationship with her was the kind diplomats call *understanding*.

"You expect me to believe a fairy tale," I said, "because it's written in a pretty hand."

"No," Theo said quietly. "I expect you to remember. That's the whole point of you, isn't it? That's why they sent *you*. Everyone else who gets near me loses me like smoke. You can't. So you are the one person in this country who can verify, all on your own, that I am telling the truth — because you'll remember every word I say tomorrow, and next week, and in eleven years, whether you want to or not."

The rain ticked on slate. Somewhere below, the tollkeeper's dog barked twice at the barge and gave up.

"And what," I said, "am I supposed to do with the truth, once verified? My orders are to bring you in. The Authority does not have a form for *prophecy confirmed, detainee released*."

"No," Theo agreed. "You'd have to invent one. I hear that's how Zephyrine got erased. Somebody invented a form."

That was a knife with my brother's name on the handle, and they couldn't have known it, and knowing they couldn't have known it did nothing whatsoever to blunt it. I felt my face change and hated that it changed, because a knight of the Provisional Authority should have a face like a locked drawer.

"There is no Zephyrine," I said. "There never was. The maps—"

"—are contraband," they finished. "You know that. Don't tell me you don't. You've seen the seizures logged. Whole crates of atlases burned, and burning paper doesn't bother anyone unless the paper matters." They stepped closer — one step, deliberate, into the reach of my blade, which is either courage or a bookkeeper's fatalism, and with them I was beginning to suspect the two ran together. "Wren Aldous. Read the line again. *Accompanied by the knight who forgets nothing.* Not captured by. Not pursued by. Accompanied. Your Authority wrote your orders before you were born, and they didn't write arrest."

"They wrote nothing," I said. "Dead men wrote poetry a century ago. It means nothing. It has never meant anything. Things mean what we file them as."

"Then file me," they said, and opened their arms wider, an invitation, a surrender, a taunt; I genuinely could not tell you which, and I remember everything. "File me as arrested. Drag me to Greyford garrison. Write your report. And then watch what happens, Knight, because within a day your superiors will receive a quiet word from the Guild, and within two days my cell will be empty and my file will be blank and you will be standing in an office being told, gently, by people who are very sorry, that you have been confused, that there was never any thief, that perhaps your famous memory has finally begun to fail you — and you will remember every second of being disbelieved, forever, because that is what your memory does. It remembers everything. Including this."

My blade was shaking. I looked down at it with something like betrayal.

Because here is the thing I have never written in any log: I did not draw it to catch a thief. Or — I did, that was the surface of it, the filed version. Underneath, the true entry, the one I keep in the stone part: I drew it because the Authority took my brother and I thought if I became perfect at its procedures I would eventually find him in them, and in six days of chasing Theo Eriksen I had felt more *real* than in eleven years of service, because pursuit at least pointed somewhere. And here stood the person my entire life had been aimed at, apparently, according to dead men's poetry, telling me the pointing had been a lie, that the Authority would erase me as easily as it erased countries, that my flawless memory was not a shield but a liability already scheduled for disposal.

"Treason," I said. My voice sounded far away. "What you're asking me to do right now, by standing there and not being arrested — it's treason. Do you understand that? If I let you walk up this road, I'm not making a mistake I can correct later. I'm choosing. Everything after this choice is built on it."

"Yes," Theo said. No flinch. No persuasion in their voice, even — just the terrible fairness of a clerk presenting accounts. "I know. I won't pretend otherwise, and I won't thank you, because thanks would make it smaller. I'll just tell you the last thing I have, and then it's yours to do with as you like." They swallowed. "Three days ago I would have let you arrest me. I'd have gone quietly, because going quietly was the only thing I was ever trained for. But I met a pickpocket in the Ashmarket who can taste lies, and she tasted the truth on me when I said I wanted to find the door out of Zephyrine, and she said — she's nine, Knight, she's nine years old — she said, *then why are you letting the sad lady with the sword stop you?*"

Rain. River. My own breath, loud as surf.

"So," Theo said. "Sad lady with the sword. What's it to be?"

And here is what I did, and I have re-remembered it every night since, turning it over like a coin looking for the flaw in the minting: I looked at the prophecy one more time — *the knight who forgets nothing*, beside, *beside* — and I thought of Tomas's chipped tooth and the drawer that never opens, and I understood, with the total recall that is my curse and apparently my commission, that I had already decided. Somewhere between *beside* and *sad lady*, between the dog and the rain, the decision had been made by some part of me that files things more honestly than I do.

I sheathed my blade. I stepped aside. I left the road clear.

"Go," I said.

Theo Eriksen looked at me for a long moment, and their eyes were wet, which undid me more thoroughly than any argument had. Then they said, "Greyford's wrong for you. When they ask — and they will ask — say you lost me at the weir, two nights ago. It's plausible. I was seen there. Let me give you a lie worth remembering."

"I remember everything," I said. "Lies included. Forever."

"I know," they said softly. "That's why I'm giving you a good one." And they went up the road into the rain, hood up, careful hands tucked away, becoming nobody with every step, until the cutting swallowed them and I stood alone with the sound of the river and the certain knowledge that I had just committed treason, gladly, at knifepoint, against myself.

* * *

I wrote the report that evening by lamplight in the Greyford garrison, and it was the finest work of fiction the Authority will never know it commissioned: the sighting at the weir two nights prior, the trail gone cold, the subject presumed moving south toward the coast. I described a taller thief, heavier, older. I invented a scar. I lied in triplicate and filed it, and watched the clerk stamp it, and felt the stamp go through my own chest.

Then I sat in my room and did the thing I never do. I took out the private notebook — the stone archive, the one with Tomas's face described in words so he cannot be taken twice — and I began a new entry.

*Day six. Subject encountered. Subject showed me a document. I allowed escape. Reasons: pending.*

*Pending.* Even alone, by lamplight, I couldn't write the word *prophecy*. Superstition, the Authority calls it. But my memory holds the Archivist's shaking hands, and the grave marked *A Faithful Servant*, and the fact — verifiable, filed, stamped — that the Authority burns atlases by the crate.

If Zephyrine had been real, then my brother had died asking a real question. And if my brother had died asking a real question, then everything I had built my life inside was not a fortress but a lid.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, remembering everything, which is what I do instead of sleeping.

And that was when I heard the boots in the corridor — two sets, unhurried, stopping at my door — and the voice of Captain Ivell, who signs the disappearance orders, saying pleasantly through the wood: "Knight Aldous. Awake? Good. We need to talk about your report. There's a discrepancy."
