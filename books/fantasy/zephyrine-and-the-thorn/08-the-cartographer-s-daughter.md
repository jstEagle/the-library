## 8. The Cartographer's Daughter

The last mapmaker in Zephyrine—or the last man who had ever drawn one, which by now amounted to the same thing—lived, according to Odile, at the top of a hill that wasn't on any road, behind a door that didn't open, in a village that had been renamed so many times it had forgotten its own original name and settled, with the weary pragmatism of the very old, on *Here*.

"You'll know the house," she'd said, pushing the barge off from the reed shallows with her boot. "It's the one that looks like it's arguing with the landscape."

I should have known better than to trust a smuggler's directions. But I trusted them anyway, because by then I had learned that the only people who told you the truth were the ones who charged for it.

Pip came with me. This was not my idea. Pip had appointed themselves to my company sometime around the second tollhouse and had resisted all attempts at dismissal with the immovable logic of a child who has decided something. Wren was supposed to come too. Wren had gone back toward the city to retrieve her horse and, she said, "some things I left buried," which was the most words she'd ever volunteered about herself, and I had been too much of a coward to ask what they were.

So it was Pip and me, walking up a hill through drizzle fine as sifted flour, toward a house that looked exactly as advertised: like an argument. It leaned into the wind at an angle no architect would sanction, its roof slates mismatched in three shades of grey, its chimney smoking with the stubbornness of a thing that refuses to admit defeat. The garden was a riot of weeds and rosemary. A sign hung over the gate, hand-painted, weathered nearly blank.

Pip squinted at it. "It says *No cartographers*."

"That seems backwards."

"It's crossed out." Pip tilted their head. "Somebody crossed out *cartographers* and wrote underneath, real small: *or anyone else sent by fate.*"

I stood there in the rain and felt the words go through me like a splinter finding its way to bone. Someone had known we were coming. Not us specifically—worse. Us generally. The category of us. People sent by prophecy, arriving at doors with our hands out and our destinies showing, wanting things.

"Maybe we should knock anyway," Pip said.

"Maybe we should be honest about what happens if we do."

"What happens?"

"I don't know," I said. "That's the problem. My whole life, when I knocked on doors, nobody remembered I'd been there. It's possible this is the first door in twenty years that's going to remember."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then they reached up and knocked for me, three sharp raps, before I could stop them, because that was Pip all the way down: a child who had learned that hesitation was just theft committed against yourself.

Footsteps inside. Slow ones. The deliberate kind, meant to be heard.

The door opened on a chain, six inches of gap, and filled with a woman about my age, maybe a little older, with ink-stained fingers and her father's stubborn jaw and eyes that looked at me the way you look at weather coming in off the sea—already calculating how much of your day it was going to ruin.

"No," she said.

"We haven't asked for anything yet."

"You're going to. You've got the look." She looked me up and down through the gap. "Guild-trained. See it in how you stand, like you're apologizing for taking up space. And you're—" her eyes narrowed— "*unmarked*. Nobody remembers you, do they? Innkeepers forget you. Friends lose your name. You could stand in a market square and be weather." Her voice went flat and hard. "You're one of them. One of the marked. The book said there'd be more."

"The book," I repeated carefully.

"My father read it until it fell apart. Read it the way other men pray. And then he spent forty years drawing maps for people like you, and every single one of them took a piece of him and gave nothing back, and now he's dead, and I'm still here, and the answer is still no." She began to close the door.

"Pip," I said quietly, "don't."

Pip knocked again.

The woman made a sound somewhere between a laugh and a scream. She unchained the door fully—not in surrender but in the manner of someone deciding to fight on open ground—and stood there blocking the threshold with her whole body, arms crossed, ink to the wrists.

"Isolde," she said, as though giving me a weapon she expected me to use against her. "Isolde Marchetti. My father was Aldous Marchetti, and he was the finest mapmaker this erased country ever produced, and if you say the word *prophecy* on my doorstep I will set the dog on you."

"Do you have a dog?"

"No. But I'll get one. I'll walk to the next village and acquire a dog specifically for this purpose."

Despite everything—the wet, the fear, the ledger-page weight of Sable's name still folded against my ribs—I felt my mouth twitch. "Theo," I said. "And this is Pip. We don't want a map."

That stopped her. It was, I realized later, possibly the only sentence in the world that could have. Everyone wanted a map. Of course they did. That was the whole economy of her grief: strangers arrived wanting her father's lines and borders, his coastlines, his door.

"Everyone wants a map," she said, echoing my thought with the sour precision of someone who has heard the same lie too many times to be surprised by it.

"We want the mapmaker. Or what he knew. There's a difference, and I think you know there is, because a map can be taken and knowledge has to be given." I held up my hands, empty, palms out—an old guild gesture, *nothing stolen here*, though my whole life was stolen goods by then. "And I'm not asking you to give anything today. I'm asking you to tell me why you hate us. Because I think you have forty years of reasons and I'd rather hear them than another riddle."

*

She let us in. Not kindly. She let us in the way you let in a draught you can't be bothered to block.

The inside of the house was the outside of her father's head. Maps everywhere—finished ones, ruined ones, ones burned at the edges, ones so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. And in the center of the main room, under a sheet, something large and flat on a table, guarded the way you guard a body.

Isolde made tea without offering any, then relented and made more tea, muttering, and pushed a cup at each of us like she was arming us. Pip wrapped both hands around theirs and watched her with those eyes that tasted lies, and after a while Pip said, conversationally:

"She's sad. Not angry. Angry's just the coat she puts over it."

"Pip," I said.

"What? It's true. She's sad like the sea is wet."

Isolde stared at the child for a long moment. "What are you?"

"A pickpocket."

"And that?"

"Also a pickpocket. Just of different pockets."

Something in Isolde cracked—not visibly, but I heard it, the way you hear ice shift on a river. She sat down across from us and put her ink-stained hands flat on the table, and she told us.

She told us about her father reading the prophecy book aloud by lamplight, his voice shaking, pointing at the date a century old that named this decade, this year, and the thief who is no one, and saying, *Issa, it's happening, it's finally happening, we were right to keep the maps.* She told us about the strangers who came after—three of them over the years, marked ones, guided by fragments and rumors, each certain they were the one. How her father drew for them, taught them, fed them, loved them a little, the way you love hope. How the first two died badly. How the third took the finished map of the northern border and sold it to the Guild for enough money to disappear forever, and how her father had sat in that chair—she pointed at it, a worn thing by the cold hearth—for eleven days without speaking, and then got up and started the map again, from memory, because that was the kind of man he was.

"He used to say being chosen was like being struck by lightning," she said. "Everyone thinks it must be wonderful to be important to the sky. Nobody asks the tree." Her hands curled slowly into fists. "He died finishing it. Third version. He was inking the eastern passes when his heart quit, mid-line, and the last stroke just—stops. Trails off into nothing." Her voice did something complicated. "They buried him with his good pens. I kept the map. And I swore I'd burn it before I gave it to another marked stranger who'd get themselves killed and take my father's life's work with them into some ditch."

The room was quiet except for the rain and Pip slurping tea with the complete indifference of childhood.

"So no," Isolde finished. "You can't have it. Not because I don't believe in your door. Because I do. That's the worst part. I believe every word of that stupid book, and belief has cost this family everything, and I am *tired*."

I understood her. That was the terrible thing. I sat there understanding her completely, because I had spent my whole life believing in the opposite religion—invisibility, unimportance, the holy state of being no one—and it had cost me everything too, just more quietly. We were both survivors of the same war, fought from opposite trenches: she had been chosen too much, and I had chosen myself too little, and neither of us had ever once been asked what we wanted.

"Can I see where the line stops?" I asked.

Her jaw tightened. For a moment I thought she'd throw us out into the rain. Then she got up, crossed the room, and pulled the sheet off the table.

The map was beautiful. That's the only word. Every line confident, every label in a hand so precise it looked printed, the true borders of Zephyrine rising out of the vellum like a country remembering itself. Rivers I'd never heard of with names that made my chest hurt for no reason I could name. And there, in the east, the final stroke—a mountain pass, half-inked, the line thinning, faltering, stopping.

Mid-word, almost. Like a sentence interrupted.

Pip leaned close. "It's warm," they whispered. "Why is it warm?"

I touched the vellum near the broken line and felt it: faint heat, like a stone that had held sun. Isolde frowned. "It does that. Since he died. I told myself it was the lamp oil. I'm very good at telling myself things."

I looked at the place where the line stopped, and the thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do: *the map isn't unfinished. It's waiting.* A door needed a frame. A frame needed every side drawn. And a man who believed hard enough might have poured the last of himself into the ink rather than leave the work undone.

"Your father didn't fail to finish it," I said slowly. "I think he did finish it. Just not on the paper."

Isolde looked at me like I'd slapped her. "Say that again."

"I think the last line is somewhere else. In something he kept close. Something he—" I hesitated, hating myself for what I was about to say, knowing what it would cost her even to consider. "Something the Guild might have taken. They collect memories, Isolde. Especially memories of borders. A mapmaker's memory of his own life's work would be worth a vault to them."

The color left her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"They took his hands' knowledge," she whispered. "They took the *drawing* out of him."

"If they did—if there's a memory in a Guild vault with your father's last line in it—then the map can be finished. Properly. All of it." I met her eyes. "But I'd have to go back into the House of Ledgers to get it. Which means you'd have to trust a marked stranger. Which means—"

"Don't," she said. "Don't ask me yet. Let me hate you a little longer. It's the only inheritance I have left that's entirely mine."

*

We slept in the barn, because Isolde was angry, not cruel. Pip was asleep in ten seconds, curled in the hay like a comma. I lay awake listening to the rain and thinking about trees and lightning, about how the prophecy had never once asked any of us whether we consented to be instruments.

Near dawn, the barn door creaked. Isolde stood there with a lantern, and she held out something flat wrapped in oilcloth.

"Not the map," she said. "Don't flatter yourself." She put it in my hands anyway. Inside was a single sheet: her father's notes on the eastern passes, the section where his ink had failed. Coordinates, sketches, marginalia in a cramped, furious hand. "If you're going to break into that vault, you'll need to know what you're looking for. He described the final line to me a hundred times. I can draw it from memory myself—but a drawing isn't proof, is it? The Guild will want to see the real thing. The *memory*." Her chin came up. "Bring it back, and I'll finish the map with you. Break your promise, and I'll burn every scrap of his work and salt the ashes."

"That's fair," I said.

"It isn't. It's grief. But it will do." She turned to go, then stopped, her back to me. "Theo. The book said the thief who is no one. Do you know what my father wrote in the margin next to that line? The only annotation he ever dared make in it?"

"No."

"*Chosen is not the same as wanted. Pray the thief learns the difference before the door does.*"

She left me alone with the lantern and the rain and the words, and I lay there in the hay beside a sleeping child who picked pockets of truth, holding a dead man's coordinates, and understood that I had just been given the truest map of all: the one that showed exactly how far I was from anywhere anyone had ever wanted me to be.

Somewhere south, a knight who forgot nothing was riding toward me with things she'd left buried. Somewhere north, a guildmaster who regretted nothing was reading my empty ledger page. And here, in a barn that smelled of rain and hay, I made a vow I didn't yet have the courage to say aloud:

*I'm going to steal your father back, Isolde. And then I'm going to learn the difference.*

The lantern guttered. The rain went on. And far away, in a vault beneath the House of Ledgers, something with my father-figure's handwriting on it waited in the dark—along with, I would soon learn, a trap that already knew my name.
