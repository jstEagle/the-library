## 9. Sable's Offer

The rain stopped at dawn, which in my experience is never a good sign. Rain is honest. It announces itself, it soaks you, it moves on. Sunlight after a night like the one we'd had felt like a hand on your shoulder and a voice saying *don't turn around.*

I turned around anyway. Habit.

Isolde was asleep sitting up, her father's half-finished map spread across her knees like a sleeping cat she didn't trust. Pip was curled in the hay loft with both boots on, because a pickpocket who sleeps barefoot deserves what happens to them. And I was standing in the barn door of a farm that did not officially exist anymore, watching the road for a woman I had spent eleven years pretending not to fear.

Sable found us before noon. Of course she did. She was Sable.

She didn't arrive with guild enforcers or a carriage or any of the theater she could have afforded. She arrived the way weather arrives—suddenly, from the wrong direction, already inside. One moment I was checking the road; the next there was a gray-cloaked figure standing at the edge of the yard, hands folded, watching me with the patient expression of someone who has already read the last page of your ledger.

"Hello, Theo," she said.

I want it noted, for the record, that I did not flinch. Much.

"You're supposed to be in Vellum," I said. "Three hundred miles north."

"I'm supposed to be a great many things." She stepped past me into the barn like she owned it, which, knowing her, she might have bought outright sometime during the night without anyone noticing the transaction. "You've been busy."

"Sightseeing. Ruined cartographers, river smugglers, the usual tourist route through a country that isn't on the map."

"Mm." She looked at Isolde—at the map—and something moved behind her eyes that I'd only seen once before, years ago, when an apprentice asked what was in the deepest vault. Not anger. Not even regret. *Recognition.* "And how is the cartographer's daughter?"

"That's none of your business."

"Everything in Zephyrine is my business. That's what being erased does. It makes you very light. You drift into everything." She smiled, and it was the smile I knew best: warm as a banker's handshake. "Sit down, child. I came to offer you absolution."

* *

Here is what nobody tells you about the person who raised you: they know exactly where you keep your soft parts.

Sable found me when I was nine, a nameless gutter-thing in the Ashmarket with quick fingers and no face worth remembering. She gave me rules the way other parents give children names. *Never be remembered. Never be named. Be the pen, never the page.* And I had followed those rules so faithfully, for so long, that by the time I understood they were also a cage, the cage had grown into my bones and called itself safety.

Now she sat across from me on an upturned feed barrel, in a barn full of witnesses who were conveniently still asleep, and laid out absolution like cards.

"The pages," she said. "All of them. The prophecy leaf, your ledger entry, the copies I know you're clever enough to have made. You return them. You come home."

"And then?"

"Then we forget." She said it gently, the way you tell someone their dog has died. "There is a memory in our vaults—clean, painless, expertly cut. A week of your life. The library, the book, this whole tiresome adventure. We remove it, we restore your ledger to its proper blankness, and by tomorrow evening you are Theo again. Just Theo. The thief who is no one."

"And the prophecy?"

"Burned. As it should always have been. As I ordered it burned before you ever saw it."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. "You ordered it burned and then sent enforcers three hundred miles after the person who read it. That's a strange way to treat ash."

Her composure flickered—one degree, no more—but I'd spent eleven years learning to read that face. I caught it. The flicker said: *he's right and I hate that he noticed.*

"The Authority wants the prophecy buried," she said. "The Authority pays very well for burials. But the Authority, Theo—" and here she leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something almost tender, which from Sable was more frightening than shouting, "—the Authority does not care about you. I do. Come home. Give back the pages. Forget. This is me, spending forty years of goodwill to buy you your life back. No one else would do it. No one else *could.*"

And God help me, some traitorous part of me wanted to say yes.

That's the thing about survival strategies: they work. That's why they're strategies. For eleven years, invisibility had kept me fed, housed, alive, while braver people than me disappeared into the Authority's quiet rooms. Every rule Sable ever taught me had saved my life at least once. Turning it down wasn't courage. It felt, from the inside, like stepping off a cliff because the ground offended you.

But I thought about Pip, asleep in the loft, tasting lies in their sleep. About Isolde, guarding a dead man's map with her whole body. About Wren Aldous, whose face I'd seen at the tollhouse when she chose treason over duty and then rode away before either of us had to name it. None of them were invisible. All of them were, at this moment, more real than I had ever managed to be.

And I thought about the empty page in my own ledger. The one with my description in century-old ink. *The thief who is no one.*

"No," I said.

Sable went very still. In the guild, stillness like hers preceded either mercy or amputation, and you rarely learned which until afterward.

"Say that again."

"No." It came out steadier the second time, the way stolen things get easier to carry the longer you hold them. "I'm not giving back the pages. I'm not forgetting. And I'm not coming home."

"To what?" The tenderness was gone now, replaced by something older and wearier. "A barn? A child thief and a grieving girl and half a map? Theo. You are a *clerk*. You keep books. That is all you have ever been permitted to be, and it is more than you would have been without me."

"You're right." I stood up. My legs were shaking. Let the record show. "You're completely right. I'm a clerk. I keep books. So let me tell you what the books say, since apparently nobody in the House of Ledgers reads them anymore.

"Forty years ago there was another name in the prophecy. Merrin Vale. You retired her—that's the word you used to me, *retired*, like she'd gotten old, like she'd moved to the coast. I pulled the actual ledgers, Sable. There is no coast. There's a vault, and a memory with her name on it, and a body I'd bet my remaining soul is under the flagstones of the counting room. That's what the guild does to loose ends. That's what 'home' means."

Something crossed her face then—not guilt. I don't think Sable has guilt. Something more like an old ache being pressed, the way you press a bruise to check if it still hurts.

"You read the deep ledgers," she said quietly. "I wondered."

"Rule one: never be remembered. Rule two, the one you never said out loud but taught me anyway: *always remember everything about the people doing the remembering.*" I took a breath. "So here's my counteroffer. Leave. Take whatever Sable-shaped thing you actually came here for and go. Because I've decided something, and it took me twenty-six years and one blank page to decide it:

"I'd rather be a loose end that belongs to itself than a safe end that belongs to you."

* *

For a long moment, neither of us moved. The barn breathed around us—hay settling, horses shifting, Pip snoring upstairs with the shameless abandon of the truly young.

Then Sable did something I had never seen her do in eleven years.

She sighed. Fully. Like a woman setting down luggage.

"There was a door once," she said, to no one in particular. "Before the erasure. Before the guild. I was younger than you. I stood where you're standing—in every way that matters—and I chose the ledger over the threshold, because the ledger was warm and the door was dark, and I told myself I could always open it later." She rose, brushing hay from her cloak with two precise strokes. "Later is a country with no borders, child. You can wander it your whole life and never arrive anywhere."

"What are you telling me?"

"Nothing. An old woman muttering." She moved toward the door, then paused beside me, close enough that I could smell the vault on her—paper, dust, and something faintly chemical, like the air where memories are stored. "The Authority's knight reached the ferry crossing an hour ago. She'll find you by nightfall. When she does, remember that I could have warned her exactly where you sleep, and didn't."

"Why?" The question cracked out of me before I could dress it up. "Why warn me? Why come all this way to offer me a way out and then just—leave?"

She looked at me for a long time. And for one unguarded instant, the guildmaster was gone, and what was left was just a tired woman in her sixties looking at a young person making a mistake she recognized down to its fingerprints.

"Because forty years ago," she said, "someone offered me the same choice, and everyone who loved me told me to take the safe road. I have often wondered what would have happened if just one person had wished me luck instead." She settled her hood. "Good luck, Theo Eriksen."

My blood went cold. "That's not my name."

"It isn't, is it." And there was the banker's smile again, but softer now, worn at the edges like a coin passed through too many hands. "Funny thing about erased countries. They leave traces. Families scatter. Records burn, but grandmothers gossip. You think I found you in the Ashmarket by accident? I have known what you were since the day I found you. The only person who didn't know was you."

She stepped out into the sunlight.

"Sable—wait—" I followed her two steps into the yard, heart slamming. "If you knew—if you've always known—then why raise me as nothing? Why make me *no one*?"

She paused at the gate, a gray shape against green fields, and answered without turning around.

"Because no one can't be hurt," she said. "It was the kindest lie I knew how to give you. I only ever lied to the people I—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. Became the guildmaster again between one word and the next. "Return the pages within the month, or the next person I send won't offer absolution. Goodbye, Theo."

And she was gone. Not dramatically. No smoke, no vanish-trick. She just walked down the lane at an old woman's pace, and the country—which has practice at this sort of thing—quietly forgot her.

* *

I stood in the yard until the shaking started, then sat down hard in the dirt and let it happen. Eleven years of fear, arriving all at once, the way water comes through a ceiling: first a drip, then the whole sky.

Pip dropped out of the hayloft without using the ladder, because of course they hadn't been asleep. "Was that her? The guildmaster?"

"Yes."

"Did she try to kill you?"

"She offered to let me stop existing comfortably instead."

"Huh." Pip considered this, chewing a piece of straw. "Weird family."

"You have no idea."

Isolde appeared in the barn doorway, map hugged to her chest, eyes red-rimmed from sleep and everything else. "Theo. What did she mean—the knight? Wren's here?"

"By nightfall." I got up. My legs held. Progress. "Which means we have maybe eight hours to decide whether she's coming to arrest us or to defect, and absolutely no way to tell which."

"So we run?"

I looked south down the long road, at the heat shimmer rising off it like the ghost of a border. Somewhere along it, a woman who forgot nothing was riding toward me with things she'd left buried. I could run. Running was the strategy. Running was eleven years of rules distilled into one instinct.

But I'd refused Sable an hour ago, and it turned out refusing was like a lockpick: once you'd used it successfully, you wanted to use it everywhere.

"No," I said. "We stay. We dig in. And when she gets here, somebody pour the tea, because I have a feeling this conversation is going to take a while—and Pip?"

"Yeah?"

"If she reaches for her sword, you taste the air and tell me if she means it."

Pip grinned, all teeth. "What if she means it?"

"Then," I said, already walking toward the barn to wake the others and plan for a guest I was dreading and—God help me—hoping for in equal measure, "we finally learn whether the prophecy knows what it's talking about."

The sun climbed. The road stayed empty. And far to the south, closing at a canter, came the only person alive who could never, ever forget me—carrying, though none of us knew it yet, a story about her brother that would change everything.
