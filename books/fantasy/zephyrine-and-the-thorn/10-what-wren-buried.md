## 10. What Wren Buried

The first thing you should know about a perfect memory is that it is not a gift. It is an attic with no door, and someone keeps carrying up boxes.

I remember everything. I remember the smell of the Provisional Authority's induction hall—wax and wet wool and the particular sourness of forty nervous recruits pretending they weren't. I remember my mother's voice on the last morning I ever heard it, complaining about the price of onions, which is the sort of sentence you don't know is sacred until it's the last one left in the jar. I remember every face of every person I have ever arrested, filed away in a mind that does not forgive, does not blur, does not do me the courtesy of forgetting.

Most people's memories are kind to them. Mine reports for duty.

* * *

My brother's name was Cassian Aldous, and he asked questions the way other men drank—too fast, at the wrong hours, in front of people who would remember.

We grew up in the Lowbridge district of Corvenne, which the maps call Corvenne and nothing else, because there is nothing else to call it. That was the whole trick, see. The country that used to be here—the one with the name that starts with Z and unspools like thread off a spool—I didn't learn that name from any book. I learned it from Cass, who learned it from a drunk cartographer who wept into his beer about coastlines he'd been ordered to straighten.

Cass was four years older than me and built like a question mark, all lean and curiosity, with ink permanently stained into the side of his right hand from copying things he wasn't supposed to read. He called his hand "the witness." He'd hold it up like an oath. *This hand saw what your eyes missed, Wrenny.*

I was eleven when he started asking. Sixteen when they took him. And I remember all five years of it with the terrible fidelity of a mind that has never once dropped anything on the floor.

* * *

It began with a map. Of course it began with a map; everything in this story eventually comes back to paper.

Cass brought it home folded eight times, soft as cloth from handling, and spread it on our kitchen table while Mother was out. It showed the northern provinces under their old names. Under our city, in faded violet ink, ran a border—a border that shouldn't exist, drawn around a country that had never officially done anything except peacefully integrate, according to every textbook in every schoolroom in the Protectorate.

"Look," he said, and his finger traced the violet line, and I remember—I always remember—the exact pressure of his fingertip, the way the paper dimpled. "They didn't redraw the borders, Wren. They redrew the *past*. You can conquer a place. But this—" he tapped the map, tapped the word beneath it, that word starting with Z, "—this is something else. This is making it so it never was."

"Put it away," I said. I was thirteen and already careful in the way that quiet children are careful, the ones who watch the doors.

"It's just paper."

Nothing is just paper. Paper is the most dangerous material in the world. Stone crumbles, steel rusts, but paper holds a sentence together for centuries, and sentences are how you kill a country—or resurrect one.

He didn't put it away.

* * *

Here is what a perfect memory does with grief: it files it. It puts it in the cabinet, alphabetized, cross-referenced, and then every night when you try to sleep it opens the drawer and reads the file aloud.

File: Cassian Aldous. Age at disappearance: twenty-one. Occupation: junior archivist, Ministry of Records. Last seen: the fourth of Frostmonth, in the doorway of our house, wearing the grey coat with the missing button, because I noticed the button, I always notice, my eyes are thieves too, they take everything—

He'd been questioned twice before. Once informally, by a man from the Bureau of Continuity—the branch of the Provisional Authority whose name still makes my teeth ache, because *continuity*, they actually called it that, the office devoted to the fact that nothing had continued. Cass came home laughing after the first questioning. "They asked if I believed in Zephyrine, Wrenny. Like it's a religion. Like believing in it is heresy." He laughed. His hand shook while he poured the tea, and the cups chattered, and I remember the sound. Two small collisions. Then a third, because he poured again.

The second time, he came home quiet.

The third time, there was no coming home. There was a knock at a civilized hour, two men in civilian grey who were not civilians, and a form for Mother to sign—they always make you sign something; the paperwork of erasure is very thorough—and then the street outside our window was empty in a way I had never known a street could be empty, as though the world itself had been redacted.

Mother signed. She sat down afterward and did not cry, and I remember that too, God help me, I remember the dry shine of her eyes and the way she smoothed the signature flat with two fingers like she was smoothing a wrinkle out of time itself.

She died eighteen months later. Quietly, officially, of a weakness of the heart. I have often wondered whether the Bureau of Continuity classifies broken hearts as natural causes. I expect they do. It's tidier.

* * *

You will want to know why I joined them. Everyone wants to know, once they find out what I was before I found out what I am. The girl whose brother was disappeared, signing herself over to the disappearers?

Because they were the only ones who knew where the files were.

That's the ugly truth, the truth I keep at the bottom of the cabinet where the nightly reading doesn't usually reach. I did not join the Authority to serve it. I joined it the way you join a fortress you intend to rob from the inside. Somewhere in the Ministry of Records, in the sealed annexes, in the vaults where the Vellum Guild sells what the state prefers to lose, there is a file with Cassian's name on it. A record of what was done to him, where he went, whether the word *still* applies to him in any tense.

A perfect memory needs a purpose or it turns on its keeper. I gave mine a warrant.

And then—this is the part I understand least, even now—it worked. I rose. I became good, genuinely good, at hunting people who stole things. Because I remembered every face. Because no glamour of forgetfulness worked on me, a fact the Authority discovered with delight and the guild discovered with horror: Theo Eriksen could walk through a room and leave it clean of themselves in every mind present, but mine kept the shape of them like wax keeps a seal. I was the only hunter in Corvenne who couldn't be robbed of my quarry. They gave me the prophecy-thief case the way you'd give a bloodhound the scent it was bred for.

They thought they were giving me a criminal. They did not know they were giving me the answer to a question I'd carried since I was sixteen, standing in a suddenly empty street.

* * *

But I'm telling this out of order, and a perfect memory hates disorder, so let me go back to the cabinet, to the file I keep third from the bottom.

After Cass was taken, I went looking. Of course I did. I was sixteen and feral with it. I wrote to ministries. I petitioned. I stood in queues with my brother's name on cards in my fist, and every queue led to another queue, and every clerk wore the same apologetic blankness: *no record, no such person, perhaps you're thinking of someone else.* As though Cass had been lifted out of the world the way a stain is lifted from cloth—as though he'd never soaked in at all.

Except.

Except that I remembered him. I was the record. There were two witnesses to Cassian Aldous alive in the world: me, and whatever clerk had processed his file into the dark. And I understood, standing in those queues, growing slowly colder, exactly what the Authority was doing, and why it was worse than killing.

Killing leaves a body. Killing leaves a grave, and graves can be visited. What they did was finer work than that. They unwrote. They reached backward into the past and pulled the thread until the whole garment of a man came apart, and everyone who loved him was left holding loose air, doubting their own hands.

I watched it happen to Mother in slow motion. Grief needs an object. She had none. You cannot mourn a man who officially never existed; mourning requires a country that agrees with you, and hers did not. So she grieved alone, silently, in a language the state had abolished, and it killed her as surely as any knife, only slower and deniably.

I decided two things at her bedside, in the hour after the sheet went over her face. First: I would never let them do that to anyone I loved again, which meant loving no one, which was easy, because who could compete with a cabinet full of ghosts? Second: I would find Cass's file if it was the last act of my life, and I would read it, and whatever was written there—even if it ended at a furnace, even if it ended nowhere—I would carry it. Someone had to be the record. If the state erased and the guild traded and the people forgot, then memory itself became contraband, and I would be the smuggler.

I was seventeen years old. I had made myself into a vault. I did not yet know that vaults are lonely places, or that the thing you lock away to protect it is also locked away from you.

* * *

There is one more thing in the cabinet. Bottom drawer. I open it maybe twice a year, on the anniversary, deliberately, the way you press a bruise to prove you're still the one in charge of it.

The night before Cass disappeared, we argued.

Not about the map—we argued about me. I'd told him I wanted to apply to the Authority academy. The pay was steady, the Lowbridge rents weren't, and I was fifteen and pragmatic in a way that made Cass grind his teeth. He said I'd be lending my hands to the machine that ate him if it ever got hungry enough. I said somebody had to eat. We were both right, which is the worst kind of argument, the kind where nobody gets to win.

He left the next morning in the grey coat with the missing button. I was asleep. I didn't hear him go. And the last thing I ever said to my brother was, "Don't wait up," said with my back already turned, already climbing the stairs, my voice carrying exactly none of the love that was in me, abundant, unsaid, unsayable at fifteen, because I had time. I had all the time in the world. Everyone assumes that.

I have replayed that morning ten thousand times. I know the angle of the light. I know the sound the stair made under my foot. I know that if I had turned around—one half-second, one rotation of a wrist—I would have seen his face, and my last sight of my brother would not be the back of his coat disappearing into fog.

A perfect memory gives you infinite replays and no revisions. That is the wound under the gift. That is why I hunt.

* * *

Which brings us, unwillingly, to the present. To the tollhouse by the river, three days ago, where I had the thief at knifepoint and let the knife down.

I've turned it over since. Every hour, some new angle. My superior, Captain Vess, watches me now with the patient attention of a woman deciding whether a horse is lame. My commission feels thinner in my hand, like paper handled too often.

Here is what happened at the tollhouse, in perfect detail, because of course in perfect detail: I had Theo Eriksen against the wall, blade at the throat, and they opened the prophecy book with the tip of their chin like some absurd scholar, and I read my own description. Not my name—my description. *The knight who forgets nothing.* Written a hundred years ago, in ink that has browned honestly with age, describing a woman who would not exist for nine decades.

And below it, a line I have read four hundred times since, in my cell, in my quarters, walking the walls at night:

*She hunts because she remembers; she will stop when she remembers why.*

I told myself it was strategy. Let the bait run and catch the whole conspiracy. Any competent officer would endorse it. I filed the treason under *tactics* and locked the drawer.

But drawers, in my head, do not stay locked. And tonight, doing the reading, I finally admitted what the file says underneath the tactics, in handwriting I don't like to claim:

I looked at Theo Eriksen—at this person who has spent a lifetime teaching the world to forget them, who carries forgetfulness like a weapon, like armor, like mercy—and I thought: *you are the opposite of me.* I keep everything and it is killing me by inches. You keep nothing and I do not know what that is, but I watched your hands shake on the prophecy book, and it isn't peace, whatever it is.

And I thought: they ask about Zephyrine. The thief asks about Zephyrine. Which means the thief might know about the annexes, the vaults, the files. Which means the thief might lead me, if I follow long enough, to a folder with a boy's name on it and a grey coat with a missing button, and proof that my brother existed, exists, existed—

*She will stop when she remembers why.*

I stopped hunting for the Authority a long time ago. I only pretended otherwise, even to myself.

Tonight I took my commission out of its case, and my lighter out of my pocket, and I stood at the window a long while, watching the Bureau of Continuity's lamps burn across the river—the building that unmade my family, lit up warm and gold like a promise.

Then I struck the flame, and the paper caught, and my name as a knight of the Provisional Authority went up in a light no bigger than my two cupped hands.

Somewhere out in the dark, past the Ashmarket, past the river, a thief who is no one is running toward a country that isn't there. And I am going to find them—not to arrest them.

To ask them the only question that has ever mattered: what do you know about the files?

And if they'll have me—a traitor, a huntress, a vault full of ghosts with nowhere left to lock them—then I suppose I'll learn, at last, what remembering is supposed to cost, and whether it can be paid in company instead of blood.

The ash drifted off my fingers and out the window, north, toward the river.

I put on my coat. Not the grey one. The black one.

And I went to find my thief.
