## 11. The Unfinished Map

**ISOLDE**

My father used to say that a map was a promise made in ink. He said it the way other men say *be careful* or *I love you*, which is to say constantly, tiresomely, and with more truth than he knew what to do with.

He also said never trust a prophecy-marked stranger who turns up at your door at dawn smelling of river and desperation. On this point, at least, I intend to honor his memory.

There were three of them on my doorstep when I opened it: the thief who is no one, wearing the particular blankness of someone who has been called destiny too many times this month; a knight of the Provisional Authority with her commission burned and her hands still shaking; and a child who looked at me the way cats look at closed doors.

"You're the cartographer's daughter," the thief said.

"I'm the woman who was sleeping," I said. "We were both accurate."

I should have shut the door. My father would have understood—he spent thirty years drawing a country that official decree insisted did not exist, and he died with half of it still unfinished, and if shutting doors had ever come naturally to him I would have grown up a quieter person in a smaller house. Instead I stood aside and let them in, because my father's map was hanging over the hearth where he'd left it, and the thief looked at it the way you look at a face you've seen only in dreams.

"Zephyrine," Theo said. Not a question. A recognition.

"That's the name," I said, "though we're not supposed to use it out loud. The Authority fines you. Then they fine your family. Then there isn't a family."

The knight—Wren, the child called her, Wren Aldous as though naming her made her safe—went to the map and stood before it for a long time. Her face did something complicated. I have spent my life around maps; I know what it looks like when someone finds their whole country laid out flat and true and knows it is about to be lost again unless strangers act.

"Your father drew this," she said.

"My father drew everything they erased. That's why they took his memory of it." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, which is what it does instead of crying. "The Vellum Guild came eleven years ago. They bought it from him politely—a memory is just goods to them, they'll tell you, they're traders, they even pay fairly—and then the Authority's men followed them out, and he came back from their questioning without the map in his head. He could hold the pen. He remembered that he had been a man who drew Zephyrine. He simply couldn't remember Zephyrine. He sat at that desk for six years trying to finish it from old notes, and every line he drew came out wrong, and one morning I found him with his head down on the vellum like a boy asleep after lessons."

Nobody spoke. Even the child, who I would later learn had never once in her life been quiet voluntarily, kept her mouth shut.

"He died believing he'd failed," I said. "So no. I don't care about prophecies. Prophecies are how strangers justify asking widows and orphans for things. Get out of my house."

But the thief didn't move toward the door. Theo stepped closer to the hearth instead, close enough that I thought they might touch the map, and said, quietly, "It ends at the border ruins. Everything past the eastern edge is blank."

"The door is there," Wren said. "In the blank part."

"I know what's in the blank part." I surprised myself by saying it. I hadn't meant to say anything. "You think I haven't read the prophecy? You think I didn't read it forty times while my father forgot? *Where the country ends, the country begins.* Yes. I know. And you want me to finish it—the last true map of Zephyrine, the one thing of value I have left in the world—so that three strangers can walk through a door and leave me here with an empty wall."

"Yes," Theo said. Simply, horribly honest. "That's what we want."

And then the thief did something no stranger had ever done in my hearing. They took something small from inside their coat and held it out on an open palm: a memory, sealed in guild-vellum, the kind I'd seen once in the hands of the men who came for my father. The wax bore no sigil. It had been peeled open once and resealed badly.

"It's the sea," Theo said. "Stolen. From someone who loved it. I've been carrying it since Ashmarket. I don't know whose it was anymore—I gave away the provenance to buy passage." They turned it over, and the light moved inside it like water. "I keep thinking about your father. About all the things in those vaults that belonged to people who are dead now, or worse. If we get into the House of Ledgers, we shouldn't take the map memory out and hand it back and call it even. We should open every vault in the building."

"That's insane," Wren said.

"Yes," said Theo. "Do you have a better idea?"

The knight opened her mouth, considered, and closed it again. The child grinned. And I looked at the little sealed vial of stolen sea, and at the map above my fire, and at my father's last unfinished line trailing off the edge of the world like a sentence interrupted mid-word, and I heard myself say:

"If you're going to rob the Guild, you'll do it properly or not at all. My father taught me vault-work along with lettering. Sit down. All of you. This will take all night."

I still maintain I agreed for my father's sake. I have been informed, repeatedly, that my face at the time said otherwise.

* * *

**THEO**

Here is what nobody tells you about finding a family: it is exactly as terrifying as being hunted, and considerably harder to run from.

We sat around Isolde's table until the candles drowned in their own wax, and I watched four strangers become, degree by degree, less strange, and I kept waiting for the part where I got forgotten and had to start over. It kept not happening. It was very disorienting.

Wren drew the House of Ledgers from memory—that flawless, cursed, miraculous memory of hers—every corridor, every guard rotation, every door. She'd walked its halls for years hunting contraband memories; she'd simply been on the wrong side of the ledger paper.

"There's a delivery entrance on the canal side," she said, charcoal whispering across the paper. "Guild barges bring acquisitions in at the fourth bell after midnight. Two porters, one tally-keeper. The porters are hired muscle, but the tally-keepers are guild-trained forgetters—they can wipe a witness between heartbeats."

"I've been a porter," Pip said, from atop the table where she had installed herself despite three separate requests. "Porters is easy. You just look stupid and lift with your legs."

"You are a child," Wren said.

"I'm a *small adult*. There's a difference and it's mostly wages." Pip plucked an apple from the bowl Isolde had set out—people kept feeding her; I'd stopped understanding it myself—and talked with her mouth full. "Tally-keeper's the problem. Tally-keepers count everything, and I taste lies, and a tally-keeper's whole life is a lie, so standing near one is like eating a spoonful of burnt hair. But." She raised one finger, grandly. "But. If somebody stands next to me and tells the truth the whole time, real loud inside their own head, it drowns it out. Like salt on bad meat."

"What kind of truth?" Isolde asked.

Pip shrugged. "Any kind. Names work good. Things you want. Things you're scared of losing." She glanced at me then, quick and sideways, and I understood that she had already guessed what I was scared of losing, and had decided, in the merciless generous way of children, not to charge me for it.

Isolde spread her father's notes across the table—the working sketches, the marginalia, thirty years of a life measured in contour lines. "The vaults are organized by acquisition date. My father's memory is eleven years old, so it'll be deep. Third sublevel, most likely, among the dead clients' holdings." Her pen stopped. "Which means the Guild considers him dead."

"They consider everyone dead eventually," I said. "It simplifies the accounts."

"Then let's complicate them," she said, and there was something in her voice then that I recognized, because I'd heard it in my own once: the sound of grief deciding to be useful.

We worked out the plan the way rivers cut valleys—not straight, but inevitable. Wren and I would enter as porters with a false acquisition, borrowed from Odile's barge stock. Pip would ride the canal gate in a delivery crate, because she was the only one of us who could taste whether the tally-keeper was about to wipe us, and because no plan survives contact with reality unless it contains at least one element that makes Wren say *absolutely not.*

"Absolutely not," Wren said.

"Noted," said Pip. "I'm doing it anyway."

"And I stay here," Isolde said, "with the map. If you fail, the Guild doesn't learn I helped. If you succeed—" she looked at the unfinished lines above her hearth, "—I need to be ready to work."

It was nearly dawn when the plan was drawn. Wren rolled up her charcoal sketch and stood, and paused at the door, and did the thing she does when duty and doubt change places inside her without anyone seeing the exchange.

"Eriksen," she said. "If it goes wrong down there—"

"It won't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that lets me sleep," I said, and she almost smiled, and I felt that smile land somewhere under my ribs and take root, which was, in hindsight, the exact moment I became a person who could lose things.

**WREN**

I have trained my whole life to remember everything, and I want to record, for whoever writes this history if any of us survive to write histories at all, the details I am afraid of sentimentalizing later. So: precisely.

Theo Eriksen fell asleep at Isolde's table at the fifth bell, sitting upright, the way people sleep who learned young that lying down is a luxury. Isolde put a blanket over their shoulders without waking them, and Theo didn't stir, and I understood that this was trust being extended like credit to a customer with no collateral, and I understood that I was watching my— my colleague. My fellow conspirator. The thief I am sworn to arrest, except that I burned the arrest warrant myself and fed it to the same fire as my commission, and some mornings I wake and reach for the warrant the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore.

Pip slept curled in the window seat like the world's smallest, most self-satisfied gargoyle. Isolde worked. She inked nothing—she said finishing even one line before the rest was recovered would be a lie, and lies curdle on maps—but she sharpened pens and arranged tools and prepared herself the way soldiers prepare, which is to say by controlling the controllable.

And I stood watch at the window, because I do not sleep well in houses, and I thought about my brother.

He asked questions about Zephyrine. That was all. He asked them at dinner, in our kitchen, in front of me, aged nineteen, with his whole face open, because he believed the way I used to believe that institutions are made of the sum of the honest people inside them. They took him on a Tuesday. I remember the Tuesday. I remember everything; that is my condition, my gift, my wound. What I could not remember, for years afterward, was his face—not because it faded but because remembering it correctly hurt too much to permit, and I learned to store it behind glass in my mind the way the Guild stores grief in ledgers. Controlled. Catalogued. Safe.

Then a thief showed me a prophecy book, and a knight hesitated at knifepoint, and now I sleep in the houses of conspirators and my brother's face keeps escaping its glass case, arriving uninvited, warm and alive and nineteen forever, and it hurts in the ordinary human way instead of the archival way, and I find—I record this with professional embarrassment—that I prefer it.

"The view's not that interesting," Pip said. She had woken without my noticing, which should be impossible.

"Go back to sleep."

"You're doing the face. Isolde does the face too. It's the face people make when they're loving people on purpose against their better judgment." She yawned enormously. "It's a good face. Don't be embarrassed about it."

"I'm not embarrassed."

"You're embarrassed," she said comfortably, and went back to sleep, and I stood watch a while longer, guarding a room full of sleeping conspirators, feeling the strangest thing I have felt since my brother disappeared, which was: unalone.

* * *

**THEO**

Morning came gray and smelling of rain. Isolde fed us eggs and gave orders like a general, and we loaded the barge supplies onto a handcart, and Odile's smuggler met us at the canal with a forged manifest and a sailor's blessing that was seventy percent profanity.

At the gangplank, Pip stopped. She looked up at Wren, then at me, and for once in her short loud life she didn't grin.

"If it goes wrong," she said, "don't do anything stupid for me. I've been caught before. I got out before. I always get out."

"We're not leaving you anywhere," I said.

"I know. I'm saying don't leave each other. That's the actual rule." She hopped aboard the barge with the lightness of someone who has never once obeyed an actual rule, and waved, and was gone down the canal toward the House of Ledgers.

Isolde stood on the bank beside me. She didn't touch my shoulder. She didn't need to; her father's compass was in my pocket, her notes were in Wren's coat, her whole inheritance was riding with us into the vaults of the people who emptied her father's head.

"Bring it back," she said. "All of it. Not just mine."

"That's the plan."

"Plans break." She looked out at the water. "People hold, if you choose them right. Choose them right, thief."

And I climbed aboard with Wren beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and the barge slid out onto the brown canal water toward the Guild's black warehouse, and I thought: I have been invisible my entire life, and somehow, in the space of two weeks, I have become a person with obligations. People who expect me back. A blank page waiting to be finished.

The House of Ledgers rose ahead of us out of the mist, tall and patient as a tombstone.

Its gates opened at the fourth bell, exactly as Wren remembered.

They were expecting someone.
