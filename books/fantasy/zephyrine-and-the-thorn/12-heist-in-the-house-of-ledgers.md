## 12. Heist in the House of Ledgers

The Vellum Guild keeps its vault beneath a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, which is the kind of joke Sable would make, so I should have known it wasn't only a joke. Wax for the seals. Tallow to burn the evidence. A building that smells of snuffed wicks and old fat, hiding a cellar full of everything anyone ever wanted to forget.

I used to walk past it three times a week without looking at it. That was the trick of me. Theo Eriksen, who is no one, who is a smudge on the corner of your eye, who could stand in the middle of Ashmarket at noon and be as memorable as weather. For eleven years I was invisible on purpose, and I had thought—God help me—that I could switch the trick back on whenever I liked. Walk into the House of Ledgers. Take Isolde's father's memory out of its vellum sleeve. Walk out. Be forgotten by morning.

But you can't be no one *on purpose* anymore. The prophecy ruined that. My name is in a book a century old, and names are sticky things; once written, they pull. Every clerk in that building would feel me like a draft from a shut window.

So we couldn't go in as no one. We went in as other people.

That was Isolde's plan, and I want it recorded—I want it written somewhere in a fair hand, in permanent ink—that her plan was good. She'd spent two weeks mapping the chandler's deliveries, because cartographers map everything, including things that don't have borders yet. The wax wagons came Tuesdays and Fridays. The cellar door took a key the chandler wore on a ribbon under his shirt, and Pip could lift a ribbon off a sleeping man's neck without waking his dreams.

"Theo takes the ledger room," Isolde said, the night before, drawing the floorplan in candle soot on the floor of Odile's barge cabin. "The memory of the map will be filed by year. Your year. You'll know the filing."

"I know the filing," I said, and hated how much I knew it. Eleven years of my neat hand cataloguing stolen griefs. Somewhere down there was my own page, too—the one I'd torn out and failed to destroy properly—and somewhere down there was a small glass phial with a dead man's last masterpiece inside it: the true borders of Zephyrine, drawn from a lifetime of loving a country that had been told it didn't exist.

"Pip watches the wagon door," Isolde went on. "Wren—"

"Outside," Wren said. "Where I'm least useful." Her jaw did the thing it does when she's swallowing an argument whole. A knight of the Provisional Authority, walking into the guild's headquarters, was a diplomatic incident wearing armor. "If it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," Pip said, with the magnificent confidence of a twelve-year-old who has never once been wrong about picking a pocket and therefore believes she will never be wrong about anything, ever, unto eternity. "It's a cellar. Cellars don't have guards, they have dust."

I should have corrected her. I should have told her what I know now, what I knew then and didn't say: that cellars full of memories have something better than guards. They have archivists. And archivists are people whose entire lives are spent noticing what's missing.

I let a child believe cellars were harmless because I wanted the plan to be simple. Note it in the margin. There will be a test later, and I failed it.

* * *

We went in on a Friday, through the wax delivery, in the hour before dawn when Candlewick Lane smells like a snuffed altar. The chandler slept through the loss of his key with the deep peace of a man who owed the guild money and dreamed, presumably, of creditors. Pip slipped in first—a shadow stealing a shadow's coat—and took up her post by the wagon doors, whistling three notes of a barge song every quarter hour to tell us all was well.

Three notes. Silence. Three notes. It was almost soothing, that little song. Down the cellar stairs I went, lamp hooded, into the smell I still can't describe without my stomach turning: vellum and vinegar and underneath it something sweeter, like fruit going bad, which is the smell a memory makes when it's been kept past its natural life.

The ledger room looked exactly as I'd left it. That was the worst part. Rows of shelving, phials racked like wine, the great ledgers open on their lecterns in Sable's rotation of record. My own handwriting marched down the pages, tidy as fencing: *Item: grief, maternal, forty years' standing, taken from a woman on Weeping Row. Condition: excellent. Buyer: pending.*

Eleven years, and this was what my diligence had built. A library of amputations.

I found the right shelf. Found the year. Found the sleeve marked in Sable's copperplate: *CARTOGRAPHY — E., ISOLDE'S FATHER — DO NOT TRADE.* Do not trade. Not because it was sacred. Because Sable was waiting for something, and I was the something, and the map was bait on a hook she'd set before I was born—or before the prophecy surfaced, anyway, which amounted to the same calendar.

I took the phial. It was lighter than I expected. Memories usually are; it's the keeping of them that weighs.

And then the lamps came up—not mine—all along the walls, gentle and golden, the way light rises in a theater, and I understood that I had never once in my life surprised Sable, and never once surprised her, and never would.

"There you are," said the guildmaster, from the doorway. "Right on schedule."

Behind her, flanked by two clerks I'd shared meals with for a decade, stood Pip. Her wrists were held. Her chin was up. Her eyes were furious and dry, and she was not whistling anything at all.

* * *

"You put her in the wagon door," Sable said. "A child at the door. That's new for you, Theo. Sentiment. It doesn't suit your file."

"Let her go."

"Do you know how I knew tonight's date?" Sable walked the aisle slowly, trailing one finger along the phial racks, and the clerks watched her the way sailors watch weather. "Not the prophecy. Prophecies are cheap; Ashmarket sells three before breakfast. I knew because you are legible, Theo. You always were. Invisible to strangers and utterly transparent to the person who raised you. You steal a general's shame and enter it in my ledger in a fair hand, same pressure on every downstroke, year after year. Then suddenly—" she snapped her fingers— "the pressure changes. Grief in the hand. And a child pickpocket starts asking after cellar keys, and a disgraced knight stops reporting to her superiors, and a cartographer's daughter buys lamp oil in quantities suggesting night work. You didn't gather allies, dear. You gathered witnesses."

Pip spat at the floor. One of the clerks tightened his grip and she kicked his shin, professional to the last.

"The exchange," Sable said, "is simple, and I'll only offer it simply once." She held out her hand, palm up, for the phial. "The map stays. The child walks. You stay too—resume your desk, resume your ledgers, and none of this ever troubled the record. Or." She smiled, and it was the smile I'd mistaken, for eleven years, for warmth. "Or you keep your trophy, and I keep her. The guild has uses for children who can taste lies. We've always lacked a taster."

The phial in my hand. The child in theirs.

Here is what nobody tells you about being invisible: you practice deciding that nothing is yours for so long that when something finally *is* yours—truly yours, chosen, loved, inconvenient—you discover you never learned what to do with it. The guild taught me to take. It taught me to catalogue. It taught me, above all, to leave things where they lay, because a thief who wants nothing can never be caught wanting.

But Pip had tasted my lies on the barge and pronounced them the boring kind. Pip had called the prophecy "a load of old homework." Pip was asleep against Isolde's shoulder not six hours ago, drooling on the last unfinished map of a drowned country, and when I'd covered her with a blanket she'd muttered, without waking, *not the arm, that's the drinking arm.*

Family isn't blood. I'd always suspected it. Now I had arithmetic to prove it: one phial weighed less than a child's wrist. Any fool could carry both, but only one could be carried out.

I'm a very good thief. Let me at least be honest about the one theft that mattered.

I threw the phial.

Not to Sable. Past her—down the long aisle, high, arcing, glass catching lamplight like a thrown star—and every clerk flinched, every eye followed it, because a hundred years of inventory instinct dies hard, and in that half-second of collective horror Sable herself turned, hands rising to catch what could not be allowed to break—

—and I moved. All those years of being forgotten taught me how absence works, how eyes track motion and surrender stillness, and I went over the lectern, through the dark between lamps, and got an arm around Pip and my shoulder into the clerk holding her, and we were both falling, and then we were both running.

Behind us I heard glass shatter and Sable's voice, cracked open for just one syllable with something that was not anger: "*Theo—*"

Then the alarm bells, and the cellar doors banging, and Pip's hand finding mine in the dark, small and sure, gripping hard.

"You threw it," she panted as we took the stairs three at a time. "You threw the map."

"No," I said. "I threw a phial. Pray Isolde's father's memory forgives me the substitution."

Because here's the thing I hadn't told even Wren, hadn't dared write down anywhere: I'd gone back to the vault two nights ago, alone, while the others argued about lanterns. I'd lifted the real phial then and replaced it with an empty twin from my own kit, and entered the substitution in the ledger myself, in my own fair hand, same pressure on every downstroke—because the one thing Sable taught me that I intend to keep is that the best place to hide a theft is in the inventory.

The phial in my coat, the real one, rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat as we burst up into the chandler's shop, past the sleeping man who would wake remembering nothing, and out into Candlewick Lane where the sky was going grey at the edges and Wren was already running toward us with her sword drawn and her face white as paper.

* * *

She killed the alarm bell on the lane's cornerpost with one clean stroke—that's a sentence I never thought I'd write about the woman I love, and yet—and then she was gripping my arms, checking me for damage with brisk soldier's hands, and finding none, shaking me instead.

"Sable?" she demanded. "Did she see you? Does she know where—"

"She knows everything," I said. "She always did. But she doesn't have the map."

Pip was laughing, ugly and breathless, bent double against the wagon wheel. "Her *face*," she managed. "When the empty one broke. Theo, her *face*—"

"Hush," Wren said, but gently, and pulled us both into the alley shadow. Above us the bells were ringing across half of Ashmarket now, and doors were opening, and somewhere behind us the House of Ledgers was learning, for the first time in its history, what it felt like to lose something.

We ran. Odile's barge was gone from the mooring—we'd paid for that contingency too—and Isolde was waiting three bridges downstream with the boat, gray-faced, oars already in the water. We piled in. Pip fell asleep before the second bridge, mid-sentence, her hand still curled around a lockpick she'd stolen off me weeks ago and that I'd let her keep.

Isolde looked at the phial when I handed it over. Looked a long time.

"This is everything," she said quietly. "Half a country. Half a border." Her thumb moved over the glass. "He drew the western ranges last. He said you can't finish a coastline until you've forgiven it." She closed her fist around her father's last work. "Chapter thirteen, then. The price."

"The price," I agreed.

Wren rowed. Behind us, Ashmarket burned its alarm-lamps along the waterfront, one fire after another, a city writing our description in light. Sable would come. Not with clerks—with herself, and forty years of regret sharpened to an edge, and I found I wasn't afraid of her anymore, only sorry, in the way you're sorry for someone reading the last page of a book they refuse to finish.

Pip woke once, near dawn, when the river went silver.

"Theo," she mumbled. "Next time we rob somebody, can it be somebody who deserves it?"

"That was somebody who deserved it."

"Yeah." She yawned. "Felt different, though. Felt like robbing family."

"That," I said, "is because it was."

She thought about that with her eyes shut. "Worth it?"

I looked at Wren's back, at Isolde's white knuckles around the phial, at the child who had cost me everything I'd spent eleven years building, which is to say: nothing worth keeping.

"Worth it," I said.

And upstream, faint on the wind, I heard hoofbeats taking the towpath at a gallop—which meant either the Authority had already tracked us, or Sable had stopped sending clerks to do a guildmaster's work.
