## 14. The Authority Closes In

The Provisional Authority does not summon you when it suspects you. It summons you when it has already decided, and the summons is merely a courtesy extended to your paperwork.

I knew this because my brother had taught it to me, in the way of older brothers: not by saying it, but by demonstrating. Corin had been summoned on a Tuesday. By Thursday his room had been reassigned to a clerk named Brast who whistled through his teeth. By the following spring, when I said his name at dinner, my mother looked at me the way you look at someone who has mentioned a ghost politely, and I understood that the erasure had gone all the way through us like a nail through a plank.

So when the letter arrived — heavy cream stock, the Authority's seal pressed deep enough to read by touch — I gave it exactly four seconds of honest dread and then began packing in my head.

"Knight-Captain Aldous." The summons began, as they all did, with a rank I had earned twice over because no one could ever remember promoting me the first time. "You will present yourself at the Ninth Office, Third Bell, for review of your ongoing inquiry into the prophecy-thief known as Eriksen."

*Ongoing inquiry.* Fourteen weeks of chasing a ledger-keeper across half a country, reduced to two words that meant *you have not caught them yet.*

And beneath the formal lines, one sentence that was not formal at all, set slightly apart in its own paragraph like a knife laid next to a plate:

*Bring with you all notes, correspondence, and records pertaining to your inquiry. These will be collected.*

Collected. Not copied. Not reviewed. Collected — which is what the Authority does with things it intends never to give back, including people.

I read the letter three times, because I am what I am, because my memory is a vault with no door from the inside, because every word I have ever read still sits in me exactly where it landed. Then I sat down at my little desk in my little quarters and made a list, because lists are how I pray.

Things I had done in fourteen weeks that the Ninth Office might find interesting:

One. I had cornered the thief at the Marlow Tollhouse and let them go.

Two. I had lied about it in my report — written that the thief had escaped across the weir, which was technically true if you considered *I stood aside and watched them go* a species of escape.

Three. I had shown them the locket with Corin's face in it, which no knight shows a fugitive, and they had said, gently, horribly, *They wrote him out too, didn't they?* and I had not denied it.

Four. I had spent one night at their camp without arresting anyone, during which we argued about whether forgetting was mercy or death, and then kissed them, which settled nothing and undid me entirely.

Five. I loved them.

There. That was the whole confession, really. Items one through four were just item five wearing armor.

* * *

The Ninth Office smelled of lamp oil and cold iron, the way all Authority buildings smell, as though the entire institution were one enormous instrument waiting to be heated. I had walked these corridors since I was nineteen. I knew which floorboards creaked and which clerks took bribes and which corners the mirrors didn't reach.

Deputy Director Hesse received me in a room with no windows, which told me everything before he opened his mouth. Windows are for people who expect to stay.

"Aldous." He didn't rise. He was arranging papers on his desk with the deliberate slowness of a man letting you watch him decide your fate. "Sit."

I sat. I kept my hands where he could see them, because that is a thing you learn, and because my hands wanted very much to be somewhere near the knife in my boot, and I was not yet ready to know that about myself.

"Fourteen weeks," Hesse said. "One confirmed sighting. Zero captures. Your reports are meticulous — I'll grant you that. Beautifully written." He tapped a folder. "Almost too beautifully. You write like someone building a record to stand behind."

"I write like someone who remembers everything," I said. "It's a burden and a professional asset."

Something flickered across his face. He knew about my memory. Everyone at the Ninth Office knew; it was why they'd given me this hunt in the first place. A knight who cannot forget cannot be made to forget. The thief's whole method — the soft-footed vanishing, the witnesses who couldn't describe them an hour later — slid off me like rain off waxed canvas. They'd thought I was the perfect net.

They had not considered what a perfect net catches.

"You were at the Marlow Tollhouse on the night of the ninth," Hesse said. "Your report places the thief crossing the weir."

"Yes."

"The tollhouse keeper says otherwise. He doesn't remember much — none of them ever do, do they?" Hesse smiled without warmth. "But he remembers a knight's coin, and he remembers it bought nothing, because nobody stopped at his toll that night at all. Funny thing, Aldous. The only witness in this whole country whose memory can't be stolen, and she's the one lying."

I watched him arrange the last paper. Squarely in the center. Like a signature.

"Here's what happens now," he said. "You're relieved of the inquiry. It goes to Captain Verre, who is, I'm told, adequately thorough. You are reassigned to garrison duty at—" he named a place so far north the maps got apologetic around it "—pending final disposition."

"Final disposition," I repeated.

"That's the term." He folded his hands. "Look, Aldous. I knew your brother."

The room went very quiet, the way it does when someone takes out something they shouldn't have and lays it on the table between you.

"He asked questions," Hesse went on, almost kindly, which was the worst part. "Good questions. Wrong questions. And I'll tell you what I wish someone had told him: the Authority does not punish failure. It punishes *inconsistency*. A knight who chases a thief badly is a problem. A knight who chases a thief badly and writes love letters in her reports is a *loose end*. You've seen what we do with those."

I had. I had a face for what the Authority did with loose ends, and the face was nineteen years old and laughing, and it hung in no corridor anywhere because they had taken it down.

"So here is your one act of grace," Hesse said. "Bring me the thief. Bring me the ledger pages, the child, the mapmaker's daughter, all of it, within six days. Do that, and your report gets filed under *complicated* instead of *disappeared*, and you get to grow old in some garrison forgetting to be interesting. Fail, and—" He spread his hands, generous, terrible. "Well. You know the process better than most. You'll be a cautionary tale with no name attached."

"And if I refuse?"

He actually laughed. "Aldous. Nobody refuses. That's the entire architecture. Refusal isn't an option the building contains."

I stood. My legs did it without consulting me, which I appreciated, because my mind was busy doing arithmetic: six days. Theo's camp was two days' hard ride. Pip could taste lies through a closed door. Isolde had half a map and all of a grudge. And somewhere ahead of them was a door out of the country that wasn't there, and a price I already knew, because Theo had told me under a sky full of stars with their hand shaking in mine, and I had said *I'll remember you anyway,* and meant it the way you mean vows.

"Thank you for your candor, Deputy Director," I heard myself say. "You've been extremely helpful."

His smile faltered, just barely. Somewhere under all that iron, a small administrative instinct noticed that I had not sounded defeated. But institutions are bad at noticing the moment their own doors get used against them. He said, "Six days," and I said, "You'll hear from me sooner," and both of us believed we were telling the truth.

* * *

Here is a thing I did not know until the night I did it: a commission burns surprisingly well. All those years of oaths and promotions and the Authority's seal, rendered down into good hot light in a rusted brazier behind a coach-house, and the smoke smells like nothing at all. Like burning paper. Like any paper. Like the difference between a knight and an arsonist is one match and a decision.

I burned it in pieces, because I wanted to watch each part go. The oath page first — *to serve, to guard, to remember* — that last word included, though I suspect whoever drafted it never imagined a knight taking a match to the remembering. Then the commendations. Then the sketch of Corin I'd kept folded in the back, which was never official property, which I burned last and slowest, and which hurt least, because I realized halfway through that I did not need it. I have his face behind my eyes at every hour of every day. I have always had it. That is my curse and, standing there in my own smoke, I decided it would also be my weapon.

Because here is what Hesse's architecture does not contain: someone with nothing left to lose and total recall of everything worth losing.

I took the uniform coat off, considered it, and left it folded on the brazier's edge — not sentimentality; evidence management. Let them find it. Let them spend a day being confused about whether Knight-Captain Aldous had died or deserted before the reports sorted themselves into the second category and the hunt began in earnest. A day's confusion, purchased with a coat. Cheap.

Then I stole a horse, which felt fitting. If I was going to become a criminal, I intended to be thorough about it, and starting with theft put me immediately in sympathy with the man I was riding toward.

* * *

Two days. I rode the river road because it was fastest and stupidest and therefore least expected, and I did not sleep more than an hour at a time, and every hour I checked the road behind me for Verre's riders, who would come, who were probably already coming, because Hesse would not have waited for my six days any more than I was waiting for his permission.

I thought, on that road, about the argument. Theo's camp, one night without running, fire low, and Theo saying that forgetting was mercy — *the widow I took grief from sleeps better now; I did her a kindness* — and me saying that forgetting was death, that a person erased is a person murdered twice, once in fact and once in memory, and Theo going very still, and then very gentle, and saying, *Wren. What if both of us are right?*

I still don't know. I have remembered every word of that argument every day since, and I still don't know, and I think — I think this is what loving someone is. Holding the argument open forever. Refusing to win it.

What I know is smaller and harder: I know Corin's laugh. I know the exact weight of the locket. I know the sound the Authority's seal makes when it presses into cream-colored paper, deep enough to read by touch, and I know that Theo Eriksen's hand fits inside mine with their thumb over my knuckles, and I know the door out of Zephyrine costs every memory tying you to the world, and I know that when I told Theo *then give them the ones that tie you to me, I'll make new ones, I'll tell you everything again, I have room* — I know they cried, and I know I meant it.

That's the ruin, then. That's what love declared looks like when declared by someone like me: not poetry. Inventory. I will hold everything you drop. I was built for it.

* * *

I found their camp at dawn, in the fold of hills above the old border road, where Isolde's maps said the ruins should be and the ruins obligingly were.

I came in on foot for the last mile, hands visible, because I had spent fourteen weeks teaching these people that I arrive with a sword, and unteaching is done in gestures. Even so, Pip materialized out of a gorse bush before I was fifty yards out, standing on a rock with their fists on their hips like the world's smallest customs official.

"You alone?" Pip asked. Then, before I answered: "No. You're not. There's a country behind you that wants you dead. But no *people*."

"Close enough," I said.

"Thought so. Lies taste like pennies." They hopped off the rock. "She's awake," they added, in the tone of someone delivering news they'd been ordered not to editorialize about, and editorialized anyway. "She's been awake since the birds started. She's been *pretending* to sleep since the birds started. It's honestly embarrassing."

Isolde's tent flap was closed; a thread of lamplight showed she was up, working, guarding her half-map the way she guarded everything, with her shoulders. Good. Some things deserved privacy.

Theo was sitting up in their bedroll with their hair doing something architecturally impossible, watching me come down the slope, and I watched their face do the thing it did — the quick inventory, threat-assessed and shelved, the accountant's flicker — and then stop doing it, because they saw my hands empty and my saddle-less horse and the ash I hadn't fully brushed off my collar, and accountants understand receipts.

"Theo," I said. My voice did not behave. I had planned sentences on the road — I am a person who plans sentences; I have planned this one since a night of stars and a shaking hand — and every one of them fell off the shelf. What came out was: "Hesse knows. Six days, or I'm disappeared like Corin. So I burned it. All of it. The commission, the oath, the— everything. I'm off the board. I'm—" and here the planning failed completely and utterly, and I stood in the grey dawn light in front of the person I had been sent to destroy, holding nothing, having destroyed my own name with my own hands, and finished: "I don't have anything left to arrest you with. I checked."

Theo got up slowly. They crossed the frost-burnt grass barefoot, which could not have been comfortable, which they did not appear to notice, and they stopped close enough that I could see dawn happening in their eyes, and they said, in the voice of someone reading the last line of a ledger and finding it balanced:

"You burned it."

"Every page."

"For me."

"For the argument," I said, because even now, especially now, I could not let them have it clean. "We haven't finished it. Whether forgetting is mercy or death. I'm not letting you leave the country with the last word."

And Theo laughed — cracked, wet, real — and pulled me in, and I held on with both arms like a woman salvaging one specific thing from a house fire, which is precisely what I was.

Over their shoulder, Pip made a noise of profound theatrical suffering and announced to the camp, to the hills, possibly to history: "Oh, thank the stars, now maybe somebody will finally *kiss* and we can eat breakfast."

Behind me, far to the north, faint as a headache, the Authority was already mounting its horses.

But that was later. That was the world's business. For one whole minute, in the fold of hills where Zephyrine used to be, I was nobody's knight, and Theo's everything, and the sun came up on the ruin of my old life like it approved of the architecture.
