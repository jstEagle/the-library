## 15. Interlude: One Night Without Running

### Theo

The camp Odile left us was a smuggler's waystation in the hollow of a dead orchard—trees that had been girdled and poisoned decades ago by some Authority edict against fruit nobody could tax. Their branches still stood, white and bare as candlewax, and the roof of the lean-to was made from the hull of a rowboat split lengthwise, so that sleeping under it felt like being inside something that had drowned.

I loved it immediately. Ruins always did like me, and I liked them back, because ruins were the only things in Zephyrine that had already been forgotten and had nothing left to lose.

Pip fell asleep before full dark, curled against Isolde with the boneless entitlement of children everywhere, one hand still fisted around a stolen brass button they refused to explain. Isolde sat sharpening her father's drafting knives with the patience of someone who believed every problem could eventually be cut. And Wren—

Wren sat on the far side of the fire, armor off for the first time since I'd met her, wearing a grey wool shirt that had been patched so many times the patches outnumbered the original cloth. Without the commission coat she looked younger. Without the sword within reach she looked almost unarmed, which I knew better than to believe, but it was a nice lie to sit inside for an evening.

Odile's barge wouldn't come for us until tomorrow night. The map fragment—the half-border Isolde had bled to recover—was wrapped in oilcloth under my shirt, warm from my own skin. For one entire day, nobody was chasing us. Or rather: everybody was chasing us, but nobody had found us yet, and there is a kind of peace available only to people standing in the exact center of the interval between two disasters.

"One night without running," Isolde said, reading my mind with the contempt of a cartographer's daughter for amateurs. "Try not to die of it."

She took first watch. I took second. And between those watches, Wren and I discovered we were both awake at the same time, which felt like a conspiracy neither of us had planned.

* * *

### Wren

I have never once in my life forgotten falling asleep. Most people get the mercy of the gap—the little amnesia between the lamp going out and the morning. Not me. I remember the exact moment sleep takes me the way I remember everything: completely, uselessly, forever. It means I go into the dark every night like a prisoner being escorted somewhere, aware of the whole journey.

So when I woke at the changing of the watch and saw Theo sitting by the coals, I knew precisely how much night remained. Four hours, eleven minutes. I could have told them to the second. Sometimes I think about lying about these things just to feel what it's like to be imprecise. I never do. That's the whole tragedy of me in one sentence.

"You should be asleep," Theo said, without turning. They always knew where I was; I'd stopped finding that unsettling and started finding it—something else. Something I hadn't let myself name yet, because naming things gives them weight, and weight slows you down when you're running.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep well in boats." They gestured up at the split hull above us. "Or boat-shaped architecture. Apparently."

I moved closer to the fire because there was no reason not to, and every reason not to, and I have spent thirty years learning that those are usually the same list read aloud in different voices. The coals were doing that thing coals do, where the light keeps rearranging itself like it can't commit.

"Tell me something true," Theo said. "You're the only person alive who can't lie twice about the same thing. I find that fascinating."

"That's not what I am."

"No?"

"I can lie fine." I fed a stick to the fire. "I just can't forget that I lied. Every lie stays in the ledger." I heard myself say *ledger* and winced, because their whole life was ledgers, guild ledgers, prophecy books, pages torn out or burned or stolen, and here was mine, internal, untheftable, running forever.

Theo noticed. Of course they noticed. "You keep your own accounts," they said softly. "That's why you hate the guild so much. They're you, if you'd been founded by thieves instead of knights."

"That's a horrible thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say. You said tell me something true. That counts."

It did count. I hated that it counted.

* * *

### Theo

Here is what nobody tells you about being forgotten your whole life: you get very good at watching people, because watching is the only relationship available to you. I had watched Wren Aldous across a tollhouse knife, across a river, across three weeks of pursuit, and then across two weeks of alliance, and I had catalogued her the way I catalogue everything—neatly, thoroughly, in columns that didn't exist anywhere but my head, because anything written down can be stolen.

Column one: she remembered everything. Column two: she had a brother she couldn't talk about without her hands changing shape. Column three: she had burned her commission four days ago and still sat with her back to walls and her face toward approaches, out of habit, out of discipline, out of grief for the person she used to be allowed to be.

"Your brother," I said, because the fire makes me honest and I resented it. "Cassian."

"Cas." She corrected me the way she always did, gently, like the name was a small animal she was protecting from being stepped on. "He hated Cassian. Said it sounded like a man who owned several coats he never wore."

"What happened to him?"

"He asked a question." Her voice went flat, which with Wren meant the opposite of calm—it meant all the feeling had been pressed flat, like flowers, like evidence. "In a public archive. About pre-annexation shipping records from the Zephyrine coast. He wanted to know where the fishing fleets went. That's all. Where the boats went." She turned her hands over in the coal-light. "They came for him on a Tuesday. I remember the Tuesday. I remember the rain. I remember the specific sound the door made. I will remember all of it until I die, and everyone tells me that's a gift."

"And you don't think it is."

"I think forgetting is a door too," she said, "and I've never been through it. Not once. Even asleep. Even drunk—I tried, after. It doesn't take." She looked at me then, and the fire did something cruel with her eyes. "You forget professionally. Tell me honestly. Is it mercy? What you do? When you take a widow's grief, a general's shame—do they wake up lighter?"

And there it was. The question I'd been avoiding for the whole length of our acquaintance, asked at last by the one person who would still be holding it in the morning. Because Wren Aldous forgot nothing, which meant whatever I answered tonight, she would still know tomorrow, and next year, and at my funeral.

"The widow wakes lighter," I said. "For about a month. Then she starts grieving again—not the loss, exactly. The hole where the loss was. She knows something's missing even though she doesn't know what. Grief has a shape, and empty space holds the shape of the thing that isn't there." I pulled my knees up. "The guild calls it mercy. The ledger column says MERCY at the top in Sable's handwriting. But I've done the accounting, Wren. We don't remove pain. We remove the referent. The pain finds new ones."

She nodded slowly. "Then forgetting is death."

"Or—" and I surprised myself, because I'd never argued this direction before, not even internally—"or it's anesthesia. Anesthesia isn't death. Nobody dies on the table who wasn't dying already. Some of those people we took from—the general with his shame—he was going to drink himself into his grave carrying it. Is a life with the wound cauterized worse than a life bleeding out? I don't actually know. I've told myself both stories. Whichever one gets me through the job."

"And which one gets you through now?" she asked. "Now that you're the one being taken from? Because that's the price at the end of Isolde's map, isn't it. Everything. Everyone. All of it."

The fire popped. Above us, the drowned boat creaked like it was dreaming of water.

"I haven't decided what story I'm telling myself about that," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it, and I knew she knew it, and the beautiful terrible thing about Wren was that she would remember that I'd lied, and love me anyway, or not, and either way it would stay in her ledger forever.

* * *

### Wren

We argued for two hours. It was the best conversation of my life.

Not because Theo won—they didn't; neither of us did—but because arguing with someone who cannot be forgotten is arguing in good faith for the first time. There's no point performing. Whatever I said would persist. Whatever they said would persist in me. It was like writing on stone after a lifetime of writing on water.

Theo believed forgetting was survivable—that identity was portable, that you could strip a person down to bare want and bare courage and the remaining thing would still be them. I believed memory was the only place a person actually lived, that a self without its history was a house with the furniture removed—same walls, but nobody home. We went around it again and again, circling the same truth from opposite sides: that the door at the end of the map required exactly what each of us feared most. For me, nothing—I'd never lose a single hour. For Theo, everything.

"You realize the symmetry," they said at some point, in the voice they used for ledgers. "Zephyrine was erased. Erasure is the crime. And the exit from erasure is more erasure. The country's whole history rhymes."

"That's not symmetry. That's cruelty."

"Most symmetry is, up close."

At some point Isolde relieved me of watch and pretended not to see us talking, which was her way of pretending not to hope, and I understood it, because I was pretending not to hope too, and pretending is exhausting work when you can never forget having done it.

The sky was doing the thing it does in the dead hours—not dark anymore but not light, the color of a page held up to a window. Theo had gone quiet, hugging their knees, looking at the fire like it owed them money.

"Ask me," they said finally.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking since the tollhouse. You look at me like an unsolved case, Wren. Ask."

I could have asked a hundred questions. Why they stayed invisible so long. Whether they were afraid. Whether the prophecy frightened them. Instead my mouth, which has never once in my life cooperated with my judgment, asked:

"If you give everything to open the door—you'll forget me. Entirely. So why does it matter what happens tonight? Why does any of this matter to you, if it won't matter to you?"

Theo didn't answer right away. They reached over and picked up a stick and drew in the ash—a line, then another line crossing it, the beginnings of a border or a letter or nothing.

"Because it matters to *me*," they said. "The me sitting here. Tonight. This me gets to have this. Future Theo is a stranger I'll never meet. I've spent my whole life being forgotten by other people; turns out I can be forgotten by myself too, and the trick is the same either way. You decide the present tense is enough. You decide the person in front of you is worth it even if the person you become won't know why." They looked up. "Present-tense me thinks you're worth it, Wren Aldous. Future Theo gets a blank page. That's their problem. I'm very sorry for them, whoever they turn out to be."

And I—who had argued all night that memory was the only real country—found that my entire philosophy had just walked across the border and defected.

"I remember everything," I said. My voice came out wrong, low and rough. "Everything I've ever seen. Every face. Do you know what that means? It means if you do this—if you pay that price—there will be one person in the world carrying all of you. Every version. Every night. Every argument. Me. I'll be your ledger, Theo. Nothing written down. Just kept."

They stared at me. The ash stick broke in their fingers.

"That's the most romantic and horrifying thing anyone has ever said to me," they said.

"I'm told I have that effect."

* * *

### Theo

Here is the thing about a countdown: it makes everything luminous. A night with no tomorrow attached would be cheap. But a night with a terrible tomorrow attached—every hour of that is lit from below, like paper over flame.

Wren crossed the ashes between us, or I crossed them; afterward we disagreed about it and each claimed the other moved first, and I intend to keep disputing it forever if forever is offered. What I remember—and I want to be accurate, because accuracy is the only gift I can give someone who keeps everything—is that her hand found my jaw the way you handle a page you're afraid of tearing, and that she hesitated, and that in the hesitation she whispered, "This one I'm going to keep," and then she kissed me.

It was not a movie kiss. It was better; it was specific. It tasted like woodsmoke and bad smuggler's tea and like someone who had decided, against all her training, against twenty years of duty and one burned commission, that some arrests were really defections.

When we broke apart, she rested her forehead against mine and said, quietly, "I can't promise I'll remember this."

"That's my line," I said.

"I know. I'm stealing it. Consider it reparations."

Above us the drowned boat creaked. Somewhere east, an Authority patrol was probably following our cold trail; somewhere west, Sable was doing sums with our names in them; somewhere ahead of us both, a door waited that ate memories like a toll. None of it mattered for the length of that kiss, and then it all mattered again immediately, which is, I've decided, simply what it costs to be alive at a bad time in a vanished country with someone you love.

Love. Present tense. Filed under things future Theo will find a blank page where the entry used to be.

Wren fell asleep against my shoulder at some point—4:51 by her own muttered estimate—and I sat very still and watched the fire die, memorizing her on purpose, deliberately, the way I'd never memorized anything, because all my life I'd trusted ledgers and vellum and theft, and here at the end I was reduced to the oldest technology there is: paying attention.

I didn't sleep. I counted the minutes instead. Four hours, eleven minutes, minus one kiss, plus one kiss, carried forward.

At dawn Isolde shook us awake with a knife-sharpening rhythm that was definitely intentional, and pointed south with the blade.

"Smoke," she said. "A lot of it. On the road behind us."

Wren was on her feet before I finished standing, already reaching for armor she no longer had the right to wear, and I saw her make the calculation—the distance, the wind, the number of hours a fire takes to travel—and saw the answer arrive in her face.

"They're not following our trail," she said. "They're burning it. Someone wants Ashmarket erased before we can come back through."

And from the child-shaped bundle beside Isolde, Pip opened one eye and said, in the flat voice of someone tasting the air: "Whoever set it—they knew we were coming back."
