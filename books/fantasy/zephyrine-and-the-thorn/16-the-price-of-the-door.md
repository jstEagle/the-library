## 16. The Price of the Door

*Theo*

I have spent my life around ledgers, so I know the shape of a price before anyone says it aloud. There is a particular silence a room makes when someone is doing arithmetic they don't want to share. Isolde had been sitting with her father's half-finished map for three hours, and the silence coming off her was the expensive kind.

We were camped in the shell of a customs house two miles from the border ruins—Pip asleep against a collapsed door that still bore, faintly, the ghost of a Zephyrine customs stamp, Wren sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, Odile gone back downriver with her barge and a promise to return at moonset. The fire was small. Small fires are a habit you learn in my profession.

"Say it," I said.

Isolde didn't look up. "You first. Tell me what you know about how doors work."

"Doors open when you push them."

"The doors of houses." She turned the map toward the light. Her father's ink had faded to the color of old tea, but along the northern border, where Zephyrine used to touch the rest of the world, he had drawn something that was not a road, not a river—a line of small interlocking circles, like chain mail, like a lock's tumblers seen from inside. "This is a threshold. He drew three of them in his life. He called this one the Door Out of Nowhere, and he wrote one sentence about it in his working notes, which I memorized at nine years old because I thought it was a fairy story."

She recited it the way you recite something you have hated for a long time: *"The door does not open outward. It opens out of someone. It will admit the exiles only through the hand of one who carries nothing—not coin, not name, not memory—for what was erased can be restored only by an equal erasure freely given."*

The fire popped. Pip, who I had believed asleep, sat up very slowly, the way children do when they understand more than the adults have agreed to allow them.

"That's nonsense," Wren said, flat, immediate, the voice of a woman building a wall out of words. "It's symbolic. Threshold language. Every cult in history has some rite where you symbolically shed your—"

"It's ledger language," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"You want to hear it plainly?" Isolde said. "Fine. Plainly. The door opens for one person who gives up everything that ties them to the world. Every memory. Their mother's face. Their first theft. The taste of bread. The people they love. All of it, taken out of them, poured into the mechanism like coin into a tollbox. That's the price. That's the whole price. And then they walk through—or they don't; the notes don't say—but either way, whoever walks out the other side will not know why they came."

* * *

*Wren*

I have a perfect memory. People think that means I remember things clearly. What it actually means is that I remember things completely—the dust in the air, the exact pitch of Theo's voice when they said *it's ledger language*, the way Isolde's hands were shaking and how she pressed them flat against the map to hide it, the sound Pip made, which was no sound at all, which was the worst part.

So I remember precisely how long it took me to stand up: four seconds. I remember precisely why those four seconds mattered: because in them, Theo was already talking.

"No," I said.

"Theo," Isolde said, at exactly the same moment, and even her voice cracked on the second syllable.

Because Theo had done the arithmetic already. Of course they had. They'd been doing it since Isolde began speaking—I could see it behind their eyes, that ledger-keeper's fluency, columns summing themselves—and here is what their arithmetic said:

Me. Not Wren, whose memory cannot be stolen, whose mind is a vault with no door. Not Pip, a child. Not Isolde, who alone could finish the map and guide the exiles through. Not Odile, who wasn't there. The circle of eligible souls was very small and contained exactly one person, and that person had spent twenty years practicing at being no one.

"It fits," Theo said, gently, as if breaking terrible news to a client. "The prophecy. *The thief who is no one.* I thought it meant I'd slip past the guards. It means I'm the key. I've been the key since before I was born."

"I don't care what it means—"

"Wren." They said my name and I hated how well it worked. "Sit down."

"I am a knight of the Provisional Authority," I said, which was a lie twice over now—I had burned my commission, and I had never been less anyone's knight than I was at that moment—and I heard myself saying it anyway, reaching for the oldest authority in the room because the true authority, which was love, was unbearable. "My brother asked one question about Zephyrine and they erased him. Erasure is not a sacrifice, it's a murder committed politely. You are not volunteering, you are—"

"Choosing," Theo said. "For once in my life, choosing first."

And I remembered—I will always remember—that they smiled. Not bravely. Wryly. The little crooked smile of a bookkeeper who has finally found the entry that balances.

* * *

*Isolde*

They argue that the volunteer should be argued out of it. Nobody tells you about the hour after, when arguing fails and everyone simply sits together in the new knowledge, the way you sit with the newly dead.

I resented prophecy-marked strangers when they came to my father's house. I want to write that down somewhere, in case the record survives and someone is tempted to make me noble: I did not want any of this. My father spent forty years drawing a country that official atlases insisted was empty grassland, and it killed him slowly, at a desk, in ink fumes, and when he died the map was unfinished and so was I.

But I finished the map. Three days ago, in this same customs house, I closed the last border line while Pip held the lamp, and the moment the final stroke dried, every interlocking circle along the threshold lit faintly under the paper like embers under ash. It is real. God help me, it is real, and it needs feeding.

Here is what my father's notes say about the mechanism, in the plainest language he ever used, which he saved for the parts that shamed him:

*"The door was built by the last cartographers and the first exiles together, in the weeks after the coup. They understood that a country erased from every record cannot be restored by force or argument—it must be re-inscribed, and inscription requires ink, and the only ink potent enough is a life's worth of remembering, given up all at once. The volunteer pours their memories into the threshold. The door drinks. And everything the volunteer carried—the faces, the voices, the loves—becomes the mortar of the restored country."*

He wrote one more line underneath, smaller, and I have not said it aloud yet. I am deciding now, watching Theo bank the fire with that terrible competence of theirs, whether mercy or honesty serves better.

Honesty. My father would say honesty; he was a cartographer; he believed in telling people where the cliffs were.

"There's something else," I said.

Theo looked up.

"The notes say the door takes memories *in order*. Not all at once. One by one, from the most recent backward, over however many hours it takes. The volunteer remains... aware. Present. Watching themselves be emptied." I made myself hold their gaze. "My father writes that most candidates, historically, changed their minds midway. And that the door does not refund partial payment."

Silence. Then Theo, softly, with something close to delight, which I will never forgive them for:

"So I get to attend my own erasure. Seated admission. How thorough of it."

* * *

*Pip*

I'm the youngest so nobody tells me things until after they've told each other, but I hear everything anyway, because listening is a professional skill and I've been a professional since I was six.

Here's what I heard tonight, lying quiet by the door with my eyes shut, doing sums of my own:

Theo thinks nobody will miss their memories much, because Theo thinks they were never really anybody. That's the trick of it. That's the whole con they've run on theirself their whole life. Grew up in a guild that taught them invisibility like scripture, spent twenty years being forgotten on purpose, and now they've found a door that agrees with them. Of course they volunteered. The door is just the guild with better theology.

But I've tasted Theo's lies—tasting lies is my thing, don't ask how, it's like copper and smoke on the tongue—and tonight I tasted the truth instead, and truth tastes like nothing you'd expect. Like rain on hot stone.

Because here's the other thing I heard. After Isolde went to sleep and the knight went out to walk the perimeter the way she does when she's frightened, Theo sat alone with the ledger they stole from the guild, the one with all their own entries in it, and read it cover to cover like a man reading letters from home. And at the end they touched one page—just one—and I tasted the truth of that touch from across the room.

Grief. Already grieving. Saying goodbye in advance to something specific, something they haven't told anyone about, something they're planning to keep secret even from the door.

I don't know what it is yet.

I'm going to find out.

* * *

*Theo*

Wren came back from the perimeter and stood over me while I pretended to inventory our supplies, which is what I do instead of sleeping, and finally she said, "Talk me out of tying you up."

"You'd have to find rope I couldn't pick."

"I'd hold the rope myself."

"And when the Authority comes—and they're coming, Wren, we both know the timetable now, the map is finished, the door is live, and Marshal Corvane does not leave loose ends—you'll need your hands free." I set down the candle stub. "One of us has to be unencumbered at dawn. That's just logistics."

"Don't you dare make this reasonable."

"It's the only thing I know how to do. Reasonable things. Balanced things." I looked at her—at the firelight on the scar through her eyebrow, at the fury she was holding the way soldiers hold a wall, shoulder to it, all of her weight against the inevitable—and I wanted, briefly and unbearably, to be a different kind of thief. The kind who steals time.

"When I was eight," I said, "the guild tested me. Sable put a memory in my hand—an orange, a kitchen, a woman singing—and told me to take it into myself and forget where it came from. And I did. And when she asked me afterward what I felt, I said, *lighter.* She wrote it in my file: *subject reports relief.* Twenty-two years, Wren, and that file has been right every single day. I am the ideal candidate. I have been training for this erasure since childhood. Everyone else in this camp would be destroyed by it. I'll just be..." I gestured at myself. "Tidier."

"You'll be gone."

"I'll be *useful*, which is the closest thing to gone that I've ever wanted to be."

Wren sat down across from me. Slowly, like her legs had been confiscated. And she said the thing I had been maneuvering us both away from for three days, because she has a perfect memory and therefore no talent whatsoever for letting go:

"At the tollhouse, when I had the knife on you. You asked me if I remembered my brother's laugh. Do you know what you said when I recited it? Word for word? You said, *No one has ever carried anything that carefully for me.*"

"I remember."

"Then carry this carefully." She leaned forward and took both my hands, and her grip was a knight's grip, oath-sworn, unbreakable. "Tomorrow I will fight you. I want you to know that now, so you can't feel ambushed later. I will fight you with everything I have, and if I lose, I will walk through that door beside you anyway and spend whatever's left of me making sure the country you bought knows your name, even if you don't. But tonight—" her voice went to pieces and got reassembled, badly, "—tonight I'm just asking. Don't do this because you think you're spare. You're not spare. You're the whole ledger."

For a moment I almost told her. About the page. About the thing I mean to hide in the hollow of my hand like a coin palmed from a mark, the one memory I've already begun teaching myself to lie about, the one the door will reach for and not find.

I didn't tell her. Some thefts you have to commit alone, or they aren't yours.

"Get some sleep," I said instead. "Dawn comes early. Doors don't open themselves."

Outside, far off toward the border ruins, something in the dark gave a low grinding sigh, like a machine remembering it had work to do.

Pip, from her corner, without opening her eyes: "It heard you."

None of us slept after that.
