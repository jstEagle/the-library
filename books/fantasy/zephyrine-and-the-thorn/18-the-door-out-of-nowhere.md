## 18. The Door Out of Nowhere

**Theo**

The border ruins of Zephyrine do not look like the end of anything. They look like a farmer gave up mid-wall: a scatter of mossy stones, a lintel lying in the grass like a fallen giant's toothbrush, and one archway still standing, which is either hopeful or cruel depending on how the next hour goes.

I have never been an optimist, so I was voting cruel.

Behind us, down the valley road, came the sound I had been dreading all night—hooves, and the particular rhythm of hooves that are not in any hurry because they already know where you're going.

"They're an hour out," Wren said, which was her way of saying ten minutes. She had always been honest about time even when it was rude to be. "Maybe less."

"Then let's not waste it with speeches," said Pip, who was eleven and had never once in her life declined to waste time with speeches.

Isolde knelt at the archway with her father's finished map spread over the stones, weighting the corners with rocks like she was setting a table for a very demanding ghost. The map was beautiful. It was also, apparently, hungry.

"It wants a payment," she said, without looking up. "You all know this. I'm saying it again so nobody can claim surprise later. Surprise is for birthday parties."

"Theo's paying," Pip said. Then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe twice a year: "Right? That was the deal. Theo's paying."

"That was the deal," I said.

Here is what I did not say: that on the barge, three weeks ago, I had done the arithmetic every thief does before a job. What am I carrying. What can I afford to lose. And I had looked at the contents of my own head—the guild, Sable, the Ashmarket, my mother's face, which I had already sold once at fourteen and bought back at twenty-two at ruinous interest—and I had found one item I could not put a price on, and so I had hidden it, the way I hide everything, folded small and tucked somewhere no ledger could reach.

More on that later. If there is a later. There is always a later; that is the one prophecy I've never found written down.

Wren took my hand. Her grip was the exact pressure of someone who has practiced holding on.

"You don't have to narrate," she said.

"I always narrate."

"I know." Her thumb moved across my knuckles. "It's one of the things."

*One of the things.* We had spent exactly one night not running, and in that night we had built a whole inventory between us—one of the things, two of the things—as if love were a heist and we were casing each other. Which, now that I write it, may be the most accurate description anyone has ever given of love, and I include everything in the Vellum Guild's catalogue.

"Sit down," Isolde told me, pointing at the threshold stone inside the arch. "And don't fight it. Fighting makes it take more. Papa's notes were very clear."

"On what?"

"On everything," she said, and for the first time since I'd met her, the cartographer's daughter looked scared.

* * *

**Wren**

I want it recorded—I am recording it, here, in the only book that will hold still for me—that I argued with him until the horses were close enough to count.

I argued that we could run. He pointed out that running was what we'd been doing for a month and the Authority was faster than grief, which was the only thing we had left in quantity. I argued that there had to be another way. Isolde read aloud from her father's notes, in her flat, furious voice, the relevant line: *The door opens only for the one who owes it nothing—who leaves the world owing nothing, holding nothing, remembering nothing.* I argued that prophecies lie. Theo said, gently, "So do ledgers. You just have to know which columns to trust."

I am a knight of the Provisional Authority. Was. Past tense burned along with my commission in a brazier outside Havistock. I have stood in formation while men I trained with disappeared other men, and I told myself it was law, and law was duty, and duty was the load-bearing wall of my whole self.

None of that prepared me for sitting on cold grass watching the person I love pay for a country.

The door began the way all true doors begin, which is to say quietly. The arch stopped being a shape and started being a place. Air moved *through* it, from nowhere into Zephyrine—or from Zephyrine into nowhere; even the wind couldn't decide—and the stones lit from within like paper held against a candle.

And Theo went pale, and then paler, and then he laughed.

"What?" I said.

"It's starting with breakfast," he said. "Somebody's eggs. Who eats eggs at a time like—" and then his face changed, because whatever egg had been attached to that memory was gone, and he felt the hole where it had been. I watched a man feel a hole. I will never unwatch it.

"My name is Wren," I said.

He blinked at me. Not blankly—he knew me, would keep knowing me for hours yet—but the way you blink when someone says something urgent and you haven't yet worked out why.

"Yes," he said slowly. "Yes, it is. Wren Aldous. Knight, formerly. Terrible at chess. Once threatened a customs official with a soup ladle."

"That was you," Pip said.

"We agreed never to discuss the ladle."

Another layer went. I could see it happen—a flicker, like a page turning in the dark. His mother's face, maybe. The smell of the guild library. Some Tuesday. Every person is mostly Tuesdays, and the door took them like a tax collector takes coins: methodically, by weight.

"My name is Wren," I said again.

"I heard you the first time," Theo said, and smiled, and then his smile cracked down the middle. "Keep saying it. Please. Whatever else falls out of my head, keep—"

"I know," I said. "I know. My name is Wren."

* * *

**Pip**

I taste lies. It's not a trick, it's like a taste in my mouth—sour milk for fibs, rot for real whoppers. I sat behind Theo while the door ate him and I kept tasting the air because I needed to know if hope tasted like anything, and here's the thing:

Nothing lied. Not Wren saying *my name is Wren*, over and over, like ringing a bell. Not Isolde reading her papa's notes. Not Theo joking about eggs with his hands shaking. Not even the door. The door was honest too, which somehow made it worse. Honest things are always worse. Lies you can dodge.

The Authority came over the ridge while the door was still only half-open. Maybe thirty riders, torches, and Captain Vess at the front with her grey coat and her grey face. I've been caught by better people than her. I've been caught by worse, too. Mostly I've been caught.

"Don't stop," Isolde said, and she stood up off the grass and walked toward thirty riders like she was going to complain to them about the price of ink.

* * *

**Isolde**

My father drew maps for forty years and died for one unfinished border. When the Authority burned his workshop, they left him alive—mercy, they called it—but they took his memory of the map itself, sealed it in a guild vault, and let him live out his last winter reaching for a thing that wasn't there anymore. I watched a man grieve his own mind. So understand that when I stood between the riders and the archway, I was not being brave. Bravery implies options.

Captain Vess reined in six feet from me. "Step aside."

"No," I said.

"This is treason."

"Everything is treason now. It's very efficient. You should put it on a sign."

She looked past me at the glowing arch, at Theo seated in the threshold with his eyes closed and Wren's hand in his, and I watched her assemble it—the prophecy, the door, the country her entire regime insists does not exist—and I watched the assembly fail, because you cannot arrest an idea with thirty men when the idea is standing in front of you made of light.

"The country isn't real," she said.

"Then you're wasting your cavalry," I said, "on a fiction. Go home, Captain. Draw your own conclusions. You're clearly capable of it, or they wouldn't have given you a grey coat."

A rider behind her shifted. Somewhere down the line a torch guttered. And I thought, absurdly, *Papa, look, your lines are holding,* because a map is just a promise about where things are, and tonight, for the first time in a hundred years, the promise was being kept.

* * *

**Theo**

Losing memories, it turns out, is not like having things stolen. Theft leaves the shape of the absence—you know something was there, you circle the gap, you mourn. This was different. This was subtraction without remainder. One moment I owned a thing; the next moment I had never owned it, and I could not miss what I could not name, except—

Except I could see Wren's face getting wetter and wetter, and I could hear her voice, *my name is Wren, my name is Wren*, steady as a metronome, and I understood dimly that she was doing arithmetic too. Counting what remained. Standing guard over the last shelf.

The door took my mother's laugh. I know that because Wren flinched, afterward, as though something had gone through her instead of me.

The door took the Ashmarket. Took Odile's barge and the smell of river tar. Took, I think, fourteen years of Tuesdays in the guild library, because suddenly the shelves of my childhood were empty architecture and Sable's voice was a sound I recognized the way you recognize a word you've seen only in print.

"Sable," I said. Testing it. "Sable. She was—" What? Mother? Jailer? Both, probably. Both was always the guild's specialty. "Was she good to me?"

"She kept you alive," Wren said carefully, "and she tried to erase you. I think both things were true."

"That sounds like her." Though I no longer knew how it sounded like her. The sentence arrived pre-grieved.

The arch blazed. Through it—not a picture, a *pull*—I saw green hills under a sky with the wrong constellations, and a harbor town, and smoke rising from chimneys in a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own survivors apologized for mentioning it. Zephyrine. Mine, apparently. Ours.

"How much is left?" I asked.

Wren didn't answer.

"Wren. How much?"

"Some," she said, which was a lie, and I could tell it was a lie because her jaw did the thing it does, and I loved her so much in that moment that I wanted to give her the lie as a gift, wanted to wrap it up and keep it forever, and then I realized I was trying to keep something and the door noticed.

Doors notice. Nobody tells you that.

It reached for the last shelf, the folded thing, the one small parcel I had hidden behind everything else, and I threw everything I had—every remaining gram of myself, which was mostly stubbornness—into standing in front of it.

*Not that. Take the stubbornness. Take the jokes. Take the ladle story, take the eggs, but not that.*

The door considered. I felt it consider, vast and patient and fair, which is the most frightening quality anything can have.

Then it took my name instead. Just the top layer—*Theo Eriksen, keeper of ledgers, thief who is no one*—peeled away like bark, and I gasped, and for one white instant I was nobody at all, which is to say I was finally, perfectly, qualified.

The door opened all the way.

* * *

**Wren**

He stood up out of the threshold like a man surfacing from deep water, and he looked at the others, and I watched him sort them—Pip he knew, Isolde he knew, his own hands he knew—and then he looked at me.

He looked at me the way you look at a room you've never been in.

"My name is Wren," I said. For the hundredth time. For the last first time.

"Hello, Wren," he said politely. Politely. To me. As though we had met at a function. Something in my chest broke with a click I heard quite clearly, the way you hear a lock turn in a door you expected to stay open forever.

But he didn't let go of my hand. That was the thing. They had taken everything with a label on it and he was holding my hand anyway, on pure instinct, like a man who wakes from surgery still gripping a rope he doesn't remember taking up.

"There's a blank space," he said slowly. "Where something should be. It's—shaped." His thumb pressed against my knuckles, testing the shape. "Did I... was it..."

"You tell him," Pip whispered. "Somebody tell him."

"Not yet," Isolde said softly. "Let him cross first. Let him be safe. Then we'll fill the page back in. Word by word, if we have to."

The exiles were coming up the valley now—not the riders, the *others*: families out of the cart-tracks and hedgerows, people Odile had ferried all month, a hundred faces I had sworn at and shared bread with. They streamed past us toward the light, and each one crossed the threshold and made a small involuntary sound, like people stepping into the sea.

Vess's riders sat their horses on the ridge and did not come down. I would like to report that this was because of Isolde's courage, or mine, or justice. Mostly, I suspect, it was because none of them wanted to be the man who arrested a doorway.

Theo stepped toward the arch. Stopped. Turned around.

"Come with me," he said—to all of us, to the dark, to the blank space shaped like a person. And then his eyes found mine again, and whatever lived behind them now, some part of him still knew how to aim.

"My name is Wren," I said, one more time, because it was the only thing I had left to give him that the door couldn't tax.

"Goodbye, Wren," he said.

And he turned, and stepped through, and the light swallowed him, and the exiles followed, dozens of them, the erased walking home through the ruins of their erasure, and it should have been the end of the story.

But I was still standing on the wrong side of the threshold.

Because I finally understood what he had paid with, and I could not walk into a country built out of his forgetting of me. I could not be welcomed home by a man whose home I cost. The door hummed behind me, patient, fair, beginning—it had begun already, I felt the draft reverse—to grind itself closed.

"Theo," I said, to his disappearing back, in a country that was no longer on any map, in a voice that was no longer anyone's duty. "My name is *Wren.*"

The door began to close.
