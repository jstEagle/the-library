## 20. The Blank Page

I woke to the smell of bread.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was that I knew it was wrong, and the third — the third took longer, and it's still taking, if I'm honest — was that I didn't know why.

Bread. Woodsmoke. Wet grass under my cheek, which meant I was lying outdoors, which meant someone had carried me, which meant there were people, and every one of those conclusions arrived with a small cold tap on the shoulder, like a clerk checking a list: *and? and? and?* As though each fact were a line item in a ledger and somewhere below it, an entry had been torn out.

I opened my eyes. Sky. A particular sky — pale at the edges, bruised blue at the center — and I thought, with a certainty that had no paperwork attached to it: *this is not Zephyrine's sky.*

Then I thought: *Zephyrine.*

The word landed in me like a coin dropped down a well. I heard it fall for a long time before it hit bottom. I knew the country. Of course I knew it; I had spent — weeks? months? — learning its borders from a woman who drew them like she was stitching a wound shut. Isolde. Isolde was here. Somewhere. Probably. The name came with a face attached, sharp-featured and soot-tempered, and relief went through me so hard it hurt, because at least *that* page hadn't been blank.

And then I reached for the other one.

I don't know how to describe what happened next except that it happened inside me the way a collapse happens inside a building: no sound, just a sudden reorganization of everything, load-bearing walls gone, dust where rooms used to be. I reached for a name and found a shape where it should have been. Not nothing — nothing would have been kinder. A shape. An absence with edges, the way a knife leaves an edge-shaped hole in a coat pocket. Someone had been there. Someone had held my hand — I could feel the ghost of pressure across my knuckles, specific, warm, *repeated*, as though a phrase had been said over and over —

— and the phrase was gone too.

I sat up too fast. Pip caught my shoulder.

"Whoa, whoa — easy, Theo, you're — you're back, you're through, we're all —"

Pip. Small, sharp-eyed Pip, whose face I could see and whose name I could say, and when I looked at them something in my chest lurched sideways, because I loved this child. I knew that the way you know your own hands. And I also knew — with the same terrible clerical certainty — that I should be feeling something else right now, some second thing standing next to the love, and it wasn't there. It was like looking at a shelf built for two objects and finding one.

"Theo." Pip's voice did something careful. "Say my name."

"Pip."

"Good. Okay. Good." They were crying, I realized. They were crying and smiling about it, which is a thing only children and liars can do convincingly, and Pip was not lying. "You're okay. You're mostly okay."

"*Mostly*," I repeated.

Isolde crouched down on my other side. Behind her, stretching away toward hills that shouldn't exist on any map I'd ever been paid to keep straight, were people. Hundreds of them. Exiles in mismatched coats, standing in the wet grass of a country that had officially never happened, some of them already kneeling to touch the soil, some of them singing, badly, in a language half of them only half remembered. The door behind us — I didn't look at it. Some instinct older than memory told me not to look at it yet.

"You gave everything," Isolde said. It wasn't a question. She'd read her father's notes; she knew the mechanism better than any of us. "Every thread. That was the price. We all watched you pay it."

"I remember paying it," I said. Which was true, in the way a scar remembers a knife. "I remember the door opening. I remember —" I stopped. My hand had gone, without my permission, to my breast pocket, where the ledger rode. Old habit. Twenty years of keeping accounts, and even stripped hollow, my body kept its own books.

The ledger was there. Sodden at the corner, cracked along the spine, but there.

I opened it.

Here is what I will say about grief without an object: it is the worst kind, and I include in that ranking every grief the Vellum Guild ever sold or stole, and I catalogued thousands. Grief with a face at least tells you where to put it. Grief without one just *leaks*. You cry at bread. You cry at the way light comes off wet grass. You cry because someone once told you, in a voice you can no longer hear, that forgetting might be mercy, and you must have argued with them, because arguing sounds like something we would have done.

The ledger fell open to a page near the end, because the book knew where it lived now, and the page was blank.

Not torn out. Blank. Clean vellum, guild-grade, unmarked by so much as a crease — except that when I tilted it against the morning light, I could see the faintest shadow of writing beneath the surface, the way a palimpsest keeps its sins. Words had been there. Words had been *scrubbed*, scoured out of existence so thoroughly that even the ink had forgotten what it had said, and the page itself seemed ashamed of it, holding its emptiness like a held breath.

I knew whose handwriting it had been.

I didn't know whose handwriting it had been.

Both of those statements were true, and I sat in the grass of a resurrected country holding both of them, and Pip put their arms around me, and I did not cry, because crying requires knowing what for, and all I had was the leak.

* * *

They gave me three days before they started pushing, which was two days more generous than I'd have expected and one day less than I wanted.

On the first day, I did what I have always done when the world breaks: I worked. There were exiles needing feeding, names needing recording — real names, restored names, people giving their Zephyrine names aloud for the first time in decades and flinching like the syllables were hot. Someone needed a ledger kept. I am, whatever else has been scraped out of me, still the best bookkeeper the Vellum Guild ever produced, and so I sat by a cookfire with a borrowed quill and wrote down the returning country, entry by entry, and it helped, the way bleeding helps a wound: technically, and at a cost.

It was during this work that I noticed the other blanks.

Small ones. A gap where a tavern's name should be. A night in Ashmarket that ended abruptly mid-sentence in my recollection, like a letter with the bottom third cut away. And one more — one more shape-with-edges, smaller than the first, tucked behind my sternum: the sensation of having once hidden something so well that even I couldn't find it. A smuggler's trick. Hide the treasure inside the inventory.

Whatever I'd hidden, the door hadn't found it either.

On the second day, Isolde sat down across the fire from me with her father's unfinished map and didn't speak for a long time, which from Isolde is a speech.

"It doesn't make sense," she finally said.

"Most things don't. It's why I got into accounting."

"The door took everything tied to you. Every thread. That's the mechanism — Papa's notes are clear, and what happened at the border confirms it. But threads don't go one direction, Theo. If someone was tied to *you* —" She turned the map over, and on its blank reverse side, in her quick cartographer's hand, she had drawn something: a knot. A single point where dozens of lines converged and then simply *stopped*, frayed into nothing. "Wren crossed the border with us and then stayed on the threshold. I saw her. Everyone saw her. She let the door close rather than walk into a country you paid for by forgetting her."

The name went through me like weather. *Wren.* I heard it and felt the shape shift behind my sternum, the knife-edge hole in the coat pocket, the ghost-pressure on my knuckles — *my name is Wren, my name is Wren,* said the way you'd say it to someone drowning, over and over —

"Do I remember her?" I asked.

"No," Isolde said. "But you *grieve* her. Look at yourself. You're grieving someone you have no evidence ever existed, and that's not how erasure works. Erasure is clean. This isn't clean. Something survived, Theo. Something you hid so well that even the door couldn't take it, or something she left in you that the door couldn't reach. Either way —" she tapped the frayed knot, hard enough to dent the paper — "a thread that survives on one end survives on both. That's how thread works. That's *literally* how thread works."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll redraw her myself," Isolde said, with the flat ferocity she usually reserved for bad geography. "My father taught me that maps aren't pictures of the world. They're arguments. And I have never lost an argument in my life."

On the third day, Pip stole the blank page.

I want to be clear that they returned it. Eventually. After several hours, during which I experienced a fresh variety of horror I had previously only sold to other people. But they took it — cut it cleanly from the binding with a pickpocket's blade while I slept, and when I woke shouting, they stood their ground at the edge of the camp holding the page up to the dawn light like a jeweler assessing a stone.

"You can see it," they said. "Under the right light. I *tasted* it, Theo. Lies taste like copper to me, you know that. Truth tastes like nothing. And this page tastes like *nothing* — but old nothing, worn nothing, nothing that used to be something for years and years. That's not erasure. That's a secret."

"Pip —"

"She wrote in it," Pip said. "Before the border. Or after. Somebody did, and then somebody scrubbed it, and scrubbing is a guild job, and you know guild work when you see it, because you *did* guild work your whole life. So the question isn't whether the words are gone." They walked over and pressed the page back into my hands, closing my fingers around it, small fierce child, eyes wet and jaw set. "The question is who scrubbed them, and why, and whether what was written can be written again."

I looked down at the blank page in my hands. The held-breath emptiness of it. Somewhere under the vellum's surface, a ghost of ink. Somewhere behind my sternum, a knot with frayed ends. Somewhere on the far side of a door that should have closed forever, a woman who chose to stay in an erased country rather than enter a home built on my forgetting.

Grief without an object. Hope without proof.

I've kept ledgers my whole life, and here is the first rule of the trade: every account balances. Nothing is truly lost; it's only moved to a column you haven't checked yet.

"Get Isolde," I said. "And bring me the prophecy book. All of it. Every page Sable didn't burn."

"Why?" Pip asked, already grinning, already running.

"Because," I said, tilting the blank page against the rising sun until the buried shadow of writing began, faintly, stubbornly, to surface — a single line, in a hand I did not remember and recognized anyway — "someone wrote themselves into my book. And I intend to find out how to read them back."
