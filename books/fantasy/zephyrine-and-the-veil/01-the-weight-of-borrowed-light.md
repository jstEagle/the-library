## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Light

The grief weighed almost nothing.

That was the first thing people never believed about Elodie's trade—that sorrow could be lighter than a coin, lighter than bread, light enough to cup in one palm like a moth with its wings folded. Widow Ameline's grief for her husband Bertrand had sat in the pawnshop ledger for eleven months, pressed flat between the record of a silver candlestick and the record of a man's good winter coat, and it had grown no heavier in all that time. Grief didn't gain weight from sitting. It only gained weight from being carried.

Elodie carried it now, two fingers pinching the ledger's corner as she read, and let it lift off the page the way steam lifts off morning soup.

The pawnshop belonged to a man named Fosco, who was pretending very hard to count coins at his counter and was actually watching her in the tarnished mirror behind him. He always watched. He liked to tell himself he was complicit in something dangerous, that his back room hosted crimes of terrible consequence. In truth, what happened in his back room was closer to laundry.

"You're sure she signed it over?" Elodie asked, not looking up. "Proper signature, proper date?"

"Signed it over, dated it, and wept on the paper for good measure." Fosco's mustache twitched, which was how he smiled. "Widow Ameline came in myself, cool as well-water. Said she'd carried it eleven months and her back wasn't what it was."

"Her back is fine. Her heart is fine. She just wants to be able to say his name without falling down in the market."

"Same thing," said Fosco.

It was not the same thing, but Elodie had learned long ago that arguing metaphysics with a fence was like arguing weather with a stone. She turned the ledger page and there it was, written in the widow's own hand because memory sold truer when the seller wrote the description herself: *Bertrand singing in the bath, badly, the winter before he died.*

That was the shape of the grief—not the death itself, which nobody could steal because death belongs to everyone—but the moment the grief lived in. The song. The bad notes. The water sloshing. The way she'd stood outside the bathroom door with laundry on her hip and thought, *I will remember this exact moment forever,* and then, eleven months later, discovered that remembering it felt like swallowing river stones.

People assumed thieves stole treasure. Elodie stole the moments around treasure. The wrapping paper. The candle smoke. The sound a door makes when someone you love comes home through it. Small recollections, lifted clean, leaving the rest of a life intact and even—this was the part she took pride in—improved.

She closed the ledger. The grief settled into her chest like a bird finding a familiar perch. Warm. Terribly sad. Singing badly.

"How much?" Fosco asked.

"For you? Eight silvers."

Fosco clutched his chest. "Eight! For a dead man's bathwater!"

"It's a good grief. Fresh grief, well-kept, single owner, no mildew. You'll get twelve from the charm-buyers up Copperlane, fourteen if you wait for the autumn festival when everybody gets sentimental about their dead." She held out her hand. "Six, then, since you're poor and suffering."

"Eight," Fosco said instantly, and counted the coins out with the reluctance of a man being robbed at his own funeral.

* * *

Elodie had three rules, and she recited them to herself the way other people recited prayers, which was to say irregularly, guiltily, and most often right after breaking the spirit of one.

Rule one: never take from the desperate. Desperation made memories thin and strange; grief taken from a drowning woman came apart in your hands like wet paper, and worse, it left the drowning woman with nothing left to hold. The desperate needed their sorrows. Their sorrows were load-bearing.

Rule two: never be seen. Vantrelle had outlawed magic so thoroughly that even the word had been filed off the signs, and what Elodie did was small magic, kitchen magic, thief's magic—the kind the wardens called *talent* in the same voice they used for tumors. Being seen meant the wards. Being seen meant questions. Being seen meant disappearing, and Elodie had developed a strong attachment to existing.

Rule three: never sell what isn't yours to sell.

She was thinking about rule three as she walked home through the Amber Quarter with eight silvers sewn into her hem, because rule three was the rule she broke every day without ever quite meaning to. Every charm she made took a thread from her own memory to bind it—a stitch of herself to hold the stolen thing together. A smell here. A color there. Seven years of small stitches, and lately she'd begun to notice the gaps. She couldn't remember her mother's face anymore, only the idea of a face. Couldn't remember the sound of her own laughter, though people told her she laughed. Somewhere under all the charms she'd sold was an Elodie-shaped hole wearing an Elodie coat, whistling past the emptiness.

She was so busy whistling past it that she nearly walked into the curfew crier.

"Sunset in one hour!" the man bawled, ringing his bell with more enthusiasm than accuracy, clipping a fruit stall's awning on the backswing. "All citizens indoors! Wardens abroad by dark! Unlicensed talent will be—" he squinted at his card, "—*addressed.*"

"Addressed," muttered a fishwife beside Elodie. "Like a letter."

"Like a summons," said her companion gloomily, and they both hurried their stalls shut.

Elodie pulled her hood up and cut down Tanner's Lane, where the light went amber and thick as honey this time of evening. This was her favorite hour, the hour she owned. Before curfew the city belonged to everyone; after it, to the wardens and their white lanterns. But this hour, the hour between, belonged to her alone—her shopping hour, her working hour, the hour in which eight silvers became flour and onions and a stub of tallow candle, and a stolen grief became somebody's comfort.

Because that was the secret of the trade, the part Fosco never understood: Elodie didn't sell grief to make people sad. She sold it to make them *less* sad. There were buyers in Copperlane—quiet women and quiet men, mostly old, mostly alone—who paid honest coin to feel, for one hour, one night, the exact shape of having loved someone. Widow Ameline's grief would go to some gray-haired gentleman who hadn't wept since his brother drowned thirty years ago, and he would hold it for an evening, the bad singing and the sloshing water and the laundry on the hip, and he would cry until his ribs ached, and then he would sleep like a child and wake feeling *clean*. Borrowed light, Maren called it. The lamp doesn't care whose oil it burns. It only cares that it shines.

Elodie had sold forty-one such charms in seven years. She kept count the way penitents keep sins.

* * *

Her room sat above a shuttered dyeworks on Sable Street, one window, one bed, one stove that worked if you spoke to it kindly and sometimes if you didn't. She climbed the outside stairs two at a time, checked the street both ways—rule two—and let herself in.

The grief was still with her. That was unusual. Usually a lifted memory rode quietly until bound into a charm, but this one had opinions. It sat in her chest and hummed a bar of Bertrand's bath-song, badly, and Elodie found her eyes stinging on her own threshold, which was ridiculous, because it wasn't *her* grief, it was inventory, it was eight silvers' worth of another woman's husband.

"All right, all right," she told it, shrugging off her cloak. "You'll be bound by midnight and gone by market. Don't get comfortable."

But she sat down at her work table instead of going straight to the binding, and she held the grief up to the last of the daylight, the way you'd hold a ring, and she thought about Widow Ameline walking out of Fosco's shop cool as well-water. Eleven months of carrying, traded away in an afternoon. And Ameline would feel lighter—Elodie guaranteed it, that was the craft of it—but lighter how? Lighter like a room with the furniture gone?

She looked around her own room and saw, with the particular clarity that comes at dusk, that she owned almost nothing. A bed. A pot. A table with grooves worn by hands that weren't hers—or were hers, once, before. Seven years she'd been in Vantrelle and she could not for the life of her remember arriving. Maren said she'd come up the south road with empty eyes and a talent for lifting and not one memory to her name, not even her own name—Maren had given her that, *Elodie*, plucked off a gravestone, and *Vasquez* off a wine crate. Everything before the walls: gone. Not stolen. Just *gone*, like a room swept before she ever saw what was in it.

Sometimes, at this hour, she pressed on that absence the way you press a bruise. Just to check it was still there. Still nothing. Still the itch under the scar of her, the sense that somewhere there was a girl who knew why the dark between the stars made her flinch.

"Bath-song," she said aloud, to stop the spiral, and the grief hummed obligingly.

Binding was simple work if you respected it. She lit the tallow stub, pricked her thumb—rule three's tax, one drop per charm—and let the grief flow through the wound into a twist of gray wool. It went reluctantly, the way a cat goes into a basket, and she talked to it while it went, the soft nonsense she talked to everything: *there you are, that's it, no one will hurt you, you're going to make an old man cry and it will be the kindest thing anyone's done for him in years.*

The charm cooled in her palm. Gray wool, unremarkable, warm as a living hand. Inside it, Bertrand sang badly forever, and somewhere across the city Widow Ameline was probably making supper without falling down, and both of those things were true at once, and only one of them cost anything.

Elodie stitched the charm's seam with ordinary thread, wrapped it in brown paper, and wrote on the paper, in her careful forger's hand: *For G., Copperlane. One hour. Do not take with spirits. Do not take twice.*

Then she counted her money—eight silvers minus flour, onions, candle, and the rent she owed the dyer's widow downstairs, which left four—and put three in the loose floorboard and one in her shoe, because a girl in her line learned early never to keep all her light in one lamp.

Outside, the sun touched the wall, and the curfew bell began its slow bronze counting.

*One,* said the bell.

She had time. Copperlane was fifteen minutes at a fast walk and the buyer, old Master Gervase, was always at his window at dusk like a plant that grew toward sadness. She could deliver, collect, and be home with ten minutes to spare—

*Two.*

—except that fast walks drew the eye, and hooded figures hurrying at dusk drew wardens, and there had been whispers all week of a sweep bigger than usual, white lanterns on every corner, conscription notices going up on the granary doors. *Talent census,* the whispers said. *They're counting us now.*

*Three.*

Elodie looked at the charm on her table, warm in its brown paper, and at the dying light on the wall, and made the calculation she always made: risk against coin, coin against rent, rent against the cold coming. Four silvers in her possession. Twelve owed by Friday.

*Four.*

"Five minutes to spare," she murmured, pulling up her hood, "or I'm a washerwoman going to her sister's," and she took the stairs down into the amber dark, quick and quiet and entirely invisible, exactly as she had done a thousand evenings before this one.

Which is why it annoyed her profoundly, three streets later, to hear boots fall into step behind her, and a woman's level voice say:

"Washerwomen don't walk on the balls of their feet."

* * *

The voice belonged to a warden's coat—white braid, high collar, the little silver starburst of the Captaincy—and to a woman perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with a face like a locked drawer. Two junior wardens hung back at the corner, pointedly studying a wall, which meant this was unofficial, which meant it was worse.

Elodie arranged her face into bafflement, the oldest armor. "Beg pardon, Captain?"

"You heard me." The warden's eyes went to the brown paper parcel. "Copperlane delivery. Charming. Give it here."

Every rule Elodie had screamed at once. Rule two, broken in open street. Rule three, about to be confiscated along with her thumbprint. And underneath them, older than rules, the animal certainty that you do not run from a warden-captain in a city of walls.

She held out the parcel.

Captain Reyne—Ilsa Reyne; Elodie knew the name the way you know the names of knives—took it, weighed it, and did not open it. Instead she looked at Elodie for a long moment with the expression of a woman reading a ledger and finding a figure that shouldn't exist.

"You don't remember me," Reyne said. "Seven years ago. South gate. A girl came up the road with nothing in her head but weather."

The bottom of the world dropped a half inch, the way it always did when someone mentioned the road, the gate, the nothing.

"I remember," Reyne went on, turning the parcel over in her gloved hands, "because my orders were to turn you back at the border, and I looked at you, and you looked at me, and you had *nothing behind your eyes*, girl. No fear. Fear needs a past to be afraid for." She tucked the charm into her own coat pocket, casually, a theft so smooth Elodie almost applauded. "You'll want this back. Later. If you're sensible."

The curfew bell rang five, somewhere above them, and the first white lanterns bloomed at the street ends like cold flowers.

"Go home, washerwoman," said Warden-Captain Ilsa Reyne. "And when the stars go out tonight—and they will go out, before morning—don't be surprised. You, of all people in this city, should know that everything falls when something is stolen from the foundation."

She walked away, boots measured, coat immaculate, leaving Elodie standing in the amber dark with one less charm, four silvers, and absolutely no memory of any south gate at all.
