## 2. Curfew Bells

There were three rules to surviving Vantrelle with your skin and most of your secrets intact, and Elodie had written them herself, in the manner of all great lawgivers: never take from the desperate, never be seen, and never—*never*—trust a man who whistles while he works.

Corvin whistled constantly.

She heard him now through the thin wall of their rooms above the dye-shop on Tanner's Row, some off-key thing about a miller's daughter that had no business being cheerful in a city where the curfew bells were due to ring in eleven minutes. Elodie counted her takings by feel rather than light—one of the advantages of selling things you carried inside your own skull was that the inventory weighed nothing and clinked not at all. A widow's grief, lifted fair from the pawnshop ledger where it had been pawned alongside a wedding ring. Two first kisses, slightly shopworn. The smell of rain on a farm road, which she'd been trying to move for three weeks because nobody in Vantrelle wanted to remember weather they could get for free.

The grief was the good piece. Widow Ameline had pawned it honestly enough—grief was legal tender in this city the way coin sometimes wasn't—but Elodie had held it only long enough to copy it into a charm-glass before giving it back, lighter than it went in, the way a midwife takes a weight without taking the child. That was the craft of it. Anyone could *take*. Taking was easy; taking was just theft with extra steps. What Elodie sold was the shape of a feeling without the teeth in it, and she charged accordingly.

The first bell rang.

It always sounded like a spoon dropped in an empty pot, the curfew bell, which seemed an undignified noise for a sound that meant *get indoors or be conscripted*, but there it was. Elodie blew out her candle out of habit even though she worked in the dark half the time anyway, and stood at the shuttered window listening to the city fold itself away. Shutters clacking. Dogs being called in. Somewhere on Cordwainer Street a drunk arguing with a warden about how the stars were still up so why wasn't he.

"The stars are always up," Elodie murmured to him through the wall, though he couldn't hear. "That's rather the point of them."

Then the second bell rang early.

She knew it immediately the way you know a wrong note in a song your mother sang—except she didn't have a mother's songs, did she, that was precisely the trouble, and yet her body knew anyway, some borrowed instinct lodged in her like a splinter. The second bell came a quarter hour after the first on any ordinary night. This one came on the heels of the first like a shove.

And under it, faint but unmistakable: boots. Many boots. In formation.

"Sweep," she said aloud, and Corvin's whistling stopped as if she'd cut a string.

He appeared in her doorway already pulling his coat on, a big grey-whiskered man built like a door that had been left open too long and learned to slouch. "Tanner's Row and Cordwainer both," he said. "I heard them turn off the Merchants' Bridge."

"You hear everything."

"I'm old. Old men's ears make up for other failures." He was already moving through the room, dousing embers, sliding the loose brick back over the hiding place where his own small hoard of charms lived. "Wardens don't sweep at night for pickpockets, girl. They sweep for talent."

Elodie's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "I'm not registered."

"Nobody is. That's what makes it a sweep." Corvin paused at the window, peering down into the alley with the expression of a man who has looked out of many windows ahead of many boots. "There's a place you can go. Maren's."

"Maren." Elodie kept her voice flat. "The fence who lives like a spider."

"The fence who keeps maps." He said *maps* the way other men said *gold*, and Elodie felt something prickle along her arms—not fear exactly, more like the moment before a sneeze, when your whole body knows something is coming that you can't see yet.

"Maps of what?"

"Streets, mostly." Corvin grinned at her, quick and crooked, the grin he used when he was deciding how much truth a given moment could carry. "And a few things underneath the streets. Go now, down the coal chute, along the drainage cut, third arch past the tannery sluice. Knock twice, then once, then wait like you're patient, which will be hard for you."

"Charming. Why aren't you coming?"

"Someone should be home when the wardens knock, looking respectable and confused. Confused is my best quality." He pressed a coin into her hand—an old silver bit with a hole drilled through it—and closed her fingers over it. "Show her that. She'll know who sent you."

"And if she asks who I am?"

"Tell her you're nobody from nowhere." His grin faltered, just at the edge, the way a table rocks when one leg is short. "You're very good at that."

He said it lightly. He always said things lightly. But Elodie stood in the dark of her room after he'd gone to play the confused old lodger downstairs, holding the drilled coin, and felt the words settle somewhere under her ribs like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It didn't come. There was no bottom to find, or none she could reach.

Seven years ago she had arrived at the gates of Vantrelle carrying nothing.

Not poor-nothing. Not runaway-nothing. *Nothing*-nothing. No name she could answer to, no face she recognized in the polished breastplate of the gate-warden who found her, no memory further back than the road itself, which ended at the walls the way a sentence ends at a period. The healers called it hollowing. The priests called it punishment. Corvin—who had been loitering near the gate with the professional air of a man waiting for lost property—called it luck, and took her in, and named her himself, Elodie, after a saint of lost things, "because you'll want a patron," he'd said, "and they don't come cheaper."

She had spent seven years filling the hollow with other people's memories. First kisses. Dead mothers' voices. Rain on farm roads. She handled them daily, wore their shapes against her mind like a dressmaker draped in cloth, and still the space behind her own eyes stayed exactly the size it was: blank, smooth, itching like a scar she couldn't stop touching. Other people's pasts fit her hands beautifully. Her own was a locked room in a house she didn't own.

The boots were getting closer. Elodie put the coin around her neck on its cord, took up her satchel of charm-glasses, and went down through the coal chute into the dark.

* * *

Vantrelle underground smelled of tallow, river, and secrets, which Elodie supposed was also a description of the city above. She moved through the drainage cut with her skirts hiked and her breath held against the stink, counting arches. One. Two. At the third she stopped, and the dark stopped with her, attentive.

Something lived in these tunnels besides rats and fences. Everyone said so and no one would say what. The dark down here had a quality of *listening* that made the hair rise on her neck, and twice—only twice, in seven years—she had the distinct sensation of being remembered by something that had never met her.

"Twice, then once," she whispered, and rapped the old iron door in the arch: knuckle, knuckle, pause, knuckle. Then she stood being patient, which was, as promised, difficult.

Long enough passed for her to compose and discard four separate explanations of her presence. Then a slot scraped open at eye height and a single brown eye regarded her with the frank appraisal of a horse trader.

"Coin," said the eye.

Elodie held up the drilled silver bit. The eye considered it, then her, then the empty tunnel behind her with a thoroughness that suggested the tunnel might contain anyone.

"Twice more," said the eye, and shut.

She knocked again. The door opened onto warmth and lamplight and the astonishing interior of Maren's establishment, which Corvin had described as a cellar and which was in fact a cellar the way an ocean is a puddle. Racks rose to the ceiling on every side, stuffed with rolled parchments, lacquered tubes, brass-bound cases, and objects whose purposes Elodie could not guess—a birdcage containing a single folded glove; a jar of keys labeled, ominously, *not yet*.

Maren herself sat behind a desk built from a ship's hatch cover, a broad woman of indeterminate age wrapped in shawls, with ink-stained fingers and the contented expression of someone surrounded by everything everyone else had lost.

"So," Maren said, before Elodie had finished deciding how to open. "Corvin's girl. The one who arrived empty." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd say *the tall one* or *the left-handed one*, and somehow that was worse. "Sit. You're dripping on my inventory."

"It's water."

"It's the Sluice Cut, and it's been through the tannery, and it's on a map I drew myself, so I know exactly what else it's been through." She shoved a stool across. "Boots off. Wardens are sweeping Cordwainer, Tanner's Row, and the Weavers' Stair. They'll work outward. You have perhaps two hours of safety and my hospitality, and I charge for both, so consider this the beginning of a tab."

Elodie sat. The lamp between them threw Maren's shadow huge across the map-racks, so that the woman seemed grafted to the walls, part of the architecture of paper. "Corvin said you keep maps."

"Of course I keep maps. Maps are the honest man's crime. Nobody outlaws geography, they just outlaw knowing it." Maren pulled a rack toward her and began, idly, rolling and unrolling a tube. "He also said, when he sent word ahead—which he did, the old spider, while you were still climbing out of a coal chute—that you'd ask questions. So ask."

Elodie had intended to say something dignified and noncommittal. What came out instead was the question that lived permanently behind her teeth, the scar-itch itself:

"What do you know about seven years ago?"

Maren's hands went still on the tube. For a moment the only sound was the lamp's small flame doing its patient work, and beneath it, far below, a hum Elodie had never noticed before—like a vast slow breath drawn through the bones of the mountain the city was built against.

"Everyone knows about seven years ago," Maren said at last. "Bad harvest. Wardens doubled the conscription. The Undercourt brokers got bold—came up into daylight markets wearing masks, buying souls and memories outright, paying in real gold. And then it stopped. All at once. Like a door closing." She unrolled the tube at last: a map of Vantrelle itself, but drawn strangely, the streets fine and exact, and beneath them, sketched in a different, older hand, passages that had no business existing—stairs going down into the mountain's root, and at the center of them all, drawn as a small coiled sigil, a shape like a sleeping dragon.

"That's a child's handwriting," Maren said quietly, tapping the lower annotations. "Whoever marked these under-passages wrote like a child. Seven years ago, give or take." She looked up, and her brown eyes were not unkind, which made them unbearable. "Funny thing, isn't it. A city full of people trading memories back and forth, and the only person in Vantrelle who arrived with none at all shows up the same season every broker in the Undercourt goes quiet."

The itch behind Elodie's eyes became a burn. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I sell maps, not meanings." Maren began rolling the tube again, briskly, as if she hadn't just reached into Elodie's chest and rearranged the furniture. "But I'll tell you something for free, since you're dripping on my floor. Those wardens out there tonight—they're not sweeping for talent to arrest. My runners say the conscription orders changed this week. New phrasing. They're not looking for criminals anymore." She tapped the little coiled dragon at the heart of the mountain. "They're looking for a key."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of earth and history, the curfew bells began ringing a third time—the all-clear, hours early, the sweep called off as suddenly as it had begun.

Neither of them moved.

"Wardens don't stop a sweep halfway," Maren said softly. "Unless they found what they were looking for."

Elodie's hand had risen without her permission to the cord at her neck, fingers closed around Corvin's drilled coin, and the blank space behind her eyes itched like a wound the whole world had decided to scratch at once.
