## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Elodie was counting a widow's grief into threes when the first star went out.

She didn't notice it at once. The pawnshop back room had no windows, only a candle stub and the smell of old brass, and she was doing delicate work—unspooling the memory of a dead husband's laugh from its wax paper wrapping without letting it fray. Grief-work paid poorly but reliably. People clung to their sorrow like it was the last warm coat of a cold year.

The second star went out, and every charm in the room rang at once.

It wasn't loud. That was the terrible part. It was like a hundred wine glasses humming in sympathy, a thin silver note that came up through the floorboards and through her teeth and settled somewhere behind her eyes where she kept the inventory of everything she'd ever stolen. She looked down at her hands. The widow's grief sat in her palm glowing faintly amber—the color of borrowed things—and then, one thread at a time, it went dark.

"Ah," said Elodie Joubert, to no one, in the tone of a woman watching a cart lose a wheel. "That's not good."

She blew out the candle on instinct, which was absurd, because what came next had nothing to do with light.

* * *

The third star did not go out quietly. Nor the fourth. They guttered like lamps in a draft—one moment burning, the next pinched to nothing—and each extinguishment made a sound Elodie felt in her sternum before she heard it: a high crystalline *crack*, like ice letting go of a pond, like the world's most expensive window breaking somewhere very far above.

She climbed the pawnshop stairs two at a time and shouldered open the roof hatch into a night that was wrong in a way her body understood before her mind agreed to look.

The sky over Vantrelle was empty.

Not clouded. Not misted. *Empty.* She knew the city's stars the way other people knew their neighbors' faces—the Shepherd's Crook hanging low over the western wall, the pale sister-pair the fishwives called the Twins, the slow red wanderer that meant the herring boats were late. All of them gone. All of them, all at once, as though someone had reached up and picked the sky clean like a plate of plums.

And beneath the vanished stars, along the whole circuit of Vantrelle's walls, the wards were falling.

You couldn't normally see wards. That was rather the point of them—they were the city's held breath, four hundred years of woven law keeping out everything that had been outlawed, which in Vantrelle's case was nearly everything: magic, mostly, and the things magic attracted, and the memories of anyone who'd ever practiced it. But dying wards showed themselves the way dying men show their bones. Elodie watched the light peel off the stones in long silver ribbons, drifting upward and apart like smoke deciding it had better places to be, and she heard—not felt this time, *heard*—the sound of the border going down.

It was exactly like breaking glass. A thousand miles of it.

Then silence, which was worse, and then Vantrelle began to scream.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about panic: it has a texture. Elodie had lived seven years inside Vantrelle's walls and she had learned the city's moods the way a pickpocket learns pockets. There was market-day bustle, coarse and cheerful. There was curfew fear, tight and private, doors closing one by one. There was even, occasionally, festival joy, which from a distance sounded remarkably like riot.

This was none of those. This was a thousand doors opening at once.

She came down off the roof into streets already flooding with nightshirts and half-buckled boots. People stood in their doorways staring upward, mouths open, and when they found nothing there to see they turned to each other instead, and what passed between neighbors in the dark was not comfort. An old man was beating his fists against his own doorframe and shouting at the wood as if the wood had personally lied to him. A woman ran past carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, and Elodie thought, distantly, that she would remember that image for the rest of her life, whatever length that turned out to be.

"The wards," someone said, over and over, like a prayer with the theology stripped out. "The wards are down. The wards are down."

"Well spotted," Elodie muttered. Her hands were shaking. She noticed it with a kind of clinical annoyance, the way you notice a blister forming during an emergency you have no time for. Seven years she had survived this city by being small, quick, forgettable—a smudge on the ledger of Vantrelle, a girl-shaped absence. She had rules. Never take from the desperate. Never be seen. Never, ever be interesting.

The stars had just made her extremely interesting.

Because here was the thing, the thing she had been not-thinking-about since the day she'd arrived at the walls with an empty head and a talent she hadn't asked for: Elodie could feel the fallen wards the way you feel a tooth after the dentist. A missing-ness. And threaded through that missing-ness, faint as smoke, she could feel *everything on the other side of it*—a vast dark attention turning toward the city like a wolf noticing a gate left open.

Her talent had never once done anything she'd wanted it to. But it had always, always told her the truth.

"They'll come through by morning," she whispered, and then hated herself for saying it aloud, because a boy of perhaps ten was standing right beside her in the crowd, and he heard, and his face did the thing children's faces do when adults confirm their worst suspicions.

"Come through where?" he asked.

"Nowhere, sweetheart." She crouched, dug a river stone out of her pocket—one of her honest stock, smooth and grey and utterly ordinary—and pressed it into his hand. "Hold this. It's lucky. I stole it myself, so you know it's good."

He laughed, one wet hiccup of a laugh, and ran back toward his mother, and Elodie straightened up feeling marginally less like the end of the world, which she suspected was the entire business model of river stones.

* * *

The wardens arrived at the hour bell, and they arrived conscripting.

She saw the first press-gang from the mouth of Cooper's Lane: six warden-lanterns bobbing through the panicked crowds, and behind them a warden-sergeant with a voice like a dropped anvil calling out the words that had been outlawed so long they sounded almost foreign.

"*Talent-tithing! By order of the Council of Nine! Any person possessing talent will present themselves for service to the city!*"

A few people actually laughed. You could hear the laughter die as it spread, the way a joke dies when everyone realizes the teller isn't smiling.

"Service," said a dyer's apprentice near Elodie, bitterly, to no one. "They outlawed us for forty years and now we're *service*."

"They outlawed the practice," corrected his companion. "Not the practitioners. There's a difference."

"There's a difference to *them*, maybe."

Elodie pulled her hood lower and began, very calmly, to move in the opposite direction of the lanterns. This required navigating against the current of the crowd, which was like swimming upstream through a river of elbows. She had a plan, and the plan was Maren—Maren who kept illegal maps and illegal brandy in roughly equal quantities, Maren whose cellar door opened onto three different streets depending on which lie you knocked with.

She was two turns from safety when the net went up.

Literally. Warden-issue catching nets, iron-weighted, dropped from the rooftops over the narrow lane ahead, and beneath them a line of wardens with lanterns raised and a young officer reading from a scroll with the doomed solemnity of a man reading his own death warrant.

"—any person possessing talent, being the sensing, shifting, holding, or *taking* of memory or matter contrary to natural law—"

"I have a talent," announced a drunk two feet from Elodie, helpfully, "for finding my way home," and walked directly into the net.

Chaos is a thief's native weather. Elodie had grown up—inasmuch as she had grown up anywhere—in chaos, and she knew its currents: go where people aren't looking, move when others freeze, freeze when others move. The drunk's capture bought her thirty seconds of warden attention and she spent them climbing a drainpipe with the unglamorous efficiency of a cat leaving a bath.

From the rooftop she looked back once. In the lantern-light below, the wardens were binding the drunk's wrists with a strip of pale cloth, and the cloth, she realized with a lurch, was *inscribed*. Ward-sigils, fresh-inked, the kind that suppressed. The kind that had been illegal to manufacture until approximately two hours ago.

Laws, it turned out, were very fast when they were frightened.

She ran.

* * *

Maren's cellar took some knocking-for. The first door was a chimney. The second was a very surprised tanner's family eating supper. The third was Maren, holding a cleaver in one hand and a bottle in the other, apparently undecided which problem to solve first.

"Oh, it's you." She lowered the cleaver, kept the bottle. "I'd heard the sky fell. Come in before something else does."

The cellar was low-ceilinged and lampless except for a brazier, and it was, as always, full of maps—maps of Vantrelle sewn into coats, printed on handkerchiefs, drawn in ink that only appeared when breathed upon. Maren was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and had the calmest face in a panicking city, which Elodie had privately concluded was because Maren had already survived several apocalypses and found them repetitive.

"Stars out," Maren said, pouring two fingers into a clay cup and handing it across. "Wards down. Wardens grabbing anyone who can do sums above their fingers. And here you come through my third door, which means you used the knock for *trouble*. So." She sat down on a crate with the sigh of a woman settling in for a story. "Which are you? Trouble, or trouble?"

"Maren." Elodie wrapped both hands around the cup without drinking. The brandy trembled; she watched it tremble, fascinated, as though it belonged to someone else. "The wards didn't just fall. I could—" She stopped. Started again. "I felt them go. All of them. Like something was pulled *out* of the walls."

Something shifted behind Maren's calm. Not much. A hairline crack in old plaster.

"Pulled out," Maren repeated.

"Keyed. Like a lock keyed to a single thing, and someone took the thing away." Elodie finally drank. The brandy burned a clean line down her chest and stopped, usefully, right where the fear lived. "And Maren—I've never told anyone this, so drink with me while I say it. I came to these walls seven years ago tonight. You know that much. What you don't know is that I came with *nothing*. No name I recognized. No mother's face. Not one single memory before the road. Whatever I was before age fourteen, somebody took it, whole, before I ever saw these gates."

The brazier popped. Somewhere far above, another piece of the sky's silence settled over the city.

"And your talent woke up empty-handed," Maren said slowly, "in a city that trades in memories. And you've spent seven years handling other people's stolen pasts, and it never once occurred to you to wonder where yours went."

"It occurred to me," Elodie said. "It occurred to me every single night. I just—" Her voice did something humiliating and small. She pressed on. "I decided whoever took it must have had a reason. That's the stupidest sentence I've ever spoken aloud, and once I sold a man a memory of himself apologizing to his wife."

Maren laughed despite herself, then reached over and gripped Elodie's wrist hard enough to leave marks. "Listen to me, girl. Tonight the whole city learned what it feels like to have something taken. Every soul out there is looking at an empty sky thinking *mine, mine, give it back.*" Her grip tightened. "If your missing past is what held those wards—and I've moved enough illegal freight under those wards to say the wards were always odd, always quiet, always listening—then whoever holds it holds more than your childhood. They hold the border. And they're going to want to negotiate."

"With whom?"

"With the city." Maren's eyes flicked upward, toward the street, toward the sound of warden-boots passing overhead. "Or with whoever's desperate enough to go down and get it back."

The word *down* sat in the cellar like a stone dropped in a well. Elodie waited for it to hit bottom. It didn't.

* * *

She left before dawn, because dawn was when the press-gangs changed shift and the gaps between them opened like shop shutters. At the cellar door Maren pressed a folded map into her palm—breath-ink, the real streets underneath the city's polite ones—and said only, "Third door's trouble. Fourth door's mine. Don't use the fourth unless it's the end of everything."

Outside, Vantrelle was grey and sleepless. Fires burned in barrels at the corners where people had gathered to stand watch against a threat coming from everywhere and nowhere. On a wall near the Tanner's Gate, someone had chalked, in enormous letters:

**GIVE BACK OUR STARS**

Below it, smaller, shakier: **give back our dead. give back our mothers. give back what was taken.**

Elodie stood in front of the chalk for a long time. Around her, the city that had outlawed memory-magic was discovering that it had been living inside a spell all along—that its peace had a price tag, and the price had come due, and the collector was standing at the border with the gate swinging open.

She felt the terror, yes. It sat in her stomach like swallowed river water.

But underneath it, shameful and bright as a coin in a beggar's cup, was the other thing. The exhilaration. For seven years she had been invisible on purpose—small, quick, forgettable, a smudge. Now the whole sky had broken, and every warden, every broker, every soul in Vantrelle was hunting for the same missing thing she'd spent seven years pretending not to miss. She was no longer a smudge. She was a clue.

*Someone out there has my childhood,* she thought, watching the empty stars' place like a wound in the world. *And tonight, everyone in this city needs exactly what I need.*

It was the first time in seven years that wanting something had felt like having a purpose, and she hated how good it felt, and she let herself feel it anyway.

Behind her, boots on cobbles—deliberate, unhurried, too controlled for a panicked night. Elodie turned.

A warden-captain stood at the end of the lane, cloak dark, insignia polished, lantern held low. Not chasing. Not shouting. Just watching her, with the particular patience of someone who had been looking for a specific girl all night and had finally, at last, found her.
