## 4. What the Warden Knew

Ilsa Reyne had been a warden for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about the people of Vantelle.

First: they would lie about anything. Second: they would lie especially about magic. And third — the lesson that had taken her longest, and cost her the most — so would she.

She stood now at the mouth of Tanner's Row with a lantern in one hand and a conscription warrant in the other, and she was lying to herself very competently indeed. The lie went something like this: *I am doing my duty. The city is frightened. Frightened cities need order.*

The truth went something like this: the stars had gone out four hours ago, the border wards had fallen with a sound she still heard when she closed her eyes — like the whole sky being dropped from a great height onto glass — and Ilsa Reyne had read the sealed file. All of it. Twice.

The file was called *Ledger Reference 9-Kestrel*, and it lived in a locked drawer beneath her desk that officially did not exist, and it said, in the dry bureaucratic handwriting of a clerk thirty years dead, that the wards of Vantelle were not keyed to stone or blood or law, as the founding statutes claimed. They were keyed to a memory. A stolen one. A child's memory, bought from a broker and pressed into the ward-stones like a flower into a hymnal, because some long-dead magistrate had discovered that grief held borders better than granite ever could.

*Subject: female, approx. age seven. Acquisition: voluntary sale by guardian. Content: withheld by order of the Undercourt liaison. Note:* — and here Ilsa had underlined it so many times the ink had bled — *do not let the subject learn what the city is built on.*

The subject would be twenty-one now. Give or take.

"Captain." One of her wardens, a young man named Pell whose helmet was perpetually too large for him, came jogging up through the smoke-haze. "Row's clear. Everyone's either sheltered or run. Except—" he consulted his slate, "—there's movement on the rooftops. Small figure. Moving fast."

"On the rooftops," Ilsa repeated.

"Yes, Captain."

"During a curfew. During *this* curfew."

"Yes, Captain."

Ilsa looked up. The sky over Vantelle was black in a way skies were not supposed to be black — no stars, no moon-glow, nothing but a smooth dark like the inside of a closed lid. People kept looking up and flinching, as if they expected the dark to look back.

A small figure on the rooftops, moving fast, during the worst night in living memory.

"Well," Ilsa said, handing Pell the warrant. "Somebody's having an interesting evening. Come along. We'll go have a word."

* * *

Elodie Joubert — though Ilsa did not yet know the name, only the file number — moved across the tiles above Cordwainer Street like a cat that had been paid to be somewhere else. She was quick, quiet, and careful, and if Ilsa had been anyone else, she might have lost her entirely.

But Ilsa had spent nineteen years learning this city's rooftops the way other people learned scripture, and she knew the shortcut the girl didn't: the gap behind the bellfounder's chimney where two roofs almost touched, but not quite. Almost was a cruel word on rooftops.

The girl hit the gap at full sprint, realized mid-leap that it was wider than it looked, made a sound Ilsa's report would later describe as "a brief vocalization," and landed — barely, gracelessly, with one hand slapping wet slate — directly into the circle of Ilsa's lantern light.

For a moment they simply regarded each other. The girl crouched low, coiled to bolt. Ilsa stood very still, the way you stand near a bird you'd rather not lose.

"Don't," Ilsa said.

"I wasn't going to do anything," the girl said.

"You're on a roof. During curfew. On the night every star in the sky died. You were absolutely going to do something."

"That's just — context," the girl said, and there it was, the thing Ilsa had been waiting nineteen years to hear again: the particular music of a liar with good instincts. "Context isn't a crime."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "It's just usually where the crime lives." She took a slow step closer, boots finding the dry patches of slate out of long habit. "Pell's down on the street. If you run, he'll shout, and then there will be six more wardens here inside a minute, and you'll spend the rest of this historic evening in a holding cell answering questions about where you were going in such a hurry."

The girl's eyes flicked past her — calculating distances, exits, odds. Ilsa recognized the arithmetic. She'd worn the same face at fourteen, standing in front of her own recruiting sergeant, deciding how much trouble she could afford.

"And if I don't run?" the girl asked.

"Then we talk. Just talk. Warden's honor."

"What's warden's honor worth?"

Ilsa considered this honestly. "On most nights? Less than the paper it's written on. Tonight?" She glanced upward at the starless dark. "Tonight I'd say it's the only currency left that hasn't collapsed."

Something shifted in the girl's expression — not trust, nothing so generous, but the wary recalibration of someone who has decided a conversation might be cheaper than an escape. She straightened slightly. Up close, Ilsa could see details: ink stains on her fingers, a charm-seller's calluses, a face that was trying very hard to be unremarkable and failing because of the eyes. The eyes kept moving. Counting things. Thieves' eyes. Her mother had had eyes like that, before the fever took her, back when Ilsa still believed the law was a wall and not, as she now suspected, a curtain.

"Name," Ilsa said.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. It's just not yours."

That earned her the ghost of a grin — quickly suppressed, but Ilsa caught it, and something in her chest loosened half a turn. *Good,* she thought. *A professional. Professionals can be reasoned with. It's the amateurs who get everyone killed.*

"Elodie," the girl said finally. Just that. Watching Ilsa's face with the intensity of someone waiting for a match to strike.

And Ilsa — who had rehearsed this moment in her head for eleven days, since the night the wards first stuttered and the file came off its shelf — kept her own face perfectly, bureaucratically blank, and said:

"Pleasure. Now walk with me, Elodie. We need to discuss what you stole last week from the pawnshop ledger on Tanner's Row."

* * *

They walked, because walking was better than standing, and standing was better than running, and everything was better than the alternative conversation Ilsa refused to start until she understood who she was talking to.

"A widow's grief," Elodie said. "Off Marla Voss's ledger. But I paid for it — well, I left compensation. A memory of her husband laughing, which I'd lifted off a neighbor years ago and never sold. Fair trade. Better than fair."

"You lift memories."

"I *trade* them. Lifting implies leaving a hole. I leave change." The girl said it with pride, and underneath the pride Ilsa heard the thin wire of something else — the sound of a person reciting rules they'd built to keep themselves from becoming what everyone already assumed they were.

"And the widow's grief you sold onward."

"To a woman on Gilt Lane whose son drowned. She wanted to know what grief felt like so she'd stop feeling guilty that hers felt wrong." Elodie shrugged, and the shrug didn't quite work; one shoulder stayed high, defensive. "Grief's grief. It fits any shape eventually. I just help it find the right door."

Ilsa stopped walking. They had come out onto the aqueduct walkway above the tanners' yards, and below them the city lay in the dark — real dark, honest dark, the kind Vantelle hadn't known since before Ilsa was born. Lanterns everywhere. Whole streets lit like festivals, except nobody was celebrating; the light poured out of windows like people shouting into a well, trying to hear an echo.

"I'm going to tell you something now," Ilsa said, "and then you're going to decide whether to keep lying to me. Understand: I don't care about the pawnshop. I don't care about your little trades. There are forty-three known talents in this city, and tonight the Magistrate's Council wants every one of them in irons and on a cart by dawn. You are currently the only one of those forty-three who isn't in irons, and that is because I have been looking for *you specifically* for eleven days, and I found you first."

Elodie had gone very still. Stillness, Ilsa noted, was clearly this girl's native language.

"Why you specifically?"

"Because of the wards." Ilsa turned to face her fully. Below them, a church bell began ringing the emergency peal — nine strokes, pause, nine strokes — the city's oldest alarm, older than the walls themselves, and tonight it sounded less like a warning than like a heartbeat trying to remember its rhythm. "You know what the wards are, Elodie. Everyone thinks they're stone-work and old law. Founders' craft. Unbreakable. I believed that too, for nineteen years, right up until I opened a file I wasn't supposed to know existed."

"The wards fell," Elodie said quietly. "Two nights ago. I heard it."

"They didn't fall. They *starved*. There is a thing at the heart of the ward-stones — a keystone, the clerks called it. Not a stone. A memory. A specific memory, taken from a specific person, pressed into the foundation the way you'd press a seal into wax. While that memory lives, the wards hold. When it began to fade—" Ilsa spread her hands at the starless sky, the gesture encompassing the dark, the panic, the bells, the whole unraveling night, "—everything built on it started coming apart. Stars first. Then the border. Then, in a month or two, if nobody acts, the walls themselves. And outside the walls, Elodie, is the Veil-march, and the things in the Veil-march have been waiting a very long time for the city to stop remembering itself."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a dog barked at the empty sky, which struck Ilsa as the most sensible response anyone in Vantelle had managed all night.

"So find another memory," Elodie said. "Press a new one in. That's how the trade works — anything can be replaced."

"It's not that kind of lock." Ilsa heard her own voice flatten, the way it did when she was carrying something heavy and didn't want her arms to shake. "The keystone isn't just *a* memory. It's a matched memory. Taken young. Taken whole. The ward-stones grew around it like a tree around a nail. You can't swap it any more than you can give a tree a new heartwood." She paused. "And there's exactly one person in the world whose mind it fits."

She watched the girl arrive at it. Watched the arithmetic move behind those counting eyes — watched Elodie Joubert understand, all at once, why a warden-captain had spent eleven days hunting a petty charm-thief instead of arresting her.

"No," Elodie said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"There's no version of this offer I—"

"You arrived at these walls seven years ago," Ilsa said, and the words came out level and cold and precise, each one placed like a stone in a wall, because if she let any feeling into them the whole structure would come down. "No records. No family. No memories — the intake wardens logged it, a child of fourteen with a head like an empty room. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how many files I cross-referenced before I found the intake note that matched the acquisition date in Ledger 9-Kestrel?"

Elodie's hand had risen without her seeming to notice, two fingers pressed to her temple, the gesture of a woman touching an old bruise. Ilsa had seen it in the mirror often enough lately.

"You're saying—" Elodie's voice cracked, steadied. "You're saying there's a hole in me. Seven years wide. And the city's been standing in it."

"I'm saying the founders built the safest city in the world," Ilsa said, "and they built it out of a stolen child. And I am saying that I have served that law my entire adult life, and I have told myself it protects people, and I have *almost*, on most nights, nearly believed it." She looked down at the lantern-lit streets, the frightened lights, all that human noise in the dark. "And I am telling you, warden to thief, that I would rather burn my own badge than put you in irons and press you into a wall like a seal in wax. Which is why you're not arrested. Which is why we're having this conversation on a rooftop instead of in a cell."

"Then what *do* you want?"

"The keystone doesn't have to fade," Ilsa said. "It was *taken*. Which means it can be reached. The trail runs down, Elodie — beneath the mountain, to whoever bought it seven years ago. The Council won't act; the Council's answer to everything is chains and carts. But I hold your freedom in one hand and a blind eye in the other, and I'm offering you both. Go down. Find what they took. Bring it back — bring *yourself* back — and the wards rise, and the stars return, and maybe, if there's any justice left in this rotten beautiful city, somebody finally remembers what we outlawed and why."

Elodie stared at her. In the lantern light her face had gone through shock and out the other side into something harder and brighter, and Ilsa thought: *there it is. Purpose. Ugly, necessary thing.*

"One question," Elodie said at last.

"One."

"If my memory is the keystone — if it's been feeding the walls for seven years—" her jaw tightened, "—then whoever sold it knew what it was for. A guardian doesn't sell a child's whole childhood by accident. Someone brokered it. Someone *chose*."

"Yes," Ilsa said.

"And you know who."

Ilsa hesitated. This was the part she had not rehearsed, because this was the part that felt, even now, like stepping off a roof.

"The acquisition record lists a seller," she said. "Name of Corvin. No surname — brokers rarely use them. He ran a memory-house off Saltpan Lane until three years ago. He's still in the city. My people lost him after the wards fell, which means either he's hiding very well—"

"Or he's not hiding at all," Elodie said softly. Something in her voice made Ilsa glad, briefly, that she was not the person Elodie was thinking about. "Saltpan Lane. The blue door with the brass knocker shaped like a moth."

Ilsa blinked. "You know him."

Elodie was already turning toward the roof's edge, already calculating, and when she answered, her voice had changed — flattened into that same cold precision Ilsa knew from her own mirror, the voice of someone loading a weight they intended to carry alone.

"Know him?" A short, terrible laugh. "Warden-Captain, he raised me. Fed me, taught me the trade, tucked me in at night — right up until the day I woke up empty and he told me I'd always been that way."

She stepped off the roof edge onto the aqueduct rail, balanced there a moment against the vast dark, a small bright figure above a city full of frightened lights.

"Tell your Council whatever keeps them off my back," Elodie said. "I'm going to have a family reunion."

And she was gone — down the far side, quick and silent as spilled water — leaving Ilsa Reyne alone on the walkway with her lantern, her blind eye, and the growing, cold certainty that she had just set something loose in Vantelle that the law had never once been built to contain.

Below her, faintly, the bells rang on.

Above her, the sky stayed empty.

And somewhere between the two, a thief walked toward the only father she'd ever known, carrying seven hollow years and a question that had just grown teeth.

* * *

Ilsa stood a while longer, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all night.

One stolen child. One lying foster-father. One court beneath the mountain that the Council insisted did not exist, on a file the Council insisted she destroy. And herself — Warden-Captain Ilsa Reyne, nineteen years of service, one forged transit pass already burning a hole in her breast pocket, because she had known, even as she handed the girl her freedom, that she was not going to sit in an office and wait to hear how it ended.

"Pell," she said, when the young warden came puffing up the stair. "Change of plans. Fetch my kit. And Pell — the ledgers, the conscription warrants, the Council orders from tonight. Lose them."

"Lose them, Captain?"

"Misplace them. Accidentally. Creatively." She snuffed the lantern. "We've been enforcing the law all wrong, Pell. Tonight we're going to try serving it instead."
