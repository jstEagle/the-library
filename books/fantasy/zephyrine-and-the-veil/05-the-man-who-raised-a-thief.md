## 5. The Man Who Raised a Thief

Corvin's shop sat at the crooked end of Tanners' Row, wedged between a chandler that had burned down twice and a locksmith who had never once been robbed, which in Vantrelle was considered either a miracle or an indictment. The sign above the door read CORVIN & SONS — ANTIQUITIES, and there were no sons, and had never been sons, and the antiquities were mostly lies. It was the first joke Elodie ever learned, and it still caught in her chest like a fishbone.

She stood across the street for a long moment, watching lamplight wobble behind the shuttered windows. The stars were still gone. Three days now, and the sky above the city was a smooth black slate, and everyone walked with their shoulders up around their ears as if the darkness might pour down on them like water. Wardens moved in packs of six instead of pairs. Somewhere to the east, a bell that nobody had rung in forty years had begun tolling on its own, and the Church insisted this was fine.

Elodie pulled her hood lower and crossed the street.

The bell over the door did not ring — Corvin had filed the clapper out of it years ago, on the principle that customers should be able to leave without being counted. Inside, the shop smelled of beeswax and old paper and the particular dust of things that had been handled too much by too many hands. Shelves leaned against shelves in mutual exhaustion. A stuffed heron presided over the counter with one glass eye aimed eternally at the door.

"Closed," said a voice from behind a stack of crates, and then Corvin rose into view like something surfacing from a pond — tall and stooped at once, gray-stubbled, his reading spectacles pushed up into wild hair. "Closed unless you're buying, in which case also closed, but politely."

"It's me."

He went very still. It lasted perhaps half a second, and then he smiled, wide and easy, the smile he'd used on every marksman and magistrate who'd ever come through that door. "Elodie! In broad— well. In dark. To what do I owe—"

"Don't." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. Good. Flat was useful. Flat didn't shake. "The back room. Now."

The smile flickered — not gone, but recalibrated, the way a card sharp recalibrates when someone in the game turns out to be counting. He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly and lift the counter hatch.

* * *

The back room hadn't changed since she was small enough to sleep under its table. Same iron stove, same kettle with the dented belly, same map of Vantrelle pinned to the wall with seventeen years of pins marking nothing anyone else could read. She used to think the map was a treasure map. When she was eight, Corvin told her it marked all the places he'd been happy. She found out at eleven that every pin sat on top of a house he'd burgled. She had laughed then. She could not remember what laughing at Corvin felt like, which struck her now as its own kind of theft.

He filled the kettle without asking, set it on the stove, and sat down across from her with his hands flat on the table like a man surrendering preemptively.

"You've heard," she said.

"I've heard the stars went out. I've heard the wards fell. I've heard they're conscripting talent off the streets, which is why I'm surprised to find you above ground, let alone visiting your dear old da." He said *dear old da* lightly, but his hands stayed flat on the table. "What have you heard?"

"That the wards were keyed to a memory." She kept her eyes on him. "That someone sold it to a broker. That the broker took it below, to the Undercourt, where it's been holding up the whole border for seven years." She paused. "And I heard whose memory it was."

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Neither of them moved to take it off.

"Whoever told you that," Corvin said carefully, "told you true, most likely. And they told you it was yours."

"Say it."

"Elodie—"

"*Say it.*"

He took off the spectacles. He cleaned them on his sleeve, which was what he did when he was buying time, which was what he was always doing; the man treated every conversation as a negotiation with an exit hidden somewhere in the middle of it. Then he put them back on and looked at her, and for once there was no smile anywhere about him.

"Yes," he said. "I sold it."

There it was. She had carried the sentence across the city like contraband, braced against it, and hearing it aloud still knocked something loose in her chest that rattled all the way down. She gripped the edge of the table.

"When?"

"Seven years ago. You came to me in—" His mouth twisted. "You know when. You arrived at the walls with nothing. Not a name you trusted, not a face you remembered. You were fourteen and empty as a blown egg, and I took you in because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because taking people in is what I do. It's how I justify the rest of it."

"Don't dress it. Just tell me what happened."

The kettle whistled properly now, shrill and insistent. Corvin got up, lifted it off the heat, and stood with his back to her for a moment longer than the task required.

"A broker came to me. Winter after you arrived. Woman, gray gloves, smelled of smoke though nothing around her burned. She said the court beneath the mountain wanted a memory of a certain kind — a childhood one, whole and unbroken, belonging to someone with talent in the taking. She said the wards needed a keystone and only that kind would hold. And she said—" his voice dropped, went rough— "she said she knew where such a memory could be found. Yours. She offered a price, and I said no, and I want you to believe me when I tell you I said no, Elodie, I said no so hard I broke two of her fingers doing it, and she thanked me for the demonstration."

"Get to it."

"The debt." He turned around. He looked older than he had an hour ago, sitting back down, as if the walk to the stove had cost him years. "You remember Maren's ledgers? No, of course you don't, you weren't there. Before you, girl, before you existed for me, I ran jobs for a man named Oskar Veyle. Fencing, mostly. Then he asked for muscle, then worse, and I got out the only way a man gets out of Veyle's books — badly. I owed him. Seven years ago he decided he was done waiting, and he put a knife to the throat of everything I loved, which by then was you, and the shop, and the stupid heron. One night. That was all he needed to make us dead. And the broker came back that same night and said: one night of stars for one night of survival."

Elodie felt the words land one at a time, like coins dropping into a deep well.

"You traded my childhood," she said, "for a night."

"I traded your childhood for your *life*. For both our lives. And I have spent seven years telling myself those were different things." He laughed, short and terrible. "They're not. I know they're not. But you were standing right there, girl, asleep upstairs, breathing, and Veyle's men were three streets off, and I thought — she'll never miss it. She doesn't even have it. How do you miss a thing that's already gone?"

The rage arrived then, and it was strange: not hot but enormous, room-filling, with nowhere to go. There was no law for this. There was no court in Vantrelle that would hear *he stole my memory*, because memories weren't property, weren't protected, weren't anything at all except the currency of desperate people, and every ledger in the city would balance exactly the way Corvin's had. She wanted to break something. The only thing in reach worth breaking was him, and she found — this surprised her — that she couldn't. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

"You could have told me," she said quietly.

"And give you a scar you couldn't sell or spend? What good—"

"You could have *told me*. I've spent seven years scratching at the inside of my own head wondering why there's nothing there. Do you know what that's like? Walking around with a hole where a person ought to be? I built myself out of other people's leftovers, Corvin. First kisses. Dead mothers' voices. I know what a hundred strangers' childhoods feel like and I don't know my own *name*—"

"You chose your name," he said. "I watched you choose it. You were fifteen, and you stood in front of that mirror Maren keeps, and you tried six names out loud, and when you said *Elodie* you stood up straighter. That wasn't stolen. That one's yours. I've held onto that the way a miser holds gold, and you can hate me for everything else, but that one I gave you honestly."

Her eyes were burning. She refused to let them do anything about it.

"The stars," she said instead, when she could trust her voice. "The broker said the wards needed a keystone. So the wards were always going to fall? Or did they fall because—"

"Because everything ends," Corvin said. "Dragon-fire doesn't burn forever. Whatever bargain that court struck, it was dying anyway, keystone or no. The broker knew it. I suspect the queen knows it best of all." He hesitated. "Which brings us to the part I like least."

"There's a part you like least than confessing you sold me?"

"There's a rumor moving through the fences' network faster than any gossip has a right to. That the Undercourt is expecting someone. That the queen's brokers have been leaving cards in the pockets of the city for years — stitched into shawls, tucked into pawned coats — cards with a dragon on them, addressed to a thief with talent in the taking." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and furious, and afraid, and she realized with a lurch that he had been afraid for her since the moment the stars went out. "They're not just expecting *someone*, Elodie. They're expecting *you*. They always were. I think maybe I sold them the memory, but they were fishing for the thief."

The stove ticked. Outside, far off, the mad bell tolled again, patient as tide.

"Why?" she whispered. "What does a soul-broker queen want with me?"

"What does anyone want with a thief?" Corvin gave her the ghost of the old smile, the one that had taught her locks and ledgers and the difference between luck and timing. "Something worth stealing."

* * *

They drank the tea. It seemed absurd, drinking tea, but Corvin poured it the way he always had — too strong, oversweetened, the cup set down in front of her with a small ceremonial thump as if to say *the world is ending but the kettle works*. She wrapped her hands around the warmth and hated how much it helped.

"Where are the cards?" she asked.

"Hm?"

"The calling cards. Dragon sigil. If they're stitched into shawls and pawned coats, then some of them passed through this shop." She watched him over the rim of her cup. "You've already found one. Haven't you."

For a moment she thought he'd deny it. He was good at denying things; he'd had a lifetime's practice. But he only sighed, heaved himself up, and went to the shelf behind the heron's taxidermied perch — third shelf, behind the row of fake reliquaries — and brought down a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

It was a shawl. Fine wool, dyed the deep red of pomegranate skins, worth more than everything else in the shop together. He laid it on the table and turned it so the candlelight caught a seam along the hem, and there, worked into the stitching itself in thread the color of old blood, was a sigil: a coiled dragon, wings folded, eye a single bead of gold.

And beneath the dragon, in letters that made the room tilt sideways, an invitation:

**To E., who takes what others cannot keep. The stair opens for you.**

The handwriting was round and careful and childish, with the doubled *l* of someone who had learned letters recently and loved them.

"That's mine," Elodie said. Her voice sounded very far away. "That's my handwriting. From when I was seven."

"Before you came to me," Corvin said softly. "Before you lost everything. Which means somebody below has had pieces of you for a long time, girl. More pieces than I sold them." He met her eyes. "I'm sorry isn't big enough. I've been looking for a word that is, and I haven't found it in sixty years of collecting. But I'll tell you what I can do." He pushed the shawl toward her, then reached under the table and came up with a second thing: a thin knife, black-hilted, the kind that opened locks as readily as throats. "I raised a thief. Best work I ever did. Let me raise another one."

She looked at the knife. At the shawl. At the coiled dragon with its single gold eye, patient as the dark above the city.

"No," she said, and Corvin's face began to fall — and then she picked up the shawl and slung it around her own shoulders, red wool against black, and picked up the knife, and stood. "You'll slow me down. And I've got a debt of my own to collect."

She was at the door when he spoke again, quiet enough that she almost missed it.

"She kept it safe, you know. Your memory. Brokers don't destroy what they hold — it's their whole religion. Whatever's left of your childhood is down there, whole, waiting." A pause. "Might not be what you hope. Memories age in strange cellars."

"Then it's aged seven years without me," Elodie said, pulling her hood up. "We have catching up to do."

She stepped out into the starless street, and the door clicked shut behind her, and above Tanners' Row the black sky lay flat as a locked lid. Somewhere beneath her feet — beneath the cobbles, the clay, the mountain's roots — something ancient and burning was keeping her childhood warm.

She started walking, and the red shawl trailed behind her like a lit fuse.
