## 6. Charms for the Drowning

By morning, the Lamplight Quarter smelled of smoke and wet wool and fear, and the pawnshop where Elodie had spent seven years learning the grammar of other people's hearts had become something else entirely: a shrine.

Not by design. Nothing in those days happened by design. The stars had gone out, the wards had come down with that terrible crystalline shriek she still heard when she blinked, and the people of Vantrelle had poured into the streets like water finding every crack in a broken bowl. Somewhere around dawn, a woman with a child on her hip had pressed her face to the pawnshop window, seen Elodie sorting charms behind the counter, and mouthed a single word through the glass.

*Please.*

Elodie had let her in. That was the whole of it. That was how it started.

Now, at midday, the line stretched from the door down Tallowmaker's Row and curled around the corner of the cooper's yard, and Maren stood at the shop's entrance with a cudgel she'd never once raised, taking stock of the crowd with the flat professional gaze of a woman who had fenced stolen goods for thirty years and had opinions about queue-jumping.

"You're doing it wrong," Maren said, appearing at Elodie's elbow inside. "You're charging nothing."

"I'm charging what people have."

"They have nothing. That's my point. You'll be out of stock by sundown and rich as a bishop in buttons." Maren set down a chipped cup of chicory coffee at Elodie's station among the display cases. The cases usually held lockets and false teeth and someone's grandmother's wedding ring; now they held rows of small glass vials, each one stoppered with wax, each one holding a curl of light no bigger than a held breath.

"Then I'll be out of stock," Elodie said. "Maren, look at them."

"I am looking at them. I'm looking at a hundred people who'd trample each other for a warm memory if we let them. Rules of trade, girl—"

"These aren't trade." Elodie lifted one vial to the grey light. Inside it, a summer afternoon shimmered: cut grass, a sprinkler, someone's mother calling them in for dinner. She'd lifted it off a dying man in the charity ward two winters ago, with his blessing and a shared cigarette, and it was one of the gentlest things she owned. "This is the only mercy left in the city. You don't put a price on the last mercy."

Maren was quiet a moment. Outside, the queue shifted and murmured like tide over stones. Through the warped glass Elodie could see the far end of Tallowmaker's Row, and beyond the rooftops, where the sky should have been, the flat black nothing where stars used to live. People kept glancing up at it and away again, the way you'd touch your tongue to a missing tooth.

"Maren," she said quietly, "when I came to these walls seven years ago, you took me in. No memories, no name anyone could verify, hands quick enough to be suspicious. You didn't charge me."

"I charged you a third of everything you stole for four years," Maren said. "Sentiment is for people who've never run a ledger."

"And yet here you are, standing guard over a charity shop with a stick."

Maren snorted, but there was no real armor in it. "Someone has to make sure you eat. Charge them *something*, Elodie. Not coin. Anything but nothing. People pay nothing and they take; people pay a little and they receive. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone."

It was, Elodie thought later, the wisest thing anyone said to her that week. Because there *was* a difference. She had spent years watching the faces of people who bought memories, and the ones who paid dearly held their little vials like sacraments, while the ones who got things free too often looked at her afterward with something close to resentment—as if she'd witnessed their need, and witnessing was itself a theft.

So she set her price: one small true thing. A button. A story. A name spoken aloud for the record of no one. And the city, drowning, began to pay.

* * *

The cobbler's widow came first, because she had been waiting outside since before the shutter went up. Elodie knew her slightly—Greta, whose husband had mended boots on Cooper's Lane for thirty years and had died in the first panic night, trampled near the eastern gate when the wards fell and everyone learned at once that they could leave and nowhere to go.

"Grief-charm?" Greta asked. Her voice was steady the way a table with one short leg is steady. "I want to hear him again. Just his voice. Just once more, saying anything. Saying my name."

Elodie's hands stilled over the case. This was the oldest rule she had, older than Maren's ledgers: *never take from the desperate.* But there was a second rule, unwritten, that lived underneath it like a stone under moss: *and never sell back what was theirs to begin with.*

"I don't have him," Elodie said gently. "I never met your husband. What I have—" she sorted quickly, laying three vials on the worn velvet like a fortune-teller laying cards, "—is this: a man teaching his daughter to whistle, so you'll hear a voice like that again, though not his. This: an old couple dancing in a kitchen to no music at all, forty years married, and the way he looks at her is the whole point. And this one's just rain on a roof and someone safe underneath it, which sounds like nothing until you hold it."

Greta stared at the vials. Then at Elodie. Then, horribly, her face began to crumple, and Elodie reached across the counter and took her hands, and Greta gripped back hard enough to hurt.

"He said my name every morning," Greta whispered. "Thirty years. Every single morning, like it was news."

"I know," Elodie said, though she didn't, not exactly—but she knew the shape of that hunger from the inside, having lived seven years with an appetite for a past she couldn't even picture. She slid the kitchen-dancing vial into Greta's palm and closed her fingers over it. "For that, I'll take a story. Tell me one about him. Any one. It doesn't leave this room—it goes into the stock, maybe, someday, for someone who needs to know that men like him existed."

And Greta told her about the winter her husband had fixed the boots of the entire warden's garrison for free, because the alternative was soldiers with cold feet, and cold-footed soldiers were cruel soldiers, and Hendrik had believed—had genuinely, foolishly, magnificently believed—that comfort was a form of lawkeeping.

When Greta left, she walked straighter. Elodie wrote on a slip of paper: *Hendrik, cobbler, gave away warmth like it grew on trees* and tucked it under the empty space where the vial had been, and found she was crying a little, and wiped her eyes before the next customer.

* * *

The day went on. It went on the way floodwater goes on.

A ferryman bought the whistling father for his son, who hadn't slept in three nights. A boy of perhaps ten paid with a bent brass button and a gap-toothed grin and got the rain-on-the-roof, and Elodie threw in a second charm free—the smell of bread baking—because ten-year-olds shouldn't have to stand in bread lines with hollow faces and she couldn't fix that, but she could do one small impossible thing.

An old schoolteacher traded a memory of her own—a genuine one, freely given, her first classroom on a September morning—and Elodie took it carefully between two fingers like something consecrated, and said, "This is worth five of mine," and the teacher said, "Then give five of yours away," and Elodie did.

Around mid-afternoon a warden came in. Not conscripting—not yet, though the word was in every mouth, the wardens pulling anyone with 'talent' off the streets and into the mountain garrison for testing. This one was young, helmet under her arm, and she stood in the shop among the grieving and the frightened and looked profoundly uncertain.

"I'm off duty," she said, as if announcing it might protect her. "I heard—you sell feelings. Safe feelings."

"Safety's the one thing running short," Elodie said. "What can I get you?"

The young warden's jaw worked. "I held the gate on the first night," she said. "When the wards fell. We couldn't hold back the crowd and—we pushed. I pushed people. I don't—" Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I haven't slept since. Every time I close my eyes."

Elodie considered her stock. Sleep-charms, mostly—people would pay nearly anything for dreamless rest these days, and she had a dozen vials of borrowed drowsiness from a retired nurse who'd sold her easy way with patients rather than let it die with her. But that wasn't what this woman was asking for.

She dug to the bottom of the case and came up with a vial she'd been saving without knowing why: a grandmother's kitchen, hands in flour, an old woman laughing at some joke forty years dead, and underneath it all, plain as floorboards, the feeling of being somewhere you were allowed to make mistakes and be loved anyway.

"This isn't sleep," Elodie said. "It's forgiveness. Secondhand. But it soaks in all the same."

The warden paid with her epaulette button and left with both hands cupped around the vial like a candle flame in wind.

"You realize," Maren said, materializing beside her with the eternal chicory, "that half your customers are going to come back tomorrow. And the next day. Charity creates dependency, girl. Ask any temple."

"Good," Elodie said. "Let them depend. On something that doesn't burn them or bleed them or test them for talent and ship them under the mountain." She didn't say the last part loudly. But Maren heard it anyway, because Maren heard everything.

* * *

It was near dusk, the queue finally thinning, the light through the windows going the color of weak tea, when the man in the fine coat arrived.

He was wrong in a dozen small ways, and Elodie's thief-sense rang like a struck bell. His coat was good wool but badly brushed, the kind of fine thing grabbed in flight. His boots were city-made, but muddy to the knee—mud from the river district, red clay, though he had the accent of the Hill. He didn't look at the vials. He looked at her, the whole time, with the pleasant fixed attention of a man reading a ledger on which her name was written.

"The famous charm-seller," he said. "Even in a catastrophe, commerce finds its priestess."

"We're not selling. We're trading stories."

"All the more charming. May I?" He didn't wait; he set a card on the counter, face down, and turned it over with one finger.

Heavy cream stock. An embossed sigil: a dragon, coiled, its tail threaded through its own heart. Beneath it, a single line in copperplate:

*The Court remembers what the city forgets.*

Elodie's mouth went dry. Corvin's confession was two days old and still an open wound in her chest—*one night of stars for one night of survival, girl, what would you have had me do* —and here was the shape of the debt made solid, embossed in gold, sitting on her counter.

"I'm not interested," she said.

"No?" The man smiled. He reached into his coat and produced, with a conjurer's delicacy, a small square of silk—a shawl corner, actually, torn, the weave familiar in a way that made her stomach drop before she knew why. "Then perhaps you're interested in what the Court is already holding. They asked me to return a sample. Proof of provenance, as it were."

He laid the shawl-corner down. Elodie knew it. Of course she knew it. She had carried it across half the city once, stolen from a merchant's stall on a dare, and wrapped it around the shoulders of a shivering boy named Tomas while he laughed at her for stealing something and then giving it away.

Seven years ago. The night everything ended and began.

"How—" she started, and stopped, because the man was already moving toward the door, unhurried, and because her voice had come out wrong.

At the threshold he paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the bruise-colored sky where stars ought to be.

"The Queen says to tell you," he remarked pleasantly, "that the stitching is your own work. You were clever with a needle, even at seven. She's kept everything, Miss Joubert. Everything you ever sold. She's simply been waiting"—the smile widened, white in the gloom—"for you to finish buying it back."

The door closed behind him. The bell above it gave one small, ordinary ring, absurdly gentle, and Elodie stood in her shop full of borrowed mercies with a dragon sigil in one hand and a scrap of a dead boy's shawl in the other, and understood, with total clarity, that she had never been hiding from Vantrelle at all.

She had been summoned.
