## 7. The Broker's Card

Corvin's clue, when I finally pried it out of him—halfway between his third cup of burnt chicory and his fourth apology that didn't quite land—was this: *"Check the shawl."*

That was it. Two words. He'd said them the way a man says *mind the gap*, like the whole catastrophe of my life could be navigated with a little care and a handrail.

"The shawl," I'd repeated. "What shawl?"

"You'll know it when you hold it." Then he'd looked at his hands the way he always did when a conversation got too close to honesty, as if checking whether they'd picked up anything incriminating in the last five minutes.

I knew it before I held it. Of course I did. There was only one shawl that mattered, the one that had come home with me seven years ago, wrapped around a girl who couldn't remember her own name, folded into the bottom of a cedar chest because something about it made my teeth ache when I touched it. Corvin had never let me sell it. Corvin, who would sell his own shadow if someone offered change for it, who had sold *my childhood* without blinking—that man had guarded one moth-eaten shawl like it was scripture.

I went to the cedar chest at dawn, while the curfew bells still muttered their last complaints and the city outside lay flat and gray under its starless sky.

* * *

The shawl smelled of cedar and something older underneath—cold stone, tallow smoke, the particular staleness of air that hasn't moved in a very long time. It was beautiful in the way of things made to be looked at rather than used: gray wool shot through with silver thread, patterns of interlocking spirals worn soft at every edge except one.

Except one corner, where the silver thread ran crisp and unbroken, too fine, too new.

I sat on the floor of my room with the shawl spread across my knees like a map of somewhere cold, and I worked the seam open with my smallest knife—the honest knife, the one I used for cutting thread and apples and never throats—and my heart was going so fast and so loud I honestly worried the wardens would hear it two streets over.

Thieves have rules about stolen goods. Rule three: know what you carry. Rule seven: if a thing is too well kept, someone wants it found eventually. I'd broken rule three for seven years because holding the shawl felt like pressing on a bruise I couldn't locate. Now I understood that Corvin hadn't been guarding it from buyers. He'd been keeping it safe until I was ready. Until the stars went out. Until there was nowhere left to hide from whatever was stitched inside it.

Inside the seam, flat against the lining, was a card.

Not paper. Something smoother, warmer than paper, the color of old ivory, thin as a wafer. A sigil was pressed into it—not inked but *embossed*, rising from the surface like a scar: a dragon coiled into a circle, tail in teeth, wings folded down so tightly they became part of the knot. The kind of image you stare at too long and start to feel watched by.

And beneath the dragon, written in small careful letters:

*Elodie Joubert is expected.*

I want to tell you I threw the card across the room. I want to tell you I laughed, or swore, or did any of the dramatic things a person does in ballads upon discovering she has been awaited by an ancient court of soul-brokers. What I actually did was sit very still on the cold floorboards with the shawl pooling around me, and read the words again, and again, and notice the thing that made my stomach drop through the floor and keep going, down past the cellar, down past Vantrelle's foundations, all the way to wherever the Undercourt was.

The handwriting was mine.

Not mine-now. Not the quick cramped scrawl I used for ledgers and false receipts. This was rounder, laborious, the letters leaning slightly left the way letters do when a child is concentrating terribly hard on each one. This was the handwriting of a girl of seven or eight. A girl who, according to every fact of my life, had not existed until seven years ago, blank-faced and nameless at the gates.

But her hand knew how to write those words. Her hand had *always* known.

* * *

I took it to Maren, because some things are too heavy to carry alone and Maren was the only person in Vantrelle whose door opened for me before I finished knocking. She was already awake—Maren slept the way cats do, in short suspicious installments—and she took one look at my face and put the kettle on without asking, which is the closest thing to tenderness her pride allowed.

"Read it," I said, and put the card on her table among her maps.

She didn't touch it. That frightened me more than anything else that terrible morning. Maren handled everything—contraband maps, warden bribes, charms so hot they steamed—but she wouldn't touch a piece of ivory the size of my palm. She bent over it instead, hands clasped behind her back like a woman inspecting a corpse.

"That's the dragon," she said. "The seal of the court under the mountain."

"You know it."

"Everyone of my trade knows it. We don't *talk* about knowing it." She straightened up and finally met my eyes. "Soul-broker's mark. Old as the walls. Older. There are stories that the mountain is hollow, that there's a court down there that trades in what people are made of, and the fire at its heart keeps the border wards standing. Nonsense stories. Bedtime stories for fences." She gave a laugh with no floor under it. "Apparently not."

"Maren." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Look at the writing."

She looked. I watched her look. I watched her go pale in her particular way—not white all at once but draining slowly, like wine out of a tipped cup.

"That's a child's hand," she said carefully.

"It's *my* child's hand. It's how I wrote before—" Before. Before the gates. Before the blankness. Before Corvin found me or I found him; even now I couldn't remember which way round it went. "Maren, I came to these walls with nothing. No name, no past. You've told me that yourself, half a dozen times, usually when you're losing an argument. So how does a girl with no memories already know how to write? How does she write *this*?" I tapped the table an inch from the card, not touching it either now. It seemed polite, suddenly, to leave it alone. "Who taught me my own letters if nobody ever taught me anything? Who taught me to say I was *expected*?"

The kettle began to whistle. Neither of us moved toward it. In my experience that's how you know a conversation has gone truly wrong: the tea gets forgotten.

"Or," Maren said at last, very quietly, sitting down across from me, "here's the question you're dancing around because it's worse. If you wrote that invitation yourself, when you were seven years old—then somebody showed you the words first. Somebody told you where you'd end up. And you wrote it down so you'd remember."

"To remember," I repeated. "Me. The memory-thief with no memories."

"You were a child," Maren said. "Children write down the things adults make them promise." She paused, and I watched her decide whether to say the next thing, and then say it anyway, because Maren has never once in her life let kindness interrupt accuracy. "And then someone took the memory away so you'd forget the promise. But they left the card where you'd find it. When it was time."

"When it was time," I said. "Which is now. Which is exactly when the stars go out and the wards fall and the only person alive who can fix it just happens to be me." I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin, the laugh of a person standing at the edge of something. "Seven years, Maren. Seven years I've been carrying my own summons around in a cedar chest, and I dusted around it every week."

"Well," said Maren, "you always were tidy."

And that—gods help us—is what broke the moment open. I laughed for real then, ugly and hiccupping, half hysteria, and Maren poured the tea that had boiled itself into bitterness, and we drank it anyway, because neither of us was raised to waste anything, least of all comfort.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about dread: it doesn't shout. It settles in quietly, like damp, and after a while you can't remember what it felt like to be dry.

All that day I went about my rounds with the card sewn into my coat lining—I wasn't leaving it anywhere, thank you, not in any chest, not under any floorboard; things that want to be found should at least suffer the indignity of being carried—and everywhere I looked I saw the dragon sigil's shape. Coiled in the iron scrollwork above the pawnshop door. Curled in the frost-melt patterns on a windowpane. Once, horribly, in the way a beggar girl in the Saltmarket had braided her hair around itself at the nape, tail-in-teeth, and when she caught me staring she smiled with far too many teeth of her own and looked away.

Maybe. Maybe not. Dread makes liars of shadows.

But the feeling underneath the paranoia was harder, truer, and it followed me home through the curfew bells: *the Undercourt has been expecting me all along.* Not hunting. Expecting. There's a difference, and the difference is the worst part. Hunters chase. Hosts simply prepare the guest room and wait, certain, patient, because they know something about your future that you don't yet.

Everything I thought I knew about my own story had inverted overnight. Yesterday I'd been a victim gathering evidence—a girl robbed of her childhood, betrayed by her foster-father, summoned by fate to set things right. Clean. Tidy. A role with clear lines to speak. Today the summons was in my own childish hand, and I couldn't shake the thought that Corvin hadn't given me a clue at all. He'd given me permission. *You'll know it when you hold it*—as if he'd been waiting seven years for me to be ready to read my own mail.

I sat up long past midnight with the card on my knee and the shawl around my shoulders—it was cold in the room, I told myself, though the shawl's chill had nothing to do with weather—and I asked the ceiling the questions that had teeth.

Did I go willingly, once? Did a seven-year-old girl stand in some lamplit room and copy out *expected* in her careful letters because a broker with a dragon ring told her she was important, chosen, needed? Did I *agree* to forget, the way I'd seen desperate people agree to terrible bargains, trading tomorrow's self for tonight's survival?

And the question under that one, the one with the longest teeth of all: when I walked down into the dark to steal back what was mine—

what if I wasn't breaking into the Undercourt at all?

What if I was only keeping my appointment?

The candle guttered. Outside, the sky stayed black and starless, and somewhere beyond the fallen wards, in the dark under the mountain, I had the sudden absolute certainty of a fire guttering low, waiting, patient as hospitality, for the guest whose name it already knew.

I snuffed the candle, sewed the card deeper into my coat, and began packing for the descent.

There was only one place left to learn the way down: Maren's maps. And one map in particular, I remembered—the one she kept folded in oilcloth at the very bottom of her strongbox, the one she'd never once let me see, the one drawn, she'd admitted once, deep in her cups, *by someone who had come back.*

In the morning I was going to ask her for it.

Gods help us both, I was going to ask her nicely.
