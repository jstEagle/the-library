## 8. Down the Stair That Isn't There

Maren's map was drawn on a piece of leather the size of a dinner napkin, and it was wrong in at least four places, and she knew it.

"That's the point, girl," she said, tapping it with a finger gone brown from decades of handling other people's contraband. "A correct map of that passage would be a lie by breakfast. I've drawn it as it *behaves*, not as it is. See here—" her nail dragged across an inked spiral that seemed to squirm when Elodie wasn't looking directly at it— "the stair turns back on itself every ninety steps or so. Sometimes it turns into something else entirely. You'll know you've gone wrong when the dark starts saying your name."

"The dark says my name."

"Only if you let it get comfortable." Maren folded the leather with the tenderness of a woman tucking in a child and pressed it into Elodie's hands. "Don't answer. That's the whole trick of it. The mountain's lonely. It wants conversation. Every word you give it costs you something, and it's not particular about what it takes in return."

Elodie turned the map over. On the reverse, in Maren's cramped hand, was a single line: *The toll is small. It only feels enormous.* Beneath it, almost as an afterthought: *Come back. I've grown accustomed to your face.*

"You could say that to me like a person," Elodie said.

"I could," Maren agreed, and went back to sorting her charms.

* * *

Ilsa Reyne was waiting at the foot of the mountain where the old quarry road gave out, standing beside a lantern as though she had simply decided, this evening, to inspect a rock.

"Warden-Captain," Elodie said, and made her feet stop moving before they could carry her anywhere foolish.

"Thief." Ilsa looked her up and down the way she probably looked at ledgers—checking for discrepancies between what a thing claimed to be and what it weighed. "You're going down."

It wasn't a question, so Elodie didn't treat it as one. "You knew I would be."

"I hoped. Hoping isn't in my job description, so I've been standing here for two hours telling myself I'm patrolling." Ilsa crouched and lifted the lantern, angling its light against the cliff face until it caught on something—a seam in the rock, thin as a hair, running down into darkness. "There are forty wardens within shouting distance. By rights I should shout."

"But?"

"But my superiors believe the solution to a broken border is more conscription, and conscription has produced three hundred terrified citizens, two riots, and one man who can make his ears glow." Ilsa's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I find my faith in shouting somewhat diminished. Go, Vasquez. In four hours the watch changes. If anyone asks, I interrogated you and you wept convincingly."

"I don't weep convincingly."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "You have the face of someone who laughs at funerals and then cries about it later, privately, where no one can bill her for it." She straightened, and for one moment the cold pragmatism cracked down the middle like river ice, and underneath it Elodie saw the fear she'd been carrying since the stars went out—the fear of a woman who had enforced a law built on theft and only now understood what had been stolen. "Whatever's down there owes the city seven years of sky. Collect it."

Then the warden turned her back, which was the largest blind eye Vantrelle had ever offered, and Elodie put her fingers into the seam in the rock, and pulled.

* * *

The door opened inward, onto stairs.

That was the first strange thing—that there were stairs, descending at a civilized angle, wide enough for two, hewn smooth and worn glossy down the center as if generations of feet had trusted them. The second strange thing was the warmth. Not dragon-fire warmth; nothing so honest. A warmth like the inside of a coat pocket. Like being remembered by someone fond.

Elodie lit her candle-stub, checked her supplies for the fourth time—Maren's map, flint, the broker's card with its dragon sigil and its invitation written in her own seven-year-old hand, a heel of bread she suspected she'd be too frightened to eat—and stepped down.

Behind her, the door sealed itself with a sound like a book closing.

"Well," she said to the dark, "that's rude."

The dark did not answer. Yet.

She counted steps as she went, because counting was a thing hands and mind could do together and left no room for imagining what the walls were made of. At step thirty she noticed the candle flame bending slightly, always toward the direction she'd come, as though it too were considering turning back. At step sixty she noticed that her boots made no sound at all, and that this was somehow worse than echoing.

At step ninety, the stair turned back on itself, exactly as Maren had promised, and Elodie felt the first tug.

It was gentle. That was the horror of it. Gentle as a pickpocket who'd done this ten thousand times—she would have known; she *was* one. Something brushed through the pockets of her mind, considering, weighing, and then withdrew, taking with it—

She stopped. Frowned. Reached inward the way she reached for a charm on a crowded shelf.

She couldn't remember the name of the pawnbroker on Chandler's Row. The fat one, with the rings, who watered his tea and called everyone "my dove." She could see his shop. She could see the ledger, the widow's grief she'd lifted from its pages. But his name was simply—gone. A shelf swept clean.

"Oh, no you don't," Elodie told the stair. "That was worth six silver bits a month."

The stair, having no pockets to turn out, said nothing. But she felt it settle, satisfied, like a cat that had been fed.

*Toll is small,* Maren had written. *It only feels enormous.* Except it hadn't felt enormous—it hadn't felt like anything. That was the trap of it. You wouldn't notice what the mountain took until you needed it, and then you'd stand in the dark reaching for a name, a face, a Tuesday afternoon, and find the shape of its absence instead.

"Fine," Elodie said. "But I'm keeping count."

She kept count. Ninety steps, one memory. She resolved to ration herself—to think hard of everything valuable before each turn, to hold her best things tight so the mountain's fingers would slide off them. She spent the next stretch of stairs reciting, silently and fiercely: *Mother's voice, humming the lullaby about the miller. The smell of Corvin's coat, pipe-smoke and rain. Tomas laughing on the rooftops the summer we were thirteen. Tomas. Tomas.*

At the second turn the mountain took the miller's lullaby.

She stood very still on the landing, listening to the hole where a song used to be. Her mother's voice remained—but the words had been scooped out of it, leaving her humming something tuneless and kind. It was such a small theft. Such a reasonable price. And it was exactly how she herself had operated for years: take what they won't miss, what they can't spare but don't know how to guard.

"I did this to people," she said aloud, and her voice came back to her flattened, apologetic. "Hundreds of them. I did this at *breakfast*."

The dark stirred around her ankles like a dog hearing its name.

"—no," Elodie said firmly. "Absolutely not. I'm not talking to you."

The dark subsided, wounded.

She walked on.

* * *

Somewhere past the third turn—the pawnbroker's face went, whole and jolly, and she grieved him more than she'd expected—Elodie began to hear the names.

They came quietly, out of the walls themselves, in voices like dust settling. *Marielle. Ondrey. Six-fingers Tam. Bess of the Low Gate.* Names spoken once, gently, and then not again, the way a name dies when the last mouth that knew it stops. The mountain was full of them. It had been collecting for centuries, she realized. Everyone who ever came down these stairs paid, and everyone who paid left something behind besides a memory, and the mountain kept the leavings the way a magpie keeps string.

Then, unmistakably, in a voice like her own but younger:

*Elodie Joubert.*

She stopped so fast the candle guttered.

"Don't," she whispered.

*Elodie Joubert,* the dark repeated, coaxing now, sweet as a broker's opening offer. *Elodie. Elody. El. Lodie. Didi.*—and at *Didi* her whole body flinched, because that was a name nobody living knew, a name from before, from the childhood she'd sold, and yet the mountain had it, had it filed away in its hoard of leavings along with every other discarded thing—

"How do you know that?" Her voice cracked on it. "Who *paid* that?"

Silence. The considerate, terrible silence of a creditor reviewing a debtor's file.

Elodie stood on the narrow stair with her heart slamming and understood, with the clarity she usually reserved for spotting a mark's tell, that the mountain already knew everything about her. That it had perhaps always known. That the invitation stitched inside the shawl—in her own handwriting, seven years old—had not been a forgery of cleverness but a retrieval. The Undercourt had simply asked the dark what it remembered, and the dark had answered fluently.

"All right," she said, steadying the candle. "All right. You want a conversation. Here's mine: I'm coming back up these stairs, and when I do, I want a refund."

The dark made a sound. She chose to interpret it as laughter rather than appetite, mostly because the alternative made her legs unreliable.

* * *

She lost track of turns somewhere around losing the taste of oranges.

That one hurt worse than she'd anticipated—more than the pawnbroker, more than the lullaby. She'd been bracing for the big things, guarding *Tomas, Tomas, Tomas* like coins clenched in a fist, and while she was clenched the mountain had slipped its fingers in and taken something ordinary and bright, and now she knew she had loved oranges without remembering a single reason why.

"It's rationing me," she muttered, walking. "The clever bastard. It takes what I'm not watching. It's *me*. It's doing what I do."

And that was the thought that kept her company down the fifth turn and the sixth, warming her in a grim way: the mountain thieved like her, sorted like her, priced like her. Which meant it could be *read* like her. Every thief has a rhythm. Every hand leaves a pattern in what it lifts. If she could learn the mountain's rhythm—what it valued, what it overlooked, where its greed outran its caution—

She nearly missed the seventh turn entirely, because it wasn't a turn.

The stair simply ended. Ahead of her, where the descent should have continued, there was a landing, and on the landing a table, and on the table a cup of tea, still steaming, and a chair pulled out at a hospitable angle.

Elodie regarded this arrangement for a long moment.

"No," she said.

The steam curled invitingly.

"I've read Maren's maps. I've heard the stories. Tea in the underdark is how you end up as a story." She leaned closer anyway—thief's curiosity, the same fatal twitch that had gotten her into half her troubles—and read the little card propped against the cup.

*For the guest who will not sit. We respect this. The tea is not a trap. The chair, however, is. — S.*

Beneath, in smaller script: *You've lost the taste of oranges. That was regrettable. The kitchen sends its apologies.*

Elodie stared at the card for a long time. Then, because she was who she was, she drank the tea. She did not touch the chair. The tea tasted like nothing at all, which meant it had already been paid for, by someone, somewhere, and that thought followed her down the eighteenth turn, and the nineteenth, all the way to the place where the stairs finally stopped pretending and opened out into a red-gold glow that breathed.

The last thing the mountain took from her, at the final step, was the sound of her own laughter.

She noticed immediately. She tried to recall it—tried to hear it, that ridiculous snorting thing Corvin had always said sounded like a kettle giving up—and found only the knowledge that she laughed, the way you might know about a country you'd never visited.

"That was cruel," she whispered. "That was the cruelest one yet."

The dark, for once, seemed to agree. It drew back from her, almost respectfully, and let her pass.

* * *

The Undercourt opened before her like the inside of a heart.

Bone-lanterns hung in their hundreds from a vaulted dark, each one burning pale, each one labeled—she could see the labels from here, little tags, like her charms, like her ledger rows—and beneath them the great fire guttered at the center of a cathedral of pillars, red-gold and struggling, and around the fire, figures moved, feeding it, tending it, and on a throne of stacked ledgers sat a woman whose beauty had the specific wrongness of a mask worn too long.

Queen Sarelle. The soul-broker. She was looking directly at Elodie, had been, Elodie understood, looking since the moment she entered—perhaps since the moment seven years ago when a frightened girl's childhood had arrived in the day's takings.

"Elodie Joubert," said the Queen of the Undercourt, and her voice was warm as a coat pocket. "You're taller than your handwriting suggested. Come down. We've been keeping your seat."

Behind the fire, among the keepers, a figure turned. Half-consumed, burning steady as a wick, a boy's face above an ember's body—and even at this distance, even after everything the mountain had scooped out of her, Elodie's heart recognized him before her mind caught up.

Tomas.

He was looking at her the way people look at weather they've been promised.

She descended the last steps into the light.
