## 9. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stair ended the way Maren's maps had promised it would not: without warning.

One moment Elodie's foot found stone; the next it found nothing, and she pitched forward into a light that had no source she could name—amber, honeyed, trembling at the edges like a candle seen through tears. She caught herself on her palms against a floor that was smooth as river-polished glass and warm. Warm, in a place where warmth should have been impossible.

She knelt there a long moment, breathing, taking inventory of herself the way she'd learned to do somewhere below the tenth tolling of the stair. Her name. Elodie Joubert. Her trade. Thief of small things held in soft places. Her purpose.

Her purpose came back to her slower than her name did, and she decided not to examine what that meant.

Then she looked up, and forgot how to breathe entirely.

* * *

The Undercourt was a cathedral, if cathedrals could be grown rather than built. Its vaulted ribs arched up and up into a dark so high the eye surrendered before reaching the top, and from those ribs hung lanterns by the hundreds—the bone-lanterns the stair-dark had whispered about, each one a cage of pale interlocking curves with a light inside that moved. Not flickered. *Moved*, the way a moth moves inside a jar, the way a thought moves behind a face.

Elodie knew what burned in them. She'd known since the third stair took her memory of her mother's laugh as toll and left her carrying an ache with no object attached to it. The lanterns were lit with souls. Small ones, dim ones, traded and spent and hung up like decorations because even a soul too worn-out to be useful made a fine little glow if you were the sort of person who thought that way.

And at the cathedral's far end, past aisles of kneeling shadows, past a congregation of figures who stood utterly still in rows like candles on an altar—

The fire.

It was small. That was the first obscenity of it. After seven years of Vantrelle's stars burning on its back, after all the wardens' hymns about the Veil and the wards and the sacred border, Elodie had expected something vast, a furnace, a sun in exile. Instead it sat in a hearth of black stone no bigger than a market brazier, a flame the color of late gold, barely taller than a child—and it guttered. It leaned left, recovered, leaned right, and every time it bent, every lantern in the cathedral dimmed with it, and every still figure in the congregation swayed slightly too, like grass under wind.

"Oh," Elodie said aloud, very quietly, to no one. "Oh, you poor thing."

That was not, she reflected later, the reaction the court was designed to provoke.

Because it *was* beautiful, in the way a spider's web is beautiful at dawn when you're the fly and not yet stuck. The architecture rose around her in sweeping curves and delicate buttresses, and the longer she looked, the more it tugged at something under her ribs—not memory, exactly. She'd sold memory's contents long ago. But the *shape* of it remained, the way a book's outline ghosts through closed covers. She recognized the curve of the arches because she had once drawn that curve in charcoal on a kitchen floor, aged six or seven, some lifetime before. She knew the rhythm of the lantern-light because she had once hummed it.

This was her childhood's architecture, built in stone and bone by someone who had bought the original.

"Rude," she whispered to the cathedral, and her voice only shook a little. "That is deeply, personally rude."

* * *

"You're admiring the transept," said a voice near her elbow.

Elodie did not jump. Jumping was for amateurs, and she had rules about being seen, and although rule two had already been comprehensively violated by her mere presence here, she clung to the principle. She turned with the unhurried grace of someone who had absolutely not just been talking to a building.

Her guide was a woman of indeterminate age dressed in gray so finely woven it looked like fog given opinions. Her eyes were the pale, washed color of old coins. Around her neck, where another woman might wear a locket, she wore a small brass ledger on a chain.

"I'm admiring the theft," Elodie said. "The transept I could take or leave."

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth. Not quite a smile. An interest in smiles, filed away for later.

"The Queen will see you," the woman said. "You've kept her waiting rather a long time."

"Seven years," Elodie agreed. "I do apologize. I didn't know I was expected."

"No one ever does," said the woman, "until they arrive."

* * *

They walked down the central aisle together, and Elodie counted her remaining memories the way a gambler counts coins with a hole in the purse. Name. Trade. Purpose. The taste of Maren's terrible tea. The weight of Ilsa Reyne's blind eye turning away from her at the mountain pass, which had cost the warden more than Elodie yet understood. Corvin's face when he'd said *I sold it, girl, I sold your stars*, and the sound of her own heart cracking along a seam she hadn't known was there.

Small things kept going missing as they walked. She couldn't tell which. That was the cruelty of the stair's tolls carried forward into the court itself: this place fed gently, continuously, a tick here and there, taking sips. She resolved to walk faster.

The congregation did not turn to watch her pass. That frightened her more than staring would have. She drifted closer to one figure as they went—a man, or what had been a man, standing in his row with his hands folded, his skin gone translucent as paper, and inside him a faint gold shimmer rising and falling with the fire's guttering breath. Traded dead, the stair-dark had called them. Sold souls standing attendance on the thing that ate them.

But as she passed, the man's head turned. Just slightly. Toward her. And his mouth shaped a word with no voice behind it, and Elodie, reading lips out of seven years' professional habit, felt her stomach drop through the warm glass floor.

It looked like *run*. Or possibly *Elodie*. She chose, with great discipline, to interpret it as *hello*, and kept walking.

* * *

The throne—if throne was the word—stood on a dais of the same black stone as the hearth, close enough to the fire that its heat should have been unbearable and wasn't, which told Elodie something important about the person sitting in it. The seat itself was carved from a single piece of pale wood, and it was empty.

For three heartbeats, Elodie allowed herself the luxurious, doomed hope that the queen was simply elsewhere.

Then the shadow standing beside the throne resolved into a person, because it had always been a person; Elodie's eyes had simply refused the information. Queen Sarelle was tall the way winter is tall—all vertical severity, no wasted line. Her gown was the gray of the ledger-woman's but older, darker, as if fog could acquire history. Her face was beautiful in the manner of a portrait: perfectly composed, subtly wrong, the eyes doing all the work of living while the rest of the face merely attended.

When she spoke, her voice was warm. That was the worst surprise of the evening. Elodie had braced for cold, for grandeur, for thunder, and instead got the voice of a grandmother calling children in from the rain.

"There you are," Sarelle said. "Elodie Joubert. You're smaller than your paperwork suggested."

"Paperwork lies," Elodie said. "Ask anyone in my profession."

The queen laughed—a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and Elodie hated how much she wanted to hear it again. "Come closer, thief. The fire wants to look at you."

"The fire can look from there. We'll wave."

"Come *closer*." Not a command. A request, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, and Elodie heard the hook inside it because hooks were her trade and she knew good bait when she heard it. She came closer anyway, because refusing twice would have told the queen exactly how much the fire frightened her, and Elodie had one weapon left in this glittering awful place: being unreadable.

Up close, the flame was worse. It didn't behave like fire. It behaved like attention. When Elodie stepped within arm's reach, it straightened, gathered itself, and turned toward her—not leaning, *turning*, the whole body of it, and the heat that rolled off it wasn't heat at all but the sensation of being remembered by someone who loved you. It went through her like a knife through ribbon.

It found the empty places. There were many. It lingered on each one like a tongue finding a missing tooth.

"Poor little hoard," the queen murmured, watching. "So much sold off. Whatever did you buy with it all, child?"

"Rent," said Elodie. "Mostly."

* * *

Sarelle descended from the dais and walked the aisle of her congregation, and Elodie fell into step beside her because it was either that or stand alone next to a fire that missed her, apparently, on a personal level.

"Do you know what I sell?" the queen asked. It was conversational, the tone of a shopkeeper making talk while wrapping a purchase.

"Grief," Elodie said. "First kisses. Dead mothers' voices. Comfort. Courage. The last conversation you ever had with someone you loved, if the price is right. I know the inventory." She paused. "I've handled the stock."

"Mm. Street-level. Charm-work. Respectable, in its way—you keep the ledgers honest at the bottom of the trade." Sarelle touched one translucent congregant as she passed, a woman whose insides glowed faint rose-gold. "But down here we handle the wholesale. Souls entire. And the fire, of course." She glanced back at the hearth, and for half a second something crossed her face that was not a portrait's expression at all—it was quick and raw and hungry and sad, all at once, like a crack running through porcelain. "The fire is my finest product. My only irreplaceable one."

"It's dying," Elodie said.

"Everything is dying. That is the entire basis of commerce." Sarelle smiled at her congregation, fond as a shepherd. "The stars above your city, the wards that kept out the hungry dark for three hundred years—they ran on this. One flame. One keeper. And keepers burn, Elodie. That is the arrangement. Someone must sit close to the fire and feed it what it needs, and what it needs—" she spread her hands elegantly, "—is everything. All of it. Every year, every love, every summer afternoon, poured in until the pourer is empty, and then we find another pourer. It is not cruel. It is arithmetic."

"And the souls in the lanterns?"

"Kindling." The queen said it without apology. "The fire likes variety. A soul is a very concentrated memory, you understand. Everything a person ever was, distilled to one bright drop. The lanterns are... storage." Her eyes slid sideways, coin-pale and knowing. "Though I suspect you didn't climb all those lying stairs for a lecture."

"No," Elodie said. Her heart was hammering and she kept her voice level through sheer spite. "I climbed them for a memory. A specific one. Mine. And for a boy named Tomas, who should never have ended up as anybody's kindling."

"Ah." Sarelle stopped walking. They stood in the center of the aisle now, midway between the empty throne and the guttering fire, surrounded by hundreds of the traded dead, and the queen looked at Elodie with an expression of such frank, warm appraisal that Elodie felt like a bolt of cloth being measured. "Yes. Tomas. He speaks of you, you know. Well—burns of you. Keepers past the fifth year lose speech. But every time your name comes near the fire, it flares green. The whole court notices. It's become quite our clock."

Elodie's throat closed. She managed, eventually: "He remembers me?"

"He remembers *everything*." Sarelle's voice softened further, and that softness was more dangerous than any blade Elodie had ever fenced with. "That is what a keeper is, dear. The one who remembers, so the rest may forget. He has held your betrayal for seven years the way he holds the flame—continuously, at cost, because someone must. Would you like to see him? He's resting between feedings. He does that now. Rests less and less."

Every instinct Elodie owned screamed that this was the hook setting properly, that the queen had led her exactly here through exactly these words to exactly this moment. She knew it, and she wanted it anyway, and that was the trap's genius: it wasn't made of lies. It was made of true things arranged in a circle around a price.

"Yes," Elodie said. "I would."

* * *

They walked toward the hearth, and the congregation turned as one—not their bodies, only their faces, hundreds of translucent visages rotating smoothly to track her passage, and every one of them mouthing something. She stopped reading lips. Some knowledge costs too much at retail.

The fire saw her coming. There was no other word for it. It rose—not taller, but *straighter*, gathering itself like a dog hearing a beloved footstep on the stairs, and its color shifted through gold into something deeper, ember-red shading toward green at the core, and the warmth that broke over Elodie then nearly put her on her knees. It smelled like a kitchen in winter. Like charcoal drawings on a floor. Like someone's hand in yours, walking home in the dark, both of you pretending you weren't afraid of the thing behind the wall.

"Tomas," she breathed, before she could stop herself, and the flame flared green from base to crown, and every bone-lantern in the cathedral blazed in answer, and the whole vast court filled with light like the inside of a struck bell.

And in the heart of the flame, for just a moment, she saw him.

A shape of brighter fire, seated—curled—wrapped around the flame's core the way a wick sits in a candle. A boy's shape, fourteen forever, arms around the light he was becoming. His face turned toward her. His eyes were still his eyes. They were full of seven years, and every hour of them was looking at her.

His mouth moved.

She read it this time, because some things you owe a person whether you want them or not, and what he mouthed was not *run* and not *hello* and not her name.

It was: *"You came."*

And Elodie Joubert, the best thief in Vantrelle, who had stolen grief from widows and courage from soldiers and never once been seen doing it, stood in the court beneath the mountain with her empty hands shaking at her sides, and understood with total clarity that whatever happened next, she had already lost the only thing she'd come here intending to protect.

Behind her, silk-soft, Queen Sarelle said:

"Now then. Shall we discuss the ledger?"
