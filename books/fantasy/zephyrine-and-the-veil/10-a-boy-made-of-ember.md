## 10. A Boy Made of Ember

The fire had a rhythm, and Tomas had learned it the way a sailor learns the sea: not because he loved it, but because the alternative was drowning.

In through the ribs, out through the ribs. The dragon-fire breathed him as much as he fed it. Four years in the Undercourt—or what felt like four years; time below the mountain pooled and stagnated like water in a boot—and he had stopped thinking of his body as his own somewhere around the second winter. It was fuel now. A wick with a boy's face.

He kept the face on purpose. That was vanity, maybe, or spite. The other keepers had let their features go soft and runny as candle wax, faces melting into the general amber glow until you couldn't tell one burning soul from another. Tomas held his. Nose, chin, the crooked tooth his mother had said made his smile interesting rather than unfortunate. He held them the way he held everything down here—clenched, white-knuckled, out of stubbornness that had nowhere else to live.

"Still brooding," said Wren, drifting past with an armful of bone-lanterns. She had been a lamplighter in Vantrelle once, back above. Now she lit lamps with herself. "Brood less. The Queen notices."

"The Queen notices everything," Tomas said. "It's her whole personality."

Wren snorted, which was a dangerous thing to do near open flame, and moved off toward the cathedral's far aisle, where the bone-lanterns hung in rows like a harvest of small pale moons. Above them coiled the fire itself—the last dragon-fire, older than Vantrelle, older than the wards, older probably than regret. It didn't burn like ordinary fire. It burned *hungry*. And its food was memory, soul, self: ladled in by keepers, spoon by agonizing spoon.

Tomas stood at the fire's edge and let it take its sip. It was like being remembered by something enormous and indifferent—a warm hand closing briefly around your whole life and squeezing. His left arm had gone translucent at the fingertips. He could watch the lantern-light through his own hand. He'd named the fingers, absurdly, so that losing them would feel like something. This was Pointer. He was fond of Pointer.

That was when he felt it.

Not heard—*felt*, the way you feel someone step on your grave. A wrongness in the court's air, a thread pulled loose in the weave. Someone new had come down the Stair That Isn't There, and the dark beneath the mountain, which knew every name that entered, had gone strangely, suddenly quiet around them.

Tomas turned.

She was smaller than he remembered. That was the first stupid thought, and he hated it—the boy of fourteen still alive somewhere in the cinders of him, saying *she's smaller, she's here, she's real.* Fourteen had a lot to answer for.

Elodie Joubert stood at the edge of the bone-lit nave with soot on her jaw and Maren's stolen maps folded somewhere against her ribs, looking up at the cathedral of the traded dead with her mouth slightly open, and she was twenty-one years old and breathing and *here*, and every ember in Tomas's chest did something complicated at once.

He did not move. The fire's hand tightened on him warningly—keepers were not permitted to leave the flame's reach for long—but he would not have moved anyway. Some part of him had rehearsed this moment for seven years and every rehearsal ended differently. In some she wept. In some she begged forgiveness and he gave it, gracious and sad. In one particularly satisfying version she arrived pursued by justice personified, which took the form of a very large goose.

None of the rehearsals accounted for how it would actually feel: like the fire reaching into him and finding, of all things, more to burn.

"She can't see us yet," murmured Ashen-Brigid, the eldest keeper, whose face had long since melted into kindlessness. "Newcomers see the court before they see the court's people. Give it a moment."

"I don't need a moment," Tomas said.

"You need several," Brigid said placidly. "Look at her. She's counting exits. Let her count. It's crueler when they realize they're surrounded."

But Elodie wasn't counting exits. She was walking toward the fire with her thief's walk—that rolling, apologetic stride she'd perfected for slipping through markets, designed to make her look like she belonged wherever she least did—and she was staring straight at the flame, and then, horribly, straight through it, straight at *him.*

Their eyes met across the last dragon-fire in the world.

"Oh," said Wren, from somewhere behind him, with the relish of a woman who had been dead of boredom for years. "Oh, this is going to be *good.*"

---

Seven years was a long time to keep a hatred warm. Tomas had managed it the way he managed everything now: imperfectly, painfully, by feeding it pieces of himself.

He had been fourteen when she sold his name. He knew that now—he hadn't known it then, that was the whole cruelty of it. One morning he'd woken in Corvin's house above the tanneries with a hole in his chest shaped like being loved, and no word for it, and everyone in the Saint-Aubin quarter treating him with the careful distance you give a stranger. His own mother had looked through him twice before he understood why. Names could be sold in Vantrelle. Whole histories could be lifted like purses. And the girl he had loved—clumsily, furiously, in the way of boys who show affection by stealing apples for you and then eating most of the apples—had needed coin, or safety, or simply to not be the one caught, and there had been his name, lying out in the open, unguarded, trusting.

He'd fled the city within the season. Fled into the borderlands, where the wardens found him, because a boy with no name to protect was exactly the sort of conscription fodder the law devoured. Talentless, they called him. Unclaimed. Which meant claimable.

And then the Queen's brokers had come to the warden pens with scales and ledgers, and offered the one trade worse than any other, and Tomas—nameless, motherless, apple-less—had said yes, because the alternative was the work camps, and because he had been told the fire's service came with forgetting, and God, he had wanted to forget.

The fire lied about the forgetting. Or the Queen did. It took and took from him, but it never touched the one thing he'd have paid anything to lose.

So he remembered Elodie pushing his hair off his forehead by the canal. He remembered her laughing so hard at the priest's funeral sermon that she had to bury her face in his shoulder, and the two of them shaking together over a dead man neither of them mourned. He remembered her hand in his at midwinter, both freezing, neither admitting it. He remembered the night before it happened, when she'd been strange and quiet and had gripped his sleeve like she wanted to say something, and he'd asked what, and she'd said *nothing, idiot,* and he had believed her, because he was fourteen and loved her and that was apparently all it took.

He remembered everything. That was the job, in the end. The fire ran on warmth, on the *memory* of warmth, and the Queen had discovered centuries ago that nothing burned brighter than a keeper who remembered love and could never have it again. Grief was ash by morning. Longing was a slow, steady flame.

Tomas had been burning steadily for four years.

Now she crossed the nave toward him, and the bone-lanterns swung in their chains, and the light moved over her face—thinner than he remembered, sharper, a scar through one eyebrow she hadn't earned yet at fourteen—and every rehearsal fell out of his head except the goose one, which he clung to briefly for sanity.

"Tomas," she said.

His name. In her voice. After everything.

"That's mine," he said. "You sold it. Finders keepers doesn't apply, I'm told, but I'm taking it back regardless."

---

She flinched. Good, some cold coal of him said. She should—

But she also *looked* at him. Really looked, the way nobody down here did anymore, and the cold coal guttered. Her eyes went to his translucent fingers, to the faint glow behind his collarbones where the fire sat in him like a swallowed star, to the places where the light showed through him a little too easily, and he watched her do the arithmetic, watched her arrive at the sum, watched her face break in the specific order faces broke: denial, comprehension, horror.

"How much of you is—" she started.

"Pointer," he said, wiggling the finger, "is mostly ceremonial these days. The rest varies. Tuesdays are bad." He heard his own voice come out light and mean at once, a knife wrapped in a joke, which was, he supposed, what four years of steady burning made of a boy. "Come closer. You look like you want to gawk properly and I'd hate to inconvenience your shopping trip."

"I didn't come to shop."

"No? Everyone comes to the Undercourt to shop. It's the only reason anyone finds the door." He stepped along the fire's rim, and its heat leaned after him, possessive. "What was the rate, by the way? I've always wondered. My name. What did it fetch?"

"Tomas—"

"Because if it was under thirty crowns I'm going to be insulted, and if it was over thirty crowns I'm going to be insulted *differently*, so really it's win-win."

"You were fifteen," she said quietly. "I was told you were fifteen when they took you. I thought—" Her hands did something helpless in the air, thief's hands, hands that had never once in their life known where to settle. "I thought you'd been arrested. I looked for you."

"Liar," he said, without heat, which somehow made it worse for both of them. "Or honest, I genuinely can't tell anymore, that's rather the point of your whole talent, isn't it?" He spread his arms, and the firelight poured through him, and he gestured at the cathedral of the dead with a showman's flourish. "Welcome to the vault. Mind the merchandise—it's all souls, and most of them used to know you."

Her face went grey. "These are people?"

"These are *prices*," said Ashen-Brigid, gliding up, kindless and courteous. "People is optimistic. You'll be wanting the Queen, I expect. Everyone who comes down the Stair wants the Queen. She's holding court in the east transept, taking appointments, ruining lives, the usual." Brigid's melted features arranged themselves into what might once have been a smile. "Though I'd have a care, girl. The dark below remembers names, and yours has a certain... resonance here."

"I know," Elodie said. "It's stitched into a shawl in my possession. I've been meaning to complain to whoever does the embroidery."

Despite himself—despite everything—something in Tomas's chest that wasn't ember shifted. Seven years, and she still met the abyss with her chin up and a complaint ready. He crushed the feeling immediately. Crushed it hard. It flared anyway, and the fire noticed, damn it, the fire noticed everything, and it drew a deeper sip from him in reward, and he staggered slightly.

She moved toward him. Instinct, plainly, unthinking—her hand coming up to catch his elbow the way she'd steadied him off a wall a hundred times, the way bodies remember what names were sold to forget.

"Don't," he said.

She stopped. Her hand hung in the air between them, six inches from his sleeve, close enough that he could feel the warmth of it—which was a joke, really, a cosmic one, because he was standing next to the last dragon-fire in creation and the only warmth in this entire cathedral came off her bare hand.

"If you touch me, it counts as tribute," he said. "Everything counts as tribute down here. The Queen's rules. And I am not," and here his voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture across four years of steady burning, "I am not giving that woman one more thing that's yours to give."

Elodie lowered her hand slowly. When she spoke, her voice was small and steady at once, which he remembered was exactly how she'd sounded the time she'd confessed to breaking the shrine window and blamed a cat.

"I came to steal you back," she said.

The nave went quiet. Even the fire seemed to pause mid-sip, an enormous indrawn breath of attention. Somewhere among the bone-lanterns, Wren whispered, *oh, this is going to be terrible,* in the tone of someone settling in with snacks.

"You can't," Tomas said.

"I've read the ledger terms."

"You've read *Maren's copy* of the ledger terms, which is wrong in eleven places, because Maren copies things while drunk on plum brandy to feel something." He laughed, and the laugh came out as sparks, actual sparks, little embers drifting from his lips and winking out in the cold air, and he saw her watch them fall and flinch at each one. "A keeper can't just be un-sold, Elodie. The fire doesn't release. It gets *spent*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There's maybe a third of me left that isn't flame. You'd be carrying home a pocketful of ash and a story."

"Then I'll steal the fire too."

"*There* it is," he said softly. "There's the great thief of Vantrelle. Walk in, lift the impossible prize, walk out. Did it occur to you to ask what happens to the city when the fire goes out? Did anyone upstairs mention that the wards drink from it? That the stars—" he gestured vaguely upward, at tons of mountain, "—are *lit by it*, in the way of things, in the old bargain nobody remembers the terms of anymore? You put out this fire, girl, and Vantrelle doesn't get its sky back. Vantrelle gets the dark. Permanently, and hungry."

"I know about the keystone," she said. "I know it was my memory. I know Corvin sold it. I've come to take it back and relight the wards."

"And my part in the plan? Where do I feature?" He couldn't help it; the fury was climbing his throat now, seven years of it, finding its voice at last. "Am I the diversion? Am I the tragic motivation? Because I've *read*, thank you, I've had four years and no books but ledgers, so I've read them all, and I know how stories like hers price a soul. Somebody burns so somebody belongs. That's the whole arithmetic of this place. So which is it, Elodie? Did you come to save me, or did you come to *need* me? Because those are different purchases, and I've already been bought once by someone who loved me, and I have opinions now about repeat business."

The words landed. He watched them land. He had wanted them to wound and they did, beautifully, terribly—and her face, as they did, was not the face of a girl caught out. It was the face of someone absorbing a blow she'd already taken once before and survived.

"You're right," she said.

That stopped him.

"You're right about all of it. About the arithmetic. About me." Her chin came up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely level. "At fourteen I chose myself over you and dressed it up as survival, and I got seven good years of not remembering that I'd done it. So I'm not going to stand here and swear this time is different out of the goodness of my heart, because I've seen my heart's itemized inventory and it's mostly receipts. But I am going to get you out of here, Tomas Ferro, and I am going to do it without paying this court one soul more than it has already stolen, and if the only way to do that is to steal the fire itself, then I will steal the *fire*, and it will be the greatest theft of my life, and afterward you can hate me in daylight, somewhere with trees."

Silence. The bone-lanterns swayed. The fire resumed its patient feeding, in through the ribs, out through the ribs.

*Tomas Ferro.* She'd said his full name. She shouldn't have known it—his name had been sold before hers was, that was the sequence, he'd checked the ledgers a hundred nights running like a man picking a scab—but there it was, spoken into the dark, and the dark below the mountain, which remembered all names, stirred at the sound of one spoken truly.

Somewhere in the cathedral's east transept, a bell that was not a bell rang once. Soft. Patient. Summons-shaped.

Ashen-Brigid sighed, a sound like a doused candle. "And there's the Queen. She heard the name too." The eldest keeper turned her kindless face toward Elodie, and even melted as it was, it managed pity. "Run along to your appointment, girl. And a word free of charge: whatever she offers you, she means it. That's the horror of her. Sarelle has never once lied down here. She doesn't need to. Truth is cheaper, and it burns longer."

Elodie hesitated. Looked at Tomas one more time—looked at him the way she must have looked at the hardest lock of her life, or maybe the way she'd looked at him at fourteen by the canal, he couldn't tell anymore, and hated that he couldn't tell.

"When I was fourteen," she said quietly, "there was a night I wanted to say something and said *nothing, idiot* instead. I remember it now. All of it." She turned toward the transept. "I'm done paying to forget."

And she walked toward the Queen.

Tomas stood in the fire's reach, blazing steadily, watching her go, and discovered that his hands were shaking, or that the light through them was, and that Wren had drifted to his side wearing the expression of a woman about to say something unbearably kind.

"Don't," he warned.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say she's different."

"...I was going to say," Wren admitted, "that she's the first person in four years who's walked into this cathedral and *argued with you*, and that you sparked when she said your name, and that you don't spark, Tomas, keepers don't spark, it's not in the nature of what we have left—"

"It's residue," he said. "Combustible gas. Chemistry."

"Sure," said Wren. "Chemistry."

Far off, in the east transept, doors of black bone opened for Elodie Joubert, and Queen Sarelle's voice came pouring out honeyed and hollow, welcoming her guest by a name no one had spoken in seven years:

"*Thief of my keystone. Come. Sit. We have so much to discuss about what things cost—and who, in the end, has to burn.*"
