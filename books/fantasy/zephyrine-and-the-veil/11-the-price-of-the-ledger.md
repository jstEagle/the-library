## 11. The Price of the Ledger

Sarelle had ruled the Undercourt for four hundred years, and in all that time she had learned one truth above every other: everyone, given enough darkness and enough time, will eventually ask how much they are worth.

Some asked too early. Those were the sad ones—the children who wandered down stairs that weren't there, offering their whole selves for a warm meal and a kind word. She turned those away, mostly. A soul spent all at once was a bonfire: bright, brief, wasteful. No, the ones she treasured were the slow bargainers. The ones who came down already hollowed out by the city above, selling a grief here, a first love there, keeping always a little back, as if they knew—without knowing—that someday they would need something left to trade.

The thief girl was going to be one of the great ones. Sarelle had known it since the ledger had first recorded the transaction, seven years ago. *One memory, childhood, whole and unbroken. Sold by Corvin of Vantrelle, foster-father, on behalf of the minor Elodie Joubert. Payment: survival, one winter.* And beneath that entry, in her own hand, she had written the note she wrote beneath every keystone purchase: *This one will come back.*

They all came back. But not all of them came back *interesting*.

She sat now on her throne—which was not a throne at all but an auditor's chair, high-backed and uncomfortable, because she had found over the centuries that comfort made brokers sloppy—and watched the girl walk into her court like someone entering a temple they'd been told was haunted and deciding, stubbornly, to be unimpressed.

Good. Stubbornness was fuel. The fire loved stubbornness.

"Elodie Joubert," Sarelle said, and her voice did what it always did—it arrived from everywhere at once, from the bone-lanterns and the guttering flame at the court's heart and the ribs of the cathedral ceiling itself, so that the girl flinched and then hated herself for flinching. "Seven years late. I had begun to think you'd died of ordinariness up there."

"I considered it," the girl said. Her chin was up. Her hands, Sarelle noted with professional appreciation, were doing something clever in her pockets—probably palming something, probably everything, probably the loose hunger off the nearest lantern if she could manage it. "Then I decided dying in Vantrelle would be redundant. The city's doing it to everyone already, now the wards are down."

"Yes." Sarelle let the word sit in the air like a coin on a counter. "That would be my fault, the way you've been telling the story to yourself on the way down. Shall I tell you the way it actually happened? It's shorter."

"I like stories."

"Everyone likes stories. That is why my business works." Sarelle rose, and the court shifted around her—not the guards, who were stone-faced and stone-hearted by design, but the lanterns themselves, leaning toward their queen like sunflowers toward a sun that had never quite risen for them. She descended the three steps of the dais. Up close, she knew what the girl saw: a woman beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful, correct in every line, faintly wrong in the eyes. She had stopped minding the wrongness a long time ago. Minding required a self to mind with, and hers had been sold in installments before this girl's grandmother was born.

"The wards of Vantrelle were not built on law," she said. "Your wardens will never admit this, but your Captain Reyne suspects it, which is why she let you pass. The wards were built on memory. One memory, keyed into the border-stones at the founding—a childhood, whole and unbroken, the memory of a city that remembered being small and safe and loved. Cities are like people. They forget why they locked their doors. So we gave them something to remember."

"You're saying my memory holds the walls."

"I'm saying a memory holds the walls. Yours, as it happens, because it was the finest I had ever handled—oh, don't make that face, I mean it as a compliment. You had a gift even as a child. Your memories *tasted* of more than other people's. When the fire feeds on them—" she gestured, lazily, at the great flame at the heart of the court, the last dragon-fire in the world, burning low and gold and hungry— "it sings. It hasn't sung in decades. Until recently."

The girl's eyes went, despite herself, to the keepers moving in the firelight—thin figures tending the flame, and among them one boy whose edges glowed faintly, whose chest showed embers through his skin like light through a shuttered window. Sarelle watched the recognition hit her. Watched her name him without naming him.

"Tomas," Elodie said quietly.

"Tomas," Sarelle agreed. "He has been very faithful. He has kept the fire alive for seven years, which is longer than most keepers manage, because he has something worth staying burned for." She smiled, and knew it was a terrible thing to see. "Guilt is excellent fuel. Better than love, honestly. Love burns clean and goes out. Guilt smolders."

"That's the bleakest thing anyone's ever said to me, and I grew up with Corvin."

Sarelle laughed—an actual laugh, surprised out of her, rusty as an old gate. Four hundred years, and still the sharp ones could catch her off guard. It was why she kept letting the sharp ones live long enough to reach the court. "Sit," she said, and a chair appeared—or rather, a chair had always been there and the girl only now noticed it, which amounted to the same thing in the Undercourt. "You want three things. You always wanted three things; I've read your file. Your memory. The boy. The city saved. You have come all this way hoping to steal all three, and you're already running the arithmetic on whether two out of three counts as victory."

Elodie sat. Slowly. Like someone sitting across from a card sharp and knowing the deck was marked. "What do you want?"

"Honesty. What a novelty. Sit still while I offer it to you." Sarelle took the auditor's chair opposite—she preferred negotiating at eye level; thrones encouraged the customer to feel robbed before the price was even named—and folded her hands. "The dragon-fire is dying. Not tonight, not this year, but soon, in the way mountains are soon. It was lit at the making of the world and it has been fed since then on souls, on memories, on whatever burns. Every keeper gives it years. Some give decades. Tomas has been giving it everything, and it is nearly done with him, and when it is done with him it will go out, and when *it* goes out, the wards fall forever, the stars stay dark, and Vantrelle becomes just another cold city full of people who can't remember why they're sad."

"So find another keeper."

"We do! Constantly. They last months. A year, if they're strong. The fire doesn't merely burn a keeper, child—it *remembers through* them. It needs someone who can hold an enormous amount of remembering without coming apart." She leaned forward. "Do you understand what I am describing? I am describing a talent. Yours. The rarest kind—the kind that can hold a whole life in one hand and not crush it. You've been using it to sell widows their dead husbands' voices for coppers. I want to use it to keep the lights on."

* * *

There it was. Sarelle watched the offer land the way she had watched it land a thousand times, in a thousand courts and pawnshops and midnight stairwells: the moment the mark understood that the terrible thing was also, somehow, the reasonable thing. That was her true craft—not the ledgers, not the lanterns, not the bone-cathedral with its awful beauty. Anyone could threaten. What Sarelle did was make the horror make sense.

"Here is the bargain," she said, gently now, the gentleness of a midwife or an undertaker, whichever the occasion demanded. "Give me yourself. Your memory stays where it is—I'll key it back into the border-stones myself, and the wards rise by morning, and the stars come home. The city is saved; no one up there ever has to know the price, and wouldn't sleep better for knowing anyway. In exchange, you take Tomas's place. He walks free. He gets to grow old in the sun and hate you or forgive you, whichever he chooses—both are his right, and I'll enforce either. And you become keeper. Not for seven years. For centuries, if you're as good as I think you are. The fire will remember through you, and you, who have spent your whole life handling other people's memories, will finally hold the oldest memory there is. The warmth at the beginning of the world."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the fire gutters, the wards fall, conscription becomes the least of Vantrelle's problems, and everyone in that city learns what the dark remembers about them." Sarelle spread her hands. "I won't pretend I'll enjoy it. I built nothing to see it broken. But someone must burn, Elodie. That is the whole of the arrangement, the one clause in every contract since the first morning. Someone must burn so that others may be warm. The only question anyone ever gets to answer is *who*, and *willingly* or *not*. You of all people know the mathematics. How many charms have you sold? How many grieving people walked out of your little transactions lighter, happier, warmer—because someone else's memory burned for them?"

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because they got to *keep* it. What you're asking is—" The girl stopped. Sarelle watched her reach for the argument and find, to her visible frustration, that it wasn't there. Good. The sharp ones always reached for it eventually. *It's different because I'm the one burning.* Yes. It was always different when it was you. That was the entire tragedy of the species, distilled into a pawnshop transaction.

"Let me tell you what nobody tells keepers before they agree," Sarelle said, softer still, because cruelty was easy and therefore boring, and she had been bored for four hundred years. "It doesn't hurt the way you fear. The fire takes your memories slowly, kindly, in order, like a story read backward. First your worst day goes, and you think: good riddance. Then your best day, and you think: wait. Then your mother's voice. Then the color of the door of the house you grew up in. And at the end there is almost nothing left, just warmth and purpose and the sense of having been useful, and I will be honest with you, child, because you've earned honesty: some nights I cannot remember whether I chose this. And it does not matter. The fire is warm. The city sleeps. That has to be enough, because it is all there is."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the cathedral, a lantern flickered hungrily and was hushed by its neighbor, the way one shushes a coughing child at a funeral.

"You believe this," Elodie said at last, and there was something in her voice Sarelle hadn't heard in centuries—not pity, exactly. Something adjacent to pity. Worse. "You actually believe someone has to burn. You're not evil. You're just—" she searched for the word— "*certain.*"

"I am old," Sarelle said. "Certainty is what's left when you've watched every alternative fail. I have seen revolutions, child. I have seen saints. I saw a man try to put the fire out once, believing the world owed itself warmth freely. The world got colder for a century. I have run the sums until the ledgers are worn through. Someone must burn." She rose, and the court rose with her, lanterns bowing. "So. The wards for the keeper's place. Tomas walks free into the sunrise. You burn warm and useful for a thousand years. Or you refuse, and I feed you to the lanterns piece by piece and the city falls anyway, and we both learn nothing. Which shall it be?"

And here—Sarelle had to give her credit, would write it in the margin of the ledger later, *this one will come back interesting*—the girl did neither. She didn't accept, and she didn't refuse.

Instead she looked past Sarelle, at the fire, at the boy standing in it with embers showing through his skin and seven years of hatred banked in his eyes like coals, and she said:

"Before I answer anything, I want to see it. The memory you bought from Corvin. Mine. If I'm going to trade my whole life on top of it, I want to look at what's already in your till. Show me what a seven-year-old me was worth."

Sarelle felt something turn over in her ancient chest, some instinct older than her certainty, whispering that this was the question she should not answer—that the ledger had rules, and among them, written in her own hand four centuries ago and never yet needed: *do not let the merchandise inspect itself.*

But she was certain, and certainty does not hear whispers.

"Done," said Queen Sarelle. "Tomas. Bring her to the fire. Let her remember."

The boy crossed the burning floor toward them, ember-lit, carrying seven years in his eyes, and Sarelle settled back onto her auditor's chair to watch the transaction unfold—never once suspecting that she had just opened the ledger to its own most dangerous page.
