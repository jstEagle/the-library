## 12. Seven Years Ago

The fire did not want to show her.

She felt its reluctance the way you feel a dog lean back against its leash—the whole weight of it pulling away from her hand. Tomas's soul was braided into the flame like a wick braided into a candle, and whatever she asked of it, she asked of him.

"Please," she said again. Her voice came out scraped and small in the vast dark of the cathedral hall. The bone-lanterns overhead swung on their chains, casting light that moved like something breathing. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I don't even know what I did. That's the whole— that's the entire problem, Tomas. Somebody took seven years of my life and I've been walking around inside the hole they left ever since."

The fire flared. Not anger, exactly. Something closer to a laugh with no humor left in it.

*You could just ask me,* the flames seemed to shape themselves into saying, though no words came. *You could ask, instead of taking.*

"I am asking."

And that—she understood at once that this was the wrong answer too, or maybe the first right one—because the fire shuddered, and settled low in its basin like an animal deciding to endure something, and gave her the memory back all at once.

* * *

Rain. She was nine years old, and it was raining the way it only rains in memories, which is to say it fell on her skin and her sodden hair and the cracked stone under her knees and somehow never touched anything else. The world beyond the rain was a smear of grey walls and greyer faces.

She knew these walls. She had arrived at them seven years ago with nothing in her pockets and less in her head—but in the memory, she was not yet the girl who arrived with nothing. In the memory, she still had everything. It was about to be taken, and she was going to be the one to take it.

A man crouched in front of her. Corvin, but younger—younger by more than the seven years since, younger than he had any right to be, his beard still mostly dark, his hands quick and clever as a conjurer's. He was not yet the tired creature who raised her. He was somebody's partner, then, in something small and criminal and warm.

"Elodie," he said gently. "Look at me, little star. Just look at me."

*I'm not supposed to talk to strangers,* said the girl who was her, out loud, in a voice Elodie had not heard in fourteen years and recognized the way you recognize your own front door. *Mama said.*

"Your mama," Corvin said, and there was real sorrow in it, "is the reason we're having this conversation."

The memory tilted, and Elodie saw what came before—not all of it, the edges were burnt away, but enough. A house outside the walls. A mother with ink-stained fingers and a talent for charms so small and honest the wardens had never thought to look for them: luck pressed into clover, courage folded into paper boats. A father already gone. And then wardens at the door anyway, because honesty was no protection when quotas needed filling, and a mother saying *run, little star, run and don't stop*, and a girl running until the city swallowed her, and then—

And then hunger. Three days of it. Four. A child alone in Vantrelle with a full heart and empty hands, learning that a full heart does not feed you, that nobody buys what a nine-year-old is selling, that the city only has one currency worth anything and children are rich in it.

Corvin had found her under the eaves of the tannery row. He hadn't taken anything. That was the thing she'd never known, the thing that rearranged everything: he had sat down next to her in the wet and shared his bread, and told her his name, and explained the trade the way you'd explain weather to a child. Gently. Honestly. As though it were simply a fact of the world, like rain.

"You have lovely things in you," the young Corvin said now, in the memory, his voice kind as a warm coat. "Memories. Firsts. They fetch a fair price if they're good ones, and yours are very good. You could eat tonight. You could eat every night."

*I don't want to sell them,* said little Elodie, stubborn even soaked through and starving. *They're mine.*

"Of course they're mine," Corvin agreed easily. "So we won't sell yours." He smiled, and there it was—the first lie, the seed of it, small as a spore. "We'll sell someone else's. That's how it's done. You find a buyer who wants a thing, and then you find a person who has it, and you make the trade. Nobody loses their own memories. Only fools lose their own."

*Whose?*

And here the memory went strange, because little Elodie—starving, frightened, nine years old and three days past her mother's voice—did not hesitate. Did not need to be convinced. Corvin mentioned a boy. A broker's client wanted a childhood friendship, something authentic, unsullied, the kind of bond that couldn't be manufactured—and there was such a boy, wasn't there, the cooper's son down the lane where she'd been sleeping, a boy who shared his supper with her the first night and his blanket the second and called her *star* because she'd told him she was named for one—

"Tomas," said little Elodie. Immediately. Before Corvin had finished describing what the buyer wanted. "Tomas would do it. He likes me. He'd give me anything."

The rain fell on nothing else in the world.

Elodie hung in the present, above the fire, above herself, and watched her own face say it, and felt something in her chest crack along a line she hadn't known was there.

*He wouldn't sell it,* Corvin said in the memory, probing, careful—a man testing ice. *It has to be given freely or it spoils. You'd have to convince him.*

*I can convince him.* Nine years old. Certain. Already good at this, already a thief in her bones before she'd stolen so much as a crust. *He trusts me.*

"And afterward?" Corvin asked softly. "Afterward they'll take the whole thread, do you understand? Not just the friendship. Everything stitched to it. He'll forget you. You'll forget him. Both sides of the coin. Is that a trade you want to make?"

Little Elodie was quiet for a moment. Rain ran off her chin.

*He'll forget his mama died,* she said. *That's in it too, isn't it? Everything stitched together. So really I'd be doing him a kindness.*

And Corvin—who had come to the tannery row to harvest a desperate child and had found, instead, a colleague—laughed. A short, startled bark of a laugh, half horror and half admiration, and Elodie watching from across fourteen years wanted to tear the sound out of the air with her hands.

*You're a monster,* the adult Elodie whispered to her small rain-soaked ghost. *You're a monster and you were mine.*

*No,* said the girl, and looked up—straight up, straight out of the memory, straight at her, which memories cannot do, which meant the fire was showing her true, which meant Tomas was letting her see it unflinched. *No. I was hungry. There's a difference. You should know. You've spent seven years telling yourself there isn't.*

* * *

The rest came in pieces, the way drowned things surface.

The broker's shop behind the fishmongers', smelling of brine and candle-wax. The transaction conducted over her head as though she weren't there, because legally—insofar as the trade had laws—she wasn't. Children couldn't hold ledgers. Corvin signed; Corvin collected; Corvin split the coin four ways the way partners did, and one share went into a clay pig with a chipped ear that she kept under a floorboard for six years.

Tomas in the cooper's yard, trusting as sunrise. *A game,* she told him. *There's a lady who pays for dreams. We split it. Half for you, half for me. Enough for boots before winter.* And Tomas laughing, saying she didn't need boots, saying she could have his coat, saying *anything for you, star, you know that*—and the terrible ease of it, the awful smooth machinery of trust turning beneath her small hands.

The moment itself. The broker's back room. Tomas's hand in hers across a scarred table, both of them nervous, both of them giggling, and the broker's voice like syrup: *think of your friend, think of everything she is to you, hold it all at once.* And Tomas closing his eyes and holding on, and Elodie feeling the thread go taut between them, feeling the pull begin—

—and understanding, one heartbeat too late, exactly what was about to happen to her side of it. Understanding that *both sides of the coin* had not been metaphor. Opening her mouth to say stop. Not saying it. Telling herself, in the last instant of her life as a girl who remembered being loved, that it was too late anyway, that the coin was already spinning, that stopping it now would mean all of this had been for nothing.

The coin came down.

The world went white.

When she woke in Corvin's rooms above the pawnshop—her rooms, eventually, the only home she'd ever known—she was empty. Clean as a licked plate. And Corvin sat beside her with his face in his hands, whether from grief or guilt or the simple professional embarrassment of a job gone sideways she had never known, because she had never known anything, because that was the point, because the hole in her had been dug by her own two hands and everyone who might have told her had been paid not to remember either.

* * *

Elodie came back to herself on the floor of the Undercourt with her cheeks wet and her throat raw and no memory of falling.

The fire burned low and steady. Watching her. Being him.

"You let me see all of it," she rasped. "Even the part where I—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "I sold you. Before he did. Before any of it. I sold you and I told myself it was a kindness."

The flames moved. Not words this time—just movement, restless, pained, a body shifting under old blankets.

"He didn't complete a transaction," Elodie said. The words came out of her slowly, each one a stone turned over. "That's what Corvin thinks he did. That's what *I* thought he did—he stood in that alley tonight and confessed like a man confessing, one night of stars for one night of survival. But he was finishing my work. I drew the ledger. I wrote the entry. I was nine years old and I was better at this than he was." A sound came out of her that was almost a laugh, and was terrible. "Seven years I've been furious at him. Seven years I built myself on top of the hole and called the hole somebody else's crime."

She got to her feet. Her legs shook. She made them hold.

"Why?" she asked the fire. "Why show me? You could have let me keep it. Let me go on thinking I was the victim. I'd have fought Sarelle for you anyway—I came down here to steal you back, that doesn't change."

But it changed. They both knew it changed. She had come down the stair that wasn't there believing she was retrieving a stolen thing, and stolen things make victims, and victims get to be righteous. She was not retrieving a stolen thing. She was returning one.

The fire spoke at last—not in words, but in shapes, in heat, and she understood it the way you understand a mother's tone before her language. It said: *because you asked to see it truly. Because he trusted you once and you spent it, and I will not spend my truth on lies, not even kind ones. Because if you are going to stand in front of the queen tomorrow and bargain for me, you will do it knowing what you are.*

"What am I?" Elodie whispered.

The fire flared bright—brighter than it had burned in years, the bone-lanterns singing in their chains, the whole cathedral of the traded dead lit gold for one impossible heartbeat—and in the heart of the light stood the outline of a boy of fourteen, arms crossed, one eyebrow raised, wearing exactly the expression he'd worn whenever she'd talked herself into trouble.

*Yours,* the fire said. *Same as ever. Mine to give.*

Then it banked, and he was gone, and she was alone in the enormous dark with a truth the size of seven years sitting on her chest.

Which was when she heard the footsteps.

Not the shuffling of the court's hollow attendants, not the silk-drag of Queen Sarelle's train. Boots. Measured ones. Coming down the stair that wasn't there, at a pace that belonged to someone who knew the stairs' tricks and had stopped being impressed by them—and behind that, fainter, a second set of steps she knew even better, uneven on the left, favoring an old knee.

Elodie wiped her face with both hands, stood straight, and prepared to be recognized by the two men in the world with the best reasons to hate her.

"Hello, little star," said Corvin's voice out of the dark, ragged and resigned. "I see you found the fire. I see the fire found you right back."
