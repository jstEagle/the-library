## 13. The Thief Who Steals Herself

The first thing Elodie stole in the Undercourt was a guard's loyalty.

She hadn't planned it. Planning had gone out the window somewhere around the fourth rearranged staircase, and what remained was the oldest instinct she owned: when cornered, pick a pocket. The guard—a broad-shouldered woman with lantern-light pooling in the hollows of her eyes—had caught Elodie crouched behind a bone-lantern stand, and instead of shouting she'd simply looked at her. Looked at her with that terrible Undercourt patience, the way a spider looks at a fly it has already decided not to hurry about.

"Queen's business?" the guard asked.

"Yes," said Elodie, who was learning that in this place, confidence was a currency too.

But the guard's hand was already rising toward the horn at her hip, and there was something in her face—not cruelty, nothing so interesting as cruelty. Just duty, worn smooth as a river stone, pointing her at Elodie like a compass needle.

Elodie reached out with her talent before she could think better of it.

It was not like lifting a memory from a pawnshop ledger. Those were loose things, unclaimed, griefs and first kisses abandoned like coats at a funeral. This was *woven in*. The guard's loyalty to Queen Sarelle was threaded through everything she was—through her posture, through the calluses on her sword hand, through the name of some dead sergeant she'd murmured in her sleep for twenty years. To take it, Elodie had to unpick the whole seam.

She did it anyway.

The loyalty came away into her cupped hands like a length of grey silk, cool and heavy, humming faintly with the memory of orders obeyed. The guard blinked. Her hand dropped from the horn. She looked at the space where her certainty used to be the way a person looks at a missing tooth—with tongue probing, bewildered, half-convinced it was never there at all.

"Was I—" the guard began.

"You were going to show me where the fire-keepers rest," Elodie said, sliding the stolen loyalty into the inner pocket of her coat, next to her heart, where all her best work went. "You were very insistent."

"I was," the guard agreed slowly, and something in her face went strange and soft. "I've been standing at this post for eleven years. I don't think anyone's ever asked me to walk anywhere."

They walked together. The guard—Bres, her name was Bres, though Elodie hadn't asked and didn't need to; names came free with loyalty, tangled in the weave—pointed out which corridors were warded, which lanterns were hungry, which of the queen's servants were hollow and which were merely tired. Bres talked more in one hour of disloyalty than she presumably had in eleven years of service, and most of what she said was complaints about the lighting.

"The lanterns eat," she explained, as they passed a bone-lantern whose flame leaned toward them like a flower toward sun. "That's why we rotate the posts. Stand too long near one and it starts sipping at you. You don't notice at first. You just stop remembering what you liked about being alive."

"Lovely place," Elodie said.

"It grows on you." Bres paused. "Or off you. One of those."

* * *

Here is what Elodie learned in the next six hours, huddled in an alcove behind a curtain of woven hair (she chose not to investigate whose), scratching notes into the margin of Maren's map with a stub of charcoal:

Her gift worked differently down here.

Above ground, she stole memories that people had set down or lost grip of—the widow's grief left in a ledger, the dead mother's voice sold by someone who'd run out of other things to sell. Loose change. Down here, in the court where souls were coin, everything was still held. Still *wanted*. Which meant every theft cost her.

Not memories. Not yet. But something. A little cold in the fingertips. A fainter taste for the honey-cakes Tomas had once bought her at the autumn fair. Small prices, itemized invisibly, and she kept a running tally in her head because a thief who doesn't know her own losses is a thief about to be robbed blind.

And here was the second lesson: the court itself could be picked.

The bone-lanterns were the proof. Each one burned with a fragment of hunger—a soul's appetite for warmth, for light, for company, harvested and bottled. And appetite, it turned out, was a thing you could lift.

She'd tested it on the smallest lantern she could find, a stubby thing burning in a niche like a votive candle in a church nobody attended. When she drew its hunger out, the flame didn't go out. It simply stopped *reaching*. Sat there in its glass prison like a cat that had been fed, utterly indifferent to the world.

Then she'd made her mistake, because she was Elodie Joubert and mistakes were her native language: she'd put the hunger in her pocket with the loyalty.

By the time she noticed, the hunger had eaten through the pocket lining, the hem of her coat, and was working on the concept of her left boot. It wasn't eating fabric—it was eating her *attention*, pulling her thoughts toward it in a warm bright stream until she caught herself leaning toward the alcove wall with her mouth slightly open like a fool at a bakery window.

She wrestled it back out and stuffed it into an empty charm-vial, corked it, and wrapped the vial in three layers of scarf. Even then she could feel it through the wool, tugging gently, patiently, like a fish on a line.

*Right,* she thought, adding to her tally. *Hunger keeps. Loyalty keeps. But they keep being themselves. They don't turn into anything else.*

A memory could be reshaped—warmed, softened, sold as comfort. These were rawer goods. Stolen loyalty stayed loyal; it just needed somewhere new to point. That gave her an idea so simple and so wicked that she laughed out loud alone in the dark, then clapped a hand over her mouth and listened hard for ten minutes.

Nothing came. In the Undercourt, apparently, even the dark minded its own business.

* * *

The second guard took an hour, and it nearly went wrong.

He was younger than Bres, posted at the mouth of the corridor that led—according to both Maren's map and Bres's grumbling directions—to the fire-pit where the keepers stood. Where Tomas stood. Elodie had been circling that corridor for two hours the way you circle a cold swim, finding reasons, and she knew exactly what she was doing and hated herself for it with a clean, familiar heat.

She couldn't see him yet. That was the whole problem. Seven years ago she had sold a boy's name to save her own skin, and she had paid good money to forget doing it, and now she was going to have to stand in front of him with his soul unraveling in a fire and say something. There were no words in any language she knew that fit the occasion. She'd checked.

So: the guard. Distraction first. Feelings later.

Except he wasn't distracted easily. He stood at his post like a nail driven into the world, and when she crept close enough to work, she found his loyalty wasn't loose thread but iron chain—forged to Sarelle so long ago that it had grown into him, roots through stone. She pulled. He turned his head.

"There you are," he said pleasantly.

Elodie froze in the posture of every thief since the dawn of thieving: the frozen crouch, the wide eyes, the profound commitment to the theory that if she couldn't see him clearly, perhaps the reverse was also true.

"You're the thief," the guard said, with the mild delight of a man spotting a famous actor at a market stall. "The queen said you'd come. She's got a wager going with the chamberlain about whether you'd cry at the gate."

"Wonderful. Who won?"

"No one yet." He hadn't moved to seize her. His loyalty was forged deep, but Elodie had hold of one strand now, and strands are strands. "Can I ask you something? While you're doing whatever that is you're doing?"

"...Go ahead."

"When you take a thing from someone"—he tapped his own chest, right over the chain she was unpicking—"does it leave a mark?"

She stopped pulling. "Why?"

"Because I've served here thirty years," he said, "and I remember being posted above ground, and I remember my daughter's face, and I do not remember loving either one. I know I did. There's paperwork." He smiled, and it was the loneliest smile Elodie had ever seen on a living man. "I just wondered if the taking feels different from the giving."

For a moment Elodie forgot she was a thief mid-job and was simply a person, standing in the dark, being asked a question that deserved an honest answer.

"It feels different," she said quietly. "Giving is a choice. Taking is—" She flexed her fingers, where the cold always started. "Taking is a debt. You carry it. Whether anyone makes you pay or not."

"Hm," said the guard, and then, because he was a courteous man underneath thirty years of rusted chain: "You should probably finish. The relief shift comes at the bell."

So she finished. The chain came out of him link by link, and it was heavy—so much heavier than Bres's fresh-spun silk—and when it was done he swayed like a man stepping off a boat after a long voyage. Then he sat down against the wall, in the middle of his post, deserting for the first time in three decades, and began to cry without any noise at all.

Elodie crouched beside him. She didn't touch him. Some things you don't get to comfort when you're the cause.

"Your daughter," she said. "What was her name?"

"I told you. I don't—"

"But you know there's a name. Reach for it. It'll be under the paperwork."

He reached. His face changed. "Marisol," he whispered, and wept harder, and it was a much better quality of weeping than before.

Elodie wound the stolen chain around her forearm, under her sleeve, where it lay cold as a shackle. Then she did something no fence had ever taught her, no rule of trade permitted, and which she intended to make up rules for later: she took Marisol's name out of the guard's remaining memories—just the name, just the sound of it, freely given with a nod—and pressed it back into him like a splint on a broken finger. Not stolen. *Lent.*

"Go find her," she said. "If she's alive up there, go find her. That's not an order. It's a suggestion from a stranger."

He went. And Elodie stood in the empty corridor, arm aching under the weight of thirty years of forged loyalty, and understood that she had just crossed some line she couldn't see and couldn't uncross.

She had started stealing *back*. Not coins, not charms. Pieces of the court itself. And every piece she took made the queen's machine a little looser, a little louder in its joints—

—and every piece was also a debt added to a ledger that already had her childhood written on it in ink she recognized as her own handwriting.

*One thing at a time,* she told herself sternly. *First the boy. Then the redemption arc.*

* * *

The lanterns were the last lesson, and the hardest.

The fire-pit corridor was lined with them—dozens of bone-lanterns, each holding a sliver of hunger, and between them, in the flickering gaps, she could hear them whispering. Not words. Appetites. *Warm, warm, warm,* they said, in the voice of every soul who had ever been cold and afraid, and Elodie walked the gauntlet with her teeth clenched and her hands fisted in her pockets, feeling like a woman wading upstream through a river of want.

Halfway down, she stopped.

Because she recognized one of the hungers.

It was small and stubborn and it wanted—not warmth, not exactly. It wanted a specific warmth. Woodsmoke and cinnamon and a particular crooked smile. It wanted an autumn fair and honey-cakes bought with coins earned honestly for once. It wanted—

It wanted what *she* remembered wanting, seven years ago, at fourteen, before she'd sold the whole day to a broker to buy herself a night of survival.

The lantern was burning a piece of her. Or a piece of someone so much like her that the distinction had stopped mattering somewhere down the long stair that wasn't there.

Elodie stood in the whispering dark and felt the full architecture of the trap she'd walked into. Sarelle hadn't built a prison. She'd built a *mirror*, hall after hall of it, and every reflection showed Elodie a version of herself she'd sold, spent, or abandoned. Come to steal a memory? Here are forty. Come to rescue a boy? Here is the girl who betrayed him, preserved in glass, still hungry.

"Fine," Elodie said aloud, to the lantern, to the mirror, to whoever was keeping score. "You want to play at theft?"

She lifted the hunger out of the lantern—her hunger, the fourteen-year-old's hunger, sweet and sharp and unbearably young—and instead of pocketing it, she did something new. She folded it, the way she'd fold a charm for a widow, the way she'd fold comfort into grief so it could be carried instead of carried away. She wrapped the little hunger in the memory of the fair itself—the smoke, the cinnamon, the crooked smile—and sealed it shut.

Then she set it back in the lantern.

The flame flared green-gold, once, twice—and settled. Not reaching anymore. *Full.* For the first time in however many years, the lantern wasn't hungry. It was just warm. It would burn like that for a while, maybe a long while, content, and no one would sip at it again.

Down the corridor, another lantern guttered in what she could have sworn was surprise.

"Oh," Elodie said softly. "Oh, that's how it works."

Not theft. *Restitution.* Take the wound, wrap it in its own medicine, give it back healed. Her talent had never been a knife. It had been a needle all along—she'd just only ever used it to pick locks.

She went down the line of lanterns, one by one, mending hungers, her fingers going colder and colder and her tally growing longer and longer, and she worked fast because she had maybe an hour before the court noticed what she was doing, and less than that before Corvin—or Ilsa, or ruin, or all three—came looking for her.

At the end of the corridor, past the last mended lantern, the fire-pit opened up before her: a cathedral of heat, and in its center a pillar of dying gold, and standing in the pillar a figure made of ember whose face she knew even now, even after everything.

Tomas turned his head.

He looked at her across seven years and one betrayal, and the first thing he said was not a curse, which she'd braced for, nor forgiveness, which she hadn't earned.

It was: "You're doing it wrong. The third lantern—you wrapped it too tight. It'll smother."

Elodie stared at him. Behind her, faintly, the third lantern coughed.

"...You can see me working?"

"Tomas has been watching the lanterns for seven years," said the boy made of ember, with a ghost of the crooked smile she'd just spent wrapping into glass. "Someone down here finally knows the difference between a knot and a stranglehold. Get over here, Elodie. We haven't got an hour. And you should know—the queen already knows you're mending things." His ember-bright eyes flicked past her shoulder, toward the dark. "And she's sent someone to stop you. Someone you know."

The shadows at the mouth of the corridor shifted, and Elodie turned to see a familiar silhouette limned in bone-light, carrying a broker's case.

Corvin.
