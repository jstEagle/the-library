## 14. Wardens at the Door

Ilsa Reyne had been a warden for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned exactly one universal truth about paperwork: the more monstrous the order, the thicker the vellum it arrived on.

The conscription writ was thick as a hymnal. It came sealed with three ribbons—grey for the Wardens, white for the Magistracy, black for whatever office had decided, in its wisdom, that the correct response to an apocalypse of darkened stars was to shake the city upside down until talented people fell out of it like loose coins.

"Read it aloud," said Deputy-Commander Harsk, who had a face like a closed ledger and a voice like a man reading his own obituary in advance. "To the assembled ranks."

They were assembled in the courtyard of the Third Wardenhouse, four hundred strong, and they were looking at her the way soldiers look at an officer when they already know what she's going to say and have already decided how much of it to believe.

Ilsa unrolled the writ. The wax seals clicked against each other like teeth.

"'By order of the Magistracy of Vantrelle,'" she read, "'all persons possessing unregistered talent are hereby remanded to the custody of the Office of Restoration, for assessment of utility in the re-keying of the border wards. Wardens are instructed to identify, detain, and deliver such persons by any means necessary.'" She paused. There was a line at the bottom, smaller, in that apologetic italic the lawyers used when even they couldn't quite look at what they'd written. "'The term *any means necessary* is understood to include force of arms.'"

She rolled the writ up again.

"That's it?" someone called from the back. It was Ferren, a young warden with cowlicked hair and the unfortunate habit of asking questions everyone else was smart enough to swallow. "That's all it says?"

"It also says good morning," Ilsa said. "Dismissed."

Harsk's jaw did something complicated. "Commander Reyne—"

"Deputy-Commander." She handed him the writ. "File it under the same drawer as the last emergency decree. You know the one. The one we swore we'd never need."

* * *

The trouble with being the best servant of a bad law, Ilsa reflected that afternoon from the roof of the Third Wardenhouse, is that nobody ever asks whether you liked the law. They only ask whether you kept it.

Below her, Vantrelle was coming apart at the seams with remarkable efficiency. The conscription sweeps had started at dawn, and by noon the city had answered with the particular fury of people who had already lost their stars and refused to lose anything else. Riots bloomed in the Salt Quarter, spread along the Tanners' Row like spilled ink, and pooled now around the gates of every wardenhouse in the city. Somewhere to the east, the old grain market was burning—not out of hunger but out of principle, which was somehow worse, or better; Ilsa hadn't decided.

And through it all, threading between rooftops and down stairwells and into the mountain itself, there was Elodie Joubert, carrying Ilsa's one hope down a staircase that probably didn't exist.

Every hour Ilsa held this pass open bought the girl another hour. Every hour cost something else.

"You're brooding," said Sergeant Ovidio, arriving beside her with two cups of chicory that he was pleased to call coffee. He was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and had served under three commanders before her, all of whom he described as "adequate." It was the highest praise he issued.

"I'm strategizing," Ilsa said.

"Strategizing is just brooding with a map." He handed her a cup. "How's our girl?"

"Down the mountain since yesterday. If the maps are honest."

"And if they're not?"

Ilsa watched a column of wardens below try to form a line against a crowd of fishwives and failed utterly, mostly because the fishwives knew all of them by name and were using it. "Then she's dead somewhere dark, and everything I'm about to do is pointless, and I'd rather not finish that sentence."

Ovidio nodded sagely and drank his chicory. "So. What's the strategy?"

"The strategy," Ilsa said, "is that the Office of Restoration has requisitioned this pass as a staging ground for their descent teams. They want to come down the mountain behind her. In strength. With lanterns and hammers and the institutional patience of a tax audit."

"That sounds bad."

"It's very bad. For her. Because the Undercourt—" and here Ilsa lowered her voice, though there was no one on the roof but pigeons, "—is a place that rearranges itself around what people bring into it. Four hundred wardens marching in formation would be a banquet. One thief walking quiet might slip through the cracks. Literally. So my strategy is to make sure no one follows her."

Ovidio looked at her for a long moment. "You're going to refuse a direct requisition. Sealed in black ribbon."

"I'm going to do something much cleverer than refusing," Ilsa said. "Refusing gets me arrested by lunchtime. I'm going to comply *slowly*."

* * *

The art of bureaucratic sabotage, as Ilsa explained to her senior staff over the evening meal, was really the art of taking an order more seriously than anyone wanted you to.

"They've asked for a staging ground at the mountain pass," she said, chalk in hand, drawing on the slate board they used for patrol rotations. "Fine. A staging ground requires certain things. Survey of the approach roads—that's a day, minimum, because the surveyors' guild has lost two men to the riots and won't travel without escort, and escort comes from us. Structural assessment of the old toll-house—that's another day, because the toll-house is four hundred years old and the masons will insist on tapping every stone. Provisions for a garrison of four hundred—" she let that hang, "—which must be requisitioned through the Quartermaster's Office, whose entire warehouse district is currently on fire."

Ferren raised his hand, then thought better of it, then raised it anyway. "Ma'am. Isn't this just... lying?"

"Lying is saying false things, Warden Ferren. I intend to say exclusively true things, slowly, in triplicate." She set down the chalk. "The Magistracy built this machine to grind people. Nobody ever specified the speed of the gears. That's ours. That's always been ours."

Sergeant Ovidio, in the corner, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been indigestion. With him it was usually both.

"There'll be consequences," said Lieutenant Casz, quietly. She was the youngest of the seniors and the most honest about fear. "When they find out. And they'll find out."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "They will." She thought of her father, who had been a warden before her, and his father before him, and the little brass plaque above her desk that read *THE WALL HOLDS* in letters worn nearly smooth by three generations of thumbs. She thought of the night the stars went out, and how she had stood in the courtyard watching four hundred wardens look up at a sky stripped bare, and how not one of them had asked *what do we do* — they had all turned and looked at *her*, waiting, trusting, and she had given them curfews and checkpoints and lies about temporary phenomena, because that was the job, because the wall holds, because somebody has to stand in the doorway between panic and the people.

But a doorway, she was beginning to understand, could hold in more than one direction.

"When they find out," she said, "I want them to find out that while we were slow, nobody got conscripted from this district. Nobody got dragged from their beds. Nobody got hurt in our name." She looked around the table. "The law is the best thing we have and the worst thing we serve. Some nights those are the same thing. Tonight they're not. Are there objections?"

There were none. Ovidio poured himself more chicory and said, "I'll get the surveyors some very thorough tea," and left.

* * *

For three days, it worked beautifully.

The survey of the approach roads took two days instead of one because Ilsa personally insisted on accompanying it—command presence, she explained, was essential—and because she walked at exactly the pace of the slowest man, who happened to be Ovidio, who had decided to develop a limp. The structural assessment of the toll-house took so long that the chief mason submitted a written apology for its length, which Ilsa filed with genuine warmth. The provisions requisition came back from the Quartermaster's Office stamped *RECEIVED, PENDING REVIEW*, which was the single most beautiful phrase in the Vantrellan language.

On the second night, refugees began coming down out of the hills toward the pass—not fleeing the city, Ilsa realized, but fleeing *toward* the mountain, toward the rumor that someone had gone down beneath it to fix what was broken above. They camped at the treeline in the hundreds. Fishermen, weavers, a whole family of candle-makers whose trade had died with the stars twice over. They had nothing. Several of them pressed small offerings on the wardens anyway: a heel of bread, a carved whistle, a child's drawing of stars done in charcoal, stuck hopefully to the toll-house door.

Ilsa stood watch over that camp each night, and each night the crowd grew, and each night she thought: *she's still walking. Or she's stopped. Either way, hold the door.*

On the third night, the black ribbon came for her.

* * *

He arrived at midnight with twelve Magistracy bailiffs and a portable writing desk, which told Ilsa everything she needed to know about him before he introduced himself. No man brings a portable writing desk to a riot city unless he believes, deep in his soul, that paper is stronger than people.

"Investigator Pell," he said, setting the desk up in the middle of her own command yard as if it were his. His voice was mild, his eyes were not. "Office of Restoration. Commander Reyne, your staging ground is three days late and counting."

"Investigator." She offered the chair she kept for difficult visitors. He declined it, preferring to stand over his desk, which she respected—it took real commitment to be that unpleasant on purpose. "The delays are documented. Survey complications. Masonry concerns. Logistics."

"Mm." He opened a folio. "Curious thing, Commander. My office reviewed your documentation. The masonry report cites structural weakness in the toll-house. The toll-house, notably, that your own garrison has been living in for a week without incident." He glanced up. "Would you like to revise anything?"

"The masons are artists," Ilsa said. "They worry."

"They do." Pell drew out a single sheet and laid it flat, smoothing it with two fingers like a man petting a cat he intended to drown. "Here's what I think, Commander. I think a thief went down your mountain three days ago. I think you knew. I think you've been building a wall of paperwork between my office and that door ever since." He tapped the sheet. "This is an arrest warrant. Yours. Drafted tonight, signed this morning by a magistrate who has developed strong opinions about obstruction."

The yard had gone very quiet. Her wardens were at their posts, but "at their posts" had a texture tonight—a stillness, a leaning-in. Ferren's hand had drifted near his baton. Ovidio hadn't moved at all, which from Ovidio was louder than shouting.

"Before you do anything rash," Pell said pleasantly, "consider: the woman downstairs in the mountain may already be dead. Whatever she's doing, it hasn't worked yet. The stars are still out, Commander. Look up. Still out. So the question before us is simple arithmetic: your career against four hundred trained descent personnel who could be through that door within the hour." He smiled. It was the worst smile Ilsa had ever seen, and she'd seen it on magistrates. "Or shall I put it another way? Your career against the city."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment.

Then she looked past him, up, at the sky. Black, starless, flat as painted tin. Three days of black. But—

Something was wrong with the black. Or right with it. High above the mountain, at the exact place where the peak cut the sky like a knife, the darkness had a seam. A hairline crack of grey. And inside the grey—

"Ferren," she said softly. "Look up."

Ferren looked. Then Ovidio. Then the whole yard, four hundred faces tilting back, and a sound went through them like wind through wheat.

In the crack above the mountain, faint as breath on glass, something glimmered. Not a star. Not yet. More like the memory of where a star should be—the sky remembering itself, one seam at a time.

"She's alive," Ilsa said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Whatever she's doing, Investigator, it's working. Slowly. From underneath. Which means the only thing standing between that light and your hammer-and-lantern expedition is me, and I regret to inform you—" she stepped forward, and Pell, to his credit, actually stepped back, "—that I am extremely slow."

Pell recovered himself. The mildness came back down over his face like a shutter. "Commander Reyne, you are under arrest for obstruction of—"

"No," said Ovidio.

It was not a dramatic word. He said it the way he said everything, like a man noting that it looked like rain. But he said it while stepping forward, and behind him, so help her, four hundred wardens of the Third Wardenhouse stepped forward too, in the shuffling, inevitable way of a tide, and Ferren planted himself directly in front of the arrest warrant like a boy guarding his dinner.

Pell looked around the yard. Twelve bailiffs looked around with him, and did arithmetic of their own.

"You'd take her side," Pell said to Ovidio. "Against the writ. Against the ribbons. You've worn the grey thirty years."

"Forty-one," Ovidio said. "And I never once wore it against the people under the wall. That's the whole job, son. Everything else is filing."

For a moment—one long, teetering moment—Ilsa genuinely believed Pell would order his bailiffs to draw. She could see him weighing it, see the desk and the folio and forty years of believing in paper all tipping toward the edge. And then the crowd at the treeline began to sing.

It started with the candle-maker children, thin voices in the dark, an old harvest song about lights coming home. Then the fishermen picked it up, and the weavers, and some of her own wardens hummed along without quite meaning to, and the sound rose off the mountainside and wrapped the yard in something that no writ covered and no ribbon sealed.

Pell packed up his desk.

He did it with tremendous dignity, closing each latch precisely, tucking the warrant back into its folio, and at the gate he turned and said, "This isn't over, Commander. The Magistracy has other passes. Other doors."

"Then I hope," Ilsa said, "that whoever holds them is slower than I am."

* * *

Afterward, she climbed the toll-house roof alone, as she had every night, and sat with her cold cup of chicory and looked at the seam in the sky.

It was wider than it had been at midnight. Faint points kindled inside it now—two, maybe three—not bright enough to name, not yet, but unmistakably *star-shaped*, in the way that a sprout is unmistakably a tree.

"Hold the door," she murmured to the mountain. To the thief inside it, and whoever else was down there in the dark with her. "We're slow up here. Take all the time you need."

Behind her, boots on the ladder—Ovidio, bringing fresh chicory and, no doubt, news.

"Commander," he said, settling beside her with a grunt, "you should hear this before anyone else does. Watchman just came in from the city. Says there's a man asking after the pass. Older fellow. Thief, by his hands. Says—" Ovidio squinted at the note in the lamplight. "Says his daughter's down the mountain, and he means to buy her out. Brought his whole life savings to pay for it, apparently. Won't say more."

Ilsa stared at the seam of grey light, and felt, for the first time in days, something that wasn't dread.

"Let him through," she said. "Quickly. Before I learn to be slow about it."
