## 15. Corvin Descends

The stair that wasn't there had, in Corvin Joubert's professional opinion, been built by someone with a grudge against knees.

He had been descending for what felt like two days and one very long hour, depending on which of his memories he consulted—and lately they disagreed with each other the way old married couples disagree, politely and with deep historical grievance. His left knee remembered a fall from a Vantrelle rooftop in his forties. His right knee insisted it had never happened. Both knees agreed, however, that the stairs were too tall by half, too narrow by a hand's width, and that they turned when they thought he wasn't looking.

"Stop that," he told the stairwell.

The stairwell did not stop. It simply rearranged itself with the smug patience of furniture that knew it would outlive him.

Corvin leaned against the wall to catch his breath and caught, instead, his reflection in the dark stone—a thin man of sixty-odd years with a thief's shoulders gone soft and a face that had once been called handsome by people who owed him money. He touched the inner pocket of his coat, where the memory sat wrapped in oilcloth like the precious, poisonous thing it was.

*Elodie's location.* That was the goods. A warden-captain's blind eye, a hidden door, a girl going down into the dark to steal back what he'd sold. He had watched her go with the same expression, he suspected, that he'd worn seven years ago when he'd taken her childhood from her sleeping head: the expression of a man doing arithmetic with things that should never be counted.

"One night of stars for one night of survival," he said aloud, to hear how it sounded.

It sounded worse down here. Everything did. The dark beneath the mountain had a way of holding your words a moment longer than necessary, like a pawnbroker examining a coin for clipping.

He kept walking. There was a toll, Maren's maps had warned him—each landing took a small memory as payment, plucked lightly from behind the ear like a coin from a mark's purse. The first landing had taken the name of his third fencing master. The second had taken the smell of rain on the Vantrelle docks, which he mourned genuinely, because it had been one of the good ones. The third had taken—

He stopped.

He had been about to think *the third had taken—* and there was nothing there. A smooth place in his mind, like a wall with the plaster scraped off. He stood a long moment, breathing, one hand flat against the cold stone.

"Well," he said. "Serves me right, probably."

He didn't know what he'd lost. That was the trick of it, the cruelty dressed as mercy: you only felt the shape of the hole. And Corvin had spent seven years being a man made mostly of holes, walking through Vantrelle with an empty room inside his chest where a daughter-shaped thing used to be, knowing only that he had sold something enormous and could not remember why it had seemed worth it at the time.

Except he *could* remember why. That was the worst part. He remembered the debt, the men with the ledger and the quiet voices, the arithmetic of survival—he remembered all of that perfectly, because the Undercourt had never asked him to pay for remembering his sins. Only for the good things.

The fourth landing took the taste of wedding cake. He had no idea whose wedding. He stood at the top of the next flight laughing until the laugh turned into something else, and then he wiped his face with his sleeve like a sensible man and kept going.

* * *

The dark began to remember his name about an hour in.

It started as a whisper at the edge of hearing, the way a market crowd sounds from three streets away. Then it grew distinct, and Corvin realized with a lurch that the walls were murmuring *Corvin, Corvin, Corvin* in a hundred different voices, some of them dead, one of them—God help him—his mother's.

"Yes, yes," he said gruffly. "Corvin Joubert, at your service, none of your business. Any of you lot know the way to the court, or do you just enjoy saying names?"

The whispers giggled. Actually giggled. Somewhere in the black, something that had once been a person found a debt-collector's discomfort funny, and Corvin decided he couldn't even blame it.

"The thief," said a voice near his elbow, dry as old paper. "The one who sells. We remember you."

"I'm flattered."

"We are not complimenting you." The voice drifted along the wall, keeping pace. "We are telling you what you are. There is a difference. You will understand the difference soon."

"Wonderful," Corvin muttered. "I've missed being threatened. It's very homey."

But his hand had gone to his coat pocket again, to the oilcloth, and the whisper laughed softly—one note of it almost kind—and let him walk on in peace.

He used the walking to rehearse.

That was the habit of a lifetime: before every job, run the script. *Madam Queen, esteemed broker, terror of the deep places—I bring you a rare commodity, freely offered, freshly harvested: the current location of the thief Elodie Joubert, who is even now descending your stairs with theft in her heart.* And the queen would pay for it, because queens always paid for intelligence, and the payment he wanted wasn't gold. Gold was useless here; he'd figured that out by the second toll. What he wanted was time. An hour. A single hour of the queen's attention pointed anywhere but at Elodie while the girl did whatever mad thing she'd come to do.

An hour bought with betrayal. He'd rehearsed that part too, and rehearsed it, and each rehearsal rang falser than the last, until finally, somewhere around the fifth landing, he had stopped rehearsing and started listening to the other voice in his head—the small, stubborn, infuriatingly familiar one that sounded exactly like a fourteen-year-old girl sitting on his kitchen counter stealing plums, saying: *You're really going to sell me out twice, old man? Twice?*

"No," Corvin told the dark. "No, I bloody well am not."

The dark, for once, said nothing back.

* * *

The Undercourt announced itself first by light.

After hours of blackness, the glow ahead was almost offensive—a deep amber seep that stained the bone-lanterns hanging along the final gallery, each one flickering with the restless light of something alive and unhappy about it. Corvin had seen soul-lanterns before, in his line of work. He had never gotten used to them, and he was glad of that. A man who got used to them was a man who should stop calling himself a thief and start calling himself what the brokers were.

Then the cathedral opened around him, vast as a held breath, and Corvin—who had cased the vaults of four banks and the bedchambers of two duchesses—stopped in the doorway and forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

Bone-lanterns by the thousand, rising in arches like ribs. The great guttering fire at the far end, huge and dim and starving, its light the color of a dying hearth. And everywhere the traded dead, moving quietly among the pillars or standing in ranks like soldiers awaiting orders that never came, their faces wearing that particular smooth patience he recognized from mirrors.

And on the fire's threshold, a boy.

Even at this distance, Corvin knew him. Tomas. He'd fed the boy plum tarts and taught him to pick a vest pocket and watched him and Elodie orbit each other that whole bright terrible summer like two sparks from the same strike. The boy was half-consumed now, ember-lit, his outline wavering the way heat makes air waver—but he stood at the fire's edge with his hands open, feeding it somehow, and the look on his ruined young face was not agony.

It was vigilance. He was watching the doors. All of them.

Watching, Corvin understood a moment later, for her.

"Oh, you absolute fool," Corvin whispered, and could not have said which of them he meant.

"You are late," said Queen Sarelle.

She had not been there. Now she was, seated on a throne of stacked ledgers near the center of the court, and she was not what Corvin expected, which was the first bad sign. He had imagined a monster. Monsters were easy. This was a woman of indeterminate age with tired, beautiful eyes and a stillness like deep water, and when she looked at him, Corvin felt inventoried down to the fillings in his teeth.

"Traffic on the stairs," he said, and bowed the exact depth a fence bows to a buyer he needs more than she needs him. "Corvin Joubert, madam. Broker of small recollections, at your—"

"I know what you are." She did not raise her voice. The whole cathedral leaned in anyway. "You sold me a child's memory once. Summer light. A river. Two children who had not yet learned to hurt each other." Her mouth curved without warmth. "It was excellent work. I have kept it burning ever since."

Corvin's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Burning?"

"Everything here burns, thief. Some things slowly." She gestured, a small motion of one ringed hand. "You've come to sell again. I can smell it on you—the wanting. What do you carry?"

He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed this forty times on the stairs, and now, with her eyes on him, the script dissolved like sugar in rain, and what came out instead was the truth, which no fence worth his salt ever says:

"My daughter is in your house."

Something moved behind the queen's stillness. Not surprise exactly. Confirmation.

"And you've brought me her location," Sarelle said gently, "to trade for her safety. Fathers are my favorite customers. They always believe the transaction will hurt less than it does."

* * *

Here is what Corvin Joubert had learned in forty years of thieving, and what he had failed to learn in seven years of living with himself: the moment you decide to double-cross a buyer, tell them nothing, show them nothing, and get out. Every minute past that decision is a minute in which your face does your talking for you.

He had made his decision on the fifth landing. He had known it absolutely since the whispers said his name.

And yet he stood there in the amber light, and his hand—the traitor, the oldest traitor he owned—went to his coat, and drew out the oilcloth packet, and unwrapped it halfway, letting the queen see the gleam of it: the memory of where Elodie was, fresh and sharp and sellable.

Because some part of him, some cowardly clerkish part, still wanted to hear the price first. Wanted to stand in this terrible beautiful place one more minute being *someone*, a player in the game, a man with goods—even if the goods were his own daughter's hiding place.

Queen Sarelle smiled at him with genuine, dreadful kindness.

"Name your payment," she said.

And Corvin looked past her—at the fire, at the boy standing vigil at its edge, at the ranks of the patient dead—and he thought about the toll-stairs, and the holes in himself, and the plum-stealing girl on the kitchen counter, and he thought: *she came down here alone, sixteen years old, to fix what I broke, and I am standing in front of the woman who broke her, holding the knife.*

"No," he said.

The word echoed strangely in the cathedral. Several lanterns flickered toward him, curious.

"No?" The queen's voice stayed soft. Soft things down here were always the dangerous ones. "Thief. Do you understand where you are? I do not need your goods. I can take the location from your head like a coin from a sleeping man's purse. I am being *courteous* with you."

"Aye," Corvin said. "I know. I've been courteous myself, in my day. It's what you do right before the part you don't want to watch." He squared his shoulders, and his knees—both of them, agreeing at last—held. "So here's my price, madam. Not selling. Buying. One hour. Your eyes off the east passage, your lanterns deaf, your whispers minding their own business, for one hour starting now. Whatever she's come to do, she gets her hour unbothered."

The queen tilted her head, and for just a moment the deep-water stillness rippled with something that looked almost like appetite. "And what," she said, "do you imagine you could possibly pay for an hour of my attention?"

"This."

He reached into his coat—not the inner pocket, the deeper one, the one sewn against his heart where he kept the hoard. Forty years of thieving, and Corvin had never spent the hoard, because the hoard wasn't memories of jobs. It was the other kind. The keepers. The taste of his mother's bread. The sound of his wife—Elodie's mother—laughing at her own joke before she finished telling it. The weight of a newborn wrapped in blanket, handed to him in a doorway twenty-odd years ago by a woman who said *take her, please, take her* and then was gone.

Every year of his life worth having. Hoarded against a rainy day, against old age, against death, against exactly this.

"You can't," the queen said, and for the first time her voice lost its smoothness. "Those are yours. A man's own years—"

"—are currency same as any other, and you taught this city that lesson yourself, madam, so don't look shocked when someone finally learns it." His hands were shaking. He let them. "Forty-one years, give or take the tolls. All of it. The bread, the laughter, the baby in the blanket. All of it, for one hour."

"That will leave you—" She stopped herself, and Corvin watched, fascinated and horrified, as something almost like pity crossed the queen's face. "You will be empty. A shell that walks. Down here, emptied of self, there is only one place left to go." Her eyes went to the great guttering fire. "You would feed it forever. You would *be* a lantern."

"Yeah," Corvin said. His throat was full of glass, but he grinned, because a thief should grin at the table, it unsettles the mark. "Well. I've been told I've a good laugh. Be a shame to waste it in the dark."

"Think," Sarelle said, and now she had risen from her ledger-throne, and the whole cathedral seemed to rise with her. "There is another way. Sell me the location, as you came to do, and I will make her a keeper too—gently, painlessly, at the fire's side, where she will want for nothing—"

"There it is." Corvin nodded slowly. "See, that's the pitch. That's always the pitch. *Want for nothing.* I heard it once already, didn't I. Seven years ago, a man in a nice coat told me one night of stars for one night of survival, and I believed him because I wanted to, and I went home and put my hand on a sleeping girl's head and took her childhood out of it." His voice cracked down the middle and he let that happen too. "So no. No more selling her. Not her location, not her future, not one more hair on her head. I'm done being the man who pays his debts with her."

He pulled the hoard fully out of the sewn pocket. In the amber light it glowed like a handful of coals, and across the cathedral, the great fire itself stirred—leaning toward him, hungry, recognizing quality.

Tomas had seen it. Corvin saw the boy see it—saw him straighten at the fire's edge, ember-bright, understanding instantly what was about to happen and who it was for. And the boy, God bless him, did not shout a warning to stop the foolish old man. He simply pressed his ruined hand to his chest in something like a salute.

"Right," Corvin said thickly. "One hour. Starting now."

He opened his hands.

It did not feel like the toll-stairs. Nothing plucked, nothing scraped. It felt like opening a window in a house he'd lived in alone for forty years and letting the wind take every letter he'd ever saved. The bread went first, then the laughter, then the doorway and the blanket and the impossible weight of a newborn girl—*oh,* he thought, *so that's what I've been carrying, oh, it was her, it was always her*—and then everything, all of it, pouring out of him in a long bright stream into the queen's open hands, and Sarelle cried out as if scalded, because no one, apparently, had ever handed her the good years freely before, and Corvin felt himself go hollow and light and strangely, wonderfully simple.

He had maybe a minute of being anyone left. He spent it looking at the boy by the fire.

"Watch her," he said—or shaped with his mouth, at least; he wasn't sure sound came out. "Both of you. Watch her. She's better than either of us."

Then the emptiness closed over him like clean water, and the thing that had been Corvin Joubert walked calmly toward the great fire, unhurried, a man coming home at last from a very long job, and the flames parted to receive him the way a crowd parts for someone they've been waiting for.

Queen Sarelle stood holding a dead man's happiness in both hands, and for a long moment the Undercourt was silent except for the fire, which burned, for the space of a breath, noticeably brighter.

Then she turned her beautiful tired eyes toward the east passage—toward the small, stubborn footsteps descending in the dark—and her face changed into something Elodie Joubert had never yet seen and would not enjoy meeting.

"One hour," the queen said softly, to no one. "Let us see what the little thief does with it."

And in the deepest alcove of the court, behind a curtain of hanging ledgers, a hollow-eyed servant of the queen slipped away through a door that did not appear on any map—heading, though no one marked it, toward the chamber where the court's oldest contract was kept sealed in bone.

The hour had begun.
