## 16. The Queen's Empty Chair

Elodie had stolen from priests, from pawnbrokers, from a duke who kept his mother's dying words in a locket he wore to bed. She had thought herself past surprise.

Then she saw the Queen of the Undercourt eat.

It was not eating as people understood it. Sarelle sat at the head of a long table set for no one—bone-lanterns guttering above, the great fire breathing its slow orange light against the far wall—and lifted nothing to her lips. Her hands stayed folded. Her mouth did not move. And yet the food before her, a plate of dark bread and honey that some silent servant had placed and would surely remove untouched, *dimmed*. Not eaten. Dimmed, like a candle going down, until what remained was not bread but the memory of bread, and then not even that.

"You're staring," said the guard whose loyalty Elodie had stolen three days ago—or was it two? Time ran strange beneath the mountain, pooling in corners.

"I'm admiring her table manners," Elodie murmured.

She had been in the Undercourt long enough to learn its rhythms. The court woke when the fire flared and slept when it sank. The bone-lanterns dimmed at what might have been evening. Petitioners came up the stair that wasn't there with their memories bundled in oilcloth like laundry, and left lighter than they'd arrived, smiling in a way that made Elodie's chest ache. And through it all, Queen Sarelle presided from her chair of black iron and dragon-bone, beautiful and terrible, her voice like coins poured across marble.

Elodie had hated her for six days now. It was a clean hatred, well-tailored, and she had worn it comfortably. The queen had bought her childhood. The queen had fed a boy to a fire. The queen sat on a throne built of other people's yesterdays and called it order.

But hatred required a person to hate. And Elodie was beginning to suspect there was very little person left on that throne.

* * *

The discovery began, as most of her discoveries did, with theft.

She had been practicing. That was the honest word for it, though she'd have bitten anyone who used it aloud. Since the night she'd stolen a guard's loyalty—a small, warm thing, like swallowing a coal that loved her—she had been testing the edges of her gift in this place where everything was already half-traded-away. She'd learned she could lift a lantern's hunger, though the lantern went dark and sullen afterward and she'd felt bad about it for an hour. She'd learned the court's servants were mostly memory themselves, held upright by habit. She'd learned that the nearer she came to the great fire, the more her own stolen pieces tugged at her, like dogs hearing their names called from another yard.

And she'd learned that Queen Sarelle's chambers could be entered, because the queen never locked anything.

*Why would she?* Elodie thought, slipping through doors of veined black stone. *What does a woman need to lock, who can take anything she wants straight out of your head?*

The queen's rooms were wrong in a way Elodie couldn't name at first. Rich, certainly—silks gone soft with age, jewelry laid out in cases, a vanity crowded with combs and mirrors. But nothing was *worn*. The silks had no creases. The jewelry had no scratches. The combs held no hair. A hundred years of a life arranged in perfect display, and not one sign of use.

Like a stage set, she thought. Like the front room of a house where nobody lives.

On the vanity, beside a mirror that showed reflections a heartbeat too slow, lay a ledger. Elodie's fingers knew ledgers the way other women's fingers knew prayer. She opened it.

It was not an account book. Or rather—it was, but every entry was written twice. On the left, in a hand that started bold and slanted and grew shakier down the centuries: *purchases*. Souls bought, memories bought, names, griefs, first loves, last words. Columns and columns of them, the ink changing color as the decades turned, quill to fountain pen to something sharp and metallic.

On the right, in a different hand entirely—smaller, rounder, and growing fainter page by page until the last entries were barely dents in the paper: *sales*.

Elodie read the first sale.

*Sarelle Vane, aged 19 years, sells: her name.*

Below it, in that same round hand, almost playful: *It never fit anyway. Mother chose it off a gravestone.*

She turned pages with hands that had begun to tremble. The sales came regularly after that, spaced like meals. *Sells: the smell of rain on her father's coat.* *Sells: twenty years of birthdays.* *Sells: her sister's laugh, all of them, every one.* Each entry priced fairly, the broker's mark neat beside each line, each payment received in full.

Payment for what?

She found the answer four pages from the end, where the round handwriting had shrunk to something a child might make, pressing hard to leave any mark at all:

*Sells: the rest. All of it. Whatever is left is not mine anymore.*

And below, in the broker's slanted script, one final notation:

*Received in exchange: continuation. The buyer acknowledges that the seller's body and will shall persist so long as the Fire is fed. Terms eternal. Signed under duress of love and grief beyond bearing.*

Duress of love. Elodie stared at those three words until the lanterns swam.

There was a story here, and stories were the one thing she couldn't steal—only be given. But the ledger gave her shapes to fill: a girl named Sarelle Vane, nineteen, selling her own name because it sat on her like a borrowed coat. Selling pieces of herself across three hundred years, patiently, deliberately, the way Elodie sold charms—one small light at a time—except Sarelle hadn't been buying bread. She'd been buying *time*. Keeping something alive. Keeping someone alive.

Or keeping herself alive, which was not always the same thing.

The last entry's price was marked paid in a currency Elodie recognized with a sick lurch: *one keeper, soul intact, per century.*

Tomas's century.

* * *

She heard the queen coming and did the only sensible thing, which was to hide badly behind a dressing screen woven from what looked suspiciously like spider-silk and regret.

Sarelle entered without ceremony. Up close, away from the throne and its shadows, the queen moved oddly—not gracefully but *economically*, like a puppeteer conserving string. She crossed to the vanity. She looked into the too-slow mirror.

And she spoke to it. To herself. To whoever a hollow thing speaks to, alone, at the hour the bone-lanterns dim.

"Three hundred and eleven years," Sarelle said. Her voice was still coin-and-marble even here, even to no one. "You promised me I wouldn't feel it."

The mirror said nothing. The reflection, Elodie noticed, was a half-second behind—and in that half-second, the face in the glass looked different. Younger. Frightened. Nineteen.

"I feel it," the queen told her reflection conversationally. "That was the trick of it, wasn't it? You took everything and left the feeling. Grief with no memory under it. Love with no face attached. I sit on that chair and I rule these poor traded dead and I do not know *why*, except that stopping would be worse." A pause. The candlelight caught her eyes, which were lovely and utterly unfathomable, like wells you could drop a scream down. "I don't even remember his face. The one I sold everything for. Isn't that funny?"

Elodie, pressed against the screen, did not laugh.

She was watching the queen's hands. They had begun, absently, to smooth the unwrinkled silk of her sleeve—the gesture of a living woman, performed by something running on the memory of gestures. And Elodie understood, all at once and completely, what she was looking at.

Not a monster. Not a queen, even. A *hull*. A ship that had given away its cargo piece by piece over three centuries and now sailed on ballast and momentum, kept afloat by a fire that ate souls, ruling a court of the traded dead because the alternative was sinking—and she didn't even remember what she'd been sailing toward.

The hatred Elodie had worn for six days suddenly fit badly. It hung off her like Corvin's old coat, which she had also once tried to fill.

*I know this,* she thought, horrified at the knowing. *God help me, I know exactly what this is. This is me if I'd had three hundred years instead of seven. This is every thief who sells a little and then a little more and then looks up one night and can't remember why she started.*

Sarelle turned from the mirror. Her gaze swept the room—over the case of unworn jewels, over the untouched bed, over the dressing screen behind which a thief was holding her breath and discovering that terror smells like copper.

"Come out," the queen said mildly. "You've been leaking curiosity at me for three days. It's very loud."

Elodie stepped out. There seemed little point otherwise; her talent was spent, her legs were shaking, and dignity was a charm she'd never managed to steal for herself.

"You knew," she said.

"Since you came down my stair eating your own memories to pay the toll." Sarelle studied her the way a jeweler studies a stone she has already decided not to buy. "You have your grandmother's chin. Did you know that? No—of course you didn't. I have her file. I bought her wedding day in 1802. Lovely affair. Rained."

"My grandmother," Elodie repeated. Of all the things. The mountain was full of horrors and this was the one that knocked her sideways—that the queen kept files, that history here was inventory, that somewhere in those columns her own family was a line item.

"Everything is filed here." Sarelle drifted to the window that faced the great fire, its glow painting her hollow face amber. "You want to hate me. I can hear it rattling around in you like a coin in a jar. Go ahead. It's more than I can manage." Something flickered across her features—an expression reaching for sorrow and finding the shape of it without the substance, like an actress playing grief in a language she'd forgotten. "Do you know what I feel, thief? When the fire flares, I feel *warmth*. Not warmth itself—I sold actual warmth in 1740, terrible price I got—but the idea of it. The word. I stand next to the hottest thing in the world and I am cold, and I remember being warm the way you remember a word in a language you used to speak."

"Why him?" Elodie asked, and her voice cracked in the middle. "Why Tomas? He was fourteen when they took his name. Fourteen when they brought him down here. What kind of—"

"The kind that keeps a fire lit," Sarelle said simply. "You've seen the keepers. You've seen what centuries of burning do. Tomas is the fourth this era. Before him there was a midwife from Harrowgate and a soldier who'd sold his fear and regretted it forever, and—" she stopped, and her hand rose halfway to her heart and hovered there, confused by its own errand. "They burn slowly. That is the mercy of it, if you want mercy. They forget being afraid first. Fear goes early. Then anger. Then everything else, until what's left is just enough self to hold the wick." Her coin-voice softened into something almost human. "He'll be a fine flame. He's stubborn. Stubbornness burns longest."

"He isn't a *flame*," Elodie said. "He's a boy. He's a boy I—" The sentence hit the wall inside her that was the hole where her childhood should be, and stopped. "He's a boy I knew."

"Yes." The queen watched her with terrible patience. "I know. I've read your file too, Elodie Joubert. Would you like to hear what you sold? Everyone wants to know what they sold. It's the cruelest question in this court and I ask it professionally."

"No," Elodie said quickly.

"It's a shame about the boy," Sarelle went on, as if Elodie hadn't spoken. "Truly. I mean that. If I were capable of meaning things, I would mean it very much. But here is what you must understand, thief, since you insist on seeing me as the villain of your story: I am not the villain. I am the *arrangement*. Someone must feed the fire or the wards fall and the veil tears and everything outside comes in—the things that were here before stars, the things the first wards were built against. Your city sleeps safely because a boy burns. I sleep not at all, because I cannot, because I sold sleep in 1761." She smiled, and it was the loneliest smile Elodie had ever seen. "Hate the arrangement, if you like. Hate the arithmetic. But do not imagine I enjoy it. Enjoyment was among the earlier sales."

*Pity displacing hatred*—that was how it happened, not as a decision but as weather. One moment Elodie was armored in fury; the next, looking at this three-hundred-year-old survivor who had eaten herself course by course to keep the world's worst bargain, she felt something worse than hatred. She felt *recognition*. The queen was what waited at the end of every trade. The queen was the bottom of every ledger.

"What was his name?" Elodie asked quietly. "The one you sold everything for. You said you don't remember his face."

For a long moment Sarelle was silent. The fire breathed against the far wall. Somewhere in the deeps of the court, a lantern chimed like a spoon against a cup.

"Aurelian," the queen said at last, and her voice did something it likely hadn't done in a century—it wobbled. "His name was Aurelian. I kept that one till the very end. Names are cheap to everyone else and priceless to yourself." She turned back to the mirror, to the frightened nineteen-year-old trailing her reflection. "Now get out of my rooms, thief. And whatever you're planning—and you are planning something, you leak plans the way other people leak sweat—know this: the fire takes what it takes. The arrangement holds. It has held for three hundred years because everyone who could change it decided instead to survive it."

"That sounds familiar," Elodie said.

Something passed over the queen's face—too quick to name, gone before it landed. "Yes," she said. "It would."

* * *

Elodie walked back through the bone-lit corridors with the ledger's shapes burning in her mind, and by the time she reached the gallery above the great fire, the shapes had become a plan—which was to say, a mad idea wearing a plan's clothing.

Because here was what the ledger had shown her, the thing the queen herself perhaps no longer understood: Sarelle had sold *everything*, but the sales had a price column, and the price column had a payer. Every year of the queen's hollow continuation had been purchased with the fire. The fire sustained her. Which meant—

Which meant the fire remembered her. All of her. Three hundred years of a woman's soul, sold not destroyed—nothing here was ever destroyed, it was only *held*—banked in the flame like coins in a vault, keeping the vault warm.

The fire was not just a fire. It was a hoard. The greatest hoard in the world, and hoards were the one thing thieves existed for.

"Tomas," she whispered to the dark, to the ember-limned figure she could just see at the fire's heart, feeding it, burning for it. "Hold on. I think I finally understand the shape of this."

Because if the fire remembered the queen entire—if it held three hundred years of Sarelle Vane, name and laughter and a man called Aurelian—then it might hold other things too.

It might hold a childhood. Hers.

And it might hold, banked and waiting in its oldest, deepest coals, the memory of warmth itself—the thing that fire was, before it became fuel, before it became arrangement, before a frightened girl-queen sold the world's comfort to buy the world's continuing.

Steal that, and you didn't free one boy. You freed everyone.

Elodie pressed her palms against the warm stone and grinned at the dark, the grin of a thief who has just spotted the one unguarded window in a fortress.

Tomorrow, she was going to rob a fire.
