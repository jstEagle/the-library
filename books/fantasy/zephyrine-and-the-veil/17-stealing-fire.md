## 17. Stealing Fire

The bone-lanterns had names.

Elodie felt them as she moved through the cathedral dark, past the hanging cages of light, each one humming faintly with what it had once been. A grandmother's Sunday afternoons. A soldier's last joke. A child's certainty that the moon followed her home. The Undercourt was not a prison of souls so much as a library of them, and Queen Sarelle the librarian who had eaten the catalog.

Elodie pressed herself into the shadow between two lanterns and did what she had done her whole crooked life: she listened.

Below the dais, in the great iron cradle where the dragon-fire guttered, Tomas knelt. Or something that had been Tomas. He was woven into the fire now—she could see it clearly from here, the way his edges smoked and reformed, the way embers drifted up through his ribs like moths leaving an open coat. His hands were cupped around the flame the way you'd hold water for someone dying of thirst. Careful. Endless. Devoted.

He looked up.

She didn't know how he knew she was there. Perhaps the fire told him. Perhaps some part of him had been waiting seven years with his face turned toward the stair that wasn't there.

"You came back," he said. His voice was strange—layered, like two people speaking in unison, one of them very old. "I told you not to."

"I've never once done what I was told." She slid down the wall, staying low. "You remember that about me, at least?"

"I remember everything." And there it was—the fury, banked but alive, glowing under the ash of him. "That's the whole job, Elodie. That's the *whole* job."

* * *

They had argued for an hour before Sarelle's court began to stir again—the hour Corvin bought with forty years of hoarded memories, burned to cinders somewhere above them on the stair. An hour. It had sounded generous in the moment. Now it felt like the width of a coin tossed down a well.

Elodie crouched beside the fire-cradle, out of the queen's line of sight, and laid out what she had stolen on the flagstones like a peddler at market: a guard's loyalty, still warm; a lantern's hunger, coiled and snapping; a door's habit of opening for anyone who smelled of the mountain. Small thefts. Practice thefts. Her fingers shook as she arranged them.

Tomas watched her with eyes that flickered orange at the rims.

"What are you doing?"

"Shopping," she said.

"For *what?* There's nothing here to buy. There's nothing here at all except—" He gestured at himself with a hand that scattered sparks. "Except this. You can't steal me out, if that's your plan. I've tried. For years I tried. The fire doesn't let go of its wick any more than a candle lets go of its—"

"I'm not stealing you." She kept her voice steady, which took everything. "I'm stealing the *fire*."

"The fire is me."

"No." She leaned close, close enough to feel the heat warp the air between them, close enough to see that underneath the ember-light his face was still fourteen, still the boy who'd shared half a stolen pear with her on the docks and called it a feast. "Listen to me. Sarelle told me herself. The fire doesn't burn on souls. Souls are just the wick—the thing that holds the flame steady. What it *burns*, what it actually eats, is memory. Warmth-memory. Every hearth anyone ever sat beside. Every summer afternoon. Every hand held in the dark. That's the fuel. You're just the candle."

Tomas went very still. Even the sparks slowed.

"And if you take the fuel," he said slowly, "the flame goes out. And if the flame goes out—"

"The wards stay down. The stars stay dead. Vantrelle stays blind and walled and terrified until someone finds another way." She swallowed. "Or forever. One of those."

"So we're back to Sarelle's bargain. Someone burns so everyone belongs. Only now it's the whole city burning instead of just me, and you think that's better?" His voice cracked along both voices at once, boy and ancient thing. "You always were good at arithmetic that made other people pay."

The words landed exactly where they were meant to. She let them.

"Yes," she said quietly. "And then I grew up and learned division."

* * *

Here is what Elodie knew about theft, knowledge bought over ten years of small crimes:

You cannot steal what a thing does not have. You cannot lift a coin from an empty pocket, a grief from a heartless man. And most important of all—you cannot steal the *whole* of anything. Every charm she'd ever cut was a fragment. A first kiss, minus the nervousness. A mother's voice, minus the argument that preceded it. Theft was always amputation. You took the part that fit in your hand.

Which meant the fire could be stolen, but only in pieces. Unless—

Unless you stole the one piece that was all the pieces. The memory underneath every other memory. The warmth before there were words for it: being carried, being held, being small and safe against something enormous that loved you without knowing how.

Every soul in this court had been fed into the fire carrying their own version of it. That was why souls made good wicks—they came pre-loaded with the fuel. But the fire itself, the dragon-fire, the original—it remembered warmth the way the sea remembers rain. Not as many memories. As *one*. One vast, singular, first warmth, older than the city, older than the wards, older than the word for it.

Steal that, and you hadn't taken a piece of the fire.

You'd taken the fire entire.

"It'll kill you," Tomas said when she explained it, whispering now, glancing toward the dais where Sarelle sat motionless in her chair like a coat hung on a hook. "Pulling a memory that size through yourself. You're not a vessel, Elodie, you're a *person*, and people have seams."

"People also have choices." She flexed her fingers. They ached. Everything ached; the stair had taken tolls she was only beginning to count—a birthday, her ability to skip stones, the taste of cinnamon, gone, gone, gone—and she had decided somewhere in the dark not to keep score anymore. "Besides. I've spent my whole life taking memories that weren't mine. One more. Just this once, I'm taking one back."

"That's not yours either."

"No," she agreed. "But nobody else is using it."

* * *

Sarelle stirred.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next—one moment the queen was a hollow shape in her high chair, and the next her head turned, slow as a millstone, and her voice filled the cathedral without bothering to pass through air.

"*Thief.*"

Elodie froze with her hand halfway to the cradle.

"You smell of my stair. Of tolls unpaid and tolls paid. Of my guard's loyalty, which is currently sitting in your pocket telling you I never watch the east archway." Something that might have been amusement moved through the hollow of her. "Clever girl. Cleverer than the man who sold you to me, though that is a low bar set in a deep hole."

"Sarelle—" Tomas started, rising, and the fire flared with him, throwing shadows like wings across the bone-lanterns.

"Stay, keeper. This does not concern the wick." The queen's gaze fixed on Elodie, and it was worse than any warden's stare, because there was nothing behind it to appeal to. No fear, no greed, no love. Just appetite wearing patience like a borrowed coat. "Let us speak plainly, as brokers do. You intend to steal from my fire. I have ruled this court since before your city had a name, and in all that time, no one has stolen from my fire. Do you know why?"

"Bad manners?"

"Because it cannot be carried." Sarelle leaned forward, and the hollow of her face caught the firelight. "A memory that old, that large—it will pour through you like a river through a teacup. You will not be a thief at the end of it. You will be a stain on these flags with excellent intentions. I have watched prouder souls than yours try smaller things."

"Then you'll forgive me," Elodie said, "if I don't take business advice from someone whose inventory is people."

For a long moment, silence. Then Sarelle did something Elodie would think about for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be.

She laughed. A dry sound, like pages turning.

"Oh, I do like you. It has been centuries since I liked anything." The queen settled back. "Very well. Try. I will even help you—I will hold my court still, and my lanterns quiet, because I confess a curiosity older than my caution. Show me what your talent can do, little thief. Show me what I might have been, had anyone ever taught me to want instead of need."

And the court went still around them, every lantern dimming to a courteous glow, every traded ghost holding its breath, and Elodie understood suddenly that this too was part of some ledger she couldn't see—that mercy from a monster was just a debt with a longer name.

Fine. Let her collect. If Elodie lost, none of it would matter anyway.

* * *

She knelt at the edge of the cradle. The heat was enormous—not painful, exactly, but insistent, like standing too close to someone crying. The fire wanted to be known. That was the terrible secret of it. It had been burning alone under a mountain for centuries, fed by strangers, tended by a boy it was slowly eating, and some vast animal part of it was *lonely*.

"Tomas," she said.

"Don't." He was shaking. Sparks fell from his shoulders and died on the stone. "Whatever you're going to say, don't. Because if you say it, I'll believe it, and then it'll hurt worse when you do this and it goes wrong."

"If it goes wrong, I won't feel it and neither will you."

"That's not comforting. That's the opposite of comforting."

She smiled at him—really smiled, for the first time since the stair—and reached out and took his unraveling hands in hers.

His hands were warm past warm. Warm like noon, like bread, like fever. And through the contact she felt him—all of him, everything the fire had shown him, seven years of kneeling here while the court traded souls overhead, and underneath all of it, at the bottom of him like a coin at the bottom of a fountain: the pear. The dock. Fourteen years old, the two of them splitting stolen fruit in the sun, and him saying *we should run away someday,* and her saying *someday,* and meaning it.

She had sold that afternoon to buy her own hiding place. He had kept his copy of it his whole life, all the way into the fire.

"I'm sorry," she said. "Not for this. For before. I'll say the rest properly later, if later exists."

"Later exists," Tomas said fiercely, gripping back with hands that were more ember than flesh. "You make it exist. That's an order from your keeper. Now *go*, before I talk myself into stopping you."

She closed her eyes and reached past him, into the fire.

* * *

Theft, at its heart, is attention. You attend to what a thing keeps and where it keeps it, and you attend so completely that the boundary between you and it grows thin, and in that thinness you simply—lift.

She attended to the fire.

And the fire, lonely, starved for witness, *attended back.*

Warmth poured into her. Not heat—warmth. The distinction mattered immediately and catastrophically, because heat stays on the skin and warmth goes wherever it likes, and this warmth went everywhere at once. It was a dragon's breastbone, aeons ago, the first creature ever to curl small against something larger. It was every hearth that had ever been built in imitation of that first curling. It was her mother—her real mother, a shape she'd forgotten she'd lost—holding her swaddled against a window in winter light. It was Tomas's cupped hands. It was the sun. It was the sun before the sun, whatever warmth meant when warmth was only an idea, patient and total, waiting for something to happen to.

Her teacup split.

She felt it go—felt the seams of herself strain and give, felt pieces of Elodie Joubert wash away down the river of it. Somewhere far away Tomas was shouting. Somewhere far away Sarelle had risen from her chair for the first time in centuries, hollow face tilted, watching. Somewhere far away there was a city full of people who needed this warmth more than they would ever know.

She held on. That was the whole of the craft, in the end. Anyone could reach. Thieves were the ones who held on.

*Give it to me,* she thought at the fire, or maybe said aloud, or maybe just was. *You've been feeding on borrowed warmth for a thousand years. Come be warm somewhere it counts.*

And the fire—

The fire chose her.

It came up through her hands like dawn coming through a window, total and gentle and absolutely irresistible, and it took with it, as it passed, things she would never get back: the gift, the talent, the larceny in her fingertips that had been hers since before she could write. She felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave. The thing that made her *her*—the thing that had kept her alive and made her small—dissolving into the light pouring through her, spent, spent, spent, the greatest theft priced at everything she had.

She held on.

Somewhere in the white-out, a boy's voice, fraying: "*Elodie—your hands—*"

She looked down. Her hands were full of fire, actual fire, cradled like the stolen pear, like the widow's grief, like every small bright thing she'd ever lifted from a pocket or a heart—and Tomas was unraveling faster now, his edges streaming upward into the space where the great flame used to be, because the flame was in her hands and his wick had nothing left to hold.

"No no no," she heard herself say. "No, you hold on, that's my line—"

"*Go.*" He was smiling. That was the worst part. He was smiling the way you smile at the end of a good story. "Up the stair. Take it to the top. It wants the sky, Elodie—it's been wanting the sky for a thousand years—"

Behind her, with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, Queen Sarelle began to fall.
