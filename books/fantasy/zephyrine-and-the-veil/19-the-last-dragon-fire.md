## 19. The Last Dragon-Fire

Tomas had forgotten what it felt like to have hands.

Not the having of them—he could see them now, pale and new in the light of Elodie's cupped flame, ten fingers where for seven years there had only been the long slow dissolution of being a wick. But the *feeling* of them. The way air moved across skin. The way his own heartbeat pushed against his palms like something knocking politely at a door.

He flexed them. They obeyed, mostly. One of them trembled.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm allowed," Elodie said. She was sitting on the floor of the dead court with her knees drawn up and her whole face streaked with soot and older griefs, and she was looking at him the way people look at things they had paid dearly to stop believing in. "Seven years of staring to catch up on. You don't get a vote."

Behind her, the Undercourt was coming apart gently, the way a held breath comes apart. Sarelle's bone-lanterns had gone dark one by one when she released them, each soul rising out of its cage of ribs and drifting up through the cathedral ceiling like sparks leaving a bonfire. Some had paused on their way out—Tomas had felt them pass, cool as moth wings—to touch him, or Elodie, or nothing at all, out of habit.

The last dragon-fire sat in its iron cradle between them, small now. Smaller than it had any right to be, after burning at the center of the world's worst bargain for three hundred years. It was about the size of a loaf of bread. It flickered the way a candle flickers in a room where someone has just said something true.

"It's dying," Elodie said. Not a question. She'd been watching it too.

"It's been dying since before I got here." Tomas tried to shrug and discovered that shoulders were also a thing he would need to relearn; the gesture came out lopsided, more apology than shrug. "The queen fed it souls because souls are loud. A soul screams all the way down, and screaming sounds like roaring if you're far enough away. But that's not what fire eats." He looked at the little flame and felt the old ache move through him—not pain anymore, just the shape pain had left, like a dent in a cushion. "It eats warmth. Remembered warmth. Somebody's kitchen in winter. A fever breaking. Hands around a mug. That's the fuel. Souls were just... kindling that happened to be shaped like people."

Elodie was quiet a moment. Then, because she was Elodie: "You're telling me this entire monstrous enterprise ran on *coziness.*"

"Run badly enough, anything can run on anything."

"That's the most depressing sentence I've ever heard."

"You sold stolen memories for pocket money. You don't get to judge supply chains."

She laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, but a real one—and Tomas found that hearing it was worth every year of ash. He'd spent so long remembering her wrong. In the fire's grip, memory curdles; you keep the facts and lose the warmth, and the boy he had been down here had known Elodie Joubert only as a name spoken in a ledger, the girl who gave his name to a broker, and the knowing had been cold all the way through. Now here she was, sooty and sharp-tongued and shaking, and the warmth came flooding back into the facts until they were a person again.

That was when he understood what the flame needed. Not a keeper on a hook. Not a soul nailed to a wick. Just somebody willing to carry the remembering of it—all of it—out into the world where remembering belonged.

* * *

He didn't tell her right away. He wanted an hour first. He figured he'd earned an hour.

They climbed up out of the great hall together, slowly, because his legs were new and untrustworthy and because Elodie kept stopping to put her hand against the walls—the architecture of her own childhood, she called it, though she still couldn't quite reach the memory itself. The fire had given her back fragments on the way down, she'd told him, and fragments were crueler than nothing: you could remember a window and not whose room it lit.

"The stair behind the vestry," she said, at a junction. "Maren's map says left."

"Maren's map is wrong. The stair rearranged itself while we were below. Everything down here rearranges." He pointed. "Right. And then it's going to pretend to be a wall for a bit, but it gets bored if you're patient."

"How do you know that?"

"Seven years, Elodie. I've watched these stairs longer than anyone alive. I know their habits." He considered. "The tall one near the bone-orchard cheats. It counts your steps and adds extra if it thinks you're not paying attention."

"You've made friends with the stairs."

"Somebody down here had to have friends."

She went quiet at that, the way she kept going quiet—at the places where his joke ended and his history began. He wished she wouldn't. He wished she'd keep talking, fill the dark corridors with her particular brand of cheerful larceny, because silence down here had a texture he knew too well.

So he filled it himself. He told her about the years she hadn't seen. About how, in the second winter, he had taught a soul-broker's clerk to whistle through his teeth, and how the clerk had traded him three minutes of birdsong for the lesson, and how those three minutes had gotten him through a decade. About the ghost of a lamplighter who wandered the lower galleries relighting wicks that weren't there, humming, content, and how Tomas had envied him more than he'd envied anyone free. About the rat.

"There was a rat?"

"There was a rat. I named him Chancellor. He stole crumbs from the queen's table, which made him, in my professional assessment, the bravest citizen of the Undercourt."

"What happened to Chancellor?"

"He retired. Went up a drain somewhere. Living well is the best revenge, and Chancellor lived *extremely* well." He glanced sideways at her. "You'd have liked him. You'd have tried to rob him."

"I would have *succeeded,*" she said, with such wounded dignity that he laughed out loud, and the sound bounced strangely off the bone-white stone, bright and alive in a place built for neither.

* * *

At the foot of the long stair that led up toward the mountain's throat—the passage Ilsa Reyne was, at this moment, bleeding to hold open—they stopped, because the fire had to be discussed, and both of them had been avoiding the discussion the way you avoid the last page of a book you love.

The flame rode in Elodie's cupped hands, dimmer now. It had burned most of what it carried getting them this far. What remained inside it was thin and precious: a hearth in a farmhouse two centuries gone. A blacksmith's forge on a morning in spring. Sunlight through wine. The specific heat of a mother's shoulder. A few dozen memories of warmth, hoarded from three hundred years of stolen souls, and when they guttered out, the last dragon-fire would gutter with them, and the wards above Vantrelle—which drank from the same deep vein—would fall forever.

"There has to be another way," Elodie said. It was the fourth time she'd said it. "I take it into myself. I carry it. My talent's already spent stealing it, but I can still—"

"Carry it where?" Tomas kept his voice gentle. "You can't hold a fire's memory in an empty hand, El. It needs somewhere to live. Somewhere that remembers how." He turned his own hands over, looked at them. New hands. Faintly translucent at the edges, if you knew to look, and he knew to look. "Somewhere that's been marinating in the stuff for seven years."

"Don't." Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Don't do the noble thing. I will actually hit you. I have very recent experience hitting people who deserve it, I've gotten *good* at it—"

"Tomas."

"—and if you think I climbed down a stair that isn't there, paid a toll in my own memories at every landing, robbed a queen blind, and gave up the only magic I ever had—just to watch you volunteer yourself into a lantern—"

"*Elodie.*"

She stopped. He hadn't used her full name in seven years either. It sounded strange in his mouth, formal and dear, like a coat that still fit.

"I'm not volunteering to burn," he said. "Listen to me. Really listen, once, honestly, the way nobody in this story has listened yet." He took the flame from her hands—it weighed nothing, it weighed everything—and held it between them. "Sarelle's mistake wasn't keeping the fire. It was keeping it *locked*. Fire in a vault dies. Fire passed hand to hand lives. All it needs is a carrier—someone walking under the open sky, remembering warmth, feeding it small honest memories the way you feed a traveling flame from hearth to hearth. That's the old way. Before brokers, before courts. Fire used to travel. People *carried* it. That's why the stars went out when the wards fell—nothing was carrying anymore. Everything was hoarded."

"So carry it," she whispered. "You carry it. You know it better than—"

"I'm fading, El." He said it simply, because he'd made peace with simple. "Look at my edges. The queen let me go, but seven years as a wick doesn't come back clean. I've got maybe a day of being a person in me. Maybe less. I can spend it becoming a lantern in a hole in the ground, alone, exactly like the last seven years—" he watched her flinch and hated himself for it and kept going, because gentleness that avoids the truth isn't kindness, Corvin's ghost had taught him that much— "or I can spend it walking up into the sunlight, with the person I—"

He stopped. Recalibrated. There would be time for that word later, if there was a later, and even if there wasn't, it deserved better than a staircase.

"With company," he finished. "Under the sky. Feeling weather. Getting rained on, probably, knowing Vantrelle. And when I run out—which I will—I'll give it the last of me gladly, and it won't be consumption, Elodie, it won't be a hook or a ledger or a debt. It'll be a gift. Do you see the difference? It's the only difference that matters. Nobody has ever given this fire anything. Everyone's only ever had things taken for it. I want to be the first one to *hand* it something."

Elodie was crying without any sound at all, which he remembered was what she did instead of admitting things.

"And the wards?" she managed.

"When I give out, the fire carries what's left of me the rest of the way. Warmth remembering warmth. It'll find the vein. It'll rise." He smiled, and found that smiling still worked, which seemed like poor planning on the universe's part and typical generosity on fate's. "And then Vantrelle gets its stars back, and I get to be part of the sky for a while, and honestly? After seven years underground, the career prospects appeal to me."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's *not*—" Her voice broke entirely. She pressed her fists against her eyes. "I betrayed you. When we were fourteen. I sold your name to save mine, and I paid to forget it, and I built a whole person on top of the hole, and you—you *remember everything*, and you're standing there making jokes about—about career prospects—how can you—"

"Because remembering honestly was always the point," Tomas said. "You asked me once—no. You'll ask me. On the mountain. I've seen how this goes; the fire shows things out of order." He stepped closer. The flame between them leaned toward her like a cat. "Here's what seven years as a wick taught me, and I'll teach it to you for free, no ledger, no toll: being consumed is what happens when warmth is taken. Being *given* is what happens when it's offered. Same fire. Completely different thing. Sarelle never learned it. Corvin half learned it, too late. You're learning it now. And me—" he looked down at his fading hands, and found he meant what he said next all the way through, which was the strangest, warmest feeling of his entire life including both of its endings— "me, I finally get to learn it first. For once in our whole sorry history, Elodie Joubert, I'm ahead of you."

A sound came out of her that was half laugh, half sob, entirely hers.

"Terms," she said at last, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, businesslike, because negotiating was how she survived feelings. "I walk with you. All the way. To whatever pass Ilsa's holding, however far you get. Non-negotiable."

"Accepted."

"And I feed it. Memories. Mine. Whatever it wants, whenever it runs low. I've got twenty-two years of them and most of them are warm if you look at them right."

"Careful," Tomas said. "That's generous. Generosity is gateway behavior."

"And when we get to the top—" She stopped. Started again. Her chin came up, and there she was, the girl from before the hole, the one who'd shared a stolen apple with him on a rooftop and split it unfairly in his favor and lied about it badly. "When we get to the top, you tell me everything I did. All of it. Every piece. No softening. And I hold all of it, and I don't look away, and I remember you *honestly*, the way you asked. That's my price."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment.

"That's not a price," he said. "That's the whole payment. That's everything I wanted." He held out his fading hand. "Deal?"

She took it. Her grip was fierce and ordinary and human, and his fingers held, and would hold, for as long as they were needed.

"Deal," she said.

The little flame between their joined hands stood up straight—no taller, no brighter, but steady, the way a fire is steady when it has finally, after three hundred years, been asked instead of taken.

* * *

They started up.

The stair behind the vestry pretended to be a wall for exactly as long as Tomas had promised, and then got bored, and unfolded. The tall one by the bone-orchard tried to cheat them of four steps; Tomas glared at it and it apologized, in its way, by flattening. Above them, faint through the mountain's bones, came the distant clangor of Ilsa Reyne's wardens holding the pass—the sound of someone keeping a door open with her whole stubborn body—and beneath that, impossibly far above, a whisper of wind through a crack in the world.

Cold wind. Outside wind. Wind that smelled, even here, even through solid rock, like rain coming.

Elodie walked beside him, one hand on his arm, feeding the flame as they climbed: the smell of Maren's illegal maps, old paper and ink; the taste of a plum stolen at midnight at eleven years old; a widow's grief, given freely back, which surprised the fire so much it flared gold for a full minute. With every memory the light grew a little stronger, and with every step Tomas felt himself grow a little thinner, and both of these things were fine. Both of these things were, for the first time in the whole long history of the last dragon-fire, *chosen.*

"How far to the top?" Elodie asked, as the stairs began, blessedly, to end.

"Far enough," Tomas said, "to finish being honest."

Ahead, at the crown of the stair, the mountain's throat opened onto a seam of grey-gold light—the hour before dawn, or the hour after catastrophe, hard to say—and through it, silhouetted against a sky still empty of stars, stood a figure in a warden's cloak with her sword drawn and her feet planted, holding the pass against the dark.

Ilsa Reyne turned at the sound of them. Her face was bloodied and set and utterly unsurprised, and when she saw the flame riding between the thief and the boy made of ember, her eyes widened with something that, in a lesser woman, might have been called hope.

"You're late," she said. "And you'd both better explain fast. The conscription army's forty minutes behind me—and the sky just started to *tremble.*"
