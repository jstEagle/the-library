## 20. Stars Over Vantrelle

Ilsa Reyne had stood watch over the mountain pass for three days without sleep, and she had discovered something in that time: exhaustion made her honest, and honesty made her furious.

"Captain," said Brannoc, the youngest of her wardens, for the eleventh time that hour, "the orders say—"

"The orders say seal the pass." Ilsa did not turn from the mouth of the cave. "I heard them too. I was standing next to you when the courier shouted them through a speaking-horn at a man who was already dead."

Brannoc shifted his weight. He was twenty-two and built like a door, and he had joined the wardens because his mother said it would give him structure. It had given him Ilsa instead, which was arguably worse.

"But we're not sealing it," he said.

"We are not."

"Because—"

"Because there's a thief down those stairs who is currently doing more for this city than the entire Council of Nine has done since the stars went out," Ilsa said, "and because if I seal this pass, I become the kind of officer who seals passes, and I have met that kind of officer, Brannoc, and they are all promoted by thirty-five and dead inside by forty."

He considered this. "You're thirty-six, Captain."

"I'm aware. It's been a narrow escape."

Above them, the sky was still a wound. Seven nights since every star had guttered out at once — seven nights of a darkness so total that people in the Lamplighters' Quarter had begun leaving their shutters open out of superstition, as if the sky might feel watched and be shamed into lighting up. The border wards had fallen with that same night's terrible sound, like a wineglass breaking somewhere enormous, and Vantrelle had been an open wound ever since: refugees pressing in from the hinterlands, conscription wagons rolling through the low districts scooping up anyone with so much as a trickling nosebleed at the wrong moment, the Council issuing proclamations about *order* and *sacrifice* written in the particular ink that meant nobody who wrote them expected to bleed.

And beneath Ilsa's boots, down a stair that rearranged itself and ate small memories like crumbs, one pickpocket from Saltrow was fighting a queen.

Ilsa had arrested forty-one people in her career for unlicensed talent. She had signed the ledgers herself. She had believed — she still mostly believed — that magic in a walled city was a lit lamp in a powder store, that law was the only wall between order and fire.

And then she had caught Elodie Joubert climbing a drainpipe with a widow's grief in her pocket, and instead of arresting her, had asked her a question Ilsa had never once asked anyone: *what exactly is it we think we're policing?*

The answer had turned out to be: a child's memory, sold by the man who loved her, to keep a fire burning that no one above ground remembered existed.

Justice, Ilsa thought, was a very tidy word for a very messy room.

"Captain!" The shout came from Hesk, her other warden — the old one, the one who'd survived two purges by having the political instincts of a cockroach. He was standing at the edge of the overlook, pointing up. "Captain, look at the *sky*."

* *

The wards rose like breath drawn after drowning.

Ilsa felt it before she saw it — a pressure along her skin, a hum in her back teeth, the particular wrongness of the air correcting itself. Then light: a thin seam of silver running along the horizon where the border stones stood, racing away north and south faster than anything had a right to move, stitching the world closed again. The seam climbed. It arched overhead. And the whole dome of the dark shivered once, like a horse shaking off rain—

—and the stars came back.

Not all at once. They came back the way feeling returns to a numb limb: one bright pinprick, then a handful, then dozens, then thousands, spilling across the black in rivers and constellations, some of them burning with the steady amber of hearth-fires, and at their center — impossibly, unmistakably — one star that burned red-gold and warm, like an ember someone had carried a very long way uphill rather than let it die.

The mountain under Ilsa's feet sighed. She would remember that afterward: not thunder, not triumph, but a sigh, long and low, the sound of something enormous finally setting down a weight it had carried for seven years.

Beside her, Warden Brannoc sat down abruptly on a rock and began, without any apparent awareness of doing so, to cry.

Hesk took off his helmet, held it against his chest, and said, in the voice of a man conducting a small private funeral for his own cynicism, "Well. There it is."

Ilsa didn't say anything. Her throat had closed entirely. She stood at the mouth of the stair-that-wasn't-there, watching the ember-star burn, and thought: *she did it. The little pickpocket actually did it.* And underneath that thought, quieter, in the voice Ilsa had spent twenty years not listening to: *and now comes the part where the city decides what to do with people like her.*

She looked at the ledgers.

They were stacked beside the command tent — eleven volumes, bound in council grey, each page a name, a district, a date of conscription. Four hundred and six souls taken off the streets of Vantrelle in seven nights to feed into the wards' machinery, or so the theory went; hauled to holding-pens beneath the Custom House, waiting for wagons that would take them down the mountain toward a court most of the Council pretended not to believe in. Some of them were children. One of them, Ilsa knew, was a baker's daughter named Sefa who could make bread rise with a song and whose only crime had been singing on a Tuesday.

The orders in her dispatch case said: proceed with transport.

Ilsa Reyne looked at the stars for a long moment. Then she picked up the first ledger, opened it to the flyleaf, and began, methodically, to tear out the pages.

* *

Word reached the city before she did, of course. News in Vantrelle traveled the way water found cracks.

By the time Ilsa rode down the switchback road at dawn, the low districts were already pouring into the streets — not rioting, not yet, but doing something stranger and more dangerous: standing in the middle of roads looking straight up. Fishwives with their baskets set down. Printers in their aprons. A whole family on a rooftop in Saltrow, arranged in a row like laundry, faces tilted to a sky none of them could have described to a stranger because none of them had bothered to look at it in years.

Vantrelle had stopped believing in its own stars sometime in Ilsa's childhood. There had been no announcement, no decree. People had simply gotten busy, and then the smoke of the forges had thickened, and then the wards had glowed so constantly along the borders that the sky felt redundant, decorative, like a painting in a room no one entered. Children grew up thinking *star* was a story-word, like *dragon*, like *mercy*.

Now a fishwife grabbed Ilsa's stirrup as she passed — grabbing a warden's stirrup being, under normal circumstances, an excellent way to lose a hand — and pointed up and said, "What are they called? The patterns? My grandmother knew the names, she used to point them out, and I never — I was always *busy*, Warden, I was always so busy—"

"That one's the Ploughman," Ilsa said, because her grandmother had known too. "And that bright one near him is the Lantern. Sailors steer by it."

"The *Lantern*," the woman repeated, tasting it. She let go of the stirrup and stood in the road, crying without shame, and Ilsa rode on through streets full of people learning the names of lights that had been there all along.

It occurred to her, somewhere around the Fountain of Nine Sorrows, that this was what justice actually looked like when it arrived — not courtroom oak panels and sealed writs, but a fishwife weeping in a street because she suddenly owed her dead grandmother an apology. No tribunal could legislate that. You couldn't sentence a city into looking up.

Her deputy was waiting at the Custom House steps, and he was pale.

"Captain. Thank the — Captain, the pens. There's a crowd forming, and the garrison wants instructions, and the Council has issued a new proclamation saying the transports should continue as planned because the wards will require ongoing—" He stopped. Squinted at her face. "Captain?"

"Ongoing maintenance," Ilsa said flatly.

"Yes. They're calling it *stewardship*."

"Naturally they are." She swung down from the saddle. Her whole body had begun to ache in the deep way that announced sleep was coming for her whether she cooperated or not, and she found she wanted, very badly, to do one specific thing before it did. "Corporal, how many wardens do you currently have inside the building?"

"Thirty, plus the garrison detachment—"

"And how many of them have spent the last hour upstairs looking out the windows at the sky?"

The corporal blinked. "...All of them, Captain."

"Wonderful." Ilsa started up the steps. "Then they'll be in a suitable mood."

* *

The holding-pens beneath the Custom House smelled of wet stone and fear, and Ilsa hated every inch of the place with a purity she usually reserved for tax collectors.

Four hundred and six names. In practice, three hundred and eighty-one people, packed into cells designed for a third of that, because nineteen had already been transported down the mountain in the first desperate nights — nineteen names she would carry now alongside the ones already engraved there, and she knew better than to hope the Undercourt had released them gently. Elodie's account had been clear about what the court fed on.

She unlocked the pen block herself, with her own key, in front of the assembled garrison officers, several of whom began simultaneously to object, and she talked over all of them in the voice she'd learned from her first commander, a woman who had once ended a mutiny by pouring the mutineers' tea into her own boot.

"The wards are restored," she said. "You may have noticed the sky. The Council's theory was that the wards run on conscripted talent. That theory is now demonstrably false, unless any of you can explain how four hundred people sitting in this basement contributed to last night's events." She let the silence sit. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, the city was cheering — actual cheering, rolling down through the foundations like distant surf. "The transports are cancelled. The ledgers are voided. These people are free to go home, and any officer who lays a hand on one of them answers to me, and after me to whatever that star up there thinks of him, which I promise you is less."

An officer in the back — Pellam, of course, Pellam with his Council connections and his voice like a filing cabinet — raised a hand as if they were at a lecture. "Captain Reyne. With respect, the writs authorizing these detentions were issued under seal. Legally, until the rescinds arrive—"

"You're quite right, Pellam." Ilsa reached into her coat and produced the conscription ledgers, all eleven volumes, and dropped them with a series of deeply satisfying thuds onto the pen-block table. "Which is why the legal record no longer exists."

"You— those are official documents—"

"They were." She drew her belt knife and opened the first volume to its flyleaf, and cut, slowly and neatly, the cords binding the signatures page. "Wardens keep records so that no one is ever taken without account. That's the whole reason we exist, Pellam — not to enforce whatever the Council needed enforced this week. The account was false. So the record goes." She looked around the room, at thirty tired faces and one appalled one, and softened by exactly one degree. "For those who need paperwork to sleep tonight: write it down tomorrow. Write that the wardens of Vantrelle, on the morning the stars returned, released every soul unlawfully conscripted, and destroyed the instruments of their taking, and accepted whatever consequences followed. Signed Reyne, Warden-Captain, alone. I'll hold the pen myself if anyone wants to watch."

Nobody spoke. From the nearest cell, a small voice — Sefa, the baker's daughter, singing voice, Tuesday crime — said, "Is she serious?"

"She's serious," said another voice, older. "That's Reyne. She once arrested a Councilor's son for selling bad charms to orphans and then sent him home with a receipt."

"I stand by the receipt," Ilsa said, and unlocked the first cell.

* *

The tearing of the remaining ledgers took two hours, and it was, unexpectedly, the best work she had done in twenty years.

Word spread through the pens faster than she could unlock doors. By the second volume, people were coming out of the cells not crouching but walking, and by the fifth, someone had started singing — the baker's daughter, naturally, a low sweet thing without words, and half the pen block picked it up in a ragged untrained harmony that would have made the Conservatory weep. Ilsa tore pages and did not cry, mostly, and told herself the stone dust was irritating her eyes.

Brannoc appeared at her elbow somewhere past midnight, holding two cups of the garrison's terrible coffee, and offered her one.

"Captain. The strangest thing is happening outside."

"I know. The stars."

"No, well. Also the stars. But there's a crowd gathered around the fountain, and they've — hm. How to put this." He drank his coffee like a man buying time. "People are leaving things. At the fountain. Little things. Someone left a candle. Someone left flowers. There's a boy there who's set up a sort of shrine to — I want to be accurate here, Captain — a *shrine to the anonymous thief of Saltrow*. People are leaving buttons and hair ribbons. One man left his hat. It's a very nice hat."

"A shrine," Ilsa said.

"There's a sign. Brannoc read from memory, with the gravity of a town crier: *'She stole back our sky.'*" He paused. "There's also, and I quote the sign, 'a collection jar for when she needs bailing out of prison,' and the jar currently contains nine coppers and the nice hat."

Isla laughed — a real laugh, rusty, surprising her own face with it — and looked out the tall window past Brannoc's shoulder, at the crowd around the fountain, at the candle flames and the ribbon-wrapped buttons, at a city that had spent seven generations outlawing the very thing that had just saved it and was now, in the smallest ways it knew how, trying to say thank you.

The Council would convene within days. There would be inquiries. There would be men like Pellam drafting resolutions about *unlicensed restoration of municipal infrastructure* and *the Reyne Incident*, and there would be warrants, probably, eventually, with her own signature logic turned around on her.

Let them come, she thought. She had watched a pickpocket walk into a hole in a mountain carrying nothing but stolen goods and other people's sins, and walk the sky back out again. Whatever tribunals wanted to make of that, they were welcome to try. She'd even bring the receipts.

"Brannoc," she said, "find out who owns that hat. When she gets back — *if* she gets back — she should have it. Consider it a commendation."

"And the collection jar?"

"Leave the jar. A thief should always have working capital."

* *

Dawn found her on the Custom House roof, because sleep had come within reach and then, characteristically, hovered just past it.

Below, Vantrelle was waking to its own sky. Shutters opening on the east faces. Bakers' chimneys already going. On some rooftop in Saltrow, the family was back in their row, and this time they weren't just staring — the father was pointing, drawing lines between lights with his finger, telling his children the names he half-remembered, getting some of them wrong, and the children were correcting him with the magnificent confidence of children inventing astronomy, and it was going to be fine. New names for old lights. Cities survived on worse.

The ember-star still burned at the center of it, red-gold, steady. Ilsa watched it and thought about the boy she'd never met, who had chosen to carry warmth up a mountain rather than let it go out, and about Corvin, whom she'd arrested twice and released twice for reasons neither of them had examined at the time, and who had burned decades of hoarded memory to buy his daughter one hour. People were so much better than their paperwork. That was the whole scandal of it. Twenty years of ledgers, and not one line in any of them had ever captured a single human being accurately.

She heard the trapdoor behind her and didn't turn.

"If it's a warrant," she said, "tell them I'll sign it after breakfast."

"It's not a warrant, Captain." Hesk's voice, and something odd in it — not alarm, exactly. Restraint. The restraint of a man carrying news he could hardly believe himself. "It's the mountain. The stair. It's — coming back."

Ilsa turned.

At the foot of the far ridge, visible even from here in the strengthening dawn, the rock face below the old watch-station was changing — folding outward, unfolding, stone arranging itself into steps like a flower deciding to bloom in reverse. And on the topmost step, small against the sunrise, two figures were emerging into the morning: one leaning heavily on the other, both of them dust-grey, both of them unmistakably alive.

One of them was a boy she had never seen before, moving carefully, like a lantern carried level.

The other was Elodie Joubert, and she was looking up at the sky as though she had personally hung every star on it — and then her eyes found the Custom House roof, and found Ilsa, and she raised one hand in a wave that managed, somehow, to be sheepish, exhausted, triumphant, and apologetic all at once.

"Captain," Hesk said carefully, "that's the thief. That's the one with the shrine. And the hat."

"I can see that, Hesk."

"What are your orders?"

Ilsa Reyne looked at the returned stars, at the reopened mountain, at the girl who had broken about nine laws and repaired something no law had ever touched. She thought of empty ledgers, of a fishwife learning the word *Lantern*, of nine coppers and a very nice hat.

"My orders," she said, heading for the stairs, "are that somebody put the kettle on. And Hesk — fetch my coat. I believe I'm about to owe a thief an apology, a pardon, and possibly breakfast, and I intend to deliver all three in person."
