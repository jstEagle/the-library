## 21. Empty Hands

Elodie woke to sunlight and the absence of something she couldn't name.

That wasn't quite true. She could name it. She simply kept reaching for it out of habit, the way a tongue returns to a socket where a tooth used to be, and finding nothing there but smooth, healed-over quiet.

For seven years her gift had been a second heartbeat. She'd felt it in her fingertips the way other people felt weather in old scars—a low hum of *takeable*, a constant awareness that everything around her was made of small, portable pieces of itself. A stranger's first snowfall. A mother's lullaby half-rotted with time. The particular gold of a particular afternoon in a garden that no longer existed. All of it loose change in other people's pockets, and her hands always open.

Now her hands were just hands.

She lay in Maren's back room—the same narrow bed, the same water-stained ceiling she'd stared at through a dozen fevers and one memorable hangover—and flexed her fingers against the morning light. Nothing hummed. Nothing reached. The sunlight fell on her palm and stayed exactly where it was put, like sunlight, like nothing at all.

"Well," she said aloud, to the ceiling. "Shit."

The ceiling did not answer. Ceilings never had. But for seven years she had been able to lift the ceiling's memory if she'd wanted—rain on tiles decades ago, the mason's name worn off the cornerstone, some long-dead apprentice's boredom soaked into the plaster. She had done it once, early on, just to see. The ceiling had remembered being a forest. She had cried for an hour afterward without knowing why, and sold the memory the next day for three silvers, and cried about that too.

She sat up. Her body ached everywhere, which felt honest. Her head was clear in a way she distrusted, the way you distrust a room after the furniture's been repossessed—more space, less reason for it.

Maren appeared in the doorway with two cups of chicory and the expression of a woman who has been standing there listening for a while.

"You're awake," Maren said. "Good. I owe Tolla four coppers."

"You *bet* on when I'd wake up?"

"Tolla said noon. I said dawn. Dawn was yesterday, mind, so we renegotiated." She handed over a cup and studied Elodie with the frank, appraising squint she usually reserved for goods of uncertain provenance. "How do you feel?"

Elodie considered the question properly, because Maren was the only person in Vantrelle who would be insulted by a lazy answer.

"Light," she said finally. "Which is worse than it sounds. Like—I keep waiting for the weight and it isn't coming." She wrapped both hands around the cup. "I tried to remember what my gift felt like this morning and I can't. Isn't that funny? I spent seven years as the thing and now I can't even recall it. It's like being asked to describe an old landlord."

"That's not funny," Maren said. "That's sad. It's good chicory, though, so drink it and be sad sitting down."

* * *

The city outside Maren's window had changed overnight, or perhaps over the eleven days Elodie had slept, or perhaps it had changed years ago and only now admitted it. The curfew bells were silent—not postponed, not suspended, but gone; she could see from the window that the bell in Tanner's Row had been taken down entirely, its rope coiled over the yoke like a hanged man given decent burial. People moved through the streets at all hours without hurrying. And above everything, obscenely ordinary, impossibly bright, the stars' absence was repaired: last night they had come back, Maren told her, every one, in a sky so clear it looked scrubbed.

"You did that," Maren said.

"The fire did that. I just stole it." Elodie heard how the sentence landed in her own mouth and winced. "Gods. Listen to me. 'Just stole it.' As if I've ever *just* stolen anything in my life."

"No," Maren agreed comfortably. "You've always stolen things completely. It's why I kept you on."

There was business to attend to, because there is always business. Maren walked her through it over breakfast: the wardens' conscription ledgers burned—officially an accident, unofficially Warden-Captain Reyne standing in the archive with a lantern and a face that dared anyone to ask questions. Queen Sarelle's Undercourt sealed under mountain and law alike, its traded souls released into bodies most of them hadn't inhabited in decades, which was causing what Maren described as "a moderate amount of municipal confusion" and what everyone else described as "the Returned walking around asking where their houses went."

"And your boy," Maren said, delicately, the way she handled anything she cared about.

"He's not my boy."

"Mm," said Maren, who had raised three daughters and recognized deflection the way a fishmonger recognized fish.

"He came down off the mountain two days ago," Maren went on, mercy winning out over teasing, as it generally did with her by the second cup. "Carried the flame himself, in a lamp, like a man delivering bread. Set it up in that ruined chapel on Candlemaker's Lane—the one with the roof half gone. Half the city's been past to look at it. It doesn't go out, Elodie. Rain comes through the roof and it doesn't go out. They're calling it a miracle. The priests are furious; it's not theirs."

Elodie looked at her chicory.

"He's been asking after you," Maren said. "Every day. Politely. He asks politely the way other men shout."

"I know what I have to say to him."

"Do you?"

"I have to tell him—" Her voice did something unhelpful and she started again. "Seven years ago I sold his name. Before Corvin. Before any of it. I was fourteen and I was scared and I fed him to the ledger to save my own skin, and then I paid to forget it, and he lived inside that memory ever since. Every terrible thing that happened to him happened because I was the first coward in the chain." She set the cup down. "And now I'm going to stand in front of him with empty hands and no charm, no trick, nothing to soften it, and say it out loud. That's the whole of what I have left. Saying things out loud."

Maren nodded slowly. Then, because she was Maren: "You'll want to wash first. You look like something the river returned."

* * *

Vantrelle received her strangely.

Not coldly—that was the disorienting part. She had built her whole life on the architecture of not being seen: side streets, downcast eyes, the gray coat that made wardens' gazes slide off her like rain off wax. She put the gray coat on out of habit and stepped out of Maren's door and the city saw her anyway.

A woman at the well stopped drawing water to look at her. A boy selling hot chestnuts pointed. Not hostilely—worse, warmly. By the time she reached the Corn Exchange she'd been thanked twice, once by a man she'd never met whose mother's grief-charm had apparently outlasted its shelf life by years, and once by a Returned woman with a young-old face who pressed a dried apple into her hand and said, "You gave us back," as if Elodie had personally done it, as if Elodie knew her name, which she did not, and could not lift, and did not want to.

"It wasn't me," Elodie kept saying. "It was the fire. It was the queen, at the end. I just—"

"Just stole it," said the chestnut boy, with reverence.

She gave up correcting people somewhere near Candlemaker's Lane, which she supposed was a kind of rebirth, or at least a kind of surrender, and the dried apple was honestly very good.

The chapel came into view the way landmarks do in dreams—larger than remembered, wrong somehow, then suddenly exactly right. Half its roof was indeed gone, open to a sky that still astonished her; the other half sheltered a nave full of candles, hundreds of them, stuck to ledges and sconces and melted into pools along the sills, because the people of Vantrelle, having spent a generation outlawing magic, had responded to one small impossible flame by covering it in devotional wax like nervous cats bringing gifts.

And in the center, on a plain iron stand, burned the last dragon-fire.

It didn't look like much. That was the thing nobody warned you about. It was the size of a cupped hand, gold at the heart and deep red at the rim, and it moved in the draft from the broken roof the way no ordinary flame moved—with patience. With memory. It guttered and righted itself and guttered again, and each time it came back it seemed to be remembering how.

Tomas knelt beside it with his sleeves rolled up, trimming candle stubs into a basket.

He had filled out since the mountain—or rather he had begun to fill out, the ember-hollowness leaving him slowly, the way color comes back to a face after fainting. His hair was still burnt-white at the temples where the fire had held him longest. His hands, moving among the candles, were careful in a way she remembered from before, from when carefulness had just been his personality and not the residue of keeping a soul alive for seven years.

He heard her boots on the stone. Of course he did. He had always heard her coming; that had been their earliest joke, back when jokes between them were cheap as air.

He turned. And Elodie discovered that she had rehearsed this moment on every step from Maren's door, and that every rehearsal had been worthless, because rehearsal assumed she would feel brave, and she did not feel brave. She felt fourteen. She felt like a girl in a market crowd watching a broker write her friend's name into a ledger while she stood very still and said nothing.

"Hi," said Tomas.

"Hi," said Elodie.

Silence. Somewhere in the rafters, a pigeon expressed an opinion.

"You look better," she said, because she was apparently going to open with small talk like a coward, like a person who had never once in her life—

"So do you." He studied her. "Tired-better. You've got the look of someone who slept for eleven days and is still owed another six."

"Everyone keeps saying that. It was ten days. Eleven, fine." She wrapped her arms around herself. The gray coat suddenly felt like a costume. "Tomas. I need to say something and I need you to let me get all the way through it, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I don't have any charms left to buy it back with."

Something moved behind his face—wariness, and under the wariness, hope trying to keep still. He set down the trimming knife. "All right."

"Seven years ago, before Corvin, before the queen, before any of it—it was me. At the Midwinter market. There was a broker's stall and I was hungry and frightened and a warden had marked me, and the broker offered me a way out, and the price was a name." Her voice shook and she let it; she had decided on the stairs, somewhere in that dark rearranging dark, that if she ever survived this she would stop smoothing her own voice over. "Your name. I wrote it myself. In my own handwriting. That's what the wards were built on—that first theft, mine. Everything after, Corvin, the court, the fire, all of it grew out of that seed. And then I paid the broker to take the memory of doing it away from me, and I got to walk around for seven years clean, believing I was only the second-worst person in my own story, while you—" Her breath caught. "While you burned. While you remembered everything, including me, including the girl who fed you to the ledger and then forgot you on purpose because forgetting was cheaper than living with it."

She ran out. That was all of it. Every coin in her purse, spent.

The fire crackled softly. It sounded, absurdly, sympathetic.

Tomas was quiet for a long time. She watched him not speak and understood that he was doing it deliberately, carefully, the way he trimmed candles—giving the moment its exact due, neither more nor less.

"I knew," he said finally.

Elodie's stomach dropped. "What?"

"On the mountain. When you touched the fire and it showed you. I knew before that." He stood, slowly, joints still learning their work. "Elodie, I *was* the memory. Do you understand? Your forgetting didn't erase it—it just filed it somewhere I was living. I've carried the moment you wrote my name for seven years. I watched you do it from inside your own head. I watched you cry afterward, and pay, and walk away lighter." He took a step toward her. "So here's what I need you to hear, and then we're going to stop doing this, because we've been doing versions of it since the market and I'm tired."

"Okay," she whispered.

"I hated you," he said simply. "On the mountain, in the fire, I hated you with everything I had left, which wasn't much, so it was efficient hatred. And then you came down the stair that isn't there, which costs memories to walk, and you came anyway, and you stood in Sarelle's court with your hands shaking and refused both bargains, and you stole the fire itself rather than leave me burning, and it cost you—" he gestured, gently, at all of her— "*this*. Everything. Your whole gift. And the hatred didn't have anywhere to stand anymore. It kept trying. It was very committed." The corner of his mouth moved. "But it turns out you can't hate someone you watched give up the only thing she loved about herself to carry you home."

"Tomas—"

"I'm not finished. I've rehearsed this too, and you interrupted me, so we're even." He closed the distance between them. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and candle wax and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of the orchard behind Corvin's house, of green apples, of a summer that had actually happened to two actual children. "You betrayed me. Truly. Badly. At fourteen, terrified, and then you compounded it for seven years by not knowing you had. Both those things are real. And also you came back for me. Both. I've decided I can hold both, and you're going to have to learn to hold both too, because I am not going to spend the rest of my life being either your wound or your absolution. I'd like to be your friend. Possibly more than your friend, eventually, if you stop flinching every time someone thanks you in the street."

"I don't flinch."

"You flinched at the chestnut boy. I could see it from here. The whole street saw it."

Despite everything—despite the ache and the rawness and the eleven days and the seven years—Elodie laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real, the first laugh she could remember that wasn't currency, that she wasn't spending on anyone, that belonged to no ledger anywhere.

"I don't know how to do this," she said. "Any of it. I've been a thief since I was nine. It's the only way I know how to be close to people—to take a little piece of them, or sell them a piece of me. I have literally nothing to offer you. My hands are empty. I checked this morning. Twice."

"Show me," said Tomas.

She held out her hands, palms up, in the light of the fire that remembered warmth for the whole world. Empty. Ordinary. Calloused at the pads of the fingers from picks and lockwork, scarred at the left thumb from a job gone wrong when she was sixteen, trembling slightly, entirely hers.

They were, she realized, the first thing she had ever owned outright.

"They're good hands," Tomas said, taking them. His grip was warm. Warm like a hearth. Warm like a thing that had chosen, freely, to keep burning. "They've stolen nearly everything in this city, and they came back empty. That's the rarest magic I know of, and I lived inside a dragon-fire for seven years."

The fire behind him flared gently, gold at the heart, and settled—as if it agreed.

Outside, the bells of Vantrelle rang noon for the first time in living memory without anyone needing to be afraid of the hour, and Tomas said, "Come on. I want to show you something," and led her toward the chapel's side door, beyond which, she could see through the broken arch, an old woman was already setting up a table in the lane—laid out with river stones and pressed flowers and small jars of honey, priced honestly, sold kindly, as if waiting for someone in particular to arrive and take the shop next door.
