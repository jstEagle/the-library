## 22. Zephyrine and the Veil

The bell above the door of the shop rang the way bells should ring in spring—bright and unhurried, like it had nowhere better to be.

Elodie looked up from her ledger, which was a real ledger now, with real sums in it, mostly small ones, and saw a woman hesitating on the threshold as though she wasn't sure shops like this were allowed to exist. Many people still weren't sure. The shop sat on Weavers' Row where the pawnbrokers thinned out and the candle-makers began, wedged between a chandler who whistled off-key and a tailor who claimed to remember when the Row had been fashionable. The sign above the door read **VASQUEZ & KEEPER — HONEST CHARMS**, and beneath it, in smaller letters that Elodie had painted herself and immediately regretted: *nothing here was ever anyone else's.*

"Come in," Elodie said. "Mind the step. It minds everyone."

Inside, the shop smelled of beeswax and river water and the dried lavender Tomas insisted on hanging in bundles from every beam, because he said a flame without something sweet nearby got lonely. Shelves ran the length of both walls, crowded with the inventory of an honest life: pressed flowers under glass, their colors gone soft and papery; river stones in bowls, smooth and grey and utterly unmagical; spools of ribbon; jars of honey from the chandler's cousin's hives; little folded paper stars that children bought for a copper each and wore on strings around their necks as though they were precious.

They were precious. That was rather the point.

"You're the one," the woman said. Not accusatory. Just stating it, the way people stated the weather.

"I'm one of many things," Elodie said. "What can I get you?"

"They say you used to—" The woman stopped herself, flushed, tried again. "My mother died in March. And my neighbor told me, she said, go to the shop on Weavers' Row, because there was a time—I know it's not possible anymore—but there was a time I could close my eyes and hear her voice, and lately I can't find it. Lately when I reach for it there's just nothing there." Her hands twisted together. "I thought maybe you'd kept some. In a jar. I know how it sounds."

"It doesn't sound like anything," Elodie said gently. "Half the city has asked me that by now. The other half is working up the courage."

She came around the counter and steered the woman to the stool by the window, the good stool, the one Tomas had cushioned with an old coat of his that had survived the mountain better than he had. Then she poured two cups of the chicory coffee always simmering on the little stove and sat down across from her, and she did not sell her anything at all, not for a while.

Here is what Elodie Joubert could no longer do: she could not lift a memory out of a person the way you'd lift a coin from a sleeping man's pocket. She could not bottle a dead mother's voice or fold a first kiss into a charm small enough to hide in a boot heel. She could not take anything, actually. Her hands, which had once been so light, so clever, so *absent*—hands that could pass through the space between a memory and its owner without leaving a mark—those hands were just hands now. Ordinary. Warm. Slightly ink-stained.

Here is what she could do, which had taken her most of the winter to learn: she could sit with a grieving woman and say, *tell me about her*, and listen until the voice came back—not in a jar, not for sale, but where it belonged, in the woman's own mouth, in the telling.

It was slower work than theft. It paid worse. She would not have traded it for the whole Undercourt's hoard.

* * *

Tomas came up from the cellar at midday, carrying the day's coal in a scuttle and trailing the smell of woodsmoke, and the woman at the window looked at him the way everyone looked at him now—with that careful, hungry curiosity, checking his hands, his eyes, the line of his jaw, looking for the ember underneath.

He let them look. He'd gotten very good at letting people look.

There was nothing dramatic to see. That was the miracle of him, the part Elodie still caught herself staring at sometimes when she thought he wasn't watching, which he always was. He was tall and thin and his hair grew back crooked where it had burned away, and his knuckles cracked in cold weather, and when he laughed too hard he coughed, and he was alive down to his ordinary, unburning bones.

In the cellar, in a hearth of black stone that Maren's stonemason cousin had built to specifications no one fully understood, burned a fire the size of a cupped pair of hands. It never went out. It never needed much feeding—a shovel of coal a week, a bundle of lavender, occasionally, at Tomas's insistence, a drop of honey. It did not roar or rage or demand souls. It sat in its little hearth like a cat that had decided, after long consideration, to stay.

People came from all over Vantrelle to see it. They paid nothing. Tomas would carry them down the stairs two at a time, warn them not to lean too close—"it likes to show off"—and they would stand before the small gold flame and weep, most of them, though none of them could have said exactly why, except that it was warm in a way that felt like being remembered.

"The last dragon-fire," the guidebooks called it now, though there was no dragon and only one fire and the guidebooks were printed by a man who had never been within a mile of the mountain. "Relic of the Old World." Pilgrims left ribbons on the fence outside. The wardens had given up trying to classify it three separate times.

Tomas set the scuttle down and kissed the top of Elodie's head, which she pretended to find annoying, which they both knew she didn't.

"Our visitor wants to know about the old days," he said to the woman, cheerful and terrible. "Shall I tell her about the time she stole a warden's boots?"

"She was asleep," Elodie said. "It hardly counts as stealing if they're asleep."

"It counts," said Tomas. "I've seen the ledger."

"There is no ledger."

"There's *always* a ledger." He grinned at the woman, and she laughed despite herself, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, the way shoulders did in this shop, eventually, always. "Her legend is much grander than she is. You'll want to be careful of that. She'll tell you she planned everything. She planned nothing. She walked into the Undercourt with a stolen shawl and a bad attitude and made it up as she went, and half of Vantrelle thinks it was destiny."

"And what do you think?"

Tomas considered this with the seriousness he brought to very few things—the fire, the lavender, Elodie.

"I think," he said, "that destiny is what people call it afterward, when it turns out well."

* * *

The stars had come back on a Tuesday.

That was how everyone remembered it, afterward—not the wards rising, not Queen Sarelle's court emptying its souls into the dark like a purse spilled open, but the stars, hundreds of them, thousands, filling the sky over Vantrelle for the first time in seven years, so that people walked out of their houses in their nightclothes and stood in the streets with their heads tipped back and their mouths open, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing any of them could have said that the sky wasn't already saying.

Elodie had watched it from the mountainside with Tomas fading in her arms and Ilsa Reyne standing ten feet away holding a lantern she no longer needed, and she had thought, with the strange clarity of exhaustion: *so that's what we were keeping up there all along.*

Seven years of dark. Seven years of a city that forgot its own sky and then forgot it had forgotten, the way cities do, the way people do—building roofs over the absence and calling it architecture. Corvin had sold her childhood for one night of that dark. A night of survival purchased with a keystone, and the keystone had been her—the small red moment of her seventh summer, a festival, a paper dragon on a pole, her hand in someone's hand—and she had spent years believing that theft was the whole story. That she was the wounded party. That the ledger only ran one direction.

Then the fire had shown her the truth of it, chapter twelve of her own life, the chapter she'd paid to lose: a frightened fourteen-year-old girl in a snowdrift behind the tanner's yard, trading a boy's name to a broker's shadow to buy her own passage out. Corvin hadn't started it. Corvin had merely finished what she'd begun, tidily, in the manner of foster-fathers, completing the transaction his daughter had opened.

She had hated him for three days after learning it. She had hated herself for longer. What she felt now was neither of those things—it was more like the way you feel about a scar on your own palm: familiar, yours, proof of a hand that healed.

Corvin himself was six months gone. He had burned his hoarded decades buying her an hour in the Undercourt, and the hour had been enough, and he had walked out of the mountain an old man with almost nothing left, and he had lived long enough to see the stars come back, and to drink one toast on Weavers' Row, and to tell her, gruffly, that the chicory coffee was an abomination, and then in the spring he had simply not woken up, comfortably, in the room above the chandler's, with the window open. Maren said he'd died the way he lived: quietly, at an angle to the law, owing nothing.

He owed plenty. But Maren said it kindly, and Elodie had chosen to let it stand, because grief, she had learned, did not require accurate accounting.

Maren herself kept the maps framed now, on the wall of her shop across the Row—the stair that wasn't there, the passages beneath the mountain, annotated in her crabbed hand with notes like *DO NOT TRUST THE THIRD LANDING* and *this bit lies.* Scholars came to copy them. She charged them. Some things, Maren maintained, should never become honest.

And Ilsa Reyne—the Warden-Captain still, though she'd refused promotion twice—came in every Thursday for river stones. Nobody knew why, including, Elodie suspected, Ilsa. She bought one grey stone a week, held it in her fist all the way back to the garrison, and Elodie had stopped asking, because some charms worked precisely because no one explained them.

* * *

Closing time came slow that evening, the way it did in spring, when the light lingered and so did the customers. The woman with her mother's voice stayed an hour and left with a packet of pressed forget-me-nots she hadn't paid for—Elodie had simply declined to take her money, and the woman had stopped arguing when she understood it wasn't charity, exactly. It was closer to arithmetic. Somewhere in the great ledger of things, a balance had shifted, and Elodie was content to leave it there.

Tomas banked the stove. Elodie counted the till, which came to less than the rent and slightly more than nothing, a margin they occupied with what Tomas called dignity and Elodie called practice.

"Zephyrine," he said, from the doorway to the cellar. He said it the way he always did, testing it, amused by it.

"Don't start."

"You named the fire after a character in your own unwritten book. There are people in this city who think Zephyrine is the fire's *given name*. There is a *plaque*."

"The plaque was the neighbors' idea."

"There's a *festival*."

"There was one festival. One. And the dragon was very well received."

"A paper dragon," Tomas said, "carried through Weavers' Row on a pole, in honor of a fire that is named after a novel you will not write."

"I am writing it."

"You have been writing it since winter. How many pages?"

Elodie counted the till again, slowly, with dignity.

"That's what I thought," said Tomas, and went down the stairs laughing, which turned into coughing, which turned into quiet, and she gave him his minute before following, because he was allowed his minute and so was she.

The cellar was warm. The fire sat in its black hearth, small and gold and patient, and Tomas crouched beside it with his sleeves rolled up, doing whatever it was he did down here of an evening—talking to it, mostly, she suspected, the way he talked to the lavender and the honey and, increasingly, to her, an easy low narration of the day's small events, as though the world were a story he was keeping the fire informed of.

She sat down next to him on the cold stone floor, because her knees were thirty-one years old and had earned the right to complain, and leaned her shoulder against his.

"What did you tell it today?" she asked.

"That the widow found her mother's voice." He poked the coals idly; the flame leaned toward his hand, fond as a dog. "That you gave away the forget-me-nots again and we'll be eating lentils until Friday. That the tailor finally admitted he doesn't remember when Weavers' Row was fashionable and made the whole thing up."

"He did *not*."

"He did. Apparently it was aspirational."

She laughed, and the fire flickered at the sound, and downstairs in the warm dark with the boy she had betrayed and been forgiven by—though *forgiven* was too clean a word for what they'd done for each other, which was less like forgiveness and more like two people agreeing to stop charging interest—Elodie felt the thought arrive that had been circling her all spring, patient as a bird.

The veil. Everyone in Vantrelle talked about the veil now, the membrane between memory and self, between what happened and what you carried of it. The scholars drew diagrams. The priests disagreed about it. Ilsa had a whole drawer of reports on it, labeled and cross-referenced, as if a thing could be governed by being filed.

But Elodie had been on both sides of it. She had passed memories through it like contraband, and she had fallen through it herself, blank and seven years old, and she had clawed back up through it into the worst truth of her life, and here was what none of the diagrams showed:

The veil had never been a wall. It was a curtain, hung by her own hand, in her own house. She had drawn it shut to survive, and other people had profited from her staying hidden behind it, and in the end no queen and no fire and no magic at all had lifted it—only a girl grown into a woman, reaching up with ordinary hands, choosing to pull it back and be seen.

The talent was gone. The veil remained, the way curtains remain in every house, between every self and every watcher. But hers stood open now. Morning and evening, lamplight and starlight, open.

"Tomas," she said.

"Mm."

"When I die, don't put me in a ledger."

He turned to look at her, firelight on the crooked new growth of his hair. "Where did that come from?"

"Nowhere. Everywhere. I was just thinking about accounts. About how I spent half my life keeping books on things that shouldn't have been for sale, and the other half pretending I wasn't." She picked up a cool pebble from the hearthstone and turned it in her fingers. "I want the opposite of that. When it's over, I want it to have been—" She searched. "Unguarded. I want to have been unguarded."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Then he took the pebble from her hand and set it at the edge of the hearth, where the fire could warm it, the way he set everything near the fire eventually.

"You already are," he said. "You're the least guarded person I know. You cry at pressed flowers. You gave a warden a discount out of pure sentiment. Last week you told a complete stranger about the snowdrift."

"I told her that in *confidence*."

"You told her in a shop. With me in the room. And the fire." He smiled. "The fire tells the lavender everything, you know."

"Then the lavender knows I'm right."

"The lavender," said Tomas gravely, "thinks you should finish the book."

Outside, above Weavers' Row, the first stars were coming out—Tuesday's stars, Thursday's, the whole returned and reliable congregation of them—and Elodie tipped her head back against the cellar wall and looked up through the little window at the strip of darkening blue, and thought: *tomorrow, pages one through three. Or four. Definitely more than three.*

The fire crackled, small and warm and eternal, and if a fire could be said to have an opinion, hers seemed to agree.

Upstairs, the shop bell rang—odd, at this hour—and Elodie heard footsteps cross the floorboards above, hesitant, and then a voice call down the cellar stairs, a voice she knew, dry as old paper and entirely impossible:

"Well," said Maren, from the top of the steps, holding a lantern and wearing an expression Elodie had learned, over one long winter of maps, to dread. "Don't either of you panic. But there's a girl at the door. Says she needs the thief. Says she's come a very long way down."
