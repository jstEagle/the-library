## 1. The Ninety-First Dish

The pheasant arrived at the ninth bell, roasted to a lacquered bronze and stuffed with figs and forced cherries, and I knew before the server set it down that it was going to try to kill me.

This is not a metaphor. I am the Queen's taster. Everything tries to kill me professionally. What made this pheasant different was that it was going to *try*, deliberately, with intent and seasoning—which, in my line of work, is practically a love letter.

Let me explain how an evening works when you are Coral Delacroix, Taster to the Crown of Ashfall, keeper of ninety-one immunities and exactly zero illusions.

The hall of the Winter Palace is long enough that the servers age visibly crossing it. The ceiling is painted with a hunt scene in which every noble face has been repainted at least twice, because usurpers make poor muses. Tonight the court smelled of beeswax, wet wool, and the particular anxiety of a court that has heard the name *Veyra* spoken too often in the last month, always quietly, always near a door.

I sat at my small table one step below the dais—the taster's station, close enough to be honored, low enough to be expendable—and watched the pheasant come toward me on its silver platter like a bride down an aisle it did not choose.

"You look grim tonight, Master Delacroix," murmured Lady Isolde two seats over, who says things like that because she thinks dread is flirtatious.

"I always look grim," I said. "It's the ledgers. Squints the soul."

Which was true, though not for the reason she'd assume. By day—well, technically by late morning, after the dawn tasting and a bath and a great deal of willow bark tea—I keep the books for the Guild of Impossible Things. Souls, years, names, shadows, first loves: if someone wants to sell it, borrow it, or steal it back, the Guild brokers the deal, and I write down what it cost. It pays poorly in coin and extravagantly in nightmares, and it means I know precisely what a human being goes for on the open market these days. (Less than you'd hope. More than most can afford.)

It also means I know poisons the way a choir master knows voices. Not just their names—their *character*.

So: the pheasant. I took the carving knife and fork, and I performed the little theater expected of me—the slice through the breast, the turn of the meat, the sniff, the pause—while my actual work happened underneath, quiet as bookkeeping.

First: henbane. Faint. Ground into the stuffing under the figs, masked by clove. A classic. Henbane kills ordinary men in an hour and leaves them grinning, which courts find embarrassing, so whoever chose it had chosen it not for the Queen but—

Second: there, riding on top of the henbane like a signature on a forgery, something rarer. Wolfsbane cut with ash of silverleaf, prepared the northern way, the way you prepare it when your target has built up tolerance and you want to slip past his body's defenses like a rumor through a small town.

That preparation has one purpose. It defeats acquired immunity.

It defeats *my* immunity.

I want to be clear about the moment: I stood in front of three hundred courtiers with a slice of poisoned pheasant on a fork, smiling the bland smile of a man thinking about nothing, and inside my skull a very calm voice said, *Ah. This one is for me.*

Ninety-one times I have swallowed something meant to kill the Queen. Nightly, for years—I take her plate's poison so she needn't fear it, and each time my body learns, the way bodies do, and the dose stops being lethal and becomes merely educational. It is a horrible trade and I have never once regretted it, which I consider proof that I don't understand myself as well as I pretend.

But no one seasons a dish for the taster unless they know the taster's habits. Unless they have studied him. Which meant somewhere in this candlelit room, watching me with well-practiced indifference, was a person who knew about Coral Delacroix's ninety-one evenings of slow suicide, and had decided to end all ninety-two of them in one course.

I ate the pheasant.

Of course I ate the pheasant. If I hadn't, the Queen's plate would have gone back suspect, the kitchens would have burned, the cook would have hanged, and whoever wrote that recipe would simply have tried again next week with better penmanship. If I *did* eat it—if my body held, if ninety-one nights of careful poisoning had built walls high enough—I bought us information instead of terror. Information is the only dish the court never tires of.

Also, and I admit this less proudly: I wanted to know if it would work.

The henbane hit first, familiar as an old debt—a warmth behind the eyes, a slight sweetening of the air. I catalogued it and set it aside. The wolfsbane-and-silverleaf came slower, a cold climbing the spine rung by rung, and here was where the evening got interesting, because my heart stuttered once—*once*—in ninety-two attempts, and I felt the whole clever architecture of my defenses lean and groan like a ship taking green water.

Then it held.

I finished the portion, chewed a sprig of mint for form's sake, and bowed to the dais. "The pheasant is sound, Your Majesty. Though the figs are showing off."

Laughter, the safe kind. The Queen ate. The hall relaxed into gluttony. And I sat back down with sweat cooling along my hairline and began, with the part of my mind not busy staying alive, to count suspects.

* * *

You learn to watch a court the way I watch a sauce: not at the surface, but at what rises to it.

Who did not look at me during the tasting? That's the first tell. Poisoners watch their work go in; amateurs watch the victim; professionals watch *nothing*, with tremendous discipline. I found four such disciplines around the room. Lord Halden, who owed the Guild six borrowed years and could not meet my eye on principle ever since. The new underchamberlain, a soft man with ink-stained cuffs he wore like camouflage. A foreign merchant whose trade papers I'd once refused to authenticate. And, at the far end, half in shadow, a woman in Veyra grey who was not on anyone's invitation list and whom everyone was pretending not to notice, because the war is coming and nobody wants to be rude to the future.

Any of the four could have reached the kitchens. Only one of them could have known the exact preparation of wolfsbane that slips past a veteran taster's blood.

I'll spare you the detective monologue. The truth is I couldn't narrow it yet, and worse, I didn't want to think too loudly about it, because there was another possibility sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, and it was this: *what if it wasn't the court at all?*

What if it was the Guild?

Think about it. I am the man who knows what everyone in three cities owes. I have written *one shadow, sold* and *three years, pawned against a daughter's marriage* until the phrases live in my hands like calluses. There are people who would pay a great deal for my silence, more for my memory, and a truly staggering amount for my absence. And unlike the court poisoners, the Guild's clients know exactly what I swallow every night and exactly what it has cost me.

Someone had cooked for Coral Delacroix specifically. Someone had studied me the way I study toxins—by character.

I drank water I did not test, because paranoia untested is just thirst with ambition, and I let the court swirl around me, and I thought about ledgers.

* * *

Here is the thing they don't tell you about building immunity: you don't get to keep it honestly. Every immunity I own was paid for in an evening of dying slowly and choosing, minute by minute, not to. Ninety-one payments. I could draw you a map of each one. Night thirty-four, ergot, the visions, the Queen's chaplain holding my hand and praying to a god we both privately considered fictional. Night sixty, the aconite scare, when my heart ran like a loom and I recited ledger entries to steady it—*two shadows, one name, half a first love*—because numbers were the only prayer I trusted.

People imagine a taster's courage is in the swallowing. It isn't. Swallowing is easy; hunger swallows. The courage is in the hour afterward, alone in the taster's quarters, keeping yourself company while your body argues with itself about whether to continue existing.

That night the argument went long. The silverleaf cold came back at midnight for a second pass, found the walls standing, and retreated grudgingly, like a creditor. I lay on my cot in the little stone room beneath the east stair—palace logic being that tasters should sleep where their deaths won't disturb anyone—and stared at the ceiling and did arithmetic I didn't like.

One: someone had aimed at me. Two: they'd known my tolerance to the grain. Three: tolerance to that degree is recorded in exactly one place outside my own veins.

In my ledgers. Where I log every dose, every night, in my own neat hand, because a professional keeps records even of his own poisoning. *Especially* of his own poisoning.

Four: my ledgers travel. Twice a month, copies go to the guildhall. Which sits, I will note, between the palace and the river, on a street anyone can walk.

I did not sleep so much as lose consciousness politely, the way one does before an early appointment.

* * *

Morning came up grey over Ashfall, and the city did what Ashfall does—rang bells that disagree with each other, fried onions, argued about the price of fish. I walked to the guildhall through streets still slick from night rain, past the criers already selling the news ("USURPER GAINS IN THE NORTH, QUEEN CONFIDENT, PHEASANT EXCELLENT"), wearing my other self like a change of coat.

At the palace I am the taster: silent, precise, faintly grim. At the Guild of Impossible Things I am Coral Delacroix, Keeper of Ledgers, and I am allowed to talk, which is why I prefer it.

The guildhall is a tall thin building that is larger inside than outside, which is not magic, it's just that the Guild bought the extra space from someone and nobody remembers whom, and the space has opinions. Shelves of contract-scrolls climb past the point where ceilings usually give up. Clients wait on benches worn smooth by the backsides of the desperate. And above the counter hangs the Guild's only law, carved in wood by a founder whose name we also sold long ago:

**EVERYTHING HAS A PRICE. THE TRAGEDY IS THAT MOST PEOPLE PAY IT TWICE.**

"Delacroix!" bellowed Maren Ostra, the guildmaster, from behind her counter, a woman built like a dock crane with a voice to match. "You're alive. Disappointing. Half the city bet otherwise."

"The city flatters itself. I'm difficult to kill; I've had practice."

"So I hear." Her eyes did a thing then—flicked down, flicked up, quickly, like a card sharp checking a deck. "Late plates again last night?"

"One course. Pheasant." I set my satchel on the counter and began unpacking the day's ledgers, watching her watch them. "Henbane and dressed wolfsbane. Northern preparation."

Maren is very good at not reacting. I've seen her not react to a man selling his own grave. But there was a half-second where her jaw set, and I am, whatever else is true of me, a professional observer of small doses.

"You knew," I said.

"I know many things. It's my establishment."

"Maren."

She sighed through her nose, the sound of a woman paying a small toll. "A client asked questions about you last week. Tolerance schedules. Dosing history. I told him the Guild doesn't discuss its staff." She straightened a stack of scrolls that didn't need it. "He paid for the answer anyway. Everyone pays, Coral. You know the law."

"What did you sell him?"

"Nothing useful," she said, which in Maren's mouth meant *something essential but deniable*. "Go record your trades. You look like death's unpaid invoice."

* * *

Ledger-keeping looks like nothing and costs everything. That afternoon I entered the night's business into the Great Book while the hall murmured around me: a cooper's apprentice selling his shadow—"it follows the wrong people," he kept saying, and I wrote *shadow, one, condition: anxious*—and a widow borrowing three years to see her son married, signing the repayment clause without reading it, and me reading it for her, out loud, twice, because that much I can do.

And then I turned to the daily reconciliation page, the dull housekeeping page where the Guild logs its own inventory, and my pen stopped.

There was an entry. In my handwriting.

I want to be precise about what that sentence means, because precision is all I had left at that moment. It was my hand—the slightly hooked *f*, the way I cross sevens, the cramped margin note style that three years of scribes have failed to imitate. My ink. My spacing. And the date at the top of the entry was sixty-one years before my birth.

*One soul, sold, court beneath the mountain.*

Seller: DELACROIX.
Price: survival of line. Paid in full.
Buyer: the Broker.
Status: **held.**

Below it, in the same impossible hand, a second line, smaller, almost an apology:

*Item is not aware it is an item.*

I read it eleven times. The hall went on around me—a client haggling over a grandmother's blessing, the shadow-boy crying quietly by the door, Maren counting coins that counted themselves—and I sat perfectly still with my whole identity doing something vertiginous underneath me, like a floor discovered to be a trapdoor.

My handwriting. My date that predates me. My soul, apparently, filed.

You would think a man who trades in souls for a living would have wondered, at least once, about the accounting of his own. But you don't. I promise you, you don't. You taste the poison, you write the ledger, you walk home, and you never once lift your own hand and think *whose mark is this?* The self is the one item no one appraises, because everyone assumes they hold the deed.

Apparently I did not hold the deed. Apparently the deed was old, and dark, and addressed to a court beneath a mountain I had never seen, and the price of it had been my family's continued breathing, paid before I drew breath to object.

"Delacroix." Maren's voice, from directly beside me. When had she come around the counter? "You've gone the color of the margins."

"There's an entry," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised both of us. "In the reconciliation book. Sixty-one years early. In my hand."

Something crossed her face—not surprise. Worse than surprise. *Confirmation.*

"Show me," she said, and then, before I could move: "No. Wait." She looked at the book, at me, at the door, where the afternoon light had gone strangely thin, the way it does before weather or before news. "Coral. Last night's pheasant. Did you wonder why anyone would bother studying a taster's tolerances?"

"It occurred to me, yes."

"Well," said Maren Ostra, guildmaster, who had told a stranger nothing useful and me nothing true, "wonder harder. Because if your ledger pages started traveling two weeks ago the way I think they did—and if the wrong reader found the reconciliation entries the way I think he did—then that wolfsbane wasn't an assassination attempt, boy. It was an appraisal."

The hall's murmur seemed to drop away from us, leaving a silence with edges.

"And whoever appraised you," she said quietly, "knows exactly what you're worth."

Behind her, in the reconciliation book, the ink of the impossible entry was drying darker, the way ink does when it has finally, after sixty-one years, been read.
