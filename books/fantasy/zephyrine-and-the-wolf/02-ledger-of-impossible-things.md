## 2. Ledger of Impossible Things

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its books in a cellar beneath a chandlery, because the best place to hide anything in this city is under something that smells worse. I descended the eleven worn steps at half past three in the morning, still tasting pheasant, still tasting the arsenic under the pheasant, and unlocked the ledger-cage with the key I keep on a cord around my neck, where it has left a pale groove in my skin that I have decided to think of as a kind of collar of office.

The ledgers do not like being read by daylight. That is not superstition; it is policy. Ink that records impossible things has a tendency to fade if exposed to honest light too often, the way a lie fades when you repeat it to the wrong audience. So we work by tallow-candle, in the smell of old wax and older debts, and we write small.

My name — my working name, my night name — is entered in the front of every volume: *Coral Delacroix, Keeper of Accounts.* It is the only line in six hundred years of records written in red, because the guildmaster insisted the keeper's name should look like blood on the page, to remind him what he is counting. Master Aldery was sentimental in an administrative way. He cried once, I'm told, at the funeral of a decimal point.

I set out the night's instruments: quill, knife for trimming, sand for drying, and the Ledger of Impossible Things itself, Volume Forty-Nine, bound in eel leather that is warm to the touch no matter the season. It should not be warm. Nothing about this cellar should be warm. But the ledger has been drinking trades for six centuries and I imagine it has developed something like a metabolism.

There were three entries waiting from tonight. There are always entries waiting. The guild does not sleep; it merely changes shifts, from the people who buy impossible things to the person who writes down that they did.

* * *

The first trade of the night belonged to Mistress Ondile Vash, widow, forty-one years of age, resident of Saltrow, who came to us last spring to have her shadow stolen. This is more common than you would think. People come to us to lose their shadows when their shadows know too much — when the silhouette cast on the bedchamber wall at dusk shows them holding a knife they have not yet admitted they want to hold, or leaning toward a door they have sworn not to open. A shadow is a confession that follows you around. Ondile wanted hers gone before her sister arrived to visit, because her sister had always been able to read her posture like scripture.

The thief we contracted — I will not name him here; the ledger names him, which is punishment enough — had delivered the shadow to our strongroom in a jar of smoked glass, where it drifted and pressed itself against the sides like a moth with opinions. My job was not to judge the theft. My job was to record it.

*Item: one (1) shadow, female-presenting, medium build, slightly frayed at the hem from years of catching on doorways. Condition: sullen but intact. Held in trust pending final payment of two hundred crowns or equivalent sorrow.*

That last clause is standard. The guild accepts payment in coin, in years, in names, or in sorrow, weighed on the sorrow-scale in the back room, which is a set of brass pans that have never once balanced and never once needed to.

I wrote the entry in my smallest hand. The ledger absorbed the ink the way dry ground absorbs rain, with a faint sigh, and somewhere in the strongroom the shadow in its jar turned over in its sleep. You learn not to look up when that happens. Looking up encourages it.

* * *

The second entry took longer, because borrowed years are fussy things to catalogue.

A cooper from the Weeping Ward — young, twenty-two, with a wife dying of the grey lung-rot — had come to the guild three weeks ago to borrow three years. Not from us directly; we don't lend years, we *broker* them, which is a different sin entirely. He had borrowed three years from a moneylender named Quillon Harrow, who is himself one of our clients, and the transaction needed recording on both ends, which meant I wrote the same entry twice in two different hands so neither lender nor borrower could match the pages. This is called double-entry bookkeeping, and it is the closest thing to magic that honest men invented before the dishonest ones got involved.

Three years, borrowed against the cooper's own future old age. When he pays, he will be an old man who lost three years of being old — he will skip, in effect, from eighty to eighty-three without the intervening winters. If he defaults, the years will be collected from his body at whatever moment the guild's collectors find convenient, and he will experience all three of them at once, compressed into a single hour, which has been known to kill men and has been known, twice, to make them saints.

*Item: three (3) years, unmarked, average quality, drawn from the account of Q. Harrow and lent to J— of the Weeping Ward against future senescence. Interest: one memory of the sea, payable annually.*

The interest made me pause. Quillon Harrow always takes memories of the sea. He has a vault of them, we believe, hundreds of them, and no one knows why, and no one asks, because a man who lends years and collects oceans is not a man you interrupt mid-arithmetic.

I recorded the transfer. The cooper would carry his wife south in the spring, where the air is thick enough to chew, and she might live out her borrowed time breathing. Love, in my experience, is the only collateral the guild has ever accepted that reliably appreciates in value. It also, reliably, gets seized. But that is a complaint for another ledger.

* * *

The third entry was mine to collect personally, and I had almost finished with the other two when I remembered it, which I note now — in hindsight, in horror — because I have never once forgotten a third entry. I forgot it the way you forget your own heartbeat: because it was too close, too constant, too much a part of the machinery of me.

Tonight, at the royal table, I had tasted the pheasant. Under the pheasant: arsenic, refined, in a quantity calibrated not to the King's weight but to *mine*. Someone had seasoned the dish for the taster. I had swallowed it anyway, as I swallow everything, one more coin in the account of immunities I keep in my head — ninety-one poisons resisted, each dose a small tuition paid toward surviving the next. And beneath the clinical arithmetic of that moment there had been a question I declined to examine, the way you decline to examine a loose tooth: *who in that hall knew my second life?*

So the third entry was simple. It was a note to the guildmaster, flagging the attempt, recommending inquiry. I dipped the quill. I wrote the date.

And then my hand stopped, because the date was already written.

Not by me — not tonight-me, sitting in this chair with ink on his cuff. The page showed the date in full, the day of the poisoned pheasant, the day after tomorrow's tomorrow rendered precisely, and beneath it, in handwriting I recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in a shop window — with a half-second delay, with a vertiginous sense of *that can't be where I'm standing* — was an entry.

I know my own hand. Sixteen years of keeping these books will do that. The slight backward lean of my capitals. The way I cross my sevens with a flick rather than a stroke. The little hook I put on the terminal letter of a word when I am writing quickly and honestly, which I do not do when I am writing carefully and lying.

The hook was there. Whoever wrote this had been telling the truth.

*One soul, sold, court beneath the mountain.*

And below that, the seller's name, entered in the column we reserve for the party parting with the goods: **Delacroix**.

I sat very still. In the strongroom, the stolen shadow pressed its palms flat against the smoked glass, and I understood suddenly that it could see me, and that it was watching the way you watch a man on a ledge.

Let me explain what that entry means, mechanically, because mechanics were all I had. Every entry in this ledger carries four parts: the thing traded, the price, the parties, and the *provenance* — the chain of ownership stretching back through every hand that ever held the thing. Souls are the guild's oldest stock. They are recorded with provenance going back to the cradle, because a soul's history determines its worth, the way a diamond's history does. I have traced souls through nine generations. I once spent a winter establishing that a merchant's soul had passed, briefly, through the possession of a shepherd dog, and adjusting its valuation accordingly.

This entry had no provenance. It had no price. It had no counterparty listed as buyer — only a destination, *court beneath the mountain*, which is not a client's name but an address, and not an address anyone in the city can give you directions to, because the mountain road does not appear on maps and the maps it appears on do not appear anywhere.

It had only the seller's name. Delacroix. And the date.

I checked the date four times. I am not proud of the fourth check; by then I was holding the ledger open with both hands like a man holding shut a door against weather. The date was sixty-two years before my birth. My grandfather's era. My grandfather, Yves Delacroix, dyer of cloth, dead before I drew breath, whose face I know only from a portrait that hangs in my aunt's hallway and whose handwriting I have seen exactly never.

But the hand on the page was mine. Not similar. Mine. The backward-leaning capitals. The flicked sevens. The hook on the last letter that means honesty.

There is a rational explanation and I constructed it in about ninety seconds, because constructing rational explanations quickly is the other half of my job. Someone with my handwriting had written this — a descendant, a forger, a clerk trained in my hand for purposes of fraud. The date was wrong or symbolic or in some calendar I don't use. The word *Delacroix* referred to any of the forty-some Delacroixes scattered through the city registers, most of whom I'd happily disown on principle.

Except.

Except that the ledger does not accept false entries. This is the first thing they teach you in the cellar, before they teach you the alphabet of trade-script. The ledger is not paper; paper is just what it wears. An entry that is untrue sits on the page like water on glass, beading, refusing to soak, until it slides off overnight and leaves the page blank. Six centuries of guildmasters have tested this with forged entries, jokes, mistakes, and one memorable act of sabotage involving a rival house and a fabricated sale of the moon. The ledger ate them all. It keeps only truth, in whatever hand the truth arrives in.

The entry sat in the page like a stone in flesh.

I got up. I walked, in a manner I would charitably call steady, to the archive wall, and pulled the registry of births for my parish, because if the date was nonsense then I wanted proof in ink that predated tonight. Delacroix, parish of Saint Ferrel, salt-tax district. I ran my finger down the columns past my own name — *Coral, son of Maren and Edouard Delacroix* — and stopped at the line above it, because there was a line above it, in a hand I didn't recognize, in faded iron-gall ink:

*Delacroix, Yves. Recorded in settlement of debt. See: court beneath the mountain. Consideration: one soul, held in trust for the line entire.*

Held in trust for the line entire.

I want to tell you what that felt like, and I find I can only tell you what it was like to be a taster doing it. It was like the fourth course of a state dinner, when you have already swallowed the suspect dish and must sit, upright, pleasant, napkin in lap, for forty more minutes while the poison decides what it intends to do with you. Everything in me screaming. Everything on me calm. The fork moving. The smile holding.

I had been tasting myself all evening without knowing it. Arsenic under the pheasant, and under the arsenic, older than both: a debt, sixty-two years deep, seasoned specifically for me.

Someone had written tonight's date in my hand before I was born. Which meant either that the ledger lies — impossible — or that somewhere ahead of me was the moment I went back, or reached across, or otherwise stood at that page and wrote those words, and that the man who wrote them already knew what I was only now learning:

That I have been living my whole life inside the terms of a contract. That my immunity, my ninety-one victories over poison, my careful clinical survival — all of it was the behavior of a man walking through a house he does not own, dusting furniture that will be repossessed.

And except — I keep adding exceptions, the way the ledger keeps soaking up ink — except that whoever wrote the entry had used the honesty-hook on the last letter. Truth, in my hand.

Which means that when I finally understand this — when the moment comes that sends me to this page to write it — I will not be recording a theft.

I will be recording a sale. My own. Willingly.

The candle guttered. Down in the strongroom, ninety jars of impossible things breathed in the dark, and one of them — I swear this, though no one would believe a taster, whose whole profession is disbelief — one of them was warm to the touch, and flickered, like a lantern remembering light.

I closed Volume Forty-Nine. I did not lock the cage. Some part of me, the part that had already signed, apparently, understood there was no longer anything in this cellar to protect.

Upstairs, dawn was coming up the color of arsenic, and on the chandlery's door, someone had nailed a notice bearing the seal of Lord Veyra, and I was still standing there reading it when I heard hooves on the cobbles — many, unhurried, arriving like a date already written.

* * *

They say the wolf-road wakes when someone walks it who owes a debt to the mountain.

I heard that from my grandmother, who told it to me as a bedtime story about a god who collects what kings cannot. I fell asleep believing it the way children believe things: completely, and then never again.

I was wrong about the second part.
