## 3. The Prophecy of Ash

The Guild of Impossible Things kept a sign on its door that read *No Refunds, No Resurrections, No Exceptions*, and every morning I polished the brass because it was the only honest sentence in the building.

I arrived at half past eight, an hour before the clerks, which was the only hour the guildhall belonged to me. The ledgers lived in the Long Room behind three locks and a superstition, and I liked to sit among them in the quiet with my tea—chamomile, brewed twice, weak enough that even my stomach's enemies couldn't find anything in it to love—and do the day's arithmetic before the day arrived to argue about it. Last night's trades needed recording: a stolen shadow retrieved from a magistrate's wife, three years borrowed against a fisherman's unborn grandson, one minor miracle brokered at a loss because the client had cried. It was good work. Clean work. Work where the numbers behaved.

I want to be clear about what I am, before the record muddies it: I am a bookkeeper. I poison myself professionally, yes, ninety-one immunities deep into a body that should have given out around immunity twelve, but that is a job like any other job. The ledgers are the *life*. Numbers don't lie to you. Numbers don't look at you across a royal table and think, *there goes the man who will save or doom us all.*

Numbers just sit there, being correct, until a scholar knocks on your door with ash under his fingernails.

* * *

He came at ten, which meant he had bribed the doorman, because clients were seen at eleven and not before. He was the sort of scholar the free cities produce in unfortunate abundance: young enough to be certain, old enough to have published, wearing a coat that had been fine two patrons ago. His name was Emeric Voss and his hands shook, though whether from nerves or coffee I couldn't say until I smelled the cup-stains and ruled out nerves entirely.

"I need an authentication," he said. "Provenance work. Century document, disputed origin."

"Then you want Master Halloway in Antiquities."

"Halloway is dead."

"Halloway has been dead for six years. His clients persist. There's a waiting list."

"I paid to move up the list." He set a purse on my desk, and the sound it made was the sound of a very large number of very small coins, which is somehow more persuasive than fewer large ones. "And they told me the man who does final verification is you. That everything in this guild passes across your desk eventually. That if anyone can tell real from false, it's the taster."

Flattery, in my experience, is what people use instead of money when they've run out of money. But he had not run out of money, so I unlocked the inner room and asked to see the document.

He unwrapped it from oilcloth the way other men unwrap children from fires.

It was written on vellum gone the color of old teeth, and it had been burned—not damaged by fire, but *written* in burn marks, as though the words had been branded through from the far side by someone on the other side of history pressing hard. Ash-prophecy, we'd have called it in Antiquities, if Antiquities still had a department. The script was archaic court-hand, pre-Usurpation, and I am ashamed to say my heart did a small unprofessional leap, because I have handled a thousand forged documents and perhaps four true ones, and there is a weight to the true ones. A gravity. Paper knows what it carries.

"Read it," Voss said. "Aloud. Please. I've read it forty times and it keeps doing something to the light."

"That's the burn lacquer," I said. "It refracts."

"Read it anyway."

So I read it.

*When the ninth dish of the ninth night is seasoned for the Taster alone,* the ash said, in letters that hurt slightly to look at, *and he swallows and does not die, then stands the Taster at the hinge of the age: the one soul sold twice, once by blood and once by choice. By his swallow the free cities live. By his refusal they are ash. Let him be found before the wolf-toll falls due, for the road is open only once in a hundred years, and the hundred years close on the first snow.*

I set it down. My hands were steady. I remember being proud of that later, in the way you are proud of strange things in the middle of a collapse—*my hands didn't shake*—as if steadiness were the virtue at stake.

"Well?" Voss said.

"It's a forgery," I said. "They're almost always forgeries."

"You haven't examined it."

"I don't need to examine it to know it's a forgery. Prophecies naming specific dates are forgeries. Prophecies using the phrase *the one soul sold twice* are forgeries, because that's not how prophecy works; prophecy is allergic to clarity, it speaks in riddles or it isn't prophecy, it's a threat." I was talking too much and I knew it, the way you know you're walking too fast. "Who wrote it? Who's paying you to authenticate it? Someone with a claim to something, presumably. Land, title, a throne—"

"Coral Delacroix."

Nobody uses my full name in the guildhall. Nobody *knows* my full name in the guildhall; the ledger lists me as C. Delacroix, Keeper of Accounts, and the clerks call me Coral and the doorman calls me sir and no one has ever said the whole thing aloud in this room, and hearing it now was like hearing a stranger recite the inside of my mouth.

"How did you—" I started.

"Ninth dish. Ninth night," Voss said gently, terribly. "That's tonight. The palace feast calendar is public record, Master Delacroix. Tonight the King's table serves nine courses, the ninth being pheasant—again—and the seating chart places you at the King's left hand, which means you taste first, alone, before any other mouth at the table. Seasoned for the Taster alone." He let that sit between us like a coin on a scale. "You were at that table last night. You'll be there tonight. And last week, according to three separate sources I paid rather a lot for, the palace kitchens changed your pheasant recipe without telling you. New spice blend. Ordered by someone above the Master of Kitchens. Tested, I'm told, on prisoners first."

The room did not tilt. I would like to report that it tilted, that the world reeled dramatically, that I clutched the desk. It did none of those things. It stayed exactly where it was, solid and familiar and lamplit, while I stood in it and became a different shape.

"Coincidence," I said.

"Read the next line."

*I looked down.* There was a next line, smaller, pressed deeper into the vellum, burned harder, as if the writer had leaned all their weight into it:

*The Taster will know himself by this sign: he will learn his own name is not his own, and the knowledge will come to him in a room full of accounts.*

I am, as established, a bookkeeper. I deal in verifiable phenomena. So I will report the verifiable phenomena: the lamp guttered, though there was no draft. The vellum was warm under my hand, though the room was cold. And somewhere in my chest, in the place where I keep the thing I think of as *me*, something shifted its weight and settled closer to listen, the way a dog lifts its head when someone says its name in another room.

"The authentication," Voss said, not unkindly. "Real or forged?"

I performed the examination. I want credit for that. I bent the vellum to the lamp and checked the burn-lacquer for modern resin; there was none. I tested the ink-shadow beneath the scorch for iron-gall tampering; there was none. I measured the letter-forms against my memory of pre-Usurpant court-hand, and they matched, including the little hesitation in the ascenders that forgers never copy because forgers write with confidence. The document was a century old. The document was genuine. The document knew things about me that I had told no one, that I had barely told myself—that I had learned only days ago, in this room, staring at an entry in my own handwriting dated before my birth.

"One soul, sold, court beneath the mountain," I said quietly. "That entry. Did you see it?"

Voss went very still, and scholars should not go still; stillness in a scholar is what froth is at the mouth of a horse. "What entry?"

"The one that proves your prophecy true. The one that means whoever wrote this a hundred years ago knew what my parents did before I was born. Which means they weren't predicting." I looked up at him. "Which means they were *parties to it*. Somebody sold me twice, Master Voss—once by blood, as your ash says, and once by choice. And I would very much like to know who's holding the second receipt."

* * *

He didn't know. Of course he didn't know. He was a hired pen, paid by a patron he'd never met to authenticate a document he'd found in an archive that officially didn't exist, and when I pressed him—the guild is very good at pressing—he gave me a name that turned out to belong to a shipping concern that had dissolved forty years ago, and then he gave me a description of the intermediary that could have fit any well-dressed person in the free cities, and then he gave me, unprompted and white-faced, the only useful thing he had.

"There's a rumor," he said, at the door, hat in his hands like an apology. "Among the archive people. That the prophecy isn't the only copy. That the original was written in duplicate, and the second copy went to the party of the second part." He swallowed. "The mountain court. Whoever holds souls down there holds the original contract, and the prophecy was the announcement. Like a—like a herald's proclamation before a debt comes due."

"And the debt comes due when?"

"The road opens once in a hundred years. The hundred years close on the first snow." He managed something that was trying to be a smile. "First snow is forecast for the week after next, Master Delacroix. The almanacs all agree. It's the only thing they agree on."

I paid him—guild policy, authentication fees, and also I wanted him gone—and I watched him go down the street through the window until he turned at the corner and was nobody, just a coat, just a citizen, just a man with nothing in particular burning under his fingernails.

Then I sat down at my desk, in my room full of accounts, and did what I do.

I opened the ledger.

Not the day-book, not the trade records. The deep ledger, the one with the entry in my own handwriting dated ninety-two years before my birth. *One soul, sold, court beneath the mountain.* I had stared at it for three nights running since I found it, telling myself it was a clerical error, a coincidence of names, a Delacroix from some other branch. Tonight I laid the prophecy beside it on the desk, the ash-burned words and the ink-written words, a century apart, agreeing with each other about me in detail.

*Sold twice. Once by blood, once by choice.*

By blood, my parents. By choice—

My hand moved before I authorized it, turning to the current day-book, to the entry I had made two nights ago and filed under routine: *Ninth-night feast, palace commission, pheasant course, standard tasting fee.* Routine. Except the palace hadn't commissioned a tasting through the guild in eleven years; the royal household employed its own tasters, plural, a whole nervous staff of them, and the fact that they had suddenly requested me specifically, by name, for the ninth dish, on the ninth night, had sat in the ledger looking exactly like ordinary business because I had chosen not to look at it directly.

I had tasted that pheasant last night. I had catalogued the new spice blend with clinical calm—cubeb, long pepper, and underneath them something bitter and mineral I couldn't name—and pronounced it clean, because it was clean, because whatever it was seasoned for, it wasn't poison.

It was seasoned for *me*. For my mouth specifically. As a test. As a key. As a question asked in the only language my body has ever spoken fluently: *will you swallow, Taster, and not die?*

And I had swallowed. Of course I had. Ninety-one times makes a habit.

I closed the ledgers. I locked the three locks. I polished the brass sign on my way out—*No Refunds, No Resurrections, No Exceptions*—because irony is a kind of courage if you do it deliberately, and then I stood on the guild steps in the evening cold and looked north, past the rooftops, past the walls, to where the mountains were putting on the first pale suggestion of the season's white.

A century-old prophecy had spent today describing me to my face, in my own office, in front of my own accounts, and the terror wasn't the armies or the ash or the dying cities. The terror was smaller and worse: that for thirty-one years I had believed myself invisible—a taster, a clerk, a man made of careful sips and balanced columns—and today I learned that I had been *seen* the entire time. Watched. Written down. Entered into someone's ledger before I drew breath, priced and promised and pending.

I know what it is to be a line item. I have been one most of my life, on both sides of the desk. I simply never expected to be the debt.

The wind came down off the peaks carrying a smell like cold iron and old fur, faint as a rumor, wrong for the city, wrong for any street. Somewhere north, on a road that supposedly opened once in a hundred years, something ancient lifted its head.

First snow, the almanacs said. The week after next.

I turned up my collar and went to taste dinner.
