## 4. What the Wolf Remembers

I have been on this road for a very long time, and I want it understood that I did not choose it.

Mortals assume a god of the mountain roads ranges where he pleases — that I am some vast gray shadow loping the passes at will, king of the high white silence. Flattering. Wrong. The road is a leash. It was laid on me before your cities had names, and it runs from the snowline gate to the court beneath the mountain and not one stride further, and in all the centuries since, no one has thought to ask whether the wolf at the roadside is there because he wishes to be.

Travelers don't ask. Travelers see my eyes in the dark between the pines and they either run or kneel, and both responses bore me equally. Running is flattery with worse manners. Kneeling is flattery with better. There is a third response, vanishingly rare, that I confess I live for: the traveler who looks at me and simply *bargains*, as though I were a toll bridge with opinions. Those I remember. I keep them the way misers keep coins, polished and counted and never spent.

But I am getting ahead of myself. There is a smell on my road tonight.

* * *

Scent, for my kind, is not one sense but a library. A man walks past and I read him like a ledger: what he ate, whom he fears, how long since he slept beside someone who loves him, whether he has ever killed anything larger than his own conscience. Most ledgers are dull. Most men are the same page copied out again and again, ink thinning with each generation.

The small dark man who came up the switchbacks tonight was not a page. He was an argument.

He smelled first of poison. This is common enough on my road; half the world seasons its supper out of spite or ambition, and kings are walking apothecaries whether they know it or not. But this man's poison was *organized*. Ninety-one distinct toxins, layered like masonry, each one seated in him so comfortably that his blood had built little houses around them and hung curtains in the windows. His body had negotiated separate peace treaties with every poison that ever tried to kill him. I have eaten gods who were less complicated inside.

And beneath the poison — beneath, the way bedrock lies beneath a city — something was missing.

You cannot smell an absence. You can only smell the shape of it, the way your tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth. Something had been taken out of this man before he was born, taken cleanly, by professionals, and the wound where it had been did not bleed. It *whistled*. A faint draft blew through the middle of him, in through the hole and out through the hole, and what the draft carried was the smell of the court beneath the mountain. Glass roots. Cold arithmetic. Lanterns.

A sold soul. Half-sold, rather — the sale complete, the goods not yet collected, sitting in their warehouse down below like casks of wine waiting on a buyer with a cellar worthy of them. The Broker does not repossess quickly. The Broker is patient the way glaciers are patient, which is to say: the patience is not a virtue, it is merely the temperature.

So. A poisoned man with a missing soul, walking my road alone at dusk in the wrong season for travelers, moving with the particular care of someone who has decided that if death wants him, death can do the walking. I settled into the shadow of the pines above the third switchback and watched him climb, and I will admit — because a god lying to his own narration is pathetic — that I was curious before he could even see me.

Then the wind turned, and I caught the rest of the scent, and curiosity curdled into something older.

Delacroix.

* * *

Let me tell you about debts, since mortals misunderstand them so consistently.

A debt is not paper. Paper is what debt wears when it goes outdoors. A debt is a weight in the world's spine, a small wrongness that everything above it must compensate for, forever, until it is paid or forgiven — and forgiveness, I can tell you from personal experience, is rarer than payment because payment ends the story and stories hate endings almost as much as gods do.

One hundred years ago, give or take the error bars of mortal memory, a family named Delacroix stood at the snowline gate in the depth of a winter that was eating their province alive. Famine, then plague, then the tax collectors, which are worse than either. They had nothing left to sell. Their fields were ash, their children numbered three, and the Broker's price for survival was posted on the gate in letters of frost:

**One soul. Any soul. The account must balance.**

I was there. I am always there; the road runs past the gate and I am the road's dog, chained to it. I watched the father argue with the Broker while the mother held the smallest child against her chest, and I heard the arithmetic done aloud, and I heard the word *unborn* spoken like a coin being tested against the teeth.

They sold the child who had not yet drawn breath. Sold him before he existed, which meant he could not refuse, which is precisely why unborn souls fetch such a fine price — the Broker's premium is always highest where consent is impossible. The family lived. The winter passed. The child was born whole in body, hollow in the middle, and grew into a man who would spend his life tasting poison for other people because a man with a hole in himself will spend his whole life trying to fill it with something, even if the something kills him slowly.

I swore an oath over that bargain. I know how that sounds — a wolf-god, swearing oaths like some parish clerk — but hear the shape of it. I could not stop the sale. The leash forbids me from meddling in contracts sealed at the gate; the road enforces the Broker's commerce as surely as it carries pilgrims. But I could witness. I said, into that frozen air, with the father weeping and the mother silent in the terrible way of mothers who have just paid for bread with flesh: *I witness this debt, and I will witness its undoing, whatever age it takes.*

The Broker smiled at me. You have not been smiled at until you have been smiled at by something that keeps souls in jars and files consent under *inconveniences*. "Then you may wait a century, wolf," it said. "Debts come due on their own schedule."

I have been waiting on schedule ever since.

* * *

The man reached my stretch of road after full dark, which takes a certain kind of nerve or a certain kind of despair, and from his walk I judged he carried both in equal measure, packed together like wet sand. He stopped when the torches of the waystation came into view below — no, he stopped before that. He stopped when he crossed the old boundary stone, the one carved with a wolf's paw worn nearly smooth, and he looked directly at the thicket where I lay.

"Either you're going to eat me," he said, in the flat, reasonable voice of a man cataloguing toxins, "or you're going to talk me to death. I've survived both kinds of company. Do as you like."

Well.

Ninety-one poisons and a spine. I stepped out of the trees.

I will describe him as I saw him then, because description is a kind of hunger and I was hungry: a narrow man, dark-haired, dressed like a clerk pretending to be a fugitive or a fugitive pretending to be a clerk, with ink stains on his fingers and the pallor of someone whose profession is dinner. He did not run. He did not kneel. He looked at me the way an accountant looks at a sum that refuses to resolve — with irritation, and underneath the irritation, a wild and carefully rationed hope.

His nostrils flared. He smelled me too, then. Whatever the guild taught him about reading the world, it worked.

"You're real," he said. "Good. I'd started negotiating with a hallucination outside Vell, and it drove a hard bargain."

"I'm told I drive a harder one." I circled him once, slow, reading the ledger of him from all sides. Up close the whistling hole in his soul was louder — metaphorically, but only just; the wind through it made the hairs along my spine stand like a hackle. "You're Delacroix."

Something moved behind his face. Fear, yes, but also the specific exhaustion of a man who has learned that being known is always the prelude to being used. "Everyone keeps saying my name like it's a verdict."

"It is. It's also a debt. Your parents opened the account before you drew breath, and the Broker holds the collateral, and I—" I stopped. Curiosity, that vulgar appetite, got its teeth in me. Here is the thing about ancient hunger: it is patient, and it is vast, and it is *boring*. I have wanted to eat this world since before the world had edges. That wanting is a room I live in. And yet this small, ruined, well-seasoned man standing in my road smelling of ninety-one survived murders and one enormous absence made the room feel, briefly, drafty. As if a window had opened somewhere in the architecture of my hunger and let in weather.

"—and you?" I finished. "What brings a half-sold taster up the wolf-road in the dead season, alone, with no offering and no escort? Men come to me to beg. Men come to me to pray. You came like a man coming to a meeting he expects to lose."

He considered me for a long moment. Snow ticked down through the pine branches between us. Somewhere below, the waystation dogs began to bark and thought better of it.

"I need passage to the court beneath the mountain," he said. "And they say the road only opens for the wolf."

"They say correctly."

"Then I'm here to bargain." He lifted his chin, and I saw the tremor he was suppressing, and I saw that he would go on suppressing it until it killed him if it came to that. "Name your price."

Ah. There it was. The third response, the rare one, the one I hoard: the traveler who bargains.

I should have named something cruel. Hunger counseled cruelty; hunger always does; hunger has excellent rhetoric and no ethics whatsoever. I could have asked for his years, his name, his taste-memory — that last would have been exquisite, taking from the taster the one thing he owned outright. The words were in my mouth. The leash hummed approval down its length, because the road loves a debtor deeper in debt.

Instead I heard myself say: "Not yet. Walk with me to the waystation, Delacroix, and tell me why a man who tastes poison for a living wants to walk into the one market that will never undervalue him again. Answer well, and I'll name a price tomorrow. Answer badly—"

"You'll eat me?"

"No. Worse. I'll answer honestly any question you ask me, all the way down the mountain. Truth is a heavier toll than most men can pay."

He laughed — one syllable, startled out of him, rusty as a hinge. And there in the falling snow, the small poisoned man with the whistling hole where his soul should be fell into step beside a god on a chain, and we walked down toward the torchlight together like the oldest joke in the world setting out to finally explain itself.

Behind us, far under the mountain, in a cathedral of glass roots, I felt a lantern flicker in its hanging place.

It had caught his scent too.

And somewhere below the snowline, riding the night wind off the plains, came a smell I had hoped never to read again in my long library of smells: iron, ink, and many marching feet. The usurper's herald had entered the foothills, and he was carrying a list of names.

Mine was on it. So, I would have wagered anything in my hoard, was the small man's.
