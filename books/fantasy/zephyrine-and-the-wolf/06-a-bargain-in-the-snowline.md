## 6. A Bargain in the Snowline

I left the city through the tanner's gate, which is the only exit in Vhalenne that no one guards, because even the usurper's men have standards. The smell alone would turn back an army of the squeamish. I had spent my whole life swallowing things that would kill lesser men, so let it be recorded that the tanneries made me gag. Fear does that. Fear strips away every immunity you've built and leaves you with the body you started with—the soft, ordinary animal that wants to run.

I ran.

Behind me, the guildhall was still burning its ledgers in the hearth, page by page, because Master Orrec had decided that if he could not protect the trades, he would at least make them unprovable. Behind me, a herald's voice was still repeating my name into the air like a man dropping stones down a well, waiting for a splash that never came. *Coral Delacroix. The Taster. The one the prophecy names.* Behind me, half the free cities were already deciding whether to crown me or open my throat, and the other half hadn't heard yet, which gave me perhaps two days.

Ahead of me: the mountain road, the snowline, and the wolf.

They say Zephyrine walks the old road above the vineyards where the fog never fully lifts, bound to it the way a river is bound to its bed—unable to leave, unable to refuse a traveler who asks properly. They say he takes tolls. They say a hundred other things too, most of them invented by people who needed a monster more than they needed the truth. I had tasted for queens long enough to know that monsters are usually just creditors with better posture.

Still, my hands shook on the switchbacks, and not from the climb.

* * *

The fog found me at midday, pouring over the ridge like something spilled from a very large cup. I had dressed poorly for this—I own three coats and had chosen the one without pockets, because when you flee a city at dawn you do not make good decisions—and by the time I reached the shrine-stone at the road's first bend, my breath was coming out in white flags of surrender.

The stone was older than the city behind me. Older, probably, than the concept of cities. Someone had carved a question into it centuries ago and worn the letters smooth with touching:

*WHAT WILL YOU PAY?*

I put my palm flat against it. It was warm. That was the worst part, somehow. Everything on this road was faintly, horribly alive.

"I know how this works," I said to the fog. "I keep books for a living. I've written ten thousand contracts. So let's skip the part where you pretend to be a riddle."

The fog said nothing, because fog is bad at dialogue.

But the road changed. That's the only way I can describe it. One moment I was walking a goat-path with loose shale underfoot, and the next the stones had settled themselves into something deliberate—a paved way, wide enough for two, running straight up through the white. And standing on it, far off but closing, was a shape that my eyes kept trying to file as *large dog* and my hindbrain kept correcting to *run, you idiot.*

Zephyrine came up the road toward me at an amble, unhurried as weather. He was wolf-shaped the way a cathedral is house-shaped—technically accurate, deeply misleading. His coat was the grey of ash before the wind takes it, and his eyes were amber, and amber, and amber again; wherever I looked in them there was another layer, like peering down a well that had opinions.

"You," he said, "smell like ninety-one poisons and a ledger."

"Eighty-seven poisons," I said. "Four antidotes. The distinction matters to me professionally."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth that on a smaller face would have been a smile. "Coral Delacroix. Son of Maren and Aldous Delacroix, who stood on this very road twenty-six years ago and did not ask properly."

"They told me nothing," I said. "That's rather the theme of my week."

"No." The wolf circled me once, slow, nostrils working. "They asked me nothing. Different failure entirely. They went around, Coral Delacroix. There are always ways around a god, if you don't mind the interest. They took the mountain path below mine and paid the Broker's price instead, and what they bought with your—" He stopped. Tilted his enormous head. "You don't know."

"I know my soul hangs in a glass root under the mountain," I said. "I read my own handwriting in a ledger dated before I was born. I'm told it's still warm. I'm told it's still dreaming." My voice did something humiliating on the last word, and I pretended it hadn't. "I want it back."

"The armies come," Zephyrine said. Not a question. He could probably smell them, forty miles off, all that oiled steel and certainty. "And the cities want to crown you or kill you, and the herald has unmade a guildmaster, and you have walked out of Vhalenne with no coat pockets, no allies, and a prophecy on your heels. And you want your soul."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I had prepared answers. I'd been drafting them since the guildhall, in the way I draft everything—three versions, ranked by plausibility. *Because a man should own himself. Because the prophecy can't use me if I'm whole. Because the usurper's leverage dies with my debt.*

What came out was none of them.

"Because I taste everything," I said, "and I have never once tasted anything that was mine."

The fog moved. The wolf watched me with all those layered ambers, and for a moment I had the distinct, vertiginous sense of being *read*—catalogued the way I catalogue toxins, entry by entry, margin note by margin note.

"Hm," said Zephyrine, in the tone of a man finding an unexpected entry in a ledger. "You ask properly after all. Very well. Passage to the court beneath the mountain. My road runs to its gates; the Broker must open for any soul who comes claiming its own. That much I can give, because I am bound to give it." He stepped closer, and the heat of him reached me like a banked oven. "But you said *anything,* small poisoned man. Before we go further, understand what I will ask. Understand it completely. I do not sell surprises."

"Name it."

"Ninety-one immunities," said the wolf.

The words landed strangely, the way a familiar word lands when someone says it in a language you almost speak.

"When your soul is reclaimed—when it is back inside you and the seal is broken—you will surrender every immunity your body has built. All of them. At once. Every poison you ever swallowed to keep your queens safe, every venom your blood learned to forgive, every night you cheated death by a swallow's measure—all of it comes due in a single hour. Your body will relive each one. You will convulse through arsenic and nightlock and the pheasant from Tuesday, and there is a real chance, Coral Delacroix, that you simply die. That is the arithmetic. Ninety-one survivals, paid for all together, now."

The wind came down the mountain and I felt it on skin that suddenly seemed very thin, very temporary, like paper over a lamp.

"That's not passage," I said. "That's a bill."

"That's a bargain. You may decline. You may walk back down and be crowned by fools or killed by cleverer ones, and die owning yourself anyway, eventually, in some ordinary way." He sat back on his haunches, patient as geology. "Or you may ride my road to the glass roots, take your dreaming soul out of its lantern, and pay me what survival has cost you. I confess I want it badly. No one has ever offered me ninety-one deaths in one body. I am very old, and I am bored, and you are the most interesting thing to walk this road since the road was young."

"And if I die paying?"

"Then I will have eaten a very interesting smell and lost a very interesting conversation, and I will grieve briefly, in my way."

* * *

We stood there a while—or I stood, and the wolf regarded me with the terrible patience of something that measures time in centuries. Below us the fog pooled in the valley like milk in a bowl, and somewhere down in it was Vhalenne, and Orrec's name that no one could say anymore, and a burning hearth full of impossible things.

Here is what nobody tells you about cowardice and courage: they're made of the same material. It's just arithmetic. Every choice I had ever made was built on the same foundation—*survive now, count the cost later.* Taste the dish. Swallow the poison. Ledger it. Move on. Ninety-one times I had survived by making death wait its turn in line.

Zephyrine was asking me to call in the whole queue at once.

"You knew my parents," I said.

"I witnessed their bargain. I witness all bargains on my road. It is why I cannot take what I want by force, and why I cannot lie about a price." Something moved behind all that amber—not quite regret; gods wear regret the way I wear the wrong coat, ill-fitting and borrowed. "They loved you, Coral Delacroix. I want that stated for the record, because you will learn it under the mountain, and it will hurt less if you have begun to believe it here, in the cold, where no one can comfort you."

"That's not comforting."

"I am a wolf, not a pillow."

Despite everything—the fear, the shaking hands, the prophecy with my name in its mouth—I laughed. A short, ugly, startled laugh, the kind that escapes before the taster in you can test it for poison. Zephyrine looked pleased, in the way avalanches look pleased.

"Terms," I said, because if I stopped negotiating I would start weeping, and I had done my weeping for the year in a guildhall ledger, privately, in pencil. "Payment renders when the soul is reclaimed. Not before. If the Broker refuses me—if the sale can't be voided—your claim lapses."

"Agreed."

"If I die during payment, my body goes unreclaimed by anyone. No resurrection bargains, no soul-trading using my corpse as collateral. I die *mine.*"

The wolf was quiet a long moment. "You would spend your last coin on that?"

"A man sold before birth should get to keep at least his ending."

"*Mine,*" Zephyrine repeated softly, tasting the word the way I taste wine. "Agreed. And you will walk beside me the whole road, and answer my questions honestly, because I have wondered about your kind for a hundred years and this is my fee for curiosity."

"Your fee for curiosity is *conversation?*"

"My fees vary. Most travelers prefer it to what I usually take." He rose, and rising, he was suddenly the size of a horse, then briefly the size of something larger than horses have ever managed, and then merely a wolf again, as if size were a coat he'd tried on and hung back up. "Well, Taster? The armies march. The cities sharpen. The Broker's lanterns burn whether you come or not. What will you pay?"

I looked down at my hands. Poisoner's hands. Taster's hands. Hands that had held steady through eighty-seven poisons because they had no idea what fear was, and were learning now.

Every immunity was a night I'd survived. Every night was a debt. I had known that since I was nineteen years old and first wrote the words *still alive, day 1* in the margins of a Guild ledger. I had simply assumed I'd never see the invoice.

"Yes," I said. "All ninety-one. When it's reclaimed, it's yours. Witnessed."

"Witnessed," said Zephyrine, and the word went into the mountain like a nail into wood, and somewhere beneath our feet I felt the road itself sign its name.

He turned up the slope, and the fog parted ahead of him with the courtesy of an old servant.

"Come, then," the wolf said over his shoulder. "It is three days to the snowline proper, and the road eats names as it goes. You'll want to decide which memories you can afford to lose, and quickly—though I warn you, it rarely lets you choose well."

I looked back once, down through the thinning fog, at the smudge of smoke that was the only proof Vhalenne had ever existed. Then I climbed after the god, carrying nothing, protected by nothing, owed everything—and for the first time in twenty-six years, every step was entirely my own.

Which lasted until the road took my mother's face.
