## 7. The Road That Eats Names

*Coral*

The wolf-road began where the mapmakers gave up.

I mean that literally. There was a survey stone at the tree line, older than the guild, and someone had chiseled *HERE THE COUNTRY LIES TO YOU* across its face, which I found charming in the way I find most honest things charming: briefly, and from a safe distance. Beyond the stone the pines went black and close, and the path beneath them was not dirt but something like packed shadow, faintly warm, faintly breathing. A road with a pulse. Of course. Everything in my life lately had a pulse it wasn't entitled to.

Zephyrine walked ahead of me, which is to say a mountain sometimes arranged itself into a wolf and walked ahead of me. He was larger than sense permitted — shoulder higher than my crown, if I'd had one — and his fur was the grey of old snow that has seen things. When he moved, the trees leaned to watch. When he stopped, the road itself seemed to sit up and pay attention, like a dog hearing its name.

"Rules," he said, without turning. His voice arrived the way thunder does, from slightly inside your own ribs. "The road takes tolls. Not coin. It prefers softer currency. It will ask you for memories, and you will pay, because refusing is possible only if you don't mind being eaten."

"How much will it take?"

"As much as you carry." He glanced back at me then, one amber eye the size of a dinner plate, and there was something in it I didn't like — not hunger exactly. Inventory. "You are carrying a great deal, taster."

"I'm carrying almost nothing. One bag."

"Worse for you, then." And he walked on.

I want to say I was brave about it. I have spent ninety-one nights swallowing death on purpose; I have a professional relationship with dread; we nod to each other at functions. But there is a difference between poison and forgetting, and the difference is this: poison wants your body and forgets you immediately after. The road wanted the parts of me I'd built out of remembering. Ninety-one immunities were, when you got down to it, ninety-one nights I refused to let go of. My whole self was an archive of things survived.

We walked. The light went strange — not dark, but *subtracted*, as though the forest had been photographed and the negatives kept. After what might have been an hour or a season, the road began to hum.

The first toll took a stranger than I expected. I was thinking about the guildhall's east stair, the way the third step creaked, and then I was thinking about a stair, just a stair, any stair, and the creak was gone. Then the memory of whose stair it had been thinned. Then it went entirely, and I stood on the breathing road mourning something I could no longer name, which is grief's cruelest party trick.

"It starts small," Zephyrine said. He had stopped and sat, patient as geology. "It always starts small. It wants you relaxed."

"Does it take you too?"

Something moved under his fur, a shrug the size of a landslide. "The road and I have an arrangement. It doesn't bill the landlord."

*Landlord.* So he belonged to the road, or the road to him, and neither arrangement sounded like freedom. I filed that away. Filing things away is most of what I do.

By the second day — I decided it was days by fiat, because the sky had stopped participating — the tolls came faster, and I learned their rhythm. The road never asked directly. It simply reached into my walking and pulled, the way you'd pluck a thread, and whatever memory was attached would come loose with a small internal sound, like a cork leaving a bottle. I lost the smell of the guildhall's ink. I lost my father's laugh, or I assumed I had, because I found myself trying to recall it and grasping a shape where a sound should be. I lost the taste of the first poison I ever swallowed, which felt like sacrilege — that taste had been my ordination.

"Fight them," Zephyrine advised, watching me grope at the hole where my father's laugh lived. "Not to keep them. To choose which ones go. The road respects preference. Take the useless ones and offer them up front, like paying with small coins."

So I did. I fed it my memory of every boring banquet, every interminable speech by the harbormaster, the entire plot of a play I'd hated. The road took them with something like disappointment. Somewhere in the dark pines a bird sang a note I almost recognized and couldn't place, and I understood that it was singing back something of mine, idly, the way a cat plays with what it has caught.

On the third night we made camp in a hollow where the shadows pooled like spilled wine. I couldn't sleep. I lay with my bag under my head — my whole worldly estate now: one knife, one ledger-page folded eight times, one dried pear — and I recited to myself, silently, the inventory of Coral Delacroix. Name. Trade. Mother.

And there it stopped. *Mother.*

*Coral*

I knew I had a mother the way you know a country exists that you've never visited. The fact was intact. Everything around the fact had been eaten. Her face — gone. Her voice — gone. Whether she was tall, whether her hands were soft, whether she called me *little ledger* the way Father claimed, or whether that was his joke alone — all of it, tolls, taken somewhere between the third stair and the third night, quietly, while I wasn't guarding the door.

I sat up in the dark and shook. Not weeping, exactly. Weeping requires knowing what you've lost. This was the body doing the grieving the soul couldn't perform, because the soul had no address to send the letter to.

"She died when I was nine," I said aloud, testing. It held. Facts held. "She died when I was nine, and she—" Nothing. A beautiful blank, smooth as river stone.

Zephyrine's eye opened in the darkness like a lantern being lit in a window. He watched me for a long time.

"You're crying over a face you can't picture," he said. Not mockery. Wonder, maybe. Or recognition.

"That's the trick of it, isn't it. The road leaves you the love and takes the beloved. Efficient. Cruel in the accounting sense — no waste."

He rose, and the whole hollow tilted toward him, shadows running uphill to be near him. He came close — closer than comfort, closer than sense — and lowered his enormous head until his breath moved my hair like weather.

"Give me your hand, taster."

Everything in me said no. I gave him my hand.

His tongue, when it touched my palm, was warm and rough as a hearthstone, and into that touch he pressed — I have no better word — a memory. Not mine. I knew it instantly as not-mine, the way you know a coat isn't yours even when it fits: the seams were wrong. It was winter. It was snow coming down fat and slow through pine branches, and a she-wolf lying on her side in a den that smelled of earth and milk, and small warm bodies climbing over her ribs, and the ache of a love so large it frightened her — frightened *him*, it was his, it was Zephyrine's, some far century of his, given to me raw and still bleeding.

"Why," I said, when I could speak. My hand was shaking. The snow-memory settled behind my eyes like weather moving in. "Why would you give me that? The road takes from travelers. You're feeding it."

"The road can't take what I give freely." He drew back, and his face did something complicated, an expression arriving across geological features like dawn crossing a plain. "Your mother's face is gone. I can't return it. I checked. I asked. The road has already digested it." He said it flatly, but flatness, in a god, is its own kind of hoarseness. "But you shouldn't lie in the dark with nothing. I know what that is. I have been lying in the dark with nothing for longer than your language has existed."

"You said you don't pay tolls."

"I don't." The amber eye blinked, slow. "That was a gift. Unbillable. Don't put it in a ledger, Delacroix. Some things die if you write them down."

Too late. I was already composing the entry. *One memory, she-wolf in snow, received gratis, purpose unknown.* I caught his eye and, for the first time in our acquaintance, felt ashamed of the habit.

"Thank you," I said instead.

"Don't thank me either." He turned away, curled with his back to me, an avalanche deciding to rest. "Sleep. Tomorrow the road gets worse."

*Coral*

It got worse.

By the fifth day I had surrendered, strategically, my memories of every royal banquet I'd ever tasted through — ninety-one evenings of terror and pheasant, gone in an afternoon, and good riddance, mostly, except each one had been a night I stayed alive for someone, and now those someones floated unanchored in my past, saved by a man who couldn't remember saving them. I kept the pear in my bag. I kept the folded page. I kept, by brute repetition, chanting it under my breath like a rosary: *my name is Coral Delacroix, I am a taster and a ledger-keeper, I sold nothing, I was sold.*

Zephyrine noticed the chant on the sixth day. His ear rotated toward me like a periscope.

"You were sold," he repeated. "Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"There's always a rest of it." He stopped, and I stopped, and the road breathed beneath us both. "A century ago I stood witness on this same road while two frightened people from your blood walked toward the mountain carrying the worst bargain of their lives. I have been waiting since for the invoice to come due. You are the invoice, taster. So say the rest."

I looked at him — really looked, up the whole grey cliff of him — and heard myself say: "My parents sold my soul to buy their lives. And they never told me. And I hate them. And I miss my mother, whose face I cannot picture, and the hating and the missing live in the same chest and I don't know how to house both."

The words came out level, ledger-neat, and then the last clause broke open and I was crying again, properly this time, ugly and human, standing on a road that ate names while a god looked down at me.

Zephyrine said nothing for a long while. The pines leaned in. Then:

"My mother's face I remember," he said quietly. "That's what I gave you a piece of. She was the first thing the road ever ate, in the first spring of the world, and I have carried her intact ever since, out of spite." A pause. "Spite keeps well, Delacroix. Better than grief. But it cooks up the same."

*Zephyrine*

Here is what the small poisoned man did not understand yet, and what I, watching him sleep against my flank that night because the cold had gotten into him and he was too proud to admit it, was beginning to fear I would have to explain eventually.

Debts bind both ends.

I had sworn witness to the Delacroix bargain — sworn it on the road itself, which makes a vow into a leash. Witness means seeing it through. Seeing it through means I could not tear the soul from the Broker's rafters by force, however much the wolf in me wanted to run to the mountain that night and eat the glass roots and the Broker and half the realm besides. A witnessed contract must be *undone*, not broken. Undone willingly, by willing hands, in the proper order. The free cities think gods are forces of nature. Forces of nature do not sign anything.

But there was a second problem, and it had a heartbeat and dry wit and currently drooled slightly on my fur.

When the soul came home to him — and it would come home, I would see to it, I had waited a century, I could wait out armies — my price would fall on him. All ninety-one immunities, surrendered in one breath. I had named that toll because it was the only currency equal to passage on my road, and I am bound to fair dealing the way rivers are bound to downhill. I had not anticipated lying awake — gods do not sleep, we merely endure the hours — feeling the small weight of him rise and fall against my ribs, and thinking: *he will scream, and it will be my teeth that cause it.*

Curiosity, I told myself. Merely curiosity. I had watched a hundred thousand mortals cross my road and lose their mothers' faces and walk on diminished, and never once cared.

But none of them had ever offered the road their boring banquets first, like coins, like a merchant counting out change. None of them had ever chanted their own name against the dark as though it were a candle that might blow out. None of them had been sold before birth and still, impossibly, insisted on choosing.

"Coral," I said, very low, to the sleeping man or perhaps to the road. "What am I going to do with you."

The road, which hears everything, hummed. Far behind us, back toward the burning maps of the free cities, something else was moving — hoofbeats, many, and among them a rider carrying a courier's satchel and a guild seal and orders she had not yet decided whether to obey.

Ahead, beneath the mountain, a lantern flickered in a cathedral of glass, and something hanging there dreamed of snow.

The road smelled all three futures at once, and licked its lips, and billed no one. Yet.
