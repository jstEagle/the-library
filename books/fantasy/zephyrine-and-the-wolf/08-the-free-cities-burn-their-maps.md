## 8. The Free Cities Burn Their Maps

I was three days out of the city when the world decided to lose its mind, and I want it on the record that I noticed before most people. Courteers always do. We're the nervous system of civilization — we feel the fever before the body knows it's sick.

My name is Sable. I carry things for the Guild of Impossible Things: letters, ledgers, stolen shadows in wax-sealed boxes, apologies so old they've fossilized into threats. I am very fast on a horse and faster with a lie, and until this week I had considered both skills reasonably safe investments.

Then the prophecy got out.

* * *

It happened the way bad news always happens: a scholar-client couldn't keep his mouth shut. Master Ellery-Three-Quills — that was his guild-name; his mother had named him something worse — paid us to authenticate the Prophecy of Ash, and then he went and authenticated it in every tavern between the guildhall and the harbor, because scholars are people who mistake knowing a thing for owning it.

By the time I saddled up for my circuit run to Vell and Corasmont and the salt-towns beyond, the text was already being copied by hand in cellars, printed on presses nobody would admit to owning, and shouted in the market square by a milkmaid who had gotten perhaps one line in five correct and improved the rest.

"The Taster," she was bellowing as I rode past, "shall come crowned in *venom*, sisters, wearing the *ninety-one deaths like a coat*—"

That part was true, actually, which somehow made it worse.

I stopped to listen, because I'm a professional. Crowds tell you more than documents do. This crowd had the specific texture of a crowd that has recently learned the future has a name, and that the name belongs to somebody, and that therefore the future can be *caught*.

"He's here already," a fishwife said, low and certain. "He's been under our noses for years. That's how they work, isn't it? Hidden."

"Whose noses?" said her neighbor. "Speak for your own nose."

"Vell wants him," said a man with the ink-stained cuffs of a scrivener. "There's talk of a procession. A coronation. He saves the free cities, the prophecy says so plain as taxes."

"Corasmont says different." The fishwife spat, expertly, into a drain. "Corasmont says if he's the ruin, better he dies small than lives large. There's a purse."

"How large a purse?"

"Large enough."

And there it was. I stood in the fish-smell with my saddlebags full of perfectly ordinary impossible correspondence and felt the ground tilt under the whole world. Because I knew who the Taster was. Of course I did. Everyone at the guildhall did, or guessed, which in a guild is the same thing with better manners. Coral Delacroix, who tasted poison every night so the Queen could eat, who kept our ledgers in handwriting like rain on glass, who once apologized to me for three consecutive minutes because he'd made me wait while he finished an entry.

They were going to crown him. Or they were going to kill him. And half the free cities hadn't even decided which, which meant both purses were open at once, and couriers are the ones who carry purses.

I got on my horse and rode, and behind me the milkmaid improved her second verse.

* * *

Vell was burning its maps when I arrived.

Not metaphorically. Someone — I never learned who, though I have suspicions involving a wine merchant and a grudge against cartographers generally — had piled the contents of the chart-house in the marketplace and set them alight, on the theory that if the prophecy named roads and rivers, then unmaking the roads and rivers might unmake what was coming down them. It is very human logic. It is also completely insane, but I confess I watched the border with Corasmont curl and blacken at the corners and felt, briefly, a wild sympathy. If I could burn the page where my own debts were written, I might do it too.

The city gate guards didn't ask for papers. They asked if I'd seen the Taster.

"No," I said, which was true, and "he's not the sort you'd notice," which was not, and they waved me through with the strange deflated look of people who had been promised a miracle and issued only weather.

Here is what nobody understands about prophecies, and what I understood standing in that gatehouse shadow: a prophecy doesn't describe the future. It describes a *hole* in the future, shaped like whatever it names. And holes get filled. Vell wanted to fill theirs with a savior. Corasmont wanted to fill theirs with a corpse. Neither of them particularly cared about the shape of the actual man, any more than you care about the personality of a cork.

I delivered my letters — two marriage settlements disguised as trade agreements, one genuine trade agreement disguised as an insult, and a box of somebody's regretted years bound for a buyer in the Salt Quarter — and I listened, because listening is the job.

At the Gilded Anchor, a woman in traveling grey held court over a map spread across three tables. She had the sun-dark hands of someone who'd ridden far and the flat eyes of someone paid to ride farther.

"The Taster serves at the Queen's table in the capital," she said. "Which means, right now, he sits inside the usurper's reach. Every day we wait is a day Lord Veyra learns what he's keeping in his pantry." She tapped the map. "We go in, we take him out, we bring him to the Conclave. Crowned before winter."

"And if he won't come?" someone asked.

The woman smiled the way knives smile. "Then we explain the alternative."

At the Weeping Sister, two streets over, a different crowd had gathered around a different speaker, a thin zealot with burn scars on his forearms and the shining certainty of a man who has found one idea and moved into it permanently.

"The prophecy does not say he *chooses* to save us," the zealot said. "Read it again. Read it as ash reads fire. The Taster is the hinge, and hinges don't choose which way the door swings — the hand on the door chooses. If we love the free cities, we take the choice away from him. Mercy, brothers. Mercy is a knife with a clean handle."

They nodded. All of them nodded. Nobody asked what the knife looked like from the other end.

I bought a cup of wine I didn't drink — occupational habit; I've seen too many ledgers — and thought about Coral Delacroix, who was at that moment either poisoning himself politely in some marble dining hall or hunched over our books by candlelight, entirely unaware that half a continent had begun arguing about whether to worship or bury him.

Or maybe not unaware. Coral notices things. It's his whole cursed profession. Which meant somewhere behind those tired eyes, he probably already knew, and was already doing the arithmetic of it, and had probably concluded — because he was Coral — that whatever came, it would be *his fault*, and he should apologize for it in advance.

I drank the wine anyway. It seemed rude not to.

* * *

Corasmont I reached four days later, and Corasmont had stopped pretending.

There were wanted bills on the customs house, fresh paste, rain-blurred but legible. Not a description — whoever wrote them had never met him, so they'd written the *prophecy* instead, which amounts to the same thing in the public mind:

*THE TASTER. COME PEACEABLY OR OTHERWISE. REWARD NEGOTIABLE. HE MAY NOT KNOW HIS OWN NATURE. DO NOT EAT OR DRINK IN HIS PRESENCE.*

That last line nearly made me laugh in the street, which would have been fatal, probably. Don't eat or drink in his presence. Gentlemen, I promise you, of all the dangers Coral Delacroix poses to your supper, none of them involve the supper.

But under the bills, the city itself had gone strange. Corasmont is a merchant town; it runs on confidence the way a mill runs on water, and the prophecy had fouled the current. Prices swung wildly. Insurance syndicates refused to underwrite anything dated past midwinter. Two rival trading houses collapsed overnight when their factor-clerks simply walked out, saying they'd rather see the end of the world outdoors. A street preacher stood on a barrel outside the exchange, weeping real tears, telling anyone who'd listen that the Taster was already among them, disguised, tasting everything.

Which, again — technically accurate. That's the horror of it. You can't even refute the madmen properly anymore.

In the guild's Corasmont chapter-house — a rented room above a dyer's shop that smelled of permanent violet — the local factor, a nervous woman named Ondile, took my sealed letters with shaking hands.

"You've come from the capital," she said. "You've *seen* him. The Taster."

"I've seen the ledger-keeper, yes."

"That's what I said."

"It isn't." I set down my cup. "Ondile. Listen to me, because I'm going to say something I'll probably be unmade for later. Whatever the prophecy says, whatever the purses say, whatever the zealots and the kingmakers and the map-burners say — Coral Delacroix is a tired man with good handwriting who has spent his whole life swallowing poison so other people could eat dinner. That's the whole of him. That's all you'd be crowning, or killing."

She looked at me with something close to pity. "Sable. Don't you understand? That's *worse*. A savior with faults might fail us. But a good man—" She pressed her hand flat against the wanted bill, against the words *HE MAY NOT KNOW HIS OWN NATURE.* "A good man can be *aimed*."

I left before I said something actionable, which is a discipline.

* * *

On the road north out of Corasmont, I passed the first of the hunting parties going the other way. Six riders, armed like a small war, flying no colors — which tells you everything, because men who fly no colors have been paid by everyone. They reined up when they saw my courier's badge.

"Guild of Impossible Things," their leader said. It wasn't a greeting. "You'd know him, then."

"I know a lot of people."

"The ledger-keeper. Delacroix. Grey man, quiet, smells faintly of bitterleaf." Bitterleaf — that was accurate, and it frightened me more than the swords. Somebody who'd stood near Coral. Somebody close. "Which way to the capital?"

"South," I said. "But you'll not get through. Veyra's sealed the passes since the herald business." The herald business. I said it lightly and my stomach dropped anyway, because the herald business was the thing none of the cities fully believed yet: that Lord Veyra's envoy had walked into our guildhall demanding the ledgers, and Guildmaster Ferrowen had said no — Ferrowen, magnificent, stubborn, doomed Ferrowen — and the herald had *unmade* him. Not killed. Unmade. Walked out and left behind a room of people straining against a hole in their memories, trying to remember who had signed their contracts, who had hired them, whose chair sat empty by the fire and why it was warm.

Grief is a luxury courier's can't afford on the road. So I filed it. I've been filing it for weeks. Some nights the drawer rattles.

The hunting-party leader chewed on the pass situation, then shrugged. "There's more ways south than maps show. Why d'you think they're burning them?" And they rode on, six armed men and one purpose, toward a tired man who smelled of bitterleaf.

I sat on my horse in the middle of the road for a long moment.

Here is what I want written down somewhere, in a ledger that lasts: they were all so *certain*. Vell certain of a crown. Corasmont certain of a grave. The zealots certain of mercy-with-a-knife-handle. The hunters certain of their purse. And underneath every certainty ran the same filthy current — relief. The sweet, shameful relief of people who have been handed a name for the thing coming to eat them. Because if the future has a name, it has a body, and bodies can be caught, crowned, killed. Anything is better than weather. Anything is better than ash falling out of a clear sky.

Nobody — not one soul in five cities — had asked the man himself a single question. What he wanted. Whether the prophecy was even about him. Whether he'd *agreed* to be the hinge.

I turned my horse around.

Not south. Not yet. First west, fast, to the relay at Harrow Fen, where the surviving guild — what was left of us, the headless, grieving, stubborn remnant — had agreed to regroup. Because there were two kinds of people riding hard that week: the ones hunting Coral Delacroix, and the ones who'd known him when his only miracle was showing up on time.

And I had just realized something the cities hadn't. Something I'd heard in fragments, in taverns, in the mouths of travelers off the mountain road: that a wolf-god had been seen on the old wolf-road, walking beside a small grey man. That the Delacroix debt was moving.

If I rode well, I could reach him before the crowns and knives did. Or after — in which case I'd need to decide, finally, what my badge actually meant: the guild's orders, which said find him and mark him, or the truth, which said find him and warn him.

I spurred the horse. Behind me, Vell's smoke still rose where the maps had burned. Ahead, the mountains waited with their buried court and their hanging lantern-souls, and somewhere in between, a tired man was learning that the whole world had decided, without asking him, exactly what he was for.

"Hold on, Coral," I said to the road, to the wind, to nobody. "Your courier's coming. Try not to taste anything."

The horse ran. The cities burned their maps behind me. And far to the north, under a sky the color of unspoken things, the snowline began.
