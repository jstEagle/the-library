## 9. Court Beneath the Mountain

The wolf-road let us go the way a fever breaks—suddenly, and with the sense that something had been wrung out of me in the night. I woke at the mouth of a cave with snow in my collar, Zephyrine's flank warm against my back like a furnace that had opinions, and no memory of my mother's face.

I had noticed the absence for three days without understanding it. It sat in my head like a book pulled from a shelf: you could see the dust-shadow where it had been. I knew her name. I knew she had died when I was nine, and that her hands had smelled of rosemary, and that she sang badly and often. But her face—the thing you'd swear you could never lose—was gone. The road had taken it as toll, and Zephyrine had paid it without asking me, because gods do not ask.

"You could have warned me," I said.

"I could have," he agreed, "and then it would have been your choice, and choices are heavier than memories. You have enough weight." He shook the snow from his coat with the violence of a man shaking off an accusation. "Besides. You'll want your hands empty where we're going."

"Which is where, exactly?"

He looked at me the way a banker looks at a client who has just asked what interest is. Then he put his nose to the stone and *pushed*, and the mountain opened like a throat.

I want to say I descended bravely. What I actually did was catalog, because cataloging is what I do instead of feeling. The passage was basalt shot through with seams of something translucent—not quartz, not ice, not glass, though glass was the closest word and the one I'd keep. The seams brightened as we went down, which is against every law of light I know, and by the time the passage widened into anything describable, I had counted four hundred and six of them and given up on laws entirely.

The court beneath the mountain is a cathedral built by roots.

That's the only sentence that survives translation out of the experience. Roots of glass, thick as temple pillars, growing down through the mountain's bones and branching into naves and aisles and side chapels of impossible geometry, every surface catching the light of what hung among them.

Souls. Sold souls, hanging like lanterns.

They were not lights exactly. They were more like—do you know the moment at a dinner party when someone opens a door to a room where a hundred candles have been burning all day, and the warmth and the smell arrive together, and you understand that this room has been *lived in*? Each soul was that moment, made visible and hung on a hook. Some burned steady. Some guttered. One near the entrance flickered in a rhythm I recognized with a lurch as laughter—a laugh stretched over centuries, still going, unable to stop.

"How many?" I asked. My voice came out smaller than I'd planned.

"More than the free cities have citizens," said Zephyrine. "Fewer than the Broker would like. He counts ambition as inventory; there is never enough."

"And mine?"

The wolf turned his great head and regarded me with an expression I had learned, over our weeks of travel, to distrust most: gentleness.

"Follow the warmth," he said. "You'll know it. A soul knows its address even after a century of tenancy elsewhere."

* * *

I should describe the Broker's court properly, because if I die—and the odds, I'm told, are now merely mortal odds—I want someone to know what it looked like, and everyone who saw it with me is either a wolf or a debt.

Imagine a cathedral where the architect hated straight lines and loved chandeliers. Imagine every chandelier lit with a person. The glass roots arched overhead and split into finer and finer branches until they were filigree, lacework, spiderweb, and from each twig hung its lantern-soul, and the light they cast was not yellow or white but the color of whatever the soul had loved most. I passed one that glowed the green of a specific garden. One the color of a letter never sent. One that was, unmistakably, the brown-gold of bread baking, and I stopped under it longer than I should have, breathing, homesick for a kitchen that wasn't mine.

Because that was the obscenity of the place. Not the horror—horror I could have managed; horror has rules, horror announces itself. The obscenity was the comfort. The whole cathedral was warm. It smelled faintly of every hearth that had ever mattered to anyone. It felt like being expected. And every lantern in it was a theft.

"They're not suffering," said a voice behind me, dry as ledger-paper. "Before you ask. They're not aware, either, mostly. A soul hung long enough stops dreaming forward and dreams *in*. Loops. Comforts. The best day it ever had, replayed." A pause. "Yours hasn't gotten there yet."

I turned. The Soul-Broker was small. That was my first absurd thought—that I had imagined a giant, a spider-thing, some colossus of contract law, and instead here was a narrow man in a grey coat, ageless the way furniture is ageless, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on a face like a folded receipt. He looked like me. That was my second thought, and I disliked it intensely. He looked like what I might become if I gave up tasting for accounting and accounting for eternity.

"Coral Delacroix," he said, consulting a card I never saw him draw. "Taster to a queen who does not deserve him. Ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things—which, incidentally, owes me eleven entries of back-tithe, but we needn't discuss that while you're grieving."

"I'm not grieving."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You're homesick. Different organ entirely." He began to walk, and the aisle of souls parted around us—not physically; the light simply bent toward him the way plants bend toward a window. "Come. Yours is this way. It always is, for the ones who come back. The others—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the thousands, the tens of thousands, "—the others never come. That's rather the point of selling."

Zephyrine did not follow. I looked back once and found the wolf seated at the threshold like a dog outside a butcher's shop, ears flat, watching me with those winter-colored eyes. He could not enter. I didn't yet know why. File that; it matters later.

* * *

My soul hung on a low branch in a side chapel off the main nave, and I knew it before the Broker pointed, the way you know your own house in a row of identical houses.

It was small. That undid me. All my life I had assumed—when I assumed anything about the hole in me, the sense of living slightly adjacent to my own existence, of reading my life like a ledger written in someone else's hand—that what was missing must be enormous. A kingdom's worth of self. Instead there it hung: a fist-sized warmth, the color of candlelight through wine, pulsing gently. Like a heart. Like a lamp in a window of a house you've been walking toward your whole life without knowing the street.

And it was dreaming. I could see that even from three steps away. The light moved in slow tides, brightening, dimming, and inside it—if you squinted past physics into whatever faculty lets a man recognize his own face—there were shapes. A table. Steam rising off something. Hands. Small hands, learning to hold a spoon.

"It's a child," I said. My voice cracked on it. "It's dreaming about being a child."

"The age it was sold," said the Broker, with the terrible mildness of a man reciting terms. "Nine years old. Souls fix at the moment of severance. Everything since—the poisons, the ledgers, the wolf-road—all of that lives in the body. This"—he nodded at the lantern—"has been having the same evening for ninety-one years. Dinner. Your parents' table. Rosemary."

Rosemary. My mother's hands. The memory the road took from my head was apparently alive and well in the thing hanging in front of me, and I pressed my palm flat against my sternum like a man checking for a leak.

"Can it see me?"

"No. Can you touch it? Also no—not yet, not legally. You're standing in my chapel holding nothing but a body, Mr. Delacroix, and a body is collateral, not currency." He folded his hands. "Though I'll grant it's a fine specimen. Ninety-one immunities. I've never seen the like. Do you know what you're worth on the open market? No—don't answer. You'd flinch, and flinching depresses the price."

I barely heard him. I had drifted closer, close enough that the warmth touched my face, and the lantern's tide of light stuttered—quickened—and settled into a rhythm I recognized with a jolt that went through me like a swallowed knife:

It was my heartbeat.

It had been beating in time with mine since I entered the chapel. Or perhaps since I entered the mountain. Perhaps since I was born, a lamp left burning in an empty house, keeping time with a tenant who never arrived.

I am a composed man. I taste arsenic with steady hands and write *bitter, almond finish, 40 seconds to onset* in a fair round script. I have watched a guildmaster be unmade into a forgetting and I recorded it in triplicate. But I stood in that glass chapel with my forehead nearly touching my own soul, both of us keeping time, and I cried the way children cry—ugly, graceless, from somewhere below craft.

Homesickness is the only honest word. I was homesick for myself. For a self I had never owned, never met, never once sat across a table from. Ninety-one years of dinners I hadn't attended, happening inside a lantern the size of my two fists.

"There, now," said the Broker, not unkindly, handing me a handkerchief that smelled of ink. "Everyone weeps at the reunion. Even the ones who sell. *Especially* the ones who sell, come to think of it. Grief is the one commodity that appreciates."

* * *

We sat afterward—he in a chair of grown glass that appeared when he wanted it, I on the floor, because my legs had resigned their commission—and he told me the shape of things in the tone of a man reading terms aloud before the signing.

"A willing seller can void a sale. That is the oldest clause in my books, older than me, older than the mountain. Your parents' bargain stands because it was willing—they signed, they received, they lived twenty more years on the proceeds. To break it requires a counter-bargain of equal weight, freely made." He peered at me over his spectacles. "You cannot steal yourself back, Mr. Delacroix. Believe me, people try. The chapel is very secure."

"And if I refuse? If I walk away?"

"Then you walk away with a hole in you and a heartbeat that isn't yours alone, and someday you'll hang here anyway, because everyone hangs here eventually—one way of purchase or another. Death, war, debt. I am patient the way geology is patient." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has already calculated your grief to the farthing. "But you won't refuse. You have the look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone who has spent his whole life tasting other people's food so that strangers might live, and who has just discovered the meal was his own." He rose, brushed off his coat, and produced—from nowhere, from the same nowhere all his paperwork came from—a single sheet of heavy paper and a pen. He set them on the glass root between us like a host setting out cups.

"Draft your offer when you're ready. Equal value. Freely given. Take whatever time you need—the usurper's army is roughly four days behind you, but I find deadlines make sellers sloppy, and sloppy contracts are how I lost the wolf, so." He paused at the edge of the aisle, lantern-light bending toward his back. "One courtesy, before I go. Ask the lantern what it wants."

"It can't want. You said—it loops. It dreams."

"Mm. I said it isn't *aware*. Wanting is older than awareness." And he was gone down the nave, a grey coat dissolving into grey roots, leaving me alone with ninety-one years of my own missed dinners.

I stayed a long time. Long enough that my knees ached and the cold of the stone climbed into my spine and Zephyrine's patience at the door wore thin enough that I felt it, faintly, like weather. I talked to the lantern, quietly, the way you talk to a sleeping child you don't want to wake. I told it about the pheasant. About Sable, and the guildhall, and the herald, and the road that ate my mother's face. I apologized—for arriving so late, for arriving at all, for the fact that everything I had to offer it was secondhand.

And near the end, half-mad with cold and wanting, I asked the Broker's question.

*What do you want?*

The lantern's tide stuttered. Quickened. And for one heartbeat—mine, or its, I couldn't tell you—the light inside it shaped itself into something almost legible. Not words. A gesture. Two small hands, the ones from the dream, held out.

Not reaching for me.

Reaching *past* me. Toward the dark of the nave, where somewhere in the tens of thousands of hanging lights, something else had begun, very faintly, to flicker in answer.

I turned. Far off in the cathedral of stolen selves, one lantern was burning a color I knew better than my mother's face: winter-grey, edged in gold.

Wolf-light.

Zephyrine had told me he couldn't enter. He hadn't told me why. But his soul—or the shard of it, or whatever a god loses when he loses himself—was hanging in the Broker's chapel, and it had just called my name.
