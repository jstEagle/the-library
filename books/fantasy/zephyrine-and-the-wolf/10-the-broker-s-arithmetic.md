## 10. The Broker's Arithmetic

The Broker did not have a face. This was, I decided somewhere between the third glass root and the ninth, a deliberate courtesy. A man with a face might have looked sorry for me, and I was not yet drunk enough on wonder to survive pity.

He was tall in the way that architecture is tall — not a body so much as a decision to occupy vertical space. His robes moved like slow water. Where his features should have been there was a smooth expanse of something like polished jet, and when he spoke, the voice arrived from everywhere at once, politely, as though it had been piped through the whole cathedral and had simply chosen the air nearest my ear.

"Delacroix," he said. "You've come about your soul."

"You say that like I've come about a delivery."

"Haven't you?" The smooth dark face tilted. Around us, the lanterns hung from roots of green glass that descended from a ceiling no one could see — souls in their hundreds, thousands perhaps, each one burning with its own color, its own temperature, its own small stubborn weather. Somewhere above us, snow fell on a mountain that had eaten three roads and a kingdom. Down here, everything was warm. "Most clients find the reunion emotional. You're cataloguing. I can hear you cataloguing."

I was, in fairness. Ninety-one immunities had taught me a habit: name the poison, name the dose, stay calm while your body decides whether tonight is the night. The Broker's voice was not a poison, but it had the shape of one — sweet at first contact, working inward after.

"I'm keeping accounts," I said. "It's what I do."

"Ah." Something in his tone shifted, and if I hadn't known better I'd have called it pleasure. "The guild's man. Then let us keep accounts together. Follow me."

* * *

We walked down an aisle of hanging light, and I will confess what I have confessed to no one since: I looked for myself before I could stop myself. Some animal part of me expected to find my own face floating in a jar of fire, and flinched from every lantern we passed, and grieved a little at each one that wasn't mine. That is the arithmetic of being sold before you were born. You spend your whole life checking shelves for goods you never knew were missing.

The Broker led me to a desk. Of course there was a desk. There is always a desk; hell is a bureaucracy with better lighting.

"Your parents," he said, opening a ledger that made mine look like a child's counting book. Its pages were vellum the color of old milk, and the ink on them moved — slowly, patiently, like things that knew they had all the time ever minted. "Renard Delacroix. Isolde Delacroix. Do you know how they came to owe this court?"

"I know they died," I said. "Debtors' fever, when I was six. The guild took me after."

"They died," the Broker agreed, "of a debt discharged. Fever is simply what the body does when a soul has been partially withdrawn and the paperwork is rushed. Your parents were in a hurry. Wars make people hurry." He turned the page with one finger that left no print. "A century ago, the free cities rebelled against the mountain kings. The rebellion failed. The reprisal was thorough. Certain families were marked for unmaking — not execution, which leaves bones and martyrs, but unmaking, which leaves nothing at all. No graves. No names in anyone's memory. Their own children would have walked past them in the street without a flicker."

"And mine was one of those families."

"Yours was the last of them. Renard and Isolde Delacroix stood before this court with nothing left to sell. No land. No years — they'd already pawned their youth, as people do. No names worth taking; yours had been struck from the rolls. And then your father said—" the Broker paused, and I realized with a lurch that he was quoting, that these words had been waiting down here in the warm dark for thirty years, filed and patient, "—'Take the one we haven't made yet.'"

* * *

The cathedral was very quiet. The lanterns burned. Somewhere far off, water dripped upward, which should tell you everything about this place.

"The one we haven't made yet," I repeated.

"You," said the Broker, gently, as though delivering news of weather. "Isolde was newly with child. Unborn souls are the rarest commodity in any market — they are unwritten, unshaped, capable of becoming anything. Worth more than a kingdom, frankly, though your parents' lives priced out somewhat lower. The court accepted. Your family's line was struck from the unmaking rolls. You were born nine months later, into safety purchased with yourself."

I put my hand on the desk because the floor had developed opinions about motion. "They didn't ask me."

"No."

"They couldn't ask me. I didn't exist."

"A technicality," said the Broker, "that has troubled jurists for a century. It is why your contract is the strangest thing in my collection, and I have the shadow of a saint and the last honest oath ever sworn by a king." He turned another page. Ink crawled across it like frost forming in reverse. "Here. Read it, if you like. Clients usually do."

I read it. My father's handwriting — I knew it from the one letter that survived him, the one that told me nothing — marched across the page in careful, desperate lines. *One soul, sold, court beneath the mountain.* And beneath it, in the same hand, smaller: *Forgive us. We chose the only future we were allowed.*

"Furious," said the Broker, watching me. "That's the usual stage. Grief comes later, or before, or sideways. Fury first, mostly. Go on. Take your time. Time is the one thing I never charge for — it charges for itself."

* * *

I was furious. Let the record show it. Not the clean fury of a man wronged, which burns bright and goes out, but the dirty, banked fury of a man who has just learned that every kindness he ever ate was bought with himself. The guild fed me. The Queen fed me. My mother sang to me over the cradle — sang, I now understood, the way you sing to something you are already grieving. They had all known. Every adult who ever smiled at little Coral Delacroix had smiled at a payment plan.

And underneath the fury, worse, quieter: the vertigo. I had spent my life tasting other people's intentions — the arsenic under the honey, the spite in the sauce. I had built ninety-one walls against the world's poisons. And the first poison ever administered to me had been administered before I drew breath, by the two people whose whole office it was to stand between me and harm. Every immunity I owned was armor forged by the theft it guarded.

"You're wondering," the Broker said, "how to void it."

"I'm wondering whether to burn this place down."

"Many do wonder that. It's structurally impossible — the cathedral grows back, like grief — but the wondering is healthy. As to voiding." He closed the great ledger, and the sound of it was like a door closing in a house where you used to live. "The law beneath the mountain is older than the mountain. A sale cannot be undone; it can only be re-traded. And here is the clause that has kept your soul hanging in my hall for thirty years, warming itself on dreams it doesn't remember making: a contract entered unwillingly by the sold party may be voided only by a willing seller."

I waited. He let me wait. Brokers do that; it's how they season you.

"Willing seller," I said finally. "Meaning what? Meaning I sell it to myself?"

"Meaning," said the Broker, "that the original transaction was made with stolen goods. You were the goods. The court has always known the sale was rotten at the root — why do you think your soul still hangs here instead of circulating? Who would buy a soul with a flaw in its title?" He spread his hands, that faceless dark face somehow conveying the shrug of every clerk in history. "But the law does not care about rotten. The law cares about willing. To void your parents' contract, you must enter a new one — freely, knowingly, with full understanding of the price. You must sell something equal."

"Equal to a soul."

"Equal to a soul," he agreed. "Not identical. Equality is a matter of appraisal. I am very good at appraising." The voice came closer, or seemed to. "A name that everyone loves. The memory of every meal you've ever tasted. Your future — all of it, undivided, which is more than most people ever had to offer. Or something I haven't thought of, which would delight me. But it must be given willingly, Coral Delacroix, with no reservation held back, no claw-back clause hidden in the heart. Willingness cannot be counterfeited down here. I would taste it in the ink."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out of here unsold and unowned, technically free, carrying a hollow where your self should be, and Veyra's armies arrive in some number of days, and the prophecy finishes writing itself without your consent. Which, I note, is exactly how the last hundred years went." He folded his hands. "Or you pay, and walk out whole, and owe the wolf-god whatever he asked you for passage. He always asks. He watched your family sign, you know. He's been waiting to watch someone unsign it."

The lanterns swayed slightly, though there was no wind. All that light, all those sold selves, burning patiently in the dark — and mine among them, still warm, still dreaming, dreaming, the Broker had said, of a life it had never gotten to live. I thought of it hanging there like a lamp in a window for someone who never comes home.

"I'll need paper," I said. "I draft contracts. It's what I do."

Something happened on that smooth dark face that I will always believe was a smile.

"Of course you do," said the Broker, and slid a blank page across the desk, and set down a pen, and did not tell me — I noticed this only later, replaying it, the way you notice the poison an hour after swallowing — that the page was already waiting for a word, and that the word would cost me more than I yet knew how to count.

*Willing,* the pen seemed to say, hovering over the vellum in my hand. *Begin with willing.*

I could not, yet, write it.
