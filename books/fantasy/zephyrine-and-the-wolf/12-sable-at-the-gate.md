## 12. Sable at the Gate

I had been a courier for the Guild of Impossible Things for six years, and in that time I had carried a stolen shadow sewn into my coat lining, three years of a dead woman's life folded into letters no bigger than moth wings, and once — and only once, thank every merciful god who'd allow it — a name so heavy I had to be paid double to sit down while holding it.

So when the guild's remnant handed me a sealed packet and told me to find Coral Delacroix before anyone else did, I assumed it was ordinary work. Find the man, deliver the thing, don't open the packet, don't ask why the seal is wax from the master's own ring when the master no longer exists as a person anyone can remember.

That last part should have been my first warning. You can't take wax from a man who has been unmade. And yet there it was, warm-red and stamped with the guild's sigil, as though the seal itself remembered him better than we did.

"He's on the wolf-road," said Vess, who had been junior ledger-keeper for four days and senior everything-else for one, since everyone above her had stopped being rememberable or started being dead. She pushed a map across the table, and then pulled it back, and then pushed it again, because grief does that to people. "Or he will be. He went up through the Snowline gate two nights ago with—" she hesitated, and her face did something complicated— "with the wolf."

"The wolf," I repeated.

"Zephyrine."

"The *wolf-god* Zephyrine. Our bookkeeper went up the mountain with the wolf-god."

"Yes."

"And the free cities have put a price on his head."

"Crown price in Ashfall. Kill-price in Coriander. In Saltmere they want him alive and unharmed, which is somehow worse." Vess rubbed her eyes. "They've read the prophecy, Sable. Or fragments of it. The Taster. Savior or ruin of the free cities. Half of them want to crown him and half of them want to cut the prophecy out of the world along with whatever carries it."

"And what am I supposed to do when I find him?"

Vess slid the packet across the table. It was lighter than a name and heavier than a shadow, which in courier terms means it was exactly the weight of a decision someone else had refused to make.

"That depends," she said, "on whether you're delivering help or a mark."

* * *

Let me explain what a mark is, since you may not have spent your life in the trade of impossible things.

A mark looks like help. A packet, a token, a sigil sewn into a collar, a phrase whispered at the right moment. What it actually does is make its bearer legible — turns a man into a location, a location into a target. The cities can't agree on whether to crown Coral Delacroix or kill him, but they agree completely that they'd like to know where he is, and a guild seal is the kind of thing their agents have learned to trust. Six years of honest deliveries buy you that.

I didn't know which kind of packet I carried. That was the design. The faction inside the guild that wanted to save Coral and the faction that wanted to spend him had each signed off on the mission, each believing the packet was theirs. Vess, bless her exhausted heart, had sealed it herself without looking inside, and told me so, and looked at me with the particular honesty of someone twenty-two years old who has already buried an entire institution.

"I could open it," I said.

"You could."

"It would take me about nine seconds."

"It would," she agreed. "And then whichever faction you disappointed would send someone after you, and you're fast, Sable, but you're not *unremembered* fast."

So I didn't open it. I put it in the inner pocket where I keep things that might be knives, and I took the north road out of Ashfall before dawn, and I told myself the knot in my stomach was just the road rations.

It wasn't the road rations. The road rations were terrible, but the knot was older than that.

Here is the thing nobody says about loyalty: it isn't a feeling. Feelings are weather. Loyalty is a ledger entry. Coral Delacroix entered my name in the guild books eleven years ago, when I was fourteen and starving and had just tried to pickpocket a man who turned out to be a professional taster of poisons — which, if you're going to rob someone, is possibly the worst possible choice, because a poison-taster notices everything, including small hands in his pocket, and reacts to nothing, which is more frightening. He caught my wrist, looked at my hands like he was cataloguing them, and said: "You have quick fingers and bad instincts. The second one is fixable."

Then he bought me dinner. Then he wrote me into the guild as a runner, at a wage, with a clause in the contract guaranteeing that the guild would never trade anything of mine without my consent — my years, my name, my shadow, my soul. I didn't even know those were commodities yet. I thought he was being poetic.

He was being careful. There's a difference, and I have spent six years learning it, and the difference is mostly that care looks boring right up until it saves your life.

So yes. I owed him a ledger entry. And I was carrying, quite possibly, the instrument of his death, sealed in wax from a man who no longer existed.

The road gave me four days to think about it, which was three too many.

* * *

You notice things on the road north. For instance: the way the villages change as you climb toward the Snowline. The way people stop naming their children after kings and start naming them after weather. The way the trees get quieter, as if the forest itself were lowering its voice out of respect, or fear, and you can't tell which, and near the snowline you stop being sure there's a difference.

For another instance: hoofprints. A lot of them. Riding hard, riding organized, riding in the pattern of soldiers rather than bandits. Bandits travel like spilled water. These traveled like a poured line.

And for a third instance, on the morning of the fourth day, half-buried in snow at the mouth of the old wolf-road: a shoe. Just one. Good leather, city-made, dropped by someone in a hurry or taken off by someone in pain, and beside it, pressed deep into the drift, a paw-print the size of a dinner plate with claws like sickles.

I stood there a long moment with my breath smoking and my hand on the packet in my inner pocket, doing arithmetic I didn't like.

The wolf-road doesn't eat names for everyone. That's the rumor, anyway, and rumors from couriers are better than most people's facts. It eats memories as tolls — takes them according to some accounting no living trader has ever decoded. Old stories say travelers come down the mountain having forgotten their mothers' faces, their first loves, the taste of bread. The guild's own records — the ones Vess let me read before I left — say the road was Zephyrine's long before it was anyone's road, and that the god walks it still, bound to it by something older than the mountain, and that everything lost upon it goes somewhere.

Into him. That was the implication. The wolf remembers what we forget. Every toll ever paid on that road is carried in that enormous head, century upon century of other people's childhoods, and I found — standing in the snow with one shoe for company — that I couldn't decide if that was the loneliest thing I'd ever heard or the holiest.

I picked up the shoe. If it was Coral's, he'd want it back; tasters are fastidious about feet, all that standing at banquet tables. If it wasn't Coral's, it was evidence, and evidence pointed somewhere.

The prints led up. Of course they did. Everything about this whole wretched affair led up, toward the court beneath the mountain, toward the Broker, toward the soul Coral Delacroix had apparently been sold before he drew his first breath — because yes, Vess had told me that part too, on my last night in the city, over tea neither of us drank.

"His parents sold him," she'd said. "Before he was born. Bought their own lives with his soul. He found it in his own handwriting in the ledgers — an entry dated before his birth."

"And now?"

"Now he's gone to get it back." She'd stared into her cup. "From the thing our entire guild exists to avoid dealing with directly."

I had asked her then, the question I probably should have opened the packet to answer: "And what do *we* want? The guild. The survivors. What do we want from Coral?"

She'd been quiet for a while. "I think," she said finally, "that some of us want him to live. And some of us want the prophecy to come true, whichever way it comes true, because a fulfilled prophecy is worth more to the cities than a saved taster. And I think the packet contains both answers at once, and I sealed it anyway, because I'm not brave enough to choose for you."

Well. Neither was I. But the mountain was going to make me choose, and soon, because the poured line of hoofprints was also heading up, and there are only so many roads beneath the snowline, and I knew whose soldiers made tracks like that.

Lord Veyra's. The usurper. Who, according to the last dispatches out of the lowlands, wasn't marching to conquer the mountain at all. He was marching for the Broker's ledger. Every soul-debt in the realm, every sold name, every mortgaged year — leverage over everyone alive, written in one place. You don't need armies when you own the arithmetic.

I climbed.

* * *

The gate to the court beneath the mountain isn't a gate so much as an argument the rock has given up on. You walk into a cleft in the mountainside expecting stone and cold and you get warmth instead, and light — a gold-green glow rising from below, like sunlight that has been underground so long it's forgotten what it was for. Glass roots grow through the cavern walls, thick as ship masts, thin as hair, all of them glowing faintly, all of them humming a note just below hearing.

I heard the humming before I saw the lanterns.

Then I saw the lanterns, and stopped walking entirely.

They hung from the glass roots by the thousand. Not glass lanterns, not metal — *souls*. That was the word my mind supplied and refused to withdraw, because there was no other word for what I was looking at: lights the size of hearts, each one pulsing with its own slow rhythm, none of them in time with any other, each one unmistakably, appallingly *somebody*. They weren't uniform. They had colors the way people have colors — this one a stubborn amber, that one a nervous violet, a whole cluster of sea-green ones hanging together like a family that had been sold in a lot. And they moved. When a draft came through the cavern, the nearest souls leaned toward each other, the way people lean together in a cold room.

I have seen the guild's warehouse of stolen shadows. I have watched a man sell sixty years of his future for his daughter's ransom and walk out lighter, literally lighter, measurably so on the guild's scales. I thought I understood what the trade was.

I hadn't understood anything. This wasn't a warehouse. It was a cathedral, and the congregation was made of everyone who had ever been spent, and they were all still *in here*, still warm, still dreaming, hung up like festival lights in a god's cellar.

Somewhere among them — I understood with a lurch — was a lantern with Coral Delacroix's name on it, if souls have names, and I suspected they did, and I suspected his was still warm.

"Sable?"

I turned so fast I nearly went down on the glass-root floor.

He looked terrible. That was my first coherent observation, and I stand by it. Coral Delacroix had always been precise the way a scalpel is precise — clean lines, controlled color, a body kept carefully between doses of poison for so long that control had become his native posture. The man standing at the edge of the root-light looked like a scalpel that had been through a furnace and bent back into roughly the shape of a knife. His coat was scorched. His hands shook. Behind him the cavern showed signs of recent catastrophe — scorch-marks on the roots, shattered lantern-hooks, and in the far dark, the sound of falling stone, as if the whole cathedral were deciding, slowly, whether to remain a cathedral.

And behind *him*, filling a passage mouth like a moon fills a window, was the wolf.

I want to describe Zephyrine properly and I'm going to fail, but here's the attempt: he was wrong in the way mountains are wrong, meaning your eyes kept insisting he was landscape and your hindbrain kept screaming *predator*, and both were correct. Grey like winter at dusk. Eyes like the memory of fire. He looked at me the way an old judge looks at a familiar case — not unkindly, but with the whole weight of precedent behind the gaze.

"You're the courier," the wolf said. His voice was low thunder with a dry wit in it, which I had not been warned about. "Sable. Eleven years in the guild. You once carried a name so heavy you were paid double to sit down."

"How do you—"

"The road told me." One vast shoulder shifted in something like a shrug. "Everything lost on the road comes to me eventually. Including, lately, a great deal of Coral."

"Don't remind me," Coral said. His voice was hoarse. "Please. I've only just stopped mourning my mother's face, I don't need the inventory read aloud."

Something in me cracked a little, hearing it said so plainly. He'd forgotten her face. The road had taken it as a toll, and he had *paid it*, walked on, kept going, because the alternative was the usurper's arithmetic winning. I looked at this bent, burned, shaking man who had eaten ninety-one poisons so a queen could eat safely, and I put my hand in my inner pocket, and I felt the packet, and I understood — finally, completely, with the hanging souls watching me with their patient heartlight — that I was standing at the exact point where loyalty stops being a ledger entry and becomes a choice.

Because here's what I hadn't known until that moment, and what I knew instantly, the way you know a stair is missing in the dark: whoever delivered that packet to Coral, and whatever it contained — help, or a mark, or both braided together — it would land on a man who had just surrendered every immunity he had ever built. Vess had told me that much before I left, in a whisper, horrified: *he paid the wolf's toll early, all of it at once, every poison he ever swallowed, to escape the collapsing court.* Which meant that the man in front of me could now be killed by a cup of wine. By a pinprick. By the dust of the wrong flower crushed into bread.

By a packet's contents, if the contents were meant to mark him for the cities' hunters. Or worse — if Veyra's agents had gotten to the guild's remnants, and the seal I'd trusted was bait.

Coral was watching my hand in my pocket. Of course he was. He noticed everything; it was the one thing about him that had never changed.

"That's guild wax," he said quietly.

"It is."

"Whose seal?"

"Master Orlan's ring."

His face went very still. Master Orlan, who no longer existed as a person anyone could remember — unmade by the herald in front of witnesses, erased so thoroughly that we who had loved him had to keep notes just to know we'd grieved. And here was his seal, warm-red, traveling north in a courier's pocket, into a war.

"Sable," Coral said. "What's in it?"

"I don't know. I was told not to look. Two factions signed off on the delivery and each believes the packet is theirs."

The wolf made a sound that I realized, absurdly, was a laugh — a single low note like a bell rung under a blanket. "Your guild," Zephyrine said, "has always had such *interesting* governance."

"Open it," Coral said. "Or give it to me, and I'll open it. Whatever it is — crown, kill-order, key, confession — I'd rather meet it with my eyes working."

And there it was. The choice, laid out on the glass-root floor between us. Deliver as instructed — hand it over sealed, let fate or faction do its work, keep my hands technically clean. Or break faith with the instructions and keep faith with the man, which any fool could tell you was the real instruction, had always been the real instruction, written into the guild's founding charter in ink nobody used anymore: *we trade in impossible things, and the first impossible thing is that we do not trade each other.*

I thought about Master Orlan, whom I could not remember, grieving him from a note in my own handwriting. I thought about a fourteen-year-old with quick fingers and bad instincts, and the fixed second one, and the dinner he bought me. I thought about the lanterns overhead, leaning together against the draft.

I broke the seal.

Inside was a single sheet, and I read it twice, and then I laughed — a short, ugly sound that echoed off the roots and made a hundred hanging souls flicker, the way a crowd flickers when someone says something true too loudly.

"Well?" Coral demanded.

"It's both." I held it out to him. "It's a safe-conduct under the guild's old authority — passage, shelter, recognition, the works. *And* it's a description. Your face, your build, your route, your destination. Written in two different hands. The safe-conduct's genuine. The description's addressed to the cities' hunters. Whoever assembled it wanted us to deliver both without noticing we were choosing."

Coral took the page. His hands were still shaking, but his voice, when he spoke, had found the old precision again, the scalpel-edge, and it was aimed at no one and everyone:

"They sent a letter that kills or saves depending on who reads it first."

"They sent *you*," Zephyrine said, "a letter. Same principle. Smaller."

I snorted despite everything, and Coral almost smiled, and for one breath the three of us stood in a dying cathedral full of stolen souls, sharing the world's bleakest joke.

Then the mountain shuddered again, harder, and dust sifted down through the root-light, and from the passage behind the wolf came a sound I knew from the lowlands and had hoped never to hear underground: the ordered tread of soldiers. Lots of them. Close.

Coral looked at the shaking walls. At the lanterns swaying on their hooks, thousands of dreaming lights that Veyra's army had come to seize like coin. At me.

"Sable," he said. "How fast can you run?"

"Fast enough," I said, folding the broken packet away, keeping the safe-conduct, burning the description in the nearest root-flame without asking permission. "Where are we running *to*?"

He turned back toward the deep of the court, toward the place where his own soul hung still warm and still dreaming, and the wolf's eyes flared like coals remembering fire, and somewhere above us the mountain groaned like a door being forced.

"To finish a bargain," said Coral Delacroix. "And this time, I intend to write the terms myself."
