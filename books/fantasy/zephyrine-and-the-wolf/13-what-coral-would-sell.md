## 13. What Coral Would Sell

The Broker's court kept hours the way the sea keeps borders—nominally, and only for those who needed them. Down among the glass roots there was no dawn, no dusk, only a slow pulse of light through the cathedral ceiling, like a heart deciding whether to bother. I had been given a cell of my own: a hollow in the root-glass with a desk grown out of the living wall, a chair that was probably comfortable by someone's anatomy, and a lamp that burned without oil, which as a guild man I found professionally obscene.

Everything down here burned without fuel. Souls made poor kindling but excellent light.

I sat at the desk with a fresh sheet of vellum—the Broker provided vellum the way an innkeeper provides towels, briskly and without comment—and a pot of ink that was very nearly black, and I tried to write the contract that would buy back my soul.

The problem, you understand, was arithmetic.

The Broker had laid it out for me the evening before with the patience of a woman who had explained compound interest to weeping men for ten thousand years. A soul could not simply be *returned*. That would be theft, and the court beneath the mountain had never once, in all its history, committed theft—it didn't need to; it had contracts. A sale could only be voided by a counter-sale of equal weight, freely made. My parents had paid for their lives with me. If I wanted me back, I had to pay for me with something else, and the something else had to weigh the same on the soul-scale.

And so I did what any bookkeeper does when the numbers refuse to behave. I made a list.

*Item the first,* I wrote. *My name.*

It seemed obvious enough. Names were currency in my trade; the Guild of Impossible Things had bought and sold them by the ream. Lord Veyra's herald had unmade our guildmaster with one, plucked Gideon Marrow out of every memory and record and left behind a hole shaped like a modest man who had liked his tea oversteeped. A name was not nothing.

But I sat there saying *my name is Coral Delacroix* to myself, testing the weight of it, and found it wanting. It was a good name. It had belonged to a grandmother and a great-uncle and a distant cousin hanged for forgery, which in the Delacroix family was considered less a disgrace than a specialty. But it was a borrowed coat, really. I had worn it ninety-one poisoned dinners running and it had never once been what kept me alive. Strip a man of his name and he becomes a stranger; strip me of mine and I'd still be the same half-sold creature blinking under glass roots, missing exactly the piece I came here to reclaim. The scale wouldn't tip. The Broker would weigh "Coral Delacroix" in her root-glass pans, find it a few ounces light of a soul, and offer me a discount voucher toward my next damnation.

I crossed it out.

*Item the second,* I wrote, and here my hand slowed. *My taste-memory.*

Every taster has one. It is the thing that cannot be taught: a library of the tongue, tens of thousands of flavors filed and cross-referenced, each one tagged with its poison or its provenance. Bitter almond and know why. Foxglove dressed as honey. The particular mineral wrongness of arsenic cut into a frangipane, which tastes, if you're wondering, like a coin left in a church. Ninety-one immunities built dish by dish, year by year, each one purchased with an evening of convulsing quietly in a servant's cot while my body argued terms with something that wanted it dead.

That library was worth more than a name. That library was *me*, more than anything else I owned. Take it away and Coral Delacroix remained—but a Coral who could eat a pheasant and taste only pheasant, who could stand at a queen's elbow and be ornamental merely, useless, unemployed, undone. A taster without the tongue is a sword without an edge, and worse: a man who no longer knows what he is for.

It would balance. I was almost sure it would balance. Half a self traded for a whole one; even theBroker couldn't fault the symmetry, and she struck me as someone who lived for symmetry the way other women lived for shoes.

But my pen hovered, and did not fall.

Because—and here is the thing I had not admitted to anyone, least of all to the wolf pacing somewhere above the snowline, patient as winter—I did not want to survive as a man who tasted nothing. There is a version of rescue that is just a slower ruin. I had spent my whole life swallowing things so that others might dine safely, and I found, sitting in that glowing hollow, that I was unwilling to swallow the last draught myself: a life with the flavor boiled out of it. What was the point of owning a soul if the mouth it lived behind had been emptied first?

Wry observation, delivered to no one: I had become a connoisseur of my own destruction's alternatives. Somewhere, my grandmother the forger was presumably proud.

I crossed out item the second, though my hand shook doing it, because crossing it out meant admitting there were things I valued above survival, and a man in my profession learns early that this admission is how tasters die. We die of the dish we hesitate over.

*Item the third. My future.*

This one frightened me properly, so naturally I wrote it with flourish, the way I do everything that frightens me.

A future is a strange commodity because nobody can inspect it before purchase. The Guild dealt in futures constantly—three borrowed years here, a promised harvest there—and the trick of it, the dirty open secret, was that most sellers never missed what they sold, because they'd been so busy dreading the future they never lived in enough of it to feel the absence. I had watched farmers sell their harvests-before-they-happened and go on walloping their donkeys without noticing the years had gone thin. Futures were the guildhall's best margin precisely because grief always arrives after the receipt.

But a whole future? All of it? Sold forward, every unwritten page? That wasn't three borrowed years. That was the entire remaining book, handed over unread.

I thought about what my future actually contained, if I was honest, and the honest inventory went: the war coming down off the passes; Veyra's soldiers; the prophecy that named me savior or ruin with the serene indifference of a stone naming itself both mountain and landslide. My future, inspected fairly, was mostly smoke. Selling it should have felt like selling floodwater.

It didn't. It felt like selling the last window in the house.

Because there were things in it. Small ones. Snowfall in the city of Ashgrace, which I had decided sometime around dish sixty that I loved, though I had never once said so aloud. Sable's laugh, which she deployed perhaps twice a year and hoarded otherwise like a miser with two coins. The ridiculous ambition—never spoken, barely thought—that when the wars were done I might keep a small shop, or a small garden, or a small life of any denomination whatsoever, and find out what I tasted like when nothing was poisoning me.

Sell the future and none of it would ever be mine. Not the snow, not the laugh, not the smallness. Someone else would live forward from this desk; I would stop here, a full stop mid-sentence, complete but concluded.

The lamp flickered. Or perhaps I flinched.

* * *

Somewhere in the third hour—I judged time by the pulse of the roots, which quickened and slowed like a sleeper turning over—I heard footsteps that were not footsteps. Zephyrine did not do well indoors; the corridors of the court had been dug for souls, not wolves, and he moved through them the way weather moves through a house. The temperature dropped politely as he entered my hollow and then rose again, as though the cold were a courtesy he was extending rather than an imposition.

"You have written three items," he said, "and crossed out three items."

"You've been reading over my shoulder."

"I have been lying in the road above your head thinking about debt. It amounts to the same posture." He settled—which is a generous word for the way several hundred pounds of god arranged themselves against a wall clearly not load-bearing for divinity—and regarded the vellum upside-down, which he could do; gods read in whatever direction amuses them. "Your arithmetic is sound. Your nerve isn't."

"Thank you for the review."

"I watched a man sell his shadow once," Zephyrine said, conversational, terrible. "He got a fine price. He spent thirty years standing in candlelight wondering why the flame cast nothing, and on the last night of his life he understood that he had sold the proof that he existed, and he died of the understanding. You are doing his arithmetic backward. You keep setting aside the things that prove you, hoping to find something left over that is only ballast." His yellow eyes found mine. "There is no ballast, little taster. Everything you own is load-bearing. That is what being poor in soul means."

"I notice," I said, "that you're not offering to lend me the difference."

Something moved through him—not quite a flinch. Wolves' faces aren't built for guilt, but they manage. "No," he said. "I'm not."

"And I suppose you can't tell me why."

"I can't." He said it flatly, and for the first time since I'd met him on the snowline, the ancient hunger in his voice sounded less like appetite and more like a chain being tested. "Ask me instead what you already know, and I will confirm it badly."

So I asked the question that had been growing in me since the ledger entry in my own pre-natal handwriting. "You knew my family. You knew about the sale. A century ago."

"A hundred years and one season ago," Zephyrine said, "I stood witness on a mountainside while two desperate people signed away a child who did not exist yet, and I swore—" He stopped. Gods shouldn't be able to stop mid-sentence; the grammar of the world is supposed to hold them up. He started again, quieter. "I witnessed the bargain. And I swore an oath about it whose terms bind me tighter than these mountains bind the road. When the hour comes you will understand what I may do and what I may not, and you will likely hate the shape of it. Until then: write your contract, Coral Delacroix. Choose what you will pay. The choosing is the only part of this that is yours."

"The choosing," I said, "is the whole of what I'm trying to buy back."

"Yes," said the wolf-god, with something horribly like tenderness. "Now you begin to see the joke."

He left the way weather leaves, which is to say the room stayed cold for a long time afterward, out of respect.

* * *

I worked until the roots dimmed to embers. Three items crossed out, and beneath them, in the manner of a clerk who has run out of elegant solutions and must now resort to honesty, I began writing what I actually had.

*Remaining inventory,* I wrote. *One body, used hard. One conscience, secondhand. One talent for surviving things, which is to say: ninety-one immunities, held jointly with death under an old and informal arrangement.*

I stopped.

Read it again.

Ninety-one immunities. The toll Zephyrine had named on the snowline, before we ever set foot below the mountain: *all of them, surrendered at once, when the soul is reclaimed.* At the time it had sounded like a price tag. Sitting at the Broker's desk with the soul-scale humming somewhere in the dark, weighing everything in the world whether I asked it to or not, I suddenly saw it differently.

What equals a soul?

A soul is every poison it ever survived. Every wound it refused to die of. Every immunity is a night the body lost and lost and finally, grudgingly, won—and ninety-one of those nights knitted together make a fabric stronger than skin, and that fabric is the closest thing to an immortal soul a mortal can build by hand. I had assumed my immunities were armor. They weren't. They were *me*—the accumulated, calcified record of every time I chose to keep going. Of course the scale would take them. Of course they'd balance. They were the same thing, seen from opposite ends: protection was just my soul worn on the outside.

Zephyrine hadn't named a cruel price. He'd named the only true one. To buy back the soul I would have to give up everything I had built in its absence—every scarred-over defense, every hardened night—and stand afterward with nothing between me and the world's malice but a single, ordinary, killable life.

One cup of wine, Sable would say later. One cup of wine could end me.

And some traitor voice in my chest, quiet and certain, said: *yes, and that is what being alive costs, and everyone else has been paying it all along.*

I dipped the pen. My hand was steady, which surprised me; I had expected it to shake, and there is a particular loneliness in discovering your own courage arrives without ceremony.

*Contract of Counter-Sale,* I wrote across the top. *Let it be known that I, Coral Delacroix, son of Maren and Elio Delacroix, being of sound mind and thoroughly unsound liver, do hereby offer in exchange for the return of my soul, held in trust by the court beneath the mountain since before my birth:*

*All immunities currently held, being ninety-one in number, surrendered together and at once, without reservation, remedy, or refund.*

There. The price was set. The scale would sing. All that remained was the clause every contract needs, the word that separated a bargain from a robbery, the word the Broker required and the law required and decency itself required, four syllables, nine letters, the easiest word in any language to write and the hardest one I had ever met:

*I, the seller, enter into this agreement freely and*

*willingly.*

My pen touched the vellum.

And stopped.

Not hesitated—stopped, the way a key stops at a lock it does not fit. I wrote *w*. I drew the stroke of an *i*. And there the nib sat, blotting a small black star into the page, because I could not make my hand finish it, and I understood with terrible clarity that this was not cowardice. Cowardice would have let me write the word and mean nothing by it. This was the opposite: some final unpoisoned chamber of myself refusing to lie on a legal document, refusing to declare willingness toward a surrender I had framed as payment when it was, when you turned it in the light, exactly what my parents' signature had been a century ago—a terrible thing done under pressure, dressed in the vocabulary of choice.

They had signed *willingly* too. I was sure of it now. The Broker's ledger would show it, tidy as arithmetic. And it had been a lie then, and if I wrote it tonight it would be a lie tonight, and the whole rotten inheritance would continue: souls sold under duress by people taught to call their desperation freedom.

I put down the pen.

The lamp burned on without fuel. In the dark beyond my hollow, the hanging lanterns of the sold souls glowed and swayed, thousands of them, each one a *willingly* somebody had written under pressure, each one dreaming of the hand that would come to strike its name from the ledger honestly or not at all.

"All right," I said aloud, to the vellum, to the wolf gone up the road, to whichever ancestor was watching over my shoulder and wincing. "Then I don't sign tonight. Then I find another way to be willing—or I find out that I can break the scale instead."

Which was, I would learn within the day, exactly the wrong thing to say in a place that could hear me—because far above us, horns were sounding on the snowline, and the first thing the mountain heard descending through the pass was the measured, patient tread of Lord Veyra's army, come not for me at all, but for the ledger.
