## 14. The Usurper Descends

I have taken three cities in my life, and I will tell you the honest arithmetic of conquest, since no one else will: a city is not taken. It is *collected*. You arrive with more soldiers than they have courage, and the city simply changes hands the way a coin changes hands—reluctantly, warmly, already spending itself in your name. The banners come down. The tax collectors learn new names. Somewhere a poet begins drafting an ode to you that is only slightly better than the one he wrote for your predecessor.

The mountain was not like that.

The mountain did not surrender, because the mountain had nothing to surrender *to*. You cannot collect a thing that does not consider itself owned. My armies—forty thousand strong, fed on six years of careful grievances, dressed in the grey-and-gold of a realm I had already finished conquering everywhere else—came up the old wolf-road in column after column, and the mountain looked at us the way an old man looks at weather.

"Lord Veyra." Captain Hesh, at my shoulder, holding the map as though the map were responsible for our discomfort. "The scouts report the road narrows ahead. There's a shrine of some kind. Wolf skulls on stakes."

"Wolf skulls on stakes," I repeated. "Whose devotion is that? Ours or theirs?"

"Theirs, presumably."

"Pity. I could have used an omen that agreed with me."

Here is what my generals did not understand, and what Hesh alone was beginning to: we had not marched four hundred leagues for the free cities. The free cities were a purse, and purses are dull. Any fool with ten thousand men can rob a purse. We had marched for the thing *under* the purse—the ledger of the Soul-Broker, the court beneath the mountain where every debt in the realm was written down in a hand older than handwriting.

Conquest gives you a kingdom. A ledger gives you *everyone*.

Think of it as I thought of it, lying awake in campaign tents while my officers snored around me like a market of hogs. Every lord who ever sold a year of his life to buy back a mistake. Every merchant who pawned her shadow against a bad harvest. Every farmer who traded his grandfather's name for rain. Somewhere under this rock, all of it was recorded—all of it, itemized, signed, *witnessed*—and whoever held that book held not an army but an index of every living person's weakness, sorted alphabetically.

Armies desert. Ledgers do not. Armies have opinions; ledgers have only facts, and facts are the most loyal soldiers ever mustered.

"You're smiling, my lord," Hesh said.

"I'm doing sums," I said. "Keep walking."

* * *

The wolf-road is a dishonest piece of engineering, which I suppose is fitting, since it was built by a god. It switchbacks up through pine and then through nothing, above the snowline, where the air gets thin enough that my veterans—who had stormed walls at Verre and drowned a rebellion in the Delta—went quiet and clingy as children. And it eats things.

Not men. We lost remarkably few men. It eats *smaller* property.

A sergeant came to me on the third day of the ascent, gray-faced, reporting that he could no longer remember his sister's face. Not that she was dead—he knew she was alive, married, in Corvassy—but her face had gone out of him like a candle pinched between wet fingers. He showed me the miniature he carried and said the painted woman looked like a stranger wearing his grief.

"Toll," said our guide, a shriveled local named Ost who cost me more per day than my quartermaster and was worth less per hour than my horse. "Road takes what you'd least afford. Always has. God's got expensive habits."

"And what," I asked, "would the road take from me?"

Ost looked at me the way men look at me when they are deciding whether honesty is survivable. "Dunno, my lord. Men like you usually don't carry anything the road wants. Road likes soft things. Memories, loves, regrets." He spat, expertly, into the wind. "You'd probably just lose a bit of ambition. If there's any loose."

My officers laughed. I let them. It is useful, occasionally, to be underestimated by geography.

But I will confess something here, into the privacy of this account, which no one will read until it is far too late to matter: that night I checked. I went through myself the way a paymaster goes through a strongbox, counting what was still mine. My mother's voice—present. The taste of the first wine I ever stole—present. The memory of my brother's funeral, the rain, the too-small grave—present, unfortunately; even ambition keeps its scars. All accounted for.

Except one. There was a room in me—I cannot describe it better than that, a small warm room somewhere behind the sternum—that had once held something, and now held the shape of holding it. I turned the shape over and over in the dark and could not find what had been taken, or when, or whether it had ever been anything but a shape.

The road had taken a toll from me after all, and I did not know its name.

I have decided not to think about this further. This decision has been, on the whole, successful.

* * *

We reached the gates—or rather, we reached the place where gates should be, and were not, because the court beneath the mountain does not believe in doors so much as in *thresholds*, which are the same thing with the courtesy removed. A cleft in the rock, breathing warm air that smelled of glass and honey and something underneath both, something mineral and patient. My army camped outside in the snow because forty thousand men cannot fit inside a legend, and because—and here I credit my own instinct over every general I possess—some places punish crowds.

I took twelve. Hesh. Two banner-captains whose families I hold in comfortable hostage-hood. A notary, because whatever we found down there, I wanted it *written down*, witnessed, sealed—paper answering paper, contract answering contract. You do not walk into a house of debts without your own ink.

And I took the herald's box.

You remember my herald—the one I sent ahead to the Guild of Impossible Things with a polite request for their ledgers, and who returned without the ledgers, without his escort, and without, when I finally got him alone, entirely intact. What came back wore his face and answered to his name, mostly, but paused too long before speaking, like a translation happening in real time. He told me the guildmaster had refused me. Then he told me the guildmaster had been *unmade*—struck from the record, struck from memory, so thoroughly deleted that my own herald could not recall the man's face, only the fact of the refusal, which apparently survives deletion the way a scar survives the death of the scarred.

He gave me one other thing. A single page, torn from a guild ledger, smuggled out by someone in the guild who understood which way the wind was blowing. An entry in a clerk's precise hand:

*One soul, sold. Court beneath the mountain. Witnessed.*

That was all. No name. No date. But the *witness mark*—the little seal beside the entry—was the same seal I had seen burned into the treaty-stones of the old kings, a century before my time. Whatever the guild was, it was older than the throne I had stolen, and it kept its true accounts not in the guildhall but down here, in the dark, in the Broker's keeping.

So: the guild had refused me and paid for it. Fine. Guilds refill. But the refusal told me the important thing, the thing I had suspected since the first winter of my campaign: the Broker does not sell to the ambitious. The Broker merely *records* us. And a man who records everything and sells nothing is the most dangerous creditor alive, because he cannot be bought, and therefore cannot be beaten—only robbed.

I intended to rob him.

"Lord Veyra." Hesh, at the threshold, torch guttering in the warm updraft. Beyond him, stairs going down into a light that had no source—amber, rootlike, branching along the walls of the descending passage like veins in a great pale wrist. "Are we—forgive me—are we certain about this?"

"Hesh. Look at them." Below us, faintly, hanging in the amber depths: lights. Hundreds. Thousands. Lantern-shaped, drifting slow as jellyfish in honeycomb cells of glass. "Every one of those is a person's best self, filed and shelved. Every noble who ever knelt to me, every general who ever defied me, every king whose crown I melted down for payroll—they're all down there in some form, and there is a book that says exactly which shelf." I stepped past him onto the first stair. "I am not going to conquer this place. Conquering it would be vulgar, and impossible. I'm going to do something far worse."

"My lord?"

"I'm going to *audit* it."

* * *

The descent taught me humility, briefly, and I resented it.

The court beneath the mountain is a cathedral built by something that never learned architecture from men. Glass roots everywhere—great columns of it, grown rather than carved, glowing from within—and among the roots, the souls. I say *souls* the way you'd say *birds*, plural, because that is what they were: individual, animate, aware. As we passed, they turned toward us. Toward *me*, mostly. I felt the attention of several thousand disembodied essences settle on my shoulders like snow that had opinions.

One of them, near the path, drifted close to its cell-wall and looked at me—not at my rank, my armor, my torch, but at *me*, the interior article, the part I keep behind the sternum next to the missing room—and made a sound like a struck wineglass saying *oh*.

"Keep moving," I said, to everyone, including myself.

At the bottom was the hall proper, and in the hall was the Broker.

I had prepared for many versions of this meeting. A monster. A corpse on a throne. A bureaucracy of veiled clerks. What I got was worse: a person of indeterminate age and impeccable calm, seated at a desk of black glass, with the ledger open before them—a book the size of a paving stone, bound in something I chose not to examine closely—and an expression I recognized instantly, because I wear it myself at negotiations I have already won.

"Lord Veyra," the Broker said. My name in their mouth sounded like an entry being written. "Forty thousand souls' worth of army on my doorstep, and you bring twelve. Efficient. Wasteful, but efficient."

"I brought twelve," I said, "because armies are for taking things from people who can die. You strike me as a different category of problem."

"Flattery by taxonomy." The Broker turned a page. The sound of it went through the whole cathedral; every lantern-soul flickered in unison, like a congregation flinching. "State your business. You've walked a long way through the cold to do it, and the mountain charges by the hour."

"Simple. I want the ledger."

"No."

"You haven't heard my offer."

"There is no offer," the Broker said, with the mildness of a tide, "because the ledger is not mine to sell. That is the entire point of a ledger, Lord Veyra. It belongs to the debts. I am merely the shelf." They looked up, then, and their eyes were the exact color of the amber light, and I understood that I was being *read*—that somewhere in that monstrous book, a fresh line was composing itself about me, in real time, describing the exact temperature of my ambition. "You want leverage over every living debtor in the realm. Very well. Here is mine over you: you are missing something. Something small and warm. You noticed its absence on the wolf-road, three nights ago, and you decided not to think about it further. Shall I tell you its name?"

The hall was very quiet. Twelve men behind me, none of them breathing.

"No," I said.

"Then we understand each other," said the Broker, and closed the ledger, and the sound of the closing was the sound of every door in the world shutting at once.

And that should have been the end of it—me, outmaneuvered by furniture, escorted upward by courtesy, defeated by a filing system. Except that as the Broker closed the book, two things happened in the same instant, and I have learned to trust instants like that.

First: high above us, far up through the glass roots, a commotion—shouting, steel, the crash of my own vanguard forcing the threshold I had been too prudent to force. Someone had disobeyed orders. Someone had seen the lights and decided the lights were loot.

Second: the ledger, closed, hummed. And in the humming I heard—faintly, unmistakably—my own name, being added somewhere near the front, in fresh ink, under a heading I couldn't read but whose shape I recognized from a hundred conquered chancelleries:

the heading for *assets seized*.

Someone else was writing in the Broker's book. Someone had been writing in it for days. And down one of those branching corridors of glass, I heard a soldier's whoop and the sound of a soul-cell cracking open like an egg, and I thought, with the peculiar clarity that visits me only in the presence of my own errors:

*The Taster is already here.*

I did not know his face. I did not know his name. I knew only what the prophecy-fragments said, what my spies had died relaying: a taster of poisons, ninety-one immunities deep, climbing the wolf-road with a god at his heel, coming to reclaim what his family sold. Coming, in short, to make a withdrawal from the very account I intended to seize.

I drew my sword—not against the Broker, who had already stopped considering me, but toward the noise, toward the crack of breaking glass, toward the future arriving ahead of schedule.

"Hesh," I said. "Change of plan. We're not robbing the vault."

"My lord?"

"We're racing the thief."
