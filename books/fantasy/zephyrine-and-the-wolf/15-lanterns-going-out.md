## 15. Lanterns Going Out

I had always assumed that if soldiers ever came for me, they would come politely. Poisoners, tasters, ledger-keepers—we are the furniture of courts. You do not kick the furniture until you have finished with the room.

The glass roots of the mountain court lit red before I heard a single boot.

They had been singing down there, the souls. I don't know what else to call it—a sound like wind through wineglasses, each lantern humming its own small note, and the Broker's cathedral of roots carrying all those notes up through the dark until the whole mountain felt like a struck bell. Zephyrine had gone still at the far end of the aisle, ears flat, and when a god goes still you pay attention.

"What is it?" Sable asked. She had her courier's knife out, which was optimistic. It was a very nice knife. It would have been useful against maybe a goose.

"The road," Zephyrine said, "has stopped eating names." His voice does something to the air when he is displeased, makes it heavier, the way a cellar is heavier than a street. "It only stops when someone has made it unnecessary. When an army comes with fire enough that it need not bargain."

"Veyra," I said.

"Veyra," he agreed, and did not sound surprised, which meant he had known, which meant gods are exactly as useless as the stories promise.

The first tremor came up through the floor of glass root like a swallowed shout. Dust fell from nowhere. Somewhere above us—or below; directions are negotiable in a cathedral grown downward—the great doors of the court, doors I had not been able to see on our arrival because they were made of the same dark as the mountain, began to open. Slowly. With enormous reluctance. Even the mountain, it seemed, knew better.

"They cannot fight," I said to the Broker's empty throne. The Broker was elsewhere—summoned upward, or sideways, by the intrusion, leaving me alone with his arithmetic and eleven thousand hanging souls and one particular lantern that had been pulling at my chest since we arrived, the way a tide pulls at a stubborn man's ankles.

Eleven thousand souls. I want that number in your head the way it got into mine, because I stood under them for what felt like a long time while the doors opened, and I counted the way I count toxins, clinically, because counting is how I keep from screaming. Eleven thousand little lights, each one a person who had been sold—by debt, by desperation, by parents, by kings—and each one warm. Each one dreaming. Mine had been among them since before I drew breath, hanging in the third row off the central root, glowing the color of a candle seen through honey, and when I had first touched its glass it had turned toward my hand like a sunflower turning toward morning.

*Still warm,* I had thought. *Still mine. Still dreaming whatever I might have dreamed.*

Now the doors were open, and men were coming through with iron nets.

* * *

You should understand something about Veyra's soldiers, which is that they were not monsters. This is the part the songs will get wrong, if any songs survive to be sung. They were tired men in gray wool with requisition orders, and their officer read his aloud in the flat voice of a customs clerk: *By authority of Lord Veyra, Protector of the Realm, these assets are hereby seized for the security of the state.*

Assets. Eleven thousand souls, called assets, by a man whose own soul I could have named the price of, had I his ledger entry, which I suddenly very much wanted.

The nets were iron, and iron is old magic, older than the Broker, and where the nets passed the lanterns went out. Not dimmed. Out. Like snuffed candles, like closed eyes, and each one made a sound as it died—not a scream, nothing so honest. A sigh. The sigh of something that had been holding hope the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room, carefully, carefully, and had finally been jostled.

"No," said Sable, beside me, in the voice of someone watching a warehouse burn full of living things.

"Stop," I said, louder, walking out into the aisle with my hands up, because I am a fool, because I have spent thirty-one years swallowing poison so other people could eat dinner and apparently I have learned nothing about self-preservation except how to perform its opposite. "Those aren't assets. Those are people. I can show you the entries—every one has a name, a date, a price. There's a woman down there who sold her shadow for her son's fever. There's a boy who sold six years. They have names."

The officer looked at me with the weary courtesy of a man being lectured by furniture. "Names aren't currency, master taster."

"No," I agreed. "But they're what currency is *for.*"

He almost smiled at that—I saw it start and die—and then behind him another net went up, thrown with a fisherman's easy grace, and four more lanterns sighed out, and the officer said, "Secure the central rows first. His lordship wants the old stock."

The old stock. The century-old souls. The ones sold in bargains like my family's.

Zephyrine moved.

I have seen the wolf-god angry twice now, and both times the air changed temperature. He did not roar—that would have been too simple, too honest for what he is. He simply walked forward down the central aisle, and the soldiers found that their legs had opinions their officers had not authorized, and the iron nets in their hands grew heavy as sin, because some things remember being hunted even if the hunters forget.

"You will not," Zephyrine said, quietly, in a register I felt in my back teeth, "take what hangs beneath my road."

And for a moment—one whole held breath—it worked. Men stopped. Nets lowered. The officer's face performed several complicated calculations involving his requisition orders and the size of the animal in front of him and arrived at no comfortable total.

Then the second wave came through the doors, the wave Veyra had surely always intended to send, the one that follows the polite wave, and these carried torches and long hooks, and they were afraid, and afraid men with hooks are the most dangerous animal there is.

The cathedral went to ruin around us in the space of ten heartbeats.

I will not narrate all of it. Some of it I am glad to have blurred. Lanterns torn down by hooks and stuffed into iron cages, souls handled like coin at a market stall—counted, bitten, bagged. Sable fighting like the courier she was, all elbows and economy, keeping three soldiers off my back without once asking whether I deserved it. Zephyrine a gray blur at the edge of vision, doing what a bound god can do, which is less than you'd hope and more than they expected. And everywhere the sighing, the lights going out row by row, the bell-note of the cathedral breaking apart into discord.

And me? I ran to the third row off the central root.

Because here is the thing about panic, and I speak as an expert: it clarifies. Thirty-one years of tasting other people's food, cataloguing other people's dangers, dying nightly in small professional increments so that a queen could eat pheasant in peace—and in the one moment when everything I had come for hung within reach and the world was ending efficiently around it, I did not think of prophecy, or the free cities, or Veyra's ledger, or the toll I owed the wolf. I thought, with a clarity that shames me still and exalts me too:

*Mine. That one is mine. And I have never once in my life kept anything.*

* * *

The lantern was swinging on its root from the concussion of boots and hooks, throwing frantic shadows, and the glass was hot under my hands—hot, alive, warmer than any lamp has a right to be, and inside it something moved the way a sleeper moves when you touch their shoulder. Turning over. Waking.

"Coral Delacroix."

That was the officer, somewhere behind me, shouting over the din, "Step away from the asset!"

I did not step away from the asset.

Instead I did the thing the Broker had told me could not be done without contract, without witness, without arithmetic—I pulled. Not with my arms. With whatever it is that has been doing my tasting all these years, the thing behind my tongue, the self that has stood every night at the edge of death and stepped back, and stepped back, and stepped back, ninety-one times, building a wall of immunities between itself and the dark. I put my hands on the glass and I pulled, the way you pull a splinter, the way you pull a drowning man, and I said—out loud, like an idiot, like a prayer:

"I know you. I'm sorry. I'm so late. Come home."

The glass cracked.

Not broke—cracked, a single line running around its equator like the seam of an egg, and through the crack came light, not lantern-light but the light of the thing itself, and it poured over my hands and up my arms and it—

All right. Here is where wry narration fails me, and I ask you to forgive the failure, because I have written eleven novels and never once had to describe this.

My soul recognized me.

Do you understand what that means? It means there is a thing in the world that is me—not my name, which is a label; not my body, which is a vehicle; not my habits, which are a costume—but *me*, the original, the referent, the dreamer of all the dreams I thought I was dreaming—and it has been hanging in a cold cathedral for thirty-one years, warm, waiting, and when I touched it, it knew my hands. It turned toward me the way you turn toward the door when someone you have mourned walks in alive.

It wept light. Down my wrists, into my sleeves, pooling at my feet, and every drop that touched me filled in something I had not known was missing. Colors got one shade deeper. The din of battle acquired bass notes I hadn't heard. I could smell the iron of the nets and underneath it the mineral sweat of the mountain and underneath *that* a green smell, impossibly, the smell of the surface in spring, my mother's garden—

My mother's garden. I remembered my mother's garden, which the wolf-road had taken from me two days ago as toll, and it came back not as memory but as fact, as ground underfoot, and I understood that a soul is where memories live, and I had been a tenant in my own house.

"Oh," I said. Very eloquent. Thirty-one years of vocabulary and all I had was *oh.*

The lantern came free of the root with a sound like a held breath released, and I held it against my chest and it fit, it *fit*, the way nothing has ever fit, the way no queen's approval or guildmaster's praise or lover's arm ever quite fit, because all of those were shaped for the man I performed being, and this was shaped for the one I actually was.

The crack widened. Light everywhere now, pouring through me instead of onto me, and I could feel the last seam thinning, feel the moment coming when the shell would fall away entirely and whatever was inside would come home to wherever it had always belonged.

Which is precisely when the hook took the root above my head, and the ceiling of glass root came down in a bright, ringing rain.

* * *

Sable saved my life again. I mention it because she will never let me forget it, so we may as well establish the record early. She hit me full in the side, took us both behind the buttress of a root thick as a tower, and the falling glass sheeted past us and shattered on the floor into a drift of dying sparks, and through the drift came boots.

"Is that—" she started, and then looked at what I was holding, and stopped. Her face did something I had never seen it do. Sable has a face for doors and a face for ledgers and a face for lying to gate guards, and none of them prepared her for standing three feet from a visible soul. "Coral. That's *you.*"

"It's me," I agreed. My voice sounded different. Everything sounded different; I was hearing it with equipment I'd just been reunited with. "It's been me the whole time. It was just… elsewhere."

Around the buttress, the seizure continued. Two soldiers dragged a cage past, and inside it a dozen lanterns had gone dark, truly dark, and one of them flickered once as it passed me—reaching, maybe, the way a plant reaches toward a window—and I felt it, felt *him*, a cooper from Marrow's Reach who'd sold nine years to pay a grain debt, and I felt him go out, and I will carry that feeling for exactly as long as I live, which as of tonight is a finite and newly negotiable quantity.

"We have to go," Sable said. "The Broker's returned—he's at the great seal, and Veyra's with him, and Coral, the *ledger*, they're moving the ledger—"

"I know what we have to do." I looked down at the lantern in my arms. The crack had gone quiet, the light steadied, and inside, something was looking back at me. Patient. It had been patient for thirty-one years; it could manage another hour, and it seemed to be telling me so, the way you'd soothe someone else. My own soul comforting me. There's a joke in there about my childhood, but I'm too tired and too full of light to make it.

The last thing, though. Before we ran for the seal, before Zephyrine's howl split the cathedral and the true chaos began—there was one moment I want set down properly, because chapters like this one tend to skip the good parts in favor of the burning.

A soldier found us. Young. Gray wool, torch in hand, close enough that I could see a scab on his knuckle and a cheap devotion-charm at his throat. He raised his torch and then his eyes found the lantern, found the light pouring out of the crack, found my face inside that light, and he stopped.

Because everyone looks like themselves, when you hold your own soul. That's the secret. All my life I had looked like a man waiting for permission to exist, and now, apparently, I looked like something else, and the soldier saw it, and his torch dipped.

"That's mine," I told him. Not a threat. Just inventory, honestly reported—the way I'd report arsenic in the soup. "It was taken before I could say no. Whatever they're paying you, it isn't enough to stand between a man and the only thing he's ever owned."

He looked at me a long moment. Then he turned around and walked the other way, and I choose to believe he is alive somewhere, because I need to believe the world keeps a few of the ones who walk the other way.

"Come on," Sable said, and pulled me up, and we ran—me cradling my own soul like a child, like contraband, like the last dish of the night, the one I finally got to keep—toward the great seal, where a usurper and a broker sat down over the debts of eleven thousand souls.

Behind us, row by row, the lanterns were going out.

Ahead of us, Zephyrine turned, and his eyes found the lantern in my arms, and then found me, and what I saw in them was not relief.

It was arithmetic.

"Toll comes due, taster," the wolf said. "Now. Before the shell breaks. You will not survive paying it *after* you're whole."
