## 16. The Toll Comes Due

I had always imagined that if death came for me, it would arrive the way it did at the royal table: politely, in a sauce, wearing its best manners. I would taste it, catalogue it, nod to the Queen that the pheasant was safe, and go home to my ledgers with another evening's arithmetic settled. Death, in my experience, was a bureaucrat. It liked forms filled out in triplicate.

What I had not imagined was paying for my own soul while the ceiling of a glass-rooted cathedral rained down around me, a god's teeth at my throat, and ninety-one poisons demanding an audience all at once.

"Coral." Zephyrine's voice was not a voice so much as a pressure behind the eyes, the way thunder is not a sound but a decision. His breath fogged the lantern-light of the Broker's court, and somewhere below us Veyra's soldiers were prying souls off their hooks like apples from a market stall. "The road is closing. Pay now or stay."

"Stay," I said, "being the option where we all die."

"Being the option where *you* die slowly, which you've had practice at." The wolf's amber eye filled half my vision. In it I could see myself reflected, small and singed and clutching a lantern that wept light down my wrists—my soul, warm as bread, humming against my chest like something that remembered being mine. "You bargained in the snowline. Ninety-one immunities, surrendered together. I am here to collect."

"I know what I signed."

"Do you?" And there was, horribly, gentleness in it. "Then begin counting."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about immunity: it is not armor. It is debt. Every night I swallowed poison so the Queen could eat, and every morning my body wrote the receipt—a little callus of survival, a small stone wall built cell by cell against aconite, arsenic, the grey cap mushroom, the lovely blue-ringed octopus venom some southern prince once sent as a diplomatic insult. Ninety-one dishes over eleven years. Ninety-one walls.

Zephyrine's toll was demolition. All walls, all at once, with me inside the house.

It began, absurdly, with the first dish. Dish one: white snakeroot, aged eleven years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of tallow and fear, when I was nineteen and shaking so badly the guildmaster had to hold my wrist steady while I drank. I felt it again—not the memory of it, the *it*. The milk-sick bitterness blooming on my tongue, the cold climbing my arms, my heart forgetting, briefly, what rhythm meant. Eleven years of tolerance unwound in four seconds, and I was nineteen again, dying again, except now there was no guildmaster's hand, only snow and firelight and a wolf watching with the patience of geology.

"That's one," Zephyrine said.

"You said—" My voice cracked. "You said surrendered at once. Not one at a time."

"At once," he agreed, "means all of them live in the same moment. I never said the moment would be short."

Dish two. Dish three. The grey caps, which I had survived by vomiting into a hedgerow while a duchess complimented my discretion; the vomit arrived on schedule, undignified as ever, and this time there was no hedgerow, only the glass roots of the Broker's court shattering around us as the mountain shook under Veyra's siege-engines. Dish seven, hemlock, and my legs went to water. Dish twelve, lead sugar, sweet and slow, and I tasted it the way I'd tasted it at twenty-three: like pennies and forgiveness.

I want to be clear about something, because I have kept ledgers my whole life and I know how numbers lie: ninety-one is not a large number. It fits on one page. But each dish contained its own weather, its own hour of dying, and when you stack ninety-one hours of dying into a single candle-length of time, you learn what the word *at once* actually weighs.

By dish thirty I had stopped standing. By dish forty I had stopped being sure I had ever stood, or that standing was a real thing bodies did, or that I was a body. The lantern of my soul pressed against my ribs, warm, insistent, and I understood suddenly why the sold souls hung in the Broker's court still dreaming—they were keeping themselves company. Someone has to, in there.

"Coral." Sable's voice, far away, from the direction of the collapsing gate. She'd come back for me. Of course she had; I'd have fired her for it. "Coral, his eyes are doing the thing—"

"The thing," Zephyrine confirmed.

"Don't—" I got a hand up. It weighed as much as a cathedral. "Don't stop. If you stop I'll have paid for nothing and I will haunt you, I know how haunting works, I've read the contracts—"

"Sixty-two," said the wolf, and did not stop.

* * *

Somewhere around dish seventy-four—the scorpion wine, Lys, nine years ago—I stopped narrating my own death and started listening to it.

This is what I heard. Underneath the convulsions, underneath the sweat freezing on my back in the mountain cold, underneath Sable shouting something about soldiers on the stair: a silence. The particular silence of a ledger after the final entry. For eleven years I had been two men—the taster who died nightly in miniature and the clerk who recorded other people's impossible trades—and I had told myself the arrangement was fair. I keep accounts. The accounts keep me. Everyone pays someone.

But lying in the ruin of the Broker's court with my own soul hammering at my chest like a bird that knows the window is open, I finally read the entry honestly. *One man, protected against everything, belonging to nothing.* Ninety-one immunities were not walls. They were the mortgage. Every night I swallowed poison so the Queen might eat, yes—but also so that I would be too valuable, too irreplaceable, too *necessary* to lose. Immunity was the leash they'd braided out of my own courage. And I had held it out to a wolf-god and called it freedom's price, because even then, even in the snowline, part of me thought: surely I can survive the payment too. Surely I'll find the loophole. Surely the ledger will forgive me.

There are no loopholes. There is only the arithmetic, and the arithmetic says: everything.

"Dish eighty-eight," Zephyrine said, and his muzzle was close now, his fur wet with my sweat. "Aconite. Your first true killer. Do you remember what you promised yourself afterward?"

I did. I remembered lying on the flagstones of the palace kitchen at midnight, heart stuttering like a moth in a jar, promising myself that if I lived I would never again let anyone else decide what my life was worth. I had broken that promise within the week, because promises are cheap and rent is due. But the promise itself—it was the truest thing I owned. Truer than my name, which the road had eaten a toll of. Truer than my face, which Sable was looking at now like she wasn't sure it was mine.

"Aconite," I whispered. "Last one?"

"Ninety-one is last. Eighty-nine is nightshade. You'll want to bite down on something."

"What?"

"Anything you don't need later."

I bit down on the word *willing*, the one I'd never managed to write in my contract, and the nightshade took me.

* * *

Dying, it turns out, is mostly bookkeeping. Somewhere past dish ninety I found myself in the white space between entries, where the ink hasn't dried and the page is still deciding. And there—of course there—was my handwriting. The hand I'd known since before I could read it, dated before I was born: *One soul, sold, court beneath the mountain.* Except now there was a second line beneath it, fresh, in the same impossible hand:

*One soul, reclaimed. Payment rendered: ninety-one immunities. Balance: zero.*

Zero. I turned the word over like a coin I didn't recognize. All my life my worth had been calculated in what I could endure. Now the column stood empty. No tolerances. No protections. A single cup of bad wine could end me, and everyone in the free cities knew my name, and half of them wanted to crown me and the other half wanted to open my veins to see if the prophecy would bleed.

And the strange thing—the thing I will defend until an actual cup of wine does end me—is that in that white space, holding my balance of zero, I felt *rich*. Because for the first time, everything left in my account belonged to me.

"No," I said, to the white space, to the ledger, to the wolf waiting at the edge of it. "I'm not done."

"You are very nearly done," Zephyrine said. "That's rather the point."

"I refuse."

Something moved in the god's face then. Not surprise—wolves do not surprise—but recognition, the look of an ancient creature seeing an old pattern walk upright. "Refusal is not among your remaining assets, Coral Delacroix."

"Then take it as a gift." I hauled myself up the inside of my own dying, hand over hand, toward the warmth hammering in my chest. "You wanted surrender. Here's the difference nobody writes down: surrender is done *to* you. This—" I put my hand flat against my sternum, against the lantern-light bleeding through my fingers, "—this I'm doing. On purpose. Count it however you like in your books. But I'm walking out of this page."

For one long moment the white space held its breath.

Then Zephyrine laughed—a sound like a glacier changing its mind—and the world came back all at once: smoke, screaming, the cathedral of glass roots folding inward like a fist, Sable's hands under my shoulders hauling me up, my soul slamming home behind my ribs with a click I felt in my teeth.

"Ninety-one," the wolf said. "Paid in full. Now run, taster. The Broker's court is coming down, and Veyra's men have found the ledger vault—and you, I think, will want to see what they take."

I ran. Mortal, unprotected, every poison in creation welcome to try me—and lighter than I had ever been in my life, running through falling glass with death finally, honestly invited, and refusing, for tonight, to collect.

Behind us, the vault doors opened. And Veyra's soldiers began carrying out the ledger of every soul-debt in the realm, page by burning page.
