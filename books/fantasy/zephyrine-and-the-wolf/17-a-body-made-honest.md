## 17. A Body Made Honest

I have carried a lot of things for the Guild of Impossible Things. Stolen shadows in salt boxes. Three borrowed years in a jar that hummed when you lied to it. Once, a name so heavy I had to walk uphill backwards to keep it from rolling out of my pack.

I had never carried a man.

"Put me down," Coral said, which was ambitious of him, considering his mouth was the only part of him still taking orders.

"Not a chance."

"Sable. The passage is narrowing. You'll—"

"I'll what, bang your elbow? Forgive me if my concern for your elbows has lapsed, given that everything else about you appears to be actively dying." I shifted him higher against my shoulder. He weighed nothing, which frightened me more than the shaking did. Couriers learn weights. A sealed letter, half a pound. A soul-lantern, whatever it wants to be. Coral Delacroix, who two days ago could have drunk a cellar of arsenic and complained only about the vintage, now weighed less than my grandmother's soup pot, and my grandmother's soup pot never once convulsed in my arms.

Behind us, the mountain court came down in stages, like an argument being lost room by room. Glass roots shattered with a sound I can only describe as bells grieving. Light poured out of the broken lanterns—not the warm dreaming light of souls at rest but a thin, panicked white, the light of things being evicted—and it ran along the tunnel walls ahead of us like water finding a drain.

Every lantern we passed that still hung, I touched. It's a courier's habit: acknowledge the cargo. Most were already dark or dimming, seized and stuffed into Veyra's lead-lined coffers like coins into a miser's purse. But one flickered as my fingers brushed the glass, and I said, quietly, "Hold on," though I don't know what I meant it toward. The souls. The mountain. Myself.

"Hold on," Coral echoed, delirious, and then his body tried to turn itself inside out around all ninety-one poisons at once, and I stopped wasting breath on encouragement and started spending it on distance.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about Coral Delacroix before that night, because it explains why carrying him felt like such a professional failure.

Everyone at the guild knew Coral. He kept our ledgers. He knew what everyone had traded, what everyone owed, what everyone feared losing—which is a dangerous kind of knowledge to concentrate in one mild, gray-eyed man who brought honey cakes on saints' days. I'd assumed the tasting was a rumor. Court tasters are supposed to be sleek and self-important, all liveried menace. Coral was the sort of person who apologized to furniture.

What I hadn't known—what nobody knew—was the arithmetic underneath. Ninety-one dishes. Ninety-one slow poisonings, absorbed one careful drop at a time, building immunity the way other men build savings. He had made himself unkillable at the table by dying a little every night for years. When he told me this, on the wolf-road, in the flat voice of a man reading his own inventory, I laughed. I thought it was a joke. He let me laugh. That should have told me something about him.

And then Zephyrine took it. All of it. Every immunity, surrendered at once, because that was the toll, because gods do not make change.

So now the most poisoned man in the free cities was, for the first time since his twelfth year, entirely ordinary flesh. A body made honest, Zephyrine called it, in that voice like a door opening onto winter. I wanted to ask the wolf what was dishonest about surviving. I didn't. Gods answer questions the way avalanches answer invitations.

The passage narrowed. I turned sideways, shielding Coral's head with my arm, and felt the mountain shudder through the rock like a held note.

"Coral. Stay with me. Name something."

"What?"

"Anything. Name anything. It keeps you in the world." This is true, by the way. Guild training. Things that are named resist being lost. It's why the usurper unmade our guildmaster by erasing his name first—it's always the name first, then the man, like unwriting a letter from the top down.

"The third shelf," Coral mumbled. "Of the ledger room. Second ledger from the left is upside down. Has been for six years. Nobody noticed but me."

"Wonderful. Keep going."

"There's a loose stone under the west window where I keep—" a spasm took him, and when it released him he finished, smaller, "—where I used to keep honey cakes. For after. In case a night went well."

"Did they ever go well?"

"Once or twice." His eyes found mine, unfocused, fever-bright. "Sable. I can taste it."

"Taste what?"

"The air. Just—the air. It tastes like iron and snow and it's *horrible*. I've never tasted anything just—" another convulsion bent him, and I braced against the wall and held on, "—just once. Everything used to be muted. Everything was always already paid for. Now it's all—" he made a sound that might have been a laugh or its opposite, "—it's all *loud*."

That was when I understood, in the way you understand things in tunnels, all at once and uselessly: he wasn't dying of the poisons leaving. Or not only. He was dying of arrival. Ninety-one flavors hitting a tongue that had spent a decade behind glass.

"Then be loud back," I said, which is terrible advice and the only kind I give.

* * *

We came out of the mountain through a servant's postern that Zephyrine had torn open for us—one gesture of one enormous paw, stone shearing away like paper, the wolf-god standing in the snowfall with blood on his jaws and something almost gentle in the way he looked at what was left of Coral. Almost. With Zephyrine you take what's on offer.

"He must be warm," the wolf had said. "Cold finishes what pain starts. Run south until the trees change. Then run further."

"You could carry him," I said.

"No." And that single syllable had the weight of a century behind it, so I stopped asking and started running.

The snowline gave way to scree, scree to black pines, pines to the first stubborn birches, exactly as promised. I counted them the way you count anything when your arms are full of a dying friend—to keep the world linear. Forty-one trees before I let myself stop.

There was a shepherd's hut, abandoned, half-swallowed by the hillside. I got us inside. I got a fire going with hands I pretended weren't shaking. I stripped off his ruined coat—he'd been dressed for a court beneath a mountain, silk and ceremony, and the ceremony had all leaked out of it—and wrapped him in every blanket the hut offered, which was three, all of them smelling of goat and decades.

He seized twice more while the fire caught. The second time was bad. His back arched, his jaw locked, and a sound came out of him that I will not describe here because I've decided some things don't get written down, even by couriers, even in wry first-person accounts. I held his head so he wouldn't crack it on the hearthstone, and I talked to him the whole time, steady, vile, funny—guild gossip, mostly, who was embezzling what, which client had tried to pay us in cursed buttons—and when it passed he lay gasping in the firelight like a man newly invented.

"Am I dying?" he asked. Genuinely asking. Clinical. Even now, cataloguing himself.

"Yes," I said. "Probably slowly. Possibly not. Hold still."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not a comfort. I'm a courier. I deliver things intact. You're the parcel."

His mouth twitched. Good. A twitch is a vital sign.

* * *

Here is the thing about mortality that nobody tells you, because the people who know it are dead and the people who don't are lying: it makes a person *precious*. Not precious like jewelry. Precious like a full cup carried across a crowded room.

All my life I'd moved through the world among people who couldn't easily die. The guild traded in years; the court marinated in protections; the cities ran on charms and countercharms and the quiet assumption that someone else would absorb the harm. Poison-tasters. Debt-sponges. Shadow-doubles. We had built a civilization out of finding cheaper things than ourselves to break.

Coral had been the cheapest thing of all, in a way. Ninety-one immunities thick. You couldn't kill him with dinner. You could barely inconvenience him with dinner. And I think—I look back and I think—that thickness had made everyone, including me, slightly careless with him. He was safe, so he could be spent. He would taste the dish, so the Queen need never fear. The whole architecture of his life was built on the assumption that he was not, in any meaningful sense, touchable.

Now he lay in a shepherd's hut breathing in shallow, expensive increments, and I discovered that a single cup of wine could end him. Any cup. Someone's careless vintage. A wedding toast. Water gone wrong in a bad well. The entire world had become a dish, and there was no one left between Coral and it.

You would think that would make me want to wrap him in wool forever, and it did, and I'm not proud of how much it did. But underneath the tenderness was something fiercer and stranger: anger. Because I'd seen the lanterns. I'd seen his soul hanging in that glass cathedral—still warm, still dreaming, dreaming *him*, some small bright version of Coral that had never once swallowed poison for anyone. His parents sold that before he was born. His queen spent his body nightly without ever once asking what the protection cost. The prophecy called him savior or ruin, as if he were weather, as if he were a coin changing hands rather than a man who liked honey cakes and noticed upside-down ledgers.

Everyone had handled Coral Delacroix as if he were not fragile.

He had always been fragile. That was the whole point of the sale. Fragile things get sold by the people meant to protect them, and the selling gets called survival, and the child grows up learning to call his own destruction *duty*.

"Stop thinking so loudly," Coral murmured. "I can hear it."

"Liar. You're half dead."

"Half dead is still more perceptive than most living people." He turned his head. The firelight moved on his face and I saw how the bones stood out now, how the gray had gone out of his complexion and left something almost translucent, almost young. "Sable. If I die tonight—"

"You won't."

"If I die tonight, the ledger—Veyra has the ledger. The debts. Everyone's souls, itemized. You have to understand what that means better than I do. You've carried the ledgers. You know what a list does to a person when the wrong hands hold it."

"I know," I said, because I did. A list is a leash with everyone's name on it. Veyra didn't want thrones. Thrones are decoration. He wanted the arithmetic underneath every life in the realm, and now he had it, and somewhere behind us the mountain was still collapsing around his soldiers and their leaden coffers, and I did not see how that ended in anything but a kingdom of puppets with their strings filed neatly by hand.

"So if I die," Coral said, with the patience of a man drafting a contract in his head, "you take the last thing I own and you spend it badly and gloriously. Promise me. Ruin his arithmetic somehow."

"What's the last thing you own?"

"My soul. What's left of it. It's in my chest now, Sable. Did you know that? Souls, reclaimed—they come home. It beats in here like it's trying to make up for eighty years of vacancy." He pressed his hand flat against his sternum, wonder-struck, terrified, alive. "It's so loud."

"Everything's loud now. You said."

"Everything's loud now," he agreed, and smiled, and then his eyes closed—not the closing of seizure but the closing of exhaustion, which is survivable, which is human, which I checked three times over the next hour by holding a polished spoon under his nose and watching it fog.

Fogging spoons. That was my new profession: monitoring the condensation of a novelist-poisoner in a stolen hut while the world's soul-ledger changed owners. My mother wanted me to go into haberdashery.

* * *

Near dawn, Zephyrine came.

I heard nothing—wolves that size don't announce themselves—but the fire leaned sideways and there he was in the doorway, too large for it, snow in his pelt, amber eyes catching the light like coals that had opinions. He looked at Coral for a long moment. Whatever he scented seemed to satisfy him.

"He lives," the wolf said.

"He lives," I said. "Barely. Loudly."

"That is the correct way." Zephyrine lowered himself onto the floor, and the hut groaned but held, and for a while none of us spoke. Snow ticked against the shutters. Coral breathed. Each breath was a small insolent act, and I found I could watch them indefinitely.

"Why him?" I asked finally. I'm a courier; I ask. "Out of everyone the prophecy could have chewed up. Why the taster?"

Zephyrine was silent long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Because a hundred years ago I watched his grandparents' grandparents sell an unborn child's soul to buy a winter's grain, and I swore to witness the undoing of that bargain, and witnessing, little bird, is a chain heavier than iron. I did not choose him. The debt chose him. I merely chose not to look away." His gaze slid to me, and being looked at by Zephyrine is like being audited by winter. "Neither did you."

"I didn't choose anything. He's a parcel. I deliver parcels."

"Mm." The wolf settled his chin on his paws, which somehow made him no less enormous. "Then deliver him carefully, courier. The road ahead eats names, and his is written in more books than he knows. Veyra holds the ledger now. And a man who owns every debt in the realm will soon learn that debts have teeth, and that the oldest debt in that book is not Coral's." Something moved behind those amber eyes—amusement, or memory, or hunger; with a god you pays your coin and takes no forecasting. "It is mine."

I wrapped my blanket tighter and decided, provisionally, not to ask.

Behind me, in his nest of goat-smelling wool, Coral stirred and said, thick with sleep and fever and something that sounded almost like peace, "the second ledger from the left... tell someone... it's upside down..."

And outside, far to the north, where the mountain court was still bleeding light into the ruins of itself, something began—a sound like ten thousand contracts tearing at once, or like a kingdom discovering, page by page, that it had been bought and sold and that the buyer had made one mistake:

he hadn't read the fine print either.
