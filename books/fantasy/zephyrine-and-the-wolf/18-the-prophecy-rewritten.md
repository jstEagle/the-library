## 18. The Prophecy Rewritten

I had spent my whole life learning to taste things I could not see. Bitterness under honey. Hemlock masked by rosewater. The faint almond ghost of cyanide dressed up as a dessert course. It was, I thought, the one skill the world could not take from me—not even after ninety-one immunities surrendered in a single convulsing hour on the mountainside, not even now, when a single cup of wine could end me as neatly as snuffing a candle.

So when Sable pressed the prophecy scroll back into my hands—rescued from the guildhall's ruins, singed at one corner, smelling of ash and panic—and said, "Read it again, Coral. Properly this time," I read it the way I tasted: slowly, cataloguing everything.

We were three days down from the mountain, sheltering in the burned shell of a wayhouse that some nameless traveler had abandoned years ago. My body still remembered every poison it had ever swallowed. That was the cruel arithmetic of Zephyrine's toll—he had taken away my *immunity*, not my memory. Each night since, my skin had rehearsed its own history: the sweat of arsenic, the itch of mercury, the slow cold crawl of nightshade reenacting its debut beneath my sternum. Mortality, it turned out, was not an absence. It was an audience seat to every performance I'd ever slept through.

Sable had built a fire in the hearth despite the season, because she said I looked like a man who needed to watch something burn that wasn't us. I loved her for that, though I told her only that the smoke would mask our scent from Veyra's outriders, which was also true. Sable tolerated my habit of converting tenderness into tactics. She'd been a courier for the Guild of Impossible Things; she knew all about carrying things whose value depended on nobody understanding them.

The scroll unrolled across my knees, and I read.

*In the hundredth year of the broken crown, the Taster shall rise, who eats the realm's poison so the realm need not. He shall hold death in his mouth nightly and live. In his hand, two things: the ledger of debts and the soul once sold beneath the mountain. Whichever he keeps, the free cities keep. Whichever he loses, the free cities lose.*

That was how I'd always read it. I knew it nearly by heart—I'd authenticated it for a scholar-client in another life, before the guildmaster was unmade, before heralds could erase a man into a shape of air. Back then I had read it with the particular terror of a man who discovers himself described in someone else's handwriting. *The Taster.* Me, down to the date. I had felt seen the way a rabbit feels seen: as something hunted.

But tonight, with a mortal body and a reclaimed soul humming in my chest like a struck bell that wouldn't quite stop ringing, I read it differently.

"Say the middle line again," Sable said. She was mending a cloak with the grim competence of someone who expected to need it for running.

"*Whichever he keeps, the free cities keep,*" I recited. "*Whichever he loses—*"

"No." She didn't look up from her needle. "Before that."

"*He shall hold death in his mouth nightly and live.*"

"Not that either. The part about his hands."

I looked down. *In his hand, two things.*

"Huh," I said.

"You're a ledger-keeper," Sable said. "You've spent your life catching what other people miss in contracts. Read it like a contract."

So I did. I made myself stop being the frightened subject of the prophecy and become its auditor. And once I did, the whole document turned inside out like a glove.

Every translation I had ever heard—and there had been songs, gods help us, songs sung in taverns in the free cities already, garbled versions where the Taster was a sword-arm or a saint—every version assumed the prophecy named a hero. A person who would *do* something. Save or doom, crown or kill, stand at the walls of the free cities holding back the dark through sheer personal virtue.

But prophecies written by soul-brokers are never biographies. They are *receipts*.

"In his hand, two things," I said aloud, tasting each word for adulterants. "Not 'he shall wield.' Not 'he shall choose.' Not 'he shall be brave.'" I tapped the vellum. "It's an inventory, Sable. It doesn't describe a savior. It describes *collateral*."

Sable's needle stopped. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the Taster isn't the protagonist of anything. He's a *location*. A place where two objects converge." My voice came out very steady, which is how I knew I was more afraid than I had been on the mountain. "One soul, sold beneath the mountain—one lantern in a cathedral full of them, indistinguishable from ten thousand others except to itself. One ledger—the Broker's book, every debt in the realm, leverage over everyone alive. The prophecy doesn't say the Taster saves anyone. It says whoever ends up *holding both* decides what happens. Not because they're chosen. Because possession is nine-tenths of prophecy, and the last tenth is arithmetic."

"The free cities think you're going to save them," Sable said slowly.

"The free cities are wrong about almost everything; it's why they're free." I kept reading, and the next lines confirmed it the way a second dish confirms the first poison. *And should the two be sundered—soul without ledger, ledger without soul—neither shall avail. Only together do they weigh.* "Look. If I held just my soul, I'd be a whole man with nothing but a good story. If Veyra held just the ledger, he'd have a book of debts—but debts need their souls attached to collect on them. The ledger is the claim. The souls are the currency. Separately, they're paper and smoke. Together—" I exhaled. "Together they're the realm's title deed. Whoever holds both can call every soul-debt in existence, and every soul must answer, because the contract binds both sides."

"And Veyra has the ledger."

"Veyra has the ledger." I let that sit in the room with the smoke. "Which means the prophecy was never about me saving the free cities. It was about whoever happened to be standing at the intersection of a stolen soul and a stolen book when the century ran out. I wasn't chosen. I was *scheduled*."

Something shifted in my chest as I said it—not grief exactly, though grief was there, and not relief exactly, though relief was there too. More like the moment when a numb limb wakes up, all pins and needles and wrong angles, and you realize the ache was only circulation returning. All winter I had carried the terror of being *seen*—named in old vellum, hunted by cities, marked by couriers, weighed by wolf-gods. Every nightmare I'd had since the scholar-client laid that scroll on the guild counter had been some variation of *they know, they know, they know.*

They didn't know. They had never known me. The prophecy couldn't pick me out of a crowd. It had simply predicted where two objects would collide, the way you can predict where a dropped cup will land without caring about the cup.

There was a strange, stinging freedom in that. And underneath the freedom, something colder took root.

Because if the prophecy named whoever held both—

Then it named Veyra now.

* * *

Sable saw it hit me. She always did. She set down the mending and said, "Out with it."

"If the Taster is defined by possession," I said, "then I lost the title the moment those soldiers pried the ledger out of the Broker's vault. Veyra holds the book. I hold the soul. We're each half of a sentence, and halves don't weigh." I stared into the fire. "Everyone hunting me—the cities crowning me in absentia, the ones sending assassins instead—they're chasing a word that stopped meaning anything three days ago. And meanwhile Veyra is sitting on the one object that actually matters, probably assuming it's inert until he finds its missing counterpart."

"Is it inert?"

"I don't know. Maybe. Souls refuse to serve a thief—that's old law, older than the Broker, maybe older than Zephyrine. But Veyra isn't a man who needs things to *work* immediately. He's a patient collector. He'll spend months learning the ledger's grammar, testing small debts, calling little loans. And the ledger will teach him. Books like that aren't records, Sable. They're *instructors*. Every page he reads, he understands the arithmetic better. And the arithmetic only has one lesson: everyone alive owes something, and whoever holds the book owns everyone."

"So we take it back."

"We?" I raised an eyebrow. "You've seen what his soldiers do. You watched them carry souls out of that cathedral like coin-purses. You helped drag me—no small task, I was mostly soup by then—"

"I did," she said, with the flat finality of a woman balancing accounts. "Which makes me a stakeholder. You don't get to die alone in a war I've invested in."

I wanted to argue. Instead I heard myself say, "Thank you," which surprised us both, and covered it by unrolling the scroll further, to the lines I'd never been able to parse.

*The Taster shall taste true at the last, and the last tasting shall undo the first sale, and the chain of sales shall break, and no soul shall stand surety for another again.*

I had read that as destiny: the Taster would perform a great act of salvation, the last tasting, and thereby break the ancient chain by which souls had been collateral for other souls since the first bargain—a chain that went back, Zephyrine had told me on the road, to a sale older than any living memory. His own.

Now I read it as instructions.

"It's not a prediction," I said quietly. "It's a *procedure*. 'Shall' in broker-tongue doesn't mean 'inevitably.' It means 'if performed.' Like a recipe. 'The dough shall rise'—if you add the leavening. 'The debtor shall be freed'—if the terms are met." My finger moved down the vellum. "'The last tasting shall undo the first sale.' The first sale is Zephyrine. The chain-breaker has to be a willing trade that voids the arithmetic of inheritance—soul for ledger, willingly given. That's why it needed a taster specifically. Not a warrior, not a king. Someone whose entire life was built on the discipline of putting known poison in his mouth on purpose, for others' sake. Someone fluent in the exact grammar of swallowing something terrible *voluntarily*."

"You," Sable said.

"Anyone with my résumé. But yes. The prophecy never chose me. It chose a *shape*—and then it waited a hundred years to see who would grow into it. Which means—" and here my voice did something I didn't authorize, went rough at the edges, "—which means it was never fate. It was a job posting. And job postings can be answered by anyone desperate enough, or refused by anyone brave enough. There's no chain of destiny around my neck, Sable. There's a chain of *debt*, which is worse, but also—which is better. Debts can be paid. Destiny can't."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind worried at the wayhouse's broken shutters like a creditor at a door.

"You realize," she said at last, "that if you're right—if it's procedure, not prophecy—then Veyra could figure it out too. He's got scholars. He's got the original documents. Sooner or later some clerk in his train is going to read that scroll the same way you just did and realize their lord is holding half a procedure."

"He won't need a clerk." I thought of Veyra's face in the collapsing court—cold, delighted, unhurried, a man finding a library where he'd expected a treasury. "He already knows the ledger is leverage. What he doesn't know yet is that it's *incomplete* without mine—or rather, without the soul I'm carrying. But he'll learn, because the ledger will teach him, and then he'll come looking for the missing half of his deed. And he won't send soldiers next time. Soldiers are loud. He'll send *terms*. A contract. An offer I can't refuse, drafted beautifully, because that's how you collect from a man who can't refuse to read."

"That sounds like a trap."

"It is a trap. It's the best kind—the kind where the prey walks in knowing." I rolled the scroll closed and found my hands were steady. Steadier than they'd been in weeks. Funny, that: mortality had taken my immunity to poison, but it seemed to have cured me of something else. I had spent thirty-one years surviving by *not dying*, hunched perpetually over my own body like a man guarding a wallet in a thieves' market. Now the wallet was gone—or rather, I finally owned what was in it—and some frantic, exhausted vigilance had gone out of me, leaving behind something quieter and harder. Not fearlessness. Fearlessness is for people with bad imaginations. This was fear that had been *filed*. Catalogued. Assigned its place in the ledger.

"Here's what I keep arriving at," I told Sable. "The free cities are burning their maps and hunting a symbol that doesn't exist. Veyra is holding a book that will eventually tell him exactly how to own the world. And the actual mechanism of salvation is sitting in my chest, warm and dreaming, waiting for me to find the right counterparty and draft the right contract." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a coat worn for the first time. "The prophecy doesn't need me to be brave. It needs me to be *accurate*. To read the terms correctly. To make the last tasting a real trade and not a gesture. And accuracy—" I tapped my tongue against my teeth, that old clinical instrument, "—accuracy is the one thing I have never lost."

"To Veyra's camp, then," Sable said. "To offer him a trade."

"To walk into the usurper's army," I agreed, "carrying the most valuable soul in the realm in a body that can be killed by a badly washed cup. And to offer Lord Veyra everything he wants."

Sable frowned. "Everything he wants? Your soul for his ledger? Why would he—"

"Because he doesn't want what I'd actually be giving him." I fed a broken chair to the fire and watched it catch. "He thinks he wants leverage over the living. But the ledger's real function—the thing the Broker built it for, the thing Zephyrine swore to undo—isn't power. It's *perpetuity*. It exists so that no debt ever dies, so every sale chains forward forever, child paying for parent, soul standing surety for soul. Veyra thinks he's acquiring a weapon. He's actually adopting a hunger. And hungers can be fed to death, if you know what they eat."

"And what does this one eat?"

"Willingness," I said. "The one thing a thief can never counterfeit. The ledger runs on willing sales—that's its engine, that's its flaw, that's why the first sale needed Zephyrine to *consent*, and why my parents' bargain needed me pre-sold before I could object. Everything in that book is legitimate. Horrible, but legitimate. Which means it can all be *voided* legitimately. One honest trade, structured correctly, unwinds the whole arithmetic." I looked at Sable across the fire. "Veyra will accept my offer because he cannot imagine that a trade offered willingly might be a trade designed to destroy him. Thieves always believe everyone else is stealing too."

Sable studied me for a while, the way you'd study a dish you suspected had been re-seasoned.

"You've changed," she said. "On the mountain you were a man trying to survive. Now you're a man trying to *conclude* something."

"I'm a taster," I said. "I've spent my whole career identifying poison. I just finally identified the batch I was born into." I stood, joints aching with borrowed history, and began rolling the prophecy with more care than it deserved. "Pack light. We leave before dawn."

"For Veyra's camp."

"For Veyra's camp." I paused at the doorway, looking north, where the horizon still wore a faint bruise of smoke from the burning maps of the free cities. Somewhere beyond it, an army was making camp around a stolen book, and a wolf-god was shadowing the tree line, waiting for a toll he'd already named, and a ledger lay closed on a general's table like a mouth deciding whether to speak.

Let the cities hunt their symbol. Let Veyra pet his prize. The prophecy had never promised me salvation—it had promised me a *procedure*, and I had finally read the terms correctly, and the terms ended with a table, a trade, and a man across from me who thought he was the buyer.

I had poisoned myself every night for eleven years so that a queen could eat safely. I knew exactly what it cost to put death in your mouth on purpose.

Tomorrow, I intended to serve.
