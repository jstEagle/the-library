## 19. Veyra's Empty Crown

I had imagined this moment for eleven years, and in all eleven imaginings, the ledger was heavier.

Not literally—though it was that too, a slab of grey vellum bound in something I chose not to examine too closely, because the binding had pores, and pores implied skin. No, I mean it was heavy the way a crown is heavy. The way a verdict is heavy. I had carried armies across three river valleys to get my hands on it, and now that I had it, it sat on the table of my command tent like a guest who intended to stay until I ruined myself feeding it.

"Read it to me," I said.

Brannoc, my secretary—a thin man with the unfortunate gift of being honest in a profession that punishes it—turned the pages by candlelight. His lips moved. Then they stopped moving.

"My lord," he said, "this is a list of everyone in the realm."

"That was rather the point."

"No, my lord. This is a list of *everyone*. Every soul-debt ever brokered. Every name sold, every year pawned, every shadow stolen and resold. The miller in Harrow's Ford owes nine months of his death. The Duchess of Calloway bought her daughter's courage back twice. There is a shepherd who—" he paused, swallowed "—there is a shepherd here who sold his grief. It says the grief was resold six times and is currently worn by a bishop."

I confess I laughed. It was not a kind laugh, but I have never much seen the point of those. "Good," I said. "Excellent. Do you know what this is, Brannoc?"

"Leverage, my lord."

"It is *everyone*," I said. "Kings have ruled by fear and by love since the beginning of both, and both are unreliable. Fear fades at night. Love dies of fever. But debt—" I put my hand flat on the cover, and I swear the thing was warm, like a sleeping animal, like a flank. "Debt does not fade. Debt does not die. Whoever holds this holds every man, woman, and child in the free cities by the throat, and they will thank me for the privilege of breathing."

Brannoc looked at me the way he always looked at me when I said things like this: as if he were taking notes for my eventual eulogy.

"The heralds, then?" he said.

"The heralds," I agreed. "Send them out at dawn. Every city, every guild, every holdfast. Read the debts aloud in the squares. Let each man hear his neighbor's bargains and his own. Tell them Lord Veyra is generous beyond their deserving: he will forgive all of it, freely, entirely—one debt forgiven for every city that opens its gates."

It was elegant. I want that on the record. When they write whatever passes for history after all this—and someone will; the victors always find a scribe with bad posture and good penmanship—I want it recorded that my plan was elegant. Simple arithmetic. Mercy priced at the cost of resistance. Every city would calculate, as every city does, and calculation always lands where I want it.

The heralds went out at dawn. By dusk, the first answers came back.

* * *

The trouble began small, the way all my troubles have begun, which is to say with a person doing something inexplicable.

A garrison captain at Thornmere—a man named Ostrey, whom I had promoted myself, whose debts I held in black and white, four years of his childhood pawned to pay his father's gambling losses—refused to open the gate. Not to the enemy. To me. He stood on the wall and read out his own terms: he would surrender the city when the ledger burned.

"He knows what we have on him?" I asked.

"He does, my lord. We read it to him. His full entry. Word for word."

"And?"

Brannoc consulted his notes with the reluctance of a man opening a door he suspects hides something dead. "He said—" He stopped. Started again. "He said, 'Tell Veyra he can keep my four years. I'd rather owe them than him.'"

I sat with that for a moment. Around me my war council sat with it too, in the particular silence of men watching their commander discover weather.

"One city," I said finally. "One sentimental mill-town. Fine. We hold Thornmere's grain routes; it will starve politely within the month. What else?"

What else. Ah, what else.

By the third day, seven cities had refused in identical language—*we would rather owe them than you*—which was impossible, because heralds did not share notes between towns fast enough to spread phrasing, and yet there it was, the same sentence wearing different mouths, like a rumor that had learned to march. By the fifth day, the refusals were not refusals anymore. They were reversals.

The Duchess of Calloway, who owed more entries in that ledger than any woman alive, marched her household guard—not against the mountain court, as I had assumed she must, to reclaim her daughter's courage by force—but against *me*. She caught my western supply train at the Fords of Ash and burned it, and when my surviving quartermaster was brought before her, she reportedly told him: "He holds my debts? Good. Then he knows exactly what I'm willing to lose. That's more than he knows about himself."

I began, around this point, to sleep badly. I mention it only because it matters to what follows. A man who sleeps badly starts noticing things. Things like the fact that his own soldiers—the two hundred veterans of the Veyran Marches, men who had followed me through swamps and sieges, men whose soul-entries I had personally reviewed for leverage—had stopped meeting my eyes.

"You've been reading to them from the ledger," Brannoc said, when I asked.

"I've been *reminding them of their terms*—"

"You've been reading them their own souls, my lord. Aloud. In front of each other." He set down his pen, which he did perhaps twice a year, and both times meant disaster. "The miller heard that the baker knows he sold his mother's blessing. The baker heard that the sergeant knows about his daughter. You didn't buy an army with that book, Lord Veyra. You taught two hundred men exactly how much they hate each other. And hate," he added, with the mildness of a man placing the last stone on a cairn, "travels upward."

* * *

Here is what I did not understand, then. Here is what I understand now, writing this by whatever light remains, which is less than I would like.

I thought the ledger was a weapon. A list. Ink describing power the way a map describes a country—you hold the map, you imagine you hold the country. But the Broker's ledger was never a map. It was a *registry of promises*, and a promise, it turns out, has a direction. It flows from the debtor to the creditor. Always. The ink records the debt but the debt itself lives in the space between two people, and it cannot be picked up and moved like a chest of coin. You can steal the record of a promise. You cannot steal the promise.

I owned every page. I possessed nothing on any of them.

I learned this properly on the ninth night, when I attempted the working.

I am not a magician. I want to be clear about that; sorcerers are a superstitious and unhygienic class, and I have built my life on the sounder principles of logistics, terror, and bookkeeping. But the ledger came with instructions—of course it did; the Broker strikes me as the sort of entity who leaves instructions the way a spider leaves silk—and the instructions were simple. Speak a name. State the debt. Command.

So I did. Alone in my tent, at the hour when even the sentries pretend not to watch you, I opened the ledger to its first page and I spoke.

"Harrow's Ford. The miller Aldous Ren. Nine months of death, held in trust. I call the debt. Serve."

The candle guttered. Something moved in the corner of the tent—something like a man-shaped absence, like a hole cut from the night in the approximate shape of a tired miller—and it turned toward me, and I felt it *look* at me, and I have been looked at by dying men and by the usurper's executioner and once, memorably, by a she-bear, and none of it compared.

"*No,*" it said.

The word was not loud. That was the worst of it. It was quiet and reasonable and infinitely final, the way a door closing is quiet.

"I hold your debt," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing near the end of the sentence.

"You hold the *paper*," the absence said. "The debt belongs to itself now. It was sold once, honestly, by a frightened man to save his family, and it was bought fairly, and it has been *counted* faithfully for forty years. Counting is holy work, lordling. Stealing a count does not make you the counter." The shape leaned closer, though it had no face to lean with. "You took the ledger from the mountain. Did you read the flyleaf?"

I had not. I said so.

"'Entered in witness,'" the miller's absence recited, "'under the eye of the wolf, who witnessed the first sale and will witness the last.' Debts know their witness, Lord Veyra. And their witness knows you are no Broker. You're only a thief with warm hands." A pause, and then, with what I can only describe as terrible gentleness: "We do not serve thieves. It isn't moral. It's *arithmetic*."

And it was gone, and the candle came back, and I sat alone in my tent with the most powerful object in the realm and discovered that it had the approximate authority of a laundry list.

* * *

They started leaving after that. Not deserting—desertion I could have handled; desertion has procedures, poles, examples made of. They simply *left*, quietly, in ones and twos, and when my riders caught them and demanded explanation, the deserters said the strangest thing. They said: "My debt told me to come home."

Their debts. Their debts were advising them. Corresponding with them. Issuing travel recommendations.

Do you see what happened to me? Do you see the shape of it, the perfect symmetry? I stole a book of chains intending to chain the world, but a chain is only metal until someone believes in the link, and the moment I touched the ledger, every soul in the realm felt the touch—felt the difference between the hand that counted them fairly for a century and mine, which reached in to grab—and every one of them recoiled. The debts refused me *because* I tried to use them. Corruption, I have come to believe, is not a thing that spreads. It is a thing that eats, and eventually it gets hungry enough to eat its own teeth, and there you are at midnight, holding a mouth with nothing in it.

By the twelfth day, half my army had gone home to serve masters they hadn't spoken to in decades—fathers, creditors, bishops wearing stolen grief. The Duchess of Calloway held the southern passes and was, according to my scouts, *forgiving debts herself*, out of the ledger's example if not its text, which I found obscene and also, in a distant way, admirable, the way one admires a fire that jumps a river.

And the ledger itself—

The ledger had begun to cool. I noticed it first as an absence: I had grown used to its warmth on the table beside my maps, the animal warmth of it, and one morning I realized my palm met only vellum. Cold. Then colder. Then, by the fifteenth day, cold as a stone in winter, and when Brannoc turned the pages, the ink was fading. Not smearing. *Fading*, entry by entry, name by name, the way frost leaves a window, and I understood—I understood with the clarity that comes only to men who have already lost—that the ledger was not a book that contained debts. It was a book the debts agreed to inhabit. And the agreement was being withdrawn.

"What happens," I asked Brannoc, "when the last page goes blank?"

He considered. My secretary was many disappointing things, but he was never quick to comfort, which I have always respected.

"I suppose," he said, "you'll be holding a very expensive blank book."

"And everything the book recorded?"

"Freed, my lord." He closed the cover with something close to reverence. Or nausea. With Brannoc the two sounded alike. "Every soul in the realm, returned to its owner. Except—" and here he hesitated in a way that made me look up.

"Except?"

"Except yours, my lord." He laid one long finger on the fading index, on the entry I had never let him read aloud, the entry near the end, in ink that had not faded at all but had instead gone *darker*, blacker, wet-looking, fresh as the day it was written. "*Lord Corvin Veyra. One soul, purchased at market rate from the Broker of the Mountain Court, terms: due and callable.*"

The tent was very quiet.

"You see," Brannoc said gently, "the Broker kept a copy of everything. And the Broker's copy doesn't need the ledger to be true. It only needs the Broker." He looked up at me, and for the first time in eleven years of service, my honest secretary smiled. "Someone is coming up the mountain road, my lord. My rider says it's a taster and a courier and a very large wolf, and that the wolf is smiling too."

I put my hand on the ledger, and it was warm again—warm as skin, warm as a trap, warm as a debt coming home.

"Ready the horses," I said.
