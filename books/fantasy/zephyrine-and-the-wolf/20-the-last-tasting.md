## 20. The Last Tasting

I had tasted ninety-one poisons in my life, and I could tell you the vintage of each one—the bitter green note of wolfsbane steeped too long, the almond lie of cyanide in a spiced wine, the mineral patience of arsenic doing its slow arithmetic in a body that refused to do sums. I knew poison the way priests know scripture: by heart, by scar, by the particular way it changed the shape of every meal forever after.

What I did not know, until I stood in the ruins of the Broker's court with my soul warm against my ribs like a stone pulled from a hearth, was what anything tasted like without fear in it.

But we will come to that.

* * *

Lord Veyra received us in what had been the Soul-Broker's audience chamber, which he had apparently decided was insufficiently intimidating and so had decorated with his soldiers. There were perhaps two hundred of them in the cathedral of glass roots, standing among the empty lantern-hooks where souls had hung like fruit, and every one of them held a soul-lantern at arm's length as though it might bite. Most of them would. Seized souls are poor prisoners.

Veyra himself sat at the Broker's desk—a thing of black glass that had probably been grown, not built—and the ledger lay open before him. The Ledger. Every soul-debt in the realm, a century of bargains, written in a hand that hurt to look at directly. He had a quill in his fingers. That was the part that frightened me. Not the army; armies are just weather. The quill. Because a man who holds a ledger and a quill holds everyone whose name appears in it, and Veyra was the sort of man who enjoyed holding things.

"Coral Delacroix," he said, without looking up. "The Taster. Do you know, when the herald described you, I expected someone larger."

"People usually do," I said. "It's the prophecy. It inflates expectations."

He looked up then. He had the kind of face that made you understand how the word "patrician" had earned its bad reputation—handsome the way a knife is handsome, all intent and no waste. His eyes went to my chest, where the lantern-soul hung under my coat, and I felt it answer him, a faint warmth, the way a dog turns its head toward anyone who whistles.

"Yours," he said. "Reclaimed. Remarkable. You have no idea what a remarkable thing you've done, taster. You've proven the ledger can be beaten. Which means, of course, that it must be held by someone who cannot be beaten." He touched the pages almost tenderly. "With this, I don't need to win the war. I need only read aloud. Every general, every guildmaster, every free city magistrate—each of them sold something, once, or their fathers did. Debts, Delacroix. Debts are the only honest map of a country."

"And you're going to call them in."

"I already have." He turned a page. My stomach turned with it. "The free cities will be kneeling by spring. No bloodshed worth mentioning. The historians will barely need ink." He smiled, and it was nearly a good smile, which is the worst kind. "You should be proud. Your prophecy led me here. 'Whoever holds both soul and ledger.' I hold the ledger. And your soul—" he nodded at my chest, courteous as a surgeon, "—will complete the set. Zephyrine's debt is written here, did you know? A century old. The wolf-god owes this book one miracle. When I own your soul, I own the collateral."

Behind me, Sable shifted her weight. I didn't turn around. Turning around would have meant admitting I wanted to run, and I had come too far and paid too much for the luxury of wanting things I couldn't have.

"Here is my counteroffer," I said.

Veyra raised an eyebrow. Around us, the glass roots hummed—empty hooks, stolen light, a cathedral gutted of its congregation.

"You want my soul because it's the last unencumbered asset in the realm," I said. "Everything else in that book is debt stacked on debt. Mine is clean. Reclaimed, willing, mine. Which means—" and here I took out the one thing I had carried up the wolf-road besides Sable's terrible cooking, which was an idea, and ideas weigh more than luggage, "—it means I can sell it. Willingly. One more time."

That got his attention. Even the soldiers stirred, the way water stirs before it remembers it can boil.

"Everyone in this room knows the first rule of the Broker's arithmetic," I said. "A willing sale binds deeper than any chain. Your whole empire runs on inherited debts—people paying for bargains struck before they were born. That's why the ledger works, Veyra. Nobody living agreed to it. But a willing sale—" I tapped the desk, right beside his hand, close enough that he flinched, "—a willing sale supersedes everything above it in the book. You know it does. Why else would you want mine?"

"Name your price," Veyra said, and his voice had gone quiet, which in a man like him is the sound of hunger.

"The ledger. Destroyed. Not seized—destroyed. Every page burned, every debt voided, every name in that book returned to its owner with interest forgiven. You walk out of this mountain with nothing but your army and your ambition, and the second one won't work anymore."

Silence. The kind that has weight. Somewhere above us the mountain groaned—I liked to think it was listening.

"You're offering your soul," Veyra said slowly, "for paper."

"I'm offering my soul for every person whose grandfather signed something desperate in a famine year. For the shadow-thief who sold her shadow to feed her brother. For the three borrowed years sitting in some stranger's chest right now, wondering whose they are." I heard my own voice do something it almost never did: rise. "You think I'm sentimental about the thing? I spent thirty years as a line item, Veyra. 'One soul, sold.' I know exactly what it's worth in that book. That's why I know what it's worth out of it."

* * *

Sable caught my arm as the soldiers were sent to fetch the Broker's brazier—the only fire hot enough, Veyra had decided with a merchant's thoroughness, to verify the destruction of so much value. He had agreed. Of course he had agreed. He thought he was winning.

"You don't have to do this," she whispered. "Coral. We found another way. Zephyrine said—"

"Zephyrine said a lot of things on that road," I said. "Most of them were about the road."

"You'll be nothing. Unclaimed, unsold, unprotected. You'll be mortal times two—you've seen what you are now, one cup of wine and you're done. Give away your soul on top of that?"

"That's rather the point, Sable." I looked at her—my friend, my colleague, the woman who had chosen me over orders in a collapsing cathedral, which is a debt no ledger records and therefore no ledger can collect. "Every protection I ever had was poison wearing a kind face. Ninety-one immunities, each one a small death I survived by dying less. I gave them all up in one convulsing hour on a mountainside, and do you know what I learned?" I laughed, and it came out ragged. "I learned that being killable is the first honest thing that ever happened to me."

"That's not brave, that's deranged."

"Yes," I agreed. "Frequently the same thing. Ask any novelist."

She didn't laugh. She gripped my arm hard enough to bruise—which, given my new condition, it would, gloriously—and said, "Then I'm staying until it's done. If you're going to be stupid, you'll do it witnessed."

"Witnessed," I repeated. "Yes. That's the other thing about willing sales. They need a witness who isn't a god." I glanced toward the shattered archway where a very large shape sat in the snow-light, patient as winter, pretending not to listen with both ears. "And one who is."

* * *

They brought the brazier, and the glass roots drank the firelight and threw it back red, and for a moment the ruined court looked like what it must have been before men like Veyra came into it: a church.

The contract was drafted on the ledger's own flyleaf, because Veyra insisted on symmetry and I insisted on nothing, having learned that the party who wants the deal least has already won half of it. I wrote the terms myself. My handwriting, for the record, has improved since chapter one of my life.

*One reclaimed soul, freely offered, willingly sold, by Coral Delacroix, taster, in exchange for: the complete and irreversible destruction of the Ledger of Impossible Things; the voiding of all debts recorded therein; the return of all held souls to their owners or heirs.*

Veyra read it twice. Then he did the thing that confirmed everything: he handed me the quill for the signature line, and watched my hand.

Because there is always a word that catches. Last time, drafting my inventory in the snowline, it had been *willing*. I had sat with the quill hovering over that word for the length of a candle and understood, finally, why my parents' bargain had been cursed from the start—they had sold me, but they had never been *willing*, and the ledger knew the difference the way a tongue knows a hair in the mouth.

This time the word came easy. That surprised me most of all. I wrote *willingly* and it cost me nothing, because I had finally understood the trick of the whole rotten arithmetic: they had all believed a soul given away is a soul lost. But a soul spent—deliberately, eyes open, on purpose, for something worth more than itself—

That is not loss. That is the only transaction a soul is actually for.

I signed. Veyra signed. The ledger shuddered under our hands like something waking.

"Witnessed," Sable said, in the flat guild-voice she used for authentications, and pressed her thumbprint beside ours.

From the archway, a voice like the closing of a very large, very old book: "*Witnessed.*"

The ledger screamed. I am aware that books do not scream. This one did—a century of debts crying out at once as the bindings began to smoke, as names lifted off pages like startled birds, as every soldier holding a stolen lantern saw it go dark and drift, gently, homeward. Veyra lunged for it—he actually lunged, dignity abandoned, hands closing on pages that dissolved into ash even as he gripped them—and I watched a man realize, in real time, that he had traded an empire of leverage for a sack of smoke.

"You fool," he breathed. Ash on his perfect face. "Do you know what you've undone?"

"Inheritance," I said. "The oldest theft there is. Somebody had to return it."

My chest had gone cold. Not painful—cold, clean, the sensation of a fever breaking, of setting down a pack after a hundred miles. My soul was leaving me through the contract, unraveling into the air like light into glass, and I stood there and let it, and I want it recorded that I did not fall down. I wobbled with great dignity.

And then—because I had expected to end there, emptied, finished, a closed account—something happened that was not in any ledger.

The light came back.

Not all of it. Not the same. But it threaded through me like spring water through cracked stone, and I felt it settle somewhere new—not in my chest, owned, but everywhere at once, lent, passing through. As if the soul, freed from the arithmetic entirely, had decided on its own authority to stay anyway. Not as property. As company.

"Oh," I said, to no one. "Oh, that's *what it tastes like*."

"What?" Sable snapped, catching my elbow.

"Nothing without fear in it," I said, which made no sense, and which she would later claim to understand completely.

Veyra was on his knees in the ash, and I confess I felt very little looking down at him—no triumph, no pity, just the mild professional interest of a man watching a customer discover that the dish he ordered contains himself. He looked up at me with the expression of someone doing sums that refuse to balance.

"The prophecy," he said. "'The taster who saves or dooms.' You've done neither. You've just—given it all away."

"Yes," I said. "Turns out that was the third option nobody wrote down."

Behind him, in the archway, the wolf-god rose—all of him, impossibly much of him, filling the broken door with winter—and I saw Zephyrine looking at me with those amber eyes, and I saw, for the first time since the snowline, not hunger and not curiosity but recognition. As if he had known, for a hundred years, that somewhere down the bloodline of a desperate family, someone would eventually do the sum correctly.

"Taster," Zephyrine said. "There is something you should know about the first soul ever sold. And something it should know about you. Come outside. The mountain is about to finish falling, and I would prefer you not be underneath it when it does."

Sable grabbed one arm. The wolf waited at the door. Behind us, Lord Veyra sat alone in the ash of every debt in the world, and ahead of us—snow, night, and a story that was, at last, no longer written in anyone's ledger but our own.
