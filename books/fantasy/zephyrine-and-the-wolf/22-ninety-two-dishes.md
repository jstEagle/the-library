## 22. Ninety-Two Dishes

There is a particular terror, I have learned, in cooking for someone when you can be killed by the meal.

I want to be clear about this: I am not brave. I was never brave. For thirty-one years I was safe, and safety made me a coward with excellent posture. I swallowed ninety-one poisons until my blood became a fortress, and I walked through the world the way a tax collector walks through a poor district—untouchable, unloved, keeping accounts. Now my body is just a body. A cup of bad wine could take me. A mushroom mistaken for its cousin. An oyster from the wrong estuary in the wrong month.

And so every morning I stand in my kitchen above the harbor, and I am afraid, and I cook anyway.

This morning it is eggs. Simple things, soft-scrambled, with chives cut so fine they dissolve into green ghosting against the yellow. Sable likes them that way. She will not admit to liking anything, my Sable—she inherited the guildmaster's chair by surviving longer than everyone else who wanted it, and survival teaches you to keep your preferences classified—but she eats every bite and then complains there wasn't more, and that is her love language, and I have learned to read it fluently.

The pan hisses. The window over the sink shows the first snow of the season coming down over the free cities, fat and unhurried, the kind of snow that has decided to stay. Somewhere out there, on the old wolf-road, something enormous is walking toward my kitchen because it is snowing, and this is apparently how gods announce visits.

I crack two more eggs. Guests require three each. This is not in any ledger; it is simply true.

* * *

After the court beneath the mountain came down—and I still cannot tell that story without my hands shaking, so most days I don't tell it—I thought I would die quickly. That was the arithmetic I had accepted: surrender the immunities, reclaim the soul, face Veyra's soldiers as one mortal man among thousands. A fair trade for a self I had never met.

Instead I lived.

Veyra's empire ate itself, which I gather is what empires do when you hand them a ledger full of debts and let them read their own names in it. The free cities argued about what to do with me for exactly eleven days before finding something newer to argue about. Sable rebuilt the Guild of Impossible Things on humbler principles—or at least humbler letterhead. And I woke up one morning in a small rented room, mortal as bread, with no position, no protection, and no idea what a person does with an ordinary Tuesday.

What I did, it turned out, was cook.

It began as necessity—mortal bodies require feeding, and no one else was going to do it—but somewhere around the fortieth dish it became something else. When you spend your life tasting poison, food is information. Bitterness means hemlock family. Almond means cyanide. Heat means capsaicin, or worse. I catalogued every meal of my life the way a customs officer catalogues cargo: looking for the knife in the shipment.

Now bitterness is just caramel gone a moment too long, and I eat it anyway, and it is good.

Last week I made a pheasant. Ninety-second dish, if I'm counting—and I am always counting, some habits survive their purpose. The same bird that nearly broke me at the royal table a lifetime ago, except this time I seasoned it myself, with rosemary and salt and a patience I did not know I owned, and I served it to six neighbors from the building, and nobody tried to kill anybody, and I tasted it first, openly, in front of everyone, the way people do who are not afraid of their dinner.

Sable says I've become insufferable. Sable says a lot of things while accepting thirds.

* * *

The honest ledger lives on a shelf above the stove. It is small—smaller than a hymnal—and bound in undyed leather, and there is nothing impossible in it anywhere.

Let me show you what I record now, since you have followed me this far and deserve to see what became of the great bookkeeper of souls:

*Third of First Snow. Bought: flour, two sacks; saffron (yes, real saffron, I have decided I have earned one luxury); a second kettle because Sable keeps taking mine. Owed to Mrs. Ondel downstairs: one loaf, promised, for watching my window plants while I was away. Received: a jar of quince preserves from the same Mrs. Ondel, which puts us even, though neither of us will say so.*

That's it. That's the ledger. Debts entered, debts discharged, no souls, no years, no names sold or stolen. It took me months to write an entry without my pen hovering, waiting for the trap—the hidden clause, the pre-dated line, the version of events written in my own hand before I was born. But the ledger stays honest because I stay honest, and I stay honest because there is finally nothing to hide behind. No immunity. No double life. Just a man, a kitchen, and the daily small arithmetic of feeding people.

Some nights I take it down and read it like scripture. Ordinary things, recorded faithfully, turn out to be the rarest texts in the world.

* * *

He arrives at dusk, because of course he does. Zephyrine has no talent for arriving quietly; the snow itself seems to lean toward him, and the gulls go silent, and Mrs. Ondel's cat, who fears nothing, decides to fear everything and relocates under the stairs.

He does not fit in my kitchen. He never fits in my kitchen. He stands outside the door with his head lowered through the window-light, amber eyes level with mine, steam rising off his grey coat into the cold, and he waits to be invited, every time, though the invitation has been standing for eleven months now.

"You're tracking snow onto my step," I tell him.

"I am snow," he says, "onto your step."

"Semantics. Come in or don't."

He comes in, the way he always comes in, which is impossibly—a god the size of a carriage folding himself into my small kitchen like winter folding into a valley, settling by the stove with the particular satisfaction of a creature returning to the one warm place he approves of in the entire world.

"The cities smell different," he observes. "Less afraid."

"The cities got what they wanted. Their Taster turned out to be a cook." I slide a plate toward him, which is ceremonial—he doesn't need food, but he eats when I offer, and I have chosen to find this moving rather than alarming. "Eggs?"

"Eggs." He considers them for a long moment. "You put the green in them again."

"Chives."

"They were better before the green."

"They were not better before the green. Nothing is better before the green." I sit across from him with my own plate, and we eat, god and taster, in a kitchen barely big enough for the argument.

Here is what I know about Zephyrine now, what I learned in the dark under the mountain when his chains broke alongside mine: he was the first soul ever sold. The Broker's original transaction, centuries before my parents' century-old bargain—the wolf-god traded away for something the ledger no longer records. He spent lifetimes bound to the wolf-road, collecting tolls of memory, watching travelers forget their mothers' faces, and giving back pieces of himself when he could bear the loneliness no longer. He was hunger walking, ancient and patient, and then a small poisoned man showed up offering anything for passage, and the god discovered curiosity, and curiosity, it turns out, was the crack in the chain all along.

"Why here?" I ask him, the way I ask every visit. "You have a road now. Unbound. The whole world."

"The road," he says, "is very large."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the whole answer. The road is very large, and this kitchen is very small, and I have spent ten thousand years in large places." His eyes catch the firelight. "Small places must be guarded carefully. Things get lost in largeness. Names. Souls." A pause. "Chives."

"You're never letting that go, are you."

"Winter is patient," says Zephyrine, and steals an egg.

* * *

Later, when Sable arrives—she always comes on snow nights, pretending it's coincidence, carrying paperwork she doesn't look at—we three end up around the table like the world's strangest guild meeting. She reports. The cities hold. The new guild takes only trades both parties can survive. Last month somebody tried to sell their grief, and Sable sent them home with a referral to a grief-counselor and a lecture she's clearly been saving for years.

"And this?" Zephyrine nods at the small ledger, open on the table between us.

"Read it if you like. It's all true."

He reads it. All of it, every entry back to the first page, with the concentration of a being who once kept the memories of the road and knows precisely what a record costs. When he finishes, he is quiet for a long time.

"There is nothing impossible in this book," he says at last.

"No."

"When they wrote the prophecy—" he taps the table with one claw, gently, gently; he is always gentle with things smaller than himself now, which is to say everything— "they imagined the Taster holding soul and ledger together would become a king. Or a ruin. Something with a crown or a crater."

"I know. I've read it. It's very dramatic. 'Savior or doom of the free cities.'" I shrug. "They left out the part where he makes soft eggs and argues about herbs."

"They usually leave that part out." Zephyrine looks at me across the table, and for a moment I see the thing under the wolf shape—the first sold soul, the oldest debt, the hunger that chose to become a guest. "Coral Delacroix. Do you know what you actually broke, under the mountain?"

"Myself, mostly."

"You broke inheritance." He says it plainly, the way he says true things. "The arithmetic that says a child pays for his parents, a soul pays for its family, a debt pays for itself forever. You paid it instead. Willingly. Once. Finished." He settles his chin on his paws. "No one had ever done that before. Everyone either flees the debt or feeds it. You cooked it."

"That's not what happened at all, but I appreciate the poetry."

"Poetry," says Zephyrine, "is just accounting that admits what it's for."

Sable laughs into her wine—her plain, unpoisoned wine, poured for a man who could die of it and drinks it anyway—and outside the snow keeps falling on the free cities, on the mountain road, on the grave of an empire, on everything.

* * *

They leave late. Sable takes the spare room because she always takes the spare room and complains about the mattress because she always complains about the mattress. Zephyrine goes out into the snowfall and lies down in the street like an avalanche deciding to rest, and children will find his tracks in the morning and invent stories about them, and the stories will be wrong, and that is fine. Some things should belong to the telling.

Before he goes, he stops at my door, snow gathering on his back, and looks at me with those old amber eyes.

"Ninety-two dishes," he says. "What comes after?"

"Ninety-three. Then ninety-four. There's no end to it. That's rather the point."

"Hm." Steam curls from his breath into the lamplight. "When I was sold—the first time, the first transaction, before the ledger had a name—there was a dish. The feast where they sealed the bargain. I remember the smell of it. Ten thousand years, and I remember the smell." His gaze drifts somewhere far past my roofline, past the city, past the age of cities. "I have wondered ever since what it tasted like."

"Come back next snowfall," I tell him. "Tell me what was in it. I'll make it."

He is quiet for a moment. Snow settles on his nose and does not melt.

"No one has ever offered me that," he says.

"Well." I lean against the doorframe, mortal, unarmored, cold air biting my throat in the exact way it bites everyone, and I find that I am smiling. "Nobody ever offered you breakfast before, either. Go on. You're letting the heat out."

He goes, slow and enormous, and his tracks fill with snow behind him, and I stand in my doorway in the falling white—a man who can be killed by a cup of wine, keeping a small honest ledger, planning a menu ten thousand years in the making—

—and it occurs to me, as I close the door on the warm kitchen and the sleeping friend and the ordinary debts of an extraordinary life, that I never asked him whether the dish at that first bargain-feast was one someone tasted first.

Somehow, I suspect I'm about to become the first person in history to cook for a god twice.

The snow keeps falling. The kettle, I notice, is missing again.
