## 1. The Escapement

The key to the observatory was heavier than it looked, and Petra Eriksen held it the whole way up the hill as though it might change its mind about belonging to him.

It was cast in brass, worn smooth at the shank by forty years of wardens' palms, and it turned in his coat pocket with every step, knocking against his hip like a small insistent heart. Behind him the city descended in terraces toward the harbor — gabled roofs furred with salt-frost, gas lamps guttering down the crooked lanes, and beyond everything the water, black and patient, breathing against the pilings of the quay. The Baltic lay flat tonight. He could hear the ice groaning out past the breakwater, a sound like furniture shifting in an empty house.

Above him, the observatory dome caught the moonlight and held it, pale as a fish's belly.

The summons had come at four o'clock that afternoon, delivered by a boy who did not wait for a reply: *the master clock is losing time*. Four words that meant more in this port than they would have meant anywhere else in the world. Every ship's chronometer was set by the time-ball that dropped from the observatory mast at noon. Every train that crossed the marsh trestle ran on the railway clock, which was corrected twice daily against the master. The telegraph office stamped its traffic with observatory time; the customs men logged their seizures by it; the harbor bells rang it out across the water to the fishing fleet. When the master clock faltered, the whole port began to disagree with itself — ships arguing with trains, trains arguing with telegrams, noon arriving in fragments instead of all at once.

And the Eriksen workshop held the contract for its maintenance. His father's signature was still on the contract, ink gone brown as old blood. Now the signature belonged to Petra, or rather Petra belonged to it, which amounted to the same thing.

The warden let him in without a word, hung his lantern-scarred coat on the peg by the door, and went home. This was the arrangement: repairs were made at night, when no one could see how the sausage of certainty was made. A city should believe its time arrived from heaven.

* * *

The clock room sat directly beneath the dome, a square stone chamber kept at a temperature that never quite forgave the sea outside. The master clock stood against the north wall like a tall grandfather accused of something — a case of black walnut rising eleven feet, its face white enamel ringed in black Roman numerals, its pendulum a rod of invar swinging with the slow contemptuous grace of something that had outlived everyone who built it.

Petra set down his bag, unwrapped his tools on the felt cloth, and simply listened for a while.

That was the first thing his father had taught him, before files, before oil, before he could even reach the bench without a stool. *Listen. The fault will tell you where it lives if you stop talking long enough to hear it.* So Petra stood in the cold and listened, and the clock spoke to him in its broken grammar: *tick... tick... tick-tick... tick.* A hesitation. A stolen beat. Somewhere in the escapement, a tooth was catching and releasing a fraction late, and each stolen fraction was falling like sediment to the bottom of the hour.

He opened the case door. The smell came out — graphite and aged brass and the particular dry sweetness of machine oil older than he was. He put on the linen gloves, clipped the pendulum steady, and began.

For two hours he worked with nothing in his head but the work itself, and those were the best two hours of his month. There was no clerk, no ledger, no mother's silence at the breakfast table, no shop rent due at Michaelmas. There was only the escape wheel lifted free in his gloved hands, turning under the lamplight, its thirty teeth fine as the edge of a spoon. He found the trouble near midnight: a pivot wearing proud through its bushing, the brass rubbed to a bright ring where metal had been kissing metal too closely for years. He re-bushed it, broached the hole true, polished the pivot with boxwood and diamantine until it gleamed like something grown rather than made.

When he stepped back to let the escapement breathe, he felt the pride come up in him quietly, the way warmth comes back into frozen fingers. *I can do this,* he thought. *Whatever else I am, whatever we are now — I can do this.*

Then he went to wind the weights, and found the weight case already open.

* * *

He stopped with his hand on the crank. That was wrong. The case was kept locked; only the warden had the second key, and the warden was home asleep in his nightshirt on the other side of the hill. Yet the panel swung an inch ajar, and inside, instead of the tidy stack of driving weights, there was disorder — one weight sitting forward of its fellows as though it had been moved and moved back carelessly.

Petra brought the lamp close. The weight was not sitting flat. Beneath it, folded into the space the casting left in the base plate, was paper.

He drew it out with two fingers, the way he'd draw a splinter.

It was a letter, sealed, and the seal had never been pressed hard enough to take properly — wax applied in haste, thumb-printed at the edge. But it was the hand on the front that stopped his breath. He knew that hand. He had grown up beneath that hand, watching it move across job tickets and parts invoices, watching it sign his school lessons and the family Bible and, once, gravely, a permission slip for a fair he was not allowed to attend. A narrow, upright, slightly cramped script, the letters leaning forward like a man hurrying somewhere.

His father's handwriting. On a letter dated nine days before the ferry went down.

Petra did not remember sitting down on the repair stool. He was simply on it, the letter on his knees, the lamp pulled so near he could feel the wax beginning to soften. Twelve years. Twelve years since the *Vasa Line* ferry had gone onto the rocks off Sker Point with seventeen souls, twelve years since his father had walked into the sea himself the following spring — or so everyone said, though they never found him, which meant everyone said it carefully, looking at Petra's mother while they said it. Twelve years of the workshop surviving on the charity of contracts like this one, because the Eriksen name was good steel even if the Eriksen luck was rotten.

And here was his father's hand, hidden in the one machine in the city that never forgot anything. Hidden where only a clockmaker would ever find it. Hidden, perhaps, where only *his son* would ever find it.

*Don't,* he told himself. *Wind the weights. Close the case. Finish the work. Whatever it says has been sleeping twelve years; it can sleep till morning.*

But his thumb was already under the flap, and the wax, warmed by the lamp, parted with hardly any resistance at all.

He read only the first lines — he could not have said afterward why he stopped there, only that the words seemed to require privacy, even from himself:

*If you are reading this, then the clock has failed, and I am sorry for what that means. You will be someone who knows gears. Know also that this gear — the great one above you — once counted down something worse than hours. We timed it by the master. Nine minutes after the half-hour bell, the pilot boat puts out from the east stair. What happened on the water that night was not weather—*

Footsteps on the stair below. Slow, iron-shod, unhurried.

Petra closed the case panel with one hand and folded the letter into his shirt with the other, and his heart was going faster than any escapement he had ever mended, while down through the floorboards the city slept on, keeping, all of it unknowing, the time of his father's clock.
