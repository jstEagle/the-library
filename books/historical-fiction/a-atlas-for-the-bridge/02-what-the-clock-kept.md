## 2. What the Clock Kept

Petra did not open the letter that night. He told himself it was prudence — the observatory loft was not his, the watchman made rounds at two, and a sealed letter bearing a dead man's handwriting was not a thing to be read by lamplight in a government building. But honesty came slowly to him in those days, and when he finally admitted the truth, it was simpler: he was afraid. The letter had sat in the weight case for twelve years, patient as lead, and he had carried it home folded inside his coat like a coal that might catch.

He hid it in the workshop instead, behind the loose panel above the polishing bench where his father had kept the small dies, and for three nights he worked with his back to it and could think of nothing else.

On the fourth night his mother went early to bed, pleading a headache, and Petra lit the lamp over the bench and drew the letter out.

The paper had yellowed to the color of old bone. The seal — plain wax, no crest — cracked under his thumbnail with a sound too loud in the quiet shop. His father's hand was unmistakable even before Petra saw the signature: the cramped, upright letters of a man who had learned penmanship from copybooks and resented every hour of it. *J. Eriksen* at the bottom, and above it, in ink gone brown, words that Petra read once and then had to read again because they refused to mean anything the first time through.

*The run is set for Thursday. I will give the signal from the tower — nine minutes past the half-hour, when the master strikes and the harbor is between bells. No one will mark an extra light on the mole if it burns only for the length of one pendulum swing.*

There was more. Much more. Names he did not know — Halvard, Brandt, a man called only "the Swede." References to crates and manifests and a fee paid in silver marks to a man whose title was written out and then scratched through, as though his father had begun to name him and lost his nerve. And near the end, the sentence that Petra read four times while the lamp hissed:

*I have kept the timing true. God forgive me if that is all I have ever kept true.*

Thursday. Nine minutes past the half-hour. Petra knew the date without looking it up; every soul in the city knew it. It was the week the *Maren Kristine* went down in the outer roads with six men aboard, the official finding drowned crew, cargo shifted, weather turning. The ferry disaster, people still called it, though the *Maren Kristine* had never been a ferry except to those who needed her to look like one.

His father had timed a smuggling run by the master clock. Had stood in the observatory loft, Petra's own loft, and used the civic heartbeat of the entire port to flash a signal to men running contraband past the customs cutters. And then the schedule — the schedule must have been sold. Sold to the very men it was meant to evade, so that a cutter waited in the dark where the *Maren Kristine* expected clear water, and six men who trusted a clockmaker's precision were given instead the exactness of their own drowning.

Petra set the letter down on the bench. His hands were steady — that was the terrible part, that his hands remained the steady hands of a craftsman while everything behind them came apart. He thought: *I am reading this in his shop, at his bench, by his lamp.* He thought: *I repaired that escapement. I brought the heart back to life.*

Every tick of the master clock now sounded, in his memory, like testimony. Somewhere above the town the great pendulum swung on, keeping the time his father had once bent to murder's purpose, and Petra had oiled its bearings and trued its beat and felt pride doing it.

He folded the letter along its original creases and put it back behind the panel, and sat in the dark until the church bells rang three, counting them against his own pulse.

* * *

The clerk from the magistrate's office came on a Tuesday, in a grey coat too fine for the neighborhood and boots that left wet prints across the workshop floor.

"Routine," he said, the way men say the word when nothing is routine. He introduced himself as Herr Kessel, assistant to the magistrate's registrar, and asked to see the ledgers. "The port authority is reviewing all contracts issued under the old administration. Repairs, maintenance, certification fees. You understand."

Petra understood nothing except that the man's eyes went everywhere — to the panel above the polishing bench, to the staircase, to the framed certificate on the wall with his father's name on it.

"The ledgers are my mother's," Petra said. "She keeps the accounts."

"Then perhaps I should speak with your mother."

"You may speak with me. I do the work; I know what was done to the clock."

Kessler smiled as if Petra had confirmed something rather than refused something, and opened his notebook. He asked about the observatory commission — who had requested it, when, whether any documents had been found in the clock case during the repair. The question landed on the bench between them like a dropped tool.

"Found," Petra repeated.

"In the case. Papers are sometimes left in old mechanisms. Instructions, correspondence." Kessel wrote something down regardless of Petra's answer, which Petra understood meant the answer didn't matter. "It would be your duty to surrender any such materials to the magistrate. Historical records, you know. The naval review is coming, and everything touching the port's timing is now a matter of state."

"There was nothing," Petra said. "Dust, and a dead spider."

Kessel closed his notebook. At the door he paused, looking at the wall of tools with the fondness of a man taking inventory. "Your father was respected here," he said. "That can be a complicated thing, being respected. One hopes the son's reputation stays simpler than the father's." Then the wet boot-prints dried behind him, and Petra stood a long while listening to the clocks tick, sixty of them, none of them agreeing entirely with another.

* * *

His mother burned papers on Thursday night, in the copper boiler in the yard, after midnight, when she believed him asleep.

He watched from the attic window, breath fogging the glass. She fed the pages in slowly, one at a time, the way she fed dough into the oven — careful, unhurried, thorough. She had changed into her black dress, the good one, the one she wore to funerals and to church on the anniversary of a death, and seeing it now, over a fire in the yard at one in the morning, told Petra more than any document could have.

He went down. She heard the door and did not startle, did not turn, only fed another page to the flames and held it there until the corners caught.

"Mother."

"It's nothing of yours." Her voice was flat. Steam and smoke rose together off the copper.

"Is it about the *Maren Kristine*?"

The poker stopped moving. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, older than it had been a month ago, as if the letter hidden upstairs had aged her by sympathy across the distance between them.

"Who said that name to you?" she asked. "Was it the clerk? Did Kessel come here saying that name?"

"He didn't say it. I found—"

"Don't tell me what you found." She said it fast, almost pleading, and then mastered herself, and the flatness returned like ice forming over water. "Petra. Some papers are burned so that sons don't have to read them. That is not deceit. That is the last work a parent can do." She pushed the last page deep into the embers and stood watching until it curled and went. "Go to bed."

"What was he?" Petra asked, to her back. "What was he actually?"

She stood at the boiler a long moment. The coals settled. Somewhere across the roofs the harbor bell rang the quarter-hour, faint and true.

"A man who loved a clock more than the people it could kill," she said at last, and gathered the poker, and walked past him into the house without touching him, leaving him alone in the yard with the smell of ash.

* * *

The summons came on Saturday, delivered by a boy in naval uniform who would not wait for a receipt. *By order of the Port Commission, Petra Eriksen, apprentice clockmaker, will attend at the observatory on Monday next to certify the master clock for the forthcoming review of the fleet. Certification to be witnessed by the magistrate's registrar or his designate.*

His designate, Petra knew, would be Herr Kessel, with his notebook and his fond eyes.

He climbed to the workshop and took out the letter and read it through twice more by daylight, committing it to memory — names, dates, the scratched-out title, every word — until he could recite it against the ticking of the shop clocks. If it was taken from him, it would still be in him. Then he wrapped it in oilcloth and looked around at the benches, the lathes, the sixty hearts beating in their cases, all of it built by his father's hands and now mortgaged to his father's sins.

Monday, the order said. Monday he would stand in the loft again, over the mechanism he had restored, with soldiers below and Kessel waiting to trade him his freedom for a public denunciation of a dead man.

And when he reached the observatory and lifted the case door, Petra found the escapement already failing again — a pin sheared clean through, a wound too neat to be accident.

Someone wanted the clock broken. Someone wanted the Eriksen name nailed to the wreckage of the port's own time.
