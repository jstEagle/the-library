## 3. Zero Hour

The summons came at four in the morning, hammered against the workshop door by a boy who did not wait for an answer. By half past, Petra was climbing the observatory stair with his tool roll under one arm and a naval lieutenant's lantern swinging ahead of him, and below in the dark city the lamps were still burning along the quay like a string of low orange stars.

"They want it certified by nine," the lieutenant said. He had a young face and a voice that had learned to be flat. "The review fleet anchors at ten. Every gun, every whistle, every telegram in this port will take its time from that clock. If it runs true, you'll be paid and thanked." He paused on the landing, letting the rest hang unspoken in the cold stone air.

"And if it doesn't."

The lieutenant did not answer that. He posted two soldiers at the foot of the stair instead, which was its own kind of sentence, and went back down into the dark.

Petra let himself into the clock room. The great case stood where he had left it three weeks before, brass-bright, pendulum hanging still — he had stopped it for the final adjustment of the escapement, meaning to set it going again within the hour. Meaning to. He lit the lamp, warmed his hands over it until the stiffness of the climb left them, and opened the movement door.

His stomach dropped before his mind understood why.

The escapement was wrong. Not broken — wrong. The pallets had been filed. Someone had taken a stone or a fine file to the anchor's locking faces, shaving them just enough that the teeth would catch, hold, then release a fraction too late, again and again, each error compounding through the hours until by nine the clock would be minutes astray and by noon it would be lying to the entire fleet. It was beautiful work. That was the worst of it. Whoever had done it had known escapements the way Petra knew them, had understood that a sabotage this small would look like wear, like negligence, like *the Eriksen boy's repair failing*.

He stood very still. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere below, the soldiers shifted their boots on the stone.

No one else could have done it. The room stayed locked; the key lived on a nail in the workshop, and the workshop had been watched since the clerk first came asking about ledgers. Which meant either someone had taken the key, or someone had done this during those long nights when Petra himself sat here with the movement open and his back to the door, reading fragments of a dead man's letter by shielded lamplight.

*They will use the clock,* his father had written, twelve years drowned in ink. *A clock cannot lie, but a man can make it speak for him.*

Petra sat down on the repair stool and put his face in his hands for exactly as long as it took to breathe ten slow breaths. Then he took out the letter — he had carried it sewn into his waistcoat since the night he found it; it had grown warm there, close as a second heartbeat — and read the passage he had read forty times already.

*...the pallets must be dressed to my hand or the whole arrangement fails. I have cut a new pair and marked them with the cross. If you are reading this, I did not live to undo what I agreed to do.*

He turned to the movement. There, behind the anchor bridge, almost invisible unless you were looking: a spare pair of pallets, wrapped in oilcloth, a tiny cross scratched into the brass.

His father had hidden the remedy inside the wound. Had stood in this same room, twelve years ago, knowing what the clock was about to be made to do, and had prepared for a repair he would never perform — or perhaps had performed and been killed for, or had simply run out of nights. Petra closed his eyes. He could feel his father's hands in the making of those pallets: the slightly heavy stroke on the exit face, the impatience in the polish, habits Petra had inherited without ever knowing their source.

* * *

He worked.

There was no other word for it, no nobler one. He worked the way his father had taught him before teaching had become silence between them: clean the pivot holes first, always, because dirt lies louder than any gear. Warm the oil. Never touch the locking faces with a bare finger. Let the work tell you what it wants.

But his hands shook. He noticed it when he reached for the screwdriver and the blade chattered against the screw head, a fine bright scratching in the quiet. He set the driver down. Below him the city was waking — gulls first, then the thump of crates on the quay, the first train shunting in the freight yard, all of it blind, all of it trusting the clock above them to keep faith. At nine a magistrate's clerk would climb these stairs with a chronometer and a form. At ten the fleet would anchor. And if the clock spoke falsely, the name Eriksen would be spoken in the same breath as the words *treason* and *sabotage*, and the workshop would burn not by accident but by sentence.

They had offered him a way out. The clerk, Herr Lindqvist, had come to the shop two evenings ago, smelling of wet wool and government paper, and had sat down uninvited.

"You are young," Lindqvist had said, arranging his cuffs. "Young men are not responsible for their fathers. A statement is all we require. A public one. That Erik Eriksen conspired with smugglers, that his work on this clock was part of it, that you repudiate him and his memory entirely. Sign it, read it aloud at the certification, and the seizure order for this workshop goes back in the drawer. You keep your mother's roof. You keep your trade." He had smiled then, kindly, the way men do when they believe kindness costs them nothing. "The dead do not need their names, boy. The living need their bread."

Petra had said nothing. His mother, at the bench behind them, had kept filing, her shoulders very straight, the rasp of the file the only answer in the room.

Now, alone in the clock room with the lamp burning and the fleet's hour closing like water over his head, he understood that the clerk's offer and the sabotaged escapement were the same instrument played twice. Break the clock, blame the father, buy the son. One choice. There was only ever one choice, and they had built the whole machine of the morning around making sure he saw it clearly.

He fitted his father's spare pallets to the anchor.

The fit was not perfect — the old man had cut them for a slightly different drop, and Petra spent twenty minutes easing the entry face with his finest stone, breath held, the shaking in his hands slowly burned away by the sheer demanding attention of the work. This was the thing no one told you about craft: it did not care about your grief or your fear. It required you so completely that there was no room left in your body for anything else, and in that emptied-out space, strangely, you could think. As he stoned the pallet face he thought: *my father chose to hide the truth rather than destroy it.* As he adjusted the drop lock by lock, listening for the clean crisp bite of tooth on steel, he thought: *they want me to choose the lie because the lie is easier to carry.*

By half past eight the escapement ran. He set the pendulum swinging, watched the beat settle — tick, tick, tick, each one landing on the second like a man stepping onto a marked paving stone — and regulated the rate against his own pocket watch until they agreed to within a breath. The sound filled the round stone room. It sounded, Petra thought, like testimony. Like every tick saying *I was here, I was true, whatever they say of the man who built me.*

At nine exactly, boots on the stairs. Two sets. Lindqvist appeared in the doorway with the lieutenant behind him, and the clerk carried his chronometer the way a priest carries a relic, cradled against his chest.

"Master Eriksen." His eyes went past Petra to the running clock, and something flickered there — disappointment, quickly folded away. "You have it running."

"I have it running true."

"We shall see." He opened the chronometer, compared, frowned, compared again. The seconds passed. Outside the salt-glazed windows the harbor was turning gold, and the first gray shapes of the fleet were standing in past the mole, guns fore and aft, flags snapping.

Lindqvist closed the cover. "It will do," he said, and then, softly, only for Petra: "The statement, Master Eriksen. Before the officers come up. Your freedom for five sentences. I advise you not to be sentimental. Sentiment is how workshops burn."

Petra looked at the clock. At the pendulum, swinging its patient arc. At his own hands, steady now, smelling of oil and brass.

"No," he said.

The word came out smaller than he had meant it to, and truer than anything else he had said in years. Lindqvist's face did not change, but he wrote something in a small black book, and the lieutenant moved his weight from one boot to the other, and far below, faint as memory, a bell on the harbor began to ring the quarter hour — true to the second, true to the clock, true to the name they were about to take everything for.

"The seizure order comes at dawn," Lindqvist said, closing his book. "I would spend tonight deciding what is worth carrying."

* * *

Petra climbed down through the bright morning with the fleet at anchor below him and the whole port ticking in time, and he knew, walking home through streets suddenly full of sailors and bunting, that he would not wait for dawn. Some things could not be carried. The ledgers could not. The lathe could not, nor the great vise bolted to the floor his grandfather had laid. But a letter could. A widow could hear it. And somewhere in the western lanes, in a house with a blue door gone gray, old Marta Voss had waited twelve years for someone to bring her the truth about the drowned crew of the *Maren*.

He touched the letter through his waistcoat, once, the way you touch a promise.

Then he turned toward the harbor bridge, and began to walk.
