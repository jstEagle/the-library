## 4. A Atlas for the Bridge

The certificate was a single sheet of heavy paper, watermarked with the imperial eagle, and it required only three things of Petra Eriksen: his signature, the date, and the hour. He wrote all three in the small square at the bottom while the clerk watched from the doorway with his arms folded and his face arranged into something that was not quite a smile.

*Certified true and correct. The master clock of the Port Observatory, keeping time for His Majesty's ships, railways, telegraph, and civic works.*

The clock ticked behind him — steady, indifferent, magnificent. Petra had rebuilt the escapement twice over in his mind before his hands ever touched it again, and now it ran as clean as the day it was first assembled, each tooth of the escape wheel catching and releasing with a sound like a held breath let go. Twelve years of accumulated drift, corrected in one night by the son of the man who had helped hide a lie inside its case.

He signed. The ink dried slowly in the cold.

"Your father," the clerk said, taking the sheet, "would have been proud. Or so people say." He tucked the certificate into his leather folder without looking at it. "You understand what happens next."

"I understand."

"There are arrangements. For your mother. The city is not without mercy." The clerk paused at the threshold, and for one moment his bureaucratic mask thinned enough to show something almost human beneath. "I have a mother also. Go where the ledgers say you cannot go, Eriksen. Be nowhere when they come."

Then he was gone, boots ringing on the observatory stair, and Petra stood alone with the clock and listened to it keep perfect time for a city that would never know what had been kept inside it.

* * *

Marta Voss lived at the far end of the fishermen's quarter, in a low house whose shutters had not been repainted since before the war scare, facing the harbor she had looked at every day for forty years and never crossed again. Petra walked there through streets still grey with early morning, the letter inside his coat, pressed against his chest like something warm-blooded. Twice he stopped. Twice he told himself that delivering it was madness — that burning it on the observatory floor would have been mercy, safety, sense. And twice he kept walking, because he had come to understand that some things carried dishonestly grow heavier with every step, and he was done carrying weight that wasn't his.

She answered the door herself, small and straight-backed, her white hair braided crown-fashion the way the old women of the outer islands wore it. Her eyes went to his face and stayed there.

"You're Henrik's boy," she said.

"You knew him."

"I knew his hands." She stepped back from the doorway. "He had a way of holding a watch when he spoke of time — as if it might bite. Come in before the whole street sees you standing there."

The room inside smelled of tar and dried herbs and decades of mornings. A photograph hung above the stove: a young man with a beard and a boat's wheel behind him, laughing at something outside the frame. *Captain Anders Voss.* Petra had seen that face before, he realized — on the memorial plaque by the harbor steps, one name among eleven, worn smooth by weather and by thumbs.

Petra took out the letter. The seal had been broken days ago; there was no unbreaking it now. He held it out across the table.

"This was in the clock. My father put it there — I'm certain of it, the hiding place was made, not found. It's about the night your husband died." His voice wanted to fail him and he would not let it. "I think you're the one it belongs to. I think maybe it always was."

Marta Voss did not touch it at once. She sat down slowly, folding her hands, and looked at the paper the way one looks at a well that has taken a child.

"Read it," she said finally. "My eyes gave up on handwriting years ago. And if it says what I have spent twelve years believing it would say, I would rather hear it in a young voice. Mine has gone rusty with waiting."

So Petra read. The letter was short — his father had never been a man who wasted words on paper — and it was dated four days before the ferry went down. It named the run. It named the men who had bought the schedule from the customs office and used it not to catch the boat but to meet it, and then to sink it, and to divide a cargo that officially had never existed. It named the price his father had been paid for timing the tides against the master clock, and it named his shame in full, without ornament, the way a workman describes a flaw in his own joinery.

And at the bottom, in a hand shakier than the rest, added later — much later, near the end:

*If I cannot speak this aloud while I live, let it keep time after me. Forgive me, Marta. He trusted me with the hours and I sold them.*

Petra's voice stopped working on the last line. He set the letter flat on the table and slid it toward her, and Marta Voss put both her old hands over it and bowed her head, and for a long time the only sound was the kettle beginning to sing on the stove and the gulls outside crying over the water that had taken everything from her.

"He came here," she said at last, without lifting her head. "Three weeks before they found him in the channel. Stood where you're standing and couldn't do it. I thought he'd come to buy fish, the fool, in his good coat." Now she raised her face, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "I know now what he was trying to say. You've said it for him. Both of them."

"They'll take our shop tomorrow," Petra said. "Maybe tonight. They want the name erased — his and mine. That's why I'm telling you, so it isn't only the magistrate's version left. What happened on that water deserves a witness who isn't being paid."

"Witnesses get drowned in this port, boy."

"Yes," Petra said. "My father taught me that too."

Marta Voss laughed — one short, hard syllable, more like the crack of ice than laughter — and stood, and took down a tin from the shelf, and put bread and smoked fish and a fold of paper coins into a cloth bag without asking whether he wanted any of it, because she had fed fishermen's families through two lean decades and had lost the habit of negotiating.

"Go north, then," she said. "Go anywhere the bells don't know your name. And when you fix clocks in whatever town takes you — and you will fix clocks, it's in the hands — you remember that time doesn't belong to the men who buy it. It belonged to my Anders for exactly as long as he lived, and no customs man got a minute of it that mattered." She pushed the bag against his chest. "Now get out. I have a letter to sit with, and I've waited twelve years to be alone with it."

* * *

They came at dawn, as the clerk had promised. Petra heard the wagon from three streets away and did not run; there was nothing left in him that wished to see it arrive, and everything in him that wished to see it begin.

He stood across the canal with his mother beside him, her hand cold in his, and watched men in the magistrate's livery nail the seizure order to the workshop door — *by decree, all property of the house of Eriksen, instruments, ledgers, tools and stock* — and then, an hour later, when the paperwork had been duly witnessed and the street had filled with silent onlookers who owed his family money or mended watches or both, watched other men carry out lanterns and oil.

The fire took the shop the way fire takes anything built of pine and honest work: greedily, with a sound like applause. The great wall of drawers went up in columns of flame, each small compartment a brief bright coffin for gears and springs and a hundred years of repairs. The sign burned last, the letters curling black one by one — *ERIKSEN & SON* — until SON was ash and ERICKSEN was ERIC and then nothing at all, and smoke rolled out over the harbor and the gulls scattered screaming, and Petra felt the heat on his face and understood that he was not grieving. Not exactly. He was being emptied. Whatever they wanted from the name, they could have; it had already been given away, honestly, to the only person entitled to receive it.

In his pocket, wrapped in a rag, rode his father's smallest file — the needle-slim one used for pallet stones, no longer than a finger, worn smooth at the grip by thirty years of another man's thumb. It was worth nothing. It was everything. It was the one tool small enough to carry across a bridge and into a life that belonged to no ledger.

His mother squeezed his hand once and turned away, and did not look back, and he loved her for it.

At dusk he crossed the harbor bridge alone, going north out of the city with the road empty ahead of him and the water darkening below. Behind him the observatory tower stood black against the last light, and as he reached the middle of the span the evening hour arrived — and the bells rang out over the port, ship bells and church bells and the great bronze voice of the observatory itself, every one of them striking together, perfectly, unmistakably true.

Petra stopped. He put his hand in his pocket and closed it around the file, and he counted the strokes all the way to twelve, and when they were done he found that his face was wet and that he was, for the first time in twelve years, entirely free.

Then he pulled his collar up against the wind coming off the sea, and walked on — north, toward towns where no one knew his name, carrying nothing but his father's smallest tool and the hour, which no decree could seize.
