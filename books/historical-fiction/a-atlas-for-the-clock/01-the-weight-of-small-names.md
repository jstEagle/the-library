## 1. The Weight of Small Names

The boy died at four in the morning, between the changing of the lamps and the first grey push of light through the ward windows. Zephyrine knew the hour precisely because she had been counting his breaths the way she counted everything now—in intervals, in small units that could be held and measured and, when necessary, survived.

Seventeen breaths a minute at midnight. Nine by two o'clock. Then gaps, longer each time, like a sentence losing its words.

She sat with him because there was no one else to sit with him. His family was somewhere north of Amiens, if the enlistment papers were to be believed, though the papers said a great many things that were not true. They said he was nineteen. They said he had died of "fever, complications." The fever was real enough. The complications had been a burst appendix left untended for three days while his regiment marched him from one end of the department to the other, because the paperwork for transfer required signatures no one would give.

"Tu es là," she said to him, more than once. You are here. It was what her mother used to say, back when her mother still spoke Japanese to her in the kitchen and French to the neighbors on the stairs. You are here. I see you. The words did nothing, medically speaking. She said them anyway.

At half past three he opened his eyes and looked at her with an expression of such ordinary curiosity—she would think about it for years afterward—that she almost smiled.

"What is it?" he asked. Or tried to. The words came out as a shape of air.

"Nothing," she said. "Sleep."

He slept. At 4:12 by the ward clock, which ran seven minutes fast and which everyone trusted anyway, he stopped.

* * *

The official report, written up that same afternoon by Dr. Ferrand in his looping, evasive hand, read: *Soldat Dubois, A., âge 19, fièvre, complications. Décédé à l'aube.*

Zephyrine stood at the duty desk reading it twice, then a third time, not because the words were difficult but because they were so clean. Décédé à l'aube. Died at dawn. As if dawn itself had done it. As if the boy had been taken gently by a natural phenomenon, a weather event, something no one could be asked to account for. There was no mention of the appendix. No mention of the three days. No mention of the fact that his actual name—the name his mother had called him across a kitchen, the name that would be spoken once over a grave and then never again—was not Albert at all but Alaric, which she knew because he had told her on his second night in the ward, embarrassed, as if it were a flaw in himself.

"They put down Albert," he'd said. "Because it's simpler."

"And is it?" she'd asked.

"It's easier," he said. Which was not the same thing.

Dr. Ferrand was not a cruel man. That was the thing Zephyrine understood early and could never explain later to anyone who asked: the machinery of omission required no cruelty at all. Ferrand was tired, overworked, answerable to a garrison commandant who wanted clean columns in his monthly returns, and a death from "fever, complications" generated no inquiry, whereas a death from untreated appendicitis in transit generated letters, questions, a board of review. So the report was clean, and the boy went into the ground under the wrong name, and the column in the ledger of the 4th Artillery balanced beautifully.

History happens elsewhere, Zephyrine told herself that evening, walking home along the seawall with the wind coming off the water hard enough to make her eyes stream. History is made in ministries, in capitals, in languages she barely spoke, by men whose names appeared in newspapers she couldn't afford. Saint-Aubin was a garrison town the way a doormat is a house—it existed to have things walked over. Whatever was coming—and everyone felt it coming, the way you feel weather in a bad tooth—would happen elsewhere. Her work was bandages and bedpans and the small mercies of the present tense. She was a nurse. She was invisible. She was half of everything and fully nothing, and invisibility, she had learned, was a kind of safety.

Her hands disagreed.

They had been disagreeing for weeks. All through the autumn they had been keeping their own count, a tally she hadn't authorized: the soldier in Ward C who died of "pneumonia" after the punishment detail in the rain; the conscript whose file said *déserteur présumé* even though she had personally changed his dressing every day for a month and knew he had never once tried to stand; the old gunner whose heart gave out and who was recorded, with a bureaucratic shrug, as *mort subite*, sudden death, cause unknown, as if men simply stopped existing without reasons, like candles.

That night, after supper, she took down from the shelf a cloth-bound ledger she had bought in the spring for ten sous from the stationer near the fish market, intending it for inventory notes. Its cover was grey canvas, soft-cornered already, the kind of object designed to be unremarkable. She sharpened a pencil, thought better of it, and chose ink instead. Pencil smudged. Pencil could be argued with. Ink committed you.

At the top of the first page she wrote the date: the twelfth of November, in the year 1913.

Then, beneath it, in her careful, upright hand—the hand her mother had drilled into her with copybooks, the hand that looked like no one's and therefore like hers alone—she wrote:

*Nakamura, Zephyrine, infirmière, Hôpital de garnison de Saint-Aubin, fait la présente note.*

It sounded absurd, officious, like something out of a court document. She almost crossed it out. But some part of her understood that this was exactly right—that what she was doing needed the costume of officialdom to hold its shape. If the world insisted on records, then she would keep one. If names were struck from reports, they would be struck into something else.

Below the header she wrote:

*Alaric Dubois, dit «Albert», soldat, 4e régiment d'artillerie. Âge véritable: dix-sept ans. Décédé le 12 novembre, 4h12 du matin (horloge du service, sept minutes en avance), de péritonite following rupture of the appendix, non traitée pendant trois jours en transit. Rapport officiel: «fièvre, complications.»*

She sat back. The lamp hissed faintly. Downstairs in the street a cart rattled past, and somewhere a dog began barking and then thought better of it.

The entry was wrong in a dozen ways—half French, half English, the grammar of a woman who lived between languages and belonged to neither. She didn't fix it. Accuracy of feeling mattered more than accuracy of syntax; she sensed this without being able to defend it.

She turned the page and wrote the others. The man from Ward C. The supposed deserter. The old gunner. Four names, then five, then a sixth she had nearly forgotten—a marine lost off the jetty in September whose drowning had been logged as *disparu*, disappeared, as though the sea were a country that issued no receipts. She worked until the candle burned low, and when she finished she read the page aloud to the empty room, quietly, the way you'd read a psalm.

It was not defiance, she told herself. Defiance was a grand word, a newspaper word. This was bookkeeping. Someone had to balance the accounts.

But her hands, ink-stained, steady, knew better than she did what they were doing.

* * *

She learned to hide it in plain sight, which was the only reliable method in a garrison town where everyone watched everyone and saw nothing. The ledger lived on her shelf among a dozen others: inventories, supply lists, duty rosters. Grey cloth among grey cloth. She wrote in it at night, by lamplight, with the door bolted—not that a bolt would stop anyone, but the ritual steadied her—and she developed a system of shorthand abbreviations that would have meant nothing to another reader. *R.O.* for rapport officiel. A dash for every discrepancy. Small marks in the margin like bird tracks.

By December she had nineteen names.

She memorized them too, all of them, testing herself on the walk to the hospital each morning: Alaric Dubois, seventeen. Marcel Fournier. Théodore Kessler, who was Alsatian and whose mother wrote letters in a language the censors flagged twice. The marine, Yves Guillou. She recited them like a rosary, though she was not Catholic and suspected God kept poor records Himself, given the state of the world.

The strange thing was how little it changed her days. She still washed wounds and folded linens and endured Dr. Ferrand's sighs and the head nurse's suspicions about her foreign blood, delivered always sideways, never frontally, because Zephyrine was useful and usefulness purchased a narrow tolerance. She still walked the seawall. She still bought bread from Madame Okafor, the laundress, whose shop smelled of starch and woodsmoke and who was the only person in Saint-Aubin who pronounced her name correctly on the first try, having one herself that no one else could manage.

"You look like a woman keeping books," Madame Okafor observed one morning, weighing flour with the gravity of a magistrate.

"I am a woman keeping books."

"For whom?"

"For whoever asks, eventually."

Madame Okafor looked at her for a long moment, then wrapped the bread in paper and said nothing, which in Saint-Aubin was the loudest form of agreement available.

On the twenty-third of December, a new patient arrived at the hospital with the evening transport: a foreigner, a cartographer, carried in on a stretcher with a fever of unknown origin and a canvas satchel strapped to his body even in delirium, as though the maps inside it were worth more than his life. He was Dutch or Flemish or something adjacent—his papers said Verhoeven, Elias, born Maastricht, profession surveyor—and the officers regarded him with the flat suspicion reserved for men without a regiment.

Zephyrine took his temperature, noted the tremor in his hands even unconscious, and registered the one detail the admitting clerk missed: that in his delirium he was not calling for help, or for God, or for his mother.

He was reciting numbers. Coordinates, she would learn later. Grid references, dates, altitudes. He was, in the grip of a burning fever, doing what she did every night at her lamplit table.

He was keeping a record no one had asked him to keep.

She pulled the blanket higher over his chest, and downstairs, in the duty room, someone stamped a form, and the sound traveled up through the floorboards like a slow knocking at a door.
