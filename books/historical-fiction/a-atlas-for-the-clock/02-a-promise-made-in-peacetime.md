## 2. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The fever broke on a Tuesday, and by Thursday Elias Verhoeven was complaining about my ink.

"It's too thick," he said, propped against his pillows with a sheet of paper balanced on a dinner tray. "You're writing names, not carving tombstones. Thin it. A nurse should know the value of a dilution."

"You are a difficult convalescent."

"I am a *bored* convalescent. There is a difference, and the difference is that boredom can be treated." He held out his hand for the little bottle I kept in my apron pocket—the one I used for the ledger, hidden at the bottom of my locker beneath a folded chemise and a broken watch. "Give it here. If you insist on keeping records, you should at least keep them legibly. Ink fades. Paper fades. Sloppy handwriting is just another way of losing someone."

I should have refused. Sister Beatrice had strict rules about patients handling their own affairs before the third clear day, and there was something in his clarity that unsettled me—that a man could burn at a hundred and four degrees for six nights and come out the other side with opinions about my penmanship. But he had that quality the well-born all had, of assuming doors would open, and mine did.

I gave him the ink.

* * *

He had been carried into Saint-Aubin in October, half-dead of fever contracted surveying the coastal marshes—a foreign cartographer in the employ of no ministry he would name, mapping terrain nobody official would admit needed mapping. The officers tolerated him the way one tolerates weather: useful, unaccountable, temporary. His papers said Dutch. His accent said nowhere in particular. His eyes, when the fever let him use them, said that he noticed everything and wrote down most of it.

I knew what that was like. Perhaps that was the whole of it, from the beginning—two people in a garrison town who understood that being invisible was a kind of work.

"Show me," he said that Thursday, when the ward had emptied toward supper. Not a question. He'd seen me writing in the cloth-bound book three nights running while he burned, and fever had made him frank.

"Show you what?"

"Whatever you hide under the chemise with the broken clasp. You check on it like a pulse. Don't blush—I check my own satchel the same way. We are the same animal, Miss Nakamura, you and I. Just different instruments."

I told him it was nothing. He waited. That was his method, I would learn: not charm, not argument, simply an unhurried patience, as if time were a landscape he was prepared to sit in until it revealed itself.

I brought him the ledger.

He read it the way other men read scripture—slowly, twice. Seventeen names by then. It had begun with the boy, Anselme, seventeen or younger, dead of a ruptured appendix after a march his body had no business surviving, struck from the regimental report because the report needed the march to have been easy. Then Corporal Duval, recorded as deserter when he had died in his sleep of his heart. Then the rest, each one erased somewhere between the ward and the filing cabinet, each one living now only in my hand.

"You organize these how?" he asked.

"They're in order of—of when I learned."

"That's a diary. This should be an atlas." He tapped the page. "Regiment. Date of death, not date of record—there's a difference and the difference will matter someday. Place, as precisely as you can get it. Cause, if cause is known and cause is *true*. Cross-references. A name that can't be found is a name that's lost anyway. You're not keeping a diary, Zephyrine. You're building an index of the disappeared, and indexes have architecture."

Nobody had spoken my given name in that hospital in two years. I let it stand in the air between us without correcting him.

"Why does it matter?" I asked. "History happens elsewhere. In ministries. In capitals. This is Saint-Aubin."

"History happens wherever someone writes it down." He turned a page with a care that undid me more than any tenderness would have. "The reports that erase these men are also history happening. Someone is keeping *their* atlas. It would be poor scholarship to concede the entire discipline to them."

* * *

We worked through November. He was meant to be transferred to the garrison's engineering office once fit; instead he lingered, extending his own recovery through sheer administrative charm, forging small delays in his discharge papers with the ease of a man who had spent his life making maps of places that preferred not to be found. Every afternoon he occupied the corner bed by the window, and every afternoon I sat on the stool beside it, and we built order out of the dead.

He taught me his craft because teaching was how he touched things. How to draw a grid true—start from the corners, never the center. How to letter so the eye moves without stumbling. How to encode: not cipher, nothing so incriminating, but a cartographer's shorthand, abbreviations and symbols of his own devising, so that a casual reader saw a dull inventory and a careful reader saw everything.

"This mark," he said, drawing a small triangle with a dot above it, "means elevation confirmed by two sources. I'm assigning it to names where the death was witnessed."

"And this?"

A circle crossed by a line. "Contested. Someone will deny this one happened. Be ready to prove it."

The ledger changed under his hands. It stopped being grief and became a document. I remember feeling guilty about that transformation—the relief of it—and then deciding that guilt was a luxury the dead could not afford to have me indulge.

One evening he drew a map for me instead. Not the coast, not the marshes. A map of the hospital itself, absurdly precise: every ward, every corridor, the broom cupboard where I slept, the window of the linen room with its loose latch. He marked my locker with a tiny symbol—his circle-and-line, contested.

"Every atlas needs a key," he said. "This is yours. If anything happens to me, everything I know about hiding paper is on this page."

"Nothing is going to happen to you. You're getting fat on Sister Beatrice's puddings."

"Zephyrine." He set down the pen. Outside, the garrison bugle sounded last post, thin in the cold. "I'm a foreigner in a country arming itself against my own. Whatever happens to me is already scheduled; I've simply not been shown the timetable. So indulge me. Humor the boring cartographer. Let us be professionals about catastrophe."

I looked at the map of my own life drawn in another man's hand and could not speak.

So he spoke for both of us, in his way—oblique, practical, a love letter disguised as procedure.

"A pact, then. Between colleagues. Whoever survives keeps the names. Not copies—*keeps*. Carries them. Answers for them. If they take your ledger, you rebuild it from memory, every entry, and if they take mine, I do the same. Two archives, one truth. Redundancy, Miss Nakamura, is the highest form of devotion known to science."

"And if we both survive?"

He smiled at the window, at the dark, at the sea beyond the wall that he would not be allowed to keep mapping. "Then we compare notes. Page by page. Somewhere with good light and bad coffee. I've always wanted to be audited by you."

It was the closest either of us came to saying it. Duty forbade the word—he was a national of a state my adoptive country was learning to call enemy, and I was half of that enemy myself in the army's arithmetic, and we were both fluent in the grammar of things that must not be written down. But we were chroniclers. We knew that what cannot be named can still be *located*, fixed precisely, kept.

He took my hand then—not to hold it, but to press it flat against the open ledger, his palm over mine, our fingers aligned along the margin like a scale bar.

"Witnessed by both parties," he said quietly. "Entered into the record."

* * *

In December they came for him—not soldiers yet, just a requisition officer with a clipboard, informing the hospital that all resident foreigners would be catalogued in the spring, in advance of "national registration measures." The word *registration* went through the ward like cold water through cloth.

Elias took the news standing at the window, hands behind his back, watching the harbor.

"They'll want my maps," he said. "And when they don't find what they want, they'll want everyone I ever spoke to. Which is a list of one, and she sleeps in the broom cupboard."

"Then give them the maps."

"I intend to give them excellent maps." He turned, and for a moment the fever-brightness was back in his eyes, that terrible clarity. "Zephyrine. The pact stands regardless of what the spring brings. Say it back to me. I need to hear it in your voice, not mine."

"Whoever survives keeps the names."

"And the rest?"

"Page by page. Good light. Bad coffee."

He nodded once, satisfied, the way he was satisfied when a survey closed true and the corners matched. That night he slept without fever for the first time since October, and I sat by his window a long while after the lamps were out, holding the ledger against my chest, listening to the garrison clock count off the minutes of the last ordinary year.

In March, they would come with clipboards for the whole town.

But I did not know that yet. All winter, I believed we had time.
