## 3. Requisition

The war came to Saint-Aubin on a Tuesday, in the form of a man with a clipboard.

Zephyrine heard him before she saw him—the particular rhythm of army boots on the hospital's stone corridor, too measured for a doctor, too unhurried for a messenger. She was folding bandages at the supply window, and her hands did not stop their work, though something in her chest went very still, the way a bird goes still when a shadow crosses the glass.

He appeared at the end of the corridor flanked by two privates and the hospital's own administrator, Dr. Fournier, who wore the expression of a man walking slightly ahead of his own execution. The officer was perhaps forty, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that had been designed to be forgotten—medium height, medium build, a mustache trimmed to regulation and no further. Only his eyes moved quickly, cataloging everything: the ward doors, the linen cupboard, the nurses, Zephyrine herself, in the space of four seconds.

"Major Corliss," Dr. Fournier said, his voice pitched to carry reassurance that his posture contradicted entirely. "Intelligence section. He's come about the requisition."

"There is no requisition order posted," Zephyrine said.

"It was posted this morning." Corliss produced a paper from his satchel without breaking stride. "On the mairie door. You'll find the town is now under military administration. The hospital falls under Schedule C—medical facilities retained for army use. All civilian records, correspondence, and documents within the occupied zone are subject to inventory." He said it the way a man reads a train timetable. "You will continue your duties as before, Nurse Nakamura. Nothing changes except the paperwork."

Nothing changes except everything, she thought, and folded another bandage.

* * *

By evening the town had learned its new grammar. Red-and-white notices bloomed on every wall like some fast-spreading rash: CURFEW AT NINE. REQUISITION OF HORSES, CARTS, GRAIN. REGISTRATION OF FOREIGN NATIONALS WITHIN SEVENTY-TWO HOURS. A boy of twelve stood in the square nailing up the last of them, and Zephyrine watched from the dispensary window as he hammered with the solemn concentration of a child building something rather than tearing something down.

The baker's wife was already in tears outside the church. The horses went first—they always do—and then the carts, and then men began appearing at the hospital with injuries from the loading docks, crushed fingers, a broken collarbone, the small brutal harvest of an army taking what it wants. Zephyrine splinted and bound them and said very little. What was there to say? The war everyone had whispered about for months had arrived not as fire but as ink.

She thought of Elias. Seventy-two hours to register as a foreign national—but Elias was gone now, recalled across the border weeks ago by letters that grew shorter and colder until they stopped altogether. She told herself it was safer that way, with the borders hardening like scar tissue around the country. She told herself many things.

That night, after lights-out, she took the ledger from its hiding place inside the false bottom of her instrument case and read it by candle stub, the way she always did, touching each name once with her fingertip before closing the cover.

Three hundred and eleven names. She would not let them become inventory.

* * *

Corliss began with the administrative offices, which was sensible, and worked outward through the hospital over three days, which was thorough. He interviewed Dr. Fournier in the morning, the matron at noon, and by the second afternoon he had reached the nursing staff—a table set up in the dispensary, a lamp, his satchel open like a patient mouth.

"Nurse Nakamura." He pronounced it carefully, having clearly rehearsed it. "Half-Japanese, I understand. Your mother was from Nagasaki?"

"Yokohama."

"Forgive me. And your father?"

"A merchant seaman. Dead eleven years."

"You've lived in Saint-Aubin since?"

"Since I was nine."

He wrote all of it down, and she watched him write it, and understood that she was watching herself being converted into a line item. *Nakamura, Z. Half-caste. Local ties: eleven years. Loyalty: unknown.* She could almost see the words forming behind his pen.

"Your duties here?"

"Ward nurse. Dispensary. Records of treatment, which you already have."

"I do." He turned a page. "Which brings me to personal effects. The schedule requires an inventory of documents held by staff. Private papers, correspondence, diaries, account books."

"Account books," Zephyrine repeated.

"Anything in which names are recorded." He looked up, mild as milk. "You'd be surprised, Nurse, how much history people keep in the wrong places."

Her hands were in her lap, folded, still. She had practiced this stillness for years—in wards where men died badly, in rooms where doctors spoke of her as though her mixed blood were a diagnosis. Stillness was her oldest skill.

"I keep accounts," she said. "Household expenses. A record of my mother's recipes, translated. Letters from an aunt in Yokohama."

"The aunt may be relevant. Foreign correspondence."

"My aunt writes about persimmons and the price of silk."

"All the same." He smiled—it was a professional smile, distributed evenly, meaning nothing. "If you could bring whatever you have tomorrow morning, we'll log it and return it. This is not a search, Nurse. It's an accounting. The army prefers to know where everything is. Including people."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat with the ledger on her knees in the dark of her room above the laundry, listening to the garrison clock strike the hours—one, two, three—and understood, with the clarity that comes only at three in the morning, that she was holding a document that could hang her.

Names of dead soldiers omitted from official reports. Dates. Regiments. In her own hand, in places annotated in a second hand—Elias's hand—marking positions, distances, the geography of omissions. She had thought of it for six years as a ledger of the lost. She saw now, as if through Corliss's eyes, how it might read otherwise: a foreigner's sister recording troop deaths, keeping regimental numbers, corresponding with a foreign cartographer. Evidence. Sedition. Treason.

Burn it, said one voice, small and practical, the voice of survival.

And lose them twice? said the other voice, older and lower, the voice that had started all this beside a seventeen-year-old boy's bed while officers upstairs decided he had never existed. They died once already, unwritten. Burn the book and they die again, and this time forever.

At dawn she made her decision the way she made most decisions—not with drama but with a kind of cold housekeeping. She kept the ledger. But she prepared the ground.

* * *

She brought Corliss a shoebox: household accounts, seven years of them, meticulous; her mother's recipe book; three letters from Yokohama, tied with string. He went through them with genuine, almost gentle attention, stamping each item, entering each into his inventory in a hand so neat it looked printed.

"You're very orderly," he observed.

"I'm a nurse."

"That too." He stamped the last letter. Then, instead of closing the satchel, he sat back and looked at her for a long moment, and she felt the temperature of the room change. "There's a question on the schedule I'm required to ask. It's routine. Have you, at any time, kept records beyond those of your official duties—records concerning military personnel, casualties, movements, or dispositions?"

The clock in the corridor ticked. Somewhere below, a ward bell rang and was answered.

"No," Zephyrine said.

It was the first lie she had ever told an officer of the army, and it came out of her smooth and quiet as water, and she found that her hands did not shake at all. She thought: Elias taught me maps, but I taught myself this.

Corliss studied her face for perhaps five seconds longer than courtesy required. Whatever he was looking for, she gave him nothing to find—only the tired, competent blankness of a woman who has been on her feet for fourteen hours.

"Thank you, Nurse." He closed the satchel. "Your items will be returned within the week. Saint-Aubin is fortunate to have staff of your... discretion."

* * *

But that was not the end of it, because men like Corliss do not ask questions they don't already suspect the answers to.

Two days later she came off night duty and found Madame Okafor waiting in the laundry, folding sheets with the violent serenity of a woman who has buried a husband and raised four sons and fears nothing God can send. The laundress knew everything that moved through the hospital—knew it in her bones, in her wash-water, in the stains she read like tea leaves.

"He was here again," Madame Okafor said, not looking up. "Your major. Asking Fournier which nurse keeps the mortality books."

"The mortality books are official. He has them."

"He asked which nurse *kept* them, past tense. Which nurse wrote things down that weren't asked for." Now she looked up, and her eyes were old and sharp and sorry. "Child, he asked me about you. About visitors. About whether anyone writes to you from across the water."

Zephyrine stood very still among the drying sheets, white shapes swaying in the draft like ghosts at a dance.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him the half-caste nurse keeps to herself, and no man visits her, and more fool them." Madame Okafor snapped a sheet flat. "I can lie to an officer. Can you lie to yourself? Because I've watched you six years, girl, and I know there's something in that instrument case you touch at night like a rosary. And I know that if he finds it, they won't return it stamped and polite. They'll return *you*."

Outside, the garrison clock struck eight, and boots crossed the courtyard in unison, and somewhere a clerk's typewriter began its dry, insect clicking—the sound, Zephyrine thought, of the ordinary world being taken apart sheet by sheet and refiled under new ownership.

She climbed to her room and opened the instrument case. The ledger lay where it always lay, cloth-bound, patient, holding its three hundred and eleven names against her palm like a heartbeat.

*They know,* she thought. *Not yet what. But soon.*

And far away, across a border she could no longer see, a man who had taught her to organize these very pages by regiment and date was, at that moment, being handed a list of suspected collaborators on which his own name sat printed in neat government type—though of this, standing in her cold room with the book warm against her chest, Zephyrine Nakamura had no way of knowing yet.
