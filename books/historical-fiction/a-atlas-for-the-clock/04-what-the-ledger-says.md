## 4. What the Ledger Says

The summons came at six in the morning, delivered by a corporal who did not knock so much as announce himself through the door, as though the building itself were now army property and she merely a fixture within it.

"Miss Nakamura. The major requests your presence in the east office. Bring any personal papers."

*Any personal papers.* Three words, and the world tilted. She dressed with hands that were almost steady—she had bandaged wounds while shells fell, she could button a collar—but the ledger's weight was already against her ribs, phantom-heavy, as if it knew.

She considered leaving it under a floorboard. She considered the stove. She stood for one full minute with the book in both hands above the cold iron grate, and then she put it back into the instrument case, because Corliss had inventoried her room once already, because hiding it would be confession, and because—this was the thought that frightened her most—it had never occurred to her that the names could need defending. They were dead men. Who prosecutes the dead?

The east office had been the matron's sewing room before the war. Now it held a desk, two chairs, an oil lamp burning even though daylight was coming up grey behind the shutters, and Major Corliss, who looked as though he had slept in his uniform and considered this a virtue.

"Sit down, please."

She sat. He opened the instrument case himself—he did not ask; she noted that—and lifted out the ledger with two fingers, the way one handles something between evidence and relic.

"Do you know what I do, Miss Nakamura?"

"You catalog things."

"I read them." He set the ledger flat. "Cataloguing is clerical work. Reading is intelligence. A man's letters tell you what he thinks. His accounts tell you what he does. And a document like this—" he rested his palm on the cloth cover without opening it, letting the silence do its work "—tells me what a town has been thinking while my government wasn't watching."

"It's a hospital record," she said. "Of deaths."

"Then we'll agree easily." He opened it.

* * *

Corliss read the way he imagined surgeons worked: systematically, without flinching, from the first page forward. He had spent eleven years in military intelligence and had learned early that the dangerous documents were never the ones marked *secret*. Those were written by professionals, for professionals, and betrayed nothing their authors didn't intend. The dangerous ones were private. Diaries. Ledgers. Love letters kept too long. Documents made by people who believed no one else would ever read them, and which therefore contained the truth entire.

This one began on the fourth of March, 1911.

*Private Tomas Beck, 2nd Garrison Company. Age, per muster roll, nineteen. Age, per his own account to nursing staff, seventeen. Died of pleurisy, 4 March. Not entered in garrison returns.*

He turned pages. The hand was fine and disciplined, the entries uniform: name, regiment, cause, date, and always that final line—*not entered*—repeated like a refrain, like a bell struck at intervals.

By the ninth page he had stopped seeing it as sentimentality. He saw columns. He saw that the deaths clustered by week, and that the clusters mapped onto supply convoys he had personally requisitioned. He saw regiments named in full—regiments currently deployed along the frontier, their strength figures a matter of strategic calculation. He saw, entered beside certain names in a smaller hand not Zephyrine's but another's—a foreigner's, the letters narrow and precise, Dutch or Flemish by the formation—notations of place. *Convoy road below the quarry. The ford at Varennes freezes last week of December.* Geography. Routes. Weaknesses.

And he saw the compiler's own name on the flyleaf, in her careful hand: *Zephyrine Nakamura, Nurse, Saint-Aubin Garrison Hospital.*

A half-caste, the file said. Father Japanese, resident of a nation whose navy kept British admirals awake at night. Employed four years inside a garrison hospital. Keeping a private register of troop deaths annotated with terrain observations, in collaboration with a foreign national—Elias Verhoeven, cartographer, subject of a neutral state bordering the enemy, currently—

Corliss closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a room where something is sleeping.

He had seen this object twice now, and each time it meant something different. In the inventory it had been *one (1) bound notebook, cloth, containing handwritten entries*. An item. A weight in grams. Opened, it was a map of everything his side did not want known about itself, kept by a woman the army trusted to pour its soldiers' medicine, annotated by a man who drew maps—the maps, he now recalled, that hung framed in the commandant's corridor, admired by every officer who passed.

The same object. Two meanings. He understood that whichever meaning won would be decided not by the truth of it but by who held the pen when the classification stamp came down. That was, when it came to it, the whole of intelligence work.

He poured himself coffee from the pot on the sideboard and did not offer her any.

* * *

Zephyrine watched him read, and what she felt was not fear, not yet, but a stranger sensation: she was watching a man she had bandaged a splinter from last autumn—the cut from opening a crate—handle the dead of Saint-Aubin as though they were cargo.

She knew every entry by heart. She knew that page thirty-one was Elias's handwriting, the notation about the ford, made at her request because she'd asked him, laughing, *where does all this happen?* and he had shown her, because he was a cartographer and showing people where things were was his way of loving the world. She knew that the boy on the first page was called Tomas and had lied about his age to escape a farm where there wasn't enough to eat, and that his mother had never been found.

Corliss turned a page. Turned another. Somewhere around page forty his reading changed—she saw it in his shoulders, a settling, the shift from courtesy to purpose—and she understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that he was no longer looking at grief.

He was looking at a network.

"The convoy road below the quarry," he said mildly. "Who wrote that?"

"A friend. A patient. He recovered and went home."

"His name."

"Elias Verhoeven. He's a mapmaker. He drew the survey maps hanging in your commandant's corridor—your own officers commissioned them."

Something flickered across Corliss's face, quickly mastered, and she realized the maps' excellence had perhaps been noted before, and by whom, and what it cost a man to draw well for one government while owing allegiance to another.

"And the deaths themselves," Corliss said. "Why record them privately? There is an official system."

"There is an official system," she agreed, "that records forty-one deaths for a period in which this hospital buried sixty-three."

The silence stretched. The lamp hissed. Outside, a bugle sounded the morning call, absurdly bright.

"You understand," Corliss said at last, and his voice had changed—not crueler, exactly, but colder, the voice of a man laying cards face-up, "how this reads from where I sit. A woman of enemy descent. A foreign cartographer. Deaths catalogued by regiment. Terrain notes beside the names of units presently on the frontier. If I showed these pages to a tribunal without telling them anything else, what would they say it is?"

She looked at the open book—at Tomas Beck's name, at the small neat *seventeen* she had written beside the official *nineteen*, the only place on earth where his real age survived—and felt the vertigo take her fully at last. It was the same book. Nothing in it had changed since yesterday, since three years ago, since the night she'd first dipped her pen. Every line meant what it had always meant. And none of it meant that anymore. Meaning, she understood now, did not live in the ink. It lived in the reader, and the reader had changed, and the country had changed, and the same pages that had been an act of love at midnight were, by lamplight, in an army office, treason.

"It's a ledger of the dead," she said. "It says: these men existed. Someone washed their bodies and wrote down their names. That is all it has ever said."

"That is not all it says." He closed it. "But we'll come to what else it says. You'll have time to consider. You'll be restricted to the hospital grounds until further notice."

"Major." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "There are three hundred and eleven names in that book. When you are finished deciding what they mean, I would ask you to remember what they are."

Corliss looked at her for a long moment—the first time, she thought, that he had actually looked rather than examined—and then he stacked the ledger square with the edge of his desk, squared it again, and said:

"Dismissed."

* * *

That evening the order came down, typed on a form she'd never seen before, stamped twice, posted on the hospital board among the laundry schedules:

*By direction of the GOC: all private registers, diaries, and correspondence within the requisitioned district are subject to seizure as documents of military value. Possession of undeclared records will be treated as espionage under Article—*

She read it twice. Around her, nurses copied out the notice in their own hands, frowning over the word *espionage*, and one of them, young Sister Aline, said, "Whatever can they mean by it? Who keeps secrets in a hospital?" and laughed a little, and no one answered her.

Zephyrine walked back past the ward windows, where the lamps were being lit row by row, and understood that the ledger was no longer hers. Whatever happened next—whatever tribunal, whatever charge—the book had crossed a border of meaning from which objects do not return. It was evidence now. It would be argued over. Men in rooms like the east office would decide whether the dead of Saint-Aubin had died of pleurisy or of conspiracy.

Unless someone moved it first.

She stood in the darkening corridor with her hands folded, wearing the calm face she wore over dying men, and began, very quietly, to plan—while upstairs, in the east office, Major Corliss sat late over the ledger by lamplight, writing out a list of questions, the first of which concerned one Elias Verhoeven, and whether a warrant could cross a border faster than a warning.
