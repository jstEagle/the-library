## 6. The Interrogation Room

They gave her three days in a cell with a window, which she understood to be a courtesy and also a message. The window faced the harbor. Beyond the glass, the requisitioned fishing boats sat at anchor under gray flags, and beyond them the sea did what the sea had always done, indifferent to paperwork. Zephyrine counted the boats each morning. Eleven, then nine, then seven. She did not let herself think about where they went or what they carried. She counted because counting was a form of prayer, and prayer was the only property they had not yet inventoried.

On the third day a guard brought her tea in a tin cup and told her, without looking at her, that the Major would see her now.

* * *

The interrogation room had once been the hospital's linen office. She knew it by the hooks on the wall, empty now, where the inventory of sheets had hung in spring. Someone had moved in a table, two chairs, an oil lamp with a green shade that turned every face at the table slightly ill. Corliss sat on one side with a folder squared before him like a man about to say grace.

He was younger than his rank suggested, with the kind of neatness that comes from discipline rather than vanity—buttons aligned, hair cropped, hands quiet on the folder. He gestured to the other chair.

"Sit, please."

She sat. The chair was cold through her skirt. She folded her hands in her lap the way she did at the bedside of dying men, and realized she had done it automatically, and decided not to correct herself. Let him read what he liked into it.

"You've been comfortable," he said. "As comfortable as circumstances allow."

"The window is nice."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite amusement, more the acknowledgment of a point scored and conceded. He opened the folder.

"Zephyrine Nakamura. Nurse, civilian, employed at the Saint-Aubin garrison hospital since 1908. Father Japanese, a merchant seaman, deceased. Mother French, deceased. No dependents. No church affiliation." He looked up. "You'll forgive me. I read files the way you'd take a history. I want to be accurate before I say anything that matters."

"Accuracy is good," she said. "In my profession we call it charting."

"I know," he said. "I've been reading yours."

He turned the folder around. Her charts, page after page of them, copied in a clerk's careful hand: temperatures, pulses, medications, notes in her small upright script. And threaded among them, flagged in red, the entries from the ledger—the dead boys, the dates, the regiments, the causes of death written plainly where the official reports said *dysentery* when she had written *starvation*, or said nothing at all where she had written *shot attempting escape, aged seventeen*.

"Do you know what I do?" Corliss asked. "Officially?"

"You find things out."

"I reconcile accounts," he said. "The army keeps records. The records must match reality, or the records must match orders. When they don't match—" he tapped the red flags "—there's a discrepancy. Discrepancies have authors."

"The discrepancies have names," Zephyrine said. "That's rather the point of my ledger."

He studied her for a moment. The lamp hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, and the sound arrived in the room like a small tide.

"Here is what puzzles me," he said. "You are not a fool. Your charts are the best in the hospital—I've seen the physicians' evaluations. You understood exactly what you were doing. So help me understand it. A half-foreign woman, alone, in an army town, keeping a private record of everything the army has decided not to record. What were you going to do with it? Publish? Smuggle it to the enemy? Who paid you?"

"No one paid me."

"Then why?"

She thought about how to answer him, and understood that any answer she gave would be weighed as evidence, and decided that this was acceptable, because it was true.

"Because a boy died here in March of 1911," she said, "and no report was ever filed. His name was Anton Reyer. He was seventeen. He came from a village near Varennes where his mother still sets a place at the table, as far as I know, because no one ever told her otherwise. If I don't write it down, then the only person in the world who knows he existed is me. And I am not sufficient. No single person is sufficient. Paper is sufficient."

Corliss was quiet for a long moment. Then he closed the folder, and his voice changed—lost its procedural edge, dropped half a register, became almost human.

"That's a fine answer," he said. "It might even be a true one. But it isn't the answer that matters today." He drew a second document from the drawer beneath the folder, and laid it flat between them. "Do you recognize this name?"

She looked. Of course she recognized it. **VERHOEVEN, ELIAS J.** Cartographer, subject of the Kingdom of —, resident abroad eleven years. Below the name, a list of charges assembled in the dry language of intelligence: possession of strategic maps; correspondence with foreign nationals; association with a suspected agent; *complicity in the compilation of seditious documents*.

Seditious documents. Three years of her handwriting, bound in cloth.

"He taught me to keep it organized," she said. "By regiment and date. That's all he did."

"That's not all he did." Corliss leaned forward slightly. "His maps show the coastal roads your ledger's geography implies. Together, the two documents constitute—" he paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—a picture. Pictures are dangerous, Nurse Nakamura. Individually these are papers. Together they are treason."

"Together," she said slowly, "they are the truth, arranged so it can be checked."

"In wartime, those are the same thing." He said it without relish, which somehow made it worse. He was a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy but trusted completely. "Which brings me to why you're here, and why you've had a window for three days instead of a wall."

She waited.

"Testify," Corliss said. "Formally, on record, against Verhoeven. State that he solicited the information, that the ledger was compiled at his direction, that he intended it for transmission to a hostile power. Do that, and the sedition charge against you dissolves. You walk out of here a witness, not an accused. You keep your position, such as it remains. You keep your freedom." He slid the paper toward her. There was a line at the bottom for her signature, and the line looked very long, the way lines do when they are meant to be crossed. "He is conscripted already. He'll fight for the other side regardless of what you say. Your testimony costs him little he hasn't already lost—and it saves you everything."

Zephyrine looked at the signature line for a long time.

She thought of Elias as she had known him, in peacetime: fever-thin in the convalescent ward, teaching her to draw coastlines with a ruling pen, laughing softly at how seriously she took the weight of a line—*a map is a promise*, he'd said, *every border is a sentence someone wrote and someone else agreed not to argue with*. She thought of the pact, made over the ledger one evening while rain worked at the windows: whoever survives keeps the names. Not *whoever survives testifies*. Not *whoever survives saves himself*.

Keeps the names.

She looked up at Corliss, and found that she was calm—not the calm of numbness but the calm of a woman who has finally located the exact center of herself and can stand on it.

"No," she said.

"Nurse Nakamura—"

"No," she said again, and there was nothing dramatic in it, no tremor, no lifted chin. It was the tone she used to tell families a patient had died. "You're asking me to make his name mean something it doesn't. I won't do that with his name, and I won't do it with theirs either."

"Whose?"

And she began, quietly, to recite.

"Anton Reyer. Lucien Fabre. Tomas Wren. Henri Delacour. Private Okonkwo, first name unrecorded, regimental number 4471. Yusuf Demir. Pieter Van Loo. Auguste Renard. Jean-Baptiste Oury. Miklós Szabó—"

"What are you doing?" Corliss asked, though his voice had gone carefully neutral, the way voices go when a man realizes he is present at something and does not yet know what.

"—Emile Gauthier. Samuel Adeyemi. Konrad Weiss. Franciszek Nowak. Dmitri Volkov. Laurent Aubry. Seamus Doyle. Rafael Costa. Jan Kowalski. Tomás Ferreira—"

"Stop."

She did not stop. The names came steadily, evenly, one breath apiece, the way she had learned to count compressions, the way she had learned to count the boats in the harbor—a rhythm that held a person together while the world tried to come apart. Some of the names she said with full particulars, dates and regiments and causes; some she could only offer naked, the surname alone, the boy behind it reduced by distance and time to that single syllable of existence. It did not matter. Each name went onto the air of the linen office like a stitch into cloth.

Corliss let her go on for some time. She counted, somewhere beneath the recitation—that part of her never stopped counting—forty-one names before he stood up, and forty-seven before he put his palm flat on the table, and sixty-three before he spoke again.

"This is obstruction," he said. "You understand that. This is you obstructing a lawful proceeding."

"Sixty-four," she said. "Étienne Marchal. Sixty-five. Gabriel Osei. Sixty-six—"

"I offered you leniency."

"—Henrik Dahl. Sixty-seven. Aleksy Zieliński. Sixty-eight—"

"Nurse." His voice cracked, very slightly, on the title—not with anger, she thought, but with something adjacent to awe, or adjacent to grief, or adjacent to both, and she understood in that instant that Major Corliss was a man who reconciled accounts because he could not bear unreconciled things, and that she had just placed sixty-eight unreconciled things on the table between them and was not finished.

She stopped anyway. Not because he asked. Because sixty-eight felt like enough for one sitting; the rest deserved a fresher audience, and she intended there to be one.

She folded her hands again. The lamp hummed. Outside, faintly, a boat horn sounded on the water, long and low, the sound a harbor makes at dusk regardless of whose flag flies over it.

"I will sign nothing," Zephyrine said. "But I will tell you something for free, since you value accuracy. Every name I just said was a living man who came through that door expecting to be recorded honestly, and was not. Your army lost the war against those men before any army ever fires a shot—it lost them on paper, in rooms like this one, by men like you, deciding which truths fit the form. I kept their names because somebody had to. Elias Verhoeven helped me arrange them because he believed, as I believe, that a name written down is a debt the world owes and cannot repudiate. You may charge me. You may shoot me. But I will not convert his name into your currency, and I will not spend theirs."

Silence. The lamp hissed faintly. Down the corridor, boots passed and faded.

Corliss gathered the papers. He squared them, tapped them flush, and slid them back into the folder with movements so controlled they seemed rehearsed—as though precision were the only dignity available to him just now. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"There's a transport leaving for the interior Thursday," he said. "Prisoners and documents go together. Inventory list is already typed." A beat. "Your ledger is item forty-one."

Then he was gone, and the guard came, and Zephyrine returned to her cell with its merciful window, where she lay awake counting boats until the dark took even that from her.

Seven remained at anchor. Item forty-one. Thursday.

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass and began, silently, from the beginning: *Anton Reyer. Lucien Fabre. Tomas Wren.* Somewhere across the border, she did not doubt, a man was saying them too.

Three days. Whoever survived keeps the names—and she intended, God willing and Madame Okafor willing, to be the one who did.
