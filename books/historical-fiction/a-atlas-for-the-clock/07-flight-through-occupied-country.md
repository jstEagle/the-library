## 7. Flight Through Occupied Country

The needle went through the ticking like it was going through skin. Madame Okafor sewed the way she did everything—without hurry, without mercy—her thick fingers drawing the waxed thread tight, and Zephyrine held the ledger flat against the sacking with both hands as if it were a wound that might close wrong.

"Breathe out," Madame Okafor said, and pushed the needle home.

The mattress was a dead thing now, stripped of its straw, stuffed instead with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth and then in wool, and around it enough horsehair to make the whole bundle read as nothing. A mattress for a refugee cart was not searched. A mattress was lifted, thumped, dropped. Nobody wanted to know what was inside a mattress.

"You understand," Madame Okafor said, biting the thread, "if they open it, we are both hanged."

"They won't open it."

"That is not understanding. That is hoping." She smoothed the seam flat with her palm, an enormous dark hand on the grey ticking, and looked up at Zephyrine with eyes that had seen three ports and two husbands buried. "I have smuggled salt through customs and my sister's children through a checkpoint at night. The trick is never to carry the thing you love. Carry what you can afford to lose, and put what you love inside it."

Zephyrine thought of that afterward, on the road, many times. It was good advice. It was also impossible. There was nothing left she could afford to lose.

* * *

They left before dawn on a Tuesday, in the column of refugees that had begun forming outside the granary—carts, wheelbarrows, prams with the wheels tied with rags so they wouldn't squeak. The occupation had learned to let people leave; it even stamped their papers, because departure was a kind of arithmetic too. Every family gone was one less mouth, one less witness.

Zephyrine wore her nurse's cloak still. It bought her a place near the front of the column, beside a farmer's cart drawn by a horse too old for requisition to bother with, and on the cart, lashed under tarpaulin, the mattress. Madame Okafor walked beside it with a hand resting on the ticking, casual as a woman steadying laundry.

The first checkpoint was at the mill, two kilometres out. Lanterns, sandbags, a young corporal with a clipboard who looked colder than anyone deserved to be.

"Papers."

Zephyrine gave him her travel pass, her hospital letter, her identity card with its photograph taken four years ago when she'd been someone else entirely.

"Profession?"

"Nurse. Saint-Aubin garrison hospital."

He wrote it down. He was writing down everyone's profession, she realized. Somewhere behind his clipboard there was a ledger too, and hers was in it, and somewhere behind that ledger was Major Corliss, who had turned pages slowly with clean hands while she sat across the table and watched her life's work become evidence against her.

"Contents of the cart?"

"Bedding," said Madame Okafor. "Clothing. A sewing machine, if you want it, but you'll have to lift it yourself."

The corporal almost smiled. He pressed the mattress with the back of his hand, the way you test flour, and moved on down the line.

It was only a kilometre past the mill that Zephyrine stopped hearing the road. She heard instead the names, running under her footsteps like water under ice. Anselm Dubreuil, seventeen years old, pleurisy, struck from the rolls because he'd lied about his age and so officially had never existed. Sergeant Pieter Maas. Corporal Halloran. Little Tomas Wren, whose mother had died believing he was on leave. Three hundred names by now, give or take, each one entered in her small even hand, dated, witnessed where possible—and one name, near the end, written in a different ink because Elias had added it himself the last week he was well: *Verhoeven, E., cartographer, fever, recovered.* A joke, then. A private joke between them, that he should be in the book of the dead as proof he wasn't in it.

Now he was in worse books. Now his name was on a list of traitors, printed, distributed, and the joke had curdled into something neither of them could take back.

The column walked. Sleet came sideways off the sea marshes. Nobody spoke much. Refugees learn quickly the economy of silence.

* * *

Elias Verhoeven had been a soldier for eleven weeks and had hated every hour of it with a completeness that surprised even him, because he had expected to hate it and hatred, once expected, becomes merely furniture.

What he had not expected was how much of soldiering was paper.

His regiment kept lists. That was the bitter engine at the centre of everything: his own country's army kept lists of suspected collaborators, and his name was on one, captured from a garrison office at Saint-Aubin in the first week of occupation, copied, circulated. *Verhoeven, Elias. Cartographer. Long residence enemy territory. Maps of border districts in his hand recovered. Suspected intelligence contact.* His maps—the ones he'd drawn for pleasure, for precision, for the pure joy of a coastline coming right under the pen—filed under sedition. And beneath his name, in the margin of the captured list, in a clerk's hand: *associate: Nakamura, Z., nurse. Ledger confiscated.*

He had read that line perhaps two hundred times. He knew the shape of her surname in that clerk's careless script better than he knew his own face.

Desertion, he understood, was not a decision. It was a slope. First he had asked his captain for leave and been refused. Then he had requested transfer to the mapping corps and been refused, because a man with his file did not get maps. Then he had begun walking at night along the supply roads, learning the patrol timings, telling himself it was habit, it was restlessness, it was anything except preparation. By the time he admitted what he was doing, he was already doing it.

He went over the wire on a Thursday in falling snow, taking nothing but bread, a compass, and a notebook bound in grey cloth—blank, bought in a market two months earlier for reasons he hadn't examined until the night he opened it and began to write.

*Maas, Pieter, sergeant,* he wrote, by lantern light in a culvert, copying from memory the names Zephyrine had recited to him across a hospital bed two winters ago, when fever had loosened her tongue and she had told him, ashamed, about the ledger, and he had sat up and said, *Then organize it, if you're going to commit the crime, do it properly* —and taught her regiments and dates and columns.

She would go to the border. He was certain of it. She had promised him no name would be lost while either of them lived, and Zephyrine Brightwater did not break promises; she filed them. Which meant she would try to reach the neutral archive before the year closed, which meant the border road, which meant Varennes, the last crossing before the marshes.

A deserter and a woman carrying treason, both making for the same bridge in the same week of winter. If he could not save her from the promise, he could at least walk it beside her, or ahead of her, or fail in front of her, which amounted to nearly the same thing.

He wrote one more line in the grey book, the first entry that was not a name:

*If found, this book contains testimony only. No orders. No codes. I am a mapmaker. I record where things are.*

Then he blew out the lantern and walked north.

* * *

On the third day the column scattered.

There was no battle, nothing so dignified. A patrol truck came up the verge at dusk with its headlamps blazing, soldiers leapt down, and the human river simply broke its banks—carts into ditches, families into beet fields, everyone running anywhere that wasn't lit. Zephyrine lost sight of Madame Okafor for six minutes that lasted a season, and found her again by voice, that great calm voice cutting through the panic: *"Girl. Girl. Here."*

They lay in a drainage ditch with the mattress half-covering them like a lid, sleet ticking on the tarpaulin, while boots ran the road above and a soldier shouted numbers at somebody. Zephyrine's face was in the wet ticking. Through the sacking she could smell the horsehair and, beneath it, faintly, the oilcloth, and beneath that—she was almost sure, she was choosing to be sure—the ghost of the hospital soap she had wrapped it in, and she thought: *they are shouting at each other ten feet above three hundred dead men and women, and they don't know, they will never know unless we fail.*

Madame Okafor's hand found hers in the dark and gripped it, hard, once, and let go.

When the trucks went, they rose and walked on.

* * *

The fourth night they slept in a barn with eleven other fugitives, and Zephyrine did not sleep at all. She lay listening to the breathing of strangers and doing sums. Two days to Varennes if the roads held. The archive closed its year on the thirty-first; after that, the neutral clerks went home to their own new year and the deposition window shut with it. Names delivered in January were history. Names delivered by midnight on the thirty-first were testimony, entered, permanent.

Somewhere east of her, she thought, Elias was moving too—if he moved at all, if he lived. She had no way to know. That was the design of war: it cut the wires between people and left the current humming in the broken ends.

"He's alive," Madame Okafor said into the dark, not opening her eyes. "You've been deciding that all night. I'm agreeing with you. Sleep."

"I didn't say anything."

"You didn't need to. It's very loud." She turned over. "Sleep, girl. Tomorrow we cross the last checkpoint, and after that it's marsh road and nobody's law, which is the best law there is."

* * *

Varennes announced itself as smoke—a thin thread of chimney smoke rising off the flat white land like a signature. Beyond it, the river, frozen bank to bank, and astride it the bridge: stone, narrow, older than any nation that claimed it. On the far side, unmarked on any map worth having, the neutral territory began. Half a kilometre of other people's peace.

They came down to it at midmorning, Zephyrine and Madame Okafor with the mattress on the old farmer's borrowed cart, and the bridge stood empty, and that emptiness was the loudest thing Zephyrine had ever heard.

"No sentries," Madame Okafor murmured.

"There's always sentries."

At the near end of the bridge, something lay in the snow. Dark coat. A figure on its side, half in the ditch, one arm flung toward the far bank as though it had been reaching.

And standing over it, stamping her boots against the cold, a woman in a major's greatcoat with a clipboard under her arm, waiting, patient as winter, at the exact middle of the only road out.
