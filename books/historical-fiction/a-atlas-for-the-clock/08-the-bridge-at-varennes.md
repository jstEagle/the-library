## 8. The Bridge at Varennes

**ZEPHYRINE**

The river at Varennes did not freeze so much as pretend to. Ice sheeted the slow bends near the banks, but at midstream the current kept a black seam open, breathing, refusing winter's paperwork. Zephyrine stood at the foot of the bridge with her collar up and the mattress ticking heavy under her coat—three hundred names stitched against her ribs—and understood that she had been walking toward this bridge since the day she opened the ledger.

She had told herself it was accident. That Madame Okafor had chosen the Varennes road because the checkpoints were thinner there, that refugees drifted like water and water finds the lowest ground. But she had known, in the wordless way nurses know a pulse is failing before the monitor says so, that Elias would come to the same conclusion. Two people keeping the same promise will arrive at the same place eventually. A promise has geography.

The bridge was iron, old, built for carts and now groaning under the boots of an army that had never been invited. Frost had furred its railings white. At the far end, a sentry box sat empty—empty being a temporary condition in occupied country, like clear skies.

She started across.

The planks over the iron deck rang differently under her boots than they had for the cart ahead of her. She counted footsteps because counting kept panic arithmetic instead of prophecy. Forty-one steps to the middle of the span. At thirty-seven, she saw him.

He came out of the river mist on the opposite end, moving fast, no longer in uniform—or rather, in a uniform stripped to its plainness: greatcoat without insignia, cap gone, hair longer than regulation had ever permitted. He had lost weight the way men lose weight when they eat standing up and sleep sitting down. He stopped dead when he saw her, and the whole war contracted into the twelve meters between them.

"Zeph."

"You're supposed to be east," she said. "They posted you east."

"They posted me where they wanted me," he said. "I read maps. I know what roads lead where." He glanced past her shoulder, up the road she'd come from, then down the black seam of open water below them. "You have it."

Not a question.

"I have the names."

"And I have—" He touched his own coat, where paper crackled flat and rectangular against his chest. "The orders. Corliss's requisition lists. The margin notes where the omissions were *authorized*. You were right about the handwriting. It's his. He signed the erasures himself."

Somewhere upstream, a whistle. Short, doubled—the sound a patrol makes when it wants another patrol to know it isn't alone.

Elias crossed the distance between them in four strides and took her hands, and even through two layers of glove she could feel how cold his were, colder than hers, cold as a man who had slept outdoors for weeks. His eyes went over her face the way he used to go over coastline: patiently, noting every change.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"For which part?"

"For all of it arriving at once." The whistle again, nearer. "Zeph. Listen. We can't both cross with everything. If they take one of us, they take the whole record. Names and proof together—that's a case. That's treason with evidence attached. Separated, each piece is just paper. Just grief."

She knew what he was going to say before he said it, because she had rehearsed this conversation alone in a hundred dark rooms without knowing she was rehearsing it.

"We split it," she said.

"The names stay with you. You're the one who can speak them—you know the faces behind every entry. I take the orders. I can testify to the geography; I drew half those maps, I can explain exactly what was erased from them and why." His thumb moved once across her knuckles. "And neither of us stops until we reach the archive. Whichever one arrives first starts the deposition. The other one corroborates."

"And if only one of us arrives?"

His jaw tightened. Below them the black seam of river breathed in and out.

"Then the names still get there," he said. "That was always the promise. Not that we'd both survive it. That the record would."

* * *

**ELIAS**

Later—he would think about this later, in the years after, when the war had grown teeth and everyone had a story about the moment their life divided—he would remember that there was no time for anything true. No declaration. No embrace worth the name. There was a whistle, and ice underfoot, and a ledger coming apart in his hands like a body being triaged: this wound is survivable, this one is not, work fast, work faster.

He pulled the maps and orders from his coat while she unpicked the stitches Madame Okafor had set into the mattress ticking. Her fingers shook—not fear, he realized, but the specific tremor of hands that had been sewing for three days without stopping. He took the folded pages of names from her, warm from her body, and felt the full obscene weight of what paper could weigh.

"Page forty-one," she said quickly. "The boy. Anselme Dubois, seventeen, died of a chest wound the report called *returned to duty*. If you get separated, if you can only carry one page out—it's page forty-one."

"It won't be one page."

"Elias."

"It won't be one page," he said again, gentler, because arguing with her about arithmetic had never once worked. "But noted. Page forty-one."

Boots on the road now. Not running yet—patrols walked, patrols were lazy, patrols assumed the world belonged to them—but walking in their direction, and the bridge offered exactly two directions and both ended in barbed wire or bayonets unless you were very fast or very lucky.

"North bank," Elias said. "There's a culvert past the mill, fifty meters downstream. It comes out in the reed beds. I mapped it in September." He almost smiled. "I mapped it for the army. Let them fund their own defeat."

"You'll fit?"

"I'll fit." He looked at her then with an expression she had seen once before, the night before his fever broke—a look that held everything a man decides not to spend. "Zephyrine Nakamura. When this is over—"

"Don't," she said. "Say it at the archive. Say it on neutral ground with witnesses. Make it part of the record."

A sound escaped him that might have been a laugh in peacetime. "Everything's a deposition with you."

"Everything's evidence," she corrected. "That's what they taught us. We're just choosing what it proves."

The patrol rounded the bend by the granary. Four soldiers, rifles slung, breath steaming. One of them raised a hand—not alarm yet, just the reflex of authority: *halt, identify, explain*.

Elias pressed the mattress ticking back into her arms, names and all, and stepped backward toward the north railing.

"New Year's Eve," he said. "The archive at the border. Whatever happens."

"Whatever happens," she said.

Then he turned and ran, and she watched him swing over the railing with a mapmaker's economy—no wasted motion, no drama—and drop toward the culvert and the reeds and the long road east that led, God willing, to the same room she was trying to reach.

* * *

**ZEPHYRINE**

The soldiers reached the bridge in under a minute. By then Zephyrine had done the thing she had decided to do somewhere around step twenty-nine of the span, the decision forming in her before her mind admitted it: she walked *toward* them.

"Halt!" The corporal in front had a young face above an older voice. "Madame, this bridge is closed to civilian traffic."

"Is it?" She let exhaustion do her acting for her; there was very little left to fake. "No one told me. My sister is ill in Varennes, I've been nursing her a week, I'm going home to my mother for the new year. Am I arrested for that?"

She watched him weigh it. She had spent six years watching men weigh things—the field surgeon weighing amputation against sepsis, the quartermaster weighing rations against morale—and she knew the anatomy of the calculation. Risk versus paperwork. Effort versus reward. A woman with a mattress roll and frostbitten cheeks was not worth a form filed in triplicate.

"Sister's name?"

"Marguerite. Ask anyone at the farm past the church." She had memorized the farm on the way through, had memorized the church, the well, the dog that barked twice. A lie holds only if it has load-bearing details.

The corporal glanced at the mattress roll, unimpressed by bedding. Behind him, the youngest soldier stared out at the river, bored, and Zephyrine thought with a strange clarity: *somewhere in my ledger there may be a boy like you, struck from a report, and you don't know it yet.*

"Go on, then," the corporal said. "South side. And madame—no crossing back."

"No crossing back," she agreed, and meant something else entirely.

She walked off the bridge onto the south road with the names against her heart and the wind pushing her forward like a hand between her shoulders. She did not look back at the culvert. Looking back was a luxury for people whose records were safe.

Behind her, faintly, she heard the patrol regrouping, a radio crackling, someone saying words she couldn't make out—*saw someone, maybe, by the water*—and then nothing, just the river and the road and the enormous gray morning.

Three hundred names south. Three hundred proofs north. One promise, halved, like bread shared so that neither mouth goes empty.

She counted her steps again. It was going to be a long way to New Year's Eve, and somewhere on a parallel road, a man who kept maps of everything was carrying the other half of the truth through reeds and frozen mud, and she would not see him again until the century turned—

—if the border guards believed a nurse with a mattress roll.

—if a deserter could outrun his own description.

—if page forty-one survived the week.

She walked faster.
