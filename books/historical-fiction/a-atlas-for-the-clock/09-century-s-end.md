## 9. Century's End

The border announced itself as a smell before a sight—woodsmoke and wet wool, the particular staleness of a customs shed where too many people had waited too long in the cold. Zephyrine came down the last hill with the refugees at dusk on the thirty-first of December, her feet gone past pain into a numbness she knew from triage work was dangerous, and the ledger rode against her chest inside her coat, sewn there now instead of in the mattress, because Madame Okafor's stitching had been meant for roads with fewer checkpoints.

The line moved like something wounded. Ahead of her, a woman held a child who no longer cried, only stared. A farmer argued over a crate of hens with a clerk who kept stamping papers out of habit, stamps that meant nothing anymore on either side of the wire. The neutral side was forty meters away. She could see the flag, could see the lamp lit in the archive building—a squat stone structure that had been a tithe barn once, then a school, and now, by some treaty clause nobody had imagined needing, a repository where civilians could deposit testimony for safekeeping until "the restoration of order."

Restoration of order. She turned the phrase over like a coin she suspected was shaved.

The guard at the checkpoint looked at her papers—a laundress's transit card, forged well enough by Madame Okafor's nephew—and then at her face, which she made deliberately blank, the face of a tired woman going nowhere important. He waved her through. Forty meters. She walked them slowly, not because her legs were bad but because she wanted to feel each one, the way you count a pulse.

Inside, the archive smelled of cold paper and lamp oil. A single clerk sat behind a desk scarred with ink, an old man with a hearing trumpet he didn't use and spectacles he did. Behind him, shelves rose to the rafters: ledgers, deed boxes, bundles tied in string. The accumulated paperwork of people who had crossed here believing that paper might outlive the century.

"I want to make a deposition," Zephyrine said.

The clerk looked up without hurry. "Depositions are taken until six o'clock."

"What time is it?"

"Half past five." He glanced at the clock on the wall—a fat municipal clock, its face yellowed, its hands steady in a way nothing else in the world seemed to be. "You'll want to be thorough, then. Thoroughness takes longer than people expect."

She unbuttoned her coat. Her fingers were clumsy; she used her teeth on the last button, the way she'd done a thousand times pulling on surgical gloves. The ledger came out wrapped in oilcloth, and when she set it on the desk and unwound it, the clerk went very still.

He had seen books before, obviously. But he looked at this one the way she had learned to look at a wound that was worse than the uniform suggested.

"Names," she said. "Three hundred and eleven names. Soldiers who died in the garrison hospital at Saint-Aubin between March 1910 and July of this year, whose deaths do not appear in any official report. I recorded them myself. I witnessed each death. I can give date, regiment where known, cause, and the name as it was spoken to me or found on their effects." She paused. "I'd like them entered into the record. Read aloud. So they're testimony, not just paper."

The clerk removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which gave him time, she understood, to decide something. "Read aloud," he repeated. "That's unusual. Writing suffices, legally."

"Writing burns," Zephyrine said. "Writing gets confiscated. I've watched it happen. If it's spoken into your record—if it's heard—then it happened in front of someone. That's different."

"It will take two hours, perhaps three."

"I have the rest of my life."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but related to one, the way a distant fire is related to warmth. He took a fresh ledger from his shelf, opened it, dipped his pen. "State your name and occupation for the record."

"Zephyrine Nakamura. Nurse. Saint-Aubin garrison hospital."

"Nakamura," he said, writing it carefully. "You understand this archive is neutral ground. What is entered here cannot be used as evidence in any belligerent court—but neither can it be suppressed by one. It will sit on these shelves until someone comes looking."

"That's all I'm asking for. That it sits somewhere, waiting."

He nodded once, and began.

* * *

She read the names aloud.

It went slower than she'd thought, because she would not hurry. Each name got its full weight: *Private Adrien Fossé, Fourth Infantry, died the ninth of March, 1910, fever, seventeen years old.* Her voice cracked on the first few and steadied after, finding a register she hadn't known she had—the register of the ward at night, when she read casualty lists to men too broken to read them themselves. Outside, the light failed completely. The clerk's pen scratched. The clock ticked with a sound like a small patient heart.

Somewhere past the hundredth name, she stopped being aware of her frozen feet. Somewhere past the two-hundredth, she realized she was crying and that it didn't matter, that tears were simply another fluid the body produced under load, and she kept reading. *Corporal Léon Marchetti. Private Tomas Brandt. Gunner Yusuf Demir.* Names from four armies, because garrisons collect everyone, because war even in peacetime collects everyone. Men whose deaths had been filed as transfers, as desertions, as clerical errors. Men who had been, in the language of Major Corliss's requisition stamps, *adjusted.*

The door opened at half past seven.

She knew his footsteps before she saw him—that unhurried military tread, boots polished even on campaign. Corliss stood in the doorway with snow on his shoulders and two soldiers behind him, and the room's temperature changed, though the stove burned the same.

Zephyrine did not stop reading.

*Private Émile Rousseau, Second Artillery, died the fourth of June, 1911—*

"Nurse Nakamura." Corliss's voice was quiet, conversational, the voice he'd used across the interrogation table. "You're in violation of a requisition order. That document was seized property. Its removal constitutes theft of state records, at minimum. At maximum—" He let the sentence stand unfinished, the way he always did, letting her supply the worst word herself.

*Treason*, she supplied, and read the next name.

The clerk looked up at Corliss over his spectacles. "Sir, this is neutral territory. This building operates under the convention. Whatever authority you carry ends at that doorstep, along with the snow."

"I'm aware of where I'm standing." Corliss stepped inside anyway, and his soldiers stayed outside, which told her something. He approached the desk slowly, reading upside-down what the clerk had written, and she watched his eyes move down the column of names—names he had seen before, in the confiscated ledger, in the file labeled SEDITION, and she saw the vertigo take him again, the same object with its two irreconcilable meanings, except that here, on this side of the wire, the meaning had been decided, and it was hers.

"You understand what you've done," he said. "Three hundred names of dead soldiers, including enemy nationals, read aloud on neutral soil. Do you know who reads archives like this? Historians. Lawyers. Widows' solicitors." His voice stayed flat, professional. "In twenty years, when whatever treaty ends this war gets signed, someone will open that book and start asking why those men weren't reported. And the answers will lead back through Saint-Aubin. Through the hospital. Through the officers who ordered the adjustments."

"Yes," Zephyrine said.

"That's not a threat against me alone."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

They regarded each other across the desk, and the clock ticked into the silence, and she understood—finally, fully—what she was looking at. Not the man who had catalogued her life into inventory lists. A man who had spent months carrying the knowledge of what those omissions meant, and who had come through the snow on New Year's Eve not because he'd been ordered to, but because he needed to see where the names would land. Whether they would land anywhere at all.

"There were orders," he said at last. Very quietly. "Above my rank. The omissions were policy. I catalogued the ledger knowing that, and I entered it as evidence anyway, because evidence is what I was given to enter." He took off his cap. Without it he looked older, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "I could have classified it differently. A clerk's discretion. I told myself I didn't have one."

"And now?"

"Now I have a deposition to log." He turned to the old clerk. "Enter it as a civilian deposition. Voluntary statement of a noncombatant, given freely, witnessed." He paused. "Note the hour, the date, and the fact that the deponent completed her statement uninterrupted."

"Uninterrupted," the clerk said, writing it.

Corliss looked at Zephyrine one last time. There was something in his face she couldn't fully read—regret, or envy, or the specific exhaustion of a man who has just chosen, ambiguously, permanently, which side of a line he stands on. "For what it's worth," he said, "the man Verhoeven. The file on him is thin. Thin files get lost in wartime. It happens more than you'd think."

Then he was gone, and the door closed, and the cold came in briefly and left again, and Zephyrine discovered she was gripping the edge of the desk hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

*Thin files get lost.*

* * *

The clerk finished at ten minutes to midnight. Three hundred and eleven names, dates, regiments, causes. He blotted the page, signed it, dated it, and slid the fresh ledger onto the shelf beside a hundred years of other people's desperate paper, where it would sit in the dark and the dust, waiting, exactly as she'd asked.

"That's it, then," he said. "Entered and sealed. You'll want a receipt."

He wrote one out on a scrap of official paper, and she folded it into her coat where the ledger had been, against her heart, so the shape of it stayed familiar. Through the small window she could see the checkpoint lamps, the snow coming down soft and indifferent over both countries, and beyond the wire, the dark hills of the occupied side where Elias was either alive or not, free or not, keeping his own copy of every name or lying in a ditch with the wind going through him.

The clerk began banking his stove. "There's a New Year custom in this town," he said, not looking up. "At midnight, people open their windows. All of them. Even in winter. Let the year in clean, they say."

"Is that true, or something you tell strangers?"

"It's true enough." He gathered his coat. "Midnight's in ten minutes. I'd stay if I were you. First minutes of a new century—no sense spending them walking."

She stayed. When the church bell began, she pushed open the archive window and the cold poured in, clean and enormous, and somewhere below a child laughed, and somewhere beyond the border she imagined—chose to imagine—another window opening.

The bell finished. The year turned.

And in the doorway, snow-covered, breathing hard, standing upright through what must have been pure refusal to fall down, was a man holding a cloth-bound book to his chest like a wound he'd been carrying for miles.
