## 10. An Atlas for the Clock

*Zephyrine*

Spring came to Saint-Aubin the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and in patches. The shelled east wall of the hospital was still open to the sky, and the townspeople had planted lettuces in the rubble rather than argue about who would pay to rebuild it. Zephyrine walked past it every morning on her way to the temporary infirmary in the old customs house, and every morning she counted the rows of green out of habit, because counting had become what her hands did when her mind wanted to rest.

Three hundred names. She no longer needed the ledger to recite them, though she kept it close—kept it under her mattress, in fact, because Madame Okafor had sewn it into a mattress once and declared the method blessed by results. The archive at the border held the certified copy now, stamped *civilian deposition* in Corliss's neat hand, and there were mornings when Zephyrine woke and had to lie still for a full minute before she believed it: that the names existed outside her body, that they would go on existing if a shell took her tomorrow.

The war had not ended. Everyone said *the spring campaign*, everyone said *when it resumes*, the way you speak of weather coming. But Saint-Aubin sat behind the lines now, half-garrison, half-graveyard, and the requisition orders had thinned to routine paper. She had been reinstated—reinstated was the word; as if she had ever stopped—as senior nurse, and Major Corliss had been transferred west without farewell, leaving behind only a filing cabinet she was not permitted to open.

She was carrying a basin of water across the customs house courtyard when she saw him.

He stood by the gate in civilian clothes that fit him like an apology—a coat too heavy for April, a hat he clearly did not own so much as wear out of necessity. Thinner than she remembered. A scar along his jaw that had not been there before. He held a parcel wrapped in oilcloth against his chest the way soldiers hold rifles, and when their eyes met he did not smile, because neither of them was made for smiling first.

"You're late," she said, and set down the basin.

"By my count," Elias Verhoeven said, "I am exactly on time."

* * *

*Elias*

He had rehearsed the journey a hundred times in the months after Varennes—the bridge, the patrol's boots on frozen planks, the moment they split the ledger between them like men dividing bread. North through the forest road, then east, then the long detention camp where he gave his name to clerks who wrote it down wrong twice. Then the armistice of paperwork that passed for release, the border, the trains running again on schedules drawn by other men's maps. He had crossed eleven frontiers in nine months. Every one of them had asked him his profession.

*Cartographer,* he said, and watched them decide whether the word was a threat.

Now he stood in the courtyard of a hospital that smelled of carbolic and new plaster, holding the thing he had carried across all those lines, and found that the speech he had prepared dissolved entirely in her presence. That had always been the trouble with Zephyrine Brightwater. She made honesty feel like the only available language.

"Open it," she said.

He unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside was a second ledger—same cloth binding, bought from the same stationer in Saint-Aubin two years ago—and beneath it, folded, a sheet of drafting paper covered in fine pencil work.

Her hand hovered over the cover but did not touch it yet. "You copied them."

"I kept them." He heard how his voice caught and let it. "After the bridge, after they took your book and I ran north with mine—I started writing everything I knew from memory. Names, regiments, dates. Everything you'd taught me to organize. And then—" He stopped. "Then I learned some things you couldn't have known. Men who died on our side of the line. Boys from my own company whose officers filed them as deserters. I put them in too. I hope that doesn't spoil the arithmetic."

"Show me."

They went up to the room she rented above the dispensary—she would remember afterward only that the stairs were narrow and that she took them too fast—and they laid the two ledgers side by side on the table in the window light. Hers, water-stained at one corner from a river crossing she had never described to anyone. His, creased down the middle from nine months inside a coat.

Page by page, they compared.

The handwriting was different—hers small and upright, his slanted, a cartographer's hand—but the columns matched. Name. Regiment. Date. Where the report said *missing*, where it said *deserted*, where it said nothing at all. Three hundred names in hers. In his: three hundred, and then forty-one more in the back, added in pencil, in order, dated through the winter.

"The extra ones," he said quietly. "I didn't invent the system. You did. It seemed wrong to let them stand outside it just because they wore the other uniform."

Zephyrine turned to the last page of his ledger and read the pencilled names, and something in her chest that had been braced for two years finally unclenched. "No," she said. "It would be wrong to leave them out. Bring your pen."

* * *

*Zephyrine*

They worked until dusk, merging the books into a third—blank pages she had been saving, because she had never quite believed the story was finished. Forty-one names entered in both hands, alternating, like stitches. When it was done she sat back and looked at the three volumes arranged on the table and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, what they actually were. Not evidence. Not testimony anymore. An atlas. Not of borders—borders had been the whole problem—but of graves, and of promises, and of every place a person had been struck from the record and written back in by hand.

"Elias," she said. "Draw me a map."

He looked up, wary the way he always was when she used the vocabulary of his old trade. "Of what?"

"Of this. All of it. No frontier lines. No fortresses." She pulled the drafting paper from beneath his ledger and spread it flat. "Start with the bridge at Varennes. Then the archive at the border. Then every marked grave you know of, on either side, and every promise we know was kept. Make me a map of what survived instead of what was taken."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he reached into his coat and produced, from an inner pocket, a pencil worn to half its length—the same pencil, she realized, with which he had written forty-one names in the dark of a conscript's winter.

"It will need a legend," he said.

"Then we'll write one."

He began to draw. She watched his hand move—sure again now, the hesitation gone—and she saw that he was not drawing the coast or the roads or anything the quartermasters would recognize. He drew the hospital with its lettuce garden in the rubble. He drew the laundress's yard where Madame Okafor had unpicked a mattress seam by lamplight. He drew a small square for the bridge, a star for the archive door, and at each grave he knew—and he knew many—he made a mark so modest it could be mistaken for a comma, and beside each mark, in letters too small for any intelligence officer's lens, he wrote a name.

Time, she thought, watching him. They had spent two years being pushed around by it—by dates, deadlines, campaigns, the clock of the century winding toward whatever came next. But a clock only mattered if someone agreed to read it. This map refused. On this map, the twelfth of March meant a boy named Aubert had been buried by strangers who were kind, and that was the whole of its meaning, and it was enough.

Outside, the evening gun sounded from the garrison, and neither of them flinched.

"There's one more thing the legend needs," Elias said. He paused, pencil lifted, and for the first time since the gate he smiled—not much, but all the way. "A title. Maps are nothing without titles."

"A Atlas for the Clock," she said, and then laughed at herself. "No—that's not right. *An* Atlas for the Clock. I've been carrying the sentence around for months. I thought it might be a book someday."

"Whose name goes on it?"

She considered the three ledgers on the table. Three hundred and forty-one people who would never sign anything again. Two living witnesses who had crossed a continent to keep faith with them.

"All of ours," she said. "Yours and mine and theirs. Print it small. We'll make room."

He printed it in the lower corner, in his careful cartographer's letters, and beneath it the date—spring, 1914—and beneath the date, because he was Elias and could not leave a document unannotated, a single line:

*All names verified against two independent records.*

"That's not strictly true," she said. "Forty-one of them appear in only yours."

"Then we'd better go find the second record." He tapped the pencilled graves clustered near his own regiment's winter positions, east of the lines, in country still closed to civilians. "Some of those boys have mothers, Zephyrine. Somebody has to tell them where the commas are."

She looked at the map, at the small marks waiting beyond its edges for the names only the two of them could carry, and at the man who had brought his half of a promise across eleven frontiers to lay it next to hers. The war was not over. The clock was still running. But somewhere above the garrison the swallows were returning to the eaves of the customs house, and on the table lay proof, in ink and pencil and two stubborn hands, that time itself could be made to answer for what it took.

"Yes," Zephyrine said, and reached for her pen. "We'd better start in the morning."
