## 2. Instructions for a Stranger

The safehouse at Vellacourt was a miller's cottage once, before the war chewed the mill apart and spat its wheel into the river. Now it was a place between places — two days' walk from the eastern ford, close enough to neutral territory that a man could smell the difference in the air, or so Sebastian told himself each night as he counted the hours until his crossing was arranged. The cottage had one good room, a hearth that smoked when the wind came from the north, and a cellar where he'd cached the cipher satchel beneath a false floor of flagstones so old they had gone smooth as bread crust.

He had been there nine days. Nine days of waiting is its own kind of labor, and Sebastian Montrose had grown skilled at all the labors no one praised: the waiting, the watching, the small economies of fear. He rose before dawn out of habit rather than necessity and stood at the window with the shutter cracked to a finger's width, looking down the cart track that wound through bare orchards toward the road. Frost had silvered the fields overnight. The river ran gray beneath it, patient, indifferent, carrying whatever the war upstream chose to send it.

Nothing moved on the track. Nothing had moved on it for three days except a heron and, once, a boy driving two goats with the bored cruelty of boys everywhere. That was good. That was what safe meant: nothing moving, nothing new.

He made tea from the dregs of the dregs — the chancellery had taught him to brew a leaf four times before surrendering it — and drank it standing, because sitting invited thought, and thought, these days, arrived wearing black. He thought of the chancellery burning even now, probably; he had watched the smoke from the western hills for half a day's travel, a smudge on the sky like a bruise forming. Everything he had memorized in those last hours was still folded somewhere behind his eyes, in the vaulted dark where he kept the things that could kill men. He did not open that door more than he had to.

The knock, when it came, was not a knock.

It was smaller than that — a sound at the door like a bird striking glass, once, and then nothing. Sebastian set down the cup without drinking the last of it. His body performed the old sequence without consulting him: weight shifting off the boards near the window, which creaked; hand finding the knife beneath his coat; breath slowed to something unremarkable. He crossed the room and put his eye to the seam of the shutter instead of the door, angling for a view of the step.

The track was empty. The orchard was empty. And on the doorstep, in the frost, sat a basket.

For a long moment he simply looked at it, the way a man looks at a thing his mind refuses to sort. A basket. A wicker basket, the kind market women carried, with a cloth over the top drawn down at the corners. It had not been there the night before; he had checked the step at moonset, as he checked everything, because paranoia was not a fault in his trade but a professional standard. Someone had come up the track in the last grey hour and left it, and someone had struck the door once — softly, deliberately, the way you wake a sleeper you do not wish to frighten — and gone.

He waited a full quarter hour. The frost did not melt. The basket did not move. But twice — twice — he saw the cloth shift.

Not a basket, then. He opened the door.

* * *

The cold came in with him like an unpaid debt. Sebastian knelt on the stone step, knife still in his fist, and pulled the cloth back with two fingers.

A child. An infant, weeks old perhaps, wrapped in a shawl that had once been fine — he could see the ghost of embroidery at its hem, silk gone gray with washing — with a face so small and red with cold that his first coherent thought was absurdly practical: *it needs warmth before it needs anything else, including questions.* Her eyes were closed. Her breath came in fast, shallow puffs that fogged the air above her lips, and one fist had worked free of the wrappings and lay against her cheek like a claim she was making on the world.

There was no one on the track. No one in the orchard. Whoever had left her had walked backward in his own footprints or vanished into the river fog, and either way he was gone, and the child was here, and the temperature was falling.

Sebastian lifted her. She weighed almost nothing — less than the cipher satchel, he thought wildly, less than the paper that had burned in the chancellery, less than any of the enormous light things this war kept producing. She stirred at the transfer, frowned the terrible newborn frown that looks like ancient grief, and did not wake.

He got her inside, fed the stove until the room began to remember warmth, and held her while he worked, because putting her down felt like a decision he wasn't ready to make. It was only when the shawl had thawed and loosened that he found the note.

It was tucked beneath the wrappings at her shoulder, folded small and sealed with plain wax — no signet, no crest, which in Vellamare was itself a signature. Anyone of consequence sealed with consequence. Plain wax said: *I have money but no name I can spend.* He broke it with his thumbnail.

One line, in a hand he almost recognized and told himself he didn't:

*Forget this face.*

No name. No instruction. No plea for care, no threat, no explanation. Four words in ink gone slightly feathered at the strokes, written fast, by a hand that knew exactly how much to say and not one syllable more.

Sebastian read it three times. Then he read it again, because his trade had trained him to look beneath lines, and beneath this line there was nothing at all, and the nothing was deafening. Whoever wrote it had chosen those words out of every possible arrangement of sympathy and urgency. Not *keep her safe.* Not *she is innocent.* Not even *please.* *Forget this face* — an instruction aimed not at the child's survival but at the survivor's. At whoever found her. At whoever had, presumably, been intended to find her.

Because it had been intended. He understood that the way he understood cipher: structurally, before emotionally. A basket left at random lands wherever it lands. This basket had been left at a safehouse whose location existed in perhaps eleven living minds, at the one hour of night when a courier checks his door, with a note sealed in wax that expected literacy, discretion, and obedience. The child had not been abandoned. She had been *delivered*. Delivered the way he delivered things — carefully, deniably, into the hands of the one person the sender could not afford to contact directly.

Which raised the question he had been holding at arm's length since the cloth shifted: who knew this house? Who knew it and trusted it and had reason to believe that Sebastian Montrose, courier of a republic mid-collapse, would take in a stranger's child on four cold words?

He went through the wrappings again, slowly, by lamplight, while the infant breathed against his forearm in a rhythm that was already, treacherously, becoming familiar. The shawl, embroidered, once expensive. A second blanket, army wool, the coarse gray of the Third Division — he noted it and filed it and hated himself for noting it. A stoppered clay bottle, milk-scented. And pinned to the innermost fold, wrapped in a twist of linen as if someone had wanted to keep it from rattling —

Something hard. Small. Brass, by the weight of it, and cold as the doorstep had been.

He unwound the linen with the same two-fingered caution he had used on the cloth, and the lamp flame leaned, and the thing in his palm threw back a small fan of light, and the room went very quiet in the way rooms go quiet before a man's life divides into before and after.

A compass rose. Not a card, not a print — a casting, the size of a coin, worked in brass with a twelve-point star, the cardinal points worn bright from handling, the interstitial points still dark. Around its rim ran a ring of letters too small to read by lamplight without a glass, though he did not need the glass. He had read them years ago, under better skies, and had given the thing away with both hands and a joke, and had never once, in all the years and all the jokes since, forgotten a single word of what he'd said when he gave it.

His hands did not shake. That surprised him. He had seen men's hands shake at far less, and he had always supposed his own would, when whatever-it-was finally came for him. Instead his hands held the compass rose very steadily, and his heart performed some slow, seismic adjustment, like a building settling onto a new fault line, and he heard himself say aloud, in the empty room, in a voice he barely recognized:

"Aurelio."

The infant opened her eyes.

They were dark — dark as river water, dark as the inside of the vaulted place behind his own eyes — and they did not, of course, focus, did not see him, saw nothing yet of the world except light and blur. But they opened, and they were aimed at his face, and something in the animal architecture of the moment closed like a lock. Sebastian stood very still with a dead man's token in one hand and the dead man's daughter in the other arm, and outside the frost began, very quietly, to melt off the orchard branches, drop by drop, like a clock counting down somewhere just below hearing.

* * *

He should think, he told himself. Think the way the chancellery had taught him: no grief, no gratitude, no sentiment — only structure. Who knew. Who sent. Why now. What it costs.

But the compass rose sat on the table where he had placed it, and it would not become merely an object, and the thinking would not come cleanly. Every thread he pulled ended in the same knot. General Aurelio Vance had known about this house — had helped choose it, in fact, two years ago, standing in this same doorway with his collar up, laughing at the state of the mill. Vance had known Sebastian's routes, or enough of them. Vance had a wife who had died the previous winter of the fever that swept the capital, and a daughter born that same autumn whom Sebastian had never met, and an aide named Perrin who handled the general's dirty linens — the errands too sensitive for staff channels — and Perrin, Sebastian now realized, with the dull click of a lock turning, was the one other living person who knew about Vellacourt.

Plain wax. Army wool. A brass rose given away at a table full of wine, years ago, to a friend who had turned it over in his fingers and said, *You carry everyone else's directions; keep one pointing home.*

And the note. *Forget this face.* Written by a man who knew that his own name had become, or was about to become, the most dangerous word in the Republic — a man who therefore could not write his name, could not write *keep her*, could not give Sebastian anything that could be traced or refused. Only a task shaped so strangely that only one reader in the world would understand its shape. Forget this face. Meaning: *you know whose face this is. You know what it will cost to be seen with her. Do it anyway, and be no one doing it.*

Sebastian fed the stove again, though the room was warm. He did it because his hands wanted employment and because watching flames was easier than watching the compass rose. When he turned back, the infant had fallen asleep in the crook of a blanket he had arranged into something like arms, and her breath made the wool rise and fall, rise and fall, the smallest possible argument against the entire century.

He sat across from her and let himself, for precisely as long as it took the kettle to cool, remember nothing useful. Let himself feel only the raw geometry of it: a friend in ruin, reaching across a country on fire, and choosing to reach toward him. Out of everyone. Out of every name in every ledger, the traitor-general — if he was a traitor, said a voice in him that was already drafting appeals; *if* — had spent his last free resources, his last trusted servant, on delivering what he loved most to a man who carried papers for the government that was hunting him.

That was not strategy. That was faith. And faith, in Sebastian's experience, was heavier than any cargo he had ever hauled.

The light through the shutters climbed from gray to white. Somewhere downriver a cart passed, heading east, and he catalogued it automatically — one horse, iron rims, moving away — and then realized he had stopped listening halfway through, because the child had made a sound in her sleep. A small sound. A sound like the beginning of a word that hadn't decided what it wanted to be.

He picked the compass rose up off the table. Turned it in the daylight. There, around the rim, the inscription he had paid a jeweler in another life to cut:

*True north does not move. Men do.*

He had meant it as a joke at his own expense — the faithful courier flattering himself with permanence. Vance had taken it seriously, the way he took everything seriously, turning it in his fingers and saying quietly, under the noise of the other officers at the table, *Then I'll keep this, and when everything else moves, we'll know which of us to blame.*

Three years ago. Peacetime, or the last skin of it. Wine on a terrace above the harbor, before Meridian, before the massacres had names, before the word *traitor* attached itself to anyone Sebastian loved.

The infant slept. The compass lay in his palm, pointing nothing, pointing everything. And Sebastian Montrose, who had memorized the death warrants of forty men in a single burning evening without his pulse rising, sat down heavily at the miller's table and understood that the Republic's war for his soul had just opened a second front.

Because the dispatches would come — they always came, riding their routine horses on their routine roads — and when they came, they would say what the chancellery had been preparing to say since the western front collapsed. He had carried enough of those orders to guess the word that would be in them.

*Treason.*

And then he would have to decide which promise was the promise.
