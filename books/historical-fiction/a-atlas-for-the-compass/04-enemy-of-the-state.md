## 4. Enemy of the State

The dispatches came with the morning milk.

Sebastian heard the courier's horse before he saw it — the particular arrhythmic clatter of a government animal, shod badly and ridden worse — and by the time he had moved the child's basket behind the woodpile and arranged his face into bureaucratic boredom, the man was already at the gate, holding out a leather satchel stamped with the chancellery seal.

"Courier Montrose?" The rider was young, maybe nineteen, with the raw wind-scoured cheeks of someone who had ridden through the passes in November without being told he could refuse. "Two packets. One for the safehouse register, one marked *personal priority*. You're to sign both."

"Personal priority," Sebastian repeated, as though tasting something spoiled. In eleven years of service he had received perhaps four such packets, and three of them had preceded executions.

"The office said you'd know."

"I always know." He signed. The boy lingered, shifting in his saddle, glancing toward the house with the frank curiosity of the very young, and Sebastian understood that the glance was not idle. Someone had told this child to look. To see whether anything at the safehouse seemed wrong. Whether anything — or anyone — was out of place.

"Bad roads ahead of you?" Sebastian asked pleasantly.

"Worse than bad." The boy lowered his voice, leaning down from the saddle as if the trees might be listening. "They've closed the Meridian road entirely. Soldiers turning everyone back. And there's talk in Vellamare that—" He stopped himself, glanced again at the house, and straightened. "Safe travels, Courier."

He was gone before Sebastian could ask what talk. Which was, he suspected, precisely the point of telling him.

* * *

He did not open the packets inside. He carried them to the cold garden behind the safehouse, where the frost had killed the last of Maren's mother's roses and left the beds looking like a field after cavalry, and he sat on an overturned barrel with the child's basket within arm's reach and broke the first seal.

It was what he expected, which is to say it was worse than anything he had imagined.

*BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL COUNCIL OF THE REPUBLIC OF VELLAMARE: General Aurelio Vance, having conspired with foreign powers to prolong the war, having ordered the destruction of loyal garrisons, having betrayed the trust of the Republic whose uniform he wore, is hereby stripped of rank, title, and citizenship. He is declared an enemy of the state. Sentence of death is decreed. Any citizen found aiding, sheltering, corresponding with, or concealing the said traitor shall be guilty of treason in the second degree, punishable by—*

Sebastian stopped reading. Not because he could not bear the rest — he had read enough death warrants in his life to know their grammar by heart, the way a priest knows the liturgy — but because the child had woken.

She was watching him from the basket with those grave dark eyes that seemed too old for her face, her small fists curled against her cheeks, and she made no sound at all. She simply watched him read the paper that made her, by the letter of the law he had served for eleven years, a piece of contraband. A thing to be confiscated. A crime with a heartbeat.

"You," he said quietly, "are going to be the death of me."

She blinked. Somewhere beyond the wall, a cart rattled past on the frozen ruts, and the sound of it — ordinary, mortal, indifferent — made the words on the page feel suddenly less like ink and more like weather. Something vast and impersonal that had begun to move toward them and would not stop for pleading.

He read the rest. The list of Vance's alleged crimes ran to two full pages, and it was masterful work; whoever had drafted it understood that a lie does not need to be believed, only repeated in the tone of a ledger. *Conspiracy with foreign powers.* *Destruction of loyal garrisons.* *Correspondence with enemy agents.* Each charge was specific enough to seem documented and vague enough to be unfalsifiable, which was not the style of military justice but the style of ministers. Of men who sat in warm rooms and decided, between courses, who would die.

And beneath the charges, the signature that turned his stomach: Corvane. Minister of Provision. Signing away a general's life with the same hand that signed grain allotments.

Sebastian folded the warrant along its original creases, very carefully, as if it were something living that might startle.

* * *

The second packet he opened standing up, because some part of him already knew what it would contain and did not want to receive it sitting down like a condemned man.

It contained his own file.

Not all of it — a selection, curated, the way a surgeon selects. His commission papers. His oath of service, copied out in a clerk's careful hand. Eleven years of commendations, each one annotated now in red ink with dates and cross-references he could not read but could certainly feel: they had been through everything. Every letter he had ever carried, every border he had crossed, every name in every safehouse register from here to the coast. And at the bottom, in fresh ink, a single line:

*COURIER MONTROSE IS REMINDED OF HIS OATH AND OF THE PENALTIES ATTENDING ITS BREACH.*

No accusation. No question. Just the reminder, laid across his life like a knife laid across a table before dinner.

"They know," he said aloud, to the empty garden, to the dead roses, to the child. "Not about you. Not yet. But they know I'm here, and they know what I am, and they're counting my breaths."

The girl kicked one foot against the blanket, unimpressed by the machinery of state.

He thought about the young courier's lingering glance. About the closed Meridian road. About the phrase *personal priority*, which was not a classification any honest office used for a courtesy copy of a public decree. This packet had not been sent to inform him. It had been sent to *weigh* him. To see how he would move under the added weight — whether he would flinch, whether he would run, whether he would do exactly what a guilty man would do.

Which meant that somewhere behind him, patient as arithmetic, someone was already keeping books on Sebastian Montrose.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he had been trained to do, which was nothing visible. He chopped wood until his shoulders burned. He walked to the village well and drew water, exchanging twelve words with the miller's wife and memorizing, without appearing to, the fact that there were strangers at the inn who spoke with capital accents and asked questions about the eastern road. He came home. He fed the stove. He changed the child, who regarded the entire operation with the solemn tolerance of a duchess enduring a clumsy valet.

And all the while, underneath the ordinary motions, the collision was happening — slow, grinding, inevitable, like two armies finding each other in fog.

His oath said: *I will serve the Republic faithfully, and bear its seals, and keep its secrets, and surrender its enemies.*

His promise said: *If it ever comes to ruin, guard what I love.*

Three years ago those sentences had lived comfortably in the same man, because three years ago the Republic and the things Aurelio Vance loved had been the same country. That was the trick of oaths, Sebastian thought, feeding another split log to the fire. No one tells you that the day will come when the words point in opposite directions, and that when it comes, no amount of courage will make them agree. Courage only decides how you stand while they tear you apart.

He looked at his hands. A courier's hands — scarred, steady, trained to carry things without opening them, to deliver without asking, to be a channel and never a source. For eleven years that had been enough. Carry the sealed thing. Ask nothing. Trust that the Republic, whatever its faults, was a better keeper of truth than any single man could be.

But he had read the first pages of his own cargo. He knew what the cipher whispered of Meridian. And now the Republic itself had put its signature on a lie — had written *traitor* across the name of the one man who had ever asked him for something personal, something human, something outside the grammar of seals and satchels.

*Guard what I love.*

There was exactly one object in this world that fit those words, and she was asleep against his shoulder, smelling of milk and woodsmoke, entirely unaware that she had become the most dangerous cargo in the Republic of Vellamare.

"If I keep you," he told her softly, "I am a traitor. If I give you up, I'm worse." He felt her breath against his collarbone, quick and warm and absurdly small. "You understand the difficulty."

She slept on. It occurred to him, with a tenderness that frightened him more than the warrant had, that this was the first conversation in years in which he had said exactly what he meant.

* * *

Toward dusk he allowed himself the luxury of thinking it through properly, the way he had been taught to think in the chancellery's back rooms: not as a heartbroken friend but as a problem in logistics.

Option one: report the child. Turn her over to the garrison at the crossroads town, declare that he had found her abandoned, let the law take its course. The law's course, for the daughter of a declared traitor, was not difficult to predict. Infants could not be executed — the Republic still flinched at that, publicly — but infants could be given to foundling houses with government names and government futures, which was execution by slower means, and which assumed, of course, that she reached the foundling house at all. Men like Corvane did not leave loose threads. A thread with Vance's eyes was not a thread they would leave.

Option two: send her onward alone. Pay a smuggler, buy silence, wash his hands. But every smuggler on this stretch of border was known to him by name and price, and a price could always be raised by someone with deeper pockets, and Corvane's pockets went down to the bedrock of the treasury. No. She would travel with him or she would not travel.

Option three: the one his hands had already decided on, days ago, when they had picked her up off the doorstep instead of sending for the magistrate. Option three was treason. Treason in the second degree, punishable by — he had stopped reading the sentence, but he knew it; everyone knew it; they had hanged a customs clerk in Vellamare's main square in October and left him there through a market day so that the arithmetic would be understood.

Eleven years of service, weighed against a promise made over wine in peacetime, in a garden that no longer existed, by two men who had believed the ruin would come from outside the walls.

"It came from inside," Sebastian said to the fire. "Aurelio. You knew it would, didn't you? That's why the promise. You weren't afraid of the enemy. You were afraid of your own side."

The fire popped. The child stirred and resettled.

And then, because he was still a courier, still a professional, still the man the Republic had trusted with its final cipher, he did the last thing remaining to him. He took the diplomatic satchel from its hiding place beneath the floorboards, set it on the table beside the death warrant, and looked at the two of them together — the Republic's secret and the Republic's sentence, lying side by side in the firelight like two halves of the same confession.

He had been carrying the cipher west for six days without opening more than its outer leaves. Duty had kept it sealed. Habit. The deep courier's instinct that the contents were never his business.

But the contents named Meridian. And the warrant on his table named Vance a destroyer of loyal garrisons. And somewhere in the overlap between those two documents — he could feel it the way he felt weather coming in an old wound — was the reason a general had smuggled his infant daughter to a border safehouse with a compass rose and three words of instruction.

*Forget this face.*

"No," Sebastian said quietly. He touched the rose where it lay on the table, its brass worn bright by three years of another man's thumb. "I don't think I will."

Outside, far down the valley road, a lantern swung — once, twice — the signal of a mounted patrol checking the crossroads, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had not yet been told whom to look for but knew they soon would be.

Sebastian banked the fire, tucked the child deep into the warm corner of the basket, and drew the cipher into his lap.

If they were going to hang him either way, he intended to know for what.

He broke the inner seal, and the first decoded line rose out of the page like a body surfacing in dark water — and it was not about troop movements, and it was not about foreign powers, and it was not anything a dying Republic should have trusted to any courier at all.
