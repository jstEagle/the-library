## 6. The Wet Nurse's Testimony

The knock came at dusk, three soft raps and then a pause — the rhythm of someone who had been taught that haste draws eyes.

Sebastian set down the cipher pages he had been pretending not to study and blew out the lamp before he crossed the room. The safehouse was a two-room cottage above a disused cooperage near the border ferry, chosen from a list of eleven such addresses he carried in his head and would never write down. It had one door, two windows shuttered against the evening, and now, in the corner by the cold hearth, a basket in which an infant girl slept with the absolute, unreasonable serenity of the very young.

He put his eye to the seam of the shutters.

A woman stood in the yard, hooded against the wind off the river, a market basket over her arm as though she had merely taken a wrong turn between the greengrocer's and nowhere. She shifted her weight. She looked up at the shutter — not at the window generally, but precisely where the seam was, which meant she knew how men watched from inside dark rooms, which meant she was either trained or terrified, and Sebastian suspected the second because it showed in her hands. They gripped the basket handle the way drowning people grip wreckage.

He considered not answering. He had spent eleven years in the Republic's service learning that every open door is a wound, and yet.

The child needed milk. He had known this since the previous night, when her small, furious hunger had reduced his competence to rubble. He had tried goat's milk warmed on the stove; she had tried his patience; neither had succeeded. Somewhere in the town of Ferren's Landing there was a woman who could feed her, and here, evidently, that woman was knocking at his door with the resigned expression of someone who already knew the answer to the question she was about to ask.

He opened the door on the chain first, then all the way, and stepped back to let her in. She came quickly, the way people enter churches in weather — relieved, unimpressed, already looking around.

"You're the courier," she said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody."

"No," she agreed, setting the basket on the table. "You're certainly somebody. Nobody has that face." She pushed back her hood. She was perhaps thirty-five, broad-shouldered, with the wind-scoured cheeks of a woman who worked outdoors and the flat, assessing gaze of one who had recently learned what looking over one's shoulder meant. "Maren Ostrevik. I kept the child for six days before I brought her to you. I fed her. I'd like to keep feeding her, if you'll allow it, because she won't take goat's milk and you have the look of a man who has tried."

"How do you know what I've tried?"

"Because every man tries goat's milk first." She unwound a scarf, sat without being asked, and began, with maddening calm, to unpack her basket: bread, hard cheese, a jar of rendered fat, a bundle of child's linens washed thin. "And because your shutters are closed at dusk in a house that's supposed to be empty. Whoever taught you tradecraft should be proud. Whoever taught you nursing should be shot."

Despite himself — despite everything — Sebastian felt his mouth twitch. He secured the door, relit the lamp low, and sat across from her. Between them, on the table, lay the whole strange inventory of his new life: bread, cheese, a wet nurse's basket, and beneath the floorboards under his chair, wrapped in oilcloth, the diplomatic cipher that was supposed to be the only thing he cared about in the world.

*Supposed to be.* The words arrived unbidden and he did not like them.

"Six days," he said. "You had her six days. Why bring her here?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide something — watched the decision move through her face like weather, the way truth does when it has been held too long behind the teeth. It reddens the gums. It makes the jaw ache. He knew the look because he wore it most days himself.

"Because I was paid to," she said. "And because I've decided the money isn't going to be enough."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way rural people tell things, with the details that mattered and none that didn't, and Sebastian listened with the particular stillness he had learned in chancelleries and border posts, the stillness that is not calm but compression — every fact packed down tighter, denser, until the silence itself becomes load-bearing.

"A man came to my farm eight days ago," Maren said. "Before the child ever arrived. He said there'd be a bundle left with me and that I was to keep it a week, feed it well, ask nothing, and say nothing, and that afterward I'd have money enough to buy out my late husband's debts twice over." She turned her cup on the table without drinking. "I asked whose child. He said it didn't matter whose. That's a lie men tell when the answer would frighten you."

"What did he look like?"

"Soldier-shaped. Not army — no insignia, but the boots were officer's boots resoled to look common. A scar through his left eyebrow, old and clean. He spoke like the capital." Her mouth thinned. "Paid in Vellamarean crowns, mind. New-minted. Nobody pays a wet nurse in fresh coin unless the coin comes straight from a treasury drawer."

Sebastian's chest tightened. "This man — did he give a name?"

"He gave the name of the man who sent him." She reached into her bodice and drew out a folded paper, worn soft at the creases, and laid it on the table between them with the care of a woman laying down a card she suspected was losing. "The aide. Captain Rell. He wrote it so I'd have proof of arrangement — said it was for my protection. Maybe he believed that. Maybe he wanted a leash. Either way, I have it, and I've read it enough times to know the hand that wrote it wasn't Rell's own. It was dictated. And the name at the bottom—" she tapped the paper once, "—is General Aurelio Vance."

The name filled the little room the way smoke fills a room: everywhere, inescapable, stinging the eyes. Sebastian did not touch the paper. He made himself not touch it.

"The General's aide arranged his daughter's hiding," he said quietly, working it through aloud because saying things aloud was how he tested them, the way a courier tests ice. "Rell brings the child to you. You keep her six days. Then—"

"Then yesterday morning there were strangers in Ferren's Landing asking questions," Maren said. "Not soldiers. Civilians, or dressed like them. Two men and a woman, well-fed, soft hands, capital accents, going shop to shop along the ferry road with a story about a missing relative's infant. Very polite. Very concerned. They showed a likeness of the child — a good one, drawn from life, which means whoever commissioned it had seen her." She let that sit. "They described me, Sebastian Montrose. They used my name."

He had not told her his name. The realization arrived cold and stayed cold. She read his face and answered the question in it.

"They said it in the chandlery, loud enough that half the street heard. 'We're seeking a woman named Maren Ostrevik, who may be traveling with a courier of the Chancellery, one Montrose.'" She finally lifted her cup and drank, as though the recitation had dried her. "So whatever protection your anonymity bought you, it was spent before I knocked on your door. Someone in the capital wrote both our names down and handed them to strangers with soft hands. And those strangers aren't looking for a lost baby. People looking for a lost baby offer reward and tears. These men offered nothing. They just *asked*, and they wrote down every answer, and they moved east along the river road toward the ford. Toward you."

The lamp guttered slightly in a draft. In her basket-corner, absurdly, the child slept on.

Sebastian sat with it. This was the discipline: to sit with a thing before acting on it, because panic is simply action taken by a man who hasn't finished thinking. *They know my name. They know the route region, if not the route. Soft hands means ministry, not military — Corvane's people, if Corvane's people are anything, and the dispatches this week suggest they are everything.* He thought of the cipher sleeping beneath the floorboards and felt its weight change character entirely. For eleven days it had been cargo. Now, suddenly, it was contraband, and he was not its guardian but its fellow accused.

"Why come to me?" he asked. "You could have gone to the garrison at Ferren's Landing. Declared the whole thing. Washed your hands."

"I could have," Maren said. "And then I'd be a witness, and witnesses to ministers' business have short lives regardless of which side they testify for." She leaned forward, and for the first time he saw fear in her undisguised, raw and specific. "Captain Rell came back the night before I brought the child to you. He'd been drinking, or pretending to be. He said, 'When the questions start, don't answer them — leave.' Not 'give her back.' Not 'surrender her to anyone.' *Leave.* A soldier of a general branded traitor this week, warning a farmwife to run from men asking polite questions." She spread her hands. "I'm a simple woman, Courier, but I can count, and that arithmetic says the men asking questions and the men who paid me are not on the same side, and that the side that paid me thinks the other side means to kill the baby."

"And what does your arithmetic say about me?"

"It says you're sitting in a house with your name known, your door unlocked long enough to let me in, and a traitor's daughter asleep ten feet away, and you haven't run yet." Something almost like respect crossed her weathered face. "Either you're the fool they'll hang first, or you're the man the General gambled everything on. I've decided to bet on the second, because I've already bet my life on it by coming here tonight, and a woman likes her wagers consistent."

* * *

They talked logistics while the wind worried the shutters. Maren would come at dawn and again at dusk, milk in covered crocks disguised as butter, taking different paths each time — the mill lane, the churchyard stile, the shingle below the cooperage where footprints washed away with the tide. She refused payment with a wave that ended discussion. "My husband's debts are paid with the first purse," she said. "The rest would be blood money, and I've buried enough." She had a sister in the hill villages north of Ashford, three days' walk, who owed her a favor and asked no questions. She wrote nothing down. When Sebastian explained, carefully, that he might need to move within days, she nodded as if he'd commented on rain.

It was only when she rose to go, rewrapping her scarf with those steady drowning-man hands gone suddenly loose, that she paused at the door and said the thing she had clearly walked all this way to say and had been circling like a bird over water.

"There's one more thing. The strangers — before they left the landing, they spoke to the harbormaster. Paid him. Whatever they asked him to watch for, he's watching for it now, and Harl Brecht would sell his mother's teeth for a tenth the price." She settled the hood over her hair. "You should know that when I knocked tonight, I watched this house for two hours from the tree line first. And in those two hours, Courier, a man rode past the cooperage lane four times. Slowly. Each time, he looked at the chimney."

"Smoke," Sebastian said. His voice sounded very far away to him.

"Smoke," Maren agreed. "You banked your fire wrong at dusk. A man who looks at chimneys knows what he's looking at." She opened the door a hand's width, letting in the river wind and the smell of coming frost. "Bank it colder. Trust fewer people. And trust me last of all — because the first thing they'll do is send someone you want to believe."

Then she was gone into the dark, footsteps fading on the shingle, and Sebastian stood in the doorway listening to the night until the cold drove him inside.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He banked the fire to almost nothing, then sat on the floor beside the child's basket with the lamp turned so low it was more memory than light, and he took stock of himself the way he had once taken stock of dispatch cases: contents, condition, exposure to damage. Contents: one cipher, partially understood, increasingly terrible. One infant, unnamed, unfed till dawn, utterly dependent. One promise, given years ago over wine in peacetime, weighing more than both. Condition: his name known, his route compromised at least at the western end, his safehouse observed by someone patient enough to count chimney smoke. Exposure: total.

For eleven years he had been the invisible man, the blank page, the courier whose gift was being forgettable — medium height, medium coloring, a face Maren had called *somebody*'s with a laugh, though in truth it was the most carefully ordinary face in Vellamare. He had crossed borders with treaties in his boot heels and declarations sewn into coat linings, and he had survived because no one ever looked at him twice. Now someone had written his name in a chandlery in a fishing town, and strangers with soft hands were walking the river road, and the whole delicate machinery of his invisibility had ground to pieces around a single variable he had never planned for and could not cipher, decode, or carry discreetly in a boot heel.

She stirred in the basket. He put one finger into her small closing fist and she gripped it with the astonishing strength of infants, and he sat there in the dying lamplight and admitted the thing he had been refusing to admit since the doorstep.

*They will find me. Sooner or later, they will find me, and when they do, everything depends on what I am holding when they do.*

So the question stopped being how to hide and became how to move — where, when, carrying what, leaving what behind as decoy or sacrifice. He began, in the ash-grey hours before dawn, to make lists in his head and burn them immediately, the old habit, the only habit that had kept men alive through three changes of government. The cipher must travel, but perhaps not whole. Perhaps split. Perhaps some of it true and some of it bait, because hunters grow careless when they think they've caught the prize—

He stopped. Stared at the floorboards. Let the idea finish forming, cold and clean as a blade coming off a whetstone.

*Split the cargo. Carry the truth in pieces no one searcher sees whole.*

It was a gambler's thought, and Sebastian Crane had never gambled in his life, which perhaps qualified him better than experience would have.

Beyond the shutters, somewhere down the cooperage lane, a horse moved at a walk. Then stopped. Then, after a long minute, moved on.

Sebastian did not breathe until the sound was gone, and when he finally slept, close by the basket, it was with the deep unease of a man who understands, for the first time in his careful life, exactly what it feels like to be the quarry — and who suspects, from the patience of that slow-riding horse, that somewhere behind the questions and the soft hands sits a mind keeping accounts, and that in its ledger, his life has already been entered as a number.

A number, and a rounding error, and a child.

Far to the east, in a office smelling of sealing wax and lilies, that mind was even now drawing its lines.
