## 7. Corvane's Arithmetic

The Ministry of Provision kept its accounts in ledgers bound in grey calfskin, and Minister Corvane had always found something soothing in the weight of them — the way a closed ledger sat flat on the desk, its pages fat with figures that could not argue back. Numbers were the only honest citizens left in Vellamare. They never fled the capital in the night. They never burned their own chancelleries to hide their cowardice. They simply waited to be counted.

He counted now, by lamplight, while the city beyond his windows performed its slow suicide.

The third ledger of the evening concerned grain shipments up the Serevan corridor — fictitious, all of them, routed through four ministries and out again as munitions, each transfer laundered through the names of men who believed themselves merchants. It was elegant work. His own work, from the years when he had still taken pleasure in elegance. He ran his thumb down the column and paused at an entry dated the seventeenth of Frostmonth: *forty tons wheat, Meridian district, requisitioned for civilian relief.* Forty tons that had never existed. Forty tons signed into being by a man named Aldous Ferrin, undersecretary, who had died of a fever eight months later in circumstances Corvane had arranged with the same care one arranges flowers.

He entered the correction in red ink. Closed the book. Called for the next.

His secretary, Pellam, brought it in without knocking, which was permitted exactly once per year, on the night of the annual reconciliation, when every hidden account in the Republic's war was balanced against every visible one. Pellam's hands trembled slightly. Young man. Clever enough to be useful, frightened enough to be loyal. Corvane had long ago concluded that fear was the more durable of the two adhesives.

"The courier dispatches, Minister. From the western circuit."

"Put them with the others."

"There is — " Pellam hesitated, and Corvane watched him hesitate the way a jeweler watches a flaw move through a stone. "There is a discrepancy. The cipher courier. Montrose. He confirmed at Halden Bridge two days ago, but he has not reported at the border safehouse since. The circuit officer says the safehouse itself has gone quiet. No signals for three days."

Corvane set down his pen.

It was a small motion, deliberate, the setting-down of a pen, and he made it slowly because he had learned decades ago that a man who moved slowly in moments of disruption owned the room's tempo, and whoever owned the tempo owned the outcome. Outside, somewhere past the blackout curtains, a bell was ringing wrong — not the hour, not the alarm, but the ragged tolling of some parish church whose bell-ringer had deserted or died. The city was full of such sounds now. Unscheduled grief.

"A discrepancy," Corvane said. "Yes. I dislike discrepancies."

* * *

He disliked them professionally, the way a physician dislikes symptoms — not because they offended him personally, but because they pointed at disease, and disease required decisions, and decisions were where careful men most often bled.

Corvane was fifty-eight years old. He had entered the Republic's service under the old President-Regent, survived three purges, two coups attempted by others, and one war begun — this last by himself, in concert with four colleagues, over a table very much like the one before him now. He was not a cruel man, whatever the pamphlets said. Cruelty was inefficient; it generated noise, and noise generated witnesses, and witnesses generated trials. What he was, was accurate. He had spent thirty years learning the exact weight of a human life in the currency of the state, and the figure was lower than sentimentalists cared to admit, and stable — that was the remarkable thing, how stable it remained across revolutions and reforms. A soldier's life weighed one season of grain. An official's, perhaps two. A minister's, in crisis, rather less than either, because ministers were replaceable by design.

He did not take pleasure in this knowledge. He took *use* of it.

Pellam stood waiting with the folder. Corvane took it, broke the seal — the wax was cheap now, the good wax requisitioned elsewhere, another small humiliation the war visited daily — and read the western circuit report twice.

Montrose. Sebastian Montrose. Corvane knew the name the way he knew all the names in his ledgers, which was to say completely and without warmth. First-rank courier, eleven years' service, zero failures. A man who had carried the Republic's secrets through two wars and one insurrection without once requiring the sort of attention Corvane's office reserved for damaged instruments. In peacetime Corvane would have called him ideal. In the present circumstances the word had changed: Montrose was *unaccounted*, and unaccounted men carrying ciphers were not people anymore. They were entries awaiting correction.

And there was a second entry, newer, written in a different hand at the bottom of the page, and it was this second entry that made Corvane read the report twice.

*A child was observed at the border town of Vessany, delivered to or near the premises of the disused customs house on Rope Lane, three nights past. Age estimated under one year. Female. Delivered by a local woman, name unconfirmed. The customs house is a known dormant asset of the western circuit.*

A child.

For a moment — a genuine moment, brief as a struck spark — Corvane felt something almost like vertigo. Because he knew what a child at a dormant safehouse meant, in the same week a general was arrested, in the same month a cipher courier went silent. He knew the shape of that sentence before its words arrived, the way an auditor knows fraud from the texture of a ledger before any single number convicts.

Vance.

Aurelio Vance, Hero of the Southern Approach, the Republic's favorite sword, currently sitting in the Citadel prison writing whatever traitors wrote, had reached out from his cell with fingers no one had thought to amputate. And he had reached toward Montrose. Of course he had. Corvane had read the personnel files years ago, during the routine audits that preceded the war — Montrose and Vance, inseparable in the old peace, photographed together at regimental dinners, corresponding by letter for a decade. He had flagged the association then as *dormant risk*, filed it, forgotten it. Now it woke up on a page in front of him wearing a child's face.

He closed the folder.

"Pellam. Who else has seen this?"

"No one, Minister. The circuit officer sent it under your private seal, as instructed."

"Good." He aligned the folder's edges with the desk's edge, a small ritual, a small pleasure. "Now attend. We are going to reason about this together, because you are young, and youth requires practice in reasoning under pressure, and I am told I explain things well."

Pellam straightened as if at inspection.

"A cipher courier has gone silent. Options." Corvane raised one finger. "One: he is dead. Bandits, deserters, the general lawlessness of the western roads. Probability, given the season and the state of the province: moderate. Consequence if true: the cipher is lost in a ditch somewhere, recoverable by whoever robs his corpse. Unpleasant but survivable — the cipher is sealed against casual reading, and the enclave will simply have to receive nothing, which changes little, because a null delivery is indistinguishable from a delayed one."

Second finger. "Two: he has defected. Eleven years of flawless service, no debts, no vices we have found, no family worth blackmailing — improbable. Men defect from grievance or greed, and Montrose has neither on record. But records can be incomplete. If he has defected, he carries our final diplomatic position into neutral hands, and that is a wound the Republic does not survive elegantly."

Third finger. "Three: he has been turned, or was always theirs — Vance's, specifically — and the silence is not failure but design. He is not lost. He is *employed*. Elsewhere."

Corvane let the third finger stand alone in the lamplight.

"And the child, Pellam? Where does the child sit among these options?"

"I — " Pellam swallowed. "I don't see how a child enters the arithmetic, Minister."

"No," said Corvane, almost gently. "You don't. That is why you are a secretary and I am a minister. The child is not an accident. Children do not deliver themselves to dormant safehouses in border towns during wartime. Someone put her there, and someone meant her to be found, and the only person alive with both the motive and the reach to arrange it from inside the Citadel prison is a general whose execution papers I signed nine days ago. Which means option three is no longer a probability, Pellam. It is a conclusion wearing a probability's coat."

He rose, walked to the window, parted the curtain a finger's width. Below, the Avenue of Ministries lay empty except for a supply cart and the soldiers escorting it, and beyond the rooftops the harbor fires still smoldered from Tuesday's riot, orange threads in the dark. A republic dying of its own manufactured wounds. Well. Manufactured wounds had been the point; the dying had simply exceeded specification. Even the best-designed instrument outran its designer eventually. He had made his peace with that irony sometime around the fall of Meridian, and peace, once made, held.

"What are your instructions, Minister?" Pellam asked.

"We do nothing loud." Corvane returned to the desk and sat. "That is the first rule, and I want you to write it somewhere inside your skull: *when the instrument misbehaves, do not break the glass of the case to adjust it.* If Montrose is dead, chasing his ghost costs men we need elsewhere. If he is running, a pursuit announced is a pursuit advertised — he would feel the net close and choose a harder road, and harder roads make martyrs. No. We watch. Quietly. The safehouse on Rope Lane stays open exactly as it is — untouched, unswept, unguarded in any way a passerby could notice. We salt it with two watchers, rotated nightly, civilians, paid from the discretionary fund. Anyone who approaches the house is noted: face, gait, direction of arrival, direction of departure. Nothing more. If Montrose returns for the child, we will know within the hour, and we will know where he goes after, and *that* road matters infinitely more than his corpse."

"And if he does not return?"

"Then the child sits in an empty house until she doesn't, and someone comes for her, and we follow that someone instead. One thread or the other will pull taut, Pellam. Threads always do." He drew a fresh sheet toward him and began, in his small exact hand, the order to the western circuit — three sentences, deniable, addressed to a shipping concern that did not exist. "Meanwhile the cipher itself ceases to matter as diplomacy and becomes a matter of hygiene. Whatever Montrose carries must never be read. Not by the enclave, not by neutral parties, not by us — understand, *especially* not by us. There are signatures in that packet. If those signatures are ever authenticated, the war acquires a different author, and authors who are hanged cannot sign pardons. So the objective is not recovery. The objective is erasure, unread."

Pellam wrote nothing, but Corvane saw him record it anyway, behind the eyes, and noted — filed, audited — that the boy now knew too much to ever be allowed to resign.

"One more question, Minister." Pellam's voice had gone carefully flat. "The child. If she is — if she is General Vance's daughter, as you imply — what is to be done with her? In the event of interception."

Corvane considered the question honestly, which was his one vanity: he always considered the questions honestly before answering them dishonestly to the world.

Arithmetic. One infant, female, related to a condemned traitor. Weighted against: the testimony she constituted, the symbol she might become if raised by the wrong hands, the precedent her existence set for every other official who imagined bloodlines outranked the state. Against, on the other side of the scale: nothing. Literally nothing. She had produced no grain, held no office, commanded no soldiers. Her entire weight upon the earth was evidentiary. And evidence, in Corvane's long experience, was the heaviest category of life there was.

"The child is a document," he said. "Documents are handled according to their danger. At present she is dangerous only if connected to a living narrative — so we sever connections. Quietly, humanely, if the word must be used; an orphanage in the eastern provinces, a new name, a fosterage paid to forget. If that proves impractical—" he dipped his pen, finished the order, sanded it, "—then the accounting adjusts. One child, Pellam, against the stability of a state of eleven million. I have buried larger sums for smaller returns. So has everyone in this building. The only difference between us and the patriots across the street is that we keep honest books about it."

Pellam said nothing. His face did what faces did: nothing, expertly.

* * *

Alone, later, Corvane poured himself the measure of plum brandy he permitted himself on reconciliation nights and sat with the ledgers closed around him like grey tombstones, and he allowed himself the luxury of thinking about Aurelio Vance by name.

They had dined together once, years ago — before the war, before everything — at the table of the Navy minister, and Vance had spent the evening arguing, pleasantly, stubbornly, that a republic deserved soldiers who told it the truth. Corvane had replied that a republic deserved officials who told it useful lies, and everyone had laughed, and Vance had not laughed, and Corvane had gone home thinking: *that one will be trouble someday.* Not evil, Vance. Worse than evil — sincere. A man who believed the stories the Republic told about itself, and worse, expected the men in this building to believe them too. Sincerity was the one corruption Corvane had never learned to purchase cheaply.

Now Vance sat in a stone room composing whatever final mischief he composed, and his masterpiece had arrived on Corvane's own desk wearing swaddling clothes: a silent courier, a hidden daughter, a trail of breadcrumbs leading east toward an enclave full of exiled clerks with authenticating seals. It was, Corvane admitted, raising his glass to the empty room, rather beautiful. The old sentimentalist had spent his last free months building a machine out of friendship, and the machine was running, and Corvane could not stop it without seeing where it went.

So: watch the house. Follow the thread. Let the courier run until the road revealed which doors he knocked on — and then close, all of them at once, quietly, in red ink.

One missing courier. Acceptable loss. One missing child. Acceptable loss. He entered both into the private ledger of the war, in columns only he would ever read, and found the books balanced.

Outside, the wrong bell tolled on. Corvane snuffed the lamp and, in the dark, began drafting in his head the warrant he would need by morning — and stopped, pen hovering in the darkness of his mind, at the fourth name he had intended to add to the watch list.

The name of Lys Arravelle, handler of the western circuit, keeper of Montrose's routes.

Because if Montrose had gone rogue, then someone had armed him — and handlers, in Corvane's experience, were the first to know, and the last to say, and the cheapest to turn. He would need to look very closely at the woman Lys. Very closely indeed.

He smiled at the dark, poured the last finger of brandy, and drank to accuracy.
