## 8. The Road East

The decision, when it finally came, did not feel like a decision at all. It felt like the last plank of a bridge giving way — not chosen so much as arrived at, after all the other planks had already rotted through.

Sebastian spent the final night at the safehouse packing and unpacking the same satchel. Outside, Vellamare's eastern sky glowed a dull orange that had nothing to do with dawn; the chancellery district was burning again, or still burning, he could no longer tell one night's fire from another. The child slept in the drawer he'd lined with his spare shirt, her breath making a small, astonishing sound in the silence, like something alive insisting on itself.

On the table lay two objects that could not travel together: the diplomatic cipher in its oilcloth wrapping, sealed with the Republic's signet, and a bundle of infant clothes Maren had left behind, smelling of woodsmoke and lye soap.

He understood, with the clarity that comes to men only once or twice in a life, that carrying both was impossible. The cipher demanded roads, checkpoints, credentials, the whole apparatus of official movement. A man carrying the Republic's last secrets moved through the world as a representative of the Republic — saluted, fed, watched. And the moment anyone looked too closely at the courier, they would find the traitor's daughter sleeping against his chest.

He could burn the cipher. He thought about it seriously, holding it over the cold stove. But some instinct older than his oath told him that whatever was written inside those pages was the reason Vance had sent his daughter to him and not to a church, not to a border, not to any of the hundred safer hands available to a general with resources. *Forget this face,* the note said. But Vance had trusted Sebastian with a face precisely because Sebastian was the kind of man who remembered faces, who carried things, who delivered. If the cipher were ash, then Elira — no, the girl, he corrected himself sternly, she was not yet anything to him but cargo — the girl would be only an orphan smuggled out of a dying country for no reason anyone could name.

So he split the difference the way a gambler splits an pot he knows he cannot win outright. He kept the cipher. And he buried the courier.

* * *

The transformation took most of the morning and cost him more than he expected.

In the safehouse's single mirror, cracked down the middle so that his face came back to him in halves, Sebastian Montrose dismantled himself piece by piece. The courier's seal went into a lead sleeve and then into the chimney flue, where no casual search would think to look but where he could retrieve it if he ever needed proof of who he had been. The grey uniform coat with its braided collar — the collar that had opened doors from here to the coast — he cut apart with Maren's sewing scissors and burned in strips, feeding them to the stove like a man feeding a fire letters from a dead lover.

What remained was a widowed clerk named Tomas Verel, late of the Customs Bureau in Harrowgate, fleeing east with what was left of his household before the armies ate the province. Widowers made excellent travelers. Nobody asked a grieving man cheerful questions. Nobody pressed a tired father about why his wife wasn't with him; they only lowered their voices and offered him soup.

He practiced the name until it stopped sounding foreign on his tongue. *Tomas Verel.* A man whose hands shook slightly — this required no acting; his hands had shaken since the child arrived. A man with red-rimmed eyes — again, no artifice necessary. The disguise was mostly grief wearing a different coat, and that was the part that shamed him: how easily it fit.

The girl he carried against his chest in a sling improvised from the burned coat's lining, hidden beneath a plain traveling cloak. An infant changes everything about a fugitive's silhouette, and there was nothing to be done about that except to become, publicly, exactly what the disguise claimed: a father, alone, ruined by war. He found, to his horror, that when she stirred and whimpered in the market square at Coldharbour Ferry, he rocked her without thinking, automatically, the way a man rocks a sack of grain to test its weight — except that it was not like that at all, and he knew it, and the knowing frightened him worse than the soldiers at the ferry checkpoint.

*Forget this face,* the note had said. He was doing the opposite. He was memorizing it — the crease of fat at her wrist, the way her left ear folded slightly forward, the furious concentration with which she nursed from the goat's-milk bottle Maren had shown him how to warm. These details felt less like tenderness than like evidence-gathering. A courier's habit applied to a child: know your cargo completely, so that if it is ever taken from you, you can describe what the world has lost.

He hated himself a little for the framing. He hated the Republic more, for making framing necessary.

* * *

They crossed out of Coldharbour province on the third morning, on a road that ran beside a canal frozen into a brown-grey ribbon. The country east of the ferry had the look of a body that had been ill for a long time and was now declining quickly. Villages passed with their shutters closed though it was barely midday. In one town, a magistrate's office had been gutted, and someone had painted across the broken door, in letters a meter high:

**THE REPUBLIC IS THE FIRE**

Sebastian read it twice and could not decide whether it was treason or prophecy.

At midday they shared a bench with an old woman selling roasted chestnuts, and she looked at the baby without surprise and said, "Your wife?"

"Gone," Sebastian said, and discovered the word worked in every direction at once. Gone west. Gone under. Gone the way everything was going.

"East, then," the woman said, wrapping chestnuts in newspaper. "Everyone sensible goes east. The neutral territories don't ask questions." She pressed an extra paper cone of chestnuts into his hand and refused his coin. "For the little one. My grandson's your age. He went east in the spring." She said nothing else, and neither did he, but he walked away feeling seen in a way that sat uneasily on a man whose entire craft was not being seen.

That night, in the loft of a barn he'd paid a farmer to overlook, Sebastian took out the cipher — not to decode further, he didn't dare, not with the girl asleep six feet away — but simply to hold it, to remind himself what he was carrying besides a child. The oilcloth was stiff with cold. Through the wrapping he could feel the pages' edges, the slight ridge of the wax seal.

Somewhere behind him, three days west, men in grey coats were reading dispatches about a missing courier. Somewhere ahead, men in other uniforms waited at bridges with lists. He was now a discrepancy in somebody's ledger, and discrepancies, in his experience, were always eventually reconciled — usually in ink, occasionally in blood.

He slept in shifts, waking at every sound, his hand finding the sling each time to confirm the small weight of her breathing.

* * *

Lys found him, or arranged to be found by him, on the fifth day, at the crossroads inn at Brant's Mill — which is to say, she had calculated his route better than he had calculated his own safety, because Lys had always been able to do arithmetic with other people's habits.

She was sitting alone at a corner table with a cup of wine she plainly wasn't drinking, dressed as a grain merchant's widow, and when Sebastian walked in with his bundle and his forged papers, she did not startle or signal. She merely turned her cup a quarter-turn on the saucer, their old signal from the Harrowgate years, meaning *sit down and be ordinary.*

His heart did something complicated and painful. Lys. His handler for six years. The only person in the Republic's apparatus who had never lied to him about anything except her own name.

"Tomas," she said pleasantly, loudly enough for the room. "You're looking well, considering."

"Lys." He sat. "How did you—"

"You take the eastern road at Brant's Mill whenever you travel east. You've done it four times in six years. You always stop here. You always order the smoked fish and complain about the price." She pushed the wine toward him. "You are the most reliable man I have ever handled, Sebastian. It has been an honor and, today, a catastrophe."

"The chancellery thinks I've defected."

"The chancellery thinks nothing. The chancellery is smoke. Corvane's office thinks you've defected, and Corvane's office does not think — it *decides.*" She kept her voice low and even, the tone she'd used to brief him through wars and purges alike. "There is a second courier on the road behind you. That's all I'll say here. That's all I *know*, God help me, and I know it because I was asked to confirm your route, and I refused, and my refusal was noted, which means my family is noted, which means—" She stopped. Her composure, that legendary composure, developed a crack running straight through the middle of it. "Which means I am having this conversation with you while my husband and daughter sit in a house in the capital that certain people can enter at any hour they choose."

Sebastian looked at her for a long time. "You didn't refuse, though. Did you."

It was not a question, and they both knew it, and her silence was the loudest confession he had ever heard.

"They have my Mira, Sebastian." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Not taken. Not threatened. Just — mentioned. Over tea. A minister's aide mentioned my daughter's school, her walking route, the color of her coat. That's all. That's all it takes now." She laughed, a dry terrible sound. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? I gave them your route over tea, with a smile, and they thanked me, and I went home and kissed my daughter goodnight and I could still taste the tea."

"And now you're warning me."

"I'm warning you because I couldn't live if I only did the first thing." She stood abruptly, smoothing her widow's skirts, becoming the grain merchant's wife again for the benefit of the room. "The second courier travels fast and travels stupid — a believer, not a professional. Believers don't check mirrors. But believers report to professionals, Sebastian, and the professionals report to Corvane, and Corvane does not want you arrested." She picked up her cup, drained the untouched wine at last like a woman taking medicine. "Arrest implies trial. Trial implies testimony. Corvane wants there to be no you at all."

She left a coin on the table for the fish they hadn't eaten, and at the door she paused, half-turned, and said — quietly, without looking back:

"When this is over, if it's ever over, don't come looking for me. Whatever I did, I did it because the alternative was worse, and I will need very badly to believe that, and seeing your face would make believing it impossible."

Then she was gone into the grey afternoon, and Sebastian sat alone with a cooling cup of wine and the smoking ruin of the only friendship the Republic had permitted him.

* * *

He rode east that evening instead of resting, because resting was now a luxury priced beyond his means.

The road climbed away from the canal into low hills furred with winter-brown scrub, and the girl woke just as the light failed, and began to cry — not the furious hunger-cry he'd learned to answer, but a thinner sound, weary, plaintive, the cry of a small creature who wanted, without being able to want anything specifically, for the world to be different.

Sebastian reined the borrowed mule to a stop at the edge of a stand of bare birches and lifted her out into the cold air, and she quieted at once, startled by the wind, staring up at him with those dark unformed eyes that saw nothing yet and would someday see everything.

"There," he said uselessly. "There."

And then, because the road was empty in both directions, because the dusk hid him, because grief will find its outlet somewhere or break the vessel containing it — he let himself feel it. All of it. The chancellery burning. Vance's face at the last dinner they'd shared, laughing at some joke about siege rations. Lys's cup turning on its saucer. The seal in the chimney flue. His own name, Sebastian Montrose, courier of the Republic of Vellamare, thirty-one years of service to an idea, folded up and burned in strips like the grey coat.

Because that was the truth he had been circling for five days, and it deserved, at least once, to be said plainly inside his own skull: the Republic called men like him loyal right up until the moment they became inconvenient, and then it called them traitors, and there was no third word, no appeal, no distinction between the man who betrayed his country and the man his country betrayed. He had abandoned his mission. He had gone dark with state property strapped to his body. By any tribunal's standard — including, someday perhaps, his own — he was now exactly what the dispatches said Vance was.

A traitor. On a dark road, holding a traitor's daughter, heading east.

"I'm sorry," he told the birches, the mule, the child, the absent ghost of Aurelio Vance. "I'm sorry it took me this long."

The girl reached up and seized a fistful of his cloak and held on with the absolute, unquestioning grip of infancy, and something in Sebastian's chest rearranged itself around that small pressure like iron around a magnet.

Below them, far back along the road they had climbed, a lantern appeared — one light, moving steadily, deliberately, the way a man moves when he is following rather than traveling.

Believers don't check mirrors, Lys had said. But they walk fast, and they walk at night, and they do not stop.

Sebastian turned the mule off the road into the trees, north of the eastern track, and rode without lights into the dark, rocking gently with each step, whispering nonsense syllables to keep the child quiet, a widowed clerk named Tomas Verel fleeing east with everything he loved and everything he feared balanced against his chest —

and behind him, patient as winter, the lantern followed.
