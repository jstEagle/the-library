## 9. Lys's Bargain

The dispatch office at Vharren Crossing smelled of tallow and wet wool, and Lys Andrade had spent eleven years learning to love that smell because it meant she was inside, at a desk, with a roof over her head and her name on a payroll ledger instead of in the mud with the conscripts. Now the smell turned her stomach. She sat before it every morning with her hands folded on the blotter like a woman waiting for confession, and every morning the confession did not come, and the smell of the tallow grew worse, or perhaps she only noticed it more.

She had betrayed Sebastian Montrose on a Tuesday. She remembered this because Tuesdays were when the ration carts came through from the eastern farms, and she had been watching them out the window — counting sacks out of habit, the way couriers counted everything — when the decision finished itself inside her. It had not felt like a decision. That was the terrible part, the part she kept turning over at night like a stone with something soft underneath. It had felt like arithmetic. Her husband Tomas was in the garrison hospital at Drevka with a shattered knee that would never hold a musket again; her daughter Sela was nine; the war had reached the stage where families of inconvenient men were quietly reassigned to towns that then burned. The Republic's protection had become a thing one purchased, and the currency was information, and she had information sitting in her breast pocket written in Sebastian Montrose's own careful hand: the route east through the Two Rivers valley, the inn he trusted, the name he would travel under.

*Widowed clerk*, he had told her, pressing the paper into her hands three days before, his face doing what all their faces did now — that careful blankness, that shuttered look of men carrying things too heavy to set down. *If anything happens to me, burn this.* But nothing had happened to him yet. Nothing had happened to him yet, and already she had copied the route twice, once for the file and once — God help her — once to keep, as if keeping it gave her some power over what she'd done, as if possession were the same as innocence.

The clerk across from her, a boy named Pell who still believed in the Republic with the whole embarrassing force of his twenty years, looked up from his stamps. "You're pale, madam. Shall I fetch you water?"

"I'm fine." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Eleven years of service taught a body to lie with its throat. "Too little sleep. The child has nightmares about the sirens."

This was true. This was the worst part — that everything she said was true. Sela did have nightmares. Tomas's knee was genuinely ruined. She was not inventing her desperation; she was only arranging it, the way a merchant arranges fruit, so that the rot faced away from the customer. She had spent three nights telling herself stories in strict order: first that she hadn't decided anything; then that she could decide differently later; then that deciding was not the same as acting; then, finally, in the small hours of Tuesday morning while Sela breathed against her shoulder, the last and most comfortable story — that Sebastian Montrose was already lost. That whatever she reported would change nothing except which widow got to keep her house. He carried the final cipher, everyone knew that much; the ministers wanted him; wanting him was enough; a man wanted by the Chancellery's inner council was a dead man walking whether or not a tired woman at Vharren Crossing pointed east. All she could do was choose who stood near him when the falling stone landed.

She had repeated that last story to herself so many times it had worn smooth as river glass. She could almost skip across it without going under.

* * *

The man from the inner council arrived on Thursday. He did not look like a minister's creature; he looked like an accountant, grey suit brushed neat, spectacles cleaned with a handkerchief he produced from his sleeve like a conjurer. He introduced himself as Ferro and declined tea, which in that office marked him immediately as someone who considered himself above poisoning.

"You are Handler Andrade." Not a question. He set a leather folio on her desk without opening it, the way a doctor sets down instruments. "You handle courier lines east of the marshes. One of your couriers left the capital eleven days ago carrying diplomatic priority."

"Courier traffic is sealed," Lys said. "I couldn't speak to contents even if—"

"I'm not interested in contents." Ferro smiled. It was a small smile, well-maintained, like the rest of him. "I'm interested in routes. Specifically, whether Courier Montrose filed a contingency itinerary with you. In the event of... complications."

The room was very quiet. Outside, the ration carts were late, and Pell was stamping something with the slow rhythm of the innocent, and Lys heard herself say, "There would be a standard fallback schedule," and watched her own mouth commit the act the way one watches one's hand touch a hot iron. Too late to stop. Already burning.

Ferro opened the folio. Inside was a single sheet, and on it a list of names, and on it — third from the top, between a quartermaster she'd known for a decade and a girl from the telegraph corps — *Tomas Andrade, invalided, Drevka garrison. Sela Andrade, minor, resident Vharren Crossing.*

Not threats. No threats at all. Just names, written plainly, the way a grocer writes a shopping list. That was how they worked now; she understood this suddenly, completely, the way you understand a trap only after the jaw closes. They didn't need to threaten anyone anymore. The list itself was the threat. Every name on it was a person whose safety existed at the Council's pleasure, and pleasure, these days, was a currency more volatile than gold.

"My husband's knee," Lys said. Her voice sounded far away, coming from someone else's steady throat. "It was broken by a supply wagon. Our own wagon. There was an inquiry."

"There was an inquiry," Ferro agreed mildly. "It found no fault. Fault is sometimes difficult to locate in fog." He closed the folio and slid it across the blotter toward her, and she understood that the document was not for filing. It was for keeping. It was to sit in her house, in her drawer, under Sela's winter stockings, so that every time she touched it she would remember exactly what was owed and to whom.

"The itinerary," Ferro said. "Whenever you're ready. There's no urgency." He rose, buttoned his coat. "That's rather the point. Men traveling fast are hard to find. Men traveling carefully, with a child—" he paused at the door, and for one moment the accountant's mask slipped and she saw beneath it something worse than cruelty: indifference, vast and polished, "—are found eventually. It would be kinder to everyone if *eventually* came soon."

He left. Lys sat with the folio under her hands for a long time. Through the window the ration carts had come at last, and soldiers were counting sacks, and one of the sacks had split and grain was spilling onto the cobbles and nobody was picking it up because there was no longer any point in picking things up, there wasn't time, there was barely enough of anything to bother gathering.

She thought of Sebastian Montrose the last time she'd seen him laugh — four years ago, at Midwinter, in the chancellery cellar where the handlers kept their private bottle, when he had done an impression of old Chancellor Bevvis trying to whistle for a carriage and failed because his teeth were false, and Sebastian had whistled through his own two fingers until the sound cut the dark like a bird escaping. He'd had a gift, Sebastian, for making the darkness feel temporary. *Just weather*, he used to say of the worst dispatches. *All weather passes.* She had believed him once. She had loved him once, in the unromantic way colleagues love each other, the way soldiers do, and loving him made what she took from her drawer that evening no easier at all.

But here was the thing she learned about betrayal in that hour, the thing she would carry for the rest of her life however long it proved to be: it did not feel like evil. It felt like weight distribution. It felt like a woman with two hands full setting down the lighter burden so she could carry the heavier one further. Sebastian was one man, brilliant and resourceful, who had chosen his road knowing the tolls. Sela was nine years old and had chosen nothing. If those were the scales, then let history judge the weighing; she had children in the balance, and children outrank history.

She copied the itinerary in her own hand. She wrote the cover identity — widowed clerk, southbound first, then east through the Two Rivers valley — and the name of the inn whose keeper owed Sebastian a debt older than the war, and the day he expected to cross at Meridian. Then she sealed it, addressed it to the Council's receiving office at Drevka, and walked it herself to the dispatch box in the courtyard, because it seemed to her a cowardice too far to let the boy Pell carry it, as though the manner of delivery changed the content, as though her feet on the stones made her honest.

The box swallowed the letter the way boxes do. Lys stood in the courtyard afterward in the grey afternoon light and waited to feel damned, and felt instead — nothing. Only lighter, horribly lighter, the specific lightness of a thing amputated. Somewhere east, Sebastian Montrose was walking toward a net she had helped tie, and she was standing in the sun feeling relief, and if there was a hell it would need to be considerably more inventive than this.

* * *

Friday she went to see Ferro's people, as instructed, at the requisitioned townhouse behind the granary. She expected to be received like a tool — used, wiped, shelved. Instead they offered her a chair and wine, and the officer in charge, a smooth-faced colonel named Brakke, thanked her warmly for her *patriotism*, and the word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

"We'll want continued reporting, naturally," Brakke said, pouring. "Anything further from Montrose's network. You understand these matters must be followed to their conclusion."

"The itinerary was everything I had."

"And we believe you." He smiled at her across the rim of his glass, and there it was again — not menace, worse, *accommodation*, the look of a man dealing with a partner. "Your husband will receive priority at the Drevka hospital. New prosthetic fittings, the Swiss regimen for the bone. Your daughter will remain in the town school regardless of relocations. The Republic remembers its friends, Andrade."

*Its friends.* She drank the wine because refusing it would have required a reason and reasons were dangerous, and it tasted like nothing, like all the wine in the world had gone to nothing. They talked a little longer — schedules, channels, a code phrase she should use if Montrose contacted her again (*tell them the harvest is early*, as if the whole ruin of everything could be dressed in farm talk) — and then she was walking home through streets that looked identical to the ones she'd walked her whole life and were not, could never be again, because she now knew what lived behind the granary and it wore a colonel's coat and said *thank you*.

Tomas met her at the door on his crutches, face open with good news. "Drevka's sent transfer papers! Priority treatment, Lys — the specialist from the coast, the one who fixed Major Renne's leg. How did this happen? Who did you bribe?" He laughed, delighted, half-suspicious, and Sela came running with her school slate to show her sums, and Lys stood in her own doorway with her family's happiness breaking over her like warm water and could not, for the life of her, remember how to breathe.

"You must have friends in high places," Tomas said.

"I have friends," Lys said.

That night she checked on Sela twice. The second time, the girl stirred and mumbled in her sleep — numbers, of all things, reciting her sums into the dark — and Lys knelt by the bed and put her forehead against the blanket and made the bargain one more time, silently, formally, the way you renew a lease. *Her safety for my soul. Her school for my name. Let him forgive me if he lives; let him never know if he doesn't.* And beneath the prayer, in the place where self-deception does its finest work, the smooth river-glass story turned once more: *he was lost anyway; I only chose who else was lost; the stone was falling before I ever touched it.*

She almost believed it. Some nights she believed it entirely, and those were the merciful nights, and she learned to hoard them.

* * *

On Sunday, a courier came through from the west with routine pouch traffic, and among the seals and manifests was a single unmarked envelope addressed to her in a hand she recognized instantly, that careful draftsman's hand, the letters upright and patient as fence posts. Her heart performed a long cold roll in her chest. She carried the envelope to the privy at the end of the yard, the one place in her life without listeners, and broke the seal with fingers that would not steady.

Sebastian wrote five lines.

*M — By the time you read this I may be someone else. Don't look for me and don't answer this. What I carry is not what they think, and what they want is not justice. If anyone asks you where I've gone, tell them the truth: that I never tell anyone where I've gone. Burn this. Weather passes. — S*

He didn't know. She read the letter three times and the mercy of it was worse than accusation would have been — he trusted her, still, unreservedly, had written *don't answer this* in the tone of a man protecting *her*, worried for *her* security, sending her instructions for her own safety while he walked toward hers. She held the paper over the lamp flame and watched his careful words curl brown and rise, and thought: *I could warn him. One line, one coded phrase, the harvest is early, and he turns north and they lose the trail and my family—* and the thought completed itself without her permission, showing her its other face: *and my family goes back on the list, and Sela's school, and Tomas's leg, and the granary colonel's mild accommodating smile.*

She flushed the ash and stood in the privy's stink with her eyes shut and understood, at last, in the place beneath all her comfortable stories, exactly what she was. Not an instrument of the Republic. Not a patriot. Not even, properly, a coward — cowards at least flee. She was a woman who had sold a friend's road for her daughter's arithmetic, and who would do it again tomorrow, and who would hate herself cleanly and thoroughly every day of her life as the fee for doing it, and had somehow agreed to pay that fee forever.

When she came back inside, Pell looked up from his stamps. "Good mail today, madam?"

"Nothing important," Lys said, and sat down, and began the week's work, and outside the ration carts were late again, and the war moved east along the roads toward Two Rivers, following, with terrible patience, the itinerary she had written out fair and true in her own steady hand.
