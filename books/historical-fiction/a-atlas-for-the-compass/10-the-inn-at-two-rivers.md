## 10. The Inn at Two Rivers

The rain had followed them for two days, thin and persistent, the kind of rain that did not fall so much as seep — into the collar of a coat, into the seams of a disguise, into the spaces between a man's thoughts where his resolve lived. Sebastian Montrose had walked out of the eastern hills at dawn with his daughter — *not* his daughter, his cargo, he corrected himself, and the correction rang false even in the privacy of his own skull — strapped against his chest beneath a clerk's grey traveling cloak, and by the time the twin rivers came into view through the murk, both of them were soaked to a shared misery.

The town of Two Rivers announced itself the way dying towns did: by smell first. Woodsmoke and wet stone and the green rot of reeds along the confluence, and beneath it all the faint sourness of a place that had learned to keep its windows shuttered. The two rivers themselves — the Vessa and the Marn, meeting in a sluggish braided knot at the town's heart — were the reason the road passed through here at all, and the reason the Republic had seen fit to plant a checkpoint on the stone bridge where the north road crossed the wider channel.

Sebastian stopped at the edge of the last field before the town, under a dripping alder, and let himself look at the thing he had been dreading.

The checkpoint was modest, as checkpoints went. A plank barricade. A brazier hissing under an iron hood. Four soldiers in the mud-brown of the Third Provisional, which meant they were fed irregularly and knew it, which meant they were dangerous in the particular way of men with nothing to lose and something to prove. One of them held a ledger under an oilcloth awning, checking papers, and the queue before him was short: a carter, two women with market baskets, an old priest.

It was not the soldiers that made Sebastian's mouth go dry. It was the memory of what his papers were.

He had built the disguise carefully, in the weeks since he had burned his old life in a brazier of his own making. Tomas Verel, a widowed junior clerk out of Corvess, traveling east to settle his late wife's family affairs. The papers were good — he had forged them himself, and he was, whatever else the Republic had made him, a master of the small lie rendered in official ink. The seal was right. The watermark was right. The dates were right.

But the papers said *Tomas Verel*, and the Republic's registries had been cross-checking names against courier rosters for eleven days now. He knew this because Lys had known the rotation of the verification offices, and because he had spent eleven days assuming the worst about the woman who had taught him half his craft. If his description had gone out — *medium height, dark hair going grey at the temples, a way of standing very still* — then no forgery in the world would save him. Papers could be perfect. Faces were not.

And against his chest, breathing her small damp breath into the wool of his shirt, was the one thing no set of papers in Vellamare could explain. Clerks did not travel with infants. Widowers did, perhaps, in stories, in songs sung at midwinter. In the mud of a checkpoint on the Vessa bridge, a widowed clerk with a baby was a riddle, and riddles invited questions, and questions were how men disappeared into the Republic's ledgers.

Elira stirred. He felt it before he heard it — the small shifting of her weight, the way her fist worked against his collarbone — and he began, silently, with the cold deliberation of a man defusing a charge, to negotiate with an infant three months old.

*Not now. Not on the bridge. Whatever grievance you have with the rain and the wet and the world, hold it until the far bank.*

She made a sound. A small one, a prelude, the opening note of a complaint that could, if permitted, become an aria.

Sebastian did the only thing he could think of. He walked.

* * *

The inn sat where the two roads met the water, a low stone building with a sign that had once depicted two rivers meeting and now depicted, through the erosion of paint and weather, something more like a pair of grey snakes quarreling. THE TWIN CURRENTS, the letters said, or had said. Sebastian stood across the road from it in the rain and made the calculation he had made a hundred times in his career: not *where is safest* but *where does no one look.*

An inn at a checkpoint was a terrible place to hide. It was also, for that exact reason, a place where a man with a baby could stop and be unremarkable. Travelers stopped at inns. Travelers had reasons. A man walking the road east with a bundle against his chest and no destination was a story; a man drinking a cup of wine at the Twin Currents while his child slept was a Tuesday.

He went in.

The common room was low-beamed and dim, lit by a fire that had been fed that morning and neglected since, and by two lamps whose oil was stretched thin enough that the light came out the color of old honey. A handful of locals sat along the near wall, silent in the way of people conserving both warmth and opinion. Behind the bar stood a woman in her fifties with forearms like a stevedore's and a face that had been handsome once and had decided, at some point in the last decade, that it had better things to do than be handsome.

She looked at Sebastian. She looked at the bundle against his chest. She looked at the rain running off his cloak onto her floor, and her mouth did something that was not quite a smile.

"Bridge is backed up," she said. It was not a question. "Soldiers are slow when it rains. Paper gets wet, they say, so they check slower, like that helps."

"I only wanted—"

"Wine or tea, and you sit by the fire, and you keep your hood down like a man with nothing wrong with his face." She jerked her chin toward the hearth. "Tea's a copper. Wine's three. The baby stays asleep or I'll teach you a lullaby that works."

Something in Sebastian's chest, a thing wound tight for eleven days, loosened by a single degree. He paid three coppers for wine he did not want, because he was learning that in a country eating itself alive, overpaying was a form of prayer.

He sat. He arranged Elira against him with the practiced geometry of the last eleven days — her weight distributed, her face turned inward, the cloak draped so that she read, to a casual eye, as a satchel or a roll of bedding. She had, in defiance of every negotiation, gone back to sleep. He drank the wine, which was sour and honest, and watched the common room the way he had once watched chancellery corridors: not for threats, but for the grain of the place, the direction of its fears.

The locals were not talking about the war. That told him something. In the capital, in the garrison towns, the war was the only conversation, conducted in whispers or in the careful loudness of the loyal. Here, the silence around the war had the texture of a wound people had stopped touching. One old man by the wall was carving something small and wooden with a patience that looked like grief. A younger woman mended a net — a net, forty miles from any sea, because the rivers gave fish and fish were food and food was the only politics left.

And the innkeeper watched the bridge.

She stood at her window with a cloth in her hands, wiping a surface that was already clean, and her eyes never left the checkpoint. Sebastian had spent twenty years reading faces across tables where the wrong reading meant death, and what he read in hers was not fear. Fear was quick and wet. What she wore was older and drier, a thing that had been carried so long it had become a feature of the skeleton. He had seen it in survivors of towns whose names would appear, later, in ciphers.

He was still reading her when the soldiers came in.

* * *

Three of them, out of the rain, leaving mud and a smell of wet wool and woodsmoke on the inn's clean floor. The fourth stayed at the bridge with the ledger. The one in front — a corporal with a face like a closed ledger himself, young through the jaw and old through the eyes — scanned the room with the flat efficiency of a man counting heads for a requisition.

Sebastian did not look down. Looking down was the first tell; he had trained couriers out of it for a decade. He looked at the corporal the way a man looks at anything: briefly, without interest, already returning to his wine. Against his chest, Elira breathed. The whole world had narrowed to the rhythm of that breathing, small and warm and utterly damning.

The corporal's gaze passed over him, snagged — Sebastian felt the snag the way a courier feels a seal catch on wax — and moved on. To the innkeeper.

"Madam Hessa."

"Corporal." She did not stop wiping her counter. "You'll want tea. You look like something the river refused."

"We're checking travelers on the east road. Anyone come through heading toward the enclave territories?"

"Everyone comes through here heading somewhere. That's what roads are."

"Anyone," the corporal said, with the patience of a man reading from a paper he had not written, "traveling with documents out of Corvess. A clerk. Recent days."

The fire ticked. The net-mender's needle paused and resumed. Sebastian drank his wine and let his shoulders be the shoulders of a tired man, and inside his chest, beneath the small sleeping weight, his heart performed its work with the discipline of twenty years: steady, steady, steady.

"Corvess," the innkeeper said. She set down her cloth. She looked at the corporal the way a woman looks at a boy who has tracked mud onto her floor, and Sebastian understood, in a single cold flash, that whatever was about to happen had nothing to do with him. "My brother had papers out of Corvess. Last spring. He had them in his hand when your provost's men put him against the wall of his own barn."

"Madam Hessa—"

"He was a grain factor. He'd sold grain to the garrison at Meridian at a fair price, and when the story about Meridian came out, someone decided a man who'd been there was a man who'd seen something. He'd seen sacks. That's what he saw. Sacks of grain." Her voice had not risen. That was the terrible thing about it. It had gone *flatter*. "So yes, Corporal. Clerks come through. Men with Corvess papers come through. And every one of them is a brother to me, and every one of them gets tea and a fire, and if you want to know more than that, you can write to the Ministry and ask them what a man is allowed to remember."

The corporal stood very still. His two soldiers stood behind him with the studied blankness of men who wished to be elsewhere. And Sebastian, watching, saw the arithmetic move behind the young man's eyes — saw him weigh the innkeeper's name, which was known to every soldier on this stretch and probably to his commanding officer, against the vague shape of an order about clerks from Corvess, and saw the order lose.

"Tea, then," the corporal said.

"Three coppers."

"That's robbery."

"That's the Republic," said Madam Hessa, and poured it.

* * *

They stayed two hours. The soldiers drank their tea in a silence that had the texture of a stalemate, and left, and the rain went on, and the bridge stayed backed up because paper gets wet, they say, so they check slower, like that helps. Sebastian sat by the fire and did not move and did not sleep and let the inn's warmth work into him the way the rain had worked out of the hills: gradually, against resistance.

It was near dusk when the innkeeper set a bowl of something — barley and river fish, honest and hot — on the table beside him, unasked, unpaid.

"For the baby, when it wakes. Mash a little into broth." She looked at Elira's bundle with an expression that had thawed perhaps a single degree from the permafrost. "You hold her like a man who's carried her a long way."

"She's—"

"Don't." The word was quiet and absolute. She sat down across from him, uninvited, and lowered her voice to the register of the room's other silences. "I've kept an inn on this bridge for thirty years. I know what a man looks like when he's carrying something the Republic wants. I knew it last spring, and I was too careful, and my brother died of my carefulness." She turned her cloth over in her hands. "So. I'm going to say some things, and you're going to listen, and then you're going to decide how much of a fool you want to be."

Sebastian said nothing. Twenty years of craft said nothing for him.

"The corporal will be back tomorrow, and the day after. They've had a circular from the Ministry — I heard the quartermaster complaining about the ink it cost. A courier. A specific one, out of the capital, traveling with..." She paused, and her eyes went, deliberately, to the bundle. "With irregular baggage. They don't have a face yet. They have a route. They think the route runs along the north road, because the north road is the fast road, and the Ministry believes everyone is in a hurry."

Sebastian's blood went very quiet. *Lys*, he thought, and could not tell if the thought was grief or fury or the coldest kind of gratitude, that the betrayal had been imprecise. *She gave them the road. Only the road. Or she gave them everything, and the Ministry is slow.*

"The south ferry crossing is closed for the season, but the ferryman still runs it for people who ask properly," the innkeeper went on. "And the north road has a checkpoint, and checkpoints have ledgers, and ledgers are copied to the Ministry every tenth day. Tomorrow is the tenth day." She looked at him. "Your papers are good. I'd wager my cellar they're good — I've seen enough bad ones to know. But good papers in a ledger are still a name, and a name in a ledger is a man the Ministry can find later, when they do have a face."

"What are you offering?" Sebastian asked. His voice came out lower than he intended.

"A night's bed. A hot meal. And in the morning, papers that say what I say they say." She said it the way she had said *three coppers*: flat, unadorned, a price already fixed in her own mind. "I was married to a man who did ink work for the county registrar before the war. I can write a hand that would fool the man who taught it. Your name becomes what you want it to be. Your purpose becomes a man going east to bury a wife's family — which, I notice, is what your papers already say, so you're not even a liar, only a man whose wife died twice."

The fire ticked. Elira slept. Somewhere across the darkening square, the soldiers on the bridge lit a lamp, and its light came through the rain like a smudge of something holy.

"Why," Sebastian said. It was the only question that mattered, and she deserved to hear it asked.

She was quiet for a while. Outside, the two rivers went on with their long argument beneath the bridge, the Vessa brown and the Marn grey, refusing, year after year, to truly merge.

"Because they made my brother's death a line in a ledger," she said at last. "One line. *Grain factor, deceased, inquiry closed.* And because I have watched this road for thirty years and seen what a country looks like when it starts eating the men who carry its paper — and a country that eats its couriers has decided the truth is contraband." She stood, and took up her cloth, and was the innkeeper again, vast and unimpressable. "Upstairs, second room. There's a cot and a basin. Feed the baby. Sleep if you can. In the morning I'll write you a new man."

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat in the dark of the second room with Elira against his chest, feeding her mashed fish in broth from the tip of his little finger, watching the rain write and rewrite itself on the window, and he thought about the word the innkeeper had used. *Contraband.* He had spent his life carrying the Republic's truth, sealed and certified, and he had told himself, on the road out of the capital, that he was still doing that — that the cipher in his satchel was his mission and the child his burden, and the two were separate.

Sitting in the dark with her mouth working at his finger, her whole small hand wrapped around his thumb with the fierce unreasonable grip of the very young, he understood that he had been lying to himself with the same skill he forged documents. There was no separation. The Republic that wanted the cipher destroyed wanted her erased with it. Every mile he had walked east had been walked for both of them at once, and the mission and the child had fused somewhere back in the rain, the way two rivers meet and never quite merge but never quite part again either.

Below, the innkeeper's lamp still burned. He could hear her moving, the deliberate sounds of a woman who cleaned at night because sleep had left her years ago. And beneath those sounds, once, faintly, he thought he heard something else — the scratch of a pen, though that made no sense at this hour, no sense at all.

He was still listening to it when Elira finally slept, when the lamp below went out, when the rain thinned toward the small hours and the checkpoint across the square became a single orange coal in the dark.

In the morning, he would become a new man. He would walk out of Two Rivers carrying papers written by a grieving woman's steady hand, and he would never be able to repay her, and he would never forget her — the way kindness, in a rotten time, does not announce itself, does not ask for thanks, simply sets a bowl of fish stew beside a stranger and says *feed the baby*.

He was almost asleep when the thought arrived, unbidden and cold, and sat up in the dark with it:

The innkeeper had said the Ministry's circular named a courier and a route. It did not name a face. But the second courier — the one he had glimpsed in the garrison town, the true believer with the soldier's walk — had been three days behind him on this same road.

Which meant that by tomorrow, the man hunting him might be sleeping on the far side of this very bridge.

The pen scratched again below. Sebastian lay back in the dark, his daughter breathing against his heart, and listened to a stranger write him a new name — and prayed it would be dry before the river gave up its dead.
