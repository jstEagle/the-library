## 12. The Second Courier

The rumor reached Sebastian the way most dangerous things did in Vellamare that winter — folded inside something harmless, carried by someone who wanted nothing more than to be paid and gone.

He had stopped at a relay house two days east of the safehouse, a low stone barn where couriers of the old network still exchanged tokens out of habit if not loyalty. Most of the men who came through now were smugglers wearing the Republic's gray like a borrowed coat. But old Hessel was there, half-blind and loyal to nothing but the road itself, and it was Hessel who drew him aside while Elira slept against Sebastian's chest in a sling of blanket, her breath warm through his shirt.

"You left the capital alive," Hessel said. It was not a question. "That puts you ahead of one man already."

"What man?"

"The quiet one. Rode out four days after you. He didn't sign the ledger." Hessel's clouded eyes found Sebastian's face with unsettling precision, the way blind men's eyes sometimes did, as though they read something deeper than light. "But I saw his boots. Chancellery boots. And he asked after a clerk traveling with a child."

The words went into Sebastian like a blade going into water — no resistance, just cold spreading outward from the entry point.

"Did you tell him?"

"I told him what he wanted to hear." Hessel smiled, gap-toothed and ancient. "Which is how I survive. Now tell me what you want to hear, and we'll both survive a little longer."

Sebastian gave him nothing. He thanked him, bought fodder he did not need, and walked out into the gray afternoon with the rumor turning over and over in his mind like a stone in a streambed, wearing its edges smooth.

Four days behind him. That was the arithmetic of pursuit, and Sebastian knew it the way a drowning man knows water. Four days meant resources. It meant horses changed at every station while he begged or bartered for his. It meant fresh orders, written in ink he could imagine — *locate, intercept, erase* — with his own description appended in his superior's tidy hand. It meant that somewhere on the road behind him was a man whose profession was identical to his own and whose conscience had been issued by the state.

He thought about that all evening, sitting in the loft of a farmer's barn with Elira asleep beside him, watching the last light fail over stubble fields. He tried to construct the hunter from fragments: a courier would have been chosen, not a soldier, because a courier could travel without raising questions. Someone who knew the relay system. Someone who knew *him*, perhaps — the ministry would want that, would want a man who could predict Sebastian's choices because they followed the same grammar of tradecraft.

And that was the thought that finally made him cold all the way through. Because there were only a handful of men in the capital who fit, and he had drunk wine with most of them. He had taught some of them.

*They know me,* he thought. *So I must become someone they don't.*

The widower-clerk disguise had been built for checkpoints. Now it would have to be built for a colleague. The next morning he burned his last set of true papers, kept the forged ones, and did something that felt like tearing out his own tongue: he began moving badly. Deliberately slow roads. A lame horse purchased at ruinous cost. A visible stop at a shrine where a careful man would never pause. If the second courier was reading his trail, Sebastian would feed him a trail written by a frightened amateur — while the real route bent north through country no clerk had business crossing.

It was the first lie he had ever told with his feet, and it grieved him more than he expected. His whole life had been the honest transmission of truth across dishonest distances. Now he was the message, and the message said *wrong man, wrong road, wrong war.*

Elira woke as he saddled the horse, and looked up at him with that grave, unblinking scrutiny infants reserved for matters of cosmic importance. She had Vance's brow. He was certain of it now — certain in the way that has nothing to do with evidence. He pulled the blanket to her chin.

"We're being hunted," he told her quietly. "By someone who was probably kind to me once, if we ever met. That's the worst kind." She made a small sound, dismissive of danger, sovereign in her ignorance, and he lifted her into the sling and rode north into thinning woods, toward Meridian, toward the truth, toward whatever was gaining on him from behind.

* * *

Dain counted days the way other men counted money.

He was four behind the courier Montrose and closing, and he knew this the way he knew his own pulse — not as hope but as fact, derived from mud and hay and the testimony of tired people who wanted only to be believed. Belief was his gift. Men looked at Dain's open, honest face and told him things they should not have, because he listened the way a priest listens, and because they could not see the certainty underneath, the load-bearing wall of it that held up everything he was.

He believed in the Republic the way other men believed in fire — as something that warmed and something that consumed, both necessary, both owed obedience. The Republic was dying; he had seen that clearly since the summer, when the ministries began burning their own archives and the generals began falling like rotten branches in a wind. But a dying thing deserved loyalty precisely because it was dying. Anyone could love what thrived.

His orders were simple and had been given to him verbally, which itself was information. *A courier named Montrose has gone traitor. He carries stolen state instruments. Locate him. Recover what he carries. Leave no report.* The minister who spoke the words — not Corvane himself but a man with Corvane's cologne and Corvane's weariness of face — had added, almost gently, "You knew him, I think. In better days."

Dain had known of him. Every courier of his generation had known of Sebastian Montrose, the way apprentices know the master whose work hangs above their own. Montrose had memorized the Liranne ciphers whole; Montrose had crossed the passes in the famine winter; Montrose was spoken of in the chancellery the way soldiers speak of dead heroes, with reverence slightly spoiled by envy.

And now Montrose was a traitor carrying a child, and Dain had been chosen to erase him, and Dain understood why without needing it explained. You sent someone who admired the man, because admiration made you thorough. Hatred got sloppy. Reverence checked every room.

At the relay house outside Veren he found the blind keeper, Hessel, and asked his questions, and received answers that were smooth as river stones and exactly as informative. A clerk, yes. A child, yes. Heading east on the low road, weeping often, hopeless with grief. Dain thanked him and rode east on the low road for half a day before the wrongness settled into him like rain into cloth.

He stopped at a ford and let his horse drink and made himself look at the trail honestly, the way he had been trained to look at everything — as an argument trying to persuade him of something.

A man who had served the Republic for twenty years did not become incompetent in a week. Yet everywhere Montrose had passed, incompetence bloomed: the lame horse bought at full price, the shrine visit logged by a talkative priest, the innkeeper who remembered a clerk too frightened to meet his eye. It was a trail composed entirely of confessions. It wanted to be followed.

Dain sat at the ford while the water talked to itself, and admitted the possibility he had been refusing since Veren: the trail was written. Which meant the man writing it knew he was followed, which meant Montrose knew of him, which meant — and here Dain felt something shift in his chest, some plank of certainty giving a quarter-inch of groan — which meant the Republic's greatest living courier considered the Republic's current orders worth evading.

There were two explanations. Either Montrose had fallen, suddenly and completely, into treason — or the order to erase him had not come from the Republic at all, but from men using the Republic's voice.

Dain did not allow himself to consider the second explanation. He noted it, the way he'd note a door in an enemy fortification, and closed it, and put his hand flat against it to feel whether it rattled. It rattled.

He turned north at the ford, off the low road, following not the trail but the logic of the man who had written it. Where would I go? What would I need? The answer arrived with unpleasant clarity: Montrose was heading toward the border enclaves, and he would go by the shortest defensible route, and the shortest defensible route passed through the district everyone else in the country was avoiding.

Meridian.

Dain shivered, though the day was mild. No one went to Meridian unless they had to, and no one who went to Meridian came back believing quite what they had believed before. The ministries called it a rebel atrocity, sealed the district, and moved on. But Dain had a cousin among the relief columns, a pious woman who wrote him one letter afterward and then never wrote again, and in that letter she had used a phrase he had never been able to forget: *the smoke was coming from places with no army in them.*

He rode north. Behind him the false trail wound on along the low road, persuading no one anymore. Ahead of him, past the hills, lay the black fields of a town whose name had become a verb in certain careful conversations.

And somewhere between the two of them, unremarked by either man, the child slept through her second morning of exile, carrying under her blanket a brass compass rose that pointed — as it had always pointed, in every season, in every weather — toward no direction that any map acknowledged.

Dain spurred his horse forward, a true believer riding at full gallop toward the place where belief would begin, very slowly, to break.
