## 13. Meridian

The road to Meridian was not marked on any map Sebastian carried, which was the first thing he understood about it. The cartographers of Vellamare had not erased the town; they had simply never gotten around to drawing it, the way a man does not bother sketching his own hand. It had always been there, a market town of four thousand souls strung along the old river road like beads on a fraying cord, and now it was nowhere at all.

He came to it on the ninth day out from the safehouse, Elira against his chest beneath the wool of his coat, her breathing a small warm metronome against his collarbone. The child had learned to sleep through motion — cart, footfall, the jolt of him stumbling in the dark — as though she had already understood, in her milk-drunk infancy, that stillness was the dangerous thing. He envied her that. He had not slept a whole night since the chancellery burned.

The first sign that something was wrong was the smell.

He had told himself, in the weeks since decoding those pages, that he understood what the cipher described. He was a man of documents. He had spent eleven years in the service of the Republic translating atrocity into clean columns of figures and dispatches, and he had believed — had needed to believe — that understanding a thing on paper was a kind of mastery of it. *Meridian, autumn of the third year, casualties consistent with artillery action, munitions traced to the Fourth Depot, signatures appended.* He could recite it. He had carried it across three provinces sewn into the lining of a satchel like a relic.

The smell was rot and ash and something sweeter underneath, the smell of a thing that had died in great number and been left to the weather, and it reached him a full mile before the town did, riding the cold wind off the river flats.

He stopped at the crest of a low hill and stood very still, and Elira stirred and made a small sound, and he pressed his lips to the top of her head without knowing he did it.

Below him, where the maps insisted a town should be, there was a bruise on the land.

* * *

He walked into Meridian the way a man wades into cold water — slowly, hating himself for every step, unable to stop.

The gates had been blown inward. Not burned — blown, the iron hinges peeled back like petals, which meant they had come with siege guns, which meant this had not been a raid or a reprisal but an operation, sanctioned, budgeted, signed. He knew the caliber of the shell casings scattered in the mud of the gateway because he had catalogued them in a depot inventory two winters ago, in another life, in a chancellery that smelled of ink and beeswax. He had filed that inventory under *routine.*

Inside, the town had been killed in sections. That was the thing the cipher had never told him, could never have told him: that atrocity has an architecture. The market square first, where the people would gather. The well, poisoned or collapsed — he could not tell which from the stones. The church with its roof stove in and the bell half-melted in its frame, rung silent. The residential streets behind, burned in a pattern too methodical to be panic. Someone had drawn this. Someone had sat at a table with a pencil and drawn the death of four thousand people in the orderly hand of a man planning a harvest.

He had read the word *massacre* in a decoded dispatch and his hands had not shaken. He thought of that now with something like disgust. The word was a drawer in a cabinet. This — the smell, the silence, a child's shoe fused to a doorstep by fire — this was the room the drawer opened into, and no one who had not opened it had any right to use the word at all.

Elira began to cry. He did not blame her. He wanted to.

He walked until he found the living, because there were living. That was the second thing the cipher had not prepared him for. He had imagined Meridian as a finished thing, a closed account, a line in a ledger. But the dead do not grieve, and grief was everywhere here, moving among the ruins like smoke looking for a chimney.

* * *

The first survivor he spoke to was an old woman sorting nails.

She knelt in the ruin of what had been a cooper's shop, drawing nails from the ash one by one and laying them in rows by size, as though order could be imposed on even this, as though the world could be made to make sense somewhere, even in a row of nails. She did not look up when his shadow fell across her.

"There's nothing to take," she said. "If you've come for that, there's nothing."

"I haven't come for anything." He crouched, keeping his distance, keeping his voice low the way one does near wounded animals. "I'm passing through. I only —" He stopped. *I only what?* He had no words in any of his languages for what he had come for.

The woman looked up then. Her face was soot and creases and two eyes that had gone past weeping into some country beyond it. She looked at the bundle at his chest and something in her expression cracked and reformed.

"Yours?" she asked.

"Yes," he said, and was surprised to find it cost him nothing to lie, and everything to say it.

"Get her out of Meridian." The old woman went back to her nails. "There's nothing here for the living. Only the ones who can't leave. The ones too old or too broken. The rest of us who can walk have walked, or we're dead under that." She nodded toward the church. "She shouldn't see this. No child should. We were a town of children, once. Four hundred in the parish school."

He did not ask. He could not ask, and she did not tell, and the silence between them held the answer the way a cupped hand holds water — imperfectly, trembling.

"The soldiers," he said at last. "Which were they? Do you know?"

"Know?" She laughed, and it was the worst sound he had heard in nine days on the road, worse than the smell, worse than the silence. "Boy, they wore our own colors. That's what nobody will say. They wore *our* colors. The ones who did this spoke Vellamaran. Some of them wept while they worked, I'm told. The ones who lived to be wept over." She picked up a nail, blew the ash from it, laid it in its row. "You go tell your masters, if you've got masters, that we saw the uniforms. We saw them plain."

* * *

He found the school because he could not not find it; the whole town seemed to arrange itself around the absence, the way a body arranges itself around a wound.

It stood at the end of a lane of burned houses, and it had not burned. That was the detail that would follow him the rest of his life, he understood at once — it had not burned, had not been shelled, had been *entered.* The door stood open. The windows were whole. Whatever had happened there had happened with the door open, in daylight, by men who did not need to hide it because no one was meant to survive to describe it.

He did not go in. He stood at the threshold with his hand on the frame and his daughter's heart beating against his own, and he understood that if he crossed that line he would never be the man who decoded the cipher anymore. He would be the man who had seen the school. And he went in anyway, because he was already that man, and the only question left was whether he would have the courage to know it.

Inside, the desks had been stacked against the walls. The floor was scrubbed — *scrubbed*, someone had come after with water and lye, and the lye had eaten into the grain of the boards and left pale ghosts of the work. On the wall above the teacher's desk, a slate still hung, and on the slate, in a child's careful hand, a lesson half-erased: the rivers of Vellamare, a list of them, *the Sere, the Ald, the Meridian —*

He stood in the scrubbed room for a long time. Elira slept. Outside, the wind moved through the ruined streets and made a sound like a crowd very far away.

When he came out, his hands were shaking, and he let them shake. There was no one left in Meridian to see a courier of the Republic weep, and if there had been, he thought, it would be the least of what they were owed.

* * *

The survivors had gathered in the cellar of a granary on the town's eastern edge — eleven of them, the old woman among them, sitting in the dark around a single candle as though around a body. They let him in because he carried a child, and a man carrying a child is not a soldier, whatever else he is.

He gave them what he had: bread, a wheel of hard cheese from his pack, the small medicine kit Lys had pressed on him at the safehouse in another life. He gave it without speech. Speech felt like an intrusion here.

It was a young man with a bandaged arm who finally spoke, when the bread was gone.

"You're from the capital." Not a question. "I can tell. You walk like the clerks used to walk. Like the ground owes them something."

"I was," Sebastian said. "I don't know what I am now."

"Then listen, whoever you are." The young man leaned forward, and the candlelight found the fever in his face. "There were papers. Before. The garrison commander — he was ours, do you understand, he was a Meridian man — he wrote to the ministry. Three times. We know because he showed us, he wanted the town to know he'd warned them. About the grain requisitions, about the troops massing at the ford, about the orders coming down that didn't have his name on them. Orders signed by men no one had ever seen." The young man's voice climbed. "He wrote to the ministry and the ministry wrote back and the answer was *proceed as instructed.* Those were the words. Proceed as instructed. And then they came, and they wore our colors, and the last thing our commander did was lock his correspondence in the church strongbox, and the church —"

He stopped. He looked at his bandaged arm as though he had forgotten it was there.

"The strongbox," Sebastian said quietly.

"Melted, or carried off, or buried, who knows. But he told his wife where the copies were. The true copies. And she got out. She got out with our reeve's daughter and a cart of hides, and if you want to find the truth of what happened to Meridian, you find her. Name's Sera Ilvane. She'll be at the enclave by now, if the roads didn't take her. Everyone who can walk is walking to the enclave." He laughed his broken laugh. "Even the dead are walking there, in a manner of speaking. In papers. In names on lists."

Sebastian sat very still. In the lining of his satchel, against his back, the cipher lay in its split halves — the true pages against his ribs, the decoys where any thief's hands would go first — and he thought: *signatures that should not exist.* He had read that phrase in candlelight in a safehouse and felt it as a chill, an abstraction, a puzzle. Now it had a name. *Proceed as instructed.* Now it had a face, a fevered young man by a candle in a granary cellar, an arm that would never work right again.

"You're going to the enclave," the young man said.

"Yes."

"Then take this." He fumbled at his neck and drew out a cord, and on the cord a small tin tag, stamped with a number. "Parish register number. My sister. She was eight." His voice did not break; that was the terrible thing; it had gone past breaking into flatness. "When someone finally writes down what was done, she should be on the list. Not a number in a column. A name. Ana Ilvane. She liked the river. Tell them she liked the river."

Sebastian took the tag. It was warm from the young man's skin, and it weighed nothing at all, and it was the heaviest thing he had ever carried.

"I'll take it," he said. "I swear it."

* * *

He left Meridian at dusk, walking east with the town's smoke still visible behind him like a pillar, and Elira woke as the light failed and demanded her supper, and he sat on a milestone and fed her in the last grey light with hands that would not entirely stop trembling.

He thought about the cipher against his ribs. He thought about the oath he had sworn at nineteen, hand on the Republic's seal, *to carry faithfully what is entrusted to me.* He had understood, until today, that the oath was about the carrying. That the courier's virtue was discretion, endurance, the clean transmission of a thing from one hand to another without stain.

But the cipher was not a thing. It had never been a thing. It was Ana Ilvane, eight years old, who liked the river. It was an old woman sorting nails in the ash of her town because the alternative was madness. It was a lesson half-erased on a slate — *the Sere, the Ald, the Meridian* — in a scrubbed room where the lye had not gone deep enough and never would.

The ministers who had signed those orders had never smelled the smoke. That was the whole of their power, he understood now: they had built a machine by which men at tables could kill towns they would never see, and the cipher in his satchel was the only document in the world that could drag them out from behind their desks and set them down in the ash of Meridian to sort nails until their knees gave out.

He had been carrying it as a duty. He had been carrying it as a promise to a dead-or-dying friend. He had told himself, on the road, in the dark, that if the cost grew too high he might yet bury the pages and vanish with the child, and no one could call it dishonor.

He was done telling himself that.

They would have to kill him for it, and they would probably succeed, and the thought sat in him now not with dread but with a terrible, clarifying calm, like the air after the first frost. He was a courier. He would carry it faithfully. He would carry it all the way.

Elira finished and sighed against his chest, milk-drunk and untroubled, and he rose, and turned east, toward the enclave, toward the truth.

He had gone perhaps half a mile when he saw, far back along the road he had traveled, a single rider where no rider should be — motionless on a rise, watching the smoke of Meridian, watching him.

Then the light went, and the rider was gone, and Sebastian walked on into the dark with the tin tag cold against his heart, and did not stop walking until morning.
