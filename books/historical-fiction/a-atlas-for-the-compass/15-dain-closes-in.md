## 15. Dain Closes In

The rain had followed Dain Ferro east for three days, as if the sky itself had been assigned to his detail and resented the posting. It came down now in grey sheets over the town of Two Rivers, hammering the shingles of the coaching inn, filling the ruts of the road with water the color of old pewter. Dain stood beneath the inn's dripping eave and let it fall on him. He had long ago stopped caring whether he arrived places wet.

Wet was honest. Wet told you the truth about the world: that it did not care who was pursuing whom, that a man could drown in a ditch for a cause he never chose, and that the Republic — the great, wounded, holy Republic that Dain had sworn himself to at seventeen with his hand on a drumhead and his heart full of drums — was, at bottom, only weather. It rained on traitors and patriots alike. It was a man's work to tell them apart.

He believed, still, in the work. That was the thing his superiors had never understood about him, the thing that made them look at him sometimes with a faint discomfort, as at a dog that loved too fiercely. They thought belief was a tool they issued along with the warrant and the travel chits. Dain knew better. Belief was not issued. It was either in your blood or it wasn't. His had been there before the war, before the purges, before the names of the dead grew too many to carry individually and had to be carried in bulk. He had been a true believer when true belief was fashionable, and he remained one now that it was merely dangerous, and this — more than his skill with locks, more than his patience, more than his memory for faces — was why Minister Corvane's office had chosen him to run down Sebastian Montrose.

That, and the fact that Dain had known him once. Shaken his hand. Shared his fire at the siege lines outside Corvess, back when Montrose was only a courier with a dry laugh and a habit of giving away his ration of tobacco. Back when the name Sebastian Montrose meant nothing worse than *reliable*.

Dain pushed open the door of the inn.

* * *

The common room smelled of wet wool, tallow, and the sour undertone of beer that had been stretched too far with water. A handful of locals sat at the tables pretending not to watch him, which in Dain's experience was how guilty men watched: too carefully, with their shoulders angled away. Behind the bar, a woman with forearms like a stevedore's dried tankards with slow, deliberate strokes. And by the hearth, half turned in his chair as though he'd been caught mid-turn, sat the innkeeper.

Dain had his description from the toll-keeper at the north road, who had it from a carter, who had it from nobody he would name. *A big man, quiet. Travelling as a widowed clerk, papers out of the Chancellery. A child with him.* The child was the detail that had made Dain sit up in the dark of his room two nights ago and read the dispatch again. Clerks did not travel with infants. Widowers did not carry babies through checkpoint country in November unless the baby was worth more than the man's own life.

Montrose. It had to be Montrose. No other courier had gone missing in that corridor; no other courier would have thought to disguise himself so plainly, so cleverly, as a nonentity. Dain remembered that about him — the way the man vanished into ordinariness the way other men vanished into crowds. You could stand next to Sebastian Montrose at a ferry queue and an hour later be unable to say what color his coat had been.

"Close the door," the barwoman said. "You're letting the weather in."

"The weather's already in," Dain said, and shook his sleeve out, and crossed to the innkeeper's table, and sat down across from him without being asked. "Master Osten? Master Harl Osten, late of the ferry house at Vellamouth?"

The innkeeper was perhaps fifty, heavy through the chest, with a face built for bluffing customs men and hands that could have cracked walnuts or skulls without much choosing between them. He looked at Dain the way men looked at uniforms they didn't like: measuring, then deciding to seem stupid.

"Osten's my brother," he said. "I'm Rennick. I keep this house."

"You're Rennick," Dain agreed pleasantly. "And your brother keeps the ferry house at Vellamouth, where he has, I'm told, recently developed a talent for losing things. Papers. Seals. Small items of government property." He set his satchel on the table between them, unbuckled it partway, and left his hand resting on the buckle like a man resting his hand on a leash. "I'm looking for what passed through here four nights ago. A clerk. A child."

Rennick's face did something small and controlled around the eyes. "We get clerks. We get children. It's a road house."

"We get soldiers too," the barwoman said from behind her tankards, not turning around. "They drink, they break a chair, they pay double and think themselves generous. What is it you're drinking, soldier?"

"Nothing yet." Dain kept his gaze on the innkeeper. "Master Rennick. Four nights ago a man slept under your roof who is carrying documents material to the security of the Republic. I don't expect you to love the Republic. These days I'm not certain the Republic expects it either. But I do expect you to understand what happens to road houses that find themselves on the wrong side of an inquiry. There's a list. Houses go on it. Houses come off it. Fire is very efficient at taking names off lists."

The hearth popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked and went silent. The locals at the tables had achieved a miraculous quality of deafness, bent over their cups like men studying scripture.

"I don't recall any clerk," Rennick said.

"That's strange," Dain said, "because your ledger recalls him."

Something moved in the room then — not a sound, not quite a motion, but the particular stillness that falls over a space when everyone in it realizes at once that a question has been answered. Dain felt it pass over his skin like a draft.

"Ledger's not for showing," Rennick said slowly.

"Everything's for showing, eventually." Dain opened his satchel fully now and drew out a folded sheet bearing the seal of the Office of Internal Circulation — a seal he had forged himself, in a barn outside Meridian, with a candle stub and a steady hand, because genuine warrants traveled slowly and suspicion traveled fast. "Article Eleven of the Roads Act. Every licensed house maintains its register open to inspection by any officer of the state, at any hour, without fee. Shall I recite the rest, or would you rather fetch the book?"

He watched the innkeeper weigh it. He was good at watching men weigh things; it was most of his trade. He saw the calculation move behind Rennick's eyes — the ledger against the fire, the fire against the list, the list against the big quiet hands that might, if it came to it, close around a throat — and he saw the moment the arithmetic failed, as it always failed, because men who kept inns were men who kept accounts, and accounts wanted balancing.

Rennick got up heavily and went behind the bar and took a ledger from a shelf, and set it down on the table between them with a thump that rattled Dain's satchel buckle.

"Four nights ago," Dain said, and opened it.

The register was written in a fair, careful hand. Dain ran his finger down the entries, past drovers and tinkers and a party of pilgrims whose piety he doubted, and found what he was looking for on the page marked with the fourth night: *S. Marlow, clerk, Chancellery annex. One infant at nurse.* And below it, in the same hand but with the ink fractionally different — newer, Dain thought, though he would want daylight and a lens to be sure — a second entry: *Marlow departed east before dawn. Settled in kind.*

*Settled in kind.* Paid in goods instead of coin. Dain read the line twice. Men paid in coin left records; men paid in kind left debts; and debts were the softest, most pliable currency in the world. But that was not what held him. What held him was that the entry should not exist at all.

A fugitive — and Montrose was a fugitive now, whatever the dispatches called him, courier or traitor, the distinction had collapsed somewhere on the road behind them both — a fugitive did not sign an inn's register under his right initial. A man clever enough to invent *Marlow* was not careless enough to give the game away with an *S*. Unless the register itself had been doctored after the fact. Unless someone had gone back into the book and written the truth where only a lie had been, so that when the hunters came — and the innkeeper had known the hunters would come, had seen this same scene play out in his imagination every night since — they would find exactly what they expected to find, and stop looking one question sooner.

Or unless someone else had written it. Someone who wanted the trail legible.

Dain closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child.

"This entry," he said. "Ink's different."

"Is it." Rennick's voice was flat as a plank.

"My eyes are my fortune," Dain said. "Who wrote it?"

Silence stretched. The barwoman had stopped drying tankards entirely now, and stood with her cloth hanging from her fist, watching her husband the way one watches a man defuse something.

"A traveler," Rennick said at last. "Paid for the amendment. Overpaid for it, if you want the truth."

"Describe him."

"Didn't see him. Came at night. Left word with my wife."

"I left word," the barwoman said, and came forward, wiping her hands, and planted them on the table's edge, and looked at Dain with the frank appraisal of a woman deciding whether to sell a horse to a man who would ride it to death. "Voice like gravel. Said a friend had stayed and might be followed, and that the book should show the friend plain, so the followers would read it and rest easy and search slow. Paid in silver. New mint."

*Search slow.* Dain sat back. It was elegant, it was audacious, and it was aimed — not at protecting Montrose, but at steering whoever hunted him. Write the truth into the record and the hunter trusts the record and stops interrogating the keeper. It assumed the hunter would be diligent but not thorough. It assumed the hunter trusted paper.

It assumed the hunter was not Dain Ferro.

"Your wife took silver from a stranger at night," Dain said to Rennick, "and neither of you thought to mention a name, a face, a direction of departure."

"Mention it to who?" the barwoman said. "Half the country's strangers now."

"And which way did this gravel-voiced stranger go?"

"West," she said, without a blink.

Dain smiled. It was not, he knew, a comforting smile. "Funny. Your ledger says the clerk went east."

"People go both ways," she said. "It's a road house."

He let the silence do its work. Around them the common room breathed — the fire, the rain, the careful cup-scrapings of the deaf locals. Then Dain reached into his coat and laid on the table, beside the forged warrant, a single coin of his own: gold, old mint, worn smooth. Payment for services rendered, in advance.

"I'll want a room," he said. "And supper. And in the morning I'll want the stable boy who saddled the clerk's horse, and the girl who carried up the bathwater, and anyone else who saw anything, one at a time, starting with whichever of them is most frightened. Frightened people remember everything; it's only the comfortable ones who forget." He rose. "And Master Rennick — the register stays where I can see it."

* * *

The girl was nineteen and terrified and talked within a quarter hour, which Dain had predicted to himself out of professional vanity and would never admit aloud. She had carried up hot water for the clerk's bath — *he washed the baby himself, sir, in the basin, held her like he'd done it all his life, sang to her, some foreign song* — and she had seen the clerk burn papers in the hearth at midnight and had crept close enough, God preserve her curious soul, to see the seal on one of the sheets before the flame took it.

"Chancellery?" Dain asked.

"No, sir." She twisted her apron. "It was a compass. A rose, like. Drawn in ink, with all the points. And words round the edge I couldn't read."

Dain sat very still.

He knew the seal. Every man in the service knew the seal of Sebastian Montrose — couriers kept private marks for authenticating, half cipher half superstition, and Montrose's had been famous in the corps for its conceit: a compass rose, drawn always slightly wrong, the south point a fraction longer than the rest, so that a forged copy would stand out to any eye that knew to look. Dain had seen it stamped on a hundred dispatch cases. Had once, at Corvess, joked about it — *a compass for a man who never loses his way* — and Montrose had laughed and said, *the compass doesn't keep you from being lost, Ferro. It only tells you how lost you are.*

A compass rose burned in an innkeeper's hearth meant one of two things. Either Montrose was destroying his credentials — abandoning the last proof of who he had been — or he was leaving them deliberately, a signature scrawled on the road behind him, for the same reason someone had amended the ledger: because he *wanted* to be followed. Wanted to be followed slowly, predictably, along a route of his own choosing, by whoever his masters sent.

By whoever *Corvane* sent. Because Dain had begun, in the wet watches of the last week, to ask himself a question he did not like, and the question had teeth: *why does the Office of Internal Circulation care this much about one courier and one child?* Erasure was cheap. This was not erasure. This was pursuit — pursuit with forged warrants and gold chits and a hunter pulled off more pressing fronts, pursuit calibrated not to catch a man quickly but to know where he was going. And there was only one destination in the east that could make a minister of the Republic spend this much attention on a wet nurse and an infant.

The Enclave at Saint-Aubin's Reach. Neutral ground. The archive. The place where, rumor said, evidence waited like a loaded gun on a table — evidence concerning Meridian, concerning signatures, concerning the authorship of atrocities that the official histories attributed to the enemy. Evidence that could hang ministers. Evidence that could, if the rumors ran one degree darker than rumor usually ran, implicate men above ministers.

Vance's evidence. The traitor general's. Everyone in the service knew Vance had been taken with papers no one would file and no one would burn, and that those papers had been shipped east under seal before his arrest, and that the seal had never been recovered. Dain had signed the inventory of the seized effects himself. One line item, entered and then struck through in a clerk's hand that trembled: *satchel, diplomatic, contents unlisted.*

Struck through, not removed. Bureaucrats never removed anything. It was their one mercy and their one confession.

* * *

He worked through the evening. The stable boy confirmed the clerk rode east at first light, leading a mule, sitting badly in the saddle — *like a sack of meal, sir, and him a big man* — which told Dain the clerk was either no horseman or playing exhaustion, and Dain suspected the latter, because Montrose had grown up in cavalry country and lied about his weaknesses the way other men lied about their sins. The carter who had shared the road with him for an hour described a man soft-spoken, well-spoken, asking questions about river crossings and bridge weights and the location of the old pilgrimage fords — questions no clerk asks and every smuggler asks. Questions, specifically, about the fords near the Reach, where the marsh country began and regular patrols thinned out and neutral soil lay a hard day's ride beyond.

East. Always east. Toward the Reach, toward the archive, toward the tribunal of exiled judges who would listen to authenticated evidence if anyone were mad enough to bring it to them.

Dain climbed to his room at midnight and spread his maps on the floor by candlelight and did the geometry himself, because he trusted no quartermaster's arithmetic and less and less did he trust the arithmetic handed down from above. From Two Rivers, the routes to the Reach funneled — marsh, river, marsh — down to three viable roads and one desperate ford. A single rider with an infant could make the Reach in six days if the weather broke and seven if it didn't. Dain, traveling light and unencumbered, could make it in five. He could be waiting at the gate when Montrose arrived, and take him on neutral soil's doorstep, where the enclave's jurisdiction technically began and practically ended, because everyone understood that a threshold was where the law was weakest.

He should have felt satisfaction. Instead he sat back on his heels and looked at the map and felt the other feeling, the cold one, settling into him like the rain had settled into his coat.

Because there was the ledger, amended to lead him east. And there was the burned compass rose, a signature left smoldering in a hearth for him to find. And there were the questions about fords, asked aloud to a carter who could repeat them to any listener. Sebastian Montrose was the most careful man Dain had ever served beside, and he was leaving breadcrumbs, and breadcrumbs are not carelessness. Breadcrumbs are invitation.

He wants me to follow. He wants *someone* to follow. Which meant Montrose was not fleeing toward the Reach at all — he was *herding* toward it. Herding his pursuers, keeping them visible, keeping them where he could predict them, because a man who knows where his hunters are is never ambushed. Or because—

Or because he needed the Republic to watch him walk through the enclave gate carrying whatever he carried, so that the watching eyes became witnesses. So that the truth, wherever it lived, would be seen arriving, and could not quietly disappear into a minister's drawer the way Vance's satchel had disappeared into an inventory line.

*He isn't running from us,* Dain thought, the candle guttering, the rain redoubling against the shutters. *He's using us. We're not his pursuers. We're his escort.*

He blew out the candle and lay down on the inn's narrow bed in the dark and listened to the rain and made himself face the question fully, the one with teeth, the one that had been circling him since the amended ledger: if Montrose wanted to be followed east to the Reach — to the one place in the world where Vance's evidence could be authenticated and read aloud before neutral judges — then what was it that Minister Corvane feared would happen there? Not exposure; a courier's flight exposed nothing. Not escape; one man and one child were nothing.

What Corvane feared — what warranted forged seals and gold and a hunter pulled from the front — was *an audience*. A reading. The evidence entering the light in front of witnesses who mattered.

And an order given in secret is only safe while it stays secret. Dain turned that over, and over, the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath. His orders were explicit: intercept Montrose, recover the cipher, ensure the contents were destroyed *unread*. Unread. Not copied, not filed, not reported. Destroyed unread. He had obeyed stranger orders in uglier places, but lying in the dark with the rain coming down, Dain Ferro heard the order speak for the first time in its own voice, and the voice said: *no one must ever know what we carried, not even you.*

At dawn he saddled his horse in the grey light while the Two Rivers ran high and brown with the storm, and he rode east along the clerk's road at a pace calculated to close one day in three, and he told himself — firmly, in the tone he used on frightened witnesses — that he was doing his duty, that the truth was whatever the Republic said it was, that a compass rose burned in an innkeeper's hearth meant nothing except a traitor tidying up after himself.

But he rode east faster than his orders required, toward the Reach, toward the gate where the law ran thin, toward whatever Sebastian Montrose was so patiently, so deliberately, leading them all to see.
