## 16. The Archive Keeper

The city of Varenholt had been built by people who believed in permanence, which was why the war had chosen it for neglect rather than destruction. Its bridges still stood, its aqueducts still carried cold mountain water, and its books — this was what mattered to Sebastian — its books had never burned. The Republic of Vellamare had burned its archives twice in the last decade alone, once officially and once in the panic of a retreating ministry, but Varenholt sat on the neutral side of the Thorn River, and neutrality, whatever else it meant, meant that paper was allowed to grow old honestly.

He came into the city at dusk, carrying Elira against his chest beneath a clerk's grey coat, her small weight so familiar now that he had stopped noticing it the way one stops noticing one's own heartbeat — until it paused, or shifted, or woke with a thin cry, and then he noticed it all at once, everywhere.

Three weeks ago he had been a courier of the Republic. Now he was a widower named Aldous Ferrin, late of the salt towns, with a dead wife's papers folded in his pocket and a forged ledger line in an innkeeper's book two hundred miles behind him. Three weeks ago the word *Meridian* had been a reference code in a diplomatic cipher. Now it was a street he had walked down. It was a woman named Sera who had shown him where the well had been filled in with the bodies of men too poor to run. He would carry that town inside him for as long as he carried anything.

And now there was a name: Ilse Vantor. Former keeper of the Chancellery Archive in Vellamare's capital. Exiled seven years ago — before the war, Sebastian realized, before almost everything — over some dispute the dispatches had described only as *doctrinal*. Lys, in the days when Lys could still be trusted, had given him three names of people abroad who could authenticate chancellery seals. Two were dead. The third lived above a bindery on the Street of Papermakers, according to a note written in his handler's careful hand, a note he had read once and burned.

*Hope with teeth,* he told himself, climbing the hill through streets that smelled of size and glue and old leather. That was what he needed. Not hope like a candle — candles went out. Hope like something that bit.

* * *

The bindery occupied the ground floor of a narrow house whose upper windows were lit amber against the blue-black evening. Inside, a journeyman of perhaps sixteen looked up from a press, took in Sebastian's coat, the bundle at his chest, and the particular exhaustion that comes from walking with a lie for three weeks, and said nothing at all — only tilted his head toward the stairs.

Sebastian climbed them.

The room at the top was not so much a room as an argument between a study and a fortress. Books rose in columns to the ceiling, some stacked flat, some shelved, some simply leaning against one another in attitudes of collapse arrested mid-fall. Every window was shuttered, and heavy curtains hung inside the shutters besides, and the lamps burned behind green glass so that the whole place seemed submerged in deep water. At the center of it, at a table scarred with the crescents of a thousand blades, sat a woman past sixty with white hair cut short as a soldier's and a magnifying loupe screwed into one eye, copying something in a hand so fine it might have been printed.

She did not look up when he entered. "If you've come about the Ledgers of the Salt Guild," she said, "they are forgeries, the guild knows they are forgeries, and I am being paid to make them convincing anyway. Morality is for people with savings."

"I've come from Vellamare."

"Then you've come from a fire." She finished her line, blotted it, set down her pen, and unscrewed the loupe, and only then raised her face to him. Her eyes were pale grey and utterly without softness, and they moved over him the way her pen moved across paper — in precise, economical strokes, missing nothing. The coat. The bundle. The way his hand rested over it. "Everyone who comes here comes from a fire, eventually. Sit down. If you're armed, sit down slowly."

"I'm not armed."

"Everyone says that." She pushed a chair out from the table with her foot. "You have a child under your coat. I can hear her breathing. Take her out; she'll suffocate in there, and then I'll have a corpse on my premises, and corpses frighten the neighbors more than soldiers do."

It was the first time anyone had spoken of Elira so plainly, and something in Sebastian — some guard he had not known he was holding — let go of a fraction of its weight. He unbuttoned the coat and lifted the girl out, and she blinked in the green lamplight and made her small sound of opinion, half complaint and half song, and reached for the lamp as if the light itself were a toy sent specially for her.

Ilse Vantor looked at the child for a long moment. Something crossed her face — not warmth, exactly. Recognition of a kind. The look of a woman who had spent her life among records of other people's children, christening registers and ration rolls and casualty lists, and knew precisely how much paper a single small life could generate, and how little of it ever told the truth.

"Wine?" she said. "I have wine and I have tea, and the tea is bad, which is why I have wine."

"Wine," said Sebastian. "Please."

She poured two glasses with the economy of long habit, set one before him, and sat back with her own held but untouched, watching him over its rim. "Now. You've walked a long way to find a dead woman's reputation. Nobody comes to me for authentication anymore except smugglers, forgers, and men carrying something the Republic wants very much to be worthless. Which are you?"

"The third," said Sebastian. "Though the Republic may want it to be genuine, depending on the day." He set his satchel on the table, and even now — even here, in this drowned-green room at the top of the world — his hands hesitated over the buckles. Three weeks of habit does not release a man in an evening. "I have a diplomatic cipher. The last one out of the Chancellery before the ministry fell. Parts of it I've decoded. Parts of it I don't dare decode until someone who understands chancellery protocols has looked at it, because if I read it wrong, or if I read it right and can't prove I've read it right, then everything it says is worth exactly the paper it's written on."

"And what does it say? The parts you've dared?"

Sebastian thought of Meridian. Of the filled-in well. Of the number that kept surfacing behind his eyes at night, four hundred and eleven, the figure the cipher gave for the civilian dead, and how he had stood in the square at Meridian and understood that the true number was larger, and softer, because the cipher counted adults of fighting age separately and children not at all.

"It says," he said carefully, "that the Meridian Massacre was not an atrocity committed by the enemy, as every gazette in the Republic has reported for two years. It says the orders were drafted in the Chancellery of Vellamare, sealed with ministerial authority, and executed by our own Third Column dressed in enemy colors. And it says that the signatures on those orders belong to men whose names have never once appeared in connection with anything."

The room was very quiet. On the table, Elira had fallen asleep against a stack of folios, one fist curled around a leather strap.

Ilse Vantor did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. Then she drank, all at once, half the glass, and set it down with a click that sounded in the silence like a lock turning.

"Names," she said.

"Not aloud. Not yet." He had learned that much. Names spoken into the wrong air traveled. "First I need to know whether you can do what I've been told you can do."

Something flickered in the archivist's face — not offense, but its opposite, a hunger. "Seven years ago," she said, "the Minister of Information decided that the Chancellery Archive contained materials injurious to public confidence. He ordered certain folios removed and destroyed. I refused. I was offered a choice between my post and my conscience, and I chose the conscience, which pays worse, and I came here, and for seven years I have authenticated seals, signatures, papers, and provenances for every fraud, exile, and desperate honest person between the mountains and the sea. Do you know how many documents pass through Varenholt in a year? Everything the war shakes loose ends up here. Everything. And I know the hands of the Chancellery the way a mother knows the footsteps on her stairs." She leaned forward. "Ministerial signatures have habits. Corvane breaks his ascenders at the third stroke. Dulane spaces his letters too evenly, like a man counting. President Maravel signs with a flourish he learned from his predecessor and never grew into. You cannot forge forty years of habit. You can copy it. You cannot *be* it. So." She put out her hand, palm up, grey eyes burning in the green light. "Show me."

* * *

They worked through the night.

It was, Sebastian thought later, the first time since he left the capital that he had felt something adjacent to safety, and even then it was only the safety of shared labor — two people bent over the same pages, bound by the same terrible arithmetic. Ilse handled the decoded fragments with tongs, gloves, and a contempt for fingerprints that struck him as almost religious. Under her green-glass lamps the cipher's contents emerged page by page, and the room filled with the specific silence of two people reading the same horror at different speeds.

The orders themselves were copies — the originals, Ilse explained without looking up, would exist in triplicate in the Chancellery vaults, or would have, until the fires — but the copies bore authenticating countersignatures, marginal notations in ministerial hands, routing marks from the military postal service, and a dozen small bureaucratic fossils that no forger would think to include because no forger had ever worked in the filing rooms of a dying republic.

"Hold the loupe," Ilse said. "There. Third notation, the amendment to the troop strength figures. Whose hand is that, courier? You've seen it before."

Sebastian looked. The letters were cramped, forward-leaning, economical — the writing of a man who resented every second a pen touched paper. He had seen it on requisition forms, on casualty summaries, on the routine memoranda that crossed his desk in the years when he was trusted enough to see routine memoranda.

"Corvane," he said, and the name tasted different in this room than it had in any other. In the capital it had been the taste of power. Here it was the taste of confirmation.

"Corvane," Ilse agreed softly. "Look at the third stroke of the *r*. Broken. Always broken — he writes fast because he considers slowness a tax paid by lesser men. And here—" she turned the page with the tongs, delicate as a surgeon— "here is the beautiful part. The amendment reduces the projected civilian presence in Meridian by a factor of six. Do you understand what that means? Before the operation, someone filed an estimate of civilians in the town. Six hundred, say. And Corvane amended it downward. Why would a man amend an estimate downward before a massacre?"

Sebastian felt the floor tilt under his understanding, gently, the way a ship leans. "So the afterward would match the before. So the count of the dead wouldn't exceed the count of the living."

"So the numbers would *balance*." Ilse sat back, and for the first time since he'd entered, she smiled, and it was the worst thing Sebastian had seen in three weeks, because it was not a cruel smile. It was the smile of a craftsman encountering flawless work. "That is Corvane's signature, courier, in everything he touches. Not the ink. The arithmetic. He does not merely sign orders for slaughter — he adjusts the ledgers so that the slaughter will appear, retroactively, to have been inevitable. Modest. Tidy. A massacre that looks, on paper, like a clerical correction." She took off her gloves, finger by finger. "I archived this man's correspondence for nine years. I have waited seven of exile to hold proof of what I always suspected about him. I began to think I would die reading forgeries of the Salt Guild instead."

"There's more," Sebastian said. "Pages I haven't decoded. The cipher splits — part of it travels openly, part of it was hidden, and the hidden part names—" he stopped himself again. Not yet. Even here. "Names higher up."

"Higher than Corvane." Ilse looked at him for a long moment, and then at the sleeping child, and he watched her perform the same calculation he had performed a hundred times since the doorstep: the cost of knowing, weighed against the cost of not knowing. It was the calculation of a woman who had already lost everything once and found she preferred bankruptcy to complicity.

"Cipher work takes time," she said at last. "Days. I'll need the original key structure, and I'll need you to stop touching things with your bare hands, and I'll need you—" she pointed the tongs at him— "to sleep, because you smell like three hundred miles of road and you're no use to me or to her if you fall down my stairs. There's a bed behind the curtain. The journeyman will bring food. The child stays where she is; she's the most sensible creature in this room."

Sebastian should have slept. Instead he sat at the edge of the bed in the dark behind the curtain and listened to the sounds of Ilse Vantor working — the tick of the tongs, the whisper of tissue paper, occasionally a low word in a language he didn't know, addressed to the documents themselves as though they were nervous animals to be gentled. He had spent his life among secrets and had come to believe that secrecy was a form of solitude, permanent and deserved. But this woman handled his secrets with more tenderness than anyone had ever handled anything of his, and he lay in the dark and understood that he was no longer carrying the cipher alone, and that this was either salvation or the beginning of a larger ruin, and that he lacked the wisdom to tell the difference.

Near dawn, Elira woke, and he fed her by the green lamp while Ilse worked on, and the archivist glanced over once and said, without pausing her pen: "What is her name?"

"She doesn't have one yet."

"Every document needs a name," Ilse said, "or it gets misfiled. Believe me. I have seen entire lives misplaced for want of a name." And she bent back over her work.

* * *

On the third morning, Ilse summoned him to the table with an expression he had not seen on her before, and it took him a moment to identify it, because it belonged to none of her professional faces. It was fear.

"Sit down," she said. "I've finished the countersignature verification on the Meridian folio. It's genuine. All of it. Corvane's hand, Dulane's hand, the routing marks, the postal seals — genuine, provable, and damning beyond anything I hoped for when I was young enough to hope for things." She laid a sheet before him covered in her tiny print: a formal attestation, drafted in the style of tribunal evidence, each point sourced and cross-referenced. Hope with teeth. This was what it looked like. It looked like footnotes.

"But," Sebastian said.

"But." She drew a second paper from under a folio — a shipping manifest, commercial, innocent. "Paper. I order my paper through a broker in the Lower Town, same stock, same supplier, eleven years. Yesterday the broker's boy delivers my consignment personally, which he has never done, and asks after my health, which no one does. And this morning I checked the manifest against the invoice." She tapped the paper. "The consignment is one ream heavier than ordered. One ream of chancellery stock, courier. The bond paper of the Vellamare Ministry of Information. Someone is telling me they know who I am. Or someone is preparing a forgery in this building and wants me to know they can reach me. Either way—" she met his eyes, and the fear in hers had hardened already into something else, something he recognized because he carried it too— "either way, we are no longer working in private. Whoever weighs my paper knows you're here, and knows what we're making, and has decided to let us keep making it. Which worries me far more than if they'd broken down the door."

"Why?"

"Because men break down doors when they want to stop you," said Ilse Vantor. "They watch you finish your work when they want to know everyone you would have told."

Outside, somewhere down the Street of the Papermakers, a church bell rang the hour, and beneath it — faint, ordinary, easy to mistake for nothing — Sebastian heard the sound of boots keeping step, more than two, unhurried, passing the door without stopping.

Elira, in her basket among the folios, laughed at the green lamp, at nothing, at the whole doomed bright world.
