## 17. Betrayal at the Ferry

The river at Vesseny was narrow enough to spit across and old enough to have drowned everyone's grandparents. It ran brown and patient between reed banks, and on its far side began the territory of no one — a strip of contested marshland that three governments claimed and none bothered to garrison, because there was nothing in it worth governing. Beyond the marsh, two days' hard walking, lay the enclave.

Sebastian had counted those days the way a condemned man counts stairs.

He came down to the water an hour before dawn with Elira strapped against his chest beneath his coat, her breath a small warm engine against his sternum. The cipher satchel rode under his left arm where it had ridden for eleven days, flattened against his ribs until he had begun to think of it as an organ — one more part of him that would kill him if it were cut out. The fog lay thick on the river, which was good. Fog was a courier's oldest colleague. He had crossed nine borders in his career and four of them had been made of weather.

The ferryman was supposed to be a woman named Osla, who took no coin and asked no questions because she hated the Ministry of Roads for drowning her village's charter twenty years ago. That was what Maren's letter had said. *Ask for Osla. Tell her the eels are fat this year.* The code was Maren's own invention; Sebastian had committed it to memory along with everything else he could not afford to write down.

But when he reached the landing — a few rotting pilings, a lean-to, a rope strung through an iron ring gone green with moss — there was no woman waiting. There was a flat-bottomed punt tied to the ring, sitting low in the water, empty.

And there was a lantern lit inside the lean-to.

Nobody lights a lantern in fog before dawn unless they want to be found.

Sebastian stopped among the reeds, forty yards short, and let the cold move up through his boots while his mind performed the arithmetic it had been trained for. Lantern. Punt ready. No ferryman visible. He turned his head slowly and read the ground: boot prints in the mud of the landing, several sets, military spacing, half-filled with the rain from two hours ago. Fresh ones over them — one man, moving recently, toward the lean-to.

*The eels are fat this year.* He had said it to a boy in the village yesterday evening, as arranged, and the boy had nodded and run off. And now here was a lit lantern like a lure over dark water.

Maren's cousin. The thought arrived without evidence but with total conviction, the way certain knowledge does. Maren had told him once, in the kitchen of the safehouse with Elira asleep between them, that her cousin Tam worked the ferry sometimes and could be trusted *for money but not for long*. She had said it laughing. Sebastian had filed it away the way he filed everything, and had done nothing, because there had been no one else to carry them across and no time to find another way.

Somewhere behind him, back along the road he had walked all night, there might already be soldiers. Somewhere ahead, across that patient brown water, there were certainly soldiers pretending to be fishermen. And in the middle sat Sebastian Montrose with a sleeping child, a satchel of paper that could unmake a government, and a choice shaped exactly like the ones he had spent his life avoiding.

Elira stirred. He felt her mouth open against his collarbone, rooting, and he eased his coat aside and gave her the honeyed rag he kept for such moments, and she quieted, working at it with fierce little concentration. Eleven months old. She had learned to sleep through walking, waking, cold, and fear, because it was the only country she knew.

"Not yet," he whispered to the top of her head. "Soon."

Then he did the thing that saved his life, and he would never know afterward whether it was instinct or craft or simply luck wearing instinct's face: he did not go to the boat.

He went into the reeds.

They were taller than a mounted man, dry-headed and rattling, and the ground beneath them was the kind of marsh mud that takes each footstep as a personal offense and announces it to the county. Sebastian moved anyway, angling downstream, away from the lantern, putting reeds between himself and the river. Twenty yards. Thirty. His lungs felt full of wet wool. Elira's weight shifted with each step and he steadied her with one hand and parted the stalks with the other and thought, absurdly, of a morning three years ago, wading through the high grass behind Vance's country house with a borrowed gun and no birds, Vance laughing at his stance. *You walk like a man reading a document, Seb. Even the pheasants pity you.*

Be quiet, he told the memory. Be very quiet.

The shout came from behind him and to his left — from the landing.

"Landing! Move, move — light! Get the light on the water!"

Not one voice. Four. Five. Boots slapping planks. The lantern in the lean-to flared as someone lifted it, and through the reeds Sebastian saw the light swing out over the river in a long yellow blade, and saw what the light found: the punt, pushing off from the far bank, coming across. A woman at the oars — Osla, it must be Osla, small and square and rowing with the grim economy of someone who has decided to stop being afraid — and beside her, hunched down in the bow, a second figure wrapped in a shawl.

A decoy. They had built a decoy traveler. Maren's cousin had sold the crossing point, and someone — Osla herself, or Maren from whatever distance she watched — had put a shape in the boat so that the ambush would spring on schedule and feel successful.

And it sprang. Figures rose out of the reeds on the far bank where they had crouched all night, and a whistle cut the fog, and the lantern-light caught the boat mid-river, and a voice with the flat authority of a man who has rehearsed this called out: "In the name of the Provisional Chancellery, halt and come about!"

Osla came about. She raised both hands off the oars, palms open, letting the boat drift. From the reeds on Sebastian's side, a soldier splashed down to the landing, and another stood on the bank above him with a carbine held crosswise, and neither of them was looking at the reeds. Why would they be? Their quarry was on the water. Their informant had promised a man with a child and a bag, and here was a boat with a shawled figure and a child-sized bundle, and men are faithful to the shape of what they have been paid to expect.

Sebastian watched it happen from thirty feet away, hip-deep in hiding, and understood with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo: they are going to take the wrong person, and the right person is going to stand in the mud and watch them do it.

He should have felt triumph. What he felt was something colder and less forgivable — the courier's reflex, the old machinery of the trade turning over in him regardless of everything else: *the bag.* While soldiers closed on the decoy boat, while Osla sat with her hands up and her chin high like a woman being arrested for a principle, Sebastian's eyes went to the satchel under his arm and asked it the only question that mattered: can you get across?

Because the boat was lost. Because Osla was lost or soon would be. But the river was still only a river, brown and narrow, and on its far side the soldiers were converging on the waterline, their backs to the southern reeds, and fifty yards downstream the fog lay thickest over a stretch of bank with no landing, no lantern, no reason for anyone to look.

It was a terrible crossing. There was no version of it that was not terrible. Sebastian worked downstream through the reeds with his thighs shaking and his breath coming in careful rationed pieces, and when the reeds ran out he knelt in the mud and lowered Elira's sling around to his back and cinched it tight, and then he slid into the river.

The cold hit like a struck bell. February water, snow-fed somewhere upstream, and it closed over his chest and squeezed the air out of him in one involuntary grunt that he bit down on with his teeth. He kept the satchel high. That was the whole of his mind now — one word, *high*, repeated like a heartbeat. High for the paper. Low for the child. He turned Elira's face out of the water with one hand cupped behind her head and she made a sound of outrage, brief and muffled against his neck, and then, mercifully, inexplicably, she pressed close and held on with both fists in his shirt and went silent.

Across the river, the drama was reaching its scene. He heard it in fragments as he kicked and hauled through the current, the mud sucking at every stroke:

"—not him, this isn't—"

"Where is he? Where's the courier?"

"I'm a fisherman's wife, I don't know any—"

"Turn out the bundle. Careful. Careful with it—"

And then, after a silence with a terrible texture to it: "It's blankets. It's nothing but blankets and a carved duck." A pause, and then the voice again, changed, thinner: "Tam. Tam, look at me. Is this a joke to you? You swore—"

"He was supposed to come to the landing!" Tam's voice cracked like a boy's. "That's the crossing, that's the only crossing, I swear it on my mother — he was supposed to be in the boat!"

Sebastian's feet found the far shore's mud and he hauled himself out of the river hand over hand through the shallows, into the southern reeds, and lay there a moment on his belly with the water running off him and Elira whimpering against his back, and looked at nothing, and breathed.

Behind him, across the water, the interrogation continued, and it was going to go badly for Osla and worse for Tam, and Sebastian lay in the mud of neutral-ish nowhere and listened to it and could do nothing, and knew that doing nothing was itself a decision he would carry. He memorized Osla's voice. He memorized the name Tam said twice more, begging. If he lived, if the truth ever weighed anything anywhere, there would be a debt with those names on it. It was not much. It was what he had.

Then Elira began to cry in earnest — the cold had reached her through the wet wool, of course it had, she had been patient past all reason — and the sound carried across the water the way a baby's cry will carry over anything, over gunfire and grief and the whole concerted noise of the world, and a soldier on the northern bank shouted, "There — did you hear that? In the reeds, south side — *there!*"

* * *

What followed took eleven minutes, and Sebastian counted them, because counting was the only thing keeping panic from taking the wheel.

He ran. Not the measured courier's trot of the first day out of the capital but the ugly flat sprint of an animal, through reeds that whipped his face, along a drainage channel knee-deep in black water, with Elira howling against his spine and the shouts behind him crossing the river in pursuit — and they would be slower, they had to be slower, they had to find boats, the punt was drifting loose now with Osla's oars gone overboard, and God, God, let that buy him the minutes.

The marsh gave him a mile of confusion — channels braiding and rebraiding, dead rushes standing in water like a congregation of the drowned — and then rose gently into scrub pine and dry sand, and Sebastian came out of the wetland into the grey of early morning with his lips blue and his legs made of rope, and stopped behind a fallen pine, and listened.

Birds. Wind. Far behind, faint, a whistle blown twice and answered once, and then nothing.

They had lost him. For now. They had lost him, but they had not lost the knowledge of where he was going; the enclave was the only destination south-east of the marsh, and a man with an infant moved slowly and left marks. The hunt would resume at noon or tomorrow. It would resume.

He got Elira out of the sling and held her against his chest, both of them soaked, and she pressed her burning-cold cheek into his neck and cried herself down to hiccupping whimpers while he chafed her back and legs and murmured nonsense into her hair. *You're well. You're well. You're the bravest person in this entire idiot republic.* When her toes were merely cold instead of catastrophic, he allowed himself to notice his own body, which was reporting, in order: the absence of the satchel.

His mind refused it at first. His hand went to his side, to the place where the strap had lain for eleven days, and found only wet shirt, and his hand went again, slower, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

Gone. The strap must have snapped in the river, torn loose in the channel, in the reeds — or, some colder voice suggested, in the scramble out of the water onto the northern bank, in the first minutes, when his hands had been full of child and current and he had not checked, had not checked even once—

He made himself sit down on the fallen pine because his knees had informed him they were no longer participating.

The cipher. Six weeks of decoding in his skull and on paper — the Meridian signatures, the supply ledgers that proved the massacre was ordered not erred, the names, the pages he had split and hidden and copied, the whole fragile architecture of proof that he had carried through checkpoints and fever and snow — somewhere in a mile of black marsh water, in the mud, in the mouths of eels, in the hands of whichever soldier waded the channel next.

Panic rose in him like floodwater. He let it rise, and he watched it, because Ilse had taught him that much in their brief desperate weeks together: watch the wave, name it, do not become it. *This is fear,* he told himself. *Fear is a courier's colleague too. Give it a job.*

So he gave it the inventory, and the inventory, item by item, began — slowly, grudgingly, like a clerk opening shop after a riot — to make sense of things.

Item: the satchel was gone, but the satchel had not contained everything. That was the first fact, and it arrived with the force of revelation. On the third night out of the capital, sleepless in a hay barn with Dain surely already on his road, Sebastian had made the decision that had seemed paranoid then and now seemed like the only sane act of his life: he had split the cargo. Half the decoded leaves — the true half, the half with the authenticated signatures, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn into the lining of Elira's sling, against her small warm body, where no searcher would dare be rough — and the other half, the copies and the partials and the deliberately ambiguous drafts, into the satchel, where they read like the real thing to anyone who did not already know better.

A gambler's arrangement. He had told himself it was prudence. Now, sitting on a dead pine with a wet baby and a missing organ, he understood it had been foresight of a kind he didn't know he possessed: some part of him had always known the satchel would be taken.

Item: whoever recovered that satchel would believe — for a while — that they held the end of the matter. Corvane's people wanted the cipher destroyed unread; that much the pattern of the pursuit had made plain. They would burn it, or bury it, or study it and find it plausible, and either way they would relax by degrees. A hunter who thinks he has the prize stops hunting like a hunter and starts hunting like a man going home.

Item: unless they studied it closely. Unless someone noticed the paper was wrong, the ink too new, the hands imperfectly remembered. Unless Dain —

Sebastian closed his eyes. He had never met Dain, but he had come to know him the way you come to know a recurring pain: the true believer, Lys had called him once, spitting, before she became something else. A true believer would not burn the papers unread. A true believer would read them, and compare them, and wonder why his masters had ordered the destruction of documents rather than the capture of the reader — and that question, that single splinter of doubt, might do more damage to Corvane than six authenticated pages.

Or it might get them all killed faster. Both outcomes remained available. They generally did.

Elira sneezed, a tiny furious sound, and pulled him back to the surface of things. Wet, both of them, in February, in open pine country, a mile from soldiers, with half a conspiracy sewn into a baby's clothing. The sun was coming up somewhere behind the fog, a pale discoloration in the east, and the road to the enclave ran south-east from here through villages that would all, by now, have seen his description or would see it within the day.

He stood. His joints protested; he overruled them. He repositioned Elira into the crook of his arm, checked the oilcloth packet in the sling lining with two fingers, feeling the crisp edges of the leaves beneath — *still there, still real, still ours* — and allowed himself, for exactly three seconds, the luxury of grief for the satchel. For the work in it. For Osla's square shoulders and Tam's cracking voice and the debt with their names on it. Three seconds. Then he buttoned the vault.

"Right," he said aloud, to the child, to the pines, to the general dead air of a republic that had tried to kill him in eleven different ways and kept nearly succeeding. "New rule. We stop losing things."

He found the cart track that the map in his head insisted connected to the coast road, and he set out along it at the pace of a tired widowed clerk, which was what he appeared to be, carrying everything that remained of the truth against the only heart in the world that trusted him completely.

Two miles on, at a shrine to a river goddess whose name no one remembered, he found the mark.

Chalked on the shrine's base, low, deliberate, meant to be found by exactly one kind of eye: the courier's sign of the broken seal — the old Republic's signal for *cargo compromised, route burned, trust nothing.* It had been years since anyone legitimate used it. It was not a soldier's mark. It was not Dain's; Dain would not warn him.

Which meant someone else was on this road. Someone who knew the old signs. Someone who knew he had lost the satchel — or who wanted him to believe he had.

Sebastian stood over the chalk a long moment, Elira's breath quick against his ear, and felt the cold clarity of the card-player settle fully into place at last, and thought: *then let's find out who's dealing.*
