## 21. Lys's Reckoning

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was the day Lys Venn had long ago learned to dread, because Tuesdays were when the Ministry of Internal Order sent its routine confirmations — the small gray envelopes that said *your file remains active* in language too polite to say *we remember you exist*. She had been receiving them for eleven months now, ever since she'd given up Sebastian Montrose's route in exchange for her family's safety. Eleven months of gray envelopes, each one a receipt for a sin she tried not to count.

This one was different.

It was not gray. It was cream-colored, heavy stock, sealed with wax rather than gummed paper, and it bore no ministry mark at all. Only a small stamped device in the corner: a balance scale with a single coin on one pan and nothing on the other. She stood in the entryway of the safehouse flat on Corvane Street — they had moved her there in the spring, "for convenience," they'd said, though she'd never asked whose — turning it over twice before she broke the seal, because some animal part of her understood that once she read what was inside, there would be no unread-ing it. There were doors that only opened one way. She had walked through one already, eleven months ago, in an office that smelled of lamp oil and lilies, trading a man's life for her own children's breath. She had told herself every night since that the arithmetic was clean.

She opened the envelope.

Inside was a single sheet, typed, unsigned. A requisition order, routed through three departments so that its origin would be deniable even to the man who'd ordered it. It authorized, effective the first of the month, the transfer of certain dependents of certain persons under protective custody to the Eastern Reclamation District.

Lys read it three times. The words were ordinary. That was what made her hands go cold — not the words but their ordinariness, the bland bureaucratic music of them, *transfer*, *dependents*, *protective custody*. She knew this language. She had worked in the Chancellery for nine years; she could hear the second meaning underneath the first the way a tuner hears the false note inside a chord. In the Ministry's vocabulary, *Eastern Reclamation District* meant the marsh camps beyond Serevan, where the state sent people it wanted to lose without wanting to be seen losing them. And *dependents under protective custody* —

Her children. Tomas, who was seven and afraid of thunder. Anka, who was four and still slept with a cloth bird she called Captain.

The requisition was dated three days ago. Effective date: the first of the month. Today was the twenty-eighth.

She stood very still in the entryway while the house ticked around her, the stove settling, water moving in pipes somewhere above, and she thought with terrible clarity: *they were never going to let us go.* Not after the trial. Not after the war. Not ever. Because a woman who had sold out one courier would always be a woman who could sell out another, and men like Corvane did not keep such instruments in their drawer — they melted them down. Her testimony against Montrose hadn't purchased safety. It had purchased a shelf in the storeroom, and shelves get cleared.

The coin on the scale. Nothing on the other pan.

She heard footsteps on the stairs — the landlady, old Frau Ostrek, who pretended deafness and missed nothing — and Lys folded the sheet into quarters, then eighths, then small enough to disappear into her glove, and climbed back up to her room as if nothing had changed, because outwardly nothing had. Outwardly everything was exactly as it had been five minutes ago. That was the horror of it. The world did not change shape when your world ended. The kettle still whistled. The snow still dripped.

Her children were forty miles south, in the village of Halven, with her sister. Forty miles. Three days.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she had been trained to do, and hated herself for how well she did it: she went about her ordinary rounds. She was, officially, a records clerk attached to the Office of Transit Verification — the office that checked couriers' seals along the eastern roads — and her handler, a soft-spoken man named Durel who wore his cruelty like a good coat fits, expected her weekly report by Friday. She wrote it. She invented a sighting of a clerk matching Montrose's description at the ferry crossing near Two Rivers, dated ten days past, corroborated by nothing, verifiable by no one. It bought goodwill. Goodwill bought time. Time bought miles between her and whatever wagon was already rolling toward Halven.

Then she sat by her window with mending she did not do and thought about Sebastian Montrose.

She had met him six times in nine years of shared service, and all six times he had struck her the same way: a man made entirely of surfaces, courteous, forgettable, his face a door closed on a lit room. She had believed, right up until the moment she signed the report that doomed him, that he was exactly what he appeared — a tired functionary carrying papers for a dying republic. It had made the betrayal easy. You can betray a surface.

But then the reports had started coming back wrong. The second courier, Dain, had recovered a satchel full of worthless decoy pages from the ferry ambush, and something in the way the recovery was received — the eagerness, the haste, the order from above that the contents be destroyed *unread*, before anyone could confirm them — had started whispers even in her little office. Clerks whispered the way mice move: soundlessly, constantly, and only when the cats are near. Lys had spent eleven months listening to mice.

And beneath the whispers, a question she had refused to ask had grown teeth: *what kind of truth is so dangerous that the man hunting it must also be erased?*

She had signed away Montrose to save her family. Now the state was sending wagons for her family anyway. Which meant the bargain had never been real, which meant — and here was the thought that finally broke something loose in her chest, a splinter working free of old wood — which meant the sin had purchased nothing at all. It was not even a coin on the scale. It was ash.

Frau Ostrek knocked and brought tea and said, in her way of saying things while looking elsewhere, "There's a man been standing across the street since morning. Grey coat. I expect he's waiting for someone else." Then she left, having delivered, as usual, precisely the message she'd been paid by her conscience to deliver.

Grey coat. Watching the house. On the twenty-eighth, three days before the requisition took effect.

They weren't going to wait for the first of the month. They were going to make sure she was on that wagon with her children, and the requisition was merely the paperwork that would explain afterward why nobody had ever heard of Lys Venn again.

* * *

Night came down over the city like a lid. Lys packed in the dark, by touch, the way she'd taught herself during the border crises years ago: one bag, nothing that rattled, wool over silk, documents sewn into the lining of Anka's coat because no soldier searching a child looks carefully at a child. She left the flat at half past eleven through the coal cellar and the yard behind, where Frau Ostrek was waiting with a lantern hooded to a coin of light and a bundle of bread and smoked fish wrapped in newspaper.

"I'll deny seeing you," the old woman said, pressing the bundle into her arms. "I'm very good at it. I've practiced being blind my whole life."

"Ostrek—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking me makes it a favor, and favors get people shot. Go."

Lys went. South, through streets she knew by their smells — the tanneries, the wet stone of the fountain quarter, the onion-and-woodsmoke breath of the river district — keeping off the boulevards where the grey coats gathered, walking with the slight stoop of a market woman going early to buy, which she had practiced in mirrors until it wasn't a performance anymore. At the south gate the sentries were warming themselves over a brazier and waved through anyone with local papers, and her papers were flawless because she had written them herself, in the office, weeks ago, on a night when she had told herself she was being paranoid.

Perhaps she had known, even then. Perhaps the body knows what the mind refuses to sign.

Halven was a day and a half by cart road, less if she cut across the fells, and she cut across the fells, sleeping in hay barns with her boots on, eating the smoked fish cold, and thinking, in the long blue hours before dawn, about what she would do when she got there. Take the children and run west, to the coast, to a boat, to anywhere — that was the plan her body kept proposing. But running was just a slower wagon ride east. Corvane's reach didn't stop at borders; it stopped wherever he decided it stopped, and lately that line had been drawn very far out indeed.

No. If she ran, she ran forever, and her children grew up in the shadow of a hunt, and someday the hunt caught up, because hunts always catch up eventually. There was only one way to make her family safe, and it was the way she should have chosen eleven months ago, and it was going to cost her everything she had left, which was nearly nothing, which meant it was almost affordable.

She had to warn Sebastian Montrose.

* * *

Finding him was the easy part, because she had built the net that was supposed to catch him and she knew its holes better than its maker. He was heading for the enclave — everyone in the whispering offices knew it, it was the worst-kept secret of a badly kept war — and the enclave had one practical gate for civilians, at the ferry town of Vess, below the old customs house. Which was precisely why Corvane had arranged matters there. Lys had seen the routing orders cross her desk two weeks ago and had not understood what she was reading; now she understood it completely. The delegates arriving from the capital with gold and warrants were the public hand. But there was a private hand too, quieter, earlier: the garrison captain at Vess had been replaced ten days ago by a man named Orlav, whose file Lys had pulled out of idle habit and whose file contained a debt to the Ministry that could only be repaid in obedience. Anyone entering the enclave through Vess would pass through Orlav's hands, and Orlav's hands were Corvane's hands, and whatever happened there would be written up later as regrettable banditry.

Montrose would walk into it trusting his papers, because his papers were always good. His papers had always been good. That was the trap of being excellent at your craft: you stopped believing craft could fail you.

She reached Halven on the third evening, mud to her knees, and found her children whole — Tomas reciting river names to his cousin, Anka asleep with the cloth bird crushed under her chin — and her sister pale with a question she'd been holding for three days: soldiers had come asking, polite ones, taking a census, they said, and had left a card and gone, and hadn't returned, and somehow that was worse than if they'd stayed.

"We leave tonight," Lys said. "All of us. Not east, not west. North, to Ilva, to Mother's cousin — don't argue, Marta, please, for once don't argue." And then, because her sister deserved more than orders: "I did a thing. Last year. I told myself it was to keep them safe, and tonight I found the paper that proves it wasn't true, that it was never going to be true. So we run, and I fix it, or I die trying, and either way they end up safer than staying."

Marta looked at her for a long moment, the lamplight making small shadows of her face. "You always were the clever one," she said at last, which in their family's grammar was forgiveness, or close enough to travel on.

* * *

The warning itself took two days to compose, because a warning is a document, and documents are her craft, and she would not send this one carelessly. She could not sign it — a signature would burn the reader as surely as the warned — so she built trust out of specifics instead. Details only she could know. The route she had falsely reported. The name of the officer at Two Rivers who had taken her report and filed it unopened. The number of the requisition order for her own children's removal, which would exist nowhere else in the world except in the files of the man who'd drafted it — and if Sebastian Montrose doubted her, let him doubt, but let him also route himself away from Vess, because doubt costs a detour and certainty costs a grave.

She wrote it out fair, memorized it, burned the draft in her sister's stove, and copied it fresh in a hand not her own, learned from nine years of forging transit verifications. Then she addressed it the only way she could think of — no name, no seal, just the salutation that couriers used among themselves in the old peacetime days, the greeting that meant *the road is watched, take the other road*:

*To the bearer of the compass.*

She sent it north with a tinker who owed Marta's cousin money and was glad of any errand that took him toward the enclave towns, with instructions to leave it at the chandler's in Brast, where travelers bound for Vess bought lamp oil, pinned under the counter among the receipts. Couriers read counters. It was a habit of the trade; she was counting on it the way a drowning woman counts on driftwood.

And then she sat down on her sister's floor, with her children breathing in the next room and the fire burning low, and did the accounting she had postponed for eleven months.

Here is what she had believed she'd purchased with her betrayal: safety. Here is what the ledger actually showed: a gray envelope, a requisition for marsh camps, a watcher outside her window, and a man walking into a trap she had helped design. The coin on the scale, and nothing — nothing — in the other pan. Corvane's arithmetic had a flaw in it, she saw now, the same flaw in all arithmetic done by men who have never been subtracted from: he believed everyone kept books the way he did. He believed fear was a currency that held its value. He had never considered that a frightened woman might wake one morning and find her fear spent, all of it, down to the last coin, and discover that what remained — anger, grief, a mother's particular and bottomless stubbornness — could not be entered in his ledgers at all.

Let him keep his books. She was done balancing.

On the last night before her children's wagon left for Ilva, Anka woke crying from a dream, and Lys held her in the dark, rocking, smelling woodsmoke and sleep in her daughter's hair, and sang the low nonsense song their mother had sung, and thought: *whatever happens to me, this is what I am for. This is the whole account.* When the girl slept again, Lys laid her down and knelt a moment longer with her hand on the small rising chest, feeling the future arrive and arrive, indifferent to ministries.

Then she rose, put on her coat, and went out into the cold to finish becoming the thing Corvane had made her and then made useless: a traitor, twice over — once to the republic, once to the republic's masters — and free.

* * *

Three hundred miles north, at the chandler's shop in Brast, a tinker set down his pack and, while the chandler weighed out lamp oil for him, slid a folded paper under the lip of the counter among the receipts, as instructed, and forgot it immediately, because tinkers forget many things and are forgotten by more.

The snow was melting off the eaves in Brast too. The drips struck the stones like a slow clock counting down, and somewhere on the road east, unaware, a man with a child asleep against his shoulder walked steadily toward a gate that had already learned his name.
