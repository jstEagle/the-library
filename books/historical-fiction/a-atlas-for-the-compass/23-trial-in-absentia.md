## 23. Trial in Absentia

The enclave had been a monastery once, and it still smelled like one — candle wax and cold stone and the faint sweetness of old paper, as if the walls themselves had learned to preserve what men brought into them. Sebastian stood in the entrance hall with Elira against his chest, wrapped in wool against the damp that no fire seemed to defeat, and waited to be judged by three people who had no country left to judge in his name.

They came down the stair at the hall's end slowly, the way old things come. The first was a woman past seventy with a face like a folded map, creases running every direction, none of them soft. The second walked with a stick he did not lean on so much as tap against the stone at each step, a metronome for a man who had spent forty years measuring other lives. The third was young — younger than Sebastian, certainly younger than his fifty years would have suggested a judge should be — and she looked frightened in a way the other two did not, which told him she still believed something could be lost.

"Ser Montrose." The old woman spoke first. Her voice carried without effort; it was a voice built for rooms like this, high-vaulted and indifferent. "I am Magistrate Ondrel. Once of the High Bench at Vellamare. This is Magistrate Corvel—" the man with the stick inclined his head a precise inch "—and Magistrate Sef. We were told you carry documents."

"I carry a cipher," Sebastian said. "And I'm told my name has traveled ahead of me faster than I have."

"Lys Varrow's letter reached us four days ago." Ondrel descended the last steps and stopped an arm's length away. She looked at Elira first, not at him — a long, unhurried look at the sleeping child, and something in her creased face moved and settled. "She wrote that you were coming. She also wrote that Minister Corvane's delegates will arrive within the fortnight, carrying gold enough to buy this enclave twice over and a warrant branding everyone under this roof an enemy of the Republic."

"A fortnight," Sebastian said.

"Or less. Delegates travel quickly when they are afraid." Ondrel turned and began walking back up the stair, speaking without looking to see if he followed. He followed. The tapping of Corvel's stick kept time behind him. "Understand what we are, courier. We are a tribunal the way a wreck is a ship. Three judges who refused to sign what we were ordered to sign, exiled here because killing us would have been impolite. We have no bailiffs. No prison. No power to compel a single witness beyond these walls. If you have come expecting justice, you have come expecting a great deal from very little."

"I've come expecting a hearing."

"Then you may be disappointed by how much a hearing costs." They reached a gallery above the hall, lined with shelves that sagged under ledgers and codices, the accumulated law of countries that no longer enforced it. Ondrel stopped before a window that looked east across the valley, where the road climbed toward the pass and, beyond it, the Republic. "The neutral powers recognize this enclave's charter — barely, grudgingly, the way one recognizes a poor relation. A verdict issued here binds no one. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the evidence is authenticated to the standard of the old charter. Signatures verified, seals confirmed, chain of custody established from origin to this room." She turned to face him fully. "We can try your general in absentia. We can clear his name or condemn it. But only if what you carry is proven true within six days of accepting jurisdiction — that is written into the charter itself, a safeguard against exactly the sort of weapon you are proposing to hand us. After six days, our finding is advisory. Worthless. And Corvane's delegates will make certain everyone knows it."

Six days. Sebastian held the number up against everything he knew: Ilse waiting two rooms away with her lenses and her reference collections and her exile-honed expertise; the cipher split across decoy satchels and hidden pages; a compass rose he had not yet found the courage to take apart. Six days was either generous or impossible, and he would not know which until he started.

"Then accept jurisdiction," he said.

Ondrel studied him. Up close her eyes were pale as riverwater, and they did not blink when they should have. "You understand what you're asking. If we hear this evidence and find it wanting — or find it authentic but incomplete — we will say so publicly. There is no half-verdict, courier. We will either give Aurelio Vance back his name or bury him under the weight of proof he cannot answer, because dead men testify poorly."

"He anticipated that."

"Dead men often do. It is their one advantage." Something that was almost a smile crossed the magistrate's face and was gone. "Very well. Tomorrow morning, in the scriptorium. Bring your documents, your archivist, and whatever explanation you possess for why a loyal courier of the Republic is handing its secrets to exiles. You'll forgive us if we ask hard questions. Suspicion is the only inheritance exile grants."

* * *

They gave him a cell of a room — a monk's cell, whitewashed, with a cot and a stool and a window slot that framed a slice of grey sky — and for the first hour after leaving the magistrates Sebastian sat on the stool and let the shaking come. It came quietly, as it always did now, more exhaustion than fear, a tremor in the hands that meant his body had run out of the currency it had been spending since the ferry. He fed Elira. He changed her. He watched her grip his finger with the fierce, unreasoning certainty of infancy and thought: six days. Six days and then either a name restored or a name destroyed, and either way Corvane's delegates arriving like weather.

Ilse found him there. She knocked once and entered without waiting, carrying an armload of reference volumes that she deposited on the cot with the confidence of a woman who had decided long ago that furniture served books, not the reverse. She was fifty-something and sharp-faced, ink stains permanent on her right hand, and she had not slept properly since the ferry crossing any more than he had.

"The magistrates spoke to me already," she said. "Ondrel sent a girl while you were being shown your cell. They want the authentication done under their eyes. Every signature, every seal, witnessed and countersigned as I work."

"That will slow you down."

"It will slow me down enormously." She lowered herself onto the stool with a sound like a rope under strain. "But it also means no one can later claim I forged what I verified. Which, given that Corvane's people will attack the verification before they attack anything else, is worth the lost hours." She rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "How many signatures are we speaking of?"

"Nineteen that matter. Chancellery hands, military adjutants, quartermaster seals. Plus the watermarks on the Meridian orders — those I can't verify at all. That's yours."

"And the missing page?"

Sebastian looked at the window slot. Grey sky, going darker. "Still missing. Still — unexplained."

"You don't believe it's lost."

"No," he said. "I believe Vance never sent it. I believe everything he did was deliberate, including this, and I haven't yet understood the design." He heard how it sounded — a man defending a ghost — and did not care. "There's a logic to him. There always was. When I find it, the page will find us."

Ilse regarded him for a long moment with the particular patience of someone accustomed to being the only careful person in the room. "Then tomorrow we begin with nineteen signatures and hope your faith in dead generals survives contact with the archive. Sleep if you can. The child sleeps; learn from her."

She gathered one volume back up — the seal registry, he saw — and paused at the door.

"Six days," she said. "I've authenticated documents in worse circumstances than comfortable exile. What I have never had is a deadline attached to history. Try not to watch me work. It makes men hover, and hovering men breathe on vellum."

* * *

Morning came cold and clean, and the scriptorium of the old monastery proved to be the one room in the enclave built for exactly this: long tables of scarred oak, south-facing windows, lamps hung on counterweighted chains so the light could be drawn close to the page without a flame ever touching it. Exiles had maintained it for decades — refugees from three different purges, Ilse said, each wave adding its reference works to the shelves — and the air held the mineral smell of lye and stone and the dry perfume of old ink.

Magistrate Ondrel sat at the far end of the principal table. Magistrate Corvel sat opposite her, his stick propped against the bench, a magnifying lens already screwed into his eye socket. Magistrate Sef took minutes in a hand so small it looked like insect tracks. And between them, laid out on cloth as carefully as relics, Sebastian opened the false bottom of his document case and produced the true cipher — the real pages, the ones Dain's people had chased across half a country and never touched.

He watched the magistrates' faces as the pages emerged. Ondrel did not move. Corvel's breath audibly changed. Sef's pen stopped.

"These are—" Sef began.

"Orders," Sebastian said. "Duplicate originals, or close enough that the distinction will be argued. Dispatch traffic between the Ministry of War and the eastern command, dated the month before the Meridian Massacre. Movement schedules. Supply denials. And this—" he laid a single page apart from the others, face up, "—a directive over three signatures instructing the eastern command that civilian columns encountered in the corridor were to be considered hostile logistics. Considered hostile logistics. Ninety-one words that turn four hundred dead farmers into a lawful engagement."

Silence held the room. Outside, wind moved through the valley grass with a sound like distant water.

"Read the third signature," Ondrel said quietly.

Sebastian read it. He had read it a hundred times, alone, in safehouses, on the road, and it had never once stopped feeling like a door opening onto a drop. "Corvane. Minister Corvane. Not his seal — his hand. The others signed; he wrote. As though he couldn't delegate even his own damnation."

"Chain of custody," Corvel said, and his voice was dry as his stick on stone. "From origin to this table, courier. Walk us through it."

So Sebastian did. He told them about the chancellery burning, about memorizing what could not be carried. He told them about Vance's aide, and the route through the border provinces, and the ambush at the ferry where he had surrendered a satchel full of blank paper dressed in cipher clothing. He watched Corvel's lens track every detail, cataloguing, and understood that the old man was doing precisely what Corvane's advocates would do later — hunting the seam where truth could be picked apart.

At noon Ilse arrived with her instruments and began, under their eyes, the slow surgery of verification. It was hypnotic to watch. She measured letter forms against exemplars with calipers and pure eye, traced watermark patterns over raking light, tested ink chemistry with reagents that bloomed color along the strokes. She worked in silence except for small, involuntary sounds — a click of the tongue, a soft *there* — and each sound landed on the table like a coin counted out.

By late afternoon she had verified five signatures. Four matched known exemplars beyond argument. The fifth — Corvane's — she worked over longest, and when she finally straightened, pressing her knuckles into her lower back, her expression was strange.

"Well?" Ondrel said.

"Authentic. Beyond any reasonable challenge — the hand habits are unmistakable, the ink period-correct, the watermark consistent with the eastern mills." Ilse set down her lens. "But that isn't why I hesitated. Look at the pressure of the strokes. Here, and here, and here." She indicated three passages with a probe of pale wood. "He pressed hardest not on the operative clauses but on his own name. Every time. A man bearing down on his own signature as if to fix it permanently into the world. I have authenticated ten thousand documents, Magistrate, and I have seen vanity, haste, anger, and fear in handwriting. I have never seen *pride* quite like this."

No one answered her. Sebastian looked at the signature again, at the deep-cut letters of a minister's name riding atop ninety-one words of murder, and thought of Corvane sitting somewhere east of the mountains with gold and warrants, and understood that they were not, in the end, fighting a different species of man. They were fighting the same animal wearing better clothes.

* * *

That night the enclave's refectory served barley soup and dark bread, and Sebastian ate it with Elira balanced on his knee, feeding her softened bread between his own spoonfuls while around them the exiled community conducted its quiet evening rituals — mending, reading, the low music of conversation in four languages. Word of the tribunal's acceptance had moved through the enclave faster than any official announcement. He felt the attention on him like sun through glass: warm at a distance, dangerous up close. These were people who had lost countries. They wanted his evidence to be true the way starving people want bread, and want, he knew, was not a reliable witness.

Magistrate Sef sat down across from him without asking. The youngest of the three, the frightened one, and up close her fear resolved into something finer — the tautness of a woman holding herself to a task.

"I drew the duty of preparing the procedural record," she said. "Which means I must ask you questions you will find insulting."

"Ask them."

"If the tribunal clears General Vance, the Republic will call this proceeding a fraud staged by traitors and exiles. Corvane's delegates will say precisely that, loudly, in every capital that matters. Some powers will believe them, because believing them is cheaper." She turned her cup in her hands. "Do you understand that a verdict here may save his name among people who already loved him, and change nothing among anyone else?"

"I've had weeks to understand it," Sebastian said. "Outcomes are not why I came. Truths are heavy, Magistrate. Someone has to carry them to a place where they can be put down."

Sef studied him for a moment, and then nodded slowly, as if a figure had finally balanced. "Ondrel thinks you're lying about something. The missing page. She believes you know more than you've said."

"She's right," Sebastian said, and watched surprise flicker across the young woman's face. "Tell her I'll say more when I understand it myself. I won't waste the tribunal's six days on a guess."

Elira chose that moment to seize his spoon and announce, loudly, her first opinion of tribunals. A few heads turned. Somewhere down the table, someone laughed — briefly, surprised by itself — and the sound moved through the room and loosened it, just slightly, the way a thaw loosens a frozen stream.

* * *

He was climbing toward his cell with a lamp when Ondrel intercepted him on the gallery landing. Alone, without her colleagues, she seemed older; the courtroom voice was packed away and what remained was simply a tired woman at the top of a staircase.

"Four signatures remain unexamined and fourteen unstarted," she said. "Your archivist is excellent — better than excellent; I remember her from the Chancellery archives, before either of us fell — but six days is six days, and the charter does not bend. So I will tell you what I did not say in front of the others." She came closer, and lowered her voice although the gallery was empty. "Twelve years ago, I sat on the panel that reviewed the conduct of the eastern campaign. Our findings were suppressed before publication. I resigned rather than sign them, and I have spent twelve years knowing that my resignation was the coward's version of what you are doing. Do not mistake this tribunal for charity, courier. We are settling our own accounts."

"Then we want the same thing."

"We want the same thing," Ondrel agreed. "Which is why you should hear the rest. A rider arrived at dusk, ahead of the mail, with a diplomatic pouch for the enclave's custodian. Official seals. Republican seals." She let that sit in the cold air between them. "The delegates' advance party is not a fortnight out, courier. They are five days out. Perhaps less, if they change horses through the nights, and men carrying Corvane's gold change horses through the nights."

Sebastian felt the arithmetic turn beneath him like a floor. "Five days. Then we don't have six."

"You have five, and part of the sixth if the weather holds. I am telling you because your archivist must reorder her priorities tonight, not tomorrow, and because you should spend whatever sleepless hours you have asking yourself what your dead general withheld, and why." Ondrel straightened, and the courtroom voice returned like armor closing. "The hearing resumes at dawn. Bring everything, Montrose. Bring even what you don't yet understand — especially that."

She went up the stairs, unhurried, her shadow sliding along the wall ahead of her. Sebastian stood on the landing with his guttering lamp and looked east through the window slot at the pass, black against the last grey of evening, where somewhere beyond sight men were riding through the night toward this pile of stones with gold in their saddlebags and a warrant in their dispatch case.

Five days. He touched the compass rose through his shirt, feeling its edges, its seams, the secret it had carried against his chest for a thousand miles while he called himself its keeper. Tomorrow he would stop carrying it and start listening to it.

Below him, faint through the floors, he could hear Elira crying for him, and he went down to her.
