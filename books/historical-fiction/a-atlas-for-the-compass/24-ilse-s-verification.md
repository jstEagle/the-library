## 24. Ilse's Verification

The enclave's archive occupied the shell of what had once been a customs house, back when the border was a line on a map instead of a wound in the world. Its vaulted ceiling was cracked from a shell that had struck the neighboring granary two winters past, and the crack ran the length of the room like a river drawn by a careless cartographer, forking and forking again above the shelves. Ilse had hung oil lamps from iron brackets along that crack, and in their light the whole room seemed to burn gently, endlessly, without consuming anything.

Sebastian had not slept. Elira had, bless her, curled in a basket Ilse had lined with a customs clerk's old coat — the Republic's eagle stitched on the collar, turned deliberately inward, against the child's cheek. Ilse had done that without comment, and Sebastian had understood it as a kind of blessing, or perhaps a small revenge: the eagle reduced to padding, warming an infant it would have condemned.

"Sit," Ilse said. She did not look up from the table where she had laid out the cipher pages in ordered rows, weighted at the corners with brass letter-openers, a magnifying lens, a dish of lamp-black, and three volumes whose spines had been repaired so many times they looked like quilts. "You will hover, and hovering makes me slow, and slowness makes you speak, and speaking makes me slow."

He sat. It was the sixth day since the tribunal's clerks had set their clock running, and the fourth since Ilse had begun. He had watched her work the way a man watches a surgeon operate on someone he loves — with a helplessness that curdled quickly into something like prayer.

Ilse Vantel had been the Republic's chief document examiner before the purges. Sebastian had known her name the way every courier knew it: as a warning. *Her eyes have ended careers,* his instructors had said, meaning signatures, meaning forgeries, meaning the quiet science of knowing which hand had held the pen. She had fled across the mountains with nothing but her lenses and her grudges, and the enclave had given her the archive, and she had given the enclave her vigilance. She was perhaps sixty, perhaps older; her hair was white as paper and cropped close, and her hands, when they moved over a page, moved with the tenderness of a woman handling something she fully intended to survive him.

"You should know," she said now, without preamble, "that the first three pages are genuine. I confirmed that yesterday. You were pacing the courtyard with the child and did not hear me say it."

"I heard you say it."

"You heard the words. You did not hear them. There is a difference, and it matters, because what I have to say today is worse, and I want you to have practiced hearing things."

Sebastian looked at the rows of pages. The cipher itself was Vance's own field system, the one they had invented together as younger men, drunk on cheap wine in a garrison that no longer existed — a substitution scheme built on the compass points, each letter a bearing, each word a route. Sebastian could read it in his sleep. He had read these pages in his sleep, four nights running, and what he had read had hollowed him: the logistics of Meridian. The requisitions. The signatures.

"Tell me anyway," he said.

Ilse drew the magnifying lens across the fourth page and stopped. "The signature of Minister Corvane is authentic. I will stake what is left of my reputation on it — the pressure of his downstrokes, the way he breaks the *r* at the shoulder, the ink. The ink is a Vellamarine chancellery blend, iron-gall with a trace of logwood, and the batch was mixed in the autumn of the massacre. I know the batch. I examined forty documents from that season before I left, and the logwood trace is like a fingerprint. No one outside the chancellery mixed it. No one inside it mixed it for private use." She lifted her eyes to him at last. "Corvane signed the order for Meridian. Not a forgery. Not a coercion. His own hand, steady, unhurried. The man was not afraid when he wrote it. That is the thing that will convict him, if anything does — the steadiness."

Sebastian found that his hands had closed into fists on his knees. He made them open. "And the others."

"Minister Havel. Authentic. Minister Ostrava — authentic, and interesting, because his signature here is dated nine days *before* the massacre, which means he signed in anticipation. They did not react to Meridian. They scheduled it." She said it the way she said everything, flat and precise, but Sebastian saw her thumb press whitely against the page's edge, holding it flat, holding herself flat. "Three ministers. Three authentic signatures. That is enough for your tribunal. That is more than enough. If the enclave's judges have spines, Corvane hangs."

"Then why," Sebastian said slowly, "do you look like a woman about to tell me my house has burned down?"

Ilse sat back. She removed her lens and set it down with a small, deliberate click, the way a judge sets down a gavel.

"Because the cipher is complete," she said, "and it is not complete. Do you understand me? Vance was a soldier, and a careful one. I never met him, but I have spent four days inside his mind, and I will tell you what I found there: a man who did everything twice. Every order referenced. Every page numbered. Your cipher pages are numbered — here, the lower corner, in his hand, small as a flea. One through eleven." She turned the pages so he could see. "But the numbering is not sequential. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten — and then twelve."

The crack in the ceiling seemed to fork again in Sebastian's vision. "Eleven is missing."

"Eleven is missing," Ilse agreed. "And it is not missing the way a page is lost, courier. Pages that are lost are torn, water-stained, burned at the edge, dropped in a river. This page was *removed*. Look." She drew the tenth page toward her and angled it into the lamplight. "The binding thread. Vance stitched these pages himself — waxed linen thread, a saddle stitch, the same stitch on every signature. Here, at the tenth, the thread enters and exits cleanly. Here at the twelfth, the same. But the thread runs *continuous* through the fold. Do you see? If page eleven had simply fallen out, the thread would have been cut or pulled free and we would see the violence of it. We do not. The thread passes through the fold where eleven should be and comes out the other side undisturbed — which means the page was never stitched in, or was unstitched and restitched with a skill I have seen perhaps twice in thirty years." She looked at him over the lamp. "Or means that Vance removed it himself, after stitching, and closed the seam so well that only a suspicious old woman with nothing but time would ever find the ghost of the needle holes. And I found them. There. And there. Three pinpricks, no larger than a flea's bite, where the thread once bit."

Sebastian stared at the fold. He could not see the pinpricks. He believed them anyway, the way one believes in a wound beneath a bandage.

"Why would he remove it?" he asked, and heard his own voice go quiet, the way it went quiet in the chancellery when the silences were the only things keeping men alive. "Why build a cipher to carry the truth and then take one page out of it?"

"Perhaps to protect it," Ilse said. "Perhaps to protect *himself* — no. Not himself. He was a dead man when he built this; a man protecting himself does not stitch ciphers for tribunals. Perhaps to protect the page. A cipher can be captured — you told me yourself you lost a satchel at the ferry, and I do not believe for one moment that the satchel you lost was the one that mattered. A man who splits his cargo does not put all of it in one fold." She tapped the ghost-holes with one fingernail. "Or perhaps — and consider this, courier, because I think it is the truth — perhaps the page was too dangerous to travel. Even encrypted. Even hidden. Perhaps what that page says is the kind of thing that gets not one courier killed, but every courier, every archivist, every judge, every child in the room."

The basket by the wall creaked. Elira stirred, sighed, and settled again, one small fist escaping the coat's collar. Sebastian found he had risen without deciding to and crossed to her, and stood there with his hand hovering above her, not touching, the way one hovers over a flame.

"The tribunal gave us six days," he said. "We have spent four. If I walk in tomorrow with three ministers condemned and the judges ask me *is that all*, and the answer is no — there is a page I cannot produce—"

"Then you will have armed Corvane," Ilse said. "He will stand before the enclave's delegates and say: the traitor Vance manufactured evidence, and even his own courier cannot produce the whole of it. A partial truth is a weapon pointed at whoever holds it." She gathered her lenses, her lamp-black, her quilted volumes, and stacked the pages with a care that was almost ceremonial. "But hear the rest, because there is a rest, and it is the reason I kept you sitting down."

He turned. Her face in the lamplight was old and lit and grim as a border stone.

"The numbering is not the only ghost," she said. "When I examined the fold of the tenth page, the needle holes told me something else. The thread that once ran through page eleven was *different*. Thinner. A different wax. I have seen that thread before — not in documents, in maps. Field dispatch maps, the kind staff officers carry into campaign, waterproofed against rain and river. Vance stitched a *map* into this cipher once, courier. Or something written on map-cloth. And then he took it out and carried it away, and the only trace of it left in the world is three pinpricks and a length of thread I am looking at through a lens." She closed the stack and bound it with cord. "Your general did not simply hide a page. He hid it somewhere he could find it again — somewhere he trusted — and he trusted almost nothing and no one. So you tell me, courier, who knew his mind. Where does a man like that keep the one truth that would burn his own republic's head off?"

Sebastian did not answer. He could not. Because something was turning over in him, slow and enormous, the way ice turns over on a river in spring — the compass rose in his pocket, the weight of it he had carried for three years, the weight he had carried through checkpoints and ambushes and a winter of snowbound decoding without once thinking of it as anything but a keepsake, a token, a promise made metal. *Guard what I love.* He had thought it meant the child. He had thought it meant the cipher. He had carried the answer against his ribs for a season and never once turned it over.

"Sebastian." Ilse's voice came to him from a distance. "You have gone the color of the page. What do you know?"

"Nothing," he said. "Not yet. Something I have to look at."

"Then look at it *after* you sleep, because—"

The door of the archive opened, and the cold came in with it, and with the cold came Dain.

The former hunter stood in the doorway with snow in his close-cropped hair and a satchel under his arm that Sebastian recognized, because he had watched it pulled from a drowned courier's saddlebag at the ferry and had spent a night persuading himself the loss was survivable. Dain had delivered Corvane's private ledger four days ago, and since then he had lived in the enclave the way a converted wolf lives among sheep: carefully, at the edge of the firelight, useful and untrusted in equal measure. He had not been invited into the archive. He had not been invited anywhere.

"Forgive me," Dain said, and the strange thing was that he seemed to mean it. "There is a rider at the gate. Vellamarine colors under a merchant's canvas — he thought the canvas hid them. He carries a warrant and a sealed case, and he asked for the tribunal's clerk by name." Dain's jaw worked. "The clerk he asked for is the one Corvane bought in the autumn. I read the man's file when I still worked for the other side. He is the one who certified the Meridian casualty lists."

Ilse rose. "How long until the delegates arrive?"

"A day," Dain said. "Two at the outside. The roads are opening." He hesitated in the doorway, snow melting off his shoulders, and looked at Sebastian with an expression that had stopped being a hunter's expression somewhere on the road and become something rawer. "Whatever it is you're missing — the page, the proof, whatever the old woman just told you that you went white over — you have a day to find it. Corvane isn't sending delegates. He's sending an ending. I've read his warrants. They don't summon. They *conclude*."

He set the drowned courier's satchel on the table — an offering, or an apology — and left the door open behind him, and the cold poured through it, and somewhere beyond the walls the enclave's single bell began to ring the hour.

Sebastian stood with his hand in his coat, closed around the compass rose, around the weight he had carried without understanding, and heard Vance's voice across three years and a garrison table: *if it ever comes to ruin, guard what I love.*

He had guarded the daughter. He had guarded the cipher.

He had not yet guarded the truth — because he had not known where Vance had hidden it. But he knew now, or he almost knew, the way a man almost knows a word on the tip of his tongue: the rose was not a token. It had never been a token. It was a lock, and somewhere in the Republic's broken heart was the door it opened, and the page that named the President was behind it.

"Sebastian," Ilse said quietly, watching his face. "Where is it?"

He drew the compass rose from his coat and laid it on the table between the lamp and the ghost-holes of the missing page, and the lamplight caught the worn brass, and the needle swung — and settled — and pointed east, toward the old campaign chapel, toward everything.

* * *

He did not sleep. Ilse had ordered him to; he had lied and said he would; and then he had sat by the archive's narrow window with Elira breathing against his shoulder and turned the compass rose over perhaps four hundred times, and each turn had shown him something new and nothing whole.

The rose was brass, the size of a pocket watch, its face engraved with the thirty-two points of the bearing. Vance had carried it through three campaigns; the needle housing was dented, the glass scratched into a fog. On the reverse, the old motto: *VERITAS DIRIGIT* — truth directs. Sebastian had read it a thousand times and heard it as sentiment. Now he heard it as instruction.

He thought about the thread. Thinner, waxed, waterproofed — the thread of field maps. He thought about the chapel at Vellamarine's eastern camp, the one Vance had rebuilt with his own hands during the armistice summers, the one where the general had kept, of all things, a reliquary — a joke between them, once, because Vance kept no saints in it, only the campaign maps he could not bear to throw away. *A man should pray over his mistakes,* he had said, laughing. *Mine are all drawn to scale.*

Sebastian had laughed then. He did not laugh now. Now the memory arrived with the cold certainty of a signature under a lens: the page was in the chapel, stitched or sealed into the cloth of a map, in a reliquary in a building that stood — if it still stood — inside territory the Republic had held for the whole of the war. Behind the lines. Behind Corvane's lines. A day's ride, if the roads were open, and the roads were opening, and Corvane's delegates were already on them.

He could send no one. There was no one to send — Dain was watched, Ilse was needed here, and the enclave's own couriers were strangers whose loyalties were a ledger with too many columns. The tribunal's clock ran out in two days. The delegates arrived in one.

Which meant, Sebastian understood, sitting in the blue hour before dawn with a sleeping child against his heart and a dead man's compass in his hand, that the choice had already been made for him — had been made three years ago over wine, had been stitched into a cipher fold, had been pointing east all along.

Elira woke at first light, and he fed her, and she watched him with Vance's gray eyes while he dressed for the road, and he told her, because there was no one else to tell, "Your father was cleverer than all of us. He knew I'd come too late to save him. So he made sure I'd come exactly in time for everything else."

The bell rang the hour again. At the gate, faintly, he could hear horses — too many horses, arriving too soon.

The delegates were early.
