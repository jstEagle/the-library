## 25. The Missing Page

The delegates' horses were still an hour out when Sebastian understood it — not gradually, as he had understood most things in that long winter, but all at once, the way a lock yields after a hundred failed keys because one morning your hand simply turns.

He was standing at Ilse's worktable with the cipher spread before him, every authenticated page laid out in sequence, and Elira asleep against his shoulder in the crook of his arm, her breath a small warm metronome against his collarbone. Ilse had pinned the final verification slip to the last sheet and said what she had said three times now: "It is complete except for the page that matters. Corvane's hand, Vance's signature, the President's absence. One leaf missing, Sebastian. The one leaf that names the head."

And he had been staring at the gap — the clean space where page eleven should have sat between ten and twelve, numbered by Vance's own meticulous hand, accounted for in his own inventory of enclosures — when the arithmetic of grief finally finished its sum.

Vance had never lost anything in his life.

* * *

Sebastian had watched the general pack for campaigns. He had seen the man account for spare horseshoes in mud up to the fetlocks, for ammunition counts down to the individual cartridge, for the exact number of men who could be fed from a depot that reported twice its actual stores. Aurelio Vance was not a man who misplaced things. He was a man who, when something could not be found, had already decided where it would be found.

So the page had not been lost. It had not been stolen from the cipher en route — Sebastian had carried it unbroken through ambush and betrayal, and the satchel Dain took at the ferry had held decoys anyway; the true pages had ridden against Sebastian's ribs the whole time, and they were all here, all present, all accounted for.

Except one.

Which meant Vance had removed it before sealing. Before sending. Before dying. He had read the tribunal's future the way he read terrain — saw the enclave, saw the authentication, saw the moment when a rump court of exiled judges would weigh his name against a republic's — and he had deliberately withheld the single document that would have made the verdict unanswerable.

Why? Sebastian had asked himself that question for two days, turning it like a stone in the pocket. Cowardice? No. Not that man. Protection of the President's office — some flickering hope that the state might survive intact if its head were spared? Perhaps. But Vance did not protect institutions. He protected people.

Then, last night, holding the compass rose under lamplight while Elira slept, turning it over and over, he had noticed what exhaustion had kept him from noticing for four months of roads.

The rose came apart.

Not broke apart — no violence required, no prying. It *unfastened*. There was a seam so fine he had taken it for a casting line, running around the circumference of the brass housing beneath the needle card, invisible unless you held the thing at the precise angle the light demanded. And on the underside of the housing, hidden until now beneath a plate stamped with the eight points of the compass, there were letters. Tiny ones. Struck into the metal with a jeweler's punch, each no taller than a grain of barley.

He had spent an hour copying them out by candlelight, Elira breathing against his chest, his hands steadier than he deserved.

They were not coordinates. They were not a cipher — he knew ciphers the way farmers knew rain, and this was simpler and crueler than any of them. They were a description. A place. Written in Vance's shorthand field notation, the same system the general had used for supply depots and artillery positions, the same system he had taught Sebastian over wine one peacetime evening years ago, laughing, saying *every courier should know how soldiers hide things, Montrose, because soldiers are sentimental and couriers are honest, and between those two vices the world manages to keep its secrets.*

Chapel of Saint Ferren. The campaign chapel outside Vellamare where the Third Army had wintered — where Vance had stood godfather to a colonel's daughter, where he had once told Sebastian, half-drunk and entirely sincere, that if he ever had a child he would want her christened in that chapel, because it was the only building in the whole cursed country that had never been used for anything but sheltering people.

The page was in the wall of the chapel where Vance had once said he wanted his daughter blessed.

Sebastian had sat very still for a long time after that, with the disassembled rose in pieces on the table and the candle burning down, and let the memory arrive.

* * *

It had rained that night too — a soft autumn rain, three years ago, when the war was a thing other people did in other provinces and the capital still smelled of bread instead of smoke. Vance's quarters above the old granary. Two bottles of Falernese red, the good vintage, saved from some dinner neither of them could remember attending. The general had been forty-one that year and looked thirty, broad through the shoulders, gray coming into his temples early in a way that made him look appointed rather than aged.

"You're leaving for the coast in the morning," Vance said. It wasn't a question. He signed Sebastian's travel warrants himself; he always had, even after Sebastian's official chain of command moved elsewhere. "Six weeks. Longer, if the passes close."

"Eight, if I'm careful."

"You're always careful." Vance turned his glass by the stem, watching the candle through the wine. "That's why I'm going to ask you something strange, and you're going to think it's the drink, and then you're going to forget it, and someday — I promise you, Montrose, someday — you're going to remember it word for word."

"I remember everything you say. It's a professional affliction. You pay me to."

"No." Vance smiled, and it was the smile that made whole mess halls go quiet with affection. "You remember everything because you're a man who keeps accounts. So keep this one." He set the glass down. "If it ever comes to ruin — the Republic, I mean. If it goes the way I've begun to fear it will, from the inside, quietly, the way fruit goes — guard what I love."

"That's not strange. That's just friendship."

"It gets stranger." He reached into his coat — the blue dress coat with the silver braid he wore only for occasions, though tonight there was no occasion — and brought out the compass. Brass, heavy, the needle card painted with the eight-pointed rose. "I bought this the week my commission was confirmed. Twenty-two years old. I've navigated by it across every province we hold. It has never once lied to me, which is more than I can say for any man who's served under me or above me." He pressed it into Sebastian's palm and closed Sebastian's fingers over it. "I want you to carry it. Not keep — carry. There's a difference. Keeping is storage. Carrying is a promise."

"Aurelio—"

"And here's the strange part, so listen." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and the candlelight made his eyes look almost black. "Guard what I love. Whatever that turns out to be, whenever it turns out to exist. A woman, maybe. A child, God willing. Or—" and here he had paused, and picked the glass up again, and his voice dropped into something quieter and harder than wine-talk, "—or nothing of flesh at all. Maybe what I love will turn out to be the truth about something terrible. Maybe guarding it will mean carrying it somewhere no one can burn it. You won't know which until the day comes. But you'll know it's the day, because I'll make sure the day announces itself."

"How?"

Vance had laughed then, full and warm, the laugh Sebastian would hear for the rest of his life in the space behind his own heartbeat. "Because I'm a general, Montrose. We announce everything. It's our one honest vice."

Sebastian had tried to give the compass back in the morning. Vance had refused, already in the saddle, already shouting orders, and had called down from the height of his horse: "Carry it! Don't lose it and don't study it — it's a compass, it does one thing, let it do that!" And Sebastian had carried it for three years through every mission, through the fall of the northern provinces, through the burning chancellery itself, never once studying it, because the man had told him not to, and obedience to Aurelio Vance had been the easiest discipline of his life.

Until a child arrived on his doorstep with the compass tucked in her blanket and a note that said *Forget this face.*

The day announcing itself.

* * *

"Guard what I love," Sebastian said aloud to the empty room, and Elira stirred against his shoulder, and he lowered his voice to almost nothing. "Guard what I love, and what I know. That was the whole sentence. I only ever heard half of it."

Because that was the trick of it — the beautiful, unbearable, entirely characteristic trick. Vance had known the cipher would reach the enclave. He had engineered its whole journey — the aide, Maren, the doorstep, the token, the trail — trusting that Sebastian would understand, and Sebastian *had* understood, and had delivered every page but the one that mattered. Vance had counted on that too. He had withheld the President's page because a tribunal given the complete truth in six days might rule completely — or might buckle, might bargain, might bury the worst of it to save the peace talks, might do exactly what tribunals do. But a tribunal left *incomplete*, left hungry, left with one named absence gnawing at its verdict — that tribunal would keep asking. Would keep the case alive past Corvane's delegates, past the gold, past the threats. Incompleteness was insurance.

And the location of the completion was entrusted not to any courier, not to any archive, not to any safe that could be cracked — but to a man carrying a brass compass across a broken country with a dead friend's daughter in his arms.

*Guard what I love.* Elira. *And what I know.* The page in the chapel wall.

One promise. Both halves. And Sebastian had walked through fire for the first half without ever hearing the second, because Vance had known that if Sebastian knew there was a second half — a page, a prize, a piece of paper worth armies — he might move differently. Might run straight for the chapel with the whole machinery of two governments at his back. Might get himself and the girl killed for a document.

No. First the child reaches safety. First the truth that can be proven is proven. Then — and only then — the rest.

"He made me earn the second half of the sentence," Sebastian whispered. "By finishing the first."

Elira sighed in her sleep, one small fist opening against his chest, and he bent his head and pressed his lips to her hair and let himself, for exactly the length of one breath, feel the whole weight of what it meant: that his friend had sat in a cell composing letters he could not send, arranging a future he would not see, and had trusted him — not with a mission, missions were for couriers — with a *daughter and a truth*, the two things a man cannot carry separately without dropping both.

Then the bell tower rang again, and below the window, in the courtyard of the enclave gate, voices were rising. Not the delegates' measured tones. Soldiers' voices. Clipped. Armed.

Ilse appeared in the doorway, chalk dust on her sleeves, her face drained of color. "There's a rider at the gate asking for you by name," she said. "Not by title. By name. And Sebastian — he's wearing Republic grey, but his papers say he's a customs inspector, and customs inspectors don't travel with six cavalry escort."

Sebastian closed his hand around the disassembled compass, feeling the hidden plate bite cold into his palm.

Dain. It had to be Dain — the hunter who had chased him across half the map, who had pulled worthless decoy pages from a river satchel and grown suspicious of his own masters. Which meant either the hunt had ended here, at the gate of the last free ground in the country —

Or something else had brought the hunter all this way, and Sebastian was about to learn which.

He kissed Elira's forehead, handed her gently to Ilse, and went down toward the sound of hooves.
