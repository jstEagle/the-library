## 30. The Rose Decoded

The compass rose did not want to open.

Sebastian had spent two nights convincing himself it would not — that he had invented its secret out of grief and sleeplessness, that a dead man's gift was only ever a dead man's gift, brass and enamel and nothing more. But Ilse's finding had left a wound in him that only one thing could close: the missing page. *One page absent from an otherwise complete dossier.* Vance had never been careless with paper in his life. He had counted dispatches the way other men counted money. If a page was missing, it was because Aurelio Vance had removed it himself.

Which meant the rose.

He sat at Ilse's worktable in the small hours, lamplight turned low so it would not reach the windows, and turned the token over in his fingers for perhaps the hundredth time. It was smaller than his palm, cast in dull bronze, shaped like the wind-roses that ornamented old sea charts — eight points radiating from a central hub, the cardinal points longer than the intercardinal, each tipped with a fleur or a barb. He had given it to Vance six years ago, bought from a stall in the Harbormarket of Vellamare on a morning when the war still seemed like something that happened to other countries. *So you can find your way home,* he had said, and Vance had laughed and said a general always knew where home was, but he had worn it anyway, hung it from his sword-belt where it clinked against the scabbard like a second heartbeat.

Now the rose lay cold in his hand, and it did not feel like a keepsake anymore. It felt like a lock waiting for a word.

*Guard what I love.*

He set it down on the felt and looked at it the way Ilse had taught him to look at documents — not for what it said, but for what it insisted upon. The points. Eight points. And the letters engraved along each point, tiny, nearly worn smooth by years of a soldier's thumb: N, E, S, W at the cardinals; and between them, half-hidden in the scrollwork, four others he had always taken for decoration — *V, A, C, E.*

Vance. Of course. Vanity, he'd thought, when he first noticed. A soldier's little signature to himself.

But vanity was not how Vance thought. Vance thought in ciphers, because Vance had spent twenty years corresponding with men who read other men's mail.

Sebastian pressed the four intercardinal points inward, gently, the way one tests a loose tooth. Nothing. He tried turning the central hub. The hub resisted — then, with a click so soft he might have imagined it, gave way a quarter-turn, like a key reaching the end of its travel. The whole rose shivered. Along the seams between points, hairline gaps appeared, dark as cracks in ice.

His heart was suddenly loud in the quiet room.

He gripped the north and south points and pulled apart, slow, afraid of breaking whatever mechanism lived inside — and the rose came apart in his hands into eight slender rods of bronze, each tapered at one end, each stamped along its length with minute characters: numbers, intervals, a sequence of turns. A key. Not one key — eight segments of one key, which assembled, when he fitted them point to point as they had fit as a rose, into a single long shaft with a complex, many-toothed bit at its head.

A chapel key. He knew it before he knew why. Some part of him had known since the night on the doorstep, since the blanket and the sleeping weight of the child and the note that said *forget this face.*

Because there was only one chapel in the world that Vance would trust with the truth about the Republic, and Sebastian had stood inside it.

* * *

It took three days to arrange the journey, and every hour of the three cost him sleep he could not spare.

Ilse argued for going herself; her argument collapsed under its own arithmetic. She was known at the enclave, expected daily at the verification table, and her absence would raise questions they could not afford while the tribunal's six days burned down. Dain offered, and Sebastian refused — Dain's face was on warrants now in three provinces, and a man built like a siege engine did not pass unnoticed through farm country. In the end it was Sebastian alone, because Sebastian had spent fifteen years becoming invisible, and because — though he did not say this aloud — there were doors that opened only for someone who had stood at Vance's right hand, and there was exactly one such person left in the world who was not dead or disgraced.

Elira cried when he handed her back to Ilse. She was walking now — staggering, rather, from furniture to trouser-leg to chair, shrieking her triumph at each arrival — and she had developed opinions about being held by anyone who was not Sebastian, opinions she expressed at volume. He knelt and let her seize his nose with both fists and pull, and he laughed because if he did anything else he would not leave.

"Three days," he told Ilse.

"The tribunal sits again on the fifth," she said. "If you're late—"

"I know what happens if I'm late."

"No," Ilse said quietly, and her eyes went past him to the child, to the scattered rods of the key wrapped in oilcloth in his satchel. "I don't think any of us knows yet what happens if we're late. Go carefully. Come back."

He traveled as a seed merchant, because seed merchants moved slowly, stopped often, and were beneath everyone's interest. The winter had loosened its grip but not released it; the roads were gray porridge of half-melted snow and mud, and the sky hung low and colorless as unwashed linen. He passed checkpoints where bored soldiers waved him through after a glance at forged papers so good they insulted their makers. He slept in barns. He fed his horse by hand in the dark and talked to her in a low voice, because talking kept the thinking from going too far down certain corridors.

On the second evening he crested a ridge and saw the valley below, and stopped, and could not move for a long time.

The camp at Haltern Reach. Vance's last command before the fall. He could see the parade ground gone to weeds, the officers' row with its roofs half-collapsed, the long sheds that had held ten thousand men — and at the eastern edge, small and white and absurdly intact among the ruin, the campaign chapel.

They had built it in the first summer of the war, when the army was young and victory seemed a matter of scheduling. Soldiers' hands raised the timber; soldiers' wives painted the walls; and Vance, who believed in God the way he believed in artillery — as a force to be respected, rarely consulted, never ignored — had paid for the roof out of his own purse and attended service every Sunday without fail, sitting in the third row because the first two belonged to the men. Sebastian had sat beside him more times than he could count, shoulder to shoulder in the smell of pine shavings and lamp oil, listening to chaplains bless a cause that Vance, even then, had begun to suspect.

*If it ever comes to ruin, guard what I love.*

He had thought, all these months, that the words meant the child. They meant the child. But they meant this too — and he understood now, with the clarity that arrives too late to be comfortable, that Vance had spoken the promise in the plural on purpose, that a man who hid a page inside a rose inside a friendship had planned his own destruction down to the last hinge, and had trusted Sebastian to follow the hinges one by one.

*What I love, and what I know.* Ilse had said it once, half-joking, and neither of them had laughed.

* * *

The chapel door was locked, and the key fit.

That was the terrible part, afterward, the part he kept returning to: how perfectly it fit. Eight segments screwed together made a shaft as long as his forearm, and the teeth caught the wards of the lock with a sequence of small, precise surrendering clicks, and then the bolt drew back and the door swung open onto darkness that smelled of cold wax and dust and, faintly, incredibly, of pine — the same pine, he supposed, the same resin worked deep into the grain, unchanged by everything that had happened since the last congregation sang here.

He lit the lamp he carried and stood in the nave and let himself, for one unguarded minute, remember.

Forty pews. He walked their length slowly, trailing his fingers along the backs, and the wood was smooth where a thousand soldiers' hands had worn it. The altar was bare except for a cloth gone gray with dust. Above it, the wall had been painted with a motto — he remembered the painting of it, a corporal with steady wrists working letter by letter while the regiment pretended not to watch:

**FOR THE REPUBLIC WE KEEP FAITH.**

Someone had thrown something at it during the collapse. A boot heel, maybe, or a stone. The plaster was starred around the word *faith*, and a long crack ran down through *keep*, and Sebastian stood looking at the damage and felt something in his chest go very quiet and very hard.

Where would a man hide a page in a chapel?

Not in the altar — too obvious, and Vance distrusted obviousness. Not under the floor; the floors here were plank over earth, searched a hundred times by scavengers. Not in the confessional or the vestry.

In the place he loved. In the place that was *his*. In the pew where he sat every Sunday, third row, left side, aisle seat — because Vance always took the aisle seat so that latecomers would have to climb past him and be reminded, however briefly, that they were expected.

Sebastian knelt at the third pew on the left, aisle side, and put his hands under the seat, and found the panel.

It was hinged, small, no bigger than a hymnal, fastened from beneath with a catch that required the last segment of the rose's key — a short stub with a single tooth — to release. Inside the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, sealed twice with black wax bearing the impression of a signet Sebastian recognized because he had seen it pressed into a thousand dispatches: the crest of the Republic itself. Vance had used the state's own seal to hide the state's own crime. No searcher would break a seal like that; no thief would dare.

He broke it anyway. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He had expected them to shake.

One page. Thin archive stock, dense with the cramped official hand of a ministry clerk, countersigned in three places. He carried it to the light of the lamp and read it standing up, because some part of him understood that if he sat down he would not get up again for a long time.

It was the missing page of the dossier. The orders for Meridian — the true orders, the originals, above the copies Corvane had doctored and the copies the cabal had burned. Supply authorizations. Troop dispositions. The timetable of the "pacification." And at the foot, in the signature line reserved for ultimate authority, the name he had served for fifteen years, the name printed on the currency and recited by schoolchildren and invoked over every grave from the capital to the coast:

*By order and under the seal of the Office of the President of the Republic.*

Signed. Countersigned. Witnessed.

Not Corvane. Corvane was on the page — his initials annotated the margins, his hand adjusted the casualty projections downward, as though massacre were a budget item requiring trimming — but Corvane had been a steward. An accountant of atrocity. The order came from above him. The order had always come from above him.

The Republic had not been betrayed. The Republic had been the weapon.

Sebastian lowered the page very carefully, the way one lowers a wounded man, and then he sat down in the third pew, in Vance's seat, and put his face in his hands.

He had prepared himself, in the way one prepares oneself for weather, for the possibility that the dossier would implicate powerful men. Corvane, certainly. The ministers whose names Ilse had verified. He had even permitted himself the darker thought that it might touch the palace — that corruption, like water, finds the lowest rooms. But this. This was not a minister's crime. This was not ambition or greed or fear. The President had signed the death of a town full of people and called it policy, and then had stood at the funeral of the town and wept, and Sebastian had watched him weep on the newsreels, and had believed.

Every courier run. Every cipher memorized and burned. Every friend lost, every safehouse abandoned, every name struck from the rolls — all of it done in service of a head of state who had ordered the horror himself and let lesser men carry the guilt like porters carrying furniture.

And Vance had known. Had known for how long? Since before his arrest — since before Meridian, maybe. Long enough to split the truth, to scatter it, to build a trail out of a compass and a promise and a daughter, because he had understood, with the terrible clarity of the condemned, that a truth this size could not survive in one piece. One piece could be stolen. One piece could be denied. But a truth distributed across a chapel and a rose and a friend and a child — that truth had to be *assembled*, and assembly required love, and love, Vance had gambled, was the one thing Corvane's people could not counterfeit.

*Forget this face.* So that no one could follow the child to the truth. Except the one person the truth was addressed to.

Sebastian sat in the chapel until the lamp guttered, and outside the winter dusk came down over the ruined camp, and somewhere in the dark a bird made a sound like a question asked twice.

When he finally rose, he folded the page into the inner lining of his coat, against his chest, where he had once carried ciphers worth provinces. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

At the door he paused and looked back at the motto on the wall — FOR THE REPUBLIC WE KEEP FAITH — and at the crack running through it, and he found, unexpectedly, that he was not angry. Anger would come later; anger was a country he would have to cross. For now there was only a vast, cold tenderness, and beneath it a resolve so clean it frightened him: the tribunal would hear this. The world would hear this. Whatever it cost, whoever it burned, the page would be read aloud in a room full of witnesses, and the name at the bottom of it would stand in the air where no gold and no office could reach it.

He locked the chapel behind him with the rose's key, and stood a moment on the step with the fragments warm in his pocket, and turned east toward the enclave.

He was two miles down the road when he saw the rider.

A single figure on a spent horse, coming along the valley road at a walk that spoke of many days' travel — and beside the saddle, catching the last gray light, the unmistakable cut of a chaplain's coat.

The man saw Sebastian and raised one hand, palm out — the gesture of a traveler, not a soldier — and reined in, and called across the distance in a voice roughened by cold:

"You're Montrose. I'd know you anywhere — the General described you to me a hundred times." He fumbled at his breast, and drew out an envelope, stained by weather, softened at the corners by handling. "He wrote this the morning they shot him. He made me swear to find you before the trial ended. He said—" the chaplain's voice caught, steadied— "he said you'd need it *before* you finished reading."

Sebastian stood in the road with the President's signature lying against his heart, and reached out, and took his dead friend's last words into his hands.
