## 32. The Tribunal Convenes

The hall they gave the tribunal had once been a customs house, and it still smelled of salt and mildew and the ghosts of ledgers. Sebastian noticed such things now. Six days of waiting had trained him to notice everything — the way a room breathed, the way light fell across a face at the moment a man decided whether to lie.

The sixth morning came grey over the enclave's harbor, and with it the sound of chairs being dragged into rows on the upper floor of the customs house. Ilse had been awake since before dawn, her wounded arm bound in a sling she refused to wear because, she said, it made her look like an invalid when she needed to look like an institution. She had arranged the cipher pages in numbered folders on a long table draped in green cloth — the only green cloth anyone could find, someone said, because green was the color of neutrality, though Sebastian suspected the color had been chosen by whoever owned the table.

He stood near the back with Elira against his chest, wrapped in wool so that only her face showed. She was seven months old now, or thereabouts; no one knew her true birthday, so Ilse had simply declared one, and on that day there had been honey cake and Dain had stood awkwardly by the window holding a gift he clearly hadn't chosen himself. She slept now through the murmur of the assembling crowd, which Sebastian took as a kind of blessing. The child would sleep through the reading of the thing that had orphaned her, and wake into whatever world came after.

The judges were five. Three were exiled chancellery jurists from Vellamare's old courts — men whose names had been struck from official rolls years ago, who now wore coats gone shiny at the elbows and carried their learning like contraband. The fourth was a Meridiani woman, Denara Voss, who had lost her town and most of her family to what the cipher described, and who had insisted on sitting despite every argument that grief disqualified her. "Grief is my qualification," she had told them, and no one had found an answer to that. The fifth was an elderly foreign observer, Magister Orlin of the Halvetic Confederation, present under seal of the neutral powers, whose presence was itself the entire reason the tribunal could claim any legitimacy at all. He was small, precise, and had hands that never stopped moving, folding and unfolding a handkerchief as though the fate of nations could be creased into order.

Sebastian had testified the day before — six hours under questioning from Chancellor Berrow, the eldest of the exiled jurists, walking line by line through the chain of custody from the burning chancellery to the safehouse to the road east to this hall. He had spoken carefully, without flourish, the way he'd been taught: a courier's testimony is only as good as his memory for detail, and detail was all he had left to give. His name was already ruined. What remained was the truth's provenance.

Now the doors opened at the ninth hour, and the hall filled.

* * *

They had expected perhaps forty people. More than two hundred came — exiles, merchants, sailors, refugees from the interior who had drifted to the enclave in rags and heard rumor of a trial. There were no seats for them; they lined the walls and stood along the windows, and when the five judges took their places beneath the old customs seal — a carved anchor furred with dust — a silence went through the room like weather.

Chancellor Berrow rose. He was nearly eighty and had to lean on the table, but his voice carried the way old voices sometimes do, worn thin everywhere except the center.

"This proceeding is convened outside the jurisdiction of the Republic of Vellamare," he said, "because within that jurisdiction, no court remains that has not been purchased. Let no one mistake what happens here for law. Law requires a state, and the state we served has eaten its own laws. What happens here is testimony. It is a record, made publicly, witnessed by neutral authority, so that when the reckoning comes — and it will come — no one can say they did not know."

Orlin unfolded his handkerchief, folded it again. Denara Voss did not move at all.

"The evidence consists of authenticated cipher fragments," Berrow continued, "decrypted and cross-verified by Archivist Ilse Vantel, formerly First Keeper of the Chancellery Records, whose credentials have been examined and sealed into the record. We begin with the winter dispatches of the third year of the war."

And then they read.

Ilse read first, her voice flat and exact, giving numbers, dates, requisition codes — the language of bureaucracy, which is the language horror speaks when it wants to keep its hands clean. Orders routed through the Ministry of Provision that never reached Meridian's garrison. Arms shipments logged as delivered that appear in no garrison ledger. A second set of manifests, in a different hand, redirecting the same shipments to depots that did not officially exist. Then the correspondence that followed: ministers discussing, in the careful circumlocution of men who know paper outlives intention, how the failure of the garrison might be *rendered instructive*.

Sebastian listened and watched the room instead. He watched a woman near the window put her hand over her mouth. He watched a fisherman in oilskins understand, slowly, in real time, that the war he had been conscripted into had been arranged for him like a meal. He watched Orlin's hands go still.

Then Denara Voss rose to read the Meridian section herself, and Sebastian understood why she had fought to sit on this tribunal. Her voice broke twice and recovered both times, and she read the casualty figures not as figures but as names — the names of streets, of parishes, of families she had grown up among — and somewhere in the middle of it the room stopped being a courtroom and became something older than law: a town square where the dead are counted aloud so the living cannot pretend they weren't listening.

Elira woke during the reading and did not cry. She looked around at the crowded hall with wide, grave eyes, as if she understood that she would spend her whole life being told about this hour, and was beginning early.

* * *

Corvane's delegates sat in the second row.

There were three of them, arrived the previous evening under diplomatic seals, dressed in the Republic's best black, attended by two clerks and four guards who were not permitted to carry arms inside and therefore carried their contempt instead. Their mandate was obvious to everyone: sit, glower, object on procedural grounds at intervals, and lend weight to the position that the enclave harbored traitors and forgers. Sebastian had seen the tall one, Delegate Rast, at functions in the capital years ago — a man whose career had been built on knowing precisely whom to bow to.

Through the morning they held formation. When Ilse presented the authentication protocols, Rast objected that the archivist was an exile with a motive. When the cipher keys were demonstrated, the second delegate objected to the translation. Each objection was answered, each answer recorded, and each time the objection died it seemed to cost them something visible, like men bailing a boat with slower and slower strokes.

It was the signatures that broke them.

Ilse had saved the authentication of the ministerial signatures for last, deliberately — Sebastian knew her well enough now to know it was deliberate — because among the orders concerning Meridian, in the hand of Minister Corvane himself, verified against a dozen exemplars of his known correspondence, was a phrase that had become infamous since the war began. *The town is an acceptable expenditure.* Nine words in the dry arithmetic of state, initialed, dated eleven days before the massacre.

When Ilse read the phrase aloud and named the hand, Rast stood up.

"This body has no standing," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, which Sebastian noted with a courier's precision, because a crack means a man has rehearsed composure and run out of it. "These documents are forgeries. This proceeding is sedition staged by exiles and deserters. The Republic will remember everyone in this hall—"

"Sit down, Delegate," said Berrow mildly, "or be remembered instead."

Rast did not sit. He gathered his papers with shaking hands, and his colleagues rose with him, and the two clerks began stacking folders they had never opened, and the four unarmed guards moved to the door with the studied indifference of men who wanted very much to be somewhere else. At the threshold Rast turned and delivered his final line to the room: "You will learn what the Republic does to traitors." But he said it quietly, and to no one in particular, and the door closed behind them before he finished.

Into the ringing silence, Denara Voss said, "Record that the delegation of the Republic withdrew rather than hear the signature of its own minister read aloud. Note the time."

Orlin's pen scratched. The record clerk wrote it down. And Sebastian felt the floor of the world shift a half-degree — the way you feel a ship change heading before your eyes confirm it — because he understood that something had just ended. Not the war, not the cabal, not the danger. But the era in which Corvane could call this forgery and be believed by anyone who mattered. The delegates had walked out of the room, but they had walked out of the argument too, and everyone in the hall knew it.

* * *

They adjourned at midday, and the wavering began.

Sebastian saw it in the antechamber afterward, in the corridor that smelled of tar and old rope. The neutral powers had sent observers besides Orlin — a Halvetic consul, a pair of Estharan trade attaches, a clergyman with letters from two southern principalities — men who had come prepared to witness a farce and depart with polite regrets. Now they clustered near the windows speaking low and fast, and Sebastian, whose whole life had been spent reading rooms, read this one easily.

The consul wanted to know if the President-naming page would be produced. It would not — not yet, not until the compass rose yielded its secret, and Vance's campaign chapel lay a hundred miles inside a country still calling them all traitors — and its absence was a crack the observers pressed at like tongues in a sore tooth. *If the archive is authentic, where is its head? If the crime reaches the ministers, why do you stop short of the man who commanded the ministers?* Orlin defended the tribunal's patience; the consul countered that patience looked like doubt; and through it all ran the colder current beneath, the one Sebastian had heard his whole career in the chancellery corridors: *which side will win, and what happens to us if we chose wrong?*

That was the wavering. Not of conscience — conscience was the story these men would tell later — but of arithmetic. Neutral powers survive by betting late. The evidence was now heavy enough to bet on. Almost. Nearly. Provided nothing went wrong before the verdict, provided the missing page surfaced or the tribunal's nerve held, provided Corvane's gold and Corvane's warrants did not arrive in the enclave before the ink dried.

Sebastian stood in the corridor with his daughter asleep against his chest and listened to freedom being weighed in drachms, and felt something he had not let himself feel since the safehouse: not fear exactly, but its exhausted cousin, the understanding of how much remained undone.

"You're scowling at the consul," said Dain, appearing beside him with two cups of terrible coffee. He handed one over. The former hunter had a way of materializing at Sebastian's elbow now, as if his instincts had simply reversed polarity and appointed him guard.

"I'm counting," Sebastian said. "Old habit."

"Counting what?"

"How many ways this can still fail. The delegates left, but they'll report to Corvane tonight. Which means tomorrow, or the day after, Corvane's real answer arrives. Gold, warrants, ships — whatever buys this port's council faster than the truth can buy the tribunals." He sipped the coffee. It was, if anything, worse than Lys used to make, and the thought of Lys — exiled now, warning delivered, family scattered — sat briefly on his chest alongside the sleeping child. "We won the morning, Dain. Mornings are cheap."

Dain looked out the window at the harbor, where a ship with unfamiliar colors had anchored the evening before, riding at the outer roads, flying no flag of any nation Sebastian could name. He had noticed it. So, apparently, had Dain.

"That ship's been signaling since dawn," Dain said. "Short signals. Repeated." He paused, and Sebastian watched the man who had once hunted him across half the republic choose his next words like steps on ice. "I was Corvane's instrument long enough to know what an instrument looks like when the hand moves. Something is coming. And there's a second thing." He lowered his voice. "One of the harbor clerks owes me — old debts, from the other life. He says a courier came in on the night packet two days ago. Carrying a sealed warrant under the Chancellor's counter-signature. Made out blank."

"Blank?"

"So it can be filled in with whatever names are necessary." Dain's mouth twisted. "That's how Corvane always worked. The paperwork arrives after the decision. Never before."

Above them, faintly, through the floor, they could hear the tribunal resuming — Berrow's voice rising again, steady, calling the roll of the dead of Meridian into the record of the living. Justice outside the law, trembling but standing, and beneath it the harbor water slapping the pilings like a slow clock.

Sebastian shifted Elira higher against his shoulder and looked out at the unnamed ship riding at anchor, patient as a debt.

"Then we don't have six days anymore," he said. "We may not have six hours. Ilse needs to see me — I think I finally understand what Aurelio hid inside the compass rose. And I think we're running out of time to find it."
