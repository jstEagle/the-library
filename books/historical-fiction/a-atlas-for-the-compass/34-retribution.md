## 34. Retribution

The border at Kolver's Ford was not a border so much as a wound that had scabbed over and forgotten it was still open. A river the color of old pewter. A customs house burned in the first autumn of the war and never rebuilt, its roofbeams black ribs against a white sky. On the far bank, the neutral territories began — marked by nothing more than a painted post, half-toppled, and the particular quality of silence that belongs to places where two jurisdictions meet and neither wishes to be first.

Sebastian came down the western road slowly, Elira against his chest beneath his coat, her breath a small warm metronome against his collarbone. Ilse walked beside him with the cipher satchel — the true one, the one Dain had chased across half a republic without ever holding — slung across her body like a bandolier. Behind them came the tribunal's escort, four exiled soldiers in mismatched grey who had volunteered for this last errand as men volunteer when they no longer believe they will live to regret anything.

They had been an hour on the road when they heard the shot.

One report only, flat and dry, swallowed almost immediately by the river's murmur. The escort froze; Sebastian did not. He knew the sound the way he knew the sound of his own pen — the crack of a service pistol, military load, fired by someone who had learned to shoot from a manual rather than a trench. There was only one man on this road who carried such a weapon and used it so precisely.

"Dain," he said, and started to run.

Elira woke and cried out at the jostling, and Sebastian slowed despite himself — he could not run with her, would never again be a man who could run with everything he loved and nothing held back — and so he arrived at the ford at a hard walk, chest heaving, in time to see the whole tableau laid out before him like a scene from some brutal morality play staged on the frozen mud.

Dain stood at the water's edge with his pistol still raised and smoking faintly. Twenty paces ahead of him, kneeling in the shallows with both hands pressed to his thigh where blood spread dark through the grey cloth, was Minister Edran Corvane.

Corvane had come south fast. That much was written all over him — the greatcoat buttoned wrong, the silver at his temples wild, one boot caked to the knee with river muck. Beside him, scattered and sinking into the mud, lay the contents of a strongbox: deeds, warrants, letters of credit drawn on banks that no longer answered, and gold enough to buy a small province. All of it worthless now, or soon to be. A man fleeing with money is a man who already knows the money has stopped meaning anything.

"Montrose." Corvane saw him first. Of course he did; even ruined, even bleeding into a frozen river, the man's instincts were those of a creature built entirely for calculation. His face assembled itself — Sebastian watched it happen, the pain receding behind composure the way water finds its level — into something almost like warmth. "Thank God. Thank God you're here. This man is deranged. I am a minister of the Republic, and this man has committed assault upon—"

"You're not a minister," Sebastian said quietly. "You haven't been for eleven days."

The tribunal verdict had gone out in triplicate over the mountains, carried by riders faster than any fugitive. Corvane knew it too; Sebastian could see the knowledge in him, filed away behind the eyes, denied the way a gambler denies the last card.

"He pulled a pistol on me on the road," Corvane said. "He demanded documents. Documents, Montrose, as if I were some common smuggler—"

"You are a common smuggler," Dain said.

His voice was the strangest thing about the scene. Sebastian had heard Dain speak perhaps a dozen times across the long pursuit — at checkpoints, across drawn blades, once through a door at the inn at Two Rivers — and every time the voice had been the same: level, certain, the voice of a man reading from a text that had never once contradicted itself. This voice was different. It was quiet and slightly hoarse, and it came from somewhere Dain had apparently not visited before.

Dain lowered the pistol until it pointed at the mud between Corvane's knees. He did not look at Sebastian or Ilse or the escort. He looked at the river.

"I have been thinking about Meridian," he said.

* * *

Later, sitting on the fallen lintel of the customs house while the tribunal escort bound Corvane's leg above the bullet wound, Dain told it plainly, in the manner of a man giving an after-action report on his own soul.

He had reached the ford the night before and taken a room in the ferryman's cottage to wait for the delegates' schedule — Corvane's flight had been predictable, he said, in the tone of a man apologizing for arithmetic. In the small hours, unable to sleep, he had read his orders again. Not the public ones; the private sheet, the one bearing Corvane's personal seal, which he had carried against his breast since autumn.

Then he had opened Corvane's strongbox instead of guarding it. It had seemed, he said, like a reasonable thing to do. He had been a clerk before he was a soldier; he understood ledgers.

And there, among the letters of credit, he had found the requisitions. Grain requisitioned from Meridian in the famine winter — not seized by enemy forces, as the official histories had it, but collected by the Republic's own commissars under sealed orders, and sold across the southern line at four hundred percent profit. The proceeds routed through accounts whose signatures Dain had spent three months learning to recognize because they appeared on his pay chits.

"My company drew rations from that grain," Dain said. "In February. We were told the depot had been raided. Two men died of what the surgeon called wasting fever, and I wrote letters to their mothers explaining that the Republic honored their sacrifice." He turned the private order sheet over in his hands, though he no longer needed to read it. "I have killed eleven people in the course of pursuing you, Montrose. I kept count. It seemed professional. And every one of them was keeping me from delivering this grain ledger back to the man who wrote it."

He looked up at Sebastian then, and his eyes were the same steady grey they had always been, but the certainty behind them had been replaced by something rawer and more durable.

"When I saw him on the road this morning," Dain said, "I did not draw my pistol to arrest him."

* * *

The river ran past them, indifferent. Corvane sat propped against the customs house wall, his leg bound, his hands — those precise, ink-stained hands Sebastian had seen sign death warrants in beautiful cursive — resting open on his knees. He had stopped performing. That was the remarkable thing. Whatever machinery had driven the performance had finally run down, and what remained was smaller than Sebastian expected: a tired, sharp-faced man of fifty with mud drying on his boots.

"You should understand the offer I'm about to refuse," Corvane said, "before your archivist's tribunal makes its little ceremony of it."

"It isn't my tribunal."

"Everything is someone's tribunal, Montrose. You of all people know that. Law is only vengeance that has learned to write its name legibly." He gestured at the scattered papers in the mud with his chin. "There are men on that tribunal who took my money for years. There are neutral powers who will take it again within the month. Kill me here, quietly, and the record dies with me — clean, ambiguous, survivable. Hand me over alive, and I will spend my trial saying things that will embarrass everyone who ever shook my hand. Including your dead general's friends. Including, possibly, your dead general." He smiled, thin as paper. "I am worth more to you dead, courier. Ask your conscience. Ask your arithmetic."

It was well made. Sebastian granted him that. It was the argument Corvane had been making his whole life, dressed now in the grammar of self-sacrifice: that power outlives its holders, that the machine matters more than the man, that everyone has a price and the wise keep a schedule of prices.

Ilse spoke from the doorway of the burnt customs house. "The tribunal has authenticated forty-one pages of your correspondence, Minister. Your signature appears on nine of them. Do you imagine we need your testimony?"

"I imagine," Corvane said, "you need witnesses more than you know."

"We have witnesses." Ilse came forward, and Sebastian saw that she was holding the compass rose — no. She was holding Elira's blanket, and Elira herself, having decided the cold was worth complaining about, was announcing it. Ilse held the child out toward the ruined man with the terrible neutrality of a good archivist producing an exhibit. "This is Aurelio Vance's daughter. He is dead. She does not know it yet, and she will grow up not hating you, because she will not be taught to. That is what we have, Minister. That is what survives your arithmetic. Now stand up. You're going to trial."

* * *

But it was not settled. Words settle nothing at riverbanks; only decisions do, and the decision belonged to the man holding the pistol.

Dain had not holstered it. Through all of Ilse's speech, through Corvane's counter-offer, through the escort's nervous shifting and the escort captain's repeated suggestion that regulations applied, Dain had stood with the weapon loose in his hand, barrel down, thumb resting along the frame — not aiming, not lowering. Waiting.

Sebastian crossed the mud to him.

He did not take the pistol. He had thought about taking it, during the walk over — he was, after all, a man who had spent a career learning what people wanted and how to give it to them or withhold it — and he had concluded that touching the weapon would be the one unforgivable thing. Some choices must be witnessed, not managed.

"Dain."

"Meridian," Dain said. It was not agreement or refusal. It was simply the fact, laid on the air between them like a stone on a grave. "Four hundred people, Montrose. They starved in a town the Republic had already written off, and the commissars took their seed grain on the way out. I have read the requisition numbers. I can recite them. 4-4-0-9 through 4-4-1-7." He looked at Sebastian, and for the first time since the road east, since the inn, since the beginning, he looked tired. "You don't forget numbers like that. I tried to be a man who forgets things. It's the whole profession, isn't it? Both our professions. You carry what mustn't be read; I erase what shouldn't have been written. And the whole time—" his hand tightened fractionally on the grip "—the whole time the numbers stay."

"Yes," Sebastian said. "They stay."

"If I do this"—a slight movement of the pistol toward Corvane—"it's done cleanly. One round. He will feel nothing. If you do it your way, he stands in front of judges for weeks, and half of what he knows comes out sideways, and the powers that be negotiate him down to a sentence and a pension, and in twenty years there is a street named after him in the new capital because the new capital needs streets named after somebody. I have watched republics, Montrose. Have you?"

"Once or twice."

"Then tell me why law is better than this." Dain's voice cracked, just barely, on the word *this*, and Sebastian understood suddenly that he was not being argued with. He was being begged. Dain wanted to be talked out of it. A true believer, cracked open, does not want vindication — he wants someone to hand him a reason to believe in something again, even something as battered and unglamorous as a tribunal of exiles in a rented hall.

Sebastian gave him the truth, which was all he had left.

"Because Meridian deserves better than our hands."

He said it quietly, and the river carried it off, and Dain stared at him.

"They deserve names," Sebastian went on. "Read aloud. Recorded. Argued over by lawyers in bad wigs. They deserve the whole tedious, humiliating, human machinery of justice — the committees, the minutes, the witnesses who contradict each other, the clerks copying it all out in fair hand. Because vengeance is quick and law is slow, and everything that matters is slow, Dain. Raising a child is slow. Grief is slow. Truth is slow. Corvane's crime was fast — that's how you know it was a crime. If we kill him here, Meridian becomes a story we tell. If he stands trial, Meridian becomes a fact that cannot be untold. I have spent my life carrying facts." He looked down at Elira, asleep again against Ilse's shoulder, one fist curled under her chin. "I'd like to end my life carrying them still."

For a long moment Dain said nothing. The wind came down the valley and moved the surface of the river in long silver seams. Somewhere upstream a bird called, absurdly ordinary, and Corvane watched them both with the fixed attention of a man listening to the terms of a contract he could not influence.

Then Dain turned, walked six paces to the water's edge, and threw the pistol into the river.

It went in without ceremony — a splash, a swirl, gone — and Dain stood looking at the place where it had vanished as though expecting it to argue.

"The tribunal will want my testimony," he said, without turning around. "Eleven days of pursuit. Names, dates, the decoy satchel, Corvane's private orders. I'll give them everything. But I want it recorded—" and now he did turn, and his voice was the old level voice again, steadied by purpose rather than certainty, which Sebastian suspected was the stronger foundation after all "—that I did not bring him in for justice. I brought him in for Meridian. Justice is what you're going to have to make of him. That part isn't mine."

"That," Sebastian said, "is the most honest sentence spoken at this ford all morning."

Behind them, Corvane laughed. It was a short, ugly sound, mostly blood-flecked coughing, but a laugh.

"Beautifully done, Montrose. You always did have the courier's gift — making delivery sound like virtue." He let the escort haul him upright; he winced, gathered himself, and produced from somewhere the last shred of his old poise, wearing it the way a ruined man wears his last clean shirt. "Enjoy your slow machinery. When it grinds to a halt — and it will, they always do — remember that I offered you the fast way, once, at a riverbank, and you chose the paperwork."

"I'll remember," Sebastian said. "That's rather the point of paperwork."

* * *

They crossed at midday.

The ferryman — a silent woman with forearms like cable, whose cottage Dain had occupied the night before and whose expression suggested she had heard every word through the wall and formed opinions — poled them over in two trips. On the far bank, the escort re-formed around their prisoner. Corvane limped between two soldiers, hands bound before him, head high, playing to an audience of reeds and grey water. Sebastian watched him go and felt — he searched himself carefully for hatred, the way one searches a pocket known to be empty — found none, and was glad, and found instead something colder and more lasting: responsibility. The trial would be long. The truth would leak and be disputed and partially buried and partially exhumed. It would be slow. It would be human. It would be, in other words, real.

Ilse fell into step beside him on the northern path, Elira bundled warm against her, the true satchel riding her hip. Dain walked ten paces back, unarmed now in a way that seemed to have rearranged his whole skeleton, watching the border recede behind them as if expecting it to follow.

"You know what happens next," Ilse said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Honors," Sebastian said. "Reinstatement. A ceremony, probably. Someone will want to pin something on me and shake my hand and call the Republic healed."

"And?"

"And I've seen what's under the bandages." Ahead, the road climbed into bare hills, and beyond the hills — a week's travel, perhaps less — lay the enclave, the tribunal's final session, the verdict, the whole bright cumbersome future. Elira stirred and made a small sound, and Sebastian reached over and rested two fingers against the top of her head, feeling the fine down of her hair through the wool. "She'll need documents eventually. Real ones. A name that belongs to no registry, a country that doesn't exist yet." He glanced at Ilse. "I'm going to need an atlas."

Ilse smiled — the rare one, the one with teeth in it. "I know an archivist who owes you a favor."

They walked on north, four figures and one infant on a road that belonged to no nation, and behind them the river kept crossing and recrossing the border, indifferent to every map ever drawn of it.
