## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The lamp had a stutter in it that night, a small hesitation between one breath of flame and the next, and Félicie Joubert had learned to work inside that hesitation the way other men learned to work inside grief — patiently, without complaint, because there was nothing else to be done. She trimmed the wick a quarter-turn, watched the light steady itself against the fog pressing at the window, and bent again over plate forty-one.

Forty-one plates finished would mean thirty-nine remained. No — forty finished, forty remained; she corrected herself with the small cold satisfaction of a woman who did not permit herself errors even in her own head. The atlas of the harbor was to run in eighty plates: soundings, channels, wharves, the great fan of moorings, the citadel with its walls drawn in section like the cross-section of a tooth. Perrin had completed fifty-two before his heart stopped him mid-stroke on the fifth of October, his pen trailing off across the paper in a line so faint it might have been an apology. She had found him like that the next morning, chin on chest, the pen still in his fingers, and her first thought — God forgive her, her first thought, before the grief, before the shouting for Madame Cadeau downstairs — had been *the plate is ruined.*

She thought of it now as she worked, because everything came back to that line. The unfinished stroke ran through the shallows off the Île des Pauvres, a hair's width of ink going nowhere, and she had spent two evenings deciding whether to scrape it away or leave it. In the end she had left it. There were things a chart ought to admit, even if charts were not in the habit of admitting anything.

Outside, the fog came up the harbor street the way it always did in November, thick as wet wool, swallowing the lamps along the quay until they were only smudges, suggestions, a rumor of light. Somewhere out in all of that gray sat the warships — hers and theirs and the Americans', lying at anchor like patient animals — and somewhere above all of it, in rooms she would never see, men were deciding whether the killing would stop this week or this month or not at all. The whole city was holding its breath. She could feel it in the streets by day: the way people stopped at the newspaper offices without going in, the way the church bells had been silent since Sunday, as though even the bells understood that whatever they announced next would have to matter.

Félicie did not think about any of it. That was the discipline.

She thought instead about depth soundings. Plate forty-one covered the eastern approaches, from the breakwater to the old powder magazine, and Perrin's field notes for it were a disgrace — figures jotted on the back of a laundry list, tide corrections done in pencil and half rubbed out, as if the man who could draw a lighthouse so finely you felt you could warm your hands at it could not be bothered to add two numbers correctly. She redid the arithmetic twice. She adjusted for the spring tide of the ninth of September, which she herself had measured, standing hip-deep in her brother's boots on the mudflats at four in the morning with a surveying rod and a lantern shuttered down to a coin of light, while the gulls screamed at her for trespassing on their cold kingdom. Nobody knew that. Nobody would ever know that. The plate would say *Perrin* in the corner, in the facsimile of his signature that she had practiced for three weeks until she could reproduce the odd loop of his *P* without thinking, and it would say *del.* beneath it, *delineavit*, drawn by, and the drawn-by would be a dead man, and the man would be a lie, and the lie was the only thing keeping the roof over the studio and the bread on the table and the atlas alive.

"Felix," she said aloud, to hear how it sat in the room. "Felix Joubert."

The name fit the way a borrowed coat fits — close enough at the shoulders if you didn't move too quickly. She had worn it now for fourteen months, since the week after the funeral, when the guild's assessor had come to inventory the studio and she had stood before him in Perrin's spare coat with her hair bound up under her collar and her voice dropped into the lower register she'd coaxed out of her throat by humming hymns with the piano lid closed. *I am his apprentice,* she had said. *Felix Joubert. He took me on two years ago; you'll find me in his books.* And the assessor, a soft man with a cold who clearly wished to be anywhere else, had looked at her — looked at her properly, for three full seconds, long enough that she had felt her pulse in her teeth — and then written *apprentice* in his ledger and gone away.

That was the thing about being invisible. It worked, mostly. But every time it worked she felt the three seconds again, the look that almost saw her, and she understood that she was not safe, only uncaught. There was a difference, and the difference was made of luck, and luck was not a substance you could chart.

She set her pen down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until green sparks swam.

* * *

The debts lived in a drawer of their own, second from the top, beneath the good erasers. She kept them the way Perrin had kept his — folded once, arranged by date, because order was the only form of prayer she still believed in. There was the paper merchant, Vidal, who was owed eleven months and had stopped writing polite notes and begun writing short ones. There was the ink maker. There was the coal, and the rent on the studio, which was due in nine days, and there was the loan against Perrin's tools that she had taken in September from a man named Brissac whose smile she did not trust and whose money she could not refuse. Two hundred and forty francs. At six percent. She had signed the note *F. Joubert* and her hand had not shaken, which frightened her more than shaking would have — how quickly the lying became ordinary, how smooth the borrowed name wore against the paper.

An apprentice could finish a master's atlas. That much was true under guild law; it happened, it was even considered honorable, the way a son finishes a father's house. But an apprentice had to be certified, examined, presented to the guild board as what he was, and what he was — the ledger said so, the assessor's soft hand had written it — was a young man of twenty-three with ink-stained fingers and no family worth mentioning. If the board summoned Felix Joubert to stand before them, if any one of the careful men of the Guild of Chartmakers and Hydrographers looked at her longer than courtesy required, then the atlas would be seized as fraudulent, the studio sealed, the plates impounded, and she would be lucky to leave the city ahead of the police. Impersonating a man was not a charming eccentricity in the winter of 1918. It was a crime, and lately the courts had time on their hands, what with all the deserters and the profiteers, and they liked variety.

So: eighty plates, thirty-nine to go after tonight — forty, she reminded herself, *forty*, and she caught the error and corrected it and hated that it had happened at all. Fatigue made liars of numbers, and numbers were the last honest thing she owned.

She rose to stretch and went to the window, because the window was the closest thing she had to company. The fog had thickened. Across the harbor the signal station on the point showed its light in ragged pulses, cut off and restored, cut off and restored, a lighthouse clearing its throat. She had drawn that station twice already — once on plate nine, once in detail on plate nineteen, its mast and its semaphore arms and the little stone cottage where the keepers brewed coffee that smelled like burnt rope. She knew its coordinates better than she knew her own birthday. Latitude and longitude, the exact angle of its light, the precise depth of water three cables east of its rocks: these she carried in her head the way other women carried the faces of their children.

History was happening out there tonight, beyond the fog, in rooms full of men whose names filled the newspapers. Armistice or no armistice; terms dictated or negotiated; the war ended with a signature or dragged on through another winter of convoys and torpedoes and telegrams folded into widows' doors. None of it would pass through this studio. None of it would pause over plate forty-one or trouble itself about the tide corrections of a dead cartographer and the apprentice who did not exist. History, Félicie had concluded sometime in her twentieth year, was a tide that visited certain shores and ignored others, and her family's shore had never once been on its itinerary. Her father had died a surveyor of mudflats. Perrin had died over a drawing. Émile had come back from the front with a limp and a silence and taken a clerk's desk in the customs house, where the largest event of his working day was a mislabeled crate of Algerian wine. The Jouberts were not the kind of people history happened to. They were the kind of people who drew accurate maps of places where it happened elsewhere.

She had found a strange comfort in that, once. She found less of it tonight.

* * *

At half past eleven she allowed herself the ritual: she unlocked the flat drawer at the bottom of the plan chest, the one with the false front she had fitted herself, and took out the field book. Not Perrin's — hers. A cheap canvas-bound notebook, water-stained, filled in her own round hand with the surveys she had made when the master's health began to fail and someone had to walk the shore with the rod and the sextant while he sat in the studio coughing and turning his sketches into marvels. For eighteen months she had been his legs, his eyes at ground level, his anonymous instrument, and he had known it, and she had known that he knew it, and neither of them had ever once said it aloud, because saying it aloud would have obliged one of them to do something about it, and doing something about it was impossible, and impossibility is easier to live with when nobody names it.

Except once. Three weeks before he died, packing up after a long afternoon on the breakwater, Perrin had stopped on the wet stones and looked at her — at her, not at the boy he saw on the street — with those pale eyes like weather glass, and said: *"You have a better eye for the mud than I ever had, boy."* And then, after a silence long enough for a wave to come and go: *"A map doesn't care whose hand drew it. Water finds its level. Truth does the same."* Then he had walked on, whistling something tuneless, and she had followed with the tripod under her arm and her heart going like a trapped bird.

She thought of that whenever the loneliness got its arms around her. Which was often. Which was now.

The studio at midnight was a country of gray shapes: the plan chest with its forty drawers, each drawer a plate sleeping under tissue; the big south window with its mullions fogged from within; Perrin's chair, which she did not sit in, ever, though she had oiled its leather and repaired its casters and resented it and loved it. On the wall hung the framed chart of the outer roads that Perrin had engraved at thirty, the work that made his name — every wavelet drawn individually, thousands of them, a sea frozen mid-gesture, patient forever. As an apprentice she had counted the wavelets once, out of boredom, and lost track past four hundred. Sometimes, in bad hours, she felt like one of them: part of a vast pattern, indistinguishable, beautiful only as part of the whole, never permitted to be the single wave.

She put the field book back and locked the false drawer and stood a moment with her palm flat against the cool wood of the chest, as if taking a pulse.

* * *

Plate forty-one wanted the border ornament finished — the compass rose in the lower corner, half-inked — and she saved it for last, always, because the roses were the only part of the work she did purely for pleasure, and pleasure was a ration like sugar, to be spent at the end of the day and not before. She mixed a fresh pool of the good sepia, tested it on scrap, and began.

The rose grew under her hand: sixteen points, the cardinal rays bold, the intercardinals fine as grass stems, the whole wheel turning on an invisible pin. Around it she laid the conventional scrollwork, and inside the scrollwork the little decorative flourishes Perrin had used for forty years — dolphins and anchors and, in the later charts, strange small substitutions of his own devising: a curlew here, a sprig of samphire there, a tiny rendered stone from some beach he had loved. His borders were famous for it. *Old man's whimsies,* the other masters called them, kindly enough, the way you speak of a colleague's harmless madness. Apprentices copied them faithfully without understanding them, and she had copied them too, flower by flower, bird by bird, thinking all the while that she was merely decorating emptiness.

Her pen slowed on the last flourish.

She looked at the border she had just completed on plate forty-one — the border she had completed following Perrin's own preparatory sketch, pinned to the easel now, curling with age — and she noticed, for the first time in fourteen months of copying his designs, that the little elements were not scattered. Or rather, that they were scattered the way stars are scattered, which is to say not at all. The curlew sat exactly where a particular rock sat on the corresponding chart. The samphire sprang from the margin at the bearing of the channel marker. The small stones marched in a sequence that matched — she counted, checked, recounted — the sequence of soundings along the eastern approach, the very figures she had spent the evening correcting, transcribed in ornament, hidden in beauty, published to the world in plain sight where no enemy and no fool would think to read them.

It was probably nothing. Old men repeated themselves. Cartographers put the land they loved into their margins the way sailors put tattooed women on their arms, and meaning was something the living imposed on the dead because the alternative was unbearable.

Probably nothing.

But her hands had gone cold around the pen, and the lamp stuttered again, and out in the fog the signal station pulsed its interrupted light — on, off, on, off — like something trying to tell her a message she couldn't yet read. Félicie set down the pen carefully, the way you set down a thing you may need to pick up quickly, and pulled Perrin's preparatory sketch closer to the flame.

There were marks on it she had never studied. Tiny annotations in the master's hand, in the corner, in a cipher of letters and figures that was either shorthand, or code, or — and the thought arrived with the weight of a body stepping onto thin ice —

Or a key.

Under the sketch, where it had rested against the easel's ledge all these months, lay a corner of stiff paper she had never noticed before: the edge of something tucked flat behind the easel's backing board, old and yellowed, addressed in Perrin's unmistakable hand.

Sealed. Never sent.

The fog pressed at the glass. The city held its breath. Félicie reached for the paper with fingers that no longer felt entirely like her own.
