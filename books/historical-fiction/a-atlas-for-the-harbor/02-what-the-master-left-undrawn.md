## 2. What the Master Left Undrawn

The guild's summons came on a Tuesday, folded into the slot beneath the shop door with the particular heaviness of paper that expects to be obeyed. *An inventory of the late Master Perrin's effects will be conducted under seal of the Guild of Hydrographers and Chartmakers, per Article Fourteen, in the presence of the estate's designated apprentice.* Designated apprentice. That was Felix Joubert — or rather, that was who Felix Joubert was supposed to be: a young man of twenty-three, lean as a drafting rule, with ink worked so deeply into the creases of his knuckles that no amount of lye soap had ever fully lifted it. In truth there was no young man at all. There was only Félicie, who had buried her name with her father's fortunes six years ago and put on Perrin's dead nephew's identity the way other people put on a coat against weather.

But the guild did not know that, and the letter did not know it either, and so she read it standing in the cold front room of the chart shop with fog pressing gray against the windows, and she thought only: *Article Fourteen. They will want everything listed. Everything counted. Everything accounted for.*

The inventory was set for Thursday. She had two days to make certain that nothing in the studio could betray what she was, and two days to grieve properly, which she had not yet managed, because grief is a luxury for people whose lives are not balanced on a knife's edge of discovery.

* * *

Perrin had been dead eleven days when the frost finally took the last of the flowers someone left on the doorstep — probably Madame Roux, though neither of them ever admitted to the small kindnesses they traded. Eleven days, and the studio above the shop still smelled like him: tobacco and linseed oil and the faint iron tang of the copper plates he never let anyone else touch. Felix climbed the stairs each morning out of habit, put the kettle on the spirit stove out of habit, laid out his apron out of habit, and then remembered, each time, with the same small fall of the stomach, that the chair by the south window would not turn when the light changed.

He had been sixty-eight and stubborn as sea ice, and he had died the way he had lived — at his desk, mid-line, a pen still loaded in the rest beside plate forty-one, the final sheet of the coastal atlas he had spent nineteen years building. The doctors said his heart. Felix suspected it was simply that he had finished waiting for the war to let him work in peace and had decided, in his efficient way, to stop spending fuel on the interval.

Now the atlas was hers. Not legally — legally it belonged to the guild until certification, and the guild belonged to men like Guildmaster Ozenne, who smiled the way bankers smile. But hers in every way that mattered: hers to finish, hers to correct, hers to carry through the checkpoints to the naval archive before the armistice was signed and the whole apparatus of wartime requisition came down on private workshops like a portcullis. Everyone in the city spoke of the armistice as if it were weather, something gathering beyond the horizon that would arrive regardless of what any of them did. Delegations were traveling. Terms were being drafted in rooms Felix would never see. History, she told herself firmly, happened elsewhere — in salons and ministries and the smoking rooms of admiralties — and her business was only paper, ink, and the honest geometry of a coastline that did not care who governed it.

She repeated this to herself often enough that it had begun to sound true.

Thursday morning arrived wrapped in fog so thick that the harbor bells sounded muffled, as though the sea itself were holding its tongue. The guild sent a clerk named Mabire — a narrow man with a ledger chained to his wrist like a prisoner's ball, who smelled of camphor and disapproval. He set up at Perrin's worktable without asking permission, spread his forms, and began reciting categories from memory: instruments, plates, papers, books of reference, finished stock, unfinished stock, correspondence.

"Correspondence," Mabire said again, tapping the form. "The guild requires all letters and documents of a personal nature to be catalogued separately. Family papers especially. You'll box them for review."

Felix felt something tighten behind her ribs and kept her face doing what faces did best, which was nothing at all. "Master Perrin kept almost no personal correspondence," she said. "He distrusted the post. Said a letter was just a rumor with better handwriting."

Mabire looked up. Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly; appraisal. The look of a man pricing a thing. "Then this should be quick."

It was quick, mostly. The instruments were easy: four brass dividers, the beam compass, the pantograph, Perrin's silver parallel rules worn smooth as river stones by forty years of his thumb. The plates went into their press-crate in order, one through forty-one, forty-one still bearing the ghost of its unfinished border. Felix narrated the inventory aloud in a voice pitched carefully low and even, and Mabire scratched his pen and asked few questions, and the fog outside thickened until noon came as a dim brightening rather than a light.

It was the charts that undid her.

They found them in the flat drawer under the plan chest, where Perrin kept the old surveys — the ones superseded, corrected, withdrawn. Mabire wanted them bundled and valued by the lot. "Old sheets fetch little," he said, "but the vellum can be scraped and reused, and the guild wastes nothing." So Felix carried them, stack by stack, to the sorting table by the window, and began going through them one at a time as the clerk demanded, checking each against the registry numbers stamped in the margins.

The fourth chart from the bottom was a survey of the eastern approaches dated 1909 — old work, drawn in Perrin's early manner, before his lines grew economical, back when he still decorated his compass roses like a man in love with ornament. Felix knew the sheet. She had traced it as an exercise her first winter in the studio, when she was still learning to hold her hand steady enough to lie about being an apprentice. But when she lifted it, the paper did not behave as paper should. It was stiff in the wrong places. Heavier. As though something lay between the chart and its linen backing that had never been part of the drawing.

She set it down. She picked it up again. Through the surface she could feel a rectangular ridge near the lower left corner, where the backing cloth met the margin — sewn in, she realized, with stitches so fine they vanished into the hatching of the coastal shading. Someone had wanted this hidden by someone who understood how eyes travel across a map. Eyes go to the coast, the soundings, the rose. Nobody reads a hemline.

"Problem?" Mabire said, without looking up.

"No," Felix said. "A repair. Old linen patch." Her voice did not shake, and she noted distantly, as from another room, that this was the most frightening thing about it — how well trained her body had become at treason's neighbor, which was concealment.

She turned the chart over. The backing was heavy canvas, and along one edge, half-hidden beneath a strip of archival paste, the stitching ran: tiny, regular, patient. Whoever had done it had used thread matched to the cloth. It would have taken time. It would have taken calm. She thought of Perrin bent over this table at night, the lamp turned low, sewing a secret into the body of an old map as neatly as a surgeon closing a wound, and something in her chest went cold and stayed cold.

She cut the stitches with the tip of her smallest divider. The blade slid under the first knot and the cloth gave up its secret in three motions, and then the thing was lying on the table among the brass instruments and the dust of scraped vellum, and there was no undoing the knowing of it.

A letter. Sealed. The wax unbroken, dark green, stamped with the anchor-and-rule that Perrin had used for his few formal certificates. The paper was good rag stock, yellowed slightly at the edges, folded with the crisp precision of a man who had folded ten thousand charts and could not help folding everything else the same way. Across the face, in the hand she knew better than her own — the strong downstrokes, the numerals with their flat-topped sevens — was written a single line:

*For the Archive, if I do not live to carry it myself.*

Not a name. No addressee. Just that — an instruction aimed past the present, past his death, straight at her, since she was the one left holding his tools.

"What is the date on that sheet?" Mabire asked, still writing. "Nineteen-oh-nine? Then mark it superseded, value nil, bundle with the rest—"

"It's a letter," Felix said. "Sewn into the backing. Perrin's hand, sealed. It falls under correspondence."

That got his attention. The pen stopped. Mabire came around the table with his camphor smell and his careful hands, and for a long moment he looked down at the sealed letter the way a man looks at a coin he suspects is clipped. "Unsent," he observed.

"Apparently."

"To whom?"

"There's no addressee. Only an instruction."

Mabire's finger hovered over the wax — the green wax, the anchor-and-rule — without touching it. "Article Fourteen requires personal documents to be boxed for guild review," he said slowly. "But this—" and here he paused, choosing his words with visible care, "—this may not be personal. If it concerns the atlas, or the trade, or matters touching the archive, then it is a professional document, and professional documents pass to the guildmaster directly. Ozenne will want to see it."

Every instinct Felix owned stood up at once, the way a flock of birds goes up off a mudflat. *Ozenne will want to see it.* Of course he would. Ozenne collected leverage the way other men collected debts, and here was a sealed letter from a dead master, hidden in the lining of an old chart, addressed to no one — a letter a careful man had thought worth sewing into canvas while the war ate the world. Whatever it was, Ozenne would find a use for it. That was the kind of man he was. That was the kind of institution the guild had become, in wartime, under men like him.

"The instruction says the Archive," Felix said. "Not the guild. If it's meant for the naval archive, opening it elsewhere would be—" she reached for the word and chose the largest one available, "—irregular."

Mabire's eyes came up and rested on her face for longer than they needed to. She had the sensation, familiar now but never comfortable, of being weighed by someone who suspected the scale was rigged. Apprentices did not lecture clerks on protocol. Apprentices did not have opinions about irregularity. She watched him decide, visibly, to file the moment away rather than spend it.

"You're right, of course," he said mildly, which was worse than argument. "Box it. Seal the box. I'll note it as *found documentation, disposition pending.* The guildmaster can rule on it Monday." He returned to his ledger, and the pen resumed its dry scratching, and Felix understood that she had perhaps four days — until Monday, until Ozenne — before the letter stopped being hers to guard and became the guild's to consume.

* * *

They finished at dusk. Mabire chained his ledger, bowed with the precise courtesy of a man who has noticed everything and reported none of it yet, and let himself out into the fog. Felix bolted the door behind him and stood a while in the darkening shop, listening to the harbor bells count five in their muffled way, and then she took the letter upstairs to the studio, because it seemed wrong to leave it below among the bundled charts like contraband, and wrong also to leave it anywhere she could not watch.

She lit the spirit stove. She did not light the lamps. The room settled into that blue hour where the fog outside became the wall and the wall became the fog, and the kettle ticked as it warmed, and the letter lay on the drafting table under her spread fingers, pale as a sounding taken over deep water.

*For the Archive, if I do not live to carry it myself.*

There were a great many things it could be. She made herself list them, because listing was what she did, what Perrin had taught her — *when the chart frightens you, sound it line by line, and the fear becomes data.* It could be a debt. It could be a quarrel with the guild, an accusation against Ozenne himself. It could be a love letter from some decade she had never known him through, though the seal argued against it; men did not hide love under an institutional stamp. It could be instructions for plate forty-one's border, some technical key she would need. It could be nothing. Old men hid odd things.

Or it could be the reason he had been careful all these years in ways that had once seemed like temperament and now, in the cold blue light, seemed like practice.

Here was the arithmetic she could not escape, and it had nothing to do with the letter's contents. She was a woman wearing a dead boy's name in a city under military law, holding an uncertified atlas worth more than the shop, awaiting the inspection of a guildmaster who smiled too readily. Any paper out of place was a threat. A sealed letter with no addressee, discovered in her possession, opened by her hand without authority — that was not a threat, that was a confession looking for a crime. If she broke the seal and the letter was innocent, she had committed a small impropriety a clerk had already half-witnessed. If she broke the seal and it was not innocent—

If it was not innocent, then knowing its contents made her a party to whatever it held. Ignorance was a country she could still claim to live in. Knowledge had borders, and crossing them was not reversible. She had built her whole life on that principle, in reverse: she knew precisely which crossings could not be undone, because she had crossed several already and could feel the water at her back.

The kettle sighed. Felix rose, took the letter to the stove, and held it over the grate.

It would be simple. Paper burns; she had burned drafts before, misdrawn plates, a whole ruined month of soundings once when the tide tables proved corrupted. Fire was the oldest corrector. She need only let the corner touch the coals, and then there would be no letter, no Monday deadline, no Ozenne, no contents to make her complicit. Perrin was dead; the dead did not audit their executors. She could tell the guild the backing had been patched against damp, nothing more, and Mabire would write *value nil* and the world would close over the whole business like water over a dropped stone. She would finish the atlas. She would deliver it. She would keep her name buried and her neck unkicked and history would continue happening elsewhere, to other people, and someday the war would end and maybe, afterward, in some slower year, she might even—

Her hand did not move. That was the thing she would remember afterward: not courage, not temptation, but the simple mechanical failure of her own hand, hanging over the grate, unwilling. Because the letter was addressed, in however oblique a fashion, to her. *If I do not live to carry it myself.* He had died at his desk eleven days ago with his pen loaded, and whether he had meant the words for a future he imagined or merely for whoever came after — and who else came after? who else had he got? — the instruction had landed, with the terrible flat accuracy of all his lines, on her. Burning it unread was not caution. It was desertion. And she had spent six years learning the difference between hiding and deserting, and it was a difference she could not afford to blur, because the day she blurred it, the disguise won and Félicie lost, and then there would be nothing left inside the coat worth keeping warm.

Besides — and this was the smaller, colder voice, the one that sounded, she realized with a jolt, exactly like Perrin — besides, a mapmaker does not burn an unread chart. A mapmaker knows that the blank space is always more dangerous than the marked one. Every wreck in every archive began with somebody deciding the water was probably fine.

She took the letter back to the table. Her heart was going hard, harder than the situation seemed to warrant, and she pressed her palm flat against the wood until it steadied.

Monday. She had until Monday. Three days in which to decide whether to open it herself, in secret, and bear whatever knowledge came out of the seal — or surrender it unbroken to Ozenne, and with it whatever power it contained, and trust a smiling man to use it gently. Neither road was safe. One of them, she suspected, was not survivable.

The fog pressed at the windows. Down in the street a newspaper boy was calling the evening editions, his voice thinned by the weather to something barely human, and Felix caught only fragments — *delegation... terms... any day now...* — the armistice coming on like weather after all, indifferent to her, indifferent to everything, arriving whether the harbor was ready or not.

She turned the letter over one more time. The green wax caught the last of the light, and for a moment she thought she saw, in the anchor-and-rule stamp, some small irregularity — a doubled stroke, a hesitation in the rule — as though the seal itself had been pressed twice, or pressed by a hand that shook. But the light was failing, and she could not be sure.

She locked it in the flat drawer under the plan chest, in the very place it had spent however many years hidden, and she sat down at Perrin's desk, in Perrin's chair, facing the window that would not need turning toward any light now, and she began to work on plate forty-one because work was the only prayer she knew.

Three days. And underneath the steady scratch of her pen, under the tick of the kettle and the muffled bells, one thought kept surfacing like a body in water:

*Whatever he sewed into that canvas, he thought someone might kill him for it.*

And now everyone who might want it killed-for knew the apprentice had found it first.
