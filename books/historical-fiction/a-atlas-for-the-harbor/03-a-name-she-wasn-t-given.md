## 3. A Name She Wasn't Given

The letter had sat on Perrin's drafting table all night, and by morning Felix had not moved it.

She had placed it there deliberately — that was the strange part, the part she kept turning over like a coin of uncertain mint. She could have left it in the drawer where she'd found it, sewn into the backing of the old chart of the Île du Large. She could have burned it in the stove and swept the ash among the charcoal dust where no one would know the difference. Instead she had carried it upstairs through the cold studio with both hands, the way one carries something that might still be breathing, and set it down beneath the north window where the light was honestest, and then she had gone to bed and lain awake listening to the foghorns call to each other like lost animals.

Now it was morning, or what passed for morning in this harbor city in the last winter of the war — a gray seepage of light through fog so thick the masts along the quay appeared only as smudged verticals, suggestions of a world rather than the world itself. The stove had gone out in the night. Her breath made a small ghost in front of her face as she crossed the studio floor, boards complaining under her boots the way they always did, the way Perrin's had before her, and she thought — as she had thought a thousand times in three years, and never once without flinching — *his ghosts are my ghosts now.*

She sat down at the table. The letter waited.

It was sealed, though the wax had cracked along one edge in its long imprisonment inside the chart's linen backing, and through that crack she could see the faintest fold of paper within, like a tongue pressed against teeth. His seal — the blunt P in a circle he used for everything, invoices and guild correspondence and once, memorably, a note excusing her from lessons when she had wept over a misprinted plate. She remembered his thumb pressing that seal down while she stood in this same room with wet eyes, and how he had said, without looking up, *A mapmaker's first duty is to the truth of the terrain, Felix, but his second is mercy.* She had thought he meant the excuse note. She had been a girl then, and stupid in the way girls who are pretending to be boys must be stupid carefully.

*Read it,* said the part of her that had carried it upstairs.

*Burn it,* said the other part, the larger part, the part that had learned to survive by keeping its eyes on the work and its back to the wall.

She got up instead and relit the stove. This took time — kindling, the blackened kettle, water from the jug that had skinned over with cold — and she let it take time, because she understood herself well enough by now to know that she was building a rampart of small tasks between herself and the thing on the table. She warmed her hands. She drank tea that tasted of iron and the harbor air. She looked at plate forty-one propped against the easel where she'd left it, the final plate, the headland and the light-station rendered in her own careful hand, and she thought: *I am nearly done. I have nearly done what he asked. Whatever is in that letter belongs to a dead man and a dead argument, and the war will end this week or next regardless of what Perrin scribbled in the dark, and my duty is to the atlas, only to the atlas—*

But duty, she had already begun to learn, is a word men use for the things they want from you, and a word you learn to use yourself for the things you cannot admit you want.

She returned to the table. She picked up the letter. The wax crack widened under her thumbnail with a sound like a knuckle popping, and then the flap came free, and she unfolded the single sheet inside, and she read.

* * *

Perrin's hand, when he wrote quickly, slanted hard to the right, as if the words were trying to run off the page ahead of him. These words were running. The ink was blotched in places where he had pressed too long between strokes, thinking, and twice there were strikethroughs so violent the pen had torn the paper. He had written it fast, and he had written it frightened, and three years later his fear came off the page like heat off a stove-iron.

*To whoever finishes the atlas — which God grant is no one but me —*

Felix stopped there. Read it again. *Which God grant is no one but me.* He had hoped she would never read this. He had sewn it into the chart the way a man buries a thing he cannot bring himself to burn, and the address at the top — her name, her working name, *Felix Joubert, apprentice* — had been added later, in calmer ink, in the measured hand he used for plate labels. So: written in terror, addressed in resignation. He had known he might die carrying it, and he had decided, at the last, that she should carry it after him.

She read on.

*There is an understanding among certain families of this harbor, and I write it here because I have discovered it and cannot unknow it, and because if it succeeds every soul in this city will pay for the pride of a few. They mean to strike the signal station on the Môle headland — wires cut, lamps broken, and worse, for they speak of powder — in the days before the armistice is signed, so that the delegation's orders arrive late or never, so that the signing stalls, so that the war breathes another week, another month, whatever their masters in the capital require of them. They call it patriotism. I have heard it called that to its face, in a cellar, through a wall I should never have been standing beside.*

Three names followed. Not initials, not hints. Full names, written in that slanting, fleeing hand, and the third one—

The third one was *Joubert.*

The room did not go dark. That was the strange thing. She had always imagined that catastrophe arrived like a lamp being snuffed, and instead it arrived like this: the fog-light continuing at the window, the kettle ticking as it cooled, the smell of char and linseed oil unchanged, the whole ordinary apparatus of the world refusing to notice that the floor had opened beneath her chair. She gripped the table's edge. The wood was real. The letter was real. Her name — not her name, her father's name, her brother's name, the name she wore like a borrowed coat even now — sat on the page in Perrin's handwriting, and below it, in a different ink, someone else had signed.

She turned the sheet over. There was a second document folded with the first, smaller, formal, the kind of paper men prepared when they wanted a thing to look like law. A pledge, it called itself. An undertaking of the undersigned to hold in confidence the purpose of the association until such time as the association's object was achieved, amen, so help them, and at the bottom four signatures, four names in four different hands, and Émile's was the second from the top.

Émile. Who had held her hand at their father's funeral and promised nothing, because promising was not something their family did, but who had come afterward to the shop and eaten bread and cheese at this very table and told her, awkwardly, tenderly, that she should leave the city, that a war's ending was no time to be whatever she was in a harbor full of soldiers. Émile, who signed his letters *your brother, E.*, as if she might forget. Émile, whose signature here was steady, unhurried, a man signing a ledger or a lease.

He had known. All this time — through the funeral, through the bread and cheese, through every stilted monthly letter — he had known what their father's name was attached to, and he had let her walk through the fog to Perrin's studio every morning believing the world was the world, believing history happened elsewhere, in the capital, at the front, anywhere but here among the herring-barrels and the rope-walks.

And her father. Their father. Dead two years before Perrin, dead before any of this could be asked of him, and yet here his name was, conscripted posthumously into a plot to blow the lamps out of the sky above the harbor. Had he signed it living? Was his hand somewhere in some other cellar, on some other pledge? Or had these men simply taken the family name the way they took everything else, as property, as leverage, as a flag to hang over their enterprise? She did not know. That was the worst of it, at first — not the knowing but the not-knowing, the way the whole architecture of her childhood tilted and refused to settle. Her father teaching her bearings on the breakwater. Her father's instruments, which she kept wrapped in flannel like relics. Her father, possibly, in a cellar with powder-talk.

*They speak of powder.* She read the sentence again and felt the true weight of it arrive, slow, like water finding a crack. Not wires merely. Not a delay, not a technical obstruction, not patriotic mischief. Powder. The signal station on the Môle — she knew it the way she knew everything on this coast, stroke by stroke, contour by contour; she had drawn it twice, once for the atlas and once for the guild examination, its lamp-room and its wire-shed and the zigzag path up from the water. It was manned. Four men and a boy, rotation of shifts, she had noted the watch-house in her survey marginalia. If powder went off on the Môle headland in these crowded, coal-smoked, armistice-feverish weeks—

She put the letter down. Her hands were not shaking, which surprised her; she observed their steadiness from a great distance, as though they belonged to someone else, some other apprentice in some other fog-bound city. Then they began to shake anyway, all at once, and she pressed them flat against the table and watched the fog brighten by fractions at the window while her heart performed its stupid animal work.

* * *

She did not burn it.

She wanted to describe, afterward, a period of deliberation — scales, arguments, the measured weighing of a careful mind — but the truth was simpler and less flattering. She sat with the letter near the stove for the better part of an hour, and the fire was hot and the paper was dry, and it would have taken ten seconds. Ten seconds and her brother's name would exist nowhere in her keeping. Ten seconds and she could return to plate forty-one and finish the atlas and deliver it and be, forever, only Perrin's boy, a footnote in the guild register, safe.

But Perrin had addressed it to her.

Not to the guild. Not to the authorities, though God knew he'd had time to send it to them and hadn't — why hadn't he? Because he feared them, perhaps; because the families named were woven through the customhouse and the guild and half the municipal offices of this city; because a mapmaker alone against three old harbor dynasties was a man standing on a sandbar at low tide. Or because he had died too suddenly, his heart giving out at the drafting table with a pen still in his hand, and the letter had simply outrun him. She would never know which. She only knew that he had sewn it into the chart instead of feeding it to this same stove, and that at the end, in the calm hand, he had written her name — the name she had chosen, the only name that was truly hers — as if to say: *I could not carry this and finish the atlas, so you will do both, and may God forgive me for the weight.*

Burning it would not unname her brother. Burning it would not empty the cellars of powder. Burning it would only make her, like everyone else in this story, a person who knew and looked away.

She thought of Madame Roux's son, then, for no reason she could name — a boy she'd seen at the quayside in October, home on leave, laughing at gulls. She thought of the four men and the boy at the signal station. She thought of the armistice delegates somewhere across the lines, moving toward a table she could not picture, and of the whole exhausted continent leaning toward that table the way sailors lean toward landfall.

Then she did something practical, because practicality was the only form of courage she had ever reliably possessed. She took a fresh sheet of paper and copied the letter out entire, in her own hand, including the pledge and the signatures — copying was second nature to her, she could reproduce a chart margin to margin without thinking, and her copy was exact, down to Perrin's blots and torn strikethroughs. The original she folded small and sewed into the lining of her coat, beneath the left arm, with waxed thread and a sailmaker's needle, working by feel because her fingers knew stitches better than her eyes did. When it was done she pressed her palm flat against the lump of it, this hard little seed riding against her ribs, and found that it hurt to touch, and resolved not to touch it again.

She fed Perrin's original letter to the stove — his actual paper, his actual ink — and watched the slanting words curl and go brown and then black, and *Joubert* was the last legible thing before the flame took it, and she felt no cleaner for it. Ash among ash. She raked the stove and went back to the table.

* * *

The rest of the day she worked, because the atlas demanded it and because work was the raft she had always clung to. She inked the light-station on plate forty-one. She corrected the soundings along the eastern channel from Perrin's older survey notes. She ground ink and washed brushes and trimmed plate edges square, and all the while the letter rode under her arm like a second heartbeat, and all the while her mind kept returning to the same unbearable coordinates.

Émile. Émile at the door of her memory, eating bread at this table. Émile saying *leave the city, little one, it's no place for you when the peace comes.* Had that been warning, or had it been — she made herself consider it — had it been clearing the ground? Did he know what Perrin had? Did they know? Was the raid on the chart shop coming the way weather comes, visible far off across the water?

No. Stop. She set down the pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the dark bloomed red. One thing at a time, the way one plots a course: fix your position first, then your heading, then your speed. Her position: she held evidence naming three families in a plot to destroy the harbor's signal station and strangle the armistice in its cradle. Her heading: unknown. Her speed: she was, she realized, sitting perfectly still in a freezing studio while the shortest days of the year drained away like water from a punctured boat.

There was also this, and she turned it over slowly, the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath: the plot needed timing. Striking the station mattered only in the days before the signing — after, it would be mere murder, pointless and punished. Which meant she had a clock, and the clock was the news itself, and the news was coming soon. Days. Perhaps hours. Every foghorn blast from the harbor was a tick.

She looked at the letter's ashes in the grate. She looked at her copy riding invisibly under her own arm. She looked at the door of the studio, plain pine, painted gray, beyond which lay the stairs and the street and the fog and the city, and somewhere out in all of it, her brother, walking around in the world with a steady hand and a signed name.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would decide whether the letter went into the fire of public record or stayed buried in cloth and thread. Tomorrow she would find Émile and look at his face while she asked him, and whatever she saw there would tell her more than any document. Tomorrow—

But tomorrow, as it happened, came early, and knocked twice, softly, the way family knocks.

Through the frosted pane of the studio door she saw the shape of a tall man in a long coat, hatless despite the cold, waiting with his hands clasped behind his back in the attitude of a man rehearsing something. She knew the shoulders before she knew anything else. She had known those shoulders since before she could read a chart.

Her hand went, without instruction, to the seam beneath her left arm.

"Coming," she called, in the low voice she had worn for three years, and she crossed the floor to open the door, feeling the floor give slightly, wonderfully, horribly, beneath her certainty — as if the whole solid world of her life had been drawn on paper after all, and someone had just lifted the corner.
