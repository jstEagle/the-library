## 4. The Guild of Careful Men

Guildmaster Aurélien Ozenne had spent thirty-one years learning that the truth, like ink, behaved differently depending on the paper it landed upon. On good vellum it held. On cheap stock it bled, feathered, became something other than what the hand had intended. The question before the Harmonied Guild of Chartists and Hydrographers this grey December evening was, in essence, a question of paper: whether the harbor atlas — the great work of the late Master Perrin, forty-one plates of coastal survey begun before the war and now demanded by the naval archive before the armistice signatures dried — could be certified as finished by an apprentice of four months' standing, or whether it must be declared incomplete, and thereby worthless.

Ozenne knew the answer already. He had known it since the first week of September, when Perrin's boy had come to the guild hall to report the master's death, standing very straight in a coat too large at the shoulders, hat brim low, voice pitched with such deliberate evenness that it was less a voice than a construction. A voice built plank by plank, like a jetty over deep water.

He had not looked down then. Courtesy, his mother had taught him, was the art of not looking down when looking down would cost you something. But he had *noticed*. And noticing, for a man in his position, was a form of capital, and capital was to be banked, not spent.

"Master Ozenne." Corbeau, the senior examiner, was speaking, tapping the sample plate they had all been passed. "Plate nineteen. The soundings off the Customhouse reach. You will observe the hand."

Ozenne observed it. The plate lay in the lamplight like a thing alive: the coastline rendered in that fine trembling line Perrin had made his signature, the depths marked in numerals so regular they might have been set by machine, the decorative border worked with compass roses and sea-monsters in the old manner. It was beautiful. It was also, to eyes trained by thirty years of examination boards, not entirely Perrin's. The lettering leaned a half-degree too far into its italic. The rose-petals of the compass were drawn with a delicacy Perrin had never possessed — a delicacy, Ozenne thought privately, that Perrin's dead apprentice-father Joubert *had* possessed, God rest him, before drink and debt had carried him off.

"The hand is close," Corbeau said. "Close is not identical."

"The hand is better," said Madame Lisle from the far end of the table, and twelve heads turned, because Madame Lisle rarely spoke and never interrupted. She was the guild's widow-in-interest, holder of her husband's seat these eleven years, and she wielded her silence like a billhook. "I will say it plainly since no one else will. The soundings on plate nineteen are more accurate than anything Perrin produced in the last three years of his life. His later work drifted. Grief does that. This plate does not drift. This plate was done by someone who walked the foreshore at low tide with a lead line and counted fathoms on their fingers."

"By someone," Corbeau repeated. "Yes. That is precisely the difficulty. Who?"

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside the tall windows the fog lay against the glass like a living thing trying to get warm. Somewhere beyond it the harbor bells were sounding through the murk, two strokes, three, marking the tide, marking time, marking the slow approach of whatever history was being negotiated in distant rooms by men whose names would outlive everyone sitting at this table.

"Perrin's boy finished the atlas," said Vidal, the treasurer, who smelled money the way sharks smell blood. "The archive wants it by the signing. If we certify it, the guild is paid, the commission is honored, and the name of this house stands on the most important cartographic work produced in this port in a generation. If we do not certify it, we return the advance, we confess ourselves unable to fulfill our obligations in wartime, and the archive commissions elsewhere. Gentlemen — Madame — I fail to see where the debate lives."

"The debate," Corbeau said, "lives in the word *certify*. Certification is not a receipt. Certification is the guild saying: this work is what it claims to be, made by whom it claims to be made by, to the standard we put our seal against. We would be certifying an atlas whose final — how many plates? Twelve? Fourteen? — were drawn by a boy of four months' apprenticeship. Four months. I served six years before my examination. We all did."

"War compresses things," Vidal said.

"War compresses things," Corbeau agreed, "and men under compression show their true grain. Which brings me to the other matter, which I have been asked — discreetly — to raise." He paused, arranging his papers with the fussiness of a man buying courage by the minute. "There are questions. About the boy himself."

Ozenne let his face settle into its customary expression of benign attention. He had worn that expression through two examinations of fraudulent surveys, one embezzlement, and a duel fought — absurdly — over the correct rendering of a sandbar. He could wear it through this.

"Questions," he said mildly.

"About the apprenticeship itself. It was entered without examination fee. Waived, on your authority, Master Ozenne, on grounds of the father's service to the guild. The boy arrived from nowhere anyone can name. No mother present at the funeral. No family came to claim mourning. He keeps no lodgings registered with the parish clerk — the shop's upper floor, apparently. He has been seen—" Corbeau consulted his paper again, though Ozenne suspected he knew the contents by heart, "—at the fish market. At the chandler's. Buying lamp oil, rope, provisions. Alone. Always alone. And there is a woman in the Rue des Salines who tells anyone who will buy her wine that Perrin's apprentice is no more a boy than she is."

Silence, of the particular quality that follows a stone dropped into still water. Ozenne counted the ripples: Vidal calculating; Lisle unmoving; young Berthier blushing furiously at the mention of women and anatomy in the same breath; Corbeau watching the Guildmaster to see which way the wind blew from him.

"You have spoken to this woman?" Ozenne asked.

"I have heard of her."

"Heard. Wine and hearsay." Ozenne folded his hands on the table. They were fine hands, well-kept, the hands of a man who had not drawn a chart in twenty years but remembered exactly what it cost. "Let me tell you what I know, since we are being plain tonight, and plainness is a courtesy I extend reluctantly and only among friends.

"I know that old Joubert — Félix Joubert, the surveyor — pulled this guild's reputation out of the mud twenty-five years ago when every chart we stamped was accused of killing ships. I know that when he drank himself into the churchyard, Auguste Perrin took in his children because Auguste Perrin owed Joubert a debt no ledger ever recorded properly. I know that Perrin wrote to me three years ago — I keep his letters, gentlemen, I keep everyone's letters — asking that if anything happened to him, the boy be taken on without fee, without examination, without question, for reasons he did not choose to give. I know that when the boy came here in September, thin as a lath and white as paper, with his master barely cold, I saw a young person in grief carrying a weight I did not ask them to describe. And I waived the fee. That is all I know, and all I required to know."

It was a masterpiece, Ozenne reflected, of saying nothing whatsoever while appearing to say everything. Every sentence true. None of them the truth.

"And the wine-soaked woman of the Rue des Salines," Madame Lisle said, into the pause, "may have seen what she says she saw. Or may have seen a slight young man and wanted a story worth her wine. In my experience the two are frequently confused." She gathered her knitting from beside her chair. "I move that the atlas be certified. The guild examines the completed work as a whole — the whole, Master Corbeau, not the parts — and stamps it if it meets standard. Which it will."

"The whole," Corbeau said slowly, "is a convenient instrument. One examines a wall, one does not interrogate every brick."

"One does," said Ozenne, "if one wishes to find excuses to tear it down. Are we in the business of tearing down walls, Corbeau, or keeping the rain off the people inside them?"

Nobody answered that. It was not, strictly, answerable.

* * *

They voted at nine o'clock. Certification, conditional on inspection of the completed atlas at month's end. Nine for, three abstaining, Corbeau's raised hand among the abstentions — a careful man's careful gesture, dissent banked rather than spent. Ozenne watched him leave with the others, chapeau brushed, gloves on, a small neat man sealing a small resentment into an envelope he would open later at the most damaging hour.

Madame Lisle lingered. She wound her scarf twice around her throat and looked at Ozenne with eyes that had watched her own husband die of politeness.

"You knew already," she said. Not a question.

Ozenne poured the last of the wine into two glasses instead of answering. She took hers.

"Twenty-five years ago," he said, "Félix Joubert stood in this room, in front of that fire, and told the guild that our charts were wrong. Told us to our faces, a junior surveyor with chalk on his cuffs, that we had been selling depth-soundings ten years stale and ships were dying of it. Half the table wanted him expelled for insolence. I was junior clerk then. I fetched him the wine myself." He turned the glass in his fingers. "His daughter would be — what — thirty-one now? Thirty-two?"

"Is that a question, Master Ozenne?"

"It is a number, Madame. Numbers are my trade." He drank. "Perrin's boy draws the rose-compass petals exactly as Félix Joubert drew them. A mannerism. A family habit of the hand. Like a way of walking, or a laugh."

"Then you have known since September."

"I have suspected since September. Knowing requires evidence, and evidence is a thing one gathers only when one intends to use it." He met her eyes. "I do not yet intend to use it."

"But you intend to intend," Lisle said, and drained her glass, and left.

* * *

Alone, Ozenne stood at the tall window with the last of the lamp burning behind him, and watched the fog work at the city. It came up off the water in the evenings now, filling the streets between the lampposts like slow milk, muffling the trams, making the harbor invisible and therefore infinite. Somewhere in that whiteness a girl was working late over a drafting table, wearing her dead brother's coat and her dead father's trade, holding up a roof with her bare hands and calling herself by a name that wasn't hers so that the roof would hold.

He did not pity her. Pity was cheap currency and he did not deal in it. But he acknowledged — privately, in the ledger he kept behind his face — a certain professional admiration. To forge a chart took skill. To forge oneself, daily, hourly, in front of fishwives and gendarmes and guild examiners, took nerve of a quality the guild had no medal for.

And yet.

The trouble with a secret of that size was not that it might be discovered. Everything was discovered, eventually; that was merely weather. The trouble was what the secret was *worth*, and to whom, and when. An armistice was coming — everyone felt it now the way one feels frost through a window — and when treaties were signed, names became either shields or targets, and families rose or drowned by the inch of paperwork they happened to be standing on. A guildmaster who held certain knowledge held leverage. Leverage, in the coming peace, might buy what thirty-one years of careful stewardship had not: a charter amendment, a seat for the guild on the new harbor commission, permanence. He was sixty-two years old. He had kept the rain off long enough. He would like, before the end, to own some of the roof.

There was also, he admitted, the matter of danger. A person living inside a forgery was a person who could be broken, and whoever broke her would learn that Ozenne had known and said nothing. Silence, unwisely invested, could bankrupt a man faster than speech.

So: he would watch. He would wait for the atlas to arrive, magnificent and incriminating, and he would receive it with full courtesy, and he would make sure the girl understood — gently, courteously, with every kindness short of honesty — exactly how much her smooth passage through the world depended upon the goodwill of the Guild of Careful Men.

Courtesy, after all, was the art of not looking down. It was not the art of not knowing what lay below.

He pinched out the lamp and went down through the dark hall, past the framed masters on the walls, past the great seal in its cabinet, out into the fog. Behind him the guild house settled into silence, and across the sleeping city, in a studio above a chart shop on the Rue des Salines, a lamp was still burning — and Ozenne noted, as he noted everything, that it burned later each night, as though someone were racing a tide only she could hear.
