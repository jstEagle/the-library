## 6. Émile at the Door

The fog came off the harbor in the late afternoon the way it always did in November, sliding up the rue des Salines in a grey tongue, tasting doorways, dimming lamps before they were lit. Émile Joubert stood across the street from his sister's shop for eleven minutes by his father's old watch, and in those eleven minutes he rehearsed four different openings and discarded all of them.

He had not been inside Perrin's studio since the funeral. He had not been inside since before that either — not since the night in September when three men had stood around a candle in the cellar of their childhood home and he had signed his name beneath his father's photograph, and the ink had seemed to go on drying forever, a small black wound on the page that never quite closed. He thought about that ink now as he crossed the street. He thought about how everything in this city was written down somewhere: manifests, ledgers, charts, pledges. The harbor kept accounts the way the sea kept bones.

The shop window was dark, but behind it a lamp moved. He could see her shadow on the frosted glass — narrow shoulders bent over the drafting table, the long arm sweeping, pausing, sweeping again. Even as a silhouette she drew the way their mother had kneaded bread: with her whole body, without hurry, as though the work would wait because it knew her.

*Félix*, they called him now. Perrin's boy. The whole quarter said it without thinking, and Émile had learned to say it too, though every time the name left his mouth he felt the small dislocation of a joint going quietly out of place. His sister. His little sister who used to stand on a stool to reach the washbasin, who cried for an hour over a sparrow the cat brought down, who had buried herself in a dead man's clothes and a dead man's trade and called it living.

He knocked.

The sweeping arm stopped. A long silence, the kind only Felix could produce — a silence with weight and edges, like a held breath in a locked room.

"Closed," said the voice from within. Low, roughened deliberately, pitched to sit below any register that might betray her. He heard, under it, the scrape of something being moved — not toward the door, he noticed. Away from it.

"Then close the door behind me," Émile said. "It's cold."

Another silence. Then the bolt went back, and the door opened a hand's width, and lamplight fell across the cobbles in a yellow wedge, and his sister looked out at him through the gap with eyes that were not surprised at all.

That was the first thing that frightened him. Not the pistol he'd imagined finding on her table, not tears or fury or denial. It was the absence of surprise. She had known he would come. She had perhaps been waiting for him the way one waits for weather.

"Félix," he said, testing the name aloud in her presence for the first time in months.

"Émile." Her voice dropped its disguise for exactly one syllable — the way she said his name, the *mi* softening into something almost girlish — and then the shutters came down again, and she stepped back to let him in. "Mind the wet on the floor. I've been washing brushes."

* * *

The studio smelled the way it had smelled for thirty years: linseed and size and the iron tang of fresh ink, cut with camphor from the flat-drawers where the finished plates slept between sheets of tissue. Perrin's ghost was everywhere in it — in the burnished hollow worn into the back of his chair, in the row of weights along the map rail, in the faint brown stain on the floorboards where a jar of walnut gall had broken in 1904 and no scrubbing ever fully lifted it. Émile had spent half his boyhood in this room, sitting on that stool, forbidden to touch anything, touching everything.

Now his sister stood in the middle of it with her sleeves rolled and her collar high and her hair cropped short enough to pass, and she watched him the way a surveyor watches a coastline that has begun, very slowly, to erode.

"You've eaten?" she asked.

"I'm not here for supper."

"I know what you're here for." She turned away, busied herself at the sink, wrung out a rag with more force than the rag deserved. "I wondered which day you'd come. Tuesday or Wednesday. You always choose the middle of things, Émile. You haven't the stomach for beginnings."

"That's unfair."

"Most true things are." She hung the rag on its nail and faced him, arms folded, and in the lamplight he saw how thin she'd got. How the bones of her face had sharpened since the summer, as though grief itself were carving her. "Say it, then. Whatever it is you've rehearsed on my doorstep for the last quarter hour. I counted your pacing. You walk the same beat as Father did before he told Mother we'd lost the Saint-Malo commission."

He had planned to be gentle. He had planned, above all, to be careful — to circle the thing, approach it from the seaward side, give her time to understand before she needed to decide. But standing in Perrin's studio with his sister's tired eyes on him, he found the careful words had all gone out of him like heat through a cracked pane.

"There's a letter," he said.

She didn't move. But something behind her eyes moved — a shutter drawn an inch, a light banked rather than extinguished.

"There's a letter," he said again, "in Perrin's effects. Sealed. In his hand. It was sewn into the backing of the old Brest road-chart, the one with the water damage. The guild's inventory clerk found the stitching gone crooked when he pressed the backing flat, and he brought it to me before he brought it anywhere else, because I'm a Joubert and he owes us." He swallowed. "You have it. Don't insult me by saying otherwise. The clerk swears he returned it to the drawer where the chart lay, and that drawer is in this room, and you are the only soul besides him who touches these drawers. And you've washed your hands twice since I knocked. You only do that when you've been handling something you're afraid of."

For a long moment the only sound was the fog pressing against the windows and, somewhere above, the slow complaint of the tide turning in the basin.

"And if I do have it," Félix said quietly, "what then? What is it to you?"

"It's death, little one. That's what it is." The old name slipped out before he could stop it, and he saw her flinch — saw the boy's coat she wore fail for an instant to cover the girl inside it. "It's a letter naming three families of this harbor in a conspiracy against the armistice. Three families, Félix. Ours among them. Our father's name in Perrin's hand, describing meetings, describing money, describing the signal station on the point and what was meant to happen there. Do you understand what that paper is? It isn't evidence. Evidence can be argued with. That letter is a verdict waiting for a courtroom."

"Or it's truth," she said. "Waiting to be told."

"Truth!" He laughed, and hated the sound of his own laugh — high, cracked, nothing like composure. "You want to talk about truth? Then let's. Let's stand in this room full of beautiful accurate lines and talk about truth. Our father died owing money to men who frightened our mother so badly she stopped opening the shutters. He died believing France had been sold from under him by politicians and profiteers, and there were nights he sat in that chair—" he pointed at Perrin's chair, absurdly, as if the two rooms were the same room, which in some way they had always been "—and said that if the war ended wrong, if the men who bled France white got to write the peace too, then everything we lost would have been lost for nothing. Four hundred thousand Frenchmen, Félix. Four hundred thousand. And our harbor gave sixty of them, and we buried them in rain, and you were there, I stood beside you."

"I remember the rain."

"Then remember what he asked of us. Remember what he made us promise at the graveside while the priest was still folding his vestments. *Whatever comes, keep faith with each other and with France.* Both. Together. He never separated them, and neither will I." Émile stepped closer. Up close he could see the ink on her fingers, worked deep into the cuticles, stains no pumice would lift — the mark of her trade, of Perrin's trade, of the life she had stolen back from the grave of a man who had loved her enough to let her steal it. "What's in that letter is faith with France. Ugly faith. Faith with its sleeves rolled up. But the men who meet in our cellar aren't monsters, whatever Perrin wrote. They're shipwrights and customs men and one very frightened widow. They love this country the way you love that atlas — obsessively, ruinously, past all sense."

"And they mean to blow up the signal station," Félix said. "To kill the boys who staff it. To blind the harbor on the night the world finally stops bleeding. Call that faith if it helps you sleep."

"They don't mean to kill anyone. They mean to delay—"

"With powder? With wire? Émile." She said his name the way one lays a card down. "I've read the letter. All of it. There's a schedule in it. There are quantities. There is a man whose task is described as *le nettoyage* — the cleaning. You don't clean a station with brooms." Her voice stayed level, and that levelness was worse than shouting; it was the voice she used over a chart when a coastline refused to agree with itself, patient and pitiless and certain. "So don't stand in this room and tell me it's patriotism. I grew up reading Father's letters. I know what his handwriting looks like when he means a thing and when he wants to mean it. His signature is on that pledge, but his hand shakes on the last stroke. He was dying, Émile, and frightened men were filling his ears, and they put his name on their paper because a dead patriot is worth ten live ones. You've signed yours under a ghost."

* * *

They had moved, without deciding to, to opposite ends of the long table where the atlas lay under its cloth. Between them stretched forty-one plates' worth of a harbor rendered in lines fine as hair — soundings, channels, moorings, the whole breathing anatomy of the place that had raised them both. Émile looked down at it and felt the vertigo he always felt in this room: the sense that the world was smaller than it seemed and more precisely known than anyone could bear.

"Perrin knew," he said at last. "That's the part I can't get past. He knew for years. He sat in guild meetings with those men, drank wine with them, drew their charts, took their fees — and all the while he was writing it down. Every meeting. Every name. Why do you think that is, Félix? A man doesn't keep a record like that unless part of him means to use it. Unless part of him is already choosing his side and just lacks the courage to announce it." He shook his head. "And now he's dead, and his courage has fallen to a girl in a borrowed coat who thinks burning a letter is the same as burning a bridge. Who do you imagine you'll save? Who, precisely? Name them."

"You," she said simply. "I'd save you. You're on that pledge in your own shaking hand, and nobody filled your ears, Émile — you were grown and sober and you signed anyway. If that letter goes where it wants to go, you hang. Or you rot in some fortress cell until you're old, and Mother's grave grows weeds, and the Joubert name becomes a cautionary tale told to apprentices. So yes. I would burn it for you. I have lain awake two nights knowing I would burn it for you, and hating myself for the knowing." Her chin came up. "But I won't. Because Perrin died keeping it, and because there's a signal station full of nineteen-year-olds on that point, and because I am so tired — God, I am so tired — of history happening to other people."

Something in Émile's chest turned over. It was the strangest sensation: pride arriving in the same instant as fear, the two of them holding hands like children crossing a road.

"Then listen to me," he said, and his voice dropped, gentled, became the voice he had used when she was six and the cat had died. "Because I came here tonight to threaten you, and I find I can't do it the way I planned, so I'll do it the way that's true instead. That letter makes you complicit whichever way you move. Burn it, and you're a conspirator — you'll have chosen them, in law and in fact, and if it ever surfaces you'll swing beside me. Deliver it, and you're a witness against your own blood, and the men in that cellar will learn of it inside a week — Madame Roux alone will see to that, she's already frantic, she's already talking — and they will come to this shop, and they will not be shipwrights knocking politely. Either way, Félix, the paper owns you. There is no innocent version of you anymore. That's what I came to say. That's the whole of it." He laid his hand flat on the atlas, on the covered plates, feeling through the cloth the faint ridges of the finished world. "The only question left is which complicity you can live inside."

She was quiet for a long time. Outside, a bell buoy spoke once, far off, a low bronze note the fog muffled and released.

"When did you become the kind of man who talks like that?" she asked finally. "Like a lawyer for despair."

"When Father died," Émile said. "Where were you?"

"Here," she said. "Drawing."

"Yes." He looked at her — really looked, past the collar and the cropped hair and the ink — and saw his sister entire, and understood that he was speaking to the bravest person he knew and possibly the most doomed. "Here. Drawing. While the rest of us signed things."

He walked to the door. His hand was on the bolt when she spoke again, and her voice had changed — lower, stripped of everything, the voice from the night of the funeral when she had stood at the graveside with rain running off her nose and not wiped it away.

"Émile. If they send someone to this shop — if you hear them planning anything against me — you'll tell me. Whatever you've pledged to them. You'll tell me first."

He stood with his back to her, forehead nearly touching the door, and the fog breathed cold through the seam of the frame.

"Go home, little one," he said. "Lock your drawers. Finish your atlas. And pray to whatever saint keeps mapmakers that no one ever asks you what's sewn into your charts."

He let himself out into the grey. Behind him he heard the bolt slide home, and then, faintly, after a pause too long to be accidental, the strike of a match.

He stood a while in the street, listening to the fog, telling himself the match meant nothing — a pipe, a candle, a warming pan. Telling himself his sister would do the sensible thing, the survivable thing, the thing the Jouberts had always done, which was to fold and endure.

But even as he turned up the rue des Salines, he could not shake the image that had settled in him like silt: Félix alone in the lamplight, the sealed letter in one hand, the flame in the other — and between them, in her steady cartographer's gaze, the look of a woman measuring a distance no chart had ever recorded, and finding, to her quiet horror, that she already knew the exact bearing she must sail.
