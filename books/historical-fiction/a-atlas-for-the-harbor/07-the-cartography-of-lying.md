## 7. The Cartography of Lying

The fog came in off the water before dawn that morning and did not leave again all day, pressing against the studio's tall windows like a visitor who had forgotten the way home. Felix lit the lamps at four o'clock in the afternoon because the light failed her, and by six the two flames were throwing doubled shadows of everything — of the drafting table, of Perrin's empty chair with its coat still draped over the back, of herself.

She had been at the table since morning. Before her lay two sheets of paper of identical size, identical weight, identical cream-white stock bought from the same stationer on the rue des Anciens. On the left sheet she was drawing the harbor as it was. On the right sheet she was drawing it as someone else would want it to be.

It had begun, as most terrible things begin, with tidiness.

She could not stop thinking about the letter. It sat where she had hidden it — inside the false back of the drawer beneath the barometer, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn there with a sailmaker's needle in a stitch Perrin himself had taught her for repairing chart cases. Every hour or so her eyes went to the barometer, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. The needle was still there. The thread held. And yet the knowledge of what lay beneath the brass instrument seemed to change the weight of the whole room, the way a sunken wreck changes the soundings of a bay long before anyone drops a line to find it.

So she worked. It was the only prayer she knew.

* * *

The true chart took shape under her hand with the ease of long habit: the eastern mole, the dredged channel, the shoal off the powder magazine marked in the careful dotted hachures Perrin had insisted upon even when the hydrographic office told him dots were old-fashioned. She drew the signal station on its headland as she had drawn it a hundred times — the low stone tower, the semaphore arms, the winding road that served it, the cistern beside it marked *eau potable*. Her pen knew these lines the way her mouth knew her own name, which was to say: not entirely, not honestly, but well enough to pass.

Then she turned to the second sheet, and her hand slowed.

She had told herself, in the grey hours of the night, that this was prudence. That a mapmaker who carried evidence through three military checkpoints ought to carry decoys, the way a courier carries a sealed envelope worth nothing so that thieves will stop at the first one. That deception, properly practiced, was simply cartography applied to human beings: survey the ground, note the obstacles, draw a route that looks like every route anyone would expect.

But there was a difference between telling yourself a thing at night and doing it by lamplight, and the difference had a name, and the name was *forgery*.

She dipped the pen. She moved the shoal.

It was a small alteration, almost modest. The dangerous bank off the powder magazine shifted half a cable's length eastward on the decoy sheet — enough to matter to any pilot, not enough to be obvious to a casual inspector. Then the channel markers: she nudged them to match. Then the soundings along the fairway, each depth adjusted by a foot or two, a coherent fiction, internally consistent, the kind of error that survives scrutiny because it is *systematic*, and systematic errors look like surveys, and surveys look like truth.

By the time the church bells rang seven she had falsified a mile of coastline, and her hand had not shaken once, and that frightened her more than anything else that had happened since she cut open the backing of that old chart and found her family's name folded inside another man's silence.

* * *

She set down the pen and pressed her palms flat against the table, against the cool paper, and made herself look at what she had done.

There was a version of this evening, she thought, in which she wept. In which the enormity of it broke over her — the forged charts, the concealed letter, the brother whose signature she could still see when she closed her eyes, the small fierce *É. Joubert* written in his schoolboy-slanted hand beneath words pledging his life and fortune to delaying the peace. There was a version of this evening in which she put her head down on Perrin's table and let the fear have her.

But the fog outside was thickening, and somewhere across the harbor men were meeting in cellars to decide how many people needed to die for their idea of honor, and weeping over clean paper helped none of them and hindered all of it. So instead she did what Perrin would have done, which was to make tea, sharpen the pencils, and think about the problem as a problem.

The problem had three parts.

First: the atlas itself must reach the naval archive finished and true. That was non-negotiable. An atlas was not paper; an atlas was a promise made to every sailor who would ever trust it in weather like tonight's. If she corrupted the atlas, she became the very thing she feared — a person willing to spend strangers' lives for her own safety. Whatever else happened, plate forty-one and its forty brothers would go to the archive honest.

Second: the letter must move. It could not stay in the drawer; Madame Roux knew of it, and a woman who begged was a woman who might also report, out of terror rather than malice, and Émile's colleagues would not send a delegation twice. The letter must travel toward the people negotiating the armistice, and it must travel soon, and it must travel past men with rifles who had been instructed — she had heard the talk in the streets, everyone had — to search anything and anyone connected with the harbor trades.

Third: she must survive the journey, and more than survive it — she must remain, afterward, a person she could live with. This was the part she kept trying not to think about and kept failing to avoid, because it sat underneath the other two like bedrock under silt.

She drank her tea while it went cold and began, at last, to plan.

* * *

Deception, she decided somewhere around the ninth o'clock bell, requires the same precision as truth. More, perhaps. A true chart can tolerate a lazy line here and there; nature forgives the draftsman because nature is generous with detail. But a lie is a structure built entirely by hand, with no sea floor underneath it, and every joint must bear weight. A sloppy truth is still mostly true. A sloppy lie is nothing at all.

So she treated the plan as a survey.

She drew, on a scrap of waste paper, the city as she now understood it: not streets but checkpoints, not neighborhoods but zones of inspection. The Ink Gate at the head of the quay, where the guild's couriers passed daily and the sentries had grown bored and patterned — papers checked at the left-hand window, parcels opened only if they clinked or smelled of metal, the sergeant reading by a lantern he never trimmed. The Customhouse on the far shore, stricter, newer, staffed by men rotated in from the front who still searched things properly, who weighed packages in their hands like fruit. And the Citadel Gate, which no private citizen passed at all, and behind which the delegates would sit in a fortnight putting their names to the end of four years of the world.

Three gates. She was, she realized, being asked to do what she did for a living: to find a passage through difficult water. The only difference was that the rocks shot back.

Her eye kept returning, on the little sketch, to the Ink Gate. Not because it was weakest — though it was; boredom was a kind of gap in any wall — but because of the lieutenant.

Lieutenant Arnaud Vasseur. She had seen him twice now, once at a distance and once near enough to count the buttons gone dull on his cuff. He stood his rounds like a man walking a beat he had memorized in some other town, and when he caught her looking — she had looked too long, a full three seconds, a cartographer's stare, measuring rather than seeing — he had not smirked or looked away quickly as soldiers did. He had simply looked back, with an expression she was still trying to chart. Interest, perhaps. Or recognition. Or the particular attention of a man who has noticed something out of place on familiar ground and has not yet decided whether it matters.

He was a hazard. She wrote him down as a hazard, on the scrap of paper, in her smallest hand, and then she sat looking at the word for a long time, because writing it did not make it behave like one.

* * *

Midnight came, and with it the practical work.

The letter could not travel loose. Loose paper was death at any checkpoint; loose paper was death anywhere. It had lived for years sewn into a chart's backing, and Perrin had chosen that hiding place not from desperation but from doctrine — the best concealment is not absence but plenitude, a thing hidden inside a great quantity of similar things, as a specific rock is lost among rocks. So: the letter would travel inside the atlas's world. Rolled within a chart. Sewn into a backing, perhaps, of a working copy — one of the decoy sheets she was already producing, sheets that had no value except to be found, and therefore perfect to be carried.

And that was the elegance of it, the cold elegance she found herself admiring even as it appalled her: the decoys were not merely cover for the letter. They were cover for each other. A soldier who opened a rolled chart and found altered soundings would conclude he had caught a smuggler of maps — a black-marketeer, a spy of the ordinary mercenary sort — and would stop looking for anything rarer. The forgery would absorb suspicion the way a grounding absorbs a swell. Her crime would protect her secret by offering itself up in its place.

She tested the idea against the man she imagined at the table: the customs officer turning the roll in his hands, slitting the canvas sleeve, frowning over the shifted shoal. Would he know? Only if he knew the harbor better than she did — and the men rotated in from the front did not, and the local men were bored, and the officers were tired, and tired men checked for contraband, not for accuracy.

It would hold. It would hold unless something went wrong, and something always went wrong, which was why a surveyor never drew one route but three.

She began a third sheet — not a chart this time but a schedule, a tide-table of her own life for the coming week. When the decoy copies would be "discovered" en route to nowhere, mailed to herself or abandoned where inspection was certain. When the true atlas would go to the archive, openly, under guild seal, because openness was the deepest hiding place of all. When she would carry the letter herself, sewn into the backing of the final delivery copy, through whichever gate proved softest on whichever day the fog cooperated.

Fog. She glanced at the windows, where the lamplight died two feet beyond the glass. The city had been wrapped for three days. Fog delayed the patrols, shortened the sentries' patience, blurred faces. In Perrin's notes on coastal surveying there was a line she had copied out as an apprentice and had never forgotten: *The observer's enemy is not darkness but uniformity; give him a field of sameness and he sees nothing.* She intended to be sameness. She intended to be the most unremarkable apprentice carrying the most unremarkable parcel through the most unremarkable gate in this entire fog-bound city.

She wrote the last entry on the schedule, blotted it, and fed it to the candle.

Watching the paper curl and brighten and go, she felt something settle in her chest that she recognized only distantly, the way one recognizes a change in pressure before rain. It was not fear, or not only fear. It was resolve — but a strange, chilled kind, without any heat of righteousness in it. She was not going to do this because she was brave. She was going to do it because the alternative, on close inspection, surveyed carefully, sounded and charted line by line, was worse.

Perrin had died holding this secret shut. Émile had signed his name to opening it. Somewhere between those two dead weights — the master's silence and the brother's pledge — there was a narrow channel, and she meant to pilot it.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when she allowed herself the last task of the day, the one she had been circling since dusk.

She unsewed the drawer's false back. The oilcloth bundle came up into the lamplight, and she unwrapped it just far enough to see the superscription in Perrin's upright, unhurried hand — the hand she had spent nine years learning to imitate, and which she now imitated without thinking whenever she wrote an official label, so that in a sense his handwriting had survived in hers like a watermark in paper.

*To whom it may concern at the signing,* it read, and beneath that, smaller: *God forgive us all, and may they read it in time.*

She had read it twice already. She knew the names. But tonight, folding it back into its cloth, she noticed something she had missed before — or something that had not been there before, which amounted to the same dread. At the foot of the attached pledge, below her father's faded signature and Émile's fresh one, there was a third mark. Not a signature. A small drawn symbol, quick and professional: a circle with a cross through it, like a compass rose reduced to its skeleton.

She knew that mark. Every guild apprentice knew it. It was the surveyor's sign for *station point* — the fixed position from which all other measurements are taken.

Someone in that cellar had drawn it as a signature. Someone who thought in maps.

Felix sat very still, the bundle in her hands, while the fog pressed at the glass and the candle leaned in a draft she could not feel. Her brother's conspiracy had a cartographer in it. And a man who thinks in maps, she thought, will eventually wonder — as I am wondering — where the safest place in this city is to hide a piece of paper.

The answer, of course, was everywhere she had just been.

She rewrapped the letter, resewed the drawer, and blew out the lamps, and stood in the dark of the studio listening to the foghorn moan across the water like something lost. Tomorrow she would begin the decoys in earnest. Tomorrow she would walk past the Ink Gate and measure the lieutenant's rounds.

Tonight she lay awake cataloguing, with a mapmaker's terrible thoroughness, every place in the city where she herself would hide a secret — and understanding, at last, why the cold feeling in her chest would not warm.

Because the list was short, and she was on it.
