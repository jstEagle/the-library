## 8. Tea with Madame Roux

The Roux house stood on the rue des Salines like a woman holding her breath. Its shutters were painted the blue of deep water, a color that had been fashionable before the war and now looked like mourning that had decided to be decorative. The gate was iron, scrolled, and locked; the garden behind it had gone to seed in the way gardens do when there is no longer money for a gardener but too much pride to let the neighbors see weeds. Madame Roux kept the front path swept herself, every morning, with a birch broom worn down to half its length. Felix had passed that gate a hundred times on survey walks and never once seen the door open.

Today it opened before she had finished knocking.

"Master Perrin's apprentice." Madame Roux said it not as a greeting but as an identification, the way a customs officer says *papers*. She was small and upright and dressed in black so dense it seemed to drink the gray afternoon light. Her hair was silvered at the temples and pinned with a severity that suggested she had made peace with age by declaring war on it. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they did not leave Felix's face from the moment the door swung wide.

"You wrote that you wished to consult me on a chart of the eastern shallows," Felix said. The lie sat comfortably enough; commissions came from everywhere now, since half the harbor's pilots had been conscripted and the other half drowned.

"I wrote what was necessary to write." Madame Roux stepped back to admit her. "Come in. Mind the hall rug; it catches."

* * *

The parlor was cold in the particular way of rooms that are heated only when company is expected and company has stopped coming. A fire had been laid but barely lit — three logs, arranged with mathematical precision, giving off more intention than warmth. On the mantel stood a photograph of Captain Auguste Roux in his dress uniform, one hand resting on a bollard, squinting slightly against a sun that no longer existed for anyone in this room. Beside it, smaller, a photograph of a young man in naval cadet's whites. Their son, Felix would learn. Lost at Jutland, or near enough to Jutland that the distinction had stopped mattering to anyone but the mother.

"Sit," Madame Roux said, indicating a chair positioned at an exact right angle to hers, close enough for confidence, far enough for dignity.

Tea arrived on a tray carried by no servant — there were no servants left, or none that showed themselves — but by Madame Roux alone, and the tray itself told a story. It was silver, badly tarnished at the edges, polished furiously in the center where the cups sat. The pot was porcelain, old Sèvres, with a hairline crack running through the lid that had been mended with gold, in the Japanese manner, so that the flaw gleamed. Someone in this house had once believed that broken things could be made more beautiful by their breaking. Felix wondered if that someone still believed it.

"Sugar?" Madame Roux asked, and held out the tongs, and Felix saw that her hands trembled — not much, not enough to spill anything, just enough to make the sugar cubes click against each other like small anxious teeth.

"No, thank you."

"You take it plain. Like my husband did." A pause, loaded as a cargo hold. "He said sweet things were for people who hadn't learned yet how bitter the world keeps its accounts."

Felix accepted the cup and said nothing. In Perrin's studio she had learned that silence was an instrument; held correctly, it made other people play their own tunes.

For a while they drank, or performed drinking, and Madame Roux spoke of nothing — the weather, which was fog; the fog, which was worse than weather; the difficulty of getting coal; the scandal of the price of butter. Felix answered in monosyllables and watched the fire's third log fail to catch. Then, without transition, without warning, Madame Roux set down her cup and said:

"How long have you been in Master Perrin's employ?"

"Four years this autumn."

"And he trusted you."

"With his work, madame. Yes."

"With everything?"

The question hung between them like fog off the water. Felix felt the first true cold of the afternoon enter through it. "With whatever he chose to give me," she said carefully.

Madame Roux nodded slowly, as though this confirmed something she had paid a great deal to confirm. She rose, went to the escritoire in the corner — Empire style, brass-mounted, its key hanging from a ribbon at her waist — unlocked a drawer, and returned with a flat object wrapped in oilcloth. She laid it on the low table between them but did not unwrap it.

"Do you know what this is?"

"A chart," Felix said. "By the weight and shape. Rolled, backed with linen."

"It is a chart of the outer roads, surveyed by my husband in '09, when he was alive and the world was simpler." Her voice did not waver, but Felix heard beneath it the effort of a woman keeping her voice level the way one keeps a boat level in a swell — constantly, invisibly, with muscles that will ache tomorrow. "It came back to me last month from a dealer in the Marché aux Cartes who claimed he bought it at an estate sale. An estate sale, Master Joubert. My husband has been dead two years. His charts do not go to estate sales unless someone put them there."

Felix said nothing. The tea had gone cold in her cup and she drank it anyway, because her mouth needed occupation.

"The dealer said the backing had been resewn. New thread, poor stitching, done in haste." Madame Roux finally unwrapped the oilcloth, and there it was: a coastal chart, hand-colored, beautiful, its linen backing bulging very slightly along one edge as though something thin lay beneath the stitches. "I had it opened. I am a widow, Master Joubert, not a fool. There was paper inside. Not mine. Not my husband's hand."

She turned the chart over. Along the margin, in pencil, faded almost to nothing, ran a single line of figures — soundings, Felix recognized, depths and distances, the kind of notation a surveyor makes and then transfers to the fair copy and erases from the working sheet. Except these had not been erased. These had been left deliberately, in a place where only another mapmaker would think to look.

"These are your master's figures," Madame Roux said. "I have compared them with the atlas plates your studio sold to the Hydrographic Office before the war. I am many things now, but I was a captain's wife, and a captain's wife learns to read a chart the way she learns to read her husband's silences." She folded her hands in her lap. "Perrin hid something in my husband's chart. Which means Perrin trusted my husband, once, with something. And now Perrin is dead, and my husband is dead, and I am sitting in a cold parlor asking an apprentice what became of the trust."

* * *

Felix had prepared for this meeting the way she prepared all meetings: with a rehearsed self, a plausible surface, answers filed under headings. She had assumed Madame Roux wanted a chart commissioned, or a valuation, or perhaps — given the family name that appeared in certain circles — a discreet inquiry into harbor traffic. She had not assumed this.

"What exactly," Felix said, "do you believe my master entrusted to your husband?"

"I believe," Madame Roux said, "that in the winter of '16, Auguste came home from a dinner at the Ozenne house looking like a man who had swallowed seawater. He did not sleep. He walked to the signal station and back twice in one night, in February, without his coat. And on the third day he took his best outer-roads chart to Perrin's studio and did not come home with it." Her colorless eyes fixed on Felix. "Three weeks later he began putting the house in order. Selling what could be sold quietly. Settling debts he claimed we didn't have. He told me he had made an error of judgment years ago and that the interest was coming due. He would not say what the error was. Two months after that he was dead. The doctor wrote *cardiac failure*. Auguste had the heart of an ox and swam in the outer roads every morning until the day he died."

The fire's third log caught at last, briefly, and threw a shudder of light across the photograph on the mantel. The dead captain seemed to squint harder.

"You think my master killed him," Felix said. "Or knew what killed him."

"I think my husband carried something for Perrin, or against Perrin, or beside Perrin — I cannot know which, and I have spent two years not knowing, and not knowing is a disease, Master Joubert, it eats a woman from the inside the way salt eats iron." She leaned forward. "And then, last month, a friend of my late husband's — a man who moves in certain circles, a man whose name I will not speak in my own parlor — let fall, over cards, that the harbor families have an arrangement. That there are men who mean to see the war end *properly*, on terms favorable to shipping, and that if the armistice comes too soon and too generous to the enemy, certain measures exist to delay it. He said it lightly. Men say such things lightly, over cards, because they believe women and apprentices do not listen." Her hands, folded in her lap, had gone white at the knuckles. "But I listened. And I remembered my husband's chart. And I came to the only person left alive who might know what Auguste carried."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled over the cobbles on the rue des Salines, and the sound seemed to pass through the walls and through Felix's chest.

"Madame," Felix said at last, "if such an arrangement existed, why come to me? Why not to the authorities?"

A smile crossed Madame Roux's face, and it was the most frightening thing Felix had seen all day, because it was gentle. "Because the authorities are the arrangement. Because half the officers at the checkpoints dine with the men whose names are in that arrangement. Because a widow who goes to the authorities with a story about harbor families and signal stations will be diagnosed, within the week, with grief-madness, and placed somewhere quiet, and her house will be sold to settle the costs of her care." She unfolded her hands. "And because you, Master Joubert, are nobody. An apprentice. A boy with ink on his fingers. Nobody watches nobodies. Nobody suspects them. You can walk through this city carrying anything at all, and the sentries see only a tradesman's lad going about his master's business."

Something in the way she said *boy* snagged on Felix like a fishhook — not suspicion, precisely, but a scrutiny that lingered a half-second past comfort. Felix had been stared at by guildsmen, by clerks, by drunk sailors; she knew the taxonomy of being looked at. This was different. This was a woman who had spent two years learning to read silences, turning that education on her.

"My master is dead," Felix said. "Whatever he entrusted died with him. I inventory his effects for the guild next week. If something surfaces, it goes to the guild, and the guild does what guilds do."

"Which is nothing," Madame Roux said, "except protect the guild." She rose, went again to the escritoire, and this time returned with an envelope — heavy paper, sealed, fat with contents. She set it on the table beside the opened chart.

"Two hundred francs," she said. "It is not a bribe. Please do not insult us both by calling it a bribe. It is payment for a professional service, rendered in advance. The service is this: whatever Perrin left behind — in his charts, in his table, in his effects — you will find it, and you will bring it to me *unopened*, unread, unreported. And I will destroy it. And no one will ever know it existed, and no one will ever know you touched it, and the families — the survivors, the widows, the children who have lost enough — will be spared what remains of their names."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I have misjudged you, and I will write to the guild and tell them that Perrin's boy has been selling copies of restricted surveys to dealers in the Marché aux Cartes, and the guild will believe me, because grief lends a widow enormous credibility, and you will lose your trade, your rooms, and your standing in a city where standing is the only currency that cannot be counterfeited." She said it without heat, without threat in the voice — the way one reads aloud the terms of a contract. "I do not want to do that. I want very much not to do that. I want to sit here and drink cold tea and believe that you are the solution and not another problem."

Felix looked at the envelope. Two hundred francs was four months of rent, coal, and food. It was the difference between finishing the atlas in warmth and finishing it watching her breath fog. It was also, she understood suddenly, the price tag Madame Roux had attached to her silence, and prices, once named, could be raised.

"Madame Roux," Felix said slowly, "what exactly do you believe is in my master's effects?"

"I believe there is a letter." The words came out of her like teeth being drawn. "I believe Perrin wrote down what my husband knew, and what my husband feared, and that he sewed it into a chart because paper hidden in paper is the safest hiding place in the world — ask any mapmaker. And I believe that letter names families. Families with children. Families who have already given sons to this war and buried them." Her composure cracked, just once, along a fault line Felix could see plainly: "My son is at the bottom of the North Sea. I will not bury his name too."

There it was. Not greed, not even fear — grief, wearing the costume of strategy. Felix had imagined conspirators as men in cellars with lanterns and pistols. She had not imagined them as mothers in cold parlors offering tarnished silver and mended porcelain and the arithmetic of survival.

"Suppose," Felix said, "that the letter exists. Suppose it names crimes — planned crimes, crimes not yet committed. Burning it doesn't undo the plan. It only blinds the city to it."

"The plan requires belief," Madame Roux said. "Belief requires signatures. Signatures require men willing to sign their names to treason, and most men will talk themselves into treason only if they believe no one is watching. Destroy the evidence, and the watching stops, and the talking stops, and the whole rotten arrangement dries up like ink in an uncapped pen." She picked up the envelope and held it out. "I am offering you the chance to prevent a crime by destroying its paperwork. That is not treachery, Master Joubert. That is the only mercy available to any of us."

Felix did not take the envelope. She looked at the photograph above the mantel instead — the captain squinting into his vanished sun — and thought about a dead man who had walked to the signal station twice in one February night without his coat, and what he must have known, and whether he had died of his heart or of his knowledge.

"I will consider it," Felix said.

"Consideration is not acceptance."

"No," Felix agreed. "But refusal, madame, is a door that only opens one way. Let me keep it closed a little longer."

Madame Roux studied her for a long moment, and Felix watched the calculation move behind those pale eyes like weather — the reassessment of an opponent previously catalogued as harmless. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped its gentleness entirely.

"Then hear the rest of the terms. You have until the armistice is signed — everyone says it is days away now, perhaps a week. After that, the arrangement either fires or dissolves, and either way the letter becomes worthless ash or priceless rope. Bring it to me before then, and we part as friends, and I will recommend you to every shipping family in this city, and your master's atlas will be commissioned and celebrated and you will want for nothing." She stood. "Fail to bring it, and I will assume you intend to use it yourself — for blackmail, for leverage, for whatever appetite drives a person to sit on a document that could hang half the harbor. And I will treat you accordingly."

"And how is that?"

"Thoroughly," said Madame Roux.

* * *

At the door, with the fog pressing against the fanlight like something drowning, Madame Roux paused with her hand on the latch. All the steel had gone out of her posture; what remained was smaller, older, tired.

"He was a good man, my Auguste," she said, to the door rather than to Felix. "Thirty years on the water. He used to say a chart was a promise — that what you drew, you owed to whoever sailed by it. He walked to that signal station in February without his coat because he had discovered his promise and someone else's promise pointed in opposite directions, and he was trying to decide which debt was real." She opened the door. Cold air moved through the hall, smelling of coal smoke and brine. "Decide quickly, Master Joubert. Debts compound."

Felix stepped out onto the swept path. Behind her, the door closed with the softness of a thing meant to be unheard, and the lock turned, and the blue shutters of the rue des Salines resumed their expression of a woman holding her breath.

She walked toward the harbor with the fog thickening around her and the envelope's weight absent from her pocket but present everywhere else — in the memory of trembling sugar tongs, in the photograph of a squinting captain, in the phrase that would not stop repeating itself: *debts compound.*

She had come expecting a customer. She was leaving having been purchased, or priced, or threatened — she genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing followed her down the rue des Salines like footsteps she couldn't quite hear.

By the time she reached the corner where the street bent toward the water, she had made one decision, small and private and already irreversible: she would not tell Arnaud Vasseur about this visit. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

And she would open the letter tonight — truly open it, read every line, every signature — because a woman could not bargain over a document she had only glimpsed, and because Madame Roux was wrong about one thing, and Felix needed to know how wrong:

Some promises, once discovered, could not be destroyed. They could only be kept or betrayed.

Behind her, in the cold parlor on the rue des Salines, Madame Roux stood at her window watching the apprentice go, and reached for her writing desk, and drew out a sheet of paper, and began — in a hand that shook only slightly — to compose a note to a man whose name she had refused to speak in her own parlor.
