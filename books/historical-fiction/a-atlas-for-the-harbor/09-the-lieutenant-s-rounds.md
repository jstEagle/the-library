## 9. The Lieutenant's Rounds

The fog came off the water at four o'clock, as it always did in winter, and by five it had swallowed the Ink Gate whole.

Lieutenant Arnaud Vasseur stood at his post with his hands clasped behind him and his breath making small ghosts in the air, and he thought — not for the first time that morning, nor the fortieth time that week — that a man could go mad standing watch over a gate that no one wished to pass through. The Ink Gate had earned its name generations ago, when the tanneries and inkworks upstream still dumped their lees into the canal, and though the trade had died, the name had clung like the smell itself seemed to have done: a faint sourness beneath the salt, something old and chemical that lived in the stones. The sentries swore you could taste it on bad days. Arnaud had stopped noticing it months ago. What he could not stop noticing was everything else — the way the fog moved in channels along the quay as if it too were following some chart only it could read, the way the gulls sat hunched on the bollards like old men waiting for a ferry that would never come, the way the city behind him stacked itself up the hill in gray tiers until the cathedral tower dissolved somewhere above the third layer of white.

He was twenty-six years old and he was guarding paper.

That was the joke of it, the joke he was not permitted to make aloud. The armistice negotiations had turned every warehouse, every customs shed, every bridge and gate in this harbor city into a fortress of documents. Delegations arrived with trunks of correspondence under armed escort. Couriers were searched twice and signed for three times. And here at the Ink Gate — the least of the three checkpoints ringing the old town, the one nobody important used — Arnaud commanded eleven men whose principal duty was to inspect the cargo of carters and clerks moving between the lower quays and the naval archive district. Charts. Ledgers. Survey plates. Reams of blank paper, for God's sake, because even blank paper was contraband now, or nearly so; a sheet of good rag stock carried more suspicion than a crate of wine.

"Quiet morning, Lieutenant," said Sergeant Brissac, materializing at his elbow with the particular cheerfulness of a man who has already eaten.

"They're all quiet mornings."

"Better than loud ones."

Arnaud did not answer. Brissac was a good sergeant, patient with the men, honest with the ledgers, and incurious in exactly the way the army prized. Arnaud had learned to be a little less curious himself, over the years. Curiosity was a quality for peacetime. In wartime it got men shot, or worse, got them thinking, and thinking soldiers were a luxury no one had budgeted for.

Except that he could not seem to stop thinking.

* * *

The rounds took an hour if he walked them properly, and he walked them properly, because his father had been a gendarme in a river town forty leagues south and had believed with religious fervor that a post unwalked was a post already lost. Arnaud had inherited the belief along with the boots, more or less. So he made the circuit: along the canal wall where the ice had grown inward from both banks until only a black ribbon of water remained, past the coal yard with its mountain of slack covered in gray snow, up the ramp to the customs shed where the inspections happened, and back down again to the barrier itself, where two sentries stood in a wooden sentry box that leaked at every seam.

The shed smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and, faintly, always, of the sea — which was strange, because the sea was a quarter mile off, but the fog carried it inland like a letter carried by hand, delivered everywhere, addressed to no one.

Inside the shed, Corporal Devaux was going through a carter's parcels with the enthusiasm of a man looking for his own lost wages. Arnaud watched for a moment without intervening. The carter stood with his cap in his hands, radiating the patience of someone who had done this every morning for a month. On the table lay the morning's accumulation: a bolt of canvas, a ham wrapped in straw, a leather satchel stamped with the arms of the harbor guild, and — Arnaud felt something shift in his chest, some small tectonic adjustment he could not have named —

— a roll of survey plates in a canvas sleeve, tied with waxed cord.

"Guild business," the carter was saying. "Charts for the archive. Perrin's shop, down on the Rue des Ancres."

"Perrin's dead," said Devaux, not unkindly. It was simply a fact in this city now, one among thousands. Half the men of a certain age were dead, and the other half were learning to say so in the flat tone the war had taught them.

"The apprentice carries on. The boy. Felix."

And there it was again, that shift, that settling of some weight inside him that had no business being there.

Arnaud had seen the apprentice four times now. He had counted, though he would not have admitted to counting. Four times in six weeks, always at odd hours, always alone, always carrying the same rolled charts and the same leather case with the guild's stamp pressed into the leather. A slight young man with a cap pulled low and a coat a size too large, as though borrowed from a taller brother. Hands that were never quite still. And the fingers — that was the thing, that was what had snagged in Arnaud's memory like a fishhook in a net — the fingers stained dark at the tips, blue-black, the unmistakable stain of India ink worked deep into the skin, the kind of staining that does not wash out, that becomes part of a man the way calluses become part of a sailor's palms.

A working hand. An artist's hand. Arnaud had grown up watching his mother draw flowers in the margins of her letters, and her fingers had carried the same shadow, graphite and violet, and he had loved her hands before he understood anything else about love, and so perhaps that was the whole explanation, perhaps it was nothing but that: a young officer far from home seeing his mother's hands on a stranger in the fog, and calling the ache it produced by some other name.

Professional curiosity. That was the name he had chosen for it. He turned the phrase over now as he turned the phrase *quiet morning* over, testing its weight, finding it insufficient, using it anyway.

"Let the plates through," he said. "Sealed guild consignment. Log it."

Devaux logged it. The carter went out into the fog. The roll of survey plates — and whoever had drawn them, whoever's stained fingers had touched the cord — went with it, toward the archive, toward the great stone building where the navy kept its memory of the coast, and Arnaud stood a moment longer than he needed to, looking at the door through which they had gone.

"Lieutenant?" Brissac, again, at his shoulder. "You're wanted at the telephone. Commandant's office."

* * *

The commandant's office occupied the ground floor of the old pilot house, and it smelled of coffee burnt twice over and of the particular staleness of rooms where men sleep in their chairs. Commandant Lefranc was fifty, heavy, and tired in the way of officers who had spent the war doing staff work and knew precisely how much and how little their work mattered. He waved Arnaud to a chair without looking up from a dispatch.

"You requested a transfer of duties," Lefranc said. "In writing. Unusual."

"Yes, sir."

"For a lieutenant to request *more* work is either ambition or madness, and I've known you long enough to rule out ambition." Lefranc looked up at last. His eyes were pale and shrewd. "You want the atlas inspection detail."

"I do, sir."

"The Perrin atlas. The coastal survey." Lefnanc set the dispatch down. "You understand what this is? The old man spent nine years on it. Nine years sounding the approaches, fixing the lights, drawing every rock from here to the headland — and now the delegation wants it finished and certified before the signing, because apparently peace requires pretty pictures." He snorted. "There are men who think the atlas matters more than the treaty. There are men who think the treaty matters more than anything. I think neither will survive contact with February. But the orders stand: every plate, every sheet that passes through your gate toward the archive, gets inspected against the guild's certification list. Page by page. You asked for this?"

"I asked for this, sir."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and Arnaud had prepared an answer for it, the way one prepares answers for all fair questions, laying them out beforehand like instruments on a tray. He gave it now, evenly. "The atlas is the single most valuable civilian document moving through my checkpoint. Its contents concern navigation, defense works, signal stations, soundings — everything a hostile party might want. If there is any channel by which information reaches the wrong hands, it passes through the Ink Gate in a canvas sleeve. I'd rather inspect it myself than trust it to rotation duty."

All true. Every word of it true, and arranged with care, and none of it the reason.

Lefranc studied him for a long moment. Outside, the fog pressed against the window glass like something wanting in. Somewhere down the quay a ship's bell rang twice, muffled, apologetic.

"You're a careful officer, Vasseur," the commandant said finally. "Careful men worry me less than clever ones. Granted. The detail is yours. Weekly inspection of all guild consignments, joint sign-off with the archive registrar. You'll report anomalies directly to me." He picked up the dispatch again, then paused. "One thing. The guildmaster — Ozenne — has been asking after security arrangements. Wants to know who inspects his shipments. Polite about it. Very polite." Lefranc's tone suggested that politeness, in his experience, was the wrapping paper on most of the trouble in the world. "Be courteous. Be thorough. Don't be friends."

"No, sir."

"And Vasseur." Lefranc did not look up. "If you're chasing something, chase it all the way. Half-chased things come back and bite."

Arnaud saluted and went out into the fog, and only when he was halfway down the quay did he allow himself to understand what he had just done: he had arranged, through channels, with paperwork, with the full weight of military authority behind it, to see the apprentice with the ink-stained fingers once a week, lawfully, indefinitely.

He told himself it was about the plates.

* * *

That evening the fog thickened until the lamps along the canal burned as mere halos, disembodied, floating in the white. Arnaud ate in the mess at the pilot house — soup, bread, a cheese whose name he preferred not to learn — and afterward he wrote the letter he wrote every Sunday and did not send every Sunday, because the mail to the occupied departments moved slowly and his mother's last address was eight months stale. He wrote anyway. It was a discipline, like walking the rounds. *Dear Maman. The work is quiet. The city is gray and smells of ink and salt. I have taken on a new duty, an inspection of charts — you would laugh, your son the map-watcher —*

He stopped. Looked at the sentence. Your son the map-watcher. It sounded absurd written down, and yet it was the truest thing in the letter, and he crossed it out carefully, ruling a single line through it the way one strikes an error on a chart, leaving the error legible beneath, because that was how his father had taught him corrections were made: never erased, only acknowledged.

In the margin, without quite deciding to, he drew the harbor as he saw it from the gate — the curve of the quay, the coal yard, the little square of the customs shed — a rough plan in pencil, quick and sure. Drawing steadied him. It had since he was a boy. When the world grew formless, you drew its edges, and the world obeyed, and became bearable.

He found he had drawn, in the corner of the margin, entirely without intention, a pair of hands. Just the suggestion of hands, holding something flat — a chart, a plate — fingers long and precise, and he looked at them for a long moment before he rubbed them out with his thumb.

The foghorn spoke from the outer roads, low and patient, a sound like the city clearing its throat before saying something it wasn't sure it ought to say.

* * *

Two days later, the apprentice came through at dusk.

Arnaud was at the barrier when the figure appeared out of the white — a slight silhouette resolving by degrees into the familiar shape: the too-large coat, the cap, the canvas sleeve with its rolled plates, the leather case with the guild stamp. Alone, as always. Walking with that particular economy of motion, as if each step had been measured and approved in advance.

"Evening," said the sentry, and stepped aside even before the papers were shown, because everyone knew the guild boy by now.

But Arnaud stepped forward. "A moment. New orders — inspection detail. All guild consignments checked against certification." He held out his hand for the pass, and their fingers did not touch, but came near enough that he noticed the cold of the evening had gotten into the kid's gloves, worn thin at the fingertips.

The apprentice handed over the pass. Said nothing. Watched him with dark, level eyes above the scarf — eyes that did not fidget the way young men's eyes usually fidgeted under an officer's gaze, but waited, calm as deep water.

"Felix Joubert," Arnaud read from the pass. "Apprentice to Master Perrin, deceased." He looked up. "You're finishing his atlas."

"I am."

"Alone?"

"His other apprentices didn't come back from the war." Flat. Not bitter. Simply a fact laid on the table like a coin.

Arnaud nodded slowly. He untied the canvas sleeve with deliberate care — giving himself permission, under the cover of procedure, to take his time — and slid out the topmost plate. It was a chart of the eastern approaches, soundings in fine figures, the coastline rendered in a line so confident it seemed less drawn than remembered. He had seen hydrographic charts all his life; his father had kept one of the river framed in the front room. But he had never seen one like this. There was something in the line — a patience, a devotion, years of mornings folded into every contour — that made him hold it a moment longer than inspection required.

"This is beautiful," he said, before he could stop himself.

Something moved behind the apprentice's eyes — surprise, quickly governed. "Master Perrin drew the coasts. I finish the borders and lettering."

"Then your lettering is beautiful too." He turned the plate to the lamplight. "My mother draws. Flowers, mostly. Margins of letters." He heard himself going on and could not make himself stop. "She says a line is honest or it isn't. That you can tell, holding a thing someone drew, whether they meant it."

The silence stretched a moment. The fog moved past the lamp in slow rivers. And then the apprentice said, quietly, in a voice Arnaud decided on the spot was merely young and tired:

"Your mother is right. A line is honest or it isn't. Most people never look closely enough to find out which."

They looked at each other across the rolled charts and the lamplight and the whole apparatus of the checkpoint, and Arnaud felt the peculiar sensation of having said something true to a stranger and being unable to account for it — as if the fog had loosened something in him that daylight kept fastened.

He re-rolled the plate. Tied the cord. Signed the log. "Go on, then. Mind the ice on the ramp."

The apprentice gathered the case and went, and Arnaud watched until the white closed over the slight figure, and stood a while longer in the cold, telling himself the thing he had arranged his week around telling himself: that this was diligence, that this was the detail he had requested, that a lieutenant who inspected charts was simply a lieutenant doing his duty well.

But that night, in the log book, under the day's entries, his pen hovered. And in the margin beside *Joubert, F., guild consignment, cleared,* he wrote — small, in pencil, the way one marks a position not yet fixed, a bearing taken but not confirmed, a light sighted and not yet identified:

*Ask about the lettering.*

He looked at the words a long time. Then, because his father had taught him never to erase, only to acknowledge, he left them there.
