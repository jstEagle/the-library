## 11. Midnight Survey

The fog came in off the water an hour after dark, the way it always did in December, rolling up from the harbor like something alive and hungry, swallowing the masts first and then the rooftops and then, at last, the lamps themselves. Felix Joubert watched it from the studio window while she cleaned her pens, and told herself — for the fourth time that evening, which was three times too many — that what she intended to do was ordinary.

A surveyor walks his ground. That was all. Perrin had walked it a hundred times; she had walked it with him twice, before her hands were steady enough to hold a theodolite alone, before he stopped taking apprentices into the field at all. A man who meant to finish plate forty-one could not finish it from memory. The harbor signal station stood on the eastern mole, and the chart required its elevations, its sightlines, the exact angle of its semaphore mast against the water tower behind it. Any inspector with the guild's seal would want those figures verified. Any honest mapmaker would go and verify them.

That no honest mapmaker would choose to do it at midnight, in fog, with the letter sewn into the lining of his coat — *that* was the part Felix declined to examine too closely. She had learned, these past weeks, that lying well required the same discipline as drawing well: you did not shade the whole lie darkly, you left most of it blank paper and let the eye fill in the rest. So she packed the instrument case with genuine work inside it — the field notebook, the plumb line, the little brass clinometer Perrin had bought in Brest thirty years ago — until the pretext had weight enough to stand on its own.

She wore the coat over it. The letter rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

The streets between the shop and the mole were empty in the way cities are empty when they are not sleeping but holding their breath. The armistice news had been coming for days now — rumors first, then denials, then rumors again, each more specific than the last — and the whole harbor seemed to have drawn itself inward around the possibility of peace the way a hand closes around a coin. In the taverns they spoke of it quietly. In the churches they lit candles without being asked. And here and there, in doorways where the gaslight did not quite reach, men stood who had no business standing in doorways at midnight, whose collars were turned up and whose eyes followed footfalls longer than curiosity warranted.

Felix kept her stride long and her shoulders square, the walk she had practiced for six years until it lived in her bones deeper than her own name. She nodded to one of the doorway men as she passed, the curt nod of one tradesman to another out late about his affairs, and did not let herself count how many others there were.

*They will strike the signal station,* the letter had said, in Perrin's cramped and careful hand. She had read it eleven times now. She knew the sentence the way she knew coastlines she'd never seen — by heart, by dread. *When the delegates come to sign, the harbor must be blind and deaf, or the signing will fail, and the war will find its reason to continue.* Three families pledged. Her father's name among them. Émile's signature beneath, still wet with ink years old.

But a letter was testimony, and testimony could be doubted, dismissed, called forgery — she had thought of every way it might be dismissed while lying awake with the candle guttering. What testimony could never be dismissed was corroboration. If she could show that the signal station's vulnerabilities matched, point for point, what the plot required — if she could put on paper, in the neutral language of contour lines and bearings, exactly how a handful of men might blind the harbor — then the letter would stop being a dead man's grievance and become a fact.

Facts were her trade. She would make this one herself, with her own hands, since no one else would make it for her.

* * *

The mole stretched out into the black water like a stone arm, and the fog lay along it in layers, thick at knee height and thinner above, so that walking it felt like wading through smoke. The signal station rose ahead of her — a squat granite block with the semaphore mast rising from its roof, forty feet of tarred timber and hinged arms that spoke the harbor's language to ships still hull-down below the horizon. By day it was nothing, a working building like any other, gulls on the railings, a keeper smoking on the steps. By night, under martial law, it was a garrison. Two sentries at the landward gate. One more somewhere on the seaward parapet. She had counted them across three evenings of watching from the fish-market roof, and tonight she would count them from close enough to touch.

She stopped at the base of the sea wall, set down the case, and opened the notebook to a fresh page. The first thing she wrote was the thing any inspector would expect: *Station observed 11 Dec., conditions fog, visibility poor, survey by lamplight incomplete.* A small lie, dated and initialed, to cover the large truth of why she was here. Then she turned the page and began the other record — the one she would keep in her head and transfer to paper only once, later, in a hand disguised as her own disguised hand, so many layers deep that even she almost wouldn't be able to prove she'd written it.

The station sat eleven feet above mean high water. She knew that from the old charts. What the charts didn't say was that the landward gate hung loose on its hinges — she could see the gap where the lamplight spilled through, an inch of yellow along the cobbles — and that the coal store built against the east wall gave a man a ladder to the parapet that no sentry watching the gate would think to guard, because no one guards the back of his own building. The fog was a gift tonight, but it would not always be a gift; whoever planned this would need a night when the fog was *thick*, thick enough that the semaphore's relief signals couldn't be read from the inner batteries even if the mast still stood. Which meant they wouldn't need to destroy the mast at all. They would only need the keeper's room, and the two minutes it took to jam the signal gears, or better, to work them — to send false signals, to call ships onto the shoal, to turn the harbor's own voice into a liar's mouth.

Felix's pen hovered over the page, and for a moment she could not make it move. It was one thing to read a conspiracy in a dead man's letter. It was another to stand in the cold and *design* it — to feel how easily her mind fell into the conspirators' shape, how naturally a mapmaker's eye found the weak seams in anything built by men. Perrin must have felt this too, she thought. He had drawn this harbor so long, loved it so precisely, that he knew everywhere it could be made to bleed. Perhaps that was why the letter existed at all. Not because he wanted to betray the families — he had known them, eaten at their tables, drawn their houses into his charts — but because he could not unknow what love had taught him.

A boot scraped on granite.

Felix went still, the notebook closed against her chest, and became, in the space of one heartbeat, part of the sea wall: shoulders against the stone, head down, the instrument case at her feet looking for all the world like a workman's kit abandoned for a moment's rest. The scrape came again — closer, on the parapet above her, the seaward sentry making his round, his boots ringing dull through the fog. She counted his steps without meaning to. Twelve to the corner. Pause. Twelve back. A soldier bored past patience, dragging his heels.

Then the boots stopped directly overhead.

"Who's there?"

The voice was young. That was the first thing she registered, absurdly — young, and cracked at the edges, and frightened underneath its authority in exactly the way she was frightened underneath hers. Fog made liars of distances; he couldn't have seen her. But he had heard the pen. Of course he had heard the pen. In a silence like this, nib on paper sounded like a mouse in a wall.

"Surveyor," she called up, keeping her voice low and flat and male, pitched to carry just far enough. "Guild work. Chart corrections." She held up the notebook as if he could see it, and after a moment's consideration, struck a match and lit the little storm lantern at her feet — because a man hiding does not light a lamp, and the light was worth more than the darkness now.

Boots on stairs. The sentry came around the corner of the station at a walk that tried very hard to be a march, rifle sloped, greatcoat buttoned wrong at the throat. Up close he was eighteen at most, cheeks chapped raw, a farm boy's face under the cap brim — from upcountry somewhere, by the accent, a boy who had never seen a harbor until the army showed him one. He looked at Felix, at the lantern, at the case, and his rifle shifted uncertainly.

"Papers."

She had prepared for this too. The guild credential was genuine — Ozenne's own seal, obtained months ago under color of routine archive delivery, one of the few kindnesses the guildmaster had ever done her without knowing its worth. She handed it over and watched him turn it in his mittened fingers, lips moving over the words, and understood with a lurch of pity that he was reading slowly because reading did not come easily to him.

"You're out late, citizen."

"The chart doesn't finish itself." She let a trace of weariness into her voice, the weariness of a tradesman interrupted. "Fog lifts the tide readings. I've a plate due at the archive before the week's out, and my master's dead, so it falls to me."

Something moved in the boy's face at *my master's dead* — sympathy, quick and unguarded, the look of someone who had lost someone himself. The war had made that look universal; Felix had seen it on bakers and barristers and now, apparently, sentries. He handed the credential back.

"Signal station's restricted ground. You can't survey here."

"I'm not on your ground." She pointed with her chin at the cobbles below the tide line, the strip of mole that belonged to the port authority and not to the army, a boundary she had verified in the harbor regulations that afternoon with the same care she'd give a coastline. "I'm on the port's stones, measuring the port's wall. Your gate is eleven feet above me and I haven't touched it. But if you'd rather I write to your captain and ask permission, I can do that tomorrow, and we'll both explain to the archive why the plate is late."

It was a gamble and she knew it. Bureaucracy was a wall that stopped everyone equally, including soldiers, and the boy's face flickered toward the arithmetic of reports and late plates and captains annoyed before breakfast. He was silent a long moment. The fog dripped off the semaphore arms above them with a sound like slow counting.

"Stay off the gate," he said finally. "And don't shine that light at the station. There's — orders. About lights."

"There are always orders about lights," Felix said, and was pleased at how much boredom she got into it.

He lingered anyway. That was the trouble. He lingered and looked at her, and she saw the question forming behind his chapped face — not suspicion exactly, but the vague animal unease of a boy who has been taught that strangers at night mean danger and cannot yet name what kind. She bent to her instruments, unfolding the tripod with a clatter of brass, giving him something ordinary to watch. She measured the tide mark on the sea wall. She sighted the clinometer at the station's roofline and wrote a number in the notebook, a true number, because the chart genuinely needed it, and there was a strange comfort in that — that even now, in the middle of her treachery, she was doing honest work.

"Bad night for it," the boy offered at last, conversationally, the way lonely people do.

"They're all bad nights till the news comes," Felix said.

"You think it'll come? The armistice?" His voice dropped on the word, reverent and afraid of its own hope. "My brother's been at Verdun two years. My mother writes to him every week and he writes back maybe once a month and half the letters arrive torn open with the censor's tape on them, and she sits with them under the lamp like they're scripture." He stopped, embarrassed, and shifted the rifle. "Anyway. You didn't hear that."

"I hear everything," Felix said. "It's my trade. Listening is half of surveying."

He almost smiled. Then the fog chose that moment to shift, a slow gray current moving through the gray air, and beyond the station, out over the water, the loom of the harbor lights appeared for three seconds — the green of the breakwater beacon, the white fan of the molehead — before the fog folded them away again. Three seconds of the harbor showing its face. The boy watched it hungrily, as though the lights were his brother, as though they might carry word.

And Felix, sighting along the clinometer with her cheek nearly touching the cold brass, used those three seconds for what she had come to use them for. The beacon's bearing from this point. Its elevation. The exact line of sight between the semaphore mast and the inner batteries, unobstructed, confirmed — the line that false signals could travel down, the line that the harbor's whole nervous system ran along. Eleven degrees west of north. She wrote it in shorthand only she could read, and closed the book, and her hands were steady, and she hated them a little for being steady.

*Two minutes,* she thought. That was all the plot required. Two minutes at the keeper's gear, on a fog-bound night, while the delegates' train waited on the far side of the hills. Two minutes, and the harbor would speak treason in its own voice, and no one would know until the ships were on the shoals and the signing was ash and the war had won another winter. Unless someone stood where she was standing now, in the cold, with a notebook, and wrote it all down first.

"Getting colder," the sentry said. He had decided about her; she could hear the decision settling in his voice, the decision that she was boring, and boring was safe. "You'll freeze out here."

"I'll live." She began folding the tripod. "Go warm yourself. You've done your duty and more."

He went — reluctantly, glancing back once, and she kept her eyes on her brasswork until his boots faded up the stairs and along the parapet and away. Then she sat down on the sea wall with the lantern between her knees and let the shaking start, the delayed shaking, the body presenting its bill after the mind had paid in full. She shook for a full minute, breath steaming, fingers clumsy on the clasps, and let it happen, because a surveyor who lied to herself about fear would eventually lie about worse things.

When it passed, she took out the notebook one last time and added a final entry to the private page, in the disguised hand: *Gate loose. Coal store climbable. Parapet rounds irregular — twelve steps, pause, twelve. Sentry young, tired, half-blind in fog. The station could be taken by four men in five minutes, or spoiled forever by one clerk filing a duplicate key list at the archive.* She underlined that last clause twice.

Because that was the thought the night had given her, the thought that turned terror into something harder and cleaner: the plot did not need soldiers to defeat it. It needed *paper*. A filed document. An inspection nobody had thought to demand. The harbor's defenses were already written down somewhere, waiting, inert — and the difference between safety and disaster was simply whether the right person read the right page before the wrong people acted on it.

She was a maker of pages. For six years she had made pages that ships trusted with their lives, that harbors trusted with their shapes, that no one ever questioned because the guild seal made them true. Well then. Let the seal make this true too.

Felix packed her instruments, doused the lantern, and started back along the mole with the fog closing behind her like water behind a keel. She was halfway to the shore before she saw the figure waiting where the mole met the quay — a man in a good coat, hatless despite the cold, standing under the fish-market lamp as though he had been there some time and expected to be joined.

Even at fifty yards, in fog, she knew the set of those shoulders. She had drawn them often enough, from memory, in the margins of charts where no margin should hold such things.

Émile. Waiting for her. At midnight. Outside the signal station.

Her brother smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes, and Felix understood that whatever the night had cost her already, the bill was not yet paid in full.
