## 12. Ozenne's Offer

Guildmaster Ozenne had a theory about paper, and it had served him for thirty years: all paper lies, but some of it lies more carefully than others, and the careful liars are the ones worth watching.

He was thinking about this on a raw December morning while a servant built up the fire in his office above the guildhall, and he was thinking about it still when the knock came — two soft raps, deferential, punctual to the minute. Perrin's boy. Of course he was punctual. Ozenne had chosen the hour himself, ten minutes before the boy's usual arrival at the archive stairs, and had sent the summons only the night before, and yet here he was, as though the appointment had been carved into his week like a tide table.

"Enter," Ozenne said, and did not rise.

Felix Joubert came in with the cold still clinging to him — cap under one arm, cheeks reddened, a leather tube of proofs slung across his back the way a soldier slings a rifle. Young men were always carrying things across their backs in this city now; Ozenne had stopped noticing, mostly. He noticed with this one. That was rather the point of the morning.

"Master Ozenne." A bow, correct but not servile. Ink on the fingers again — he always seemed to have ink on his fingers, as if it grew there, some lichen of the trade. "You asked to see the proofs of plate forty-one."

"I asked to see you," Ozenne said pleasantly. "The proofs are an excuse. Sit."

Something crossed the young face — quick, controlled, gone in half a second, but Ozenne had spent three decades reading faces across commission tables and bankruptcy hearings, and he knew what that something was. Not surprise. Recognition. The boy had been waiting for this meeting too, then, or at least dreading it. Good. Fear made negotiations honest; it was the confident men who lied to themselves first and to everyone else afterward.

* * *

Ozenne poured wine himself — a small ceremony, deliberate. He wanted the boy watching his hands, comfortable, cataloguing the courtesy. Courtesy was a currency and he paid it out precisely where it bought the most. He set the cup down and turned instead to the long table beneath the window, where the guild's records lay in their ledgers, and drew out the commission book for 1911.

"Perrin's last survey contract," he said, tapping the entry without reading it. He knew it by heart. "Signed eleven years ago. Co-financed — do you recall? No, of course you wouldn't, you'd have been what, fourteen? Fifteen?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Co-financed by the Joubert family. Your family. Two thousand francs toward a coastal atlas that was never delivered, because Perrin was slow, and then because Perrin was old, and then because Perrin was dead." He let that sit. "The guild has been patient about that debt."

"Master Perrin's accounts are being settled," Felix said. His voice was steady. Slightly lower than it needed to be, pitched with care, the way a man pitches his voice in a room where acoustics are against him. "Plate forty-one completes the atlas. Delivery is scheduled for—"

"I know when delivery is scheduled." Ozenne smiled and took his own chair at last, folding his hands over his stomach. Through the window behind him the harbor lay under its winter gauze, masts dissolving upward into fog, the signal station's flagpole a gray line drawn against nothing. "Sit down, Joubert. Drink your wine. I have not summoned you to discuss schedules."

The boy sat. He held the wine cup and did not drink from it — noted, filed — and looked at Ozenne with the particular blankness of a map that has been deliberately left empty. Ozenne admired that. He had always admired good workmanship, even workmanship deployed against him.

"You've done remarkable work," he said. "Perrin's hand, they'll say, when the atlas is certified. And it will be certified, because the guild will certify it, because I will instruct the board to certify it. Forty-one plates, and no one — not the naval archivists, not the admiralty's own surveyors — no one has questioned that Perrin produced them all. Do you know why?"

Felix said nothing.

"Because grief makes poor inspectors," Ozenne said. "A dead master's unfinished work is a sacred thing. Men want it finished and they want it untouched, and those two desires cancel out all doubt. You have been working inside that cancellation for months. It was well done." He raised his cup slightly, a toast to craft. "It was also noticed. By me. Some time ago."

* * *

He watched the boy's throat move. Watched the hands around the cup go very still, the way small animals go still — not calm, but calculating whether stillness would save them.

"Noticed," Felix repeated.

"That you are left-handed." Ozenne said it mildly, almost tenderly, and enjoyed the flicker of confusion — relief, even — before he drove the point home. "Perrin was right-handed. Every deposition in his early plates shows it. The later ones show a left hand learning to imitate a right — beautifully, mind you, beautifully, hatching strokes that run the wrong direction but land exactly where they should. Only a man who reads hands the way I do would see it. Or—" he leaned forward, and let his voice drop into the register he used for closing deals, "—only someone who already suspected, and went looking."

Silence. Outside, a tram bell rang somewhere down by the fish market, muffled and mournful in the fog.

"I don't know what you mean, Master Ozenne."

"No," Ozenne agreed. "You wouldn't. It's safer not to know what people mean. That's a lesson your master never learned — Perrin always knew exactly what everyone meant, and it killed him." He saw the flinch, small as a pulse beat, and understood that he had just handed the boy something: confirmation that others had wondered too, perhaps worse than wondered. He pressed on before it could be used. "Let me spare us both an afternoon of theater. I am seventy-one years old. I buried a wife and a son and I have outlived every man who ever tried to deceive me, which means I am either very wise or very lucky, and I have never believed in luck. So."

He rose, went to the iron strongbox behind the arras — an old-fashioned flourish, but flourishes worked on the young — and returned with a single sheet of paper, which he laid face-down on the table between them like a card.

"Two years ago," he said, "a hospital nurse in Rochefort wrote to the guild requesting verification of credentials. She claimed to have nursed a woman through childbirth in that town — a woman registered in the harbor parish rolls as Félicie Joubert, sister to Émile Joubert, daughter of the late Auguste Joubert. She wanted to know whether the family might acknowledge the child. Customary inquiry. These things happen in wartime." He turned the paper over. It was a fair copy, in the guild clerk's hand, of the original query — the original itself, he suspected, had long since been burned by whoever had handled it before him. "The inquiry was answered by my predecessor's clerk with a form letter stating that no person of that name appeared in the guild registry. Which was true. And technically sufficient. And entirely beside the point."

Felix looked at the page. Did not touch it. Ozenne noted the discipline of that — not touching — and admired it, and disliked admiring it.

"The name on the parish roll," Ozenne continued softly, "has a brother who walked into this guild hall four years ago calling himself Felix Joubert, apprenticed to Auguste Joubert's old associate Master Perrin, with papers signed in a hand that any notary in this city could match to a girl's school copybook within an afternoon. I have had four years to make that afternoon happen. I have chosen not to. I would like you to consider why."

* * *

The boy — the woman, he corrected himself, the word mattered now that it was spoken between them, even unspoken-aloud — the woman lifted her eyes, and for the first time dropped the performance entirely, and Ozenne found himself looking at someone considerably more formidable than an apprentice.

"Why haven't you?" she asked. Her voice had come down out of its careful register. It was a good voice. It deserved better work than it had been given.

"Because the guild needs Perrin's atlas more than it needs your ruin," Ozenne said simply. "Because a scandal in the middle of an armistice would drown this whole harbor in military tribunals, and tribunals are bad for shipping, and shipping is the guild. Because—" he spread his hands, "—because competence deserves protection. I have always protected competence. Ask anyone I have ruined; they will tell you I only ever ruined fools."

"And what does protection cost?"

Ah. There it was. Not *am I discovered* — she had accepted that in the space of a breath — but *what is the price*. Ozenne felt something very like respect, and banked it carefully, because respect was another currency and he did not spend it for free.

"A letter," he said.

The stillness that came over her then was total. Even the fog outside seemed to pause against the window glass.

"I don't know what you—"

"Yes, you do," Ozenne said, gently, terribly. "Perrin was my friend for thirty years, and in thirty years he told me everything except the things that mattered, which he kept the way misers keep coin — hidden so well that only death could make him spend them. He died with something on his mind. I sat with the body, girl; I have seen a hundred men die and I know the expression of one who has carried a secret past the point where carrying it became the whole of him. And then his studio goes quiet, and his apprentice starts refusing visitors, and a certain widow of a certain family begins making calls at hours that widows do not usually make calls." He watched her absorb that — Madame Roux, he meant; let her wonder how much he knew. "I don't need to read the letter. I only need to know it exists, and to hold it. Or to hold whoever holds it."

"And do what with it?" The question came out flat, hard, stripped of apprenticeship altogether.

"What the guild has always done with leverage," Ozenne said. "Trade it. There are three families in this city — I won't insult you by pretending I don't know their names — who believe the armistice can be broken like a promise, and there are men in this government who believe the same, and whichever way the signing falls, someone will owe a great deal to whoever holds proof of what was attempted. If the plot fails, the letter damns traitors and the guild stands beside the Republic. If the plot succeeds—" he shrugged, a small, economical movement, "—the letter becomes a receipt for services rendered. Either way, the atlas gets certified. Either way, a certain apprentice keeps her trade, her name, and her neck. Either way, the guild survives, which is the only duty I have ever sworn."

* * *

She stood up. She did it slowly, deliberately, taking back the height the bow had surrendered, and Ozenne stayed seated and let her, because there were moments when standing was itself a move on the board and he preferred to see how she played it.

"You're asking me to hand you a weapon," she said, "that points at my own family."

"I'm asking you to give it to a man who knows how to keep weapons from going off," Ozenne said. "Consider the alternative. You keep it. You carry it through three checkpoints to men who hang people for less than what's sewn into your charts. You are one searched parcel from the scaffold, one suspicious inspector, one word out of Madame Roux's terrified mouth. Alone, that letter is a noose with your name on it and your brother's. With me, it is insurance. I am offering to be the wall between you and everything in this city that wants you dead, and you are looking at me as though I were the thing that wants you dead." He permitted himself a dry smile. "Which suggests you are not a fool. Fools always take the wall for the cage."

"Isn't it?" she said quietly.

And there it was — the whole negotiation inverted in six words, and Ozenne sat with it for a moment longer than he intended, feeling the cold come off the window glass and into the room. He thought, absurdly, of his son, dead at Verdun eight years now, who had once looked at him across a supper table with exactly that expression and said, *Father, you don't collect friends, you collect debts,* and had been right, and had died anyway, debts and all.

"The cage and the wall," Ozenne said, rising at last, "are the same object, mademoiselle. The difference is whether the door locks from your side or mine." He extended his hand toward the door, courteous to the end. "You needn't answer today. Deliveries keep their own schedule, and yours is close — I know the date as well as you do. But understand me clearly, because I will not offer twice: whatever you decide, I will know what you decided. I have known everything else."

She paused at the door, cap in her hands, ink-stained fingers white-knuckled around the brim. For a moment she looked younger than her years, and colder than the fog, and Ozenne understood suddenly that he had made an error he had not made in thirty years of reading people: he had assumed fear was the lever, when for this one, fear had stopped working long ago. Something else moved her. He did not yet know what. That ignorance displeased him more than the refusal had.

"You're right about one thing, Master Ozenne," she said. "You won't offer twice. Because I won't need to hear it again to refuse it."

The door closed behind her, soft as falling snow.

Ozenne stood a long while by the window, watching the fog take her — a small figure with a survey tube on its back, moving down the quay toward the ink gate, toward the checkpoints, toward whatever she imagined lay beyond his reach. He drank the last of the wine and set the cup down with a click.

"She'll burn it," murmured the servant, clearing glasses.

"She won't," Ozenne said. "People who burn letters sleep well, and that one hasn't slept since November." He turned back to the harbor, where the signal station's flag hung becalmed in the murk, and began, slowly, to plan what he would do if she proved harder to hold than he had priced. "No. She'll carry it. And when she carries it, we shall see which of us the harbor belongs to."

He was still standing there when the first bell of noon rang out over the water — and stopped, mid-peal, and did not finish.
