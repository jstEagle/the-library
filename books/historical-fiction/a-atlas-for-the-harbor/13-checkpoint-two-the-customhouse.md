## 13. Checkpoint Two: The Customhouse

The customhouse smelled of tar and wet rope and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by too many frightened hands.

Felix had chosen her vantage with a cartographer's care. There was a stack of empty herring barrels against the seaward wall, and behind them a gap of shadow deep enough to hold a thin man in a coat — thinner than she would have liked, but she had been eating less these weeks, and hunger had its uses. From there she could see the whole choreography of the checkpoint: the long counter where parcels were laid open, the scales with their brass pans gone green at the rims, the two inspectors who worked the morning shift, the soldier who stood at the far end of the quay with his rifle slung and his attention on gulls.

She had come before dawn, when the fog still lay on the harbor like a sheet drawn up to the chin, and she had watched for three hours while the city's small commerce passed under inspection. Bolts of cloth. A coffin bound for a village upriver, its contents — if any — not questioned; grief was one of the few cargoes that moved freely now. Sacks of flour, each one stabbed with a long steel probe that came out trailing dust. A woman with a hamper of eggs who argued, wept, and paid.

Felix drew it all. Not on good paper — she used the backs of old proofs, Perrin's failed plates, the harbor drawn wrong until he'd gotten it right — but she drew it as she would draw anything she meant to know completely. The counter. The scales. The door behind the inspectors through which seized goods went and did not return. The stove in the corner, black iron, radiating a circle of warmth that the inspectors drifted toward between examinations like moths that had learned moderation.

And she noted what could not be drawn but only understood: that the tall inspector, Moutier, was thorough and slow and proud of both. That the short one, whose name she didn't yet have, was quick and careless before noon and suspicious after, as though doubt were something he ate at lunch. That the probe went into every sack of flour but not into anything sealed with wax bearing an official stamp, because wax impressed with authority was, in this winter of 1918, more sacred than scripture.

That was the weakness. Not the men — the *belief*. A customs house trusted its own seals the way a church trusted its own saints. Felix wrote this in the margin of her proof, in the tiny compressed hand she used for field notes, and felt the cold satisfaction that was the nearest thing she had to comfort anymore.

Then she made the parcel.

* * *

It was nothing much. A roll of drafting linen, genuinely hers, genuinely blank — or near enough; she had washed an old practice chart back to ghost. She wrapped it in brown paper, tied it with the plain twine that no one official would use, and addressed it in a disguised hand to a shipping agent on the rue des Salines whose name she had taken from a waybill blowing along the quay. Inside, sewn into the hem of the linen where a letter would go if she ever dared such a thing, she placed a folded slip of paper bearing four words in her own hand: *This is a test.*

If they found it, she would lose nothing but her nerve, which she could ill afford but could rebuild. If they didn't find it —

Then she would know the shape of the hole in the wall.

She joined the queue with her collar up and her shoulders rounded into the stoop she wore in public, the stoop that said *I am nobody, I am tired, I am a clerk's shadow*. The fog had burned off to a grey gauze, and the light came down flat and sourceless, erasing shadows, flattening faces. Good weather for passing unseen. Bad weather for drawing. She resented it professionally.

Ahead of her a fishmonger argued about ice. The short inspector probed his crates with visible boredom. The line shuffled forward the way lines do in wartime — not moving so much as being slowly absorbed.

"Félix Joubert."

Her blood stopped, then resumed, louder.

Not the inspectors. She turned, and there — absurdly, out of all proportion to the size of the world — stood Lieutenant Arnaud Vasseur, in a different greatcoat than the one he'd worn at the ink gate, holding a leather folder, looking at her with the particular expression of a man who has just confirmed something pleasant against his expectations.

"Lieutenant." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "You've moved."

"Transferred." He said it lightly, but something crossed his face — the flicker of a man deciding how much of his life is public property. "They're spreading us thin. Every gate, every quay." He glanced past her at the line, then back, and lowered his voice by a degree that was either courtesy or conspiracy. "Are you delivering?"

"Charts. For the archive survey." She lifted the decoy parcel slightly. "Routine."

"Nothing's routine lately." But he stepped aside rather than forward, and gestured her toward the counter with a motion so nearly courteous that Moutier looked up from his scales and frowned at it. "Go on. I'll watch you clear."

*I'll watch you clear.* She heard the sentence twice — once as kindness, once as surveillance — and understood that with this man they might always be the same sentence. That was the trouble with him. He made watching look like warmth.

She laid the parcel on the counter.

Moutier turned it over once with thick fingers. "Contents?"

"Drafting linen. Blank."

"Blank linen going to a shipping agent." His eyes came up, flat and grey as the morning. "Odd trade."

"The archive buys in bulk. The agent warehouses it." The lie arrived without rehearsal, dressed and shod, and Felix hated how well it fit. Perrin would have said a lie should be drafted like a chart — every line consistent with every other line, no ornament, no flourish. She had learned from a dead man how to do this cleanly. It was not a skill she wanted to be grateful for.

Moutier weighed the parcel. He turned it again. His thumb ran along the twine, and Felix felt the hem of the linen inside like a heartbeat, the four words folded against their own discovery.

Then the short inspector called him away — a dispute over the fishmonger's ice, escalating — and Moutier set the parcel down, stamped the paper with the customhouse seal without opening it, and waved her through.

The seal. The sacred wax. The belief in the house's own saints.

"Thank you," she said to no one in particular, and took her parcel, and walked.

Behind her she heard Arnaud's voice, mild and unhurried: "Moutier. You didn't open it."

"It's linen, Lieutenant. It weighs what linen weighs."

"Open it anyway."

A pause. The sound of twine being cut. Paper tearing. Then Moutier's voice, grudging: "Linen. Blank."

"And the hem? These rolls are stitched at the ends. Feel the stitching."

Another pause, longer. Felix kept walking, at the pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be, while her pulse performed its own private insurrection. She reached the corner of the quay. She heard:

"...stitching's fine. Nothing in it."

A beat. Then Arnaud, closer now, calling after her — and she had to stop, because stopping was innocent and running was confession:

"Monsieur Joubert!"

She turned.

He came toward her holding the rewrapped parcel, and there was something in his face she couldn't file under any heading she owned. Not suspicion exactly. Curiosity wearing suspicion's uniform, or the other way around.

"You stitch your own rolls?" he asked.

"I stitch everything. Good work keeps charts flat." She took the parcel. Their fingers didn't touch, but the space between them was charged the way air is before weather. "You have a cartographer's instincts, Lieutenant."

"My father sold rope." He shrugged, and the shrug contained a whole biography he wasn't going to tell her. "Rope teaches you to feel for what's hidden in a coil. Knots lie. Weight doesn't."

"That's the first honest rule of inspection I've heard at this gate."

"You've inspected many gates?"

"Professionally," she said, "I inspect everything."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, as though the laugh had gotten out before the officer could catch it. And then, because the morning was flat and grey and neither of them seemed able to end it, he fell into step beside her, and she let him, and together they walked the length of the quay like two people with nothing better to do, while behind them the customhouse went on weighing the war's small debris.

* * *

"You're from here," he said, after a silence that had stopped feeling awkward somewhere around the second bollard. "The harbor. You draw it like someone who knows where the bodies of drowned streets are."

"There are drowned streets?"

"Every old harbor has them. My grandmother swore half the rue Basse sank in 'sixty-one and they just built over the doors." He looked out at the water, at the moored hulls sleeping in their reflections. "I'm from inland. Vineyard country. Everything there is measured in rows. You can see the whole shape of your life from the top of the hill, and it looks like — stripes."

"And you preferred fog?"

"I preferred not knowing." He said it simply, without self-pity, which made it worse somehow. "In the vineyards, everyone knows everything about everyone since before anyone was born. Here—" he gestured at the harbor, the cranes, the fog-thinned skyline, "—here a man can be new every morning. Or that's what I thought at nineteen."

"And now?"

"Now I think the fog just moves the horizon. It doesn't remove it." He glanced at her sidelong. "Your master Perrin. They say he was the best eye on this coast."

"They say correctly."

"They say also that he died suddenly, and that his apprentice finished what he started, and that the guild can't decide whether to be impressed or insulted." Arnaud's tone stayed light, but she felt the question inside it, patient as a probe in a sack of flour. "Which are you hoping for?"

"I'm hoping for certification. Impressed or insulted, certified pays the same."

"Spoken like a man who's hungry."

"Spoken like a man who's honest."

He stopped walking. They had reached the breakwater's root, where the stones began, and beyond them the water worked patiently at the harbor mouth. Somewhere a buoy bell clanked, irregular, like a clock with doubts.

"Monsieur Joubert." He turned to face her fully, and the flat light made his face hard to read, which she appreciated; she was tired of reading faces accurately. "At the ink gate you stared at me for eleven seconds. I counted."

"Did you."

"I count things. Rope habit." His eyes held hers, and there was no accusation in them, only that same unbearable curiosity, and underneath it something warmer that he was clearly pretending was professional. "Eleven seconds is a long time to stare at a stranger, monsieur. Either you were memorizing my face, or you were mapping me. And I've been trying to work out which, because — frankly — I've done some staring of my own since."

The fog chose that moment to lift its hem, and sun came through in a single pale blade, laying a stripe of light across the stones between them like a border drawn on disputed territory.

"Mapmakers memorize faces," Felix said carefully. "It's a professional deformation. Everyone becomes contour lines eventually."

"Is that what I am? Contours?"

"Elevation changes," she heard herself say, and then wished the harbor would open and take her, because it sounded like flirtation, and worse, it *was*.

He smiled. Not the officer's smile — the other one, the nineteen-year-old who'd fled the vineyard stripes. "When the atlas is delivered," he said, "and everything's simpler — will you survey me properly? Draw the coast as it actually runs?"

"Everything will be simpler," she agreed, and heard Perrin in her memory, silent in his grave, declining to contradict her. "After the signing. When there's peace to map."

"After the signing," he repeated, and offered his hand, and she took it, and his grip was dry and warm and careful, the grip of a man accustomed to handling things that could break or explode.

She walked home with the blank linen under her arm and the test slip still sewn undetected in its hem, and she told herself the heat in her face was the exercise.

* * *

In the studio she unrolled the linen, unpicked the stitches, and read her own four words back to herself: *This is a test.* The ink had creased where the fold had pressed it. She fed it to the candle and watched it curl into a black petal and go.

So. The customhouse opened parcels only when pushed; the seal trumped suspicion; Moutier yielded to distraction; and Vasseur — Vasseur saw stitching. Saw weight against appearance. Counted seconds. If she carried the letter through that gate sewn anywhere obvious, he would find it, and finding it, he would have to choose, and she refused to make him choose. That thought arrived unbidden and she examined it with alarm, the way one examines a crack in a foundation stone.

No. If she went through Checkpoint Two, she would go through with the letter hidden where even rope-honed fingers wouldn't think to feel — rolled into the spine of the atlas itself, in the binding, in the one place in the world that belonged to her trade and therefore to her.

She sat down at the drafting table and pulled Perrin's plate forty-one toward her, the final plate, unfinished, waiting. The decorative border waited too, its scrollwork half-drawn, its coordinates — she knew now, she had known since midnight three nights ago — threaded through the flourishes like veins through a leaf.

Outside, the fog was closing in again over the harbor, and somewhere beyond it, in rooms she would never enter, men were deciding whether the century would begin with peace or with fire.

And below her window, unhurried footsteps paused — just for a moment, just long enough — before moving on.

She did not look up. But her hand, resting on plate forty-one, went very still, like a hand over a heartbeat that isn't sure it wants to be found.
