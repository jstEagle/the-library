## 16. A Room Above the Chart Shop

He came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the fog had begun to think about lifting and hadn't yet committed to it.

Felix heard the knock from the drafting room and knew before she opened the door that it would be him. Not because of anything so foolish as premonition — because of the knock itself. Guild messengers knocked twice, quick and impatient, as though time were money they were spending on her behalf. The woman who sold milk at the corner didn't knock at all; she simply called up the stairwell in a voice like a gull's. But this knock was three beats, measured, almost apologetic, the knock of a man who had rehearsed it on the stairs.

Lieutenant Arnaud Vasseur stood on the landing with his cap under his arm and an official paper in his hand, and behind him the harbor lay wrapped in its winter wool, masts dissolving into white, the whole city holding its breath for news from the north.

"Mademoiselle—" He stopped. Corrected himself with visible effort. "Monsieur Joubert."

The slip hung between them like a dropped instrument. Felix watched him hear it, watched the color climb his neck, and did something she had never done in four years of passing: she let it go unremarked. A wiser woman would have bristled, made a joke of it, nailed the lid down tight. Instead she stood in her doorway with ink to the second knuckle and said, "You'd better come up. It's cold enough out there to freeze the varnish."

* * *

The room above the chart shop was not designed for guests. It held Perrin's old worktable, now hers; two chairs, one of which had been built for a man considerably wider than either of them; the iron stove that ate coal like a debt collector; and shelves upon shelves of rolled charts, their brass caps catching what little light came through the salt-hazed window. She cleared a space among the proofs of plate thirty-eight, made tea in the dented pot Perrin had used for forty years, and was acutely aware of every object in the room as though she had never seen them before — aware, most of all, of how much of herself was written into them, if anyone knew how to read.

Arnaud sat carefully, the way soldiers sit in civilian rooms, as though the furniture might be mined. He set the official paper on the table between them.

"The archive wants confirmation of delivery dates," he said. "For the atlas. There's a form." He said the word *form* with the weariness of a man apologizing for bureaucracy the way one apologizes for weather. "I volunteered for the errand."

"You volunteered."

"I requested the inspection detail some days ago. This is — adjacent to it." He looked at his hands, then at her, and the honesty in his face was almost indecent. "The truth is I wanted to see where the charts are born. I've been looking at your master's work since I was posted here. The soundings off the breakwater — there's one, a deep pocket just past the third buoy, that no other surveyor ever found. It saved my patrol boat twice last spring. I wanted to know what kind of man finds a thing like that."

"Perrin didn't find it," Felix said, before she could stop herself. "I did."

The silence that followed had a texture to it, like wet silk. She heard what she had said, heard how it sounded — an apprentice claiming credit, a boy boasting — and moved quickly to bury it. "That is. I ran the lead line that season. Master Perrin drew the plate, but the finding of a sounding is the surveyor's, not the draftsman's. That's the rule of the trade. The hand that holds the line owns the depth."

"And you held the line."

"Someone had to. He was sixty and his knees were shot." She poured the tea. Her hands were steady. Her heart was behaving like a fish in a bucket.

Arnaud turned his cup slowly, watching the surface. "My father was a wine merchant," he said. "In Cahors. He believed everything worth knowing could be learned from the river Lot — where it ran shallow, where it lied about its depth. I used to think that was just something fathers said." He glanced up. "Then I started reading charts, and I understood he'd been talking about cartography his whole life without knowing it."

"What did he make of you joining the navy?"

"He made of it what fathers make of everything: he worried, and he paid for my kit anyway." A small smile, quickly folded away. "He died in March. The influenza took him and my mother inside a week."

"I'm sorry."

"So am I. Though mostly I'm sorry that I wasn't there. One is always somewhere else when the important things happen. That seems to be the design of the war." He said it lightly, but Felix heard the weight underneath, the same weight she carried in her own chest — the weight of a man who had spent years being told that history happened elsewhere, at the front, in the ministries, in rooms with better furniture than his.

She should have kept the conversation in safe water. She knew that even then. Instead she found herself saying, "My father died in '15. At Verdun, officially. Unofficially—" She stopped. The unofficial version lived in a sealed letter sewn into the backing of an old chart three feet from where he sat, and she could feel it the way one feels a tooth that has begun to ache.

"Unofficially?" he prompted gently.

"He drowned. Taking soundings off the Île de Ré in bad weather. The navy recorded it as a casualty of the war because it was tidier that way, and my family let them, because the pension was tidier too." It was a lie shaped exactly like the truth, which was the only kind of lie she permitted herself anymore, and she hated how easily it left her, hated more how easily he received it.

"He died doing the work," Arnaud said quietly. "There are worse things."

"There are no worse things," Felix said, "and that's the trouble."

* * *

The afternoon went soft around them. The fog outside thickened again until the window became a wall of pearl, and the stove ticked and settled, and they talked the way people talk when neither of them will admit they are talking about anything but themselves.

He asked about the atlas — how many plates, how long the survey had taken, whether the guild would certify it. She answered with the fluency of long rehearsal, and every fluent answer was a small stone laid over something buried. Yes, the plates were nearly done. No, she had no family in the city besides — she hesitated, and chose — a brother, occupied with shipping interests. Yes, she intended to keep the studio on after certification. When he asked why a young man would chain himself to a dying trade in a dying city, she gave him the answer she had given the guildmaster, the answer she had given herself: because the harbor changes every year and someone must write it down, because a chart is a promise made to strangers who will never meet you, because —

"Because it's the only honest work left," she finished, and heard the irony land somewhere below her own ribs.

"That's why I requested the inspection detail," Arnaud said. "Did I tell you? Not the errand — the detail. Someone has to verify the atlas against the archive's holdings before it goes forward to the delegation. They gave it to me because nobody else wanted the paperwork." He smiled. "I suspect I'll be the only man in the city who has read every plate of it."

Something cold walked down Felix's spine wearing warm gloves. *Every plate.* Plate forty-one, with its decorative border of compass roses and sea-monsters, its hidden coordinates stitched into the scrollwork like a confession written in a language only she could read. If he read closely — if he compared — 

But no. Even Perrin had needed the letter to make the meaning plain. The border was a lock, and the letter was the key, and Arnaud would hold only the lock.

"You'll be thorough," she said. It came out flat, almost accusatory, and she softened it with a smile she did not feel. "Perrin would have liked that. He said a chart unverified is a rumor with good manners."

"A chart unverified is a rumor with good manners," he repeated, tasting it. "I'm going to steal that. I'll attribute it to a wise apprentice I know."

They were quiet for a moment. In the quiet, Felix became aware of a dangerous fact: she was happy. Not the fierce, guarded satisfaction of finished work, but the plain animal happiness of sitting across a table from someone whose attention felt like sunlight through glass — warming without burning. It terrified her more than any checkpoint. Love, she thought, was a complication she had never budgeted for, an expense item that appeared on no ledger, and she was a woman who had survived four years by accounting for everything.

"You're staring at me," Arnaud said.

"You're sitting in my master's chair."

"It's a good chair."

"It's a terrible chair. It's trying to kill you. Move left."

He moved left, laughing, and the laugh did something to the room — loosened it, warmed it, made the fog outside seem less like a shroud and more like a curtain drawn for privacy. And Felix, watching him laugh, made the mistake of imagining, for one full unguarded second, a life in which she was Félicie Joubert in fact and not merely in the privacy of her own skull, in which the chair was terrible and the tea was weak and none of it mattered because nothing was hidden.

Then the stove popped, and the second passed, and she was Felix again, and the letter was still three feet away, sewn into its chart like a knife into a sheath.

* * *

The light failed by degrees, the way it does in fog — not a sunset but a dimming, the world turned down like a lamp. Neither of them moved to light the overhead lamp. The dark was companionable. That was the trouble with it.

"Tell me about Cahors," she said. "You've told me about the river and the wine. Tell me something true."

He considered. "There's a bridge there, the Pont Valentré, with a tower the devil is supposed to have built. The mason made a bargain with him — the devil carries stones, the mason gets his bridge. And the mason tricked him at the end, sent him for water in a sieve, and the devil in fury adds a stone to the tower every night, so the bridge can never be finished." He shrugged. "When I was a boy I thought it was a story about the devil. Now I think it's a story about unfinished things. About how everything we build has one stone in it that doesn't belong to us."

"What stone doesn't belong to your bridge?" she asked, and immediately wished she hadn't, because it was too perceptive, too much the question of someone with secrets of her own, and he looked at her a moment too long in the gathering dark.

"Duty," he said finally. "Duty is the stone the devil carries. You can't build without it, but you never stop wondering whose design it serves." Then, lightly, turning it away: "And yours? What's your unborrowed stone?"

"My brother," she said, before she could choose a safer answer. And then, because the truth was already halfway out and lying on top of it would be like laying fresh snow over a corpse: "He's not a bad man. Whatever he's mixed up in. He's a man who loved our father too much and our father's memory more, and memory is a country with no honest maps. Everyone draws it larger than it was."

Arnaud was silent. Outside, somewhere down toward the water, a ship's bell rang the hour, muffled to almost nothing by the fog.

"Monsieur Joubert," he said — and this time he did not stumble on the name, which somehow made it worse — "may I say something that isn't in any regulation?"

"You may."

"When the armistice comes — and it's coming, everyone can smell it, the whole city smells like a house before company arrives — everything will change. The checkpoints, the restrictions, all of it. Everything will be simpler." He paused. "I'd like to call on you then. Properly. Not with a form."

Felix's heart performed a maneuver no naval vessel could match. *Everything will be simpler.* She thought of the letter, of Émile's signature on the pledge, of the three checkpoints standing between her and the truth like the towers of some modern Valentré, each with its own devil carrying stones. After the armistice. If the armistice came. If the signal station still stood. If the men her father had signed with — the men her brother had signed with — did not burn the harbor's eyes out first.

"Yes," she said. "After. Everything will be simpler then."

It was the largest lie she had ever told him, and it tasted like a promise, and she hated herself for how gently she delivered it, and hated him — not him, never him, the situation, the war, the century — for believing her.

* * *

He stayed until full dark, and then a little past it, and left with the form signed and the tea gone cold and the fog swallowing him at the bottom of the stairs. Felix listened to his boots fade on the cobbles and stood in the doorway breathing the cold until her lungs ached.

Then she went inside, lit the lamp, and sat down at the worktable. Three feet away, in the backing of the old chart, the letter waited with the patience of all dead men's words.

She had told him everything would be simpler after the armistice. She had watched him believe it, and she had understood, sitting in the lamplight with his cup still warm beside her hand, that belief was a thing she could spend — that his trust was currency, and she was about to counterfeit with it on a scale that would hang him if she were caught.

She pulled the old chart from the shelf and laid it on the table, her fingers finding the seam of the backing by touch alone.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would decide what to do with it. Burn it, or carry it. Spare her brother, or betray her master. But even as she thought it, she knew the decision had already been made somewhere beneath her knowing, the way a tide has already turned while the surface still looks calm — and that tomorrow she would sit down at this table with a candle, and the candle would ask her the only question that mattered.
