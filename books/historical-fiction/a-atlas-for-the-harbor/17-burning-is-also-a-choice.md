## 17. Burning Is Also a Choice

The candle was the work of a coward's imagination: small, tallow-smelling, its flame no larger than a thumb. Felix had chosen it deliberately. A man about to destroy evidence that could hang three families would want, she reasoned, a proper fire — logs in the grate, a blaze worth the name. But she was not going to give herself the dignity of a bonfire. If the thing was to be done, it would be done meanly, quietly, with a flame she could smother between two fingers if her nerve failed.

She sat down at Perrin's drafting table at half past eleven, when even the insomniacs of the harbor district had banked their lamps, and unrolled the old chart of the southern roads with its split backing, and took out the letter, and set it beside the candle.

It looked so small. That was the first obscenity of it — that a document capable of unmaking everything should weigh less than a sheet of plate paper, should sit on the table among her dividers and her burnisher as though it belonged there, one more tool of the trade.

*To whom it may concern,* it began, in Perrin's upright hand, the hand she had imitated for three years until her own letters had forgotten their native slant. She had read it four times now, and each reading had left a different bruise. The first time she read it as evidence. The second time she read it as accusation. The third time she read only Émile's signature at the foot of the attached pledge, the familiar downstroke of the *É*, the way he pressed too hard on his final consonants the way their father had, and she had to put the letter face-down on the table and breathe at the ceiling beams for a quarter of an hour.

Tonight would be the fourth reading. Tonight she would decide.

*Burn it or carry it.* She said the words aloud, to hear whether they sounded like a choice or like the two halves of a wound.

The stove had gone cold hours ago. The studio kept its own weather now that winter had settled into the city's bones — a damp that came up through the floorboards from the harbor itself, as if the sea were trying to reach the charts and reclaim them, water calling to its oldest portraitist. Her breath made a faint ghost in the candlelight. Somewhere out in the fog a buoy bell tolled, patient and idiotic, marking a rock no ship could see.

Felix pulled her stool closer and smoothed the letter flat with both hands, the way she would smooth a creased survey sheet, and made herself think like a cartographer, because cartography was the only discipline she trusted. A mapmaker's first law: *do not draw what you wish were there.* Draw the coast as it is, however cruel the coastline.

So. The coast as it was.

If she burned the letter, the plot went forward. Three families — the Roux money, the Delaunay connections, and the Joubert name, her name, Émile's signature under her father's portrait — would move against the signal station at whatever hour the armistice delegation came within sight of the harbor, and either they would succeed and the negotiations would collapse in blood and recrimination, or they would fail and be caught, and the letter's absence would change nothing except that the tribunal would have one fewer exhibit. In neither case would she have saved anyone. Burning it bought silence, not safety. She understood that now; she had spent two nights understanding it. The fire would not erase Émile's signature from the pledge copy the others held. It would only erase Perrin's warning — the one voice that had tried, dying, to shout across the water.

And yet.

She picked up the corner of the letter and held it toward the flame, not close enough to catch, just close enough to feel the heat press against her knuckles, and let herself imagine it honestly, because honesty was the whole point of the exercise.

Imagine the letter gone. Imagine the ash folded into the stove cinders, the chart resewn, the shelf restored. Imagine waking tomorrow with nothing in her possession but an atlas nearly finished and a brother still living in the ordinary sense of the word — not spared by intervention, merely unprosecuted. She would never have to stand before a checkpoint officer with treason sewn into her coat. She would never have to choose between the truth and Arnaud Vasseur's careful hands. Madame Roux's offer — the money, the soft-voiced plea, *think of the survivors, think of the wives* — would simply become the future, accepted by other means. And Émile would come to the house at Christmas if there was a Christmas, and he would put his hand on her shoulder the way he did, heavy and awkward, and call her *little brother* in front of the neighbors, and none of it would ever have cracked open.

It was a good life, that imaginary one. She held it up to the light and turned it, the way she'd turn a fine brass instrument, looking for the flaw.

The flaw was Perrin.

Not Perrin the dead man, exactly — Perrin the mapmaker. Because here was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that made the candle an interrogation rather than a temptation: Perrin had not written this letter to save himself. He had written it knowing he might not live to send it. He had sewn it into the backing of a superseded chart — the southern roads, a plate no one had ordered since '14 — which meant he had chosen his hiding place the way he chose all his hiding places, for permanence rather than access. He had not wanted it found quickly. He had wanted it found *rightly.* Found by whoever finished the atlas. Found by hands that understood what a document was for.

And underneath the letter, folded into the same seam, she had found the scrap of tracing paper — she had not told anyone about the tracing paper — on which Perrin had drawn, in the shorthand of his margins, a single line of bearings running from the harbor mouth to the signal station on the headland, annotated with a tide table and a time: the exact window in which the station's watch changed and its lamp went dark for eleven minutes. He had solved the conspiracy's geometry before they had finished arranging it. He had known where the blow would fall, and when, and he had died with the knowledge sewn shut against his knowledge's own spine.

To burn the letter was to burn the bearings. To burn the bearings was to finish what the conspirators had started when they arranged — she still did not know how, and might never know — the accident on the wet stairs at the dockside warehouse. They had killed the man. Burning the letter would kill the map.

"You made me your instrument," she said to the empty studio, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You might have asked."

But he *had* asked. That was the terrible courtesy of it. Every year of apprenticeship, every *again, boy, and this time mind the graticule*, every hour he had stood behind her shoulder breathing pipe smoke while she hatched coastlines until her eyes swam — all of it had been the asking, and all of it had been her answering yes.

The candle guttered in some small movement of air, and the shadows of the hanging charts swung along the walls like slow-marching figures, and Felix set the letter down again, untouched.

She got up and walked the length of the studio, because the body needed somewhere to put what the mind refused to hold. Past the flat files with their drawers of rolled surveys. Past the window where the fog had erased the harbor so completely that the glass showed her only her own reflection — a thin man in a borrowed coat, candlelit, pacing. Past the little mirror she kept covered these days, and did not uncover.

Her reflection stopped at the stove, at the cold iron of it, and she rested her hand on the flue as though checking a fever.

Sparing Émile. That was the phrase she kept using, and tonight, alone, she made herself test it. Was burning the letter sparing him? Émile had signed. Émile attended the meetings in the cellar — she knew about the meetings now, had known since the summer, had told herself they were veterans' grief finding an outlet, drink and grievance and the particular fever of men who had survived a war by inventing reasons it shouldn't have ended. If the thing went wrong, Émile would hang with or without Perrin's letter. The letter could not spare him. Only she could — by getting it to someone who would stop the plot before it happened, who would arrest the planners for conspiracy rather than the survivors for treason. There was exactly one road that led to her brother living past the spring, and it ran through the letter delivered, not burned.

Burning it would spare no one. It would only spare *her*. It would spare her the checkpoints, the risk, the unbearable arithmetic of loving a lieutenant stationed against her. It would let her keep both her secret and her skin.

"There it is," she said quietly. "That's the true bearing."

The candle flame stood very straight now, as if attending.

She thought about Madame Roux's hands around the teacup, how they had trembled, and how kindness had looked sitting in that parlor wearing grief like a widow's weeds over armor. *Think of the survivors.* But the survivors had chosen. That was the sentence she had not said aloud to the widow and could not unsay to herself now. Her father had chosen too — she remembered him coming home smelling of the cellar meetings, before the war, before the sickness, before any of it, and she remembered being nine years old and proud that Papa had important friends. Children mistake conspiracies for importance. She had been doing it her whole life, perhaps.

And Perrin — Perrin had chosen the opposite. Had chosen the letter over the friendship, the truth over the harbor families, and had paid the full price without complaint, without even the vanity of leaving a confession. Just bearings. Just a warning. Just work, passed forward, the way work is always passed forward in this trade: here is the coast as I found it; correct my errors if you can; do not falsify the soundings, for men's lives ride on your lines.

Felix came back to the table. She stood over the letter with the candle lifted, and she brought them together — flame and paper, the old temptation, the clean solution — and she watched the corner begin to warm, the paper taking on that amber translucence that comes a half-second before ignition, and she held it there, held it there, giving herself every chance.

Smoke, thin as thread.

And in the smoke she saw, absurdly, plate forty-one — the final plate, the harbor mouth at dusk, which she had been finishing the evening she found the seam — and she saw that she could not do it. Not because she was brave. She examined herself for courage and found very little, found mostly fear and fatigue and an ache in her chest that had Arnaud's name on it. But because the atlas existed. Because forty-one plates of honest coastline would go to the naval archive within the fortnight, and somewhere in Perrin's decorative borders — she was almost sure now, had begun to suspect in the last days, in the compass roses and the flourished sea-serpents — the master had inked truths no tribunal could extract from him after death. The letter was not separate from the atlas. It was the atlas's key, its legend, the little box in the corner that explained the symbols. Burn the legend and the map became beautiful nonsense.

An unfinished atlas is a lie. An unfinished warning is a murder postponed.

She pinched the flame out between her fingers.

The dark rushed in, and the pain arrived a moment later, two small white crescents cooling on her thumb and forefinger, and she stood in the black studio holding an undelivered letter and her scorched fingers, laughing once — a short, unsteady sound that the fog outside had the grace to ignore.

*No,* she told the room, told Perrin, told Émile, told her own reflection somewhere in the blinded window. *I won't burn it. God help me, I'm going to deliver it.*

She relit the candle with a spill from the embers she raked in the stove, and the studio reassembled itself around her, familiar and unchanged, though nothing in it was unchanged, least of all her.

Then she sat down and began to work, because decision without method is just panic standing at attention. If the letter would travel, it must travel the way she traveled best: disguised as the trade itself. She fetched a fresh chart roller, the kind used for delivering finished plates, and measured its interior with her dividers, and measured the letter, and measured the folds a document could take and still lie flat beneath a backing sheet of linen. She sketched in pencil, on scrap, the anatomy of the deception: chart on top, letter below, stitched at the margin in a pattern that would read as repair to any eye but hers. She noted the checkpoints between the studio and the citadel — the Ink Gate at the naval yard, the Customhouse on the quay road, the citadel gate itself — and opposite each, in her neat professional hand, she wrote what she knew of its inspections, its routines, its officers.

At the third line her pen hesitated. She wrote *Checkpoint One — Lt. A. Vasseur* and then sat looking at the name, the candle burning low, the buoy bell tolling its idiot patience out in the fog.

He had caught her staring, that first day, and had not reported it. He had spoken to her about maps as though maps mattered, as though she were a colleague and not cargo to be searched. When she carried the atlas to its destination — and she understood now, with the clarity of the recently condemned, that she would carry it herself, through every gate, because no courier could be trusted with the letter and no stranger would believe the borders — she would have to pass his hands. His careful, ink-curious hands. Either he would find it and she would hang, or he would not find it and she would deceive him, and she genuinely could not say which outcome she dreaded more.

"That's a problem for another night," she said, and closed the notebook, and heard how hollow the reassurance rang.

The candle had burned down to a stub. As she pinched it out a second time — easier, this once, and done on purpose — she became aware of a sound below the window, muffled by fog: footsteps on the quay, unhurried, then stopping directly outside the shop. She stood still in the dark and listened. No knock came. After a long moment the footsteps resumed, fading toward the harbor stairs, and she told herself it was a drunkard finding his way home, and believed it about four-fifths of the way.

But in the morning, when she opened the shutters, she would find the fog lying against the glass like something waiting, and on the step below, plain in the wet stone, the print of a woman's boot — narrow-heeled, recently resoled — pointed at her door.

Madame Roux had come in the night, and stood a while in the dark, and gone away again.
