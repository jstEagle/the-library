## 18. The Widow's Warning

The fog came off the water that evening like a thing with intentions.

Marguerite Roux stood at her window on the rue des Salines and watched it pour between the rooftops, filling the streets as though the harbor itself had grown tired of holding its breath and was now exhaling over everything at once. Somewhere below, a lamplighter moved through the white murk with his pole, and his flame appeared and vanished, appeared and vanished, a small stubborn heart refusing to stop beating in all that blindness.

She knew how he felt.

Three days had passed since the girl — since *Felix*, she must remember to think of the apprentice as Felix, even alone, even here, even in the privacy of her own skull where no one could hang her for what she thought — three days since Felix Joubert had sat across this very room with her cap straight and her hands folded and refused, politely, gently, with that terrible quiet competence of hers, to destroy the letter.

*It is not mine to burn, Madame Roux.*

As though ownership were the question. As though anything in this city still belonged to anyone but fear.

Marguerite turned from the window. The fire had burned low; she fed it a log and watched the resin catch with a sound like whispered gossip. On the mantel stood Henri's portrait, painted twenty years ago when his hair was still brown and his smile still reached his eyes. He had been a customs surveyor then, a careful man, a man who believed that ledgers balanced and that nations kept their word the way tides kept their schedule. He had signed things. That was the whole of it, the whole rotten architecture of her present terror: her dead husband had signed a pledge in a cellar full of frightened important men, and his signature was now a hook set so deep in her flesh that no surgeon alive could draw it out.

They had told him it would never come to anything. A show of solidarity, they'd said. Insurance against a peace that would rob them, they'd said. Henri had come home from that cellar looking like a man who'd walked through cobwebs he couldn't brush off, and he had said, *Marguerite, if anything ever happens to me, burn my papers.* She had laughed at him. She had actually laughed, and asked what papers could possibly matter to a widow with nothing left to lose.

She was not laughing now.

* * *

The letter had been delivered to the conspirators' attention once already — or rather, word of its existence had traveled, as word does in a harbor town, along the greased channels of old friendship and older debt. Marguerite had heard it secondhand herself, from Madame Vidal, whose son-in-law drank with one of the signal-station clerks: that old Perrin, before he died, had known something; that Perrin's boy had inherited more than an atlas.

She had not known how much until Felix sat in her parlor with her ink-stained fingers laced together and told her, in essence, everything. Not the details. The shape. Enough that Marguerite had understood the house she lived in was built on a cliff edge, and the cliff was crumbling, and the girl across from her was holding the only rope.

*I will not use it against your family, Madame. I promise you that much. But I cannot pretend it doesn't exist.*

And now, three days later, Marguerite poured herself the small glass of marc she permitted herself after dark and admitted what she had been refusing to admit since the door closed behind that young woman:

Felix Joubert was going to get them all killed.

Not because she was malicious. God knew, the girl was the opposite of malicious — she was precise, which was worse. Malice blunders; precision finds you in the dark. If Felix carried that letter to whatever authority she imagined would handle it cleanly — if she walked it past soldiers, past clerks, past the great grinding machinery of military justice that was, at this moment in history, hungrier than it had ever been — then names would be read aloud. Names were read aloud these days in courtyards before rows of rifles. Henri's name among them. And after the name came the widow, and after the widow came the confiscation, the interrogation, the questions asked in rooms without windows. Her sister's children in Lyon. The little house at Bénodet she meant to die in.

Or — the other road, the one she kept trying not to look down — if Felix failed. If the conspirators found her first.

Either way, the letter was a lit fuse, and Marguerite Roux had spent sixty-one years learning that the only thing to do with a fuse was to cut it or run.

She could not run. So she picked up the scissors.

* * *

She did not go herself. That was the coward's arithmetic she performed while dressing, and she performed it honestly, hating every term of the equation and solving it anyway. A widow who called on men at night became a story by morning. But a widow who wrote a note asking Monsieur Delaunay's man to call regarding *the matter her late husband had concerned himself with* — that was merely grief seeking counsel, grief being the one currency no one begrudged a woman in black.

Delaunay. She chose him because he was the most afraid of them all, and afraid men move fastest. A shipowner with two vessels impounded and a third rotting at anchor for want of cargo, he had joined the pledge the way weak men join anything — swept along, flattering himself later that he had steered. His hands shook when he signed documents; she had seen it at the funeral, at the guild dinners, everywhere hands were visible. He would understand urgency. He would act on it before his braver colleagues could debate it into paralysis.

She wrote the note by firelight, her pen scratching like something trapped:

*Monsieur — Forgive an old woman's importunity. It concerns the papers of my late husband, and a certain apprentice who has come into possession of what should have burned with him. Time is shorter than any of us wishes. Mme H. Roux.*

She read it four times. Each reading, the sentence *what should have burned with him* looked less like discretion and more like a confession, and each time she told herself that confession was exactly what was needed — a little guilt laid on thick enough to spur them.

Then she sealed it, and gave it to her maid's nephew, a fish-boy with quick legs and no curiosity, with two francs and instructions not to be followed, which of course guaranteed that half the street watched him go.

* * *

He came at nine o'clock, Delaunay's man — not Delaunay himself, which stung, though she supposed she hadn't earned better. A lean, soft-spoken fellow named Corbier with the face of a notary and the eyes of a ferret, who removed his hat and declined her chair and stood by the fire warming hands he clearly did not need warmed.

"Madame Roux. Your note troubled Monsieur Delaunay greatly."

"It was meant to trouble him."

"He asks me to say" — Corbier smiled faintly — "that the matter is understood, and being handled."

"Handled." She let the word sit between them like a stone dropped in shallow water. "Monsieur Corbier, I am too old for euphemism. Do you understand what the apprentice has? Do you truly?"

"The pledge, madame. The correspondence."

"And the atlas." She saw his eyes flicker — a fraction, no more, but she had spent forty years reading men across customhouse counters beside Henri, and she caught it the way one catches a fish rising. "You didn't know about the atlas. Sit down, monsieur."

"I prefer—"

"*Sit down.*"

He sat. She remained standing, because standing gave her height and height gave her the only authority remaining to a woman whose entire life had been conducted in other people's sentences.

"Perrin hid coordinates inside it," she said. "I don't know precisely how — the girl said borders, decorations, some trick of the engraving — but Perrin was Perrin, and he never drew a line without meaning two things by it. The atlas is not a book, monsieur. It is a testimony. Every plate of it points at the signal station like a drawn finger. And it is due at the naval archive within the fortnight, under the apprentice's hand, certified by the guild." She paused. "Do you see? Even if you take the letter, the atlas speaks. Even if you silence the girl, the atlas sits in an archive with her name on the flyleaf, waiting for one curious clerk with a loupe and a grudge."

Corbier was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Out in the fog, a ship's bell counted the quarter-hour, muffled, as though sounding underwater.

"You are remarkably well informed, madame," he said at last, and there was something new in his voice — not gratitude. Calculation. The tone of a man re-pricing a commodity.

"I am a widow," Marguerite said, "and widows survive by knowing things. What I am *not*, monsieur, is well protected. My husband's name is on that pledge, and I will tell you plainly what I want: I want it gone. Not copied, not filed, not *leveraged* — I know there are men in your company who dream of using it as a shield. Leverage is just a slower way of hanging. Burn it, scatter them, do whatever you must, but end it, and end it soon, before the girl decides she has a conscience bigger than her self-preservation."

"She has already decided that, I think," Corbier said softly.

The words went through Marguerite like cold water through a cracked hull.

"Then you understand the urgency."

"I understand something." He rose, took up his hat, and turned it in his long fingers. "Monsieur Delaunay thanks you for your loyalty, madame. You will hear nothing further from us, which is how you will know matters have resolved."

"That is not comforting."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It seldom is." At the door he paused, half-turned, fog breathing in around him. "One question, if you'll indulge me. The apprentice — she visited you. Sat in that chair, I imagine. Talked."

"She warned me. In her fashion."

"And did she warn anyone else, do you suppose? The lieutenant, perhaps? There is talk of a young officer who takes his rounds past the chart shop rather often for a man with no interest in maps."

Marguerite felt her face perform the lie before she had chosen it — the small baffled frown, the shrug of a woman who notices nothing. Forty years of practice. "Lieutenants are young, monsieur. Young men walk where walking is pleasant. I couldn't say more than that."

"No," Corbier said. "I imagine you couldn't." And he bowed, and was gone into the fog, and the door closed behind him with the soft finality of a lid.

* * *

She slept badly, which was to say she slept in the manner of a woman lying awake who occasionally loses consciousness out of exhaustion. Toward four in the morning she dreamed of Henri — not the portrait-Henri on the mantel but the living one, young, standing on the breakwater in his surveying coat, pointing out at the shipping lanes and saying something she couldn't hear over the wind. In the dream she kept stepping closer to catch the words and the wind grew louder each step, until she woke with her heart slamming and the fire dead and the room gray-blue with the hour before light.

She lay still and made herself look at what she had done.

She had sold the girl. Not to the gallows — not necessarily, not deliberately — but she had sold her, the way one sells a horse that kicks: *here, take her, she is dangerous, deal with her.* And the men she had sold her to were not horse-dealers. They were frightened powerful men with access to sailors willing to do ugly work for coin and conviction both, and somewhere out in that fog, right now, Corbier's ferret mind was turning her information into a plan, and the plan would have Felix Joubert's name at the center of it.

*The people she's trying to save,* Marguerite thought, staring at the ceiling. *That is who will come for her. She carries their rope and they mean to take it from her and tie her with it.*

Could she warn her? Send the fish-boy again, another note — *leave the city, child, tonight, take nothing, tell no one*? She composed it in her head as she lay there, and then she heard Corbier's voice again: *there is talk of a young officer who takes his rounds past the chart shop.* They were watching the shop already. Perhaps they had been watching it before she ever wrote her note; perhaps her note had merely confirmed what they suspected and told them what they lacked. A warning sent now might arrive ahead of the knife, or it might simply prove to the knife's owners that the widow and the apprentice were in correspondence — that Marguerite Roux was not a frightened ally but a loose thread, and loose threads, in this winter of all winters, got cut.

Her hands, she noticed, had closed themselves into fists atop the coverlet, as if gripping something invisible. She opened them finger by finger.

There was still one thing. One thing she could do that required no notes, no messengers, no threads for anyone to pull. Henri's old courier credentials — his customs pass, stamped and sealed, valid for the bearer on official document transport, folded in oilcloth at the back of his desk drawer these six years because she could not bear to throw away anything with his handwriting on it. The girl would need papers before this ended. Papers to move through checkpoints. Marguerite had promised herself nothing last night; promises were beyond her now. But she could hold the pass, and wait, and pray for a moment when giving it away cost less than keeping it.

*If she lives long enough to need it.*

Marguerite rose, dressed in the dark, and knelt briefly — not quite a prayer, more an accounting — at the foot of the bed. Then she went to the window.

The fog was thinning. Across the roofs, eastward, the first light found the masts of the fleet and turned them briefly gold, and beneath them the harbor lay flat and innocent, and beyond it, invisible, the signal station stood on its headland doing its patient semaphore work, ignorant of how many people in the sleeping city below had reasons, good ones, desperate ones, to see it dark.

She watched the light spread and felt no cleaner for it. Safety, she understood now, was not a wall one hid behind; it was a tide, and she had spent last night digging the sand out from under her own feet and calling it strategy.

Somewhere down in that waking city, a girl with ink-stained fingers was rolling charts she believed would save everyone.

Marguerite pressed her forehead against the cold glass.

"Run, child," she whispered, to no one, to the fog, to the gold-tipped masts. "Run tonight. While there is still a tonight to run in."

But the harbor did not carry warnings. Only ships. And hers had already sailed.

* * *

By noon, the fish-boy's legs had done their work twice over. By two o'clock, a cart with no markings stood for a long while in the lane behind the chart shop, its driver reading a newspaper he never turned the pages of. And by dusk, when Félicie Joubert lit her lamp over plate forty-one and bent to her work, believing the worst thing coming for her was her own conscience — three men were already agreeing, in a cellar that smelled of salt wine and gun oil, that the apprentice of the late Master Perrin knew too much, held too much, and would have to be persuaded to give it up before the week was out.

Persuaded. That was the word they used.

What they actually meant was already rolling toward the rue des Salines in the back of an unmarked cart, and its name was midnight.
