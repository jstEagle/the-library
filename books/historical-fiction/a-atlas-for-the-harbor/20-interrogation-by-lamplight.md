## 20. Interrogation by Lamplight

The room they gave him was not a room so much as a refusal of one. Four walls of grey plaster, a table scarred by a decade of boot heels and knuckles, a single window so high and narrow that the fog outside seemed to have been poured into it and left there to set. A lamp stood at the table's center — a brass officer's lamp, its shade dented, its flame trimmed low — and it threw a circle of yellow light across the wood like a coin dropped into deep water.

Arnaud Vasseur had been summoned at half past six. It was now past eight, and no one had come. He understood the arithmetic of that. He had sat on the other side of enough tables to know that the waiting was not an oversight; it was the first question, and it was asked in silence, and the only wrong answer was to fidget.

So he did not fidget. He sat with his hands flat on his knees, his cap on the table before him, and he watched the flame of the lamp bend and recover, bend and recover, like something breathing. Outside, somewhere below the window, a tram bell rang and faded. The harbor fog came through the stone itself, or seemed to; the room smelled of it, of brine and coal smoke and the cold iron tang of the sea in winter.

He thought, absurdly, of ink-stained fingers.

He had been trying not to think of them for six days, and he was failing at it with the same disciplined thoroughness he brought to everything else. The apprentice from the map shop — the one who had stared at him across the barrier at the Ink Gate with an expression Arnaud had turned over in his mind a hundred times since, the way a man turns a stone in his hand looking for what's underneath. Not fear. Not the dull resentment most civilians wore like a coat in the presence of the military police. Something closer to *recognition*, as though Arnaud were a landmark she had charted long ago and was surprised to find still standing.

*She.* He caught himself at it, the pronoun, and corrected it with a small internal frown. *He.* The apprentice. Perrin's boy. Everyone said the old man had taken on a nephew, or a cousin's son, some lean relation from the country who had a gift for the pen. Arnaud had seen him twice now — once at the Ink Gate, once at the Customhouse, where they had spoken of maps and of the fog and of nothing that mattered, and where Arnaud had walked away feeling lighter than he had any right to feel in the winter of 1918, in a city holding its breath for the word *peace* like a coin held between the teeth.

The door opened without a knock.

Two men came in. The first was a captain Arnaud did not know — a heavy man with a face like a closed ledger, wearing the horizon-blue of the line with the black armband of the Intelligence Section, which was a contradiction the war had long since stopped bothering to resolve. The second was a clerk, thin as a rule, who carried a folder and a second lamp, which he set on the table so that its light crossed the first and threw Arnaud's shadow in two directions at once, as if he were already two men.

"Lieutenant Vasseur." The captain did not offer his hand. He sat, and the chair complained under him. "You'll forgive the hour. And the theater." He gestured at the lamps, the high window, the whole grey apparatus of the room. "Orders. Everything is orders this month. Sit, clerk."

The clerk sat. The folder stayed closed under his hand like a sleeping animal.

"You reported something," the captain said. "Nine days ago. A fragment from the mail interception at the Customhouse. You'll tell it again, please, from the beginning. Slowly. The clerk writes slowly."

Arnaud told it. He had told it once already, in writing, and he told it now the same way, because the facts had not changed and because he had learned in five years of service that the truth, repeated in a flat voice, was the only armor that never dented.

The Customhouse ran a mail cage — every letter leaving the city for the coast, for the interior, for the occupied territories, passed through the hands of two censors and a lamp like the one on this table. Most of what they caught was nothing: a woman's complaint about the price of coal, a soldier's lie about a medal, love letters whose only crime was bad poetry. Arnaud had drawn the evening shift twice a week because it was quiet, because it let him sit with his back to the wall and read the city's whole heart by lamplight, and because — though he had not written this in his report — it was the only hour of his day that did not feel like standing in the rain.

The fragment had come out of a letter torn open by accident — a page from a longer letter, the rest of it lost or burned or never written. The hand was careful, educated. The ink had been blotted in haste. And in the middle of a paragraph of pleasantries — inquiries after an aunt's health, complaints about the fog — three lines that had nothing to do with either:

*The harbor families will not sit idle while Paris signs away what our fathers built. When the hour comes, the station will speak first, and the city will know who kept its voice.*

Arnaud had read it three times. Then he had done what the regulations required, which was to carry it to his section chief, who had carried it to the Intelligence Section, who had — evidently, eventually — carried it here.

"The station," the captain said now, when Arnaud finished. "You understand what that means."

"The signal station. The mast on the point. Yes, sir."

"Then you understand that in eleven days the armistice delegation passes through this city, and that a certain kind of man would very much like the whole country to watch the harbor burn on the same morning Paris signs." The captain's voice was level, unhurried, almost gentle, which was the worst thing about it. "The fragment mentions *the harbor families*. Plural. Old names. Shipping money, dock leases, guild seats — the families who have owned this harbor since before there was a France to own it in. Someone in this city, Lieutenant, possibly several someones in very good coats, are planning to give the peace a funeral."

The lamp flame bent and recovered. Arnaud kept his eyes on it.

"My report is complete, sir."

"I'm sure it is. That's not why you're here." The captain opened the folder at last, and the clerk leaned in, and for a long moment the only sound was paper. "You drew the mail shift on the fourth and the eleventh. You also serve at the Ink Gate on alternating days. You requested the atlas inspection detail — I have the requisition here, in your own hand. You asked to be the one who examines the survey plates moving to the naval archive. Why?"

"Professional interest, sir." The answer was ready, because it was true, and because he had rehearsed it, and because he had learned that a rehearsed truth was more dangerous than a rehearsed lie and he had not yet decided what that made him. "My father was a hydrographer. Before the war. I grew up with charts on every wall of the house. When I saw the Perrin atlas coming through — plates for the coastal survey — it was the first thing in this city that felt like the world before. I wanted to see it finished."

"Sentiment," the captain said, without contempt, which was somehow worse than contempt. "Very well. Here is what your sentiment has purchased." He turned the folder around and slid a single sheet across the table into the crossed lamplight.

It was a list. Not a long one. Seven names, seven addresses, written in the clerk's narrow hand. Arnaud read down it and felt the room tilt very slightly, the way the deck of a ship tilts when the swell comes from a direction the helmsman wasn't watching.

The fourth name was *Joubert*. The address was the map shop. The annotation beside it, in the same narrow hand, read: *Perrin's late apprentice — see attached.*

There was no attached. There was only the word, and the lamp, and the captain watching him across the table with the patience of a man who has all night and intends to use it.

"You know the shop," the captain said. It was not a question.

"I've inspected shipments from it, sir. Twice."

"You've spoken with the apprentice."

"Once. Briefly. At the Customhouse. He was transporting survey plates under guild seal. Everything was in order."

"Everything was in order," the captain repeated, softly, tasting it. "Lieutenant, I'm going to tell you how these things go, because you're young, and because in five days you're going to be standing at a barrier while half the government of France rolls through it, and I'd rather you did that with your eyes open." He folded his heavy hands on the folder. "The fragment is one thread. We pull it, we find the families — old Roux money, old Joubert money, names I won't recite to a junior officer. We pull the Joubert thread and we find a dead mapmaker, Perrin, who drowned in October in a fog so thick the coroner couldn't say whether it was an accident, and whose studio is still operating, still shipping, still moving documents through *your* checkpoints under a boy nobody in this city seems to have met before this year. We pull that thread and we find a lieutenant with a hydrographer's father who has requested, personally, to handle everything that comes out of that shop." The captain leaned back. The chair complained again. "Do you see the shape of it, Lieutenant? From where I sit, you are either the best-placed man in this investigation or a complication in it. I haven't decided which. Help me decide."

The lamp flame bent. Recovered. Bent.

Arnaud heard himself say, "I don't understand what you're asking, sir."

"I'm asking what you saw. At the Ink Gate, at the Customhouse, in the shop if you went into the shop. I'm asking what the apprentice is. I'm asking whether a boy who appears out of nowhere the same autumn his master drowns, who finishes a dead man's atlas with a dead man's hand, who moves through military checkpoints like a man who has mapped them — I'm asking whether that is a boy, Lieutenant, or a door."

*A door.* Arnaud kept his face still, and behind the stillness something moved that he did not have a name for yet. He thought of the hands. He had looked at them — he admitted it to himself now, in the crossed lamplight, with the captain watching — he had looked at the apprentice's hands longer than an inspection required. Ink in the creases, in the half-moons of the nails, the stains of a trade. But the wrists above those hands had been fine. The throat above the collar, when the apprentice had looked up at the fog, had been — 

He shut the thought the way you shut a drawer on something that will keep.

"I saw an apprentice, sir. Careful. Quiet. Tired, like everyone in this city. He asked me about the archive's cataloguing system because he wanted the atlas shelved correctly. That was the whole of it."

"Cataloguing," the captain said. "Charming." He drew the list back across the table, and Arnaud watched the name *Joubert* slide out of the light and into the folder and felt something in his chest close over it like water. "Then here is your order, and it is a simple one. The atlas is due for delivery to the naval archive within the week — the guild has petitioned to have it presented at the signing, God help us, as some kind of gesture. Every case, every plate, every parcel out of that shop passes through your hands between now and then. You will inspect them. You will log them. And if you find anything — a letter, a loose page, an anomaly in a chart, a word out of place — you will bring it to me before you bring it to anyone, including your section chief. Including your father's ghost. Am I understood?"

"Yes, sir."

"You reported the fragment promptly and correctly. I'm recording that. It's the only reason you're sitting in a chair and not on the other side of that table." The captain rose, and the room seemed to rearrange itself around his weight. At the door he stopped, hand on the frame, and looked back, and for one moment the closed-ledger face opened a single page. "One more thing, Lieutenant. Off the record, which means it never happened, which means you never heard it." He paused. "The Roux widow called on the map shop last week. Two hours. Tea, the neighbors say. The Roux whose name is second on that list." He let it sit in the air between them, in the lamplight, in the fog-smell. "A widow calls on an apprentice to talk about tea. In this city, this winter, I don't believe in tea. Good night."

The door closed. The clerk gathered his folder, gave Arnaud a glance that held neither sympathy nor curiosity — the glance of a man who has watched a hundred lieutenants sit where Arnaud sat and expects to watch a hundred more — and followed.

* * *

Arnaud did not go back to the barracks. He told himself it was because the fog was thick and the tram was slow and his quarters were cold, and all of that was true, and none of it was the reason. He walked. The city at night in the fog was a city dissolved — gas lamps reduced to smears of amber, the cathedral a rumor, the harbor a sound without a shape, masts creaking somewhere out in the white like a fleet of ghosts at anchor. He walked without a destination and arrived, in the way that a man always arrives where he has already decided to go, at the mouth of the street where the map shop stood.

He did not go in. There was no reason to go in; the shop was dark, had been dark for hours, and there was no order that sent him there and no honest errand that could explain a lieutenant at a mapmaker's door at ten at night in the fog. He stood across the street instead, under a lamp that gave less light than it promised, and he looked at the dark windows, and he let the question the captain had planted do what planted questions do.

*A boy, or a door.*

He had held the apprentice's chart-rolls in his own hands. He had watched those hands unroll a plate of the outer roads — soundings in a fine sure script, the coast rendered with a tenderness Arnaud had not seen since his father's table — and he had felt, standing there in the Customhouse with the fog pressing at the windows, something he had not felt in five years of war and checkpoints and the mail of strangers: that he was looking at the work of someone who loved the same thing he loved, and loved it better.

And now the Intelligence Section had written *Joubert* on a list between a shipping magnate and a dock-lease holder, and a widow had drunk two hours of tea in that dark shop, and somewhere out in the white silence of the harbor a signal mast stood with its lamps dark, waiting to see what kind of winter this would be.

Arnaud understood, standing there, that he had already answered the captain's question. He had answered it in the room, with his flat voice and his rehearsed truth — *I saw an apprentice, sir* — and the answer had been a half-truth, and a half-truth told to an Intelligence Section under lamplight was not a small thing. It was a stone set into a wall. It was the first stone. There was no order he had ever received, no regulation he had ever read, that covered what he had done at that table, and he knew its name anyway, the old name, the name they gave it in the field when a sentry let a shadow pass unchallenged because the shadow had kind eyes:

Treachery.

He turned it over. He had expected it to feel like falling. It did not. It felt like a chart with a coastline on it that the official maps did not show — wrong, and yet surveyed, and yet *true*, and his, drawn in his own hand.

The fog moved. Down the street, above the shop door, the lamp over the sign — PERRIN & JOUBERT, CARTOGRAPHERS, in gold gone brown with age — swam into view and out again. And Arnaud saw, or thought he saw, a light behind the dark window of the upper floor: a small yellow bloom, there and gone, like the flame of a lamp being carried past a window, or set down, or lowered over a letter.

He stood a long time in the cold, telling himself it was a reflection, a trick of the fog, a city of ten thousand windows and every one of them capable of lying.

Then he turned up his collar and walked away, and did not write it in his log, and did not report it, and knew as he walked that he had just set the second stone.

In five days, the delegation would come. In five days, everything that shop contained would pass through his hands — and he would have to decide, case by case, plate by plate, what he was: the officer the captain had just made him, or the man the fog had shown him in a window.

Behind him, the light in the upper room burned on, small and yellow and secret, like a flame cupped against the wind — or like a fuse, he thought, and could not stop thinking, all the way home.
