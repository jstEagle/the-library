## 24. Raid on the Studio

The knock came at half past nine, and it was not a customer's knock.

Felix knew the difference the way she knew the difference between a tide chart and a tide itself — not by any single feature but by weight, by rhythm, by the particular gravity of intention behind it. Customers knocked twice, apologetically, the way one taps on a door of a shop they aren't sure is open in winter. This was three blows, spaced evenly, struck with the flat of a fist, and then silence that was worse than a fourth blow would have been, because silence meant they were listening.

She was at the drafting table with plate forty-one spread before her, the burnisher warm in her hand, the last coastline of the atlas nearly finished under the hiss of the oil lamp. Her first instinct — absurd, human, hopeless — was to keep working. To finish the stroke she was on. As though the ink could argue for her, as though a completed chart were a kind of alibi.

The second knock was the same as the first, and between them she heard boots in the street outside. Not two pairs. Five or six, shifting, settling, taking up positions the way soldiers take up positions, which was precisely what they were doing. One at the mouth of the alley that ran behind the studio, where the coal chute offered the only other way out. One — she heard him cough — beneath the window with its panes of old glass, warped now, that turned every passing lantern into a smeared ghost.

Felix set down the burnisher with the care of a woman defusing something.

The letter was upstairs. That was the first mercy and the last. She had sewn it into nothing yet; it still lay where it had lain these many weeks, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of Perrin's old instrument chest, and the chest stood in the back room among the rolls of paper like any piece of dead inventory. If they found it, she was dead, and Émile was dead, and everything their father had built and Perrin had died protecting would be ash with no one left to sign its name.

But they had not found it yet, and the door was about to open whether she wished it or not.

There was nowhere to go. The back alley was watched; the window was watched; the cellar stairs creaked in three places she could have named from memory because she had mapped them herself, years ago, out of a mapmaker's habit of knowing every escape her prison did not contain. What remained was the drawers.

Perrin had built them himself, the great bank of chart drawers along the north wall — shallow, wide, deep enough for a rolled sheet of double elephant paper and no deeper. Except that Perrin was Perrin, and Perrin did not build anything without concealing something in it. The bottom drawer, the one that held the obsolete surveys of the outer shoals, sat proud of the others by the width of a fingernail, and behind it, where a sensible man would have put a wall, there was a cavity — a foot deep, two feet wide, lined with cedar to keep the damp off whatever he had hidden there in the years when hiding things had mattered. She had found it in her first month, cataloguing, and had thought: *the old fox.* She had never once needed it.

She needed it now, and there was no time to think better of needing it.

The third knock splintered the latch rather than waiting past it. Felix crossed the studio in six strides, slid the bottom drawer open, pulled the rolled charts out onto the floor, and folded herself into the cavity knees to chest, drawing the drawer shut behind her by its brass handle until it seated with a click so soft she prayed to a God she only spoke to in emergencies that it was soft enough.

Then there was nothing to do but breathe — slowly, through the nose, the way you breathe when you are trying to make a small quantity of air last — and listen to her life be taken apart around her.

* * *

They were not quiet about it. That was the first thing she understood, and understanding it frightened her more than stealth would have, because men who ransack quietly are afraid of being overheard, and men who ransack loudly believe no one will ever call them to account.

The door went back against the wall. Boots came in — four sets, maybe five, the snow and grit of the street ground into the floorboards she had scrubbed that very morning. A voice she did not know said, "Start with the table. He'll have worked here," and another voice said, "Careful with the wet ones, you idiot, ink smears," and Felix understood that they believed the letter might still be *in progress* — that they imagined Perrin's apprentice sitting over it night after night, pen in hand, copying out their names in her careful round hand. They did not know what she had. That was worth more than gold. That was the whole war, right there: they were searching for a document that existed, but they did not know its shape, and a searcher who does not know the shape of the thing searches everywhere, and a man who searches everywhere takes longer, and longer was time, and time was the only currency she had.

A drawer slammed. Then another. The drafting table creaked under someone's knee. Paper tore — actually tore, the sound of it going through her like a blade through wet card, because that was vellum, that was the proof impression of plate nineteen, the estuary survey that had taken Perrin two winters — and a voice laughed shortly and said, "Nothing," and moved on.

Felix breathed against the cedar smell and the dust and the dark, and made herself do the thing she was best at in all the world: she made a map of it. Boots by the table, heavy, the one who tore things. Two at the plan chest along the west wall, lighter on their feet, methodical, pulling each drawer and running a hand along the underside the way an inspector runs a hand under a table for a hidden message. One at the shelves, flipping through folios. And the fifth — the fifth was standing still.

She knew the weight of a standing body even through an inch of oak. She had spent her whole life reading stances across a room, learning how men planted themselves, because a disguise is mostly a matter of knowing what people expect to see. The fifth man stood near the hearth, arms likely crossed, head likely tilted, watching the others work and saying nothing, and when he finally spoke, she knew the voice before she had finished telling herself she didn't want to.

"Not the chest," Émile said. "He'll check the chest himself."

* * *

Betrayal, Felix had always supposed, would feel like fire. It felt instead like cold water finding the seam of a boot — a single thin line of ice against the ankle, precise, intimate, impossible to warm.

Her brother was standing in Perrin's studio, in the lamplight under which she had apprenticed for eleven years, directing the search for the document that would hang their family, and he was directing it *competently*, and the competence was the worst of it. He knew the room. He had carried paper for Perrin as a boy; their father had bought charts here; Émile knew exactly where a mapmaker would hide a letter, and he was sparing the others the labor of guessing.

Unless —

God help her, unless he was steering them. Unless every instruction was a misdirection dressed as expertise. She could not know. Through the drawer's seam she could see nothing but a blade of lamplight and the shadow of a shoulder crossing it, and she lay in the dark of Perrin's clever cavity and tried to read a man's soul through the soles of his boots.

"Where, then?" the heavy one asked. His voice had the flattened vowels of the docks, a man promoted from muscle to management.

"The chest, I told you. And the backing of the old charts." Émie's footsteps crossed the room — she tracked them, hating herself for tracking them, unable not to. "Our friend sewed things into chart backing. It was a habit of the trade before it was anything else. Take the rollers off the plan chest and look at the linings."

Paper rustled. Something metal clinked — the rollers coming off their brackets. Then the heavy voice again, closer, directly above her now, so near that she could hear him breathing through his mouth, could smell the cheap tobacco on him: "And the apprentice? Where's the boy?"

"Gone out," Émile said. Too quickly. Much too quickly, and Felix felt the pause that followed travel up through the floor of the world into her own chest, because the heavy one had heard it too.

"Gone out," the man repeated. "At this hour. In this fog. With his shop full of—"

"He keeps hours his master kept. The trade makes monks of them. Check the room upstairs if it pleases you, but mind the landlady next door, she screams at soldiers."

Boots took the stairs. Overhead, floorboards complained; a mattress was heaved back; a chamber pot was kicked and rolled. Below, Émile's footsteps came to a stop beside the great bank of drawers, close enough that if she had reached out through the wood she could have touched his knee.

She held her breath until colors bloomed behind her eyes.

* * *

What happened then took less than a minute and lasted the rest of her life.

Émile opened the bottom drawer.

She knew it by the change in the light at the seam, by the faint grind of the runner, by the sudden proximity of warmth — his hands, inches from her face, reaching into the drawer past her hip, past the space her body filled, gathering the rolled charts she had left lying there. His fingers brushed the paper. His fingers brushed, unmistakably, the fold of her coat sleeve where it pressed against the drawer's inner wall.

Everything stopped. The raiding went on overhead, the muffled violence of strangers in her bedroom, but down here the world contracted to a hand and a sleeve and a quarter inch of pine board.

Émile did not move. Ten seconds. Twenty. She could hear him thinking; she would have sworn it under oath, the way you can hear weather coming in a rigging. And then, in a voice pitched for the room but carrying, deliberately, unmistakably, to whoever might be listening from places men don't look:

"Empty. Old shoals, outdated junk — nothing." And he rolled the charts tighter in his hands, tucking her sleeve-crease inside them, folding the evidence of her into the evidence of paper, and closed the drawer, and stood, and said, "Take the chest apart if you want. You won't find it, because it isn't here."

"You're certain of that?" the heavy one said, and now the courtesy in his voice was the dangerous kind, the kind that comes just before men stop pretending.

"I'm certain," Émile said, "that if the apprentice had it, it would be on him, not in the shop. Men carry what they can't afford to lose." A pause. "We should have taken him days ago. We should take him tonight."

* * *

*Men carry what they can't afford to lose.*

She lay in the cedar dark and felt the sentence go through her like a sounding line dropping to the seabed. He knew. He had known since his fingers touched her sleeve — known she was there, known she had heard everything, known she was lying an inch from his hand listening to him decide, in front of these men, whether tonight was the night his sister disappeared into the harbor.

And he had said *we should,* and the heavy one had grunted, and Émile had added — his voice perfectly level, beautifully level, the levelness itself a message — "Tomorrow. In daylight, on the street, like it's chance. A missing apprentice the night his shop is searched is a story. An apprentice who drowns in the fog is weather."

Weather. She was to be weather. And tomorrow, in daylight, on the street, her brother would walk beside the men who made her into it, wearing whatever face he wore to them, and she would not know until the water closed over her whether the face was true.

The chest came apart with a crowbar. She heard Perrin's instruments scatter across the boards — the plane, the dividers, the ruling pens rolling in circles like dropped coins — and heard the false bottom splinter, and heard the men's disappointment when it held nothing but sandpaper and spare nibs. Upstairs they overturned everything that would overturn. Someone found the bottle of walnut ink and drank from it and spat. Through all of it Émile stayed near the drawers, near her, a sentry who had appointed himself, and she understood that he was standing guard over the one place he could not admit existed, and that his vigilance and his threat were the same act, and that she would never, as long as she lived, be able to separate her brother's love from his treachery again. They had been poured into the same mold. They had come out one shape.

It took them most of an hour to be satisfied, which is to say to fail thoroughly and convince themselves they hadn't. At the end the heavy one stood in the middle of the ruined studio — she could tell by the way his voice bounced now, unsoftened by furniture — and said, "The guildmaster says the boy delivers the atlas within the week. If the letter exists, it travels with it."

"Then we'll be waiting at every gate between here and the citadel," another said.

"And the boy?"

A silence. In it, Felix heard Émile turn — heard the boards give under the pivot of his weight — and heard him say, quietly, "The boy is my concern. Leave him to me."

Boots receded. The door closed. Latchless now, it drifted against its frame, and the fog crept in behind it smelling of coal smoke and low tide.

She counted to three hundred. She listened to the stairs, to the street, to the sentry at the window who coughed once more and then, mercifully, moved off with the rest. Only when the silence had gone from watchful to empty did she press her palms to the drawer and push, and slide out into her ruined kingdom stiff and cold and shaking, and stand in the lamplight among the wreckage of eleven years.

The charts Émile had re-rolled lay on the plan chest, squared neatly, considerately, damningly. On top of them, weighted with Perrin's silver divider — placed where she could not fail to see it — was a scrap of paper torn from a survey margin, and on it, in her brother's hand, five words:

*Burn it. Leave by dawn.*

Felix stood a long time with the note in her fingers while the lamp hissed and the fog leaned against the windows, and somewhere across the sleeping city the signal station burned its light toward ships that could not see it, and she understood at last, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had until morning to choose — and that whichever choice she made, the brother who loved her would be standing on the other side of it.
