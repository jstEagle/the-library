## 26. Émile's Choice

The order came at dusk, folded inside a tobacco tin, passed to him by a man whose name Émile had never been told and whose hands smelled of lamp oil.

He read it twice on the rue des Anciennes Halles, under the crooked gas lamp that had been dark since the coal shortages began, because he could not trust himself to read it once. The paper was thin as onion skin. The handwriting was not a handwriting at all but the flat, anonymous print of a man trained never to let his letters be known — each character built from straight strokes, no loops, nothing for an examiner to seize upon.

*The apprentice has it. Silence the apprentice permanently. Before the week is out. Before the signing.*

*The apprentice.*

Not *the sister*. Not *Felix Joubert*. They had all, in three years of cellar meetings and whispered pledges and candle-lit toasts to the father's memory, learned to say *the apprentice* or *Perrin's boy*, and the words had worn smooth in their mouths like river stones, until none of them — least of all Émile — had noticed what they were doing. Saying it that way made it possible. A name was a person. An apprentice was a problem.

Émile folded the order along its crease and put it back in the tobacco tin, and put the tin in his coat pocket, and stood a while longer under the dead lamp watching the fog come up off the harbor in long grey rollers, like slow water climbing a beach.

Somewhere out in that fog, beyond the breakwater, beyond the mined channel and the swept channel and the buoys his father had helped place twenty years ago, ships were waiting. He knew their numbers by heart now; he had counted them off a hundred times on his fingers in the cellar, in the tone of a man reciting grievances: four dreadnoughts, eleven destroyers, a hospital ship with her lights doused. Waiting for the armistice to be signed so they could steam in past the signal station and take possession of the harbor in the name of a peace Émile had been persuaded, three winters ago, was a betrayal.

His father's voice had done the persuading. Not the living voice — his father had been nine years in the churchyard at Montlevon — but the voice that lived in Émile's chest, in the litany of dinner-table grievances that had been the household music of his boyhood. *They drew the maps,* his father used to say, tapping the atlas open on his knee, *and then they drew the borders, and nobody asked the men who bled for the ground whether the lines pleased them.* His father had come home from two wars with a stiff knee and a bitterness that deepened like a well. And when he died, the bitterness was the only thing in the estate that divided evenly between his children.

Except that wasn't true either, Émile thought now. Felix had inherited something else entirely. Felix had inherited the steady hands.

He began to walk. He did not decide to walk toward the chart shop; his feet decided, and he let them, because the alternative was to stand under the dead lamp until the fog swallowed him and admit what the order meant, which was that three years of careful, honorable, secret patriotism had arrived at last at its destination, and the destination was his sister.

* * *

The harbor city in winter kept two kinds of silence, and Émile knew both intimately. There was the silence of curfew, which had edges and a clock to it, boots on cobbles somewhere distant keeping the time. And there was the silence of fog, which had no clock, only a soft white pressure against the ears, as if the whole world had been wrapped in wool and posted to nowhere. Tonight both silences lay over the streets together, layered, and Émile moved through them the way he had learned to move through everything these past years: quietly, head down, a man practicing at being invisible.

He had been good at it. That was the bitter joke of it. He had taken to conspiracy the way some men took to farming or the sea — naturally, without struggle, as if the talent had always been there waiting. He could sit in a cellar with eight strangers and keep his voice level. He could carry papers sewn into his coat lining through two checkpoints without his pulse rising enough to notice. He could listen to men talk about the signal station — about charges placed in the sea wall, about the precise hour the delegation would pass beneath the heights — and feel nothing worse than a coldness in his stomach that he had told himself was resolve.

Resolve. He turned the word over as he walked, testing it the way you test a coin with your teeth.

Three weeks ago, at the meeting in the cellar of the old Dupont house, he had watched the roster go around the table, each man adding his name in pencil to the pledge that would bind his family to the act. When it came to Émile he wrote *E. Joubert* in his neat surveyor's hand and passed the paper on, and only afterward — too late, the way one always notices — did he see that below his name another line had been added in someone else's writing. *F. Joubert.* His sister. Added without her knowledge, without her consent, entered onto the roster of conspirators as casually as a name on a delivery docket.

He had asked Perrin's old journeyman about it afterward, in the doorway, breath steaming. *She keeps the shop,* the man had said with a shrug. *The shop is part of it. She's part of it.* As if that settled anything. As if a woman who spent fourteen hours a day bending over copper plates had somehow signed her name in her sleep.

Émile had gone home that night and sat in his cold room above the cooperage and looked at his hands for a long time, and what frightened him was not what they had done but how little they trembled.

Now, passing the shuttered fish market, he understood that the trembling had only been postponed. It had waited for this tobacco tin. It had waited for the word *permanently*, printed in strokes as straight and unrepentant as fence palings.

* * *

There was a light burning in the studio window of the chart shop. Of course there was. His sister kept hours that would have killed a stoker — up before the lamplighters, still at the drafting table when the night watch made its second round. He had teased her about it once, years ago, before everything: *You'll die at that table, little one, with a pen in your fist and your last coastline half-finished.* She had laughed and said there were worse fates than dying mid-line, and he hadn't understood her then, and he understood her completely now, and the understanding went through him like a bayonet.

He did not knock with his knuckles. He knocked the old way, the way they had knocked at each other as children through the plaster wall between their rooms — two quick, one slow, two quick — a rhythm their father had never learned and therefore never interrupted.

The light shifted behind the curtain. A shadow crossed, and the door opened on its chain, and one eye appeared in the gap — his sister's eye, sharp and grey and ringed with sleeplessness, set in a face she had been presenting to the world as a young man's face for over a year now. Even expecting it, even knowing, Émile felt the small vertigo of it: the close-cropped hair, the high-collared shirt, the jaw he remembered round and soft from childhood now deliberately squared by posture and shadow. She had remade herself the way she remade a chart, line by patient line, and the result was convincing enough that guildmen tipped their hats to her and called her *monsieur*.

But the eye was the same eye that had watched him across the dinner table while their father ranted about borders. The eye that had seen him whole.

The chain came off. The door opened.

"Émile." Her voice was pitched low, half a register down where it needed to live now, but underneath the performance it was her voice, and it said his name with no surprise at all, which frightened him more than anything else could have. "I wondered how long it would take."

"You knew I'd come."

"I knew someone would." She stepped back to let him in. "Eventually one of you would count the plates and realize one was missing from the archive schedule, and do the arithmetic." She closed the door and shot the bolt and stood with her hand on it a moment, her back to him, gathering herself. "Or," she added quietly, "one of you would stop being able to bear it."

* * *

The studio smelled as it had always smelled — linseed and lampblack, the ghost of Perrin's pipe tobacco soaked into the beams, the faint mineral cold of damp stone. Émile had loved this room as a boy. He had sat on that stool by the press and watched old Perrin's burin move across copper, cutting coastlines into metal, and thought it was the finest magic in the world: that a man could hold the sea in his hands.

The room had changed. Charts were rolled everywhere, bundled in twine, stacked against the walls like cordwood. The great table bore plate forty-one under its sheet of glass, the final plate of the harbor atlas, and even in the lamplight Émile could see the workmanship was extraordinary — every buoy, every sounding, every contour of the approaches rendered with a fidelity that made his chest ache. Around the decorative border ran a filigree of compass roses and sea-monsters and knotted rope-work, delicate as lace.

He almost didn't look at it closely. Some part of him already knew, and wanted very much not to know.

"Félix," he said.

"That's not my name."

"It's the name I've called you for a year. Let me keep it a few minutes longer." He heard his own voice crack on the word and hated it. He had rehearsed a dozen openings on the walk over — indignant, brotherly, stern, pleading — and every one of them had evaporated the moment the door opened. What remained was only the truth, laid out raw. "They know about the letter. Roux talked — or Ozenne, we don't know which, maybe both. The order came tonight. It's been given to me." He reached into his coat and brought out the tobacco tin and set it on the corner of the drafting table, next to the pen tray, next to the little silver instruments in their velvet grooves. "To me, Félix. They gave it to me because I'm the one with a key to you. Because I'm your blood."

She looked at the tin. She did not touch it. When she raised her eyes to his again they were terribly calm, the calm of deep water, and he recognized it because it was their mother's calm, the stillness their mother had worn through two wars and a funeral and ten lean years, the stillness of women who had learned that panic was a luxury purchased at everyone else's expense.

"And what does it say?" she asked.

"You know what it says."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear whether you can."

"Silence the apprentice permanently," he said. The words came out of him like splinters. "Before the week is out."

*Permanently.* The word hung in the lamplight between them. His sister did not flinch. She picked up the tin instead, opened it, read the order with the unhurried attention she gave to a tide table, and set it down again, and Émile realized with a lurch that she had already prepared herself for exactly this — that somewhere in the long sleepless weeks since she had found the letter, she had run this scene ahead of time the way she ran a survey ahead of drawing the chart, sounding every depth, marking every hazard.

"So," she said. "This is the shape of it. This is what we've become. Our father's bitterness, come to harvest." She sat down slowly at the table, and for the first time he saw that her hands were not steady — that the calm was a construction, a seawall, and behind it the flood pressed hard. "Tell me about the signal station, Émile. Tell me the timing. Tell me everything, and then tell me why I shouldn't carry this tin to the naval archive tomorrow morning."

"Because they'd hang you with it in your hand."

"They might hang me anyway, apparently."

"Not if you leave." He heard himself say it before he had decided to say it, and having said it, found that the whole architecture of what he had walked here to do stood suddenly complete around him, as if it had been building itself for months and he had only just walked inside. "Leave the city. Tonight. By the north road, before first light, before they post anyone on it. Take the atlas or don't, burn the letter or don't, I don't care anymore which, Félix, I—" His voice failed and restarted lower. "I care that you're breathing at the end of the winter. That's the whole of my patriotism now. That's everything left in me."

She stared at him. "And you? If I vanish the night before the signing, with the letter missing, where does their suspicion fall?"

"On me." He said it simply, because it was simple. "Let it. I'll swear you fled alone. I'll say I drove you out. God knows it will sound true — I'm the one who brought you into all this by keeping my mouth shut three years ago." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Do you know what I was going to tell myself on the walk here? That I'd come to warn you out of loyalty to Father. That the plot is one thing but kin is kin. And that's a lie, Félix. I came because I saw the roster, three weeks ago, with your name on it in a stranger's handwriting, and I've been dead ever since. Everything since has just been the body walking around."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The fog pressed at the windows. Somewhere down the street the curfew patrol passed, boot-heels crisp on the cobbles, and they both went still as painted figures until the sound faded, and Émile thought: *this is what we are now. Two people standing frozen in a lamplit room, listening for the state.*

"I can't leave," his sister said at last.

"You can. North road, the fog will—"

"I can't." She stood, and crossed to the great table, and pulled the glass sheet off plate forty-one, and turned it toward the lamp so the border caught the light. "Look at it. Look at the border, Émile. Really look."

He leaned close. The filigree resolved, the way engravings do when you finally find the right distance — knots resolving into figures, scrollwork into letters. Numbers. Sounding marks, arranged in a sequence no honest chart would use, marching around the frame of the plate like beads on a string. Coordinates. The signal station, hidden in the decoration, in the master's own hand, distributed through forty-one plates of the most beautiful atlas the harbor had ever produced.

"He knew," Émile whispered.

"He knew for years. Father knew. That letter isn't a discovery, it's a testament — he died holding it, and he hid the proof in plain sight, in the one place no conspirator would think to look, because to them the atlas is furniture. Background." Her finger traced the border numbers. "I can't leave, Émile, because an unfinished atlas is a lie, and a delivered lie is a betrayal, and I have spent a year learning exactly how much of my life a lie costs. I'm going to carry this to the signing. Both halves. The charts and the letter. And I'm going to do it under my own name, which is Félicie, which you may as well start practicing."

"The citadel gate is impassable," he said hoarsely. "You don't know what the security is like—"

"I know precisely what the security is like. I've mapped all three checkpoints better than their own commanders have." Something kindled in her face then, something he had not seen since before their father died — not fear, though fear lived under it, but the fierce clean heat of purpose. "You can't help me. I won't ask you to, and if you offered I'd refuse, because one of us should survive to bury the other. But you can give me days. Tell them the apprentice suspects nothing yet. Tell them she's timid. Buy me a week and I won't need it."

Émile looked at his sister — at the ink stains on her fingers, at the borrowed collar, at the atlas of the harbor lying open under the lamp with the whole truth sewn invisibly into its beauty — and understood that he had come here believing he was the one with something to give. Warning. A road north. A brother's last protection.

But she had never needed protecting from the world. Only from their father's ghost, and from him, and she had freed herself from both on her own.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When it's done — when the arrests are made and the armistice holds and they publish the damned book — you put Perrin's name on it. And yours. And you let me watch from the crowd. Whatever happens to me after." He swallowed. "Let me see you stand in the light once, little one. Before whatever comes."

Her eyes shone. She did not cry; neither of them had been raised to it. She put out her hand instead, ink-stained and steady now, and he took it, and they gripped forearms the way sailors grip, and neither trusted themselves to speak.

Then she pushed the tobacco tin back across the table toward him. "Take their order with you. If they search me on the road, I'd rather not be carrying the sentence of my own death written in their alphabet."

He took it. At the door, unbarring it, she paused with her hand on the bolt.

"Émile. If they send someone else instead — if they don't believe you bought them time — you come to the breakwater. Any hour. You shout my name, the real one, across the water, and I'll hear it. Promise me that much."

"I promise," he said, and meant it as fully as he had ever meant anything.

The fog took him the moment he stepped out. Behind him the studio light went dim, then dark — she would work by shaded lamp now, unseen, finishing the last of it. He turned up his collar and walked north along the quay, toward his cold room and his lies and the men he would have to face in the morning with a straight voice and empty hands, and he told himself the worst was decided, the choice made, love's last victory sealed.

He did not see the figure detach itself from the shadow of the customhouse wall and follow him, soft-footed, into the fog.

And three streets away, at Checkpoint One, Lieutenant Arnaud Vasseur looked up from his ledger at the sound of footsteps on the wet stones, and checked his watch, and noted in his log, in a small precise hand: *Movement on the quay, curfew hour. Unidentified.*
