## 29. The Plan of Three Gates

Dawn came to the studio the way it always did in winter — not arriving so much as being permitted, a grey concession pressed against the frosted glass of the north window. Felix had not slept. She sat at Perrin's drafting table with three sheets of fresh paper before her and the candle burned down to a puckered coin of wax, and she understood, with the clarity that comes only after the body has spent all its fear and has nothing left but arithmetic, that what she was about to do was not courage.

It was engineering.

A bridge is not brave. A bridge is calculated — load, span, tide, frost. It either holds or it does not, and the men who build it do not pray at the abutments; they check them twice. She had spent eleven years learning to draw the world as it truly was, and if the world could be drawn truly then it could be read truly, and if it could be read truly then three gates between her and the citadel were nothing more than a survey problem with unusually high consequences for error.

She sharpened her pencil. She ruled a margin. At the top of the first sheet, in her best hand — the hand Perrin had drilled into her, the hand the guild believed belonged to a boy — she wrote: *The Plan of Three Gates.* Below it, smaller: *Load: one atlas, one letter, one liar.*

Then she began to build.

* * *

The first gate was the Ink Gate, and the Ink Gate's weakness was its pride.

She drew it from memory first, then from knowledge — because Arnaud, in his rounds, in their conversations across the barrier of his own duty, had told her more than he knew. Men always did. He had complained, half laughing, about the way inspections were logged: every parcel entered in the ledger by hour, by sender, by declared contents, and how the sergeant of the gate treated the ledger as scripture. *If it's written down, it's true,* he'd said, in the tone of a man who found this both comforting and faintly absurd. *The paper says the coffee is coffee, so the coffee is coffee.*

Paper says. That was the whole hinge of it, laid out for her over tea he thought was idle conversation.

Felix sketched the Ink Gate's rhythm as she had observed it on four passes: the queue forming at shift change when the night sentries were cold and wanting their relief; the inspection table set under the lamp because the archway's interior had never been wired; the two-man search team working parcels while a third checked papers — and here was the seam — the searchers took visible pride in what they found. A contraband discovery made an afternoon. Made a report. Made a name.

So she would give them something to find.

On the second sheet she drew the decoy parcel: a document case, good leather, government-looking, locked, addressed to the naval archive in a hand she would disguise just slightly — imperfectly — so that it would invite scrutiny rather than deflect it. Inside, harmless papers: duplicate plates of the atlas's coastal views, the decorative borders, the tide tables. Work a searcher could examine for an hour and feel clever about. And sewn into the lining where a determined man would find it, a letter — not the letter, a forgery of one, vague enough to be ambiguous, alarming enough to satisfy. Let them triumph over it. Let the whole machinery of Checkpoint One congratulate itself at precisely the hour her cart rolled through in the wake of the commotion, carrying the real atlas rolled in plain brown paper among the guild's routine deliveries, declared in the ledger, stamped, waved past, uninteresting.

*A crowd gathers where the fire is,* Perrin used to say, teaching her to place compass roses. *So put your fire where you want the crowd.*

She tested the timing three times, working backward from the archive's delivery window. Shift change at the Ink Gate: quarter past six. The decoy discovered: within twenty minutes, if the searcher was worth his pride. The guild's delivery cart cleared the gate at seven, when every eye and every lantern and every suspicion in the place would be fixed on the leather case and its scandalous lining.

It would work if no one looked too closely at her face. It would work if the fog held. It would work if the forged letter fooled a reader for the forty minutes she needed and not one minute more, because after the archive opened it and understood, the alarm would go up, and everything downstream of the Ink Gate would tighten like a fist.

Forty minutes. She wrote the number in the margin and circled it, and beneath it: *Margin of error: none. Acceptable.*

Her hand did not shake. She noticed that, and was briefly frightened by it — not of the plan, but of the woman making it, who had once feared a guild inspection the way others feared weather and now sat calculating the subornation of two military checkpoints with cold, tidy strokes of a pencil.

*Émile,* she thought, and let the thought sit for exactly as long as it took to hurt, and then ruled another line.

* * *

The second gate was harder, because the Customhouse did not run on pride. It ran on money, and money ran out.

Arnaud had been transferred there in the autumn — those few weeks before they pulled him back to One — and he had spoken of it with the weary contempt of an honest man in a dishonest room. *You don't bribe a customhouse officer,* he'd said. *You rent him. Rates vary by the health of his conscience and the size of his debts.* He'd meant it as a joke, mostly. He'd have died before telling her which officers could be rented, but he hadn't needed to tell her; he'd told her the system, and the system was legible. Everything was legible. That was the terrible gift of her trade: she could not stop reading the world into plans.

She drew the Customhouse long-house: the canal door, the rail yard door, the central hall where cargo accumulated in drifts like tidewrack. She marked the inspector's rotations — two reliable men, one lazy one, one whose gloves were too fine for his pay. The gloves told her everything the ledger would have. And she remembered, from her decoy run weeks ago, the exact angle of the lamplight on the examination bench, the exact width of the gap between the coal store and the wall where a person might stand and not be seen, and the fact — small, crucial — that the inspectors searched what arrived, not what was already inside.

Which meant the letter should not arrive at the Customhouse at all. It should already be there.

But that required passage through the officers' side entrance, the one reserved for credentialed couriers and port officials, and for that she needed credentials she did not have and could not forge well enough to survive the new inspector Arnaud had warned her about — the transfer, the strict one, the man who checked seals twice.

She sat back. The candle guttered and steadied. Outside, the fog had begun to lift off the harbor in slow rags, and somewhere down the hill a bell buoy clanged its patient idiot note, marking a rock no ship could see.

There was exactly one person in this city who possessed legitimate courier credentials and a reason to help her, and that person had betrayed her to the conspirators once already, out of terror, and knew it, and had wept on Felix's floor over it. Madame Roux. Her dead husband had ridden dispatches for the port authority for thirty years; his credentials would be old, perhaps expired, perhaps not. Whether the widow of a named conspirator would surrender them — whether shame could be converted into courage — Felix could not calculate. That was the flaw in the bridge, the one span built on something that was not stone.

She wrote it down anyway: *Gate Two — passage by credential. Source: M.R. Probability: unknown.*

Underneath, after a moment, she added: *Ask her as herself.* Not as Perrin's boy, whom Roux had begged and bribed and betrayed. As Félicie Joubert, whose name was already on the conspirators' roster whether she claimed it or not. If she was asking a woman to risk her last possession, the least honesty owed was her own.

Her heart objected. She overruled it. There would be time to be afraid on the breakwater afterward, or in prison, or never.

* * *

The third gate was no gate at all. That was the whole horror of it.

The citadel gate admitted only what the armistice delegation's own schedule admitted: official vehicles, official persons, official cargo, inspected by officers who answered to the delegation itself. No decoys — the guards there had seen every trick the war had taught, and the war had taught everything. No bribes — these men were chosen for having nothing outstanding against their names. No fog thin enough, no night dark enough; the signing would happen at noon, in front of the world's newspapers.

There was one way through, and it was so simple it had been invisible for weeks, hidden the way the signal station's coordinates had been hidden in Perrin's decorative border — inside the thing everyone was looking at.

The atlas itself.

The naval archive had commissioned the coastal atlas; the archive was presenting a bound copy to the delegation at the signing, a ceremonial gesture, the harbor giving the peace its own portrait. Guildmaster Ozenne had grumbled about the deadline for months. The delivery cart, the official escort, the sealed presentation copy — all of it arranged, scheduled, sanctified by paperwork. Paper says. The paper said the atlas was an atlas.

And it would be. That was the beauty that had kept her awake with something close to joy. She did not need to smuggle the letter *past* the citadel gate inside some separate hiding place that a thorough officer like Arnaud might find. She needed only to bind the letter into the atlas itself — sewn beneath the endpaper, flat, invisible, part of the book's own anatomy — so that when the inspecting officer opened the covers to verify that the harbor's portrait was indeed a portrait, he would look directly at the letter and see nothing but the marbled paper of a binding. You cannot confiscate the inside of a legitimate object.

And she need not send it with strangers. The atlas was hers to deliver — the guild would certify Perrin's final work, and 'Perrin's boy' would carry the presentation copy, riding in the official cart through the third gate, past the third checkpoint, into the courtyard where history was waiting to happen to someone. She had simply decided it would happen to her.

She drew the binding diagram: the hollowed board construction Perrin had used once, years ago, for a client who wanted to hide love letters from a wife. She had thought it a shameful commission then. Now it was a technique, and techniques are innocent; it is only hands that are guilty or not.

Three gates. Three loads. The decoy to feed Gate One's pride. The credential to open Gate Two's pocket. The atlas itself to walk through Gate Three's ceremony. Each span resting on the next, and the whole structure resting finally on one foundation stone that could not be engineered, only chosen: that when the inspecting officer at the citadel lifted the cover — and it would be Arnaud, she knew it would be, they would assign him, fate had a mapmaker's taste for irony — he would see the letter, and see her face above it, and understand everything at once, and decide in a single breath what kind of man he was.

That breath was the one part of the bridge she could not build. She could only arrive at it.

* * *

She worked through the morning. By noon the three sheets were covered — timings, load diagrams, contingency lines in red for the places where the plan might crack. If the fog failed: delay the decoy by one cycle, accept a narrower margin. If Roux refused: no second source existed, and the red line simply stopped, an unfinished coastline on an otherwise complete chart. *Here be dragons,* the old maps said. Her dragon had a name and a mourning dress and a debt she did not yet know she wanted to pay.

She rolled the plans, tied them, and stood at the north window while the harbor performed its afternoon miracle of light — sun breaking low through the fog to gild the masts, the water running molten between the moles, the signal station standing on its headland with its arms folded, patient as a spider. Tomorrow night, if the plan held, the spider would starve. The city below went about its business, buying fish, arguing about the news, waiting for peace the way it waited for spring — believing it would come from somewhere else, from large rooms with large men in them.

History happens elsewhere, she had believed all her life. She had been wrong the way only a mapmaker can be wrong: by drawing the world without herself in it.

She put on her coat, bound her chest, pinned her collar, settled her master's cap low — the costume she had worn for eleven years and would wear, God willing, five days more. In the looking glass by the door, 'Felix Joubert' looked back at her, pale, ink-stained, resolute. Behind that face, faint as a watermark, Félicie waited.

One errand remained before the plan could live. The hardest span. The one built on borrowed courage.

Felix took her coat from the peg, checked that the atlas plates were locked in the press, and set out down the wet street toward Madame Roux's house — rehearsing as she walked the words she had never dared say aloud to anyone:

*My name is Félicie Joubert, and I need your husband's credentials.*
