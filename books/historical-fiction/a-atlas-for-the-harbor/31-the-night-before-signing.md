## 31. The Night Before Signing

The lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil by the time Félicie Joubert set her pen down for the last time that night — or so she believed. The studio smelled of tallow and cold iron and the particular sweetness of fresh sizing on vellum, a smell she had breathed so long it had become indistinguishable from the smell of her own thoughts. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows the way a hand presses against cloth to feel whether anything living is beneath.

She sat back and looked at what lay before her on the drafting table: the completed atlas. Forty-one plates, each one a harbor rendered so faithfully that a sailor could trust his life to its lines — had trusted, some of them, in the years since Perrin began the work. Plate forty-one was still warm from the press, its ink not yet fully cured, and when she tilted the lamp toward it the decorative border caught the light and threw back, for anyone who knew how to read it, the coordinates of the signal station at Pointe des Corbeaux. She had checked them three times against her midnight survey. They were exact. Of course they were exact. Perrin had never drawn a line he could not defend with his life, and in the end had defended them with precisely that.

"You old fox," she said softly, to the empty room, to the chair where he had sat for thirty years and where she had sat for four of them wearing his trade like a borrowed coat. "You drew the truth into the ornament."

That was the thing about master mapmakers, she thought. Anyone could hide a secret in a locked drawer. Only a man who understood that all secrets are eventually opened would hide one in plain sight, folded into flourishes, tucked into the curl of a compass rose where no censor would ever think to look because no censor had ever been taught to *see*. The conspirators could burn the letter. They could ransack the studio — they had ransacked the studio; she still found splinters of the doorframe in the cracks of the floorboards, and she had stopped sweeping them away because they reminded her what she was up against. But they would have to burn every copy of the atlas itself, and there were copies already promised to the naval archive, to the admiralty, to three merchant houses. The truth was out of the drawer now. It had been out for weeks. It simply hadn't yet been *read*.

Tomorrow it would be read.

Her hand, she noticed, was trembling. Not badly — a fine tremor, the kind that ruined hairlines on engraved copper if you let it persist. She flexed her fingers, pressed them flat against the cool surface of the table until the shaking quieted. There was work yet. There was always work yet; that was the mercy of the trade, that it demanded your hands even when your heart wanted nothing but to sit in the dark and be afraid.

* * *

She began, as ritual demanded, with the title page.

It was a small ceremony she had performed forty times before, once for each finished plate — the laying out of the sheet, the testing of the nib, the first stroke made without hesitation because hesitation showed forever in ink. But tonight the blank page looked different. Tonight it looked like a door.

She dipped the pen. She wrote:

*An Atlas of the Harbor and Its Approaches.*

Then, beneath, in smaller letters, the attribution that the guild required, the attribution Ozenne had fought over in his careful, courteous way: *Begun by Master Aurélien Perrin, Cartographer to the Admiralty.*

And then the space where the second name went. The space she had left blank through four years of nights, filling it in her mind always with the same two words — *his apprentice* — words that were true and insufficient, true and evasive, true the way a chart of only half a coastline is true.

Félicie thought of her father's correspondence spread across this same table a fortnight ago, the letters she had deciphered by lamplight while the fog thickened outside like something poured. Perrin had known. Perrin had discovered the plot years ago and had said nothing to anyone but the page itself, and had died — or been allowed to die; she no longer pretended to herself that the word was *accident* — keeping it. And in the margins of those old letters, in her father's cramped and hurried hand, she had found the sentence that had rearranged her life around a new axis: *Perrin says the girl has the eye. He says she should have the name too.*

*The girl.* Her father had known as well. Had known and signed his pledges anyway, had traded his daughter's inheritance for a patriotism owed to dead men's grievances. She had wept over that discovery, once, briefly, standing in the cellar among the wine racks where Émile and the others had met, and then she had dried her face on her sleeve and gone back upstairs, because grief was a luxury scheduled for after, and after kept moving further away.

But the name. He had wanted her to have the name. Not Joubert — she had never wanted less than she wanted that name now, with Émile's signature on a pledge to strike the signal station, with Madame Roux's terror and Roux's borrowed courage sitting in the drawer beside her — but the *trade's* name. The cartographer's name. The name written under the atlas.

Her hand hovered above the page.

Here was the arithmetic she had been doing all night, all month, all four years: the cost of the lie weighed against the cost of the truth. The lie had kept her fed. The lie had kept the studio open, had kept Perrin's commissions flowing, had kept her brother from asking too many questions about why his sister spent her evenings hunched over copper plates instead of learning to run a household. The lie had been, for four years, a kind of shelter — and like every shelter in this city this winter, it had a price of admission that rose daily.

And the truth. The truth would hang her, if the wrong men heard it first. Not for being a woman at a drafting table — the guild would merely sneer, and Ozenne would merely smile his terrible courteous smile, and the certification would die quietly in committee — but for everything the woman had done while wearing the man: the forged signatures on delivery receipts, the false name on the guild rolls, the deception practiced upon officers of the republic at three checkpoints, the smuggling of a sealed letter sewn into a chart binding. Men had been shot for less in this winter of ours. Men *had* been shot for less; she had seen the notices pasted on the customhouse wall, names and crimes and dates, the state's own grim atlas of its citizens.

But the truth was also the only thing that made the atlas mean anything. An unsigned atlas was an orphan. A signed lie was worse — it was a poison in the well of the whole trade, because every measurement she had ever taken under a false name became evidence that women could not be trusted with instruments, that the guild's careful men had been right all along. If she delivered the atlas tomorrow and let *Perrin's boy* take the credit one final time, then vanished, then whatever came after — the inquiry, the arrests, the quiet restoration of order — would happen over the head of a person who did not exist, and the studio would pass to the guild, and the trade would close behind her like water closing over a stone.

Whereas if she signed —

Her hand came down.

She wrote: *Completed by Félicie Joubert.*

Fourteen letters. She watched the ink dry on them and felt nothing at first, which frightened her more than fear would have; she had expected trumpets, or at least thunder. What she felt instead, as the seconds passed, was something smaller and stranger — the sensation of setting down a weight she had carried so long her shoulders had forgotten its shape. She rolled them. She breathed. The fog ticked softly against the glass, patient as tide.

"Félicie," she said aloud, trying the sound of it in the empty studio, and the room took the name and gave back nothing, and that was right. Names were not given meaning by rooms. They were given meaning by work.

* * *

There remained the letter.

She took it from the drawer where it lived now — the drawer with the false bottom, the one place in the studio the raiders had somehow not found, though she suspected they had torn the place apart looking for exactly it. It lay in its wrapping of oilcloth like a thing asleep. She unwrapped it slowly, the way one approaches any object that has cost lives already and may cost more.

Aurélien Perrin's seal, unbroken. His handwriting on the face of it, addressed to the President of the Armistice Commission, dated eleven days before his death. Inside, the names of three families — hers among them — and the plan entire: the signal station struck in the hour before the delegation's arrival, the harbor blinded, the negotiations thrown into confusion long enough for certain interests to reposition themselves, to salvage, to profit from chaos dressed as grievance. Émile's signature on the pledge below. Her father's signature on an older one.

She had held this letter perhaps a hundred times since finding it sewn into the backing of the old chart of the southern roads, and every time it had felt different in her hands. Heavy, then light. Hot, then cold. Once, over the candle, on the night she had come within a breath of feeding it to the flame — sparing Émile, sparing herself, betraying the dead man who had trusted paper more than people — it had felt like a bird, alive and beating, desperate to be free or destroyed, anything but held.

Tonight it felt like what it was: paper. Ink. The most dangerous substance in the harbor city, and the simplest. Tomorrow, if the fates permitted, it would stop being hers and become history's, and history, she had learned, was not gentle with documents or their couriers.

But first, the binding.

She cleared a space on the table and brought out the atlas's cover boards — heavy pasteboard faced in navy cloth, the corners guarded in brass. The spine was still loose, awaiting final assembly; that had been deliberate, her one small vanity of craftsmanship, a reason to keep the book unfinished until the very last hour. Unfinished things could not be seized and examined as evidence. Finished things must stand in the world and answer for themselves. Tomorrow it would finish, and answer.

She worked quickly and precisely, because precision was the whole of her religion now. A thin blade along the inner spine channel. The cavity — shallow, barely the depth of two fingers' breadth of folded paper, invisible against the swell of a bound atlas. The letter, folded once along Perrin's original crease, slid in with a whisper. Then the strip of matching endpaper, pasted down over it, smoothed with the bone folder until the seam vanished entirely. She turned the cover over, ran her palm across the inside face. Nothing. Smooth cloth, smooth paper, a book like ten thousand other books.

Only her hands knew. She looked at them — ink-stained, nicked, the right forefinger calloused into a ridge by fifteen years of pens and blades — hands that had lied so long they had almost convinced themselves. Tomorrow these hands would sign a receipt at the archive gate, or they would be tied behind her back in a cell, or — she refused the third possibility the way one refuses to look down from a high place. Refusal was not denial. Refusal was navigation. You plotted the course you intended to sail and you let the other courses remain hypothetical, gray lines fading off the edge of the chart.

She assembled the atlas. Sewing the signatures to the prepared cover took an hour; pressing it took another, the wooden planks weighted with the flat irons, and while the press did its silent work she cleaned the studio.

This, too, was ritual. Every tool wiped and laid in its fitted place in the instrument case: the dividers, the parallel rule, the beam compass, the little brass protractor worn silver-bright at its pivot by Perrin's thumb and then by hers. Except — she paused, her hand resting on the case — except the instruments that were gone. Sold, three checkpoints ago, to a customs inspector with soft eyes and hard orders, Perrin's silver instruments surrendered across a scarred table while she smiled and lied and hated herself with a fluency that frightened her. The case had gaps in it now, like a mouth missing teeth. She had told herself she would buy them back after. She told herself a great many things about *after*, and had begun to suspect that *after* was simply the country on the far side of tomorrow, and that no one returned from it unchanged enough to want back what they'd pawned to get there.

Still. The gaps were honest. She closed the case on them.

* * *

Toward the smallest hours she grew restless in the way she always did when the work was done and the waiting had begun — that terrible hourless stretch when the body wants sleep and the mind keeps sounding depths. She lit a second lamp, against all economy, and carried the atlas to the window table where the light was best, though there was no light to speak of beyond the glass but fog and the far-off smudge of the harbor beacons, winking their idiot faithful patterns through the murk.

Pointe des Corbeaux was out there somewhere in the dark. Somewhere beyond it, the delegates were arriving — had perhaps already arrived, escorted through the citadel gate she had studied until she could draw it blindfolded, the gate she could not pass by force or stealth and so must pass *inside the cargo*, inside the official delivery, inside the book itself. Arnaud would be there. She let herself think of him now, in this last private hour, the way one allows oneself a single glass of wine before a fast. Arnaud Vasseur, who walked his rounds like a man reading a difficult text; who had talked to her about maps and home as though home were a country maps could reach; who had waved her through a checkpoint and then doubted, and questioned her by lamplight and released her, gambling his career on her half-truths because something in him — she still didn't know what, and wouldn't ask, and would spend a lifetime not asking, if lifetimes were granted — something in him had decided she was worth the wager.

He would be at the third gate tomorrow. Ordered to inspect every case personally. She had built her entire plan around that fact, had counted on his hands opening the atlas, on his eyes finding the letter's faint rectangular ghost beneath the endpaper if he looked hard enough, on the collision she dreaded more than arrest. Because here was the truth beneath the plan, the coordinate she had never dared print on any chart: she did not want to deceive him again. Deceiving him had become the most expensive thing she did, dearer than silver instruments, dearer than the name she wore. And so the plan's final gate was designed not as a trick survived but as a truth delivered — to him first, alone, before the delegates, before the soldiers, before history got its hands on any of it. Let him open the book. Let him see. Whatever came after, let it begin honestly.

She touched the cover of the atlas once, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you are about to wake.

Then she turned to the title page one last time and read what she had written, and this time the feeling arrived — late, off schedule, overwhelming. *Completed by Félicie Joubert.* Her name. Her actual name, in her actual hand, on the only document in the world that mattered, and no disguise between the ink and the reader. Four years of *Felix* — of squared shoulders and lowered voice and the constant low hum of vigilance, of listening to men discuss her as *the boy* and agreeing with them — and now fourteen letters of plain ink had undone all of it, and undone it *in advance*, before the danger, before the gates, before anyone could protect her by keeping the secret she was finished keeping.

Her eyes stung. She laughed at herself, one dry note in the empty room, and wiped them with the heel of her hand, and blew her nose on her cuff like Perrin used to do, and decided that if she was going to weep she might at least weep efficiently, and got most of it done in a minute.

The fog thinned just enough, in that way fog does near dawn, to show her the harbor: masts like a winter forest, the signal station's dark silhouette on its point, the first gray suggestion of the citadel walls. A city holding its breath. Tomorrow the armistice would be signed in it or sabotaged in it, and either way it would be her atlas lying open on the table when the moment came, her name at the bottom of the title page, her father's sins and her brother's and her own all bound together in navy cloth and brass.

She packed the atlas into its oilcloth, then into the courier's satchel with Roux's credentials in the outer pocket and Perrin's last roll of spare charts padding it snug. She banked the fire. She put on her coat, and stood a moment in the doorway looking back at the studio — at the drafting table scarred by thirty-five years of blades, at the chair, at the dark windows — knowing she might never see it as it was tonight, and finding, strangely, that she could bear it. The studio was not the trade. She was the trade. Wherever she stood tomorrow, the trade would stand there too.

The streets were empty and blue-gray and utterly silent as she walked toward the archive road, her breath smoking, the satchel's strap crossing her chest like a bandolier. At the corner by the shuttered chandlery she stopped and looked back once at the harbor, committing it to memory — the masts, the mist, the waking gulls — the way a surveyor commits a coastline: not because she might lose it, but because it deserved to be remembered accurately.

Then the bells began. Early — a full hour early — ringing not the measured peal she knew but a rapid, broken pattern, alarm and assembly braided together, rolling across the water from the direction of the citadel.

Down at the signal station, a light that should not have been there flickered twice and went dark.

Félicie pulled the strap tight and started to run.
