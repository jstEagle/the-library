## 33. Gate Two and the Cost

The fog came off the water in slow grey sheets, folding the customhouse into itself until only the lanterns were real — a chain of them strung along the quay like beads on a wet black cord, each one haloed in its own small weather. Félicie Joubert stopped fifty paces short of the gate and let the handcart's handle rest against her hip, and she did what she always did before a thing went wrong or right: she counted.

One lantern by the barrier arm. Two sentries beneath it, stamping their boots against the cold. A third man on the steps of the customhouse itself, taller than the others, wearing an officer's cap that the fog had beaded with moisture so that it shone like lacquer. That was new. That was the new inspector Marchetti had warned her about in his theatrical way — *a stickler, my boy, a man who counts the teeth of his own comb* — and she had spent the walk from the ink gate telling herself that a stickler was merely a man who read carefully, and she had learned to survive careful readers.

She counted again because counting steadied her. One lantern. Two sentries. One inspector.

And in the cart, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, one atlas finished three days early and bound tight as a coffin lid, with a letter sewn into its spine that could hang every person she loved — herself included — from the yardarm of some naval tender if it were opened by the wrong hands under the wrong light.

She breathed out through her mouth, the way Perrin had taught her to breathe when ruling long lines, slow and even, so the hand would not tremble. *The line remembers everything you feel,* he used to say, standing behind her shoulder in the studio with the smell of ink and tallow about him. *So feel nothing, or feel only the line.*

"Credentials," said the sentry nearest her when she reached the barrier, and his voice was young and bored and entirely ordinary, and for one foolish moment she thought the morning might pass like all the others.

Madame Roux's courier book was in her breast pocket, wrapped in waxed paper to keep the damp from the stamps. She produced it now with both hands, holding it the way a priest holds a breviary, and watched the young sentry open it and turn past the faded endorsements — Roux & Fils, Marine Couriers, est. 1879; the husband long dead, the business a ghost that still, until this morning, walked.

"Roux," the sentry said, and looked up at her with the mild suspicion of a man who has been told this morning, not ten minutes ago, to look more closely at things. "You're not old Roux."

"I'm his man," Félicie said. Her voice held — it always held, that low steady register she had built like a seawall over eleven years of being called *boy* and *young master* and once, memorably, *sir*, by men who never thought to look further than the flatness of her coat and the ink on her fingers. "Felix Joubert. Apprentice to the late Master Perrin. I've carried for Madame Roux since October."

"Since October." The sentry turned the book over as though the back cover might contradict him. "Wait here."

He carried the book up the steps to the tall man in the shining cap, and Félicie stood in the fog with her hands folded on the handcart's handle and listened to the harbor breathe. Somewhere out in the murk a buoy bell clanged, patient and idiotic. Gulls argued over something she couldn't see. The tide was going out; she could tell by the smell of it, the exposed mud and weed underneath the salt, the harbor showing the underside of itself the way a city does at dawn when it thinks no one is watching.

*Feel nothing, or feel only the line.*

The tall man came down the steps slowly, turning pages with one finger, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked at her.

Up close he was younger than the cap suggested — forty perhaps, with a face like a closed ledger and eyes that did arithmetic. He smelled of coffee and lamp oil and, faintly, of the government issue soap that turned men's collars grey.

"Joubert," he said, trying the name as if weighing coin. "The mapmaker's apprentice."

"The same, sir."

"Perrin's apprentice." He said the dead man's name with a small nod, almost respect, and something in Félicie's chest pulled tight and painful, the way a mooring line pulls when the tide turns. "Perrin was a careful man."

"He was, sir."

"And you are carrying for Roux." He held up the credential book between two fingers. "Who has not presented a courier at this gate in six weeks. Who, I am given to understand, has let his premises on the rue Saint-Clair. Who is, unless my information is stale, dead."

The fog moved between them like something living. Félicie felt the cold come up through the cobbles, through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her feet, and settle in the place where her heart worked.

"Dead men's businesses still have debts, sir," she said. "Debts travel. Someone has to carry them."

It was a good answer. She knew it was a good answer even as she said it, smooth and sad and true in every particular except the one that mattered, and she watched it fail anyway — watched it land on the inspector's closed face and slide off like rain off slate, because he had not stopped her this morning to hear answers. He had stopped her because a stickler counts the teeth of his comb, and somewhere in the machinery of this fog-bound city an order had come down — from whom she could guess, and the guessing made her ill — that parcels leaving the map trade were to be opened, and couriers questioned, and names taken down in ledgers with grey-collared care.

"Open the cart," the inspector said.

*

They did it on a trestle table dragged out onto the quay, under the lantern, because the inspector wanted light and there was no light anywhere but the lanterns. Two sentries lifted the oilcloth while Félicie stood where they put her, four paces back, close enough to see and far enough to be seen seeing, and inside her chest her heart performed the only honest arithmetic left to it: *the atlas is in the cart, the letter is in the atlas, and if they unroll it far enough to reach the binding—*

But they didn't go for the atlas first. They went for the survey plates, because the manifest — forged two nights ago in a fair imitation of Roux's dead clerk's hand, and she hated how well she had done it, hated the fluency of her own treachery — declared them plates, and inspectors check manifests the way children check presents: they take the biggest box first.

The plates came out in their stiff paper sleeves. The sentries laid them on the table one by one, and the fog dripped off the awning above them with a sound like slow footsteps, and Félicie stood with her hands loose at her sides and her face arranged into the mild boredom of a workingman watching other men work, and she catalogued.

That was the trick of it, she had learned. Terror wants to run; terror wants to scream, to weep, to bolt for the water. You do not let it. You give terror a task instead. You make it count things. Twelve sleeves. Six charts, coastal approaches, soundings current to November. Four harbor plans. Two elevation profiles of the headland forts — legitimate, certified, dull as sermons. And rolled separately at the bottom, wrapped in its own oilcloth, the atlas itself, which the manifest described as *one (1) bound proof copy, delivery to Naval Archive, per guild order 44-C.*

The inspector picked up a sleeve, slid a plate halfway out, squinted at the linework in the lantern light.

"Whose hand?"

"Mine, sir. After the master's style. He trained me to it."

"Mm." He slid the plate back. Took another. This one he drew fully out, and held it at arm's length, and tilted it so the raking light caught the ruled lines, and Félicie watched his eyes move along the coast she had drawn — the little bays, the soundings in their neat descending numbers, the lighthouse with its radiating arcs — and she understood suddenly, with a clarity like ice water, that he knew exactly what he was looking at. Not a soldier glancing at paper. A man reading a chart the way she read them, top to bottom, edge to edge, hunting the discrepancy.

"You rule a clean line," he said.

"Thank you, sir."

"That wasn't praise." He set the plate down. "Clean lines are how lies get believed. I knew a quartermaster in '16 who falsified grain weights with beautiful handwriting. They gave him six years." He turned, at last, to the wrapped bundle at the bottom of the cart. "This is the atlas."

"It is, sir."

"Guild order says it goes to the archive sealed. Sealed deliveries get a receipt and a wave-through." He paused, and the pause was a door left deliberately ajar. "Unless the seal is broken. Or unless the courier's papers don't bear weight. In either case—" he gestured, unhurried, almost gentle, "—I inspect."

Félicie looked at the atlas on the table, at the oilcloth dark with fog-damp, at the binding beneath it where a letter sewn into the spine lay against the signatures like a knife against a throat. She looked at the inspector's ledger-face. She looked at the young sentry, who had lost interest and was watching the gulls. She looked out past the barrier arm to where the fog swallowed the road to the citadel, the road to the signing, the road to the only future in which any of this had meant anything.

And then she did the thing she had sworn she would not do, the thing she had rehearsed refusing in the dark of the studio for three nights running, lying awake with Perrin's instruments racked beside her bed like a sleeping congregation: she reached into her coat.

"Sir," she said, "before you cut that binding — which will cost your archive its atlas, and cost me my neck under guild order 44-C, and cost neither of us anything but time — I wonder if I might offer you something else instead."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "A bribe."

"A sale," Félicie said. "There's a difference. A bribe is a secret. A sale is a receipt."

*

The instruments came out of their case one at a time, and each one hurt worse than the last, and she made herself look at none of them longer than a merchant would.

The case itself was walnut, worn silver-grey at the corners where thirty years of Perrin's hands had polished it, lined in green baize gone bald at the folds. In it, nested like relics: the brass parallel rule with its ivory wheels. The beam compass, its steel points capped in cork. The set of dividers with handles of whalebone, yellowed now, warm-looking even in the cold. The proportional scale, that beautiful useless machine of interlocking legs that Perrin had used for teaching, opening and closing it under lamplight while he talked about proportion as a form of honesty. And at the center, in a fitted socket worn to the exact shape of it, the silver-plated planimeter — his prize, bought secondhand in Marseille the year Félicie was born, engraved along the barrel with initials that were not his: *A.P. → J.P.*, father to son, son to apprentice, an inheritance passed hand to hand like a candle flame.

The inspector turned the planimeter over twice.

"This is silver," he said.

"Silver plate."

"Engraved."

"My master's marks."

"Your master," the inspector said slowly, "died owing money to this customs house. Did you know that? Duties unpaid on imported paper stock. It's in my predecessor's ledger. Small sums, but they accumulate, and the estate—" he glanced at her coat, at her boots, at the handcart, cataloguing poverty with a professional eye, "—the estate appears insolvent."

Félicie said nothing. The fog ticked against the awning. Out on the water the buoy bell rang again, closer now, or maybe just louder, hard to say in weather like this.

"These are worth," the inspector said, laying the planimeter down with unexpected delicacy atop the baize, "perhaps sixty francs to a collector. More to a working draftsman. Less to me personally, since I don't draw." He looked at her then, directly, and for the first time the ledger of his face opened a page. What was written on it was not kindness. It was recognition — the flat, tired recognition of one careful person meeting another across a table where both were doing sums. "You're buying passage for a parcel you won't let me open, with tools you can't work without, for a dead man whose debts you're settling in silver. Have I summarized it correctly?"

"You've improved on it, sir."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It was not quite a smile. It was the place where a smile would go if a man could no longer remember the construction.

"Here is my difficulty, Joubert." He began packing the instruments back into their sockets, precisely, wheel to wheel, point to cork, as if he'd packed a draftsman's case all his life — and perhaps he had; everyone in this city had been something else before the war. "If I wave you through and your parcel contains what I think it might contain, I have sold this checkpoint for a handful of silver and I will hang for it by spring. If I open it and it contains what I think it might contain, I must arrest you, and impound the atlas, and write reports, and somewhere above me someone will decide whether those reports inconvenience certain families in this harbor — families with friends — and I suspect, Joubert, I strongly suspect, that the reports will be misfiled, and I will have made enemies for a parcel of paper, and gained nothing but trouble." He closed the walnut case. Set it on the table between them like a chess move concluded. "Whereas if I accept payment of Master Perrin's outstanding duties—"

"Instruments in kind," Félicie said. "Against the debt. With a receipt."

"—then this morning becomes a boring transaction, properly recorded, that no one above me will ever bother to read." He took out his notebook, licked his thumb, turned to a fresh page. "Name?"

"Felix Joubert."

"Apprentice to?"

"The late Augustin Perrin, master chartmaker, guild-certified."

"Goods conveyed?"

"One bound proof atlas, per guild order 44-C, to the naval archive. Survey plates, twelve, miscellaneous, duty paid."

"Duties settled by?" He looked up. "Say it plainly."

Félicie's throat closed around it. Say it plainly. Put it in the ledger. Make it real, irrevocable, a line ruled in ink that cannot be ruled out again. Perrin's tools. Thirty years of his hands. The proportional scale he'd opened and closed while teaching her honesty. The planimeter with his father's initials, the flame handed down. Her livelihood — not metaphorically, not poetically, but in the plain sense that a mapmaker without instruments is a woman without hands, and the guild would not certify her, and no shop would hire her, and the trade he had died protecting would pass out of her reach forever, traded away this morning for a hundred yards of fog-covered cobblestone.

She thought: *burning is also a choice,* and this is only another burning.

She heard herself say, in the flat even voice she used for ruling long lines: "Duties settled by the apprentice, in kind, from the master's estate. Instruments of the trade, itemized. Let me spell them for you."

He spelled them wrong and she corrected him — the parallel rule, the beam compass, the dividers, the proportional scale, the planimeter — and each correction was a small door closing behind her, and she stood straight through every one of them. He wrote in his beautiful bureaucratic hand, blotted it, signed it, tore the carbon copy free, and held it out to her.

"Take it," he said. "Anyone asks, you paid Perrin's duties and got a receipt. That's all this morning was." He nodded once at the atlas, still wrapped, untouched. "Roll your cart, Joubert. The barrier lifts in two minutes and I have eleven more carts after yours, and one of them smells like cheese and I already hate him."

*

She got twenty paces past the barrier before her legs informed her they were considering mutiny. She kept walking. The fog took the customhouse behind her, then the lantern-chain, then the young sentry's voice calling the next cart forward, then everything but the buoy bell and the sound of the wheels and her own breath, which came ragged now that no one could see it, now that the performance was over and the woman alone in her body could shake.

She stopped under the sea wall where the fog was thickest and put both hands flat on the cold stone and breathed until the shaking slowed, and then she unwrapped the carbon copy from her pocket and read it by the grey light, though she knew every word.

*Duties settled in kind. Instruments of the trade, itemized.*

Gone. All of it. The tools that had been her excuse, her cover, her craft, her inheritance-in-waiting — traded for passage, and the passage bought, and the letter riding safe in its sewn spine toward the citadel, toward the signing, toward whatever waited there.

*You can buy new tools,* she told herself, and it was true, and it was also a lie, because these tools could not be bought again. They were not objects. They were Perrin's hands, and she had sold his hands this morning, and the worst part — the part she would not let herself examine, walking on through the fog with the receipt folded against her heart next to the ashes of Madame Roux's credentials — the worst part was the relief.

Because the tools had been the last thing tying her to the old life, the disguised life, the life where she was Perrin's boy and nothing else, competent and invisible and safe. Without them she had nothing left to lose except the atlas, the letter, her name, and the truth — and those, she was beginning to understand, had never really been hers to lose. They were hers to carry.

The road climbed ahead of her toward the third gate. Through a tear in the fog she could see it: the citadel wall, grey stone rising out of the murk like the hull of a beached ship, and at its base the tiny figures of sentries, and above the gate — she narrowed her eyes, wiped the fog-wet from her lashes and looked again — above the gate, a detail of officers, and among them, unmistakable even at this distance, even wrapped to the chin in an officer's greatcoat, a tall figure with a familiar slope to the shoulders who was studying the passing carts with a clipboard in his hand.

Arnaud Vasseur. At the citadel gate. Inspecting every case personally.

Félicie stood on the road with her empty instrument case under her arm and the atlas in the cart at her side, and understood that she had paid everything she owned to reach the one gate in this city guarded by the one man who knew her handwriting, her face, and her lies — and who was, at this moment, waving forward the first cart of the morning.
