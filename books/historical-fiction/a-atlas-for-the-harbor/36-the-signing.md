## 36. The Signing

The citadel hall had been dressed for history the way a body is dressed for burial — with care, with ceremony, and with the unspoken understanding that what lay beneath would not be looked at too closely.

Félicie Joubert stood at the edge of it all, inside the borrowed gray coat of a naval courier, her heart beating so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Bunting in three colors hung from the vaulted ceiling, drooping slightly where the fog had followed them in through the doors. Long tables had been arranged on a dais at the far end of the hall, covered in green baize, set with inkwells of cut glass, pens laid out like surgical instruments. Delegations moved along the walls in slow constellations — French, British, American, each with their adjutants and their interpreters and their faces carved into expressions of gravity appropriate to the ending of a war.

The atlas rode against her chest, bound in its boards and wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the cord Perrin himself had used for deliveries she'd made for him when she was sixteen and still believed the world was drawn to scale.

*You are here*, she told herself, in the language of every chart she had ever made. *This is your position. Mark it.*

She had spent four days inside the delivery cart from the customhouse — no. That was not true, and precision mattered now more than ever; Perrin would have struck through any line so careless. She had spent one night and one day, cramped among the sealed cases of the official consignment, breathing through her mouth against the smell of packing straw and old varnish, listening to the wheels change their note as they crossed from cobbles to flagstone at the citadel gate while Lieutenant Vasseur opened cases not six feet from where she curled behind the false back of the chart press. She had heard his voice say, *This one next,* and had felt the whole architecture of her plan balance on a knife's edge — and then he had passed the case by, because she had built that case herself, and she had built it well.

And then she had walked out of the cart in the confusion of unloading, wearing Roux's courier credentials, carrying the atlas openly, as if she belonged to the machinery of the morning. No one stopped her. No one ever stops a person who walks as though they have somewhere to be.

Now she was somewhere to be, and it was here: the great hall of the citadel, the eleventh hour of the eleventh day approaching, and the letter — sewn flat beneath the binding of the atlas's back cover, where she had placed it two nights ago with hands so steady she hadn't recognized them as her own — waiting to become either evidence or nothing at all.

An aide-de-camp hurried past her with a stack of seating cards. A wireless operator stood in the corner with his apparatus, forbidden from transmitting but present in case the world outside needed reaching. Somewhere beyond the walls, the harbor waited under its fog, and at the tip of the breakwater the signal station stood exactly as she had surveyed it at midnight, its coordinates hidden in the decorative border of plate forty-one in roses and acanthus leaves, where only a person who knew what they were looking at would ever find them.

Perrin had known. Perrin had died knowing. And this morning, if her nerve held, everyone who mattered would know too.

* * *

The trouble with history, Félicie had thought for years, was that it happened elsewhere — in capitals she'd never seen, to people whose names were printed before they were earned. She had believed it the way one believes the frame of a map: that the territory beyond the neat line simply wasn't hers to draw.

She watched the delegates take their seats and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that the frame itself had been a kind of lie. History did not happen elsewhere. It happened wherever someone decided what would be written down. It happened at drafting tables. It happened in cellars where frightened men signed pledges. It happened in the space between a sealed letter and the flame of a candle.

It was happening right now, to her, on purpose, because she had chosen it.

Colonel Berthier — she had learned his name from the seating protocol she had read upside down over an aide's shoulder — rose and called the session to order. His voice carried the particular hollowness of a man performing solemnity. The delegates arranged themselves. Pens were uncapped. An American admiral coughed once, apologetically, into the silence.

Félicie moved.

Not fast — speed was for people without a plan. She moved the way she had taught herself to move for five years: like a man with an errand, shoulders square, eyes forward, the atlas carried at the angle couriers carried things that were expected rather than remarkable. She crossed the width of the hall toward the document tables where clerks sat with the treaty folders, and she said, in the low, bored voice of routine, "Cartographic annex for the delegation. Final corrected plates. Signature required on receipt."

The clerk nearest her was young, exhausted, and drowning in paper. He looked up at her with eyes that said *not another one* and held out a hand.

"It goes to the chair directly," she said. "Master Perrin's harbor survey. The guild sent word ahead."

Had they sent word? Almost certainly not. But the name Perrin meant something even here — the atlas had been commissioned for precisely this purpose before its maker died — and exhaustion is a country where borders are drawn loosely. The clerk gestured toward the head table. "Put it there. He'll want to see it before—" He glanced at the clock. "Before."

*Before.* The word fell into the room like a coin into deep water. Eleven o'clock was coming whether the ink was dry or not.

Félicie set the atlas down on the green baize at the end of the head table, within arm's reach of Colonel Berthier's elbow. Then she stepped back — one step, two — and did the hardest thing she had done since she had first bound her chest with linen in front of a mirror in a dead man's house.

She waited to be seen.

* * *

It took eleven minutes. She counted them by the pendulum of the citadel clock, which swung with the heavy patience of something that had outlived sieges.

In those minutes, the delegations settled into the order of signatures. Names were murmured. Positions adjusted. Through the tall windows the fog pressed like a face against glass, and Félicie thought absurdly of the harbor beyond it — of the fishing boats that wouldn't put out today, of Madame Roux sitting alone in her parlor with a cup of cold tea, of Émile, God help him, wherever Émile was, and whether he was watching the doors.

Then Colonel Berthier turned, reached idly for whatever had been left at his elbow — the reflex of a man tidying his desk before an important task — and pulled the atlas toward him.

He frowned at the binding. It was, Félicie allowed herself to think, a beautiful object; even his frown was a tribute. He untied the cord. He opened the cover. The title page faced him:

*An Atlas for the Harbor. Surveyed and Drawn by Master A. Perrin, Cartographer to the Port. Completed by F. Joubert.*

Not *Perrin's boy*. Not *his apprentice*. Her name, in her own hand, in full, spelled the way her mother had spelled it over a cradle twenty-nine years ago. She had written it two nights ago by candlelight, and her hand had not shaken until after the last stroke, and then it had shaken for a long time.

Berthier's frown deepened. He turned past the title page. Plate forty-one came under his thumb — the signal station, the decorative border, the roses and acanthus that were also latitude and longitude to four decimal places. Beside it, tucked into a guard of tissue paper, the key she had drafted separately: a small explanatory diagram showing exactly how the ornament encoded the coordinates, drawn in her master's precise hand, found by her in the same hiding place as the letter, and copied faithfully.

Berthier looked at the diagram for a long moment. Then he looked up, scanning the hall, and his gaze snagged — as she had known it would, eventually, on some axis or other — on the cluster of civilians seated near the far wall under guild credentials. Guildmaster Ozenne sat among them, silver-haired, immaculate, smiling faintly at the proceedings like a man watching his investment ripen. Beside him, half-shadowed, two men whose names appeared in Perrin's letter. One of them was Monsieur Delacour, whose family shared a pew with the Jouberts had once, years ago, in another life.

"Who delivered this?" Berthier said. His voice was not loud, but the hall had gone quiet enough that quiet things carried.

"I did," Félicie said.

Every head within thirty feet turned. She felt the attention of the room arrive on her like weather — a pressure, a change in the air. Under it, absurdly, she noticed that her boots were wet at the seams from the harbor fog, and that a thread had come loose on the courier coat's second button, and that Lieutenant Arnaud Vasseur was standing by the far doors in full dress uniform, very still, watching her with an expression she could not afford to read.

"You are?" Berthier said.

"Félicie Joubert," she said. "Cartographer. Late apprentice to Master Perrin." She heard her own voice come out level, and marveled at it. "There is a document sewn into the back binding of that atlas, Colonel. I ask you to open it before anyone signs anything. And I ask you to do it quietly."

For a heartbeat she thought he would laugh, or call for her removal, and everything she had built would collapse in a single embarrassed gesture. Instead — because armies are machines for following instructions, and because something in her voice had the ring of a requisition — Berthier drew his penknife from his breast pocket, slit the stitching she had sewn with waxed thread and a sailmaker's needle, and drew out the letter.

Perrin's handwriting. Perrin's seal, unbroken these three years. Three family names. The pledge bearing Émile's signature — *Émile Joubert, who signs for his father* — and the coordinates, and the date, which was today.

Félicie watched the color leave the colonel's face in stages, like tide going out.

* * *

What happened next happened the way she had begged it to happen in the letter she had written to Berthier herself, folded inside the atlas's front board — quietly.

That had been the whole design, in the end. Not drama. Drama kills armistices. If the hall erupted, if the delegations scattered, if the word *plot* escaped these walls before noon, then the conspirators would have won anyway, because the signing would fail, and a failed signing was the same as a sabotaged one. Perrin had understood this; she understood it now; she had written it out in her best round hand: *The crime here is not only the station and the men. The crime is the delay. Therefore the remedy must not delay.*

So Berthier read. Then he passed the letter to the French delegate beside him, who read, and went gray, and passed it on. Aides were summoned with gestures so small they might have been adjusting cuffs. Two military policemen appeared at the doors — appearing, Félicie realized later, exactly as they had been positioned an hour earlier, because Arnaud Vasseur had reported to Berthier's adjutant at dawn with a warning about the harbor families, off the record, out of channels, gambling his career on a mapmaker's half-truths.

Monsieur Delacour rose during the third signature, muttering something about a message he must send, and was met at the aisle by the two gendarmes as though by ushers. There was a low murmur, a brief exchange, and Delacour was walked from the hall with his coat over his arm and his dignity intact and his wrists, she noted, not yet touched. The other names went the same way, one at a time, spaced between signatures, extracted from the room like splinters drawn slowly so the wound doesn't tear.

Ozenne she watched especially. The guildmaster did not run. He sat very still through it all, and she saw the exact moment he understood that his leverage had evaporated — that the letter he had schemed to possess had been surrendered freely, publicly, by the person he had tried to cage, and that a secret revealed belongs to no one. His smile stayed on his face the way paint stays on a wall. When a gendarme finally leaned to speak to him, he nodded, gathered his hat and gloves, and rose with the courtesy of a man leaving a dull lecture. At the door he paused and looked back across the hall, and found her, and held her eyes for a long second.

She inclined her head to him — barely. Courtesy was cheap, and she could afford it now.

The clock's pendulum swung. The pens scratched on. The American delegate signed with a flourish that tore the paper slightly and was blotted with excessive care. The British admiral signed twice because his first signature was judged too faint. And at nine minutes past eleven — history being punctual but not instant — the final name dried on the page, and someone near the doors began, hoarsely, to sing, and the hall emptied onto the citadel steps into the thinning fog while the bells of the harbor city began to count the hour that the guns stopped.

Félicie stood apart from it, on the top step, holding nothing. The atlas remained inside on the document table, entered formally into the record at Berthier's instruction, its plates to be studied, its border decoded, its maker's name spoken aloud in a military proceeding. She had done what she came to do. The letter was out of her hands forever. Émile's fate was out of her hands too — surrendered, like the atlas, like the instruments, like every weight she had carried for three years — and she found that surrender, which she had dreaded above all things, felt less like falling and more like setting something down.

Arnaud found her there. Of course he found her there.

"You wrote your name," he said.

"In the atlas, yes."

"Not only in the atlas." He was looking at her the way he had looked at her charts that evening above the shop — with the particular attention of a man reading terrain he intends to cross carefully and keep. "Félicie."

"Félicie," she agreed. It sounded different in his voice than it had in her own. Everything did.

Below them the crowd roared at the bells, and strangers embraced, and a sailor wept into his cap without shame. Above them the fog was breaking apart into islands, and through the gaps the harbor showed itself — masts and water and the little signal station at the breakwater's end, standing undamaged, flying a new flag.

"There will be questions," Arnaud said. "About me. About you. About how the letter traveled and who helped it travel. Days of them, probably. Weeks."

"I know," she said. "I've drawn out the schedule. You'd be surprised how much of it I can predict."

"I'm never surprised by you," he said. "Only by myself." He offered his arm — formally, absurdly, in front of several hundred cheering people and at least three officers who knew him by name. "Walk with me, mademoiselle. The city appears to be having a celebration, and I find I don't want to be a lieutenant in it. Only a man."

Félicie looked at the harbor, at the water taking back its light as the fog lifted, at the city she had spent five years mapping in secret and one terrible month mapping in earnest. Then she took his arm.

They had descended perhaps half the steps when a military policeman detached himself from the crowd below and started up toward them, moving with the unhurried purpose of a man carrying a summons — and she saw, over his shoulder, coming up the hill through the thinning fog, a figure she knew better than she knew her own charts.

Émile. Walking out of the crowd with his hands visible and empty, coming to surrender himself — or coming, she could not yet tell, to warn her of one last thing.
