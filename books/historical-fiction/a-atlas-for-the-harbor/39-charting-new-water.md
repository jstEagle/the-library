## 39. Charting New Water

The studio smelled of nothing when Félicie first opened its door in the spring — of nothing at all, which was the strangest thing about it. For eleven years that room had held a weather of its own: lamp oil and linseed, the sharp green bite of fresh ink, the dust of paper shavings that gathered in the corners like grey snow, the ghost-tobacco that clung to Perrin's waistcoat and would not leave the upholstery even after his death. Now it smelled only of cold stone and shut windows, of the winter that had passed through it and gone.

She stood on the threshold with her key still in the lock and let herself feel the emptiness properly, the way she had been taught to feel an unsurveyed coastline — without flinching from it, without inventing features that were not there. Perrin had believed that a mapmaker's first duty was honesty about what she did not know. *Blank water is not failure, girl,* he used to say, tapping the open sea beyond the harbor mouth with his pipe stem. *Blank water is an invitation.* She had hated the phrase when she was fifteen. She was twenty-six now, and the word *girl* no longer stung, because she had earned the right to be called by her own name, and names, she had learned, could hold more than they seemed built to carry.

Félicie Joubert. The guild rolls bore it now, inked beneath Auguste Perrin's in the certification ledger, the first woman's name in two hundred years of careful men. It had been written there in March, in Ozenne's hand — Ozenne, who had once offered to cage her for her own protection, and who now signed her papers with a bow so precise it was almost an apology. She did not forgive him, quite. But she had stopped rehearsing arguments with him in the bath, which she supposed was a kind of peace.

She crossed the floor and opened the shutters one by one. Light came in off the harbor in long pale blades, catching the motes, finding the great table where forty-one plates had been born, the racks of rolled charts, the drawers she had hidden inside while men tore her life apart looking for a letter. There was a scar across the surface of the drafting table where a bayonet tip had gouged the wood during the raid; she ran her thumb along it and decided, on the spot, not to have it planed out. Some lines belonged on the map.

The instruments were gone. That was the price of Gate Two — Perrin's silver dividers, his beam compass, his protractor weighted with lead and engraved with initials that were not hers and never had been, surrendered to a fat inspector's palm in exchange for thirty seconds of looking-away. She had mourned them harder than she had expected to, harder even than she had mourned certain people, because tools are honest in a way that people struggle to be. But mourning was for things that could not return, and last week a crate had arrived from Madame Roux containing, wrapped in three layers of linen and a widow's note of apology, a set of instruments bought new from a Paris maker. Not Perrin's. Never his. Hers. Brass instead of silver, the protractor engraved *F. JOUBERT* in letters she had traced four times before she could look away.

She unrolled the new survey book on the table, pinned its corners, and looked out the window at the harbor.

It was changing already. Everyone said the war had frozen the world in place for four years, but the harbor disproved the saying; the harbor could not hold still if it tried. Three of the net barrages had been cut loose in January. The minefield buoys were being lifted by a salvage barge that sat off the breakwater like a patient bird, its crane swinging all day in slow arcs, hauling iron death up into daylight where it could be dismantled and forgotten. Two blockships that had scowled across the channel entrance since 1914 were scheduled to be raised in June. Dredgers worked the silted fairway near the customhouse quay. And everywhere, on every quay and slipway, men in peacetime clothes moved with the tentative, half-embarrassed energy of people relearning how to build things rather than break them.

None of the existing charts showed any of it. Plate forty-one — her plate, finished in fury and terror and sealed under her master's name and then her own — showed the harbor as it had been in November 1918, armed to the teeth, bristling, correct for exactly as long as it took history to move again. An atlas is always a portrait of something that has just stopped existing. That was the grief and the glory of the trade.

Which meant there was work. Which meant, God help her, that there was a reason to get up in the morning that required no argument at all.

She sharpened a pencil, opened the survey book to its first blank page, and wrote at the head of it, in her neatest hand:

*New Survey of the Harbor of ——, begun this 14th day of April, 1919. Surveyor: F. Joubert.*

Then she sat down and began, very quietly, with the salvage barge's moorings, to draw the world back in.

* * *

The knock came at eleven, the particular knock of someone who had knocked at closed doors many times in his life and expected little. Félicie found a boy on the step — fourteen perhaps, or the kind of fourteen that hardship made look older — with hollows under his eyes and a canvas satchel hanging off one shoulder like a dead weight he had grown used to carrying.

"You're the mapmaker," he said. Not quite a question.

"I'm the mapmaker," she agreed. "You're the boy who's been standing across the street every morning this week, watching my window."

He had the grace to flush. "I wanted to see if you did real work or just drew pictures."

"And your verdict?"

"Can't tell yet. You mostly sit." He shifted the satchel. Inside it, she could hear the metallic whisper of instruments — cheap ones, secondhand ones, the sound of poverty trying anyway. "My name's Lucien Aubry. My father was a pilot on the dredger *Marguerite* before the mine took her crew in 'sixteen. I've been working the quays since. I can row, I can read a tide table, and I can hold a boat steady in chop while another man sights, which is more than most boys can say." He recited it like a catechism, and she recognized the rhythm, because she had invented that same rhythm for herself at fifteen, standing in front of Auguste Perrin with her hair bound up and her voice pitched low, listing everything she could do in hopes it would outweigh everything she was not permitted to be.

"Who told you to come?"

"No one." His jaw set. "I came because I want to learn true work. And because—" he hesitated, and pride fought need across his face, and need won, barely, "—because everyone says you're the one who carried the atlas through the gates. My mother cried when she read about it in the *Courier*. She says you saved the armistice. She says—"

"Your mother is generous," Félicie said, before the legend could grow another limb. What she had done had been necessary, and terrifying, and it had also, in the retelling, swollen into something she barely recognized. In the popular version there was no fog, no shaking hands, no hour spent on the breakwater failing to tell Arnaud Vasseur the truth, no moment at the citadel when her whole future had hung on the angle of a lieutenant's jaw as he looked down at a letter sewn into a binding. In the popular version she was brave the whole time. She wished she could meet that woman someday. She suspected they would get along poorly.

"The guild hasn't authorized apprenticeships under my name," she said carefully. "There's a form. There will be a committee. Ozenne will want to inspect your penmanship, and Ozenne inspects penmanship the way customs men inspect parcels."

"I don't care about the guild."

"You should. The guild is how a chart gets believed. A map without certification is just a rumor with coordinates." She looked at him a long moment — at the ink stains already ghosting his fingertips, at the way his eyes kept drifting past her shoulder toward the lit interior of the studio, hungry and measuring. Then she stepped aside. "Come in anyway. Show me your hand. If you're hopeless, I'll teach you hopelessness first and we'll go from there."

He crossed the threshold the way she had once crossed thresholds — as though the floor might be pulled from under him, as though rooms like this belonged to other people. She remembered the exact temperature of that feeling. She watched him set his satchel down on Perrin's old bench and begin, unprompted, to unwrap his instruments with the reverence of a boy handling relics, and something in her chest turned over and settled in a new position.

"Well then, Lucien Aubry," she said, and picked up her own pencil. "First lesson. Blank water is not failure."

* * *

They worked through April and into May. She taught him lettering first, because Perrin had taught her lettering first — the discipline of making ten thousand identical characters before being trusted with a single line of coast. He chafed, as she had chafed. She let him chafe, as Perrin had let her, and privately enjoyed every sulking minute of it.

Twice a week they took the studio's borrowed dinghy out onto the gray water, Lucien holding her steady while she sighted bearings on the new channel markers, and the harbor gave up its secrets slowly, grudgingly, one sounding at a time. The war's scars were legible everywhere if you knew how to read them, and she was teaching herself to read them even as she taught him letters. Here, a stretch of seabed where the salvage crews had dragged, marked by a softness in the echo. There, the drowned hulk of a barge the blockade had sunk, which the charts recorded as a hazard but which the fisherman's boys had already renamed *the Cathedral*, because at low spring tides its broken mast ribs broke the surface like architecture. The harbor was composing its own postwar geography without waiting for anyone's permission, and her job — her trade, her inheritance, her name — was to catch it in the act.

Some days Arnaud came with them.

He was on half-pay until summer, attached to the port commission that was deciding which fortifications to dismantle and which to keep, and he wore civilian clothes badly, like a man perpetually surprised by his own sleeves. On the water he was useful without hovering; he could handle a line, he had an eye for distances, and he asked questions that were better than most grown men's questions. *Why does the current shear there when the tide's making? Why do you take the bearing twice?* Once, watching her plot a fix, he had said quietly, "You look like yourself when you're doing this. You didn't, before. Not entirely." And she had kissed him with salt on both their mouths, because some answers do not require coordinates.

But it was on a Thursday in late May, alone in the studio with the evening light going amber across the table, that the work itself finally caught up with her and did its quiet surgery.

Lucien had gone home. She was fair-copying the day's soundings — the recovered fairway, deep and clean again, ready to receive ships from nations that had recently been enemies — when she realized she was humming. Something tuneless, something she had never hummed in her life. Her pencil moved in steady strokes, each depth figure placed with the small satisfaction of a stone set true in a wall, and her shoulders, which had been hunched around her ears since November of 1918, were — she noticed them deliberately, the way one notices a pain only after it stops — lowered. At ease. The room smelled of ink again. Of linseed. Of work.

She thought about Émile, in the garrison town where he was serving out a sentence that justice, mercifully unimaginative, had reduced to labor and time. She had written to him twice. He had answered the second letter, four lines, stiff as a new chart: *I am well. The masons' work suits me better than I deserve. Tell your apprentice the tide tables are wrong for the outer roads, my father always said so and he was right.* She had laughed and then wept, briefly and efficiently, and corrected the tables. That was what healing looked like in her family, apparently — corrections issued through third parties, love smuggled inside technical criticism. She would take it. She had learned at the checkpoints that cargo matters more than wrapping.

She thought about Perrin, whose grave now bore a stone reading not merely *Auguste Perrin, Master Chartmaker* but, beneath it, in smaller letters she had insisted upon, *Who Kept the Truth*. The atlas sat in the naval archive with both their names tooled into its spine, and cadets, she was told, were already being shown the decorative border of plate forty-one and invited to find the coordinates hidden in the acanthus scrolls — a teaching exercise now, a legend, a story with the fear boiled out of it.

And she thought, as the light failed and she reached to trim the lamp, of the page in her survey book — the first page, the one headed with her own name — and of how much white space remained on it. Years of it. Decades, if she was fortunate. Channels not yet dredged, quays not yet built, a harbor that would keep changing as long as the sea bothered to move, which was to say forever. Blank water. An invitation.

Perrin had been right about that, as about most things. She forgave him the word *girl*, finally and completely, somewhere between one sounding and the next.

* * *

In June the blockships were raised, and Félicie and Lucien surveyed the operation from the studio roof with a telescope and a shared bag of cherries, arguing cheerfully about how to render a half-sunken hull mid-lift. In July the first liberty steamers came through the new fairway, and the harbor filled with flags that meant nothing worse than commerce. In August she sent the first three sheets of the new survey to the guild, and Ozenne returned them certified with a note so brief it was almost tender: *Accurate. Signed, at last, by the right name. — G.O.*

On a bright morning in September, she spread the sheets out on the great table and stood over them with Lucien at her elbow, and saw, for the first time, the whole shape of what they were making — not a memorial, not an evidence, not a weapon or a confession or an inheritance defended at gunpoint. Just a map. Just the harbor, honest and new, drawn by a woman and a dockyard boy for anyone alive enough to need it.

"There," she said, and touched the empty margin at the edge of the largest sheet, the white space beyond the harbor mouth where the known world ended. "That's where we go next."

Lucien leaned in, squinting at the blankness. "What's out there?"

"I haven't the faintest idea," said Félicie Joubert, and began to sharpen her pencils.
