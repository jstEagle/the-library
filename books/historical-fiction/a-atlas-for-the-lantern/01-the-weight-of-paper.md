## 1. The Weight of Paper

The summons came at dusk, as the good ones always did.

Vesper Joubert had learned long ago that a courier's life was measured not in years but in envelopes, and that the most dangerous of them arrived wrapped in ordinariness—a plain folded paper slipped beneath a door, a nod across a crowded café, a name spoken once in a stairwell and never repeated. This one came as all his final assignments had come: through the mouth of a woman who smelled of ink and cold tea, in a room with no windows worth mentioning, on the last floor of a building that officially housed nothing but the Ministry of Weights and Measures.

He climbed the four flights out of habit, counting them without meaning to. Sixty-two steps. The banister was loose at the fortieth; he had reported it twice, in the manner of a man who wished to be remembered for small, correct things rather than large, interesting ones. Nobody had fixed it. Nobody had fixed anything in the Ministry of Weights and Measures for three years, because the Ministry had stopped measuring weights some time ago and now weighed other matters entirely: the movement of couriers, the drift of secrets, the slow collapse of a republic that still insisted on calling itself by its old name in official correspondence, as if naming were the same as keeping.

The door at the top had no number. He knocked twice, paused, knocked once.

"Enter, Joubert."

Madame Corvess sat behind a desk bare except for a lamp, a ledger, and a cup of tea gone cold enough to grow a skin. She was sixty if she was a day, small as a folded letter, with hands that looked soft and were not. She had been handling couriers since before Vesper could read a timetable, and she had buried more of them than she would ever admit, each one filed away under a designation rather than a name, because names were liabilities and liabilities were the one currency the republic no longer possessed.

"You look well," she said, which was a lie they exchanged every time, and which he answered with the customary lie.

"The weather agrees with me."

"Winter is coming to disagree." She gestured at the chair opposite her desk. He sat, keeping his spine off the backrest out of an old habit—couriers who slouched looked like men with somewhere comfortable to be, and men with somewhere comfortable to be got remembered. "How much do you know about the situation east?"

"That it isn't one," Vesper said. "Officially."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, the Third Army hasn't sent dispatches in eleven days. Unofficially, the northern garrisons are trading their horses for bread. Unofficially—" He stopped himself, the way he'd been trained to stop himself, at the edge of the sentence that mattered.

"Go on," said Madame Corvess. "In this room you may finish sentences."

"Unofficially, it's finished."

She looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—not surprise, which she was far too old for, but a kind of weary recognition, the way one recognizes a season. Then she opened the ledger and slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. On it, written in the flat administrative hand of a dying bureaucracy, was his assignment.

*Courier J.V., seventh circuit. One item, sealed, non-negotiable. Destination: Halvard workshop, beyond the Sereval pass. Delivery by hand only. Confirmation by seal exchange. Duration: twelve days.*

"Halvard," Vesper said. He knew the name the way he knew half the names in the borderlands—from timetables, from route memorization, from the great mental atlas every courier carried and no courier ever wrote down. A map house. An engraving shop. A place where paper went in and paper came out, and nobody asked what the paper said because maps were considered innocent, and innocence was the best camouflage a thing could wear.

"You'll carry sealed cipher materials," Madame Corvess said. "Diplomatic codes, current rotation. They must be deciphered and delivered onward before the season's last bridge closes. You know the schedule better than I do—the crossing at Sereval shuts within the fortnight, and after that, nothing moves until spring except snow."

"And if I miss it?"

She didn't answer immediately. Instead she rose, went to the cold tea, poured it into a potted plant that had died sometime during the previous administration, and stood with her back to him, looking at the wall where a map of the republic had once hung. There was a paler rectangle there now, the ghost of the map's outline, the way a painting leaves its shadow on plaster.

"If you miss it," she said finally, "you winter on the wrong side of the river with a satchel full of paper that will be worthless by thaw. If you make it, you confirm delivery and come home." She turned around. "That's the whole of it, Joubert. That's the mission."

"It seems small."

"It is small." She came back to the desk and sat, folding her soft-looking hands. "And I'm going to tell you something I'm not required to tell you, because you've carried for me eleven years without losing so much as a receipt." Her voice dropped into the register she used for truths too heavy to say at full volume. "The republic is finished. Not threatened. Not endangered. Finished. What we're doing here—" she tapped the ledger, tapped the assignment, "—is formality. The codes are real, the seals are real, the deadline is real. But the government that issued them has perhaps a season left, maybe two, and everyone in this building knows it, including the Minister, including me, including the man who signs your pay chits with someone else's signature. You are carrying paper across a mountain for a country that will not exist when you return. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Vesper understood several things at once, and chose to answer the simplest. "You're telling me the mission is a formality."

"I'm telling you it's worse than that. It's an epitaph." She pushed a small oilcloth packet across the desk. Inside, when he unwrapped it, lay the seals: two discs of dull lead, unmarked to any eye that didn't know to press them into wax at the correct angle. Beneath them, the cipher sheets themselves—folded twice, bound in grey board, looking for all the world like a merchant's inventory. "You are the last courier we're sending east. After you, we stop pretending. So whatever you feel about this mission, feel it tonight, at home, alone, and leave it behind tomorrow morning. I need the man, not the man's opinions."

"I don't have opinions, madame."

"Eleven years ago you didn't. Now you have them and hide them well, which is nearly as good." She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to affection their profession permitted, and he received it the way he received everything: quietly, without leaving fingerprints.

* * *

He walked home through streets that smelled of coal smoke and wet stone, past shuttered shops and queues that formed even where there was nothing to queue for, because queuing had become a form of hope and the people of the capital clung to their hopes in lines. A newsboy was selling papers whose headlines contradicted the ones sold that morning, and nobody seemed to mind. Two soldiers stood outside a requisitioned warehouse arguing softly about whether desertion was theft. Somewhere above it all, church bells rang the hour, six strokes, unhurried, indifferent, as bells had been indifferent to every empire that ever fell beneath them.

Vesper's lodging was a single room over a cobbler's shop, furnished the way doctrine demanded: a bed, a chair, a table, nothing on the walls, nothing in the drawers that couldn't be burned in thirty seconds. He had lived in it four years and could have left it in ninety. He had always taken pride in that—ninety seconds, bed to street, nothing owed to any object, nothing owed to any person. *Carry nothing, owe nothing, be nothing.* That was the first law of the courier's trade, taught to him by a handler older than Corvess, dead now, or transferred, which amounted to the same thing.

But tonight, sitting on the narrow bed with the oilcloth packet on his knees, weighing the seals in his palm like small cold coins, he found the first law suddenly difficult to recite.

It wasn't fear. Fear was a tool; he kept his sharpened and oiled like everything else he owned. It was something quieter and stranger: the sense of holding weight that had nothing to do with mass. The packet couldn't have been more than four hundred grams. He knew because he knew weights the way priests know psalms. Four hundred grams of paper and lead, and somewhere inside those folded sheets was the current diplomatic rotation of a republic's entire voice abroad—every code, every key, every sentence it might still wish to say to friends who were rapidly becoming strangers. Four hundred grams, and it was heavier than any refugee's bundle on any road he'd ever walked.

Because refugees carried what they loved. He carried what everyone else had decided to stop loving.

He caught himself at the thought and examined it, turning it over the way he'd turn over a suspect coin. That was new. That was eleven years too late. Doctrine said a courier's cargo was cargo—sealed, silent, unconsidered. To think about the contents was to start caring about them, and to care was to hesitate, and hesitation killed more couriers than cavalry ever had. He had watched men die on roads because they paused to wonder whether what they carried mattered. He had promised himself, at twenty-three, standing over the body of a colleague named Aurel whose name he was never permitted to speak again, that he would never wonder. Cargo was cargo. The republic thought; he carried.

And yet.

He set the packet on the table, lit the lamp, and did what he always did before a run: he prepared. Boots resoled but not new. Coat mended but not fine. He ate a plain meal of bread and hard cheese, chewing without tasting, and laid out his papers—a grain merchant's travel warrant, stamped with authentic boredom, describing a man named Verel Jonas who bought and sold barley and had no opinions and no history and no face anyone would recall five minutes after he passed. He practiced being Verel in the dark window glass: shoulders slightly rounded, gaze level but unambitious, the expression of a man perpetually calculating margins. It fit him the way water fits a cup. It had always fit him. That was the trouble, he realized—he could no longer remember whether it was a costume or whether the costume had eaten the man whole.

Outside, snow began—not the real snow of the pass, just the city's dirty preliminary dusting, already browning at the edges. Winter coming to disagree, as Corvess had said. Twelve days to cross the mountains and reach Sereval before the bridge closed. Twelve days of being Verel Jonas, of sleeping in inns under a false name, of answering questions with questions until people stopped asking. Twelve days, and then confirmation, and then home—to what? To a ministry emptying itself floor by floor, to handlers burning files in copper basins, to a republic conducting its own quiet funeral while insisting the patient felt fine?

He blew out the lamp and lay down clothed, as he always did the night before a run, and listened to the snow tick against the glass like fingers trying the latch.

Somewhere below, the cobbler was still working, hammering heels into the small hours, and the rhythm of it—tap, tap, tap, patient as a heartbeat—kept time with Vesper's thoughts. *The republic is finished.* She had said it plainly, and he had absorbed it professionally, and only now, in the dark, did it land where such words actually live: in the chest, in the hands, in the sudden animal awareness that everything solid is held up by agreement, and agreements end. He had spent his whole adult life being the invisible servant of a visible thing. Take away the thing, and what was invisible serving then? What was a courier of a country that had stopped existing—a smuggler? A ghost? A man carrying mail for a house already burned?

Sleep took him near dawn, shallow and brief, full of bridges.

* * *

He presented himself at the freight yard before first light, as arranged, wearing Verel Jonas's face. The duty clerk—an elderly man with ink-stained cuffs and the thousand-yard calm of someone who had stopped expecting the war to make sense—checked his warrant against a list, found him suitably boring, and waved him toward the eastbound goods train.

"You'll change at Kolvary," the clerk said. "Then the mountain road. No passenger service beyond the pass after Thursday, if there's a service at all. Roads are... uncertain."

"Uncertain how?"

The clerk looked at him with mild, exhausted pity. "Merchant. How do you think?"

The train pulled out through the grey morning, past the gasworks and the cattle pens, past the last suburbs where laundry hung frozen on lines like flags of small defeated nations. Vesper rode in the guard's van among crates of tinned fish and machine parts, the oilcloth packet sewn flat against his ribs beneath his coat, where it rode like a second heart—smaller, colder, beating to a different clock. Twelve days. He counted them forward from the yard, subtracting delays the way a miser subtracts coins: a day for Kolvary, two for the mountain road if the snow held off, margin for the unexpected, which in the borderlands was never unexpected, merely unscheduled.

At the first halt, a farming town with a name he forgot on principle, refugees came down from the hills and stood along the platform waiting for a westbound train that the schedules still promised and the railway had already abandoned. They had bundles. Of course they had bundles—blankets, pots, photographs in tin boxes, a birdcage with the bird still in it, absurd and beloved. One old woman held a framed portrait wrapped in an apron, and she held it the way you hold a child, and Vesper looked at her longer than doctrine allowed, and then made himself look away.

*They carry everything,* he thought. *I carry nothing.* And then, with the quiet dread settling deeper beneath the professional calm, like sediment sinking through still water: *and yet mine weighs more.*

The train jolted forward. East, toward the pass, toward Sereval, toward the Halvard workshop where paper went in and paper came out. Against his ribs, the seals pressed their small cold circles into his skin, and Vesper Joubert—Verel Jonas, merchant of barley, man of no consequence—closed his eyes and began, methodically, to forget himself.
