## 2. What Can Be Carried

The doctrine had nine articles. Vesper Joubert could recite them in the dark, and often did, on nights when the road gave him no roof and his own heartbeat was the only clock he trusted.

Travel light. Speak little. Carry nothing that can be read. Carry nothing that can be wept over. Be neither first nor last through a gate. Learn three trades badly rather than one well. Give your name to no one; let them invent one for you and be that. Sleep facing the door. And the ninth article, the one the trainers in the Ministry's cellar had repeated until it lost its shape as language: *what you do not carry cannot be taken from you.*

He had lived by it for eleven years. It had kept him alive through two changes of government, one border war, and the long slow rot of a republic that still printed its eagle on documents no one believed in anymore. He owned, in the whole world, what hung on his body and what sat against his back: a coat of undyed wool, two shirts, a razor of no particular quality, bread wrapped in cloth, flint, a folding knife with a broken corkscrew, and inside the oilskin lining of the satchel's inner flap—though this he did not think of as belonging to him—the sealed packet of cipher leaves bound for the Halvard workshop beyond the pass.

The satchel weighed less than four pounds. He knew because he weighed it every morning out of habit, standing on the brass scale in whatever room he'd slept in, adjusting the straps so the weight sat symmetrical between his shoulders. A courier who favored one side developed a gait, and a gait was a signature, and a signature was a name, and a name was a debt. He walked balanced, therefore, like a man carrying water across ice, which in a sense he was.

The valley road east of Cormelle ran along the river for a day and then turned north into hill country where the villages had names in an older tongue and the church bells rang off-key from disrepair. He made twelve miles the first day, fifteen the second, keeping to the pace of a man going somewhere for unremarkable reasons—a factor visiting a tannery, a son checking on a mother's property. He had rehearsed all three identities before dawn and discarded two by noon. The third, a surveyor's assistant named Pell, fit him like the coat: loose enough not to bind.

It was on the third morning, in the grey hour when the frost gives up its argument with the grass, that he came over the shoulder of a hill and saw the road below him full of people.

For a moment his mind refused the arithmetic of it. The road bent along a stream and disappeared around a wooded point, and in the visible half-mile of it there were perhaps two hundred souls, and the sound reached him a beat later—the low composite murmur of many families moving together, cart wheels, a donkey complaining, a child crying in the flat exhausted way of a child who has been crying for days. They were moving west, toward Cormelle, toward the capital, away from the pass he himself was climbing toward.

Refugees. From the north country. From the direction of the war that official bulletins insisted was stabilizing.

Vesper stopped at the top of the hill and did what he had been trained to do: he looked without appearing to look. He counted carts (nine), soldiers in civilian coats (two, possibly three), able-bodied men traveling alone (none—that registered, and the reason for it settled in his stomach like swallowed stone). He noted the direction of their glances, which was everywhere behind them, over their shoulders, back up the road they had come from, as if the thing they fled might be walking at the speed of rumor and could arrive any minute on the next hill.

Then he went down among them, because the only thing stranger than meeting the flood was standing still while it passed.

* * *

They parted around him the way water parts around a post. He was going the wrong way; that alone made him invisible, or nearly. He became, briefly, a curiosity—a man walking east with one small satchel while the whole world walked west with everything it owned—and he heard the things curious people say to such a man.

"War's behind you, friend," said a carter whose wagon bed held a stove, a cradle, four chickens in a slatted coop, and an old woman wrapped in two blankets despite the climb making her flush. "Whatever you're carrying, it ain't worth the road you're on."

"Just trade," Vesper said, in Pell's mild voice. "Paperwork for a mill owner."

The carter laughed, short and bitter. "Paperwork." He said the word again, softer, as if tasting something spoiled, and flicked his reins, and the wagon groaned past.

But others stopped longer. An old man walking beside a handcart asked him if the bridges at Sereval were still open, and Vesper said he believed so, and the old man thanked him and told him, unprompted, that the ferryman's cousin had said the crossing would shut with the first real snow, and the first real snow was due within the fortnight if the geese were honest. Vesper filed this the way he filed everything, automatically, in the ledger behind his eyes: *Sereval closes soon. Count days like coins.* But the filing cost him something this time, because the old man's hands were shaking, and the handcart held exactly three possessions: a portrait in an oval frame, face-down, wrapped in sacking; a locked box the size of a hymnal; and a bundle of seed potatoes wrapped in the man's good coat.

A woman with two children asked if there was food to be had in Cormelle. He said the city was full, that prices were high, that she should try the church hospices first. She thanked him with the terrible courtesy of people who have learned that strangers owe them nothing, and her children stared at his satchel—at how small it was, how light—with an expression he had never been on the receiving end of before. It took him a quarter mile of walking to name the expression. It was envy. Clean, unashamed envy. To them he was the lightest creature on the road, a man who had somehow gotten ahead of the great law that says everyone must carry everything, forever.

*What you do not carry cannot be taken from you.*

He walked among them all that day and part of the next, because their pace set his, the road being too thick with carts to pass, and somewhere in those miles the ninth article began, very quietly, to come apart in his hands.

* * *

It happened at a well outside a hamlet whose name sign had been shot through, so that it read only *—mont*, which could have been anything. A family had drawn water and was resting in the trampled verge: a father, a mother, a girl of maybe fourteen, a boy of eight asleep under the cart. The mother saw Vesper's canteen and offered him bread before he had asked for anything—he understood later that she offered because to give food was to prove to yourself you still had some—and he accepted, because refusing hospitality drew more notice than accepting it.

"You're the fourth man we've seen going east," the father said. "All of you alone. All of you light."

"Tradesmen," Vesper said.

The father nodded slowly, and looked at the road behind Vesper, north, toward the pass and the hills and whatever was coming down out of them. "My brother was a light man once," he said. "Carried nothing. Said the same as anyone says—that what you don't own can't be burned, what you don't love can't be buried." He turned his cup in his hands. "When the requisitions came, they took his horse anyway. When the conscription came, they took him. Lightness didn't protect him. It just meant nobody came to help him, because nobody knew he was gone."

The mother touched the father's arm—not to silence him, Vesper thought, but to steady him, the way you steady a lamp.

"We're heavy," the father went on, gesturing at the overloaded cart, the sleeping boy, the girl already standing watch by the roadside with a wood axe across her knees because she had appointed herself sentry and no one had argued. "We're so heavy it takes us all day to move eight miles. Every stick of this is somebody's whole life and every mile is a negotiation about what gets left behind. Last night my wife's mother chose to stay with the house." He said it without inflection, which was worse than any inflection. "Chose. That's the word we're using."

Vesper heard himself say, "I'm sorry," and heard how it sounded: the exact phrase, pitched at the exact frequency, that a man named Pell would offer a stranger. Professional sorrow. Weighed to the dram.

The father looked at him then, really looked, and Vesper felt the old fear rise—the fear of being *seen*, the cardinal sin of his trade—and braced for the question that would unravel him. Instead the man said only:

"Who carries yours?"

"I beg your pardon."

"When you get wherever you're going. Who waits for you? Who'd know to look for you if you didn't come?"

"No one," Vesper said, before he could choose a better answer. It was true, and truth, in his experience, was safest precisely because it required no maintenance.

The father nodded again, and there was something in the nod—not pity, exactly, but its colder cousin, recognition—that stayed with Vesper the rest of the day like a stone in his boot.

* * *

That night he slept in a hay barn two fields off the road, sharing the loft with six other travelers who asked him nothing, having nothing left over for questions. He positioned himself facing the ladder, per the seventh and eighth articles, and lay listening to the sounds of strangers breathing around him, and did the accounting he did every night.

Satchel: four pounds. Papers carried: one packet, sealed, unreadable, deniable. If seized, the story: apprentice bookbinder transporting sample quires for a master in Halvard. Plausible. Practiced. The seals themselves were worked into the stitching of the flap, wax under thread under leather, invisible unless you knew—unless someone showed you where to cut.

Name carried: none. Pell by day, Joubert never, the true name spoken last aloud eleven years ago in a cellar office in the Ministry district, by a handler named Corvez, on the day of his first commission. *Forget it*, Corvez had said. *Not hide it. Forget it. A hidden name can be found. A forgotten one has to be rebuilt from nothing, and no one will pay that price.* Vesper had obeyed. He was fairly sure he had obeyed. Some nights, drifting toward sleep, a name would surface that felt warm, that felt like it fit his mouth, and he would let it sink back down without testing whether it was his.

Obligations carried: none. Debts: none. People owed grief by him, people owed him grief: none on either ledger. This was the doctrine's deepest promise, the one Corvez had saved for last, the one that had sounded like freedom to a nineteen-year-old with nothing: *carry nothing, owe nothing*. The republic asks everything of the heavy and nothing of the light. Be weather. Be freight that ships itself.

He lay in the hay and listened to the family nearest him in the loft—a mother and three children, though he couldn't see them in the dark, only hear the arrangement of breath—and found himself doing the other accounting, the one he had never done before, the one with no article number.

The old man's portrait, face-down, wrapped in sacking. Wrapped, Vesper understood, not to protect it from rain but so the owner wouldn't have to keep looking at what he was spending his strength to save. Three possessions in all the world, and one of them chosen knowing it would hurt.

The carter's stove. Who takes a stove on a road going nowhere certain, uphill, in the first week of winter? A man who has decided that the difference between fleeing and exile is whether you bring the thing you cook on. A man insisting, with two hundred pounds of iron, that he intends to live somewhere, cook something, be someone with a kitchen, again, someday.

The girl with the axe, standing her watch in the cold because her father had not told her no.

And against all this, himself: four pounds, no portrait, no stove, no axe, no one. The lightest thing on the road. The thing the refugees envied.

He tried to feel what they imagined he must feel, and could not find it. What he found instead, turning over slowly in the dark of the loft like something rising from pond bottom, was the father's question wearing a different grammar now. Not *who carries yours?* but—who had ever chosen him to be light? Not God, not fate, not philosophy. The Ministry. Corvez. The republic, in its cellar offices, selecting men with no anchors because anchored men ask questions, anchored men refuse assignments, anchored men, when disowned—and couriers were always eventually disowned, this was not cynicism but arithmetic—had someone to make noise about it.

Lightness wasn't freedom. Lightness was what remained after everyone who might have objected had been subtracted.

He was thirty-two years old, and he was learning this on a barn floor full of straw and other people's breath, and the learning felt less like a revelation than like a bill arriving for something consumed long ago.

* * *

He was on the road before light, as the doctrine preferred, and the fifth day brought him up out of the hill country toward the river plain, and the refugee stream thinned to trickles, and then the road was nearly his alone—which should have been relief, and was, and also was not.

At midday he topped a rise and saw, far off across the grey plain, the smoke of a town in a river bend: Sereval, bridge town, last crossing before the high pass. Even at this distance he could pick out the bridge itself, a dark stitched line across the bright scar of the frozen river, and the small fort at its southern end, and the cluster of roofs that fed on the crossing like pilot fish on a whale.

He stood a long moment doing the arithmetic. Six days' hard walking to the Halvard workshop beyond the pass, assuming weather. The geese, if honest, gave him a fortnight. Perhaps ten days. Margin enough, barely, if nothing failed.

Behind him, far to the north, the hills he had come down from held the whole displaced country, walking west with everything, heavy as scripture. Ahead of him lay the town where a ferryman's cousin traded rumors of closing dates, and beyond that the pass, and beyond that a house full of maps where men would take his four pounds of sealed paper and do he-knew-not-what, for he was not permitted to know, and had never wanted to know, and was beginning—with the specific nausea of a man discovering the floor was thinner than believed—to want to know very much indeed.

He adjusted the satchel straps until the weight sat symmetrical between his shoulders, out of habit, and started down toward the river, and it was only after a mile that he noticed what his hands were doing: holding the flap of the satchel closed, gently, the way you'd hold a stranger's hand.
