## 3. The Bridge at Sereval

The snow began at the top of the last ridge, not in flakes but in grains, a fine dry hiss against the pines, as though the sky were sifting flour over the world. Vesper Joubert stopped on the trail and let it land on his shoulders without brushing it away. Doctrine said stop when the light changed. Doctrine said read the weather the way you would read a strange room: before it reads you.

Below him, half a valley deep, the town of Sereval lay along the river like coins spilled from a torn purse — grey roofs, grey smoke, the black seam of the water running through the middle of everything. And there, just visible even at this distance, the bridge: a long pale spine of stone and timber crossing from the republic's shore to the far bank, where the road climbed toward the pass and, three days beyond it, the Halvard workshop.

He counted what he could see out of habit. Eleven chimneys smoking near the waterfront. Two barges tied at the lower dock, both riding high, unloaded. A line of carts on the near approach to the bridge, waiting. He counted because counting kept the other thing quiet — the thing that had been growing in him since he left the capital, since his handler's last words in that windowless room: *The republic is finished, Vesper. This is a formality. Deliver the seals, come home by the winter road if it's still open.*

A formality. As if paper could be formal at a funeral.

He went down into the town.

* * *

Sereval smelled of wet rope, coal smoke, and the particular sourness of a place that lived off a crossing and feared losing it. The main street ran parallel to the river, and every third doorway seemed to sell something a traveller needed at the last moment: oilskins, dried fish, lamp oil, boots, prayers printed on cheap paper. The refugees had passed through here weeks ago — Vesper had been walking among them, slightly apart from them, for two days now, sharing their roads but never their fires — and their traces were everywhere. A child's mitten frozen into the gutter. Wheel ruts cut so deep by loaded carts that they'd become channels for meltwater. A notice pasted to the customs house wall, its ink bleeding in the damp, announcing revised tolls for the winter season.

Vesper read it without appearing to read it. That was a skill too, and perhaps the first one he'd ever learned: how to let his eyes take things in while his body told the world he was interested in nothing at all. *Notice to travellers. By order of the River Commission. The crossing at Sereval will close to all traffic upon the first sustained freeze of the channel, such closure to continue until the spring thaw.* Below that, an official's signature with a flourish like a bird's broken wing.

*First sustained freeze.* The grains of snow on his collar felt suddenly heavier.

The ferryman's shed sat where the street gave up and became the river landing. Vesper knew it was the ferryman's shed because of the man himself, who sat outside it on an upturned crate, mending a net he clearly did not expect to use again this season. He was old in the way river men got old — bent at the shoulder, hands like knotted rope, eyes the colour of the water he'd spent sixty years arguing with. He watched Vesper approach the way the whole town watched strangers now: with arithmetic.

"You'll want the bridge, then," the ferryman said, before Vesper had said anything. "Everyone wants the bridge."

"I want to cross it," Vesper said. His voice came out the way it always did — quiet, unremarkable, pitched to be forgotten before it finished arriving. "Is it still open?"

"Open." The ferryman pulled a knot tight and bit the thread off. "That's a generous word. It's open the way a mouth is open when a man's deciding whether to speak or spit." He looked up at the sky, then at the river, as if consulting both. "Water's still moving under the ice at the edges. Commission says three days, maybe four, maybe tomorrow morning if the cold comes down hard off the pass like it means it. Then they close it. Chains across both ends, guard house locked, no traffic till thaw. Not wagons, not horses, not couriers carrying whatever it is you're carrying in that little bag you keep touching."

Vesper's hand went still against the satchel strap. It had moved without his permission — a small betrayal by his own body, which was supposed to be trained better than that.

"Furs," he said. "Samples."

"Mm," said the ferryman, in the tone of a man agreeing to nothing. He laid the net across his knees and studied Vesper with something that wasn't quite unkindness. It was more like recognition — one professional appraising another, because a ferryman sees every kind of person that a road can produce, and a courier is only a road wearing a coat. "You're not the first this month. Had a woman through last week, same look. Walking fast, checking the sky, doing sums behind her eyes. She made it across with half a day to spare and I heard she didn't stop walking until the far side of the pass." He paused. "Weather's turning faster than the sums this year."

"How long to reach the workshop road?" Vesper asked. "Beyond the pass. Halvard's."

The ferryman's eyebrows rose a fraction — not much, just enough to show he'd heard the name and filed it somewhere. "Three days in good weather. Four in snow. Five if the drifts have closed the switchbacks and you have to wait for the road crews, and the road crews are mostly deserters these days, so don't count on them hurrying for anyone." He stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and gestured at the bridge with his chin. "So. You've got three days of crossing, four days of mountain, and a door that shuts sometime between tonight and Friday. Do your own arithmetic, furs-and-samples. I stopped doing other people's sums years ago. Bad for the heart."

"I can make it," Vesper said.

"Everyone says that." The ferryman gathered his net and turned toward the shed. At the door he paused, his back a question mark against the dim interior. "There's a bed at the Salt House, two streets back, if you're wise enough to wait out the worst of this weather and cross after. There's a road up the pass right now, if you're not." He looked back over his shoulder, and for a moment his face held something almost gentle. "I've ferried forty years, son. Only advice the river ever taught me: the ones who rush the crossing are the ones the crossing keeps."

Then he was gone inside, and the door shut, and Vesper stood alone on the landing with the snow thickening around him and his heart doing exactly what the old man had warned him about.

* * *

He found a bench under the eave of a shuttered chandlery and sat, and made himself do the work properly instead of the way fear wanted it done.

Fear did arithmetic like a gambler: optimistically, rounding down the costs, rounding up the luck. So he set fear aside — folded it away the way he'd fold a map, along creases worn soft with use — and worked the problem as doctrine demanded. *Know the ground. Know the clock. Know yourself, and assume yourself is the least reliable of the three.*

Ground: the road through Sereval's outskirts to the bridge's north end, one hour if the cart traffic moved, three if it didn't. The far-bank road climbing toward the pass: graded once, poorly maintained, likely already rutted with refugee carts going the other way — south, always south now, everyone fleeing the very direction he was being paid to walk toward, which should have told him something and hadn't yet. The pass itself: a day and a half in clear weather, two or more in snow, with one shelter station at the halfway cairn that might or might not be manned. Then the descent to the valley of the workshop: another day.

Clock: the crossing closing within days. Three, the ferryman guessed. Maybe four. Maybe less — the snow was coming down harder now, real flakes at last, fat and unhurried, settling on the river in a slow grey veil. Cold like this, sustained, could skin the channel with ice in a night. He'd seen it happen on smaller water in the northern campaigns, back when he'd been young enough to believe the republic would win them.

And himself: one man, lightly laden, hardened to roads, capable of eighteen hours a day on foot if the footing allowed. Capable of more if it didn't, though the cost of that came later, and later always arrived.

It balanced. Barely. It balanced the way a knife balances on its edge — perfectly, until anything trembled.

But here was the number the ferryman hadn't known to include, the number Vesper carried pressed flat against his ribs beneath the satchel, and it changed all the others: the seals could not wait out a winter on the wrong side of a closed bridge. Whatever was written into those sealed sheets — orders, ciphers, the last intentions of a government clearing its desk like a man fleeing a burning house — they were addressed to Halvard's workshop, and they were timed. His handler had said so without saying so, in the language handlers used: *before the season's last bridge closes*. Which meant the people waiting beyond the pass were working against the same calendar he was, and if the calendar beat him, then the mission didn't fail loudly, with arrests and gunfire. It failed quietly, the way most things in a dying republic failed: on time, in good order, and completely useless.

A formality, his handler had said. But formalities were how the republic did its dying — with stamps and signatures and schedules — and someone, somewhere, had signed the schedule that put him on this bench in this snow.

Vesper took out a piece of bread and ate it without tasting it, watching the bridge.

* * *

It was a handsome thing, the bridge at Sereval. Older than the republic, older probably than any nation that claimed it; five arches of pale stone with timber reinforcements bolted on by successive generations of engineers who each trusted iron more than their grandfathers' masonry. Lanterns hung from posts along its rail, unlit now in the daylight, waiting for dusk the way everything in the town seemed to be waiting for something — the ice, the spring, the war, the end. Carts crawled across it in single file, and from this distance the people on it had no faces, just shapes wrapped against the cold, each one carrying something. That was the difference between him and them, he thought, and the thought had teeth in it. They crossed carrying everything they had left in the world. He crossed carrying almost nothing — a satchel, bread, a knife, flint, a change of stockings, and the seals. No letters. No keepsakes. No name worth speaking aloud.

Carry nothing, owe nothing. That had been the first doctrine ever taught to him, before he was old enough to understand it was also a philosophy. He had believed it the way other men believed in saints. Light hands, light heart. The courier who carries nothing cannot be robbed of anything except his errand, and the errand was never really his.

Except that yesterday, on the ridge above the valley of Telch, he had passed a family by a burned-out wagon — a grandmother, a man missing an arm, two children — and the grandmother had been carrying, of all things, a framed portrait of a house. Just a house, painted by some amateur, windows glowing yellow in the paint. The glass had cracked across it and she carried it anyway, wrapped in her shawl against further damage, and when she'd caught Vesper looking she had lifted it slightly, defiantly, as if daring him to ask why. He hadn't asked. He'd walked on. But the image had lodged in him like a splinter: everything she had left was a picture of something already gone, and she carried it anyway, uphill, in the snow.

His satchel weighed almost nothing. He had checked it against theirs and congratulated himself once, in the arrogance of his training. Now the arithmetic felt different. They carried everything because they had lost everything. He carried nothing because — because whose loss had paid for that lightness? Whose names had been unwritten so his ledger stayed clean?

He shut the thought away. It was not useful. Useful thoughts only, on the road; that was doctrine too, and doctrine had kept him alive through six years of errands that no one would ever admit to.

But the splinter stayed.

* * *

By late afternoon the snow had committed itself fully. Sereval's roofs wore it white; the river's far bank was dissolving into a smudge; the lanterns along the bridge came alight one by one, halos of weak gold in the falling grey. From the customs house came shouting — officials pointing at the sky, at the water, arguing about dates. A boy ran the length of the waterfront ringing a handbell, calling the news the town already knew: *ice forming at the upper shallows, ice at the upper shallows.*

Vesper bought a second loaf, a wedge of hard cheese, and a flask of the local spirit from a grocer who overcharged him by exactly as much as the grocer thought his coat could bear, which was flattering and irrelevant. He asked, casually, about road conditions past the bridge, and the grocer told him what the ferryman had told him, and a carter told him what the grocer had told him, and everyone agreed that the pass was passable today and a gamble tomorrow and a grave by the weekend. Everyone said it in the tone people used for weather — impersonal, fatalistic, faintly pleased to have something certain to report in a season when nothing else was.

At dusk he walked to the north end of the bridge itself, past the guard house where two soldiers sat warming their hands over a brazier, chains coiled at their feet like sleeping animals. One of them glanced at Vesper's satchel and lost interest immediately, which meant Vesper's training was holding, which meant he was invisible, which meant — and the thought arrived unbidden, cold as the river air — that he could stand here and watch the door of the world close, and nobody would stop him, and nobody would miss him, and if he froze white in a snowdrift on the pass, the seals would simply never arrive, and the republic would die of a missed appointment and never know it had been keeping one.

Invisible. He had been proud of that word once.

He turned away from the bridge before the feeling in his chest could grow a name.

* * *

He did not go to the Salt House. He ate standing up in the lee of the chandlery, drank half the spirit to warm the blood and left the rest, and by full dark he was walking — out the north road, past the last lamps of Sereval, following the lantern-glow of the bridge behind him and the blind dark of the pass ahead. The snow fell steadily, erasing his footprints within minutes, which suited him; a courier's first trail should end nowhere.

He walked and he counted. Hours to the bridge's far side: one. Hours to the foothills: nine. To the cairn shelter: thirty, allowing for the refugee traffic and the ruined stretches of road. To the workshop: seventy-two, if the weather held its current temper and his legs held theirs. Against him: the closing of the crossing, measured now not in days but in degrees of frost, in the sound the river would make when it went silent. He caught himself listening for it as he walked — listening for the exact moment the water under the ice stopped talking, as if he could hear it from ten miles away, as if the whole mission had narrowed to that single sound.

Days like coins, he thought. Spend them carefully. Don't drop any.

Somewhere behind him, Sereval slept beside its dying river, and the chains waited in their coils, and the ferryman mended his useless net by lamplight. Somewhere ahead, up in the white throat of the pass, a road climbed toward a house full of maps and lanterns, where people he had never met were waiting for seals he was forbidden to understand.

Vesper pulled his collar up, put his head down into the snowfall, and gave the night his first coin.

Behind him, faint and far, the wind carried a sound like iron — the guard house bell, or the ice beginning, or only the wind lying. He did not stop to find out. Stopping cost more than it ever returned now, and he had so little left to spend.
