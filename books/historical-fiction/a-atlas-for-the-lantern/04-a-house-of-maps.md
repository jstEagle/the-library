## 4. A House of Maps

The workshop announced itself before he saw it.

Vesper smelled it first—ink and hot metal and the resinous bite of pine pitch from the lanterns—a smell so foreign after nine days of snow and woodsmoke that he stopped on the trail without meaning to, the way a man stops when he hears his own name spoken in an unfamiliar voice. Then came the sound: a low, rhythmic clatter beneath the wind, like rain on a tin roof, though there was no roof out here to catch it. It was the sound of presses. He had heard presses only twice in his life, both times in the capital, both times through walls, and he had not known until this moment that the sound had stayed with him, waiting.

The house sat in a fold of the mountain where two slopes leaned together like conspirators. It was larger than he had imagined—larger than any building had a right to be at this altitude—and it was built wrong for a mapmaker's shop, or right for one, depending on how you looked at it. The windows were small and set high, the way they were in fortresses, but the light that leaked through them was warm and gold and steady, and where most houses in the high country kept their shutters closed against the cold, these were open just enough to breathe. Smoke rose from three chimneys. Lanterns hung under the eaves even in daylight, unlit, waiting for the short afternoon to fail.

He stood in the trampled snow at the edge of the clearing for longer than doctrine allowed. A courier did not linger at doors. A courier arrived as if he had always been expected, because the fastest way to be questioned was to look like a man who feared questions. Vesper straightened his shoulders, resettled the satchel against his ribs—the seals riding against his heart like a second, colder heartbeat—and walked into the yard.

A boy was splitting kindling by the side door, or pretending to; the axe moved more often than it fell. He looked up as Vesper crossed the yard, and did not call out, and did not go inside, which meant someone had already seen him coming and told the boy to keep chopping. Everything about the place said *we knew you were on the road*. The path to the main door had been swept. The swept path was what frightened him, afterward, when he thought back on it. No one sweeps a path for a stranger in weather like this unless they know the hour the stranger will arrive.

The door opened before he knocked.

"You'll be the courier." The woman who spoke was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders in the way of people who have worked standing all their lives, with grey hair pinned back severely and ink stains on her fingers that no amount of washing would ever fully surrender. She looked at him the way he imagined she looked at proofs: quickly, thoroughly, cataloguing every flaw. "You're late."

"Three days," Vesper said. "The pass above Sereval was drifted shut. I went around by the shepherd stairs."

"I know. You came down the north face." Something flickered behind her eyes—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. "Ilse Halvard. Come in before the cold finishes what the mountain started."

*She knows the route I took.* He filed it away with the swept path, in the growing drawer of things that did not fit, and stepped over the threshold into warmth so dense it was almost a substance.

* * *

The interior of the Halvard workshop was nothing like the offices of the Republic's chancellery, where Vesper had received his instructions in a room so bare it might have been a cell with ambitions. Here, every surface was working surface. Long tables ran the length of the main room under hanging lanterns, their green-shaded glass throwing pools of light onto paper—paper everywhere, in stacks and sheets and rolls, maps half-finished with mountains rising out of blankness, coastlines waiting for their names. Engravers bent over copper plates with burins held like pens, and the small bright sounds of their work wove through the deeper rhythm of the press at the far end, where two men fed sheets into its iron jaws and pulled them out printed, warm, faintly damp.

No one looked up. That was the first mercy. In the capital, a stranger's entrance turned heads the way a dropped tray turns heads; here, his arrival registered as barely a ripple, and Vesper felt some knot in his chest he hadn't known was tied begin, very slightly, to loosen. This was a house that understood the value of not looking. He could be invisible here. He had been invisible for eleven years, and he had almost forgotten how much effort invisibility cost until he walked into a room where no one required him to perform it.

Almost forgotten. Not entirely. Because the boy with the axe had come in behind him and was now standing by the door, watching him with the frank, unhurried attention of someone too young to have learned discretion, and because Ilse Halvard was leading him through the room at a pace that suggested she wanted him seen—or wanted to see how others saw him.

"The seals," she said, stopping at a table near the press. She held out her hand, palm up, without looking at him.

Vesper unslung the satchel. His fingers found the lacings by touch, the way they always did, and he drew out the oilcloth packet: flat, heavy, sealed twice with the chancellor's mark pressed deep in black wax, the wax itself stamped again with the counter-seal of the Foreign Office, a wheel within a star. Eleven days he had carried it against his body. It weighed less than a loaf of bread. It had never once let him forget it.

He set it in her palm.

Ilse Halvard did not break the seals. He had expected her to—at least to turn the packet over, to test the wax with a thumbnail, to do whatever it was that the recipient of state cipher did upon receipt. Instead she weighed it in her hand, once, briefly, the way a merchant weighs a coin whose denomination she already knows, and set it down on the table beside a stack of finished charts of the northern lakes.

"You'll want food," she said. "And sleep. The codebreakers will begin tonight—the light is better after dark, oddly enough. Fewer shadows inside the letters, Tomas says." She glanced toward the far end of the room, where an old man with white hair and a leather apron was cleaning a plate with a rag, moving with the slow economy of long habit. "Tomas. This is the courier from the capital."

The old engraver looked up. His eyes were pale blue and very clear, and they rested on Vesper for a moment with an expression Vesper could not immediately name—recognition, almost, though they had never met. Recognition of a type, then. Of a category. *Courier*, the eyes said. *One of those.*

"Courier," Tomas said, and returned to his plate.

"He's charming," Ilse said dryly, and Vesper understood it was a joke offered for his benefit, a small courtesy, and that the courtesy itself was strange. Hosts did not joke with couriers. Couriers were furniture that walked. Handlers had told him so, in so many words, across eleven years: *no one remembers the man who carries the letter, and that is your armor, wear it well.* But this woman was fitting him into her household like a guest, and guests were remembered, and remembered men could be described, and described men could be found.

"Thank you," he said, because doctrine supplied nothing better.

"There's a room at the back, past the drying loft. It's small and it's cold but the stove works. Maren—" she raised her voice slightly, and the apprentice girl who had been trimming paper at the second table looked up. She was perhaps seventeen, sharp-faced, with ink to the second knuckle of her right hand and a smudge of it along her jaw like a bruise she'd decided to keep. "Maren will show you. Supper is at six bells. We eat together here—all hours are the same in winter, so we make our own."

She turned back to her table as if he had already left, already begun to be forgotten, and Vesper stood for a moment holding the absence of the satchel against his chest like a phantom limb. Eleven days it had ridden there. Now it lay on a stranger's table between charts of lakes he would never see, and he felt lighter and more naked than he had felt since the chancellor's anteroom, and he could not decide which sensation frightened him more.

* * *

Maren led him through the workshop's depths in silence, past rooms he catalogued automatically because cataloguing was the one discipline handlers had never managed to beat out of him. Drying loft, hung with printed sheets like laundry. Paper store, reeking of size and alum. A stair going down into darkness that breathed cold air and the smell of metal—cellar, probably, plates and presses in storage. And everywhere the lanterns, dozens of them, green and yellow glass, each one trimmed and filled and ready, a fleet of small ships anchored in the dark waiting for night.

"You came over the shepherd stairs," Maren said suddenly. Not a question.

"It was the only way through."

"People die on the shepherd stairs."

"One man died ahead of me. I saw where he fell." He had. He had stepped around the frozen shape with the respect of one professional for another, and said nothing, and walked on. He did not tell her this.

She glanced sideways at him, reassessing. "Ilse doesn't send for couriers often. Twice, since I've been here. The last one stayed a night and left before dawn and I never saw his face—he wore a hood indoors, which Tomas said was theater." She stopped at a low door and pushed it open. "This is yours. There's water in the jug and the stove draws well if you feed it. Don't put anything on the shelf over the bed; the chimney leaks soot when the wind's from the east."

The room was exactly as advertised: narrow, clean, a cot, a stool, a stove with a blackened door, a window set high and thick with frost. On the wall beside the window, someone had pinned a map—a child's map, drawn in a schoolroom hand, the mountains rendered as rows of little humps with names in careful script. It was old. The pin holes around it suggested it had hung there through many winters, through many occupants of the cot.

"Who stayed here?" Vesper asked. "Before me."

Maren paused in the doorway. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer, and then she said, "Apprentices, mostly, when the loft was full. And once a surveyor who wintered over. He drew that." She nodded at the map. "He left in spring." A beat. "Most people leave in spring here."

She went, and her footsteps faded into the general hum of the house, and Vesper stood alone in the small cold room and made himself do the thing he had been refusing to do since the yard.

He took inventory.

The path swept before his arrival. Ilse knowing his route down the north face. The seals received without examination—as if she already knew what was in the packet, as if the weight of it had been described to her in advance, down to the double seal and the wheel within the star. *Something in her manner suggests she expected different cargo,* whispered a voice that sounded unpleasantly like his handler's, and he silenced it, because the alternative reading was worse: that she had expected heavier cargo, or lighter, or none at all, and the republic's final errand had arrived at its destination already compromised, already priced, already sold.

But the fire was lit now. The stove drew as promised, and heat crept into the room, and exhaustion rose in him like floodwater. He ate the bread and hard sausage Maren had left—she had left them, he noticed, without being asked, another courtesy, another entry in the ledger of things that didn't fit—and lay down on the cot under his coat, and slept the dreamless sleep of the truly spent.

* * *

Six bells woke him, or rather the change in the house's sound did: the presses stilled, the burins set down, voices gathering. He washed his face with water cold enough to ache, checked the window—the frost had grown ferns and feathers while he slept, sealing him in like a specimen—and followed the noise to the main room.

Supper was served at the long worktable, papers cleared to one end, bowls of barley soup and dark bread passed hand to hand among perhaps twenty people. Vesper sat where a space was made for him, between Tomas the engraver and a young printer with burn-scarred forearms, and listened. This too was doctrine: eat little, speak less, learn everything. Around him the household talked the way families talk—complaints about damp paper, an argument over the spelling of a village name, laughter at some joke whose setup had happened before he entered the room. Ordinary. Warm. A house full of people making maps of a country that was, by every report he carried in his own head, ceasing to exist.

"Do you draw?" Maren asked him, across the table.

"No."

"Everyone can draw. Most people just do it badly and stop."

"That describes my entire education."

The corner of her mouth moved. Tomas, beside him, said nothing, but Vesper felt the old man's pale eyes rest on him a moment longer than comfort allowed, and return to his bowl.

It was near the end of the meal, the bowls being scraped, the bread gone, that Vesper noticed the ledger.

It lay at the head of the table where Ilse sat, a thick book bound in grey boards, and it was ordinary except that Ilse, in the whole course of the meal, had opened it twice—briefly, checking something, closing it—and both times she had angled it away from the table, away from the apprentices, away from him, with a smoothness so practiced it might have been unconscious. Might have been. Vesper had spent eleven years learning to read the small evasions of bodies, and this one read clearly: *this book is not for your eyes.*

There would be a first ledger, of course. Orders, accounts, wages, paper stock. Every workshop kept one. But the way her forearm curled around the grey boards was not the way a woman guards her grocery accounts.

Ilse looked up and caught him looking. One instant—the candlelight between them, her face unreadable, his own face doing what it was trained to do, which was nothing at all. Then she smiled, a hostess's smile, and said, "You'll want to see the codebreakers start. Come. It's the best show in the house."

She rose, and the ledger went with her, tucked under her arm like a hymnal, and the household began clearing bowls, and Vesper followed her toward the far end of the room where a lamp burned behind a screen of glass and the sealed packet waited on its table, patient as a buried thing.

Behind him, he heard Maren ask someone, quietly, what the courier was carrying.

He heard no answer. That, too, he filed away—in the drawer with the swept path, with the weighed seals, with the grey ledger—among the things that did not fit, in a house that fit him altogether too well.
