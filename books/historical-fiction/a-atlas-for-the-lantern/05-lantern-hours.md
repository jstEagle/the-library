## 5. Lantern Hours

They took his seals at the door and gave him a chair.

That was the whole of it. Master Ilse Halvard had turned the wax tablets over in her hands the way a moneylender turns an unfamiliar coin—weighing them, tilting them toward the lamplight, reading what was written on their faces rather than in them—and then she had passed them to a gray-bearded man called Oskar without a word of explanation, and Oskar had carried them away into the interior of the house, and that was that. Vesper Joubert had crossed a republic's worth of frozen country to deliver those seals, and the delivery had taken less time than the unlacing of his boots.

"You'll wait," Ilse had said. It was not quite a request and not quite an order; it occupied the comfortable territory between, where hospitality and command wear each other smooth. "The work takes as long as it takes. Days, sometimes. You'll have a bed, meals, the run of the front rooms. The rest of the house—" she had smiled then, a small precise smile like a paper cut, "—the rest of the house is the workshop's business."

He understood. He had been trained to understand. A courier who knew nothing could be caught, searched, beaten, broken, and would yield nothing worth the breaking. Ignorance was armor. For eleven years he had worn it so long it had grown to him like a second skin, and he had told himself it was a kind of freedom: the man who carries nothing owns nothing, owes nothing, is owed nothing.

But now he sat in a stranger's house with his armor on and felt, for the first time, how heavy it was.

* * *

The front room they gave him looked out over the courtyard through a window of diamond-paned glass gone wavy with age. Below, the yard lay under a skin of new snow, crossed and recrossed by boot-prints: paths between the main house and the drying shed, the drying shed and the ink kitchen, the ink kitchen and the privy, all of it worn into a map of the workshop's daily life that no cartographer had needed to draw because everyone simply walked it. Vesper found himself studying those prints the way he studied everything now—with the particular hunger of a man forbidden to look.

The room itself was plainly furnished but not poorly. A bed with a wool coverlet dyed the deep blue of expensive cloth. A washstand. A desk with a candle stub and a taper, and—this surprised him—a shelf of books. He pulled one out. A gazetteer of the northern provinces, twenty years out of date, its margins dense with pencil corrections in three different hands. Someone here cared about being right about the world. Someone here corrected the world in pencil when it refused to be corrected.

He put it back and went down.

No one stopped him. That was the first strange thing. In the border towns he had passed through, doors were doors, and behind doors were things, and strangers were watched. But the Halvard house absorbed him the way a river absorbs a dropped leaf—one swirl, one eddy, and he was simply part of the current. A woman carrying a tray of fresh-cast type nodded to him. An old man sharpening a burin on an oilstone glanced up, assessed him in half a second, and returned to his steel. Nobody asked his name. Nobody asked his business. Either Ilse had told them everything, or Ilse had told them nothing, and both possibilities unsettled him equally.

He learned the rhythms the way he learned all things: by standing still and letting them move past him.

The day had a pulse. Before dawn, the ink kitchen woke first—the smell of boiled linseed and lampblack seeping up through the floorboards, sweet and burnt at once, a smell that got into his clothes and stayed there until he dreamed of it. Then the compositors came down, two brothers by the look of them, moving through the cold halls with their collars up, saying nothing, because the first hour belonged to silence and the setting of type required a mind like still water. After sunrise the drying shed opened and the smell shifted: size and alum, wet paper hung on lines like strange pale laundry, sheets that were nothing yet and could become anything. Maps. Proclamations. Ledgers. Prayers. Lies.

By midmorning the house was fully alive, and Vesper walked its permitted edges like a man walking the rim of a well.

He saw the map wall in the long room south-facing windows made bright—a whole wall of drawers, hundreds of them, each labeled in a fine copperplate hand, and above them pinned sheets in every stage of becoming: coastlines half-engraved, mountain ranges waiting for their hachures, a great chart of the river country with the bridge at Sereval rendered as a single confident stroke. He stood before that one longer than he meant to. His own crossing, drawn by someone else's hand, reduced to a line and a name. *Sereval.* Even the letters looked temporary.

"Careful," said a voice near his elbow. "You'll get ink on your sleeve if you lean like that."

He straightened. The speaker was a girl of perhaps seventeen, dark-haired, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with the flat competent gaze of someone who had been working since childhood. She was carrying a portfolio of proofs against her hip.

"I wasn't going to touch anything."

"No." She said it agreeably, without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Visitors never do. They only lean." She shifted the portfolio. "You're the courier."

"I'm a guest."

"You're the courier," she repeated, mildly, the way one repeats a fact of weather. "Oskar's been working on your seals since yesterday afternoon. He barely ate supper. Whatever you brought us, it isn't ordinary freight." And before he could arrange his face into anything, she added, "I'm Maren. I grind the colors and pull the proofs nobody wants to pull. If you need anything, ask me, because everyone else will pretend you're furniture."

She went off down the corridor, and Vesper stood a moment longer before the map of Sereval, feeling the strange sensation of having been seen precisely when he believed himself invisible.

* * *

The days arranged themselves, and he let them.

It was doctrine to wait passively. Doctrine said: eat what is offered, sleep when the house sleeps, mark the exits without seeming to, and want nothing visibly. Doctrine had kept him alive through two changes of government, three purges, and one war that everyone insisted was nearly over and no one believed. So he waited. He sat in the common room in the evenings while the workshop's people gathered around the stove, and he listened the way stone listens to water.

They talked, these people. That was the second strange thing. In the ministries he had served, conversation was a currency spent carefully and counterfeited constantly. Here they argued openly—about ink formulas, about the war, about whether the price of paper would ruin them all by spring. A young engraver named Piet held forth on the treachery of zinc plates. The gray-bearded Oskar, when he emerged from the cipher room, blinking like a mole, drank one glass of plum brandy and answered questions about geography for an hour, patiently, endlessly, as though the world's rivers were his own children. Only two subjects produced silences: the war's outcome, spoken of in the careful neutral grammar of people who intended to survive whoever won; and the second ledger.

Vesper found it on his third evening, or rather he found its absence shaped like a presence.

Ilse kept the accounts in the office off the print room, a small cold chamber smelling of glue and iron gall. Her door stood open most mornings—he noted this the way he noted exits—and through it he could see her at the desk with the great household ledger open before her: paper stock, wages, ink, coal, the honest arithmetic of any business. But twice, when he passed at particular hours, he saw her with a different book entirely. Smaller. Bound in something darker than calfskin. And both times, the moment his shadow crossed the doorway, the smaller book was gone—not hidden with guilt, exactly, but put away with the brisk finality of a hand closing a drawer on silverware.

He did not ask. Asking was a lantern raised in a dark room, and it showed you the room, yes—but it showed the room *you*, too.

Still. Curiosity in a courier is a disease, his first handler had told him, back when Vesper was nineteen and eager and full of questions. It starts as a fever and ends as a symptom. The curious courier begins by wondering what he carries and ends by opening the satchel, and opened satchels hang men. He had believed it absolutely for eleven years. He believed it still. But belief, he was discovering, is like the ice on the river at Sereval—sound enough to walk on right up until the sound it makes is the last sound it makes.

Because here was the thing doctrine had not prepared him for: he was not carrying the satchel anymore. The seals were gone from him, handed over, out of his keeping. For the first time in his working life, Vesper Joubert was a man with nothing to protect and nowhere to be, and the ignorance that had been armor now had nothing left to guard. It was just weight. Just a habit of blindness worn long after the battle had moved elsewhere. And into that vacancy, curiosity crept the way cold crept under doors—not violently, not all at once, but steadily, patiently, finding every gap he hadn't caulked.

He began, without deciding to, to keep count.

The cipher room was the closed heart of the house: two rooms at the back, north-facing, where Oskar and two others worked in shifts and where the lamps burned late. Meals went in; plates came out untouched. Once, passing in the corridor, he heard through the door not voices but a sound he knew from his training years—the dry tick of a counting frame, beads sliding, numbers being married to numbers. They were making progress. Progress meant departure, and departure meant the bridge, and the bridge meant the season's arithmetic he performed each night like a miser counting coins: days of snow, days of light, days until Sereval shut.

And the second ledger appeared in his count too, though he could not have said why it mattered. Three times more he glimpsed it. Once in Ilse's hands in the corridor, once on her desk beneath a map proof she seemed to be checking against it, and once—this was the one that lodged in him like a splinter—in the hands of a man he had never seen before and never saw again, a broad traveler in a good coat who came after dark, stayed forty minutes in the office, and left with snow unmelted on his shoulders. Through the gap of the office door, Vesper saw the dark book lying open between Ilse and the stranger, and saw Ilse's finger move down a column while the man nodded, and saw neither of them smile, because whatever lived in that book was not the kind of thing one smiled about.

Household accounts did not travel after dark. Household accounts did not make visitors nod like men confirming cargo.

* * *

On the sixth morning the storm came down off the high passes and sealed the house in white, and everything changed shape.

Work indoors. The drying lines strung across the common room, hung with sheets that steamed faintly. The compositors working by lamplight at midday. And Vesper, restless in a way he distrusted, walking the permitted edges again, finding them narrower in winter, finding himself—though the walls had not moved—more confined.

It was in the corridor outside the copy room that he saw it. Not the ledger itself this time, but its shadow.

The copy room door stood ajar—carelessly, he thought at first, until he understood that nothing in this house was careless. Inside, visible in the gap, was the end of a worktable, and on the worktable lay a sheet of paper under a weighting board, and the corner of the sheet that protruded bore a fragment of ruled columns and a symbol he recognized with a jolt that went through him like cold water: a cipher convention. His cipher's convention. The seal-group markers used by his own ministry, the ones he had carried across the pass without knowing their meaning, reproduced here in another hand, on other paper, in a house that was supposed to be merely *reading* what he'd brought, not writing it.

He stood very still. Snow ticked against the window at the corridor's end. Somewhere below, the press began its slow rhythmic breathing, the heartbeat of the building.

He was aware, distantly, that he should step back. That looking was the fever, and the fever was winning. That whatever was on that table was none of his business, could not be his business, must not be—

"You breathe loudly," said Maren, behind him, "for a man trained not to exist."

He turned. She stood with an armful of clean rags and an expression of perfect innocence that fooled him not at all. How long had she been there? Long enough. Long enough to see him look, and to see how long he looked.

"The door was open," he said.

"Doors are always open here." She came forward and pushed the copy room door shut with her hip, gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child—or a sleeping dog. "Master Halvard says a house with secrets keeps them better in plain sight. People don't look at what they're allowed to see." She adjusted the rags in her arms. "You've been counting, courier. Six days. You think nobody notices a guest who walks the same circuit every morning and stands at the same doors?"

"And if I have been?"

Maren considered him. Up close her eyes were a surprising gray, the gray of the paper they sized in the shed, and there was something in them he had not seen before—something measured and deliberate, a patience beyond her years, as though she were waiting for him to arrive somewhere he didn't yet know he was traveling.

"If you have," she said quietly, "then you've also noticed that Oskar works nights now, and that Master Halvard burned correspondence in the yard stove two evenings ago instead of filing it, and that the man who visited after dark wore boots with foreign heels." She shifted the rags again. "You notice very well for furniture."

Down the corridor, a door opened. Voices—Ilse's, low and even, and Oskar's answering, tired. Maren stepped back from him in the same motion, and her face rearranged itself into the pleasant blankness of an apprentice with chores, and when Ilse Halvard came around the corner with a lamp in her hand and snowmelt dripping from her hem, she found only her guest standing politely by a closed door and a girl hurrying past with rags.

"Storm's set in," Ilse said to Vesper, in the tone of a hostess discussing weather. "We'll be some days yet. The work goes slower in the dark months—the codes you brought are old-fashioned, all arithmetic and patience." She smiled her small paper-cut smile. "You'll have your answer and your road soon enough. Nothing to do but wait. That shouldn't trouble a man in your profession."

"No," Vesper said. "Waiting is my profession."

She passed on toward her office, lamp swinging, and Vesper remained in the corridor a moment, listening to the press breathe below, feeling the splinter of the copy room working deeper with every hour.

That night he lay awake under the blue coverlet doing the arithmetic he could not stop doing: days of snow, days of light, days until Sereval. And beneath it, unbidden, a second sum presented itself, in figures he did not want and could not refuse: a cipher convention copied onto foreign paper, a dark ledger consulted after dark, a house that survived by serving anyone who paid—and himself, the invisible man, sitting at the center of it like a fly that has wandered into a web and not yet felt the threads begin to draw.

In the morning, he resolved, he would ask Maren nothing. He would walk his circuit, sit by the stove, be furniture.

But sleep did not come easily, and when it finally took him, he dreamed of the bridge at Sereval—drawn in fine copperplate on a sheet of paper, a single confident stroke—and in the dream, even as he watched, a hand holding a pen began, patiently, to erase it.
