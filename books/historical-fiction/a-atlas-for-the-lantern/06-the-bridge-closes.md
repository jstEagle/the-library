## 6. The Bridge Closes

The news came with the milk.

That was how things arrived at the Halvard workshop—not by trumpet or courier's horn, but folded into the ordinary traffic of the day: a boy from the village with two covered pails, a woman selling salted fish off a handcart, a tinker who mended the press-room stove and stayed to gossip. Vesper had learned the workshop's rhythms in four days of waiting, and he had learned that nothing important was ever announced here. It seeped in, like damp through stone, until one morning you noticed the walls were wet and could not say when it had started.

He was in the drying loft that morning, the long room above the press where printed sheets hung from lines like strange laundry—maps of river deltas, star charts, a survey of some coastline that no longer belonged to anyone. He had taken to coming here because it was warm, because the smell of ink and wet paper reminded him of nothing, and because from the small window he could see the road down toward Sereval, a white thread stitched through grey forest. Every morning he counted the travelers on it. Every morning there were fewer.

The boy set his pails down by the kitchen stair and said something to the cook—a girl with flour on her forearms—and the girl stopped kneading. Vesper watched through the loft window as the message traveled the way messages travel in small places: from kitchen to corridor, from corridor to Ilse Halvard's office, carried by Maren, who crossed the yard at a pace just short of running, her breath pluming in the cold.

Vesper did not need to hear the words. He had been counting days like coins for a week now, and he knew which coin had just run out.

*The bridge.* He made himself say it plainly, inside his own skull, where no one could mistake him for a man who hoped. *The bridge at Sereval is closed.*

He went downstairs anyway. Doctrine said a courier confirmed all intelligence personally; doctrine also said a courier never revealed urgency, and so he walked the length of the corridor at the pace of a man going to look at weather.

In the yard, the fish-seller was packing up her cart, and the tinker stood talking to Tomas, the old engraver, who held a mug of something steaming in both hands as if the cold were a personal enemy. Vesper slowed near them without stopping, the way he'd been trained to move through markets and garrison towns—one more unremarkable body in the flow of the day.

"—closed it yesterday evening," the tinker was saying. "Ice took the piers at Sereval. Ferryman pulled his boat out himself, dragged it up on the bank with his own hands. Said he wasn't losing another one to the floes, not after what happened to Delaunay's boy." The tinker spat into the snow. "No crossing till the thaw. That's the word."

Tomas grunted. His eyes, pale as skimmed milk, slid across Vesper and moved on, giving him nothing—which was its own kind of courtesy, Vesper supposed, though he couldn't have said why he felt that way.

Vesper kept walking. Past the woodpile, past the well with its rope frozen stiff as an iron bar, to the gate, where he stood and looked down the valley at the road he had come by, the road that was now, for all practical purposes, a rumor.

The arithmetic was simple and it did not change under repetition. The pass behind him had been marginal when he crossed it nine days ago; by now it would be drifted shut, impassable even for a man willing to die trying, and dying trying was not a service the republic had paid him for. Ahead of him, the bridge at Sereval—the only crossing over the river that would hold a man and his satchel through winter—was closed until the thaw. Three months, perhaps four. The mission he carried sat in the workshop's cipher room at this very moment, being worked by lamplight by men who spoke in numbers, and its delivery date had just been pushed from *days* to *seasons*, and there was not one thing Vesper Joubert could do about any of it except stand at a gate in the cold and feel the trap close around him with the softness of falling snow.

That was the thing about traps, he thought. No one ever described them correctly. In the training houses they taught you to fear the loud ones—the checkpoint, the challenge, the hand on your shoulder in a dark street. Nobody warned you that the efficient trap was quiet. That it might look like shelter. That it might smell of bread.

"Courier."

Ilse Halvard stood at the kitchen door, arms crossed against the cold, her grey head bare. She was not a tall woman, but she had the stillness of tall women, the kind of presence that made you straighten your spine without knowing why.

"You'll have heard," she said. It was not a question.

"The bridge is closed," Vesper said. "Until spring."

"Until spring." She let the words sit in the air between them, then uncrossed her arms. "Come inside. There's no sense conducting the rest of this conversation where your face will freeze into an expression you didn't choose."

* * *

The kitchen was the warmest room in the workshop and everyone in it pretended otherwise, drifting through on errands so they could stand near the stove for a minute at a time. Ilse led Vesper past it to her office, a narrow room lined floor to ceiling with map drawers, each labeled in her own small precise hand. A lamp burned even at midday. On the desk lay a letter she had evidently been reading when Maren interrupted her, and she turned it face-down with a motion so smooth it might have been absent-minded, though Vesper doubted she did anything absent-mindedly.

"You understand what this means," she began.

"That I can't leave."

"That you can't leave, that your commission is suspended whether anyone in the capital admits it or not, and that the men working your cipher are now working it for a deadline that has evaporated." She sat behind the desk and gestured to the chair opposite, and Vesper took it, because refusing a offered seat was a kind of information too, and he was not yet ready to give her information. "Which raises a question I've been turning over since the day you arrived, courier, and which I may as well ask plainly, since we appear to be spending a winter together."

She waited. He said nothing. She almost smiled.

"What happens to a man like you," she said, "when the thing he was carrying stops having anywhere to go?"

"I wait for instructions."

"From whom? The republic that sent you is finishing its last accounts. You know that. Your handler told you—I saw it in your face the night you arrived, that particular flinch men get when they've been told the truth they already suspected." She leaned back. "You're waiting for instructions from a corpse, and the corpse is three hundred miles away on the wrong side of a mountain range, and the snow between you and it doesn't care about your loyalty. So I'll make you a proposal, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide what I am."

Vesper kept his hands loose in his lap and his breathing even. *A courier confirms nothing,* doctrine said. *A courier anticipates everything.*

"The workshop takes lodgers in winter. Always has—it's how we survived the last war, and the one before that. Rooms above the drying loft, meals at the common table, work for anyone willing to do it. You'd sweep floors, carry paper, hold lamps. Nothing touching the cipher room, nothing touching the presses. In exchange—" she spread her hands, palms up, a gesture of such deliberate openness that Vesper felt the hair rise on his neck, "—you spend the winter as one of us. Fed, warm, unnamed if you prefer. And in spring, when the bridge opens, you walk across it with whatever you're carrying and whatever you've decided, and no one here follows you, and no one here asks."

"As one of us," Vesper repeated.

It was the phrase itself he was testing, turning it over the way a money-changer tests a suspect coin, listening for the false ring. Ilse heard the test. To her credit—or her craft—she didn't flinch from it.

"You think it's a cage," she said. "I'd think so too, in your position. But consider the alternative. You have no papers that would survive scrutiny in any town on this side of the river—you told me yourself you travel light, and I believe you, because I've met a hundred men who travel light and every one of them was carrying something heavier than luggage. If you leave here, you are a stranger with no trade, no name, and no destination, walking into a countryside full of deserters, requisition parties, and men who shoot strangers on principle. Whereas here—" she nodded at the wall of drawers, at the muffled sounds of the workshop going about its day beyond it, "—here you are simply the quiet fellow who sweeps the loft. Winter is long, courier. It is much longer outside than in."

"And the cost?"

"There isn't one. Not today." She said it so evenly that he believed her, which frightened him more than any price would have. "Not tomorrow either. One day, perhaps, I'll ask you for something. Everyone in this house owes me something by spring—that's not cruelty, it's bookkeeping. But I'm telling you the debt exists before you incur it, which is more honesty than you'll get from anyone else between here and the sea."

Vesper looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, through the frost-furred window, the light was already failing at two in the afternoon, the northern dark gathering itself for its five-month reign. Somewhere below, a press began to turn, its rhythm steady as a heart.

"All right," he said.

He heard the word leave his mouth and marveled, distantly, at how easily it came. No struggle, no negotiation, no pause for dignity. Just the soft click of a door closing that he had, in some part of himself, already walked through.

Ilse rose and shook his hand. Her grip was dry and strong and brief. "Maren will show you the loft room," she said. "Supper's at six. We eat together—all ranks, all trades. You'll find we're good company, for people who live in a house full of secrets."

At the door she paused, her back half-turned, and added, without any particular weight, as though remarking on the weather:

"The cipher work goes slowly. Your cargo may take weeks yet. I thought you'd want to know."

Then she was gone down the corridor, and Vesper stood alone among the map drawers, understanding that he had just been told, gently, precisely, that his mission was now living on her schedule. That the trap had not sprung today. That it had been sprung the moment he handed her the seals, and everything since—the lodging, the warmth, the phrase *as one of us*—was only the closing of it, done so slowly and so kindly that a man might live inside it for years before noticing the bars.

* * *

The loft room was small and clean: a cot, a chest, a shelf, a window looking south toward the pass he could no longer cross. Maren brought him bedding and a candle stub and set them down without ceremony, but she lingered at the door, wiping her hands on her apron, watching him with that frank, measuring gaze he had caught her using before.

"You said yes," she observed.

"It seemed the sensible thing."

"It always does." She glanced down the stairs, then back at him, and lowered her voice—not dramatically, just enough. "Master says that to everyone. *As one of us.* The tinker heard it eleven winters ago. He's still sweeping floors."

"And you?"

"I was born here." Something flickered across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Born into *us*. Which is different, if you're wondering. Being given the words and being raised inside them." She hoisted the empty basket onto her hip. "Supper's at six. Sit anywhere except across from Brenner—he talks about pig feed. Goodnight, courier."

She went. Vesper stood at his window while the light died, and watched the road south until he couldn't see it anymore, and then watched the darkness where it had been, because watching was the only instruction left to follow.

Below, the workshop glowed with lantern light, window after window, a ship becalmed in a sea of snow. It looked, he thought, like safety. It looked like the picture of safety, drawn by someone skilled, in a hand he was beginning to recognize.

Somewhere in that glow, men were bending over his cipher by lamplight, and somewhere behind those men stood Ilse Halvard, counting weeks instead of days, and somewhere in the dark beyond the river waited buyers whose names no ledger admitted to.

He blew out the candle and lay down on the cot, and the last thing he heard, as sleep took him, was the press turning below, patient as a clock, printing the world over again in whatever image its masters required.
