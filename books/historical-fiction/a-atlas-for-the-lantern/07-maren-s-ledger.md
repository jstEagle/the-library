## 7. Maren's Ledger

Maren had been watching Vesper Joubert for six days, and in that time she had learned to read him the way her mother had taught her to read weather: by what he did not say.

He was not a talker. He asked no questions about the work that went on around him, and the not-asking had a quality of discipline to it, like a man holding his breath underwater. He ate what was set before him in the long kitchen, thanked whoever had cooked it without looking at them, and spent his days in the narrow orbit of his cot, the corridor, and the yard where the apprentices took their smoke. He had the stillness of something hunted that had decided hunting was beneath its dignity.

But he watched. She had seen him watch. He watched the way the couriers came and went from the eastern door at odd hours—there were three others wintering in the house, and none of them spoke to him, and he did not speak to them, and this too told Maren something. He watched the second ledger.

That was how she thought of it, though it had no such name in the workshop. It lived in Ilse's office, in a locked drawer of the walnut desk, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, and it went up to the master's quarters every night at half past nine and came down again at seven. Maren knew its schedule because for two years it had been her job to carry it, and for one year before that her job to pretend she did not know what it was while she sharpened the pen that wrote in it.

She was seventeen, and she had been at Halvard's since she was eleven, when her father—a paper-maker whose mill had burned in the first year of the war, who could no longer feed four children on the wages of a ruined trade—had sold her to the workshop as an apprentice with the gentle fiction of an indenture. She had been grateful. That was the part she could never explain to anyone who had not been hungry: that gratitude came first, and everything else grew out of it like rot through a floorboard, softening what you stood on until one day you went through.

The workshop had fed her. The workshop had taught her letters, and engraving, and the chemistry of inks that would fade or endure according to the buyer's needs. The workshop had taught her that maps were arguments, that a border was a line drawn twice—once on the plate and once in the ledger—and that the second line was worth more than the first.

And now there was this courier, this forgettable man, lying awake in the room above the press-room with his candle lit too late, listening to the machines turn, and Maren had decided—somewhere between the sixth night and the seventh morning, somewhere in the arithmetic of a girl who had run out of ways to lie to herself—that he was going to be the knife or he was going to be nothing at all.

* * *

She found him on the morning of the seventh day in the drying loft, where the printed sheets hung in rows like laundry, ink curing in the cold air. It was her duty to inspect them; she had made sure it would be her duty alone, having risen early and sent the younger apprentice, Kasper, off to fetch size from the cellar on an errand that did not exist.

Vesper stood among the hanging sheets with one hand raised, as if he might touch them, though he did not. He turned when the stair creaked.

"They're the river valleys," Maren said. "North range. For the army quartermasters—it doesn't matter which army." She let that sit. "They're the same maps."

"I hadn't asked."

"No," she said. "You're very good at not asking. I've been admiring it."

He looked at her then, fully, for the first time since she had begun looking at him, and she saw the assessment move behind his eyes like a fish under ice: *apprentice, girl, danger, how much danger.* "You shouldn't talk to me," he said. "It isn't safe."

"Neither is being here," Maren said. "But here we are, both of us, all winter." She moved down the row of sheets, touching each one lightly at the corner as she counted, an old habit, a choreography of innocence. "You've been counting days. I've seen you doing it. Marking them somewhere in your head. You think the bridge will open early. It won't. It never does. Master Halvard has kept this house through three winters of the war, and she knows exactly how long the ice takes, and she has planned her year around your imprisonment the way a farmer plans around rain."

"You should stop talking," Vesper said quietly.

"Yes," said Maren. "Everyone tells me that. Kasper says it. Tomas says it. My own head says it at night, in my father's voice." She finished the row and turned to face him, her hands folded in her apron the way Ilse liked to see them, obedient hands, trustworthy hands. "I showed you the drying loft because I wanted you to see these before I show you the other thing. These are honest. Whatever army buys them, whatever they do with them—the map is true. The river is where the river is. That used to matter to me. I want you to know it used to matter to me, so you understand what it means when I show you the thing that doesn't."

* * *

She led him at midday, during the hour when the workshop emptied toward the kitchen and the corridors stood vacant, through the engraving room with its smell of acid and beeswax, past the great press with its iron screw worn bright at the handle by ten thousand turns of human hands. She walked the way she had been taught to walk past Ilse's door: evenly, without hurry, a person with legitimate business and nowhere forbidden to be.

Ilse was at the far end of the house. Maren had checked. Ilse took her dinner at noon with the senior men in her office, and she drank two glasses of the rough plum brandy and no more, and for precisely forty minutes the second floor belonged to no one.

Maren stopped at the office door and stood before it a moment with her hand flat against the wood, and Vesper, behind her, said nothing, which she took for consent or cowardice and could not tell between. The lock was nothing. Ilse believed in locks the way she believed in laws—as things that governed other people. Maren had opened this one since she was thirteen.

Inside, the office smelled of ink and sealing wax and, faintly, of the plum brandy. The shutters were drawn against the glare off the snow, and the room existed in a brown lamplight that made everything in it look already archived, already historical. The walnut desk. The chair with the worn arms. And Maren went to the drawer—not the locked one, that would be stupid, they had perhaps thirty minutes—she went to the false back of the shelf beside the desk, which she herself had helped Tomas build three years ago, for the keeping of plates too valuable to leave in the racks, and she drew out the oilcloth packet and laid it on the desk and unwrapped it.

The ledger was plain. That was always the first shock, she thought, watching Vesper's face. You expected contraband to look like something. It was a quarter-bound book of the same stock the workshop used for order tallies, ruled in the same faint grids, and the hand inside was Ilse's own—small, upright, merciless in its neatness.

"Read it," Maren said.

"I don't want—"

"I know what you don't want. Read it anyway. You're the only person in this house who didn't write any of it. That makes you the only clean pair of eyes it will ever see."

He opened it. She watched him read, and watched the reading happen to him: the small contraction at the eyes, the slow settling of the shoulders, the way his thumb pressed whiter against the page's edge.

The entries began three winters back. *Buyer, across-river, via the ferryman's brother: twelve sheets, northern passes, first printing.* Then, further down, in the same unhurried hand: *Duplicate cipher keys, per agreement, delivered sealed. Payment held at Grissen's, in salt scrip.* Page after page. Maps sold to quartermasters of one republic and, six entries later, corrected maps sold to the officers opposing it—the same rivers, the same passes, shifted by a finger's width where a ford had been allowed to silt or a road left unmarked where an ambush had been promised. Cipher sheets copied while the originals waited upstairs under seal, the copies traveling south and east to buyers whose names appeared only as initials, or as trades, or not at all. *The workshop survives,* Ilse had written once, in a summary entry at the bottom of a page, as if balancing accounts aloud to herself, *because we are useful to men who cannot afford to lose us.*

"It's the whole war," Vesper said. His voice had gone strange and flat. "In one book."

"It's one shop's share of it," Maren said. "There are others like us. There have to be. But this is ours. Every sheet there crossed this floor. I carried the ledger upstairs at half past nine for two years. I sharpened the pen. When I was fourteen I asked Tomas what the second book was for, and he told me it was the master's private accounts, and I believed him, because believing is easier than knowing and I was fourteen and full." She heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, made herself stand still with her hands in her apron. "There. Now you know. Now it's in you too, and you can't give it back."

Vesper closed the ledger. He did it carefully, aligning the covers, the way a man closes a door on a sickroom. "Why?" he said. "Why show me? What do you imagine I can do?"

"I don't imagine anything," Maren said. That was a lie, and she felt it go out of her like warmth, and hated how much better lying had gotten. "I wanted to see whether you'd look away."

"And if I do?"

"Then you're like the rest of us, and I'll know, and it will change nothing, and the bridge will open in spring and you'll cross and forget this room." She took the ledger from him and wrapped it, her fingers suddenly clumsy on the oilcloth. "But if you don't look away—if you keep looking at it the way you're looking now—then somebody in this house knows besides me, and Tomas, and God, and God has been no use at all."

Below them, faintly, the press began to turn again. Above everything, muffled by floors and wool and snow, the house went on with its industrious, double-faced quiet.

"We have to put it back," Maren said. "She counts the wrapping creases."

* * *

They were fastening the shelf's false panel when the floorboard sounded in the corridor—not a footstep, quite; more the complaint of an old house adjusting its weight, the kind of sound you could believe in or ignore. Maren froze with her fingertips still on the wood. Beside her, Vesper had gone absolutely still, and she understood in that moment that his stillness was trained, that somewhere a handler had drilled into him the art of becoming furniture, and that the training was useless now, because she had shown him the ledger, and a man who knows cannot be furniture ever again.

The sound came again, closer. A step, deliberate, unhurried, with the slight drag of the left heel that Maren would have known anywhere in the world, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea.

Ilse Halvard stopped outside her own office door.

Maren's mind ran the excuses the way a gambler runs cards: she had come for the wax, for the plates, for the inventory list; the courier had followed her, demanded to see the workshop's accounts, threatened her. All of them thin. All of them dead on arrival, because Ilse Halvard had built this entire house out of knowing what people would say before they said it.

The door did not open.

A full minute passed—Maren counted her heartbeats against it, eleven, fifteen, twenty—and then, through the door, unhurried, almost gentle, came the master's voice:

"Maren. The Kasper boy is looking for you. The size delivery."

Silence. Then the drag of the left heel, receding down the corridor toward the office where her guests sat over plum brandy, and Maren stood with her hand still pressed to the panel that was supposed to conceal everything, understanding that concealment was over, that Ilse had known the moment the drawer lock had failed to catch—or had known before that, had perhaps known since the night a forgettable courier arrived carrying seals that were meant to be something else—and that the single sentence through the door was not mercy but an opening bid.

Vesper's lips were white. "She knew," he whispered. "She—" 

"She knows everything," Maren said. Her voice did not shake; she was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride. "That's what this house is. Come away from there. Walk normally."

She led him out into the corridor, into the ordinary afternoon light falling gray through the stairwell windows, and behind them the office door stood shut and quiet, and somewhere beyond it, behind the walnut desk, the second ledger lay wrapped in its oilcloth, patient as the ice on the river, waiting for spring.
