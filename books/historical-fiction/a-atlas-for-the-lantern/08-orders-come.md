## 8. Orders Come

Ilse Halvard had learned long ago that the best place to conduct a negotiation was at a table where food was being served. A table laid with bread and smoked fish and the good dark honey from the southern valleys said things no words could say: that she was unhurried, that she was generous, that whatever was about to be asked of her guest had already been decided before he sat down. Hunger made men honest, but hospitality made them pliable, and it was pliability Ilse wanted tonight.

She had dressed for it too—not in her working apron, stiff with size and ink, but in the grey wool dress she kept for buyers and officials, the one with the high collar fastened by her mother's silver clasp. She wore her hair coiled and pinned. These were small vanities, and she permitted them precisely because they were strategic: Vesper Joubert had spent three weeks in her house learning to read her moods through the state of her cuffs and the ink on her fingers. Tonight she would give him nothing so legible.

He came when Tomas fetched him, as she'd known he would. That was another thing she had counted: the courier obeyed summons the way a door obeys its hinges. He entered the small dining room off the map archive—the room with no windows onto the courtyard, chosen for that reason—and stopped just inside the threshold, taking inventory the way his kind always did. Exits. Faces. The weight of the room.

"Sit," Ilse said. "Eat first. We'll talk after."

"I'm not hungry."

"Then sit while I eat. Hunger shared is less rude than hunger refused." She poured the wine herself, two cups, filling his fuller than hers—a gesture from the old country that meant either trust or a debt acknowledged, depending on which side of the river you'd been born on. He would know that. He was trained to know such things. It was one of the reasons he unsettled her, this courier who had sat in her workshop for three weeks being nobody, being so thoroughly nobody that she had nearly believed it herself.

Nearly.

She ate slowly, deliberately, letting him watch her hands—clean tonight, no ink, no engraver's grit. Let him wonder what that meant. The fish was good; the bread was yesterday's but toasted. Outside, the wind worked at the shutters with the patient malice of winter, and somewhere below, the presses were silent because she had ordered them silent. She wanted the whole house listening to nothing but this room.

Vesper sat across from her with his cup untouched, and she gave him credit for that too. A man who wouldn't drink poured wine at least understood what wine could be for.

"You've settled in well," she said. "Better than most guests. You learned the press schedule in a week. You know which apprentices can be spoken to and which ones report to me." She smiled to take the sting from it, or to sharpen it—she had never fully decided what her smiles were for. "You know we keep two ledgers."

Something moved behind his eyes, quickly banked. "I know you run a business."

"I know what you know, Master Joubert. That has been the difficulty of these past weeks." She set down her fork and folded her hands, and let the silence do its work. It was an old tool and it had never once failed her. Men filled silence the way water fills a hollow; they rushed into it carrying everything they'd been holding back.

He didn't rush. She waited longer. He still didn't rush. Finally she laughed—softly, genuinely, because it had been years since someone made her work for it.

"All right," she said. "We'll do it plainly. Plainness is a courtesy I extend to almost no one, so consider yourself honored." She rose, went to the sideboard, and took from the drawer there a single sheet of paper—not the second ledger itself, which stayed under Maren's key, but a summary in her own hand, written that afternoon in a cipher of her own devising, because some habits were the load-bearing walls of a life. She did not give it to him. She only set it on the table between them, face down, like a card.

"In the autumn," she said, "a rider came through the pass ahead of the snows. Young. Nervous. Carrying dispatches for the garrison at Sereval, which no longer exists, though he didn't know that yet. He carried also a description—a partial one, garbled in the telling—of a courier expected to follow later, traveling light, unremarkable, bearing sealed codes to my workshop from your ministry." She turned the sheet over. On it she had drawn, in six strokes of the pen, a face. Not his face exactly. The absence of a face. Two eyes that watched without being seen, a mouth that asked nothing, shoulders arranged to take up no room. Her own hand had caught it better than she'd expected. She had surprised herself with the drawing and then surprised herself again with how much she disliked it.

"The republic described you the way it uses you," she said. "By describing no one."

Vesper looked at the little drawing for a long moment. When he looked up, his expression was the same smooth professional calm she had catalogued weeks ago, but she was fluent now in the dialect beneath it. "If you already knew what I carry," he said, "why wait until now to say so?"

"Because at first I didn't know whether you were worth the trouble." She sat back down and took up her wine. "Codes arrive here the way weather arrives. Some seasons bring nothing. But I had you watched, and I will tell you honestly what I concluded, because honesty is the coin I prefer to pay in—it costs more than lies and therefore buys more. I concluded that you are not merely a carrier. A carrier would have tried to peek. They all try to peek; it's human nature wearing a satchel. You never once tried. You slept with your back to the wall and your boots on and your hand near the bag, and you never once tried." She tilted her head. "That tells me the cargo matters enough that your ministry frightened you properly. And it tells me something else, something sadder—that they frightened you so well you don't even know what the fear is for."

"You don't know what I carry."

"No," Ilse agreed pleasantly. "But I know what it's worth, and I know who wants it, and I know that both of those numbers are larger than anything that has ever come through my doors. Do you understand what this valley is, Master Joubert? Before the war it was a place where maps were made because the paper was good and the water was soft and my father's hands were steady. Now it is a place where the war comes to be priced. Both sides send buyers. Both sides pay. My workshop survives because I have made it useful to everyone and indispensable to no one—or rather, indispensable to everyone, which is the only neutrality that holds." She leaned forward. "And then you walked in out of the snow carrying the one thing that could tip all of it, and I thought: here at last is something that cannot be sold twice. Here at last is a decision."

The wind threw itself against the shutters. Down in the yard, a chain clinked against a post; the dog, making its rounds. Vesper's cup was still untouched. His hands were quiet on the table—too quiet, the stillness of a man keeping his hands quiet on purpose.

"So here is what I offer," Ilse said. "In spring, when the bridge opens, you leave. Everyone leaves; that is what spring is *for*. But how you leave depends entirely on what happens between now and then. If you continue to be a guest—polite, blind, forgettable—then in April you walk out onto that bridge with nothing, back to a republic that has already disowned you, and I wish you luck with real feeling, because you'll need it and I bear you none. But if you let my people copy what you carry—if you simply stop guarding it for three nights, which is all we need, three nights, you needn't even know it happened—then you leave in April with passage arranged, papers that will survive scrutiny, money that will spend, and a name that opens doors instead of closing them." She spread her hands, palms up, the gesture of a merchant displaying honest wares. "Passage out. That's the whole price. You were sent here to deliver paper; deliver it, and let us deliver ours."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you remain a guest," she said warmly. "For as long as the season requires. The mountains are very closed in winter, as you've noticed. Guests can be accommodated indefinitely."

"That's not mercy," Vesper said quietly. "Mercy doesn't come with conditions."

"It's the only mercy there is," Ilse said. "Everything else is a fable told by people who have never needed any." 

She heard the edge in her own voice and was displeased with it, so she softened, and the softening was not entirely performance. There was something in this man that pulled at her, the way the ledger pulled at Maren—the sight of a person being erased in slow motion and calling it duty. She had been erased once. She knew the arithmetic of it: what it cost, who collected.

"Let me tell you what your republic is," she said, gentler now. "I was in Marrenhof when the purges came through. You're too young to remember them clearly, perhaps. My father drew charts for their navy—coastlines, soundings, honest work. My mother taught the daughters of ministers to play the harpsichord. None of it mattered. A list was drawn up, and names were read aloud in a square, and by the time the reading ended my family existed only in the way I exist now—as testimony." She touched the silver clasp at her throat, an old gesture, worn smooth. "I survived because a clerk liked my father's maps and struck our name from the list. One clerk. One pen stroke. Your republic's justice is a clerk's pen, Master Joubert, and you are carrying its ink across a mountain. You call that loyalty. I call it carrying water for the flood."

"The republic you're selling me to escape," Vesper said, "is paying your buyers across the river to end it."

"Yes." She did not flinch from it; flinching was for people who still expected to be forgiven. "Both things are true. I serve the war and I despise it. I sell to both sides and I would burn every buyer's contract if burning them would end the war instead of ending me. You want me to be a monster or a patriot so that your choice stays simple. I am neither. I am a woman with forty-one people under this roof who eats because this house sells paper to everyone with gold, and I will not apologize for the arithmetic." She paused. "Though I will tell you this, since plainness is tonight's courtesy: the ones I sell to will not stop buying because you refuse me. They will simply buy elsewhere, or worse—they will take. And you will still be here when they do, alone, guarding a bag, in a house full of people whose survival you will have declined to purchase."

She let that sit. Then she reached across the table and, for the first time, touched him—a light hand on his wrist, brief, deliberate. Under her fingers his pulse was steady, which impressed her more than anything else he had done in three weeks.

"Think of it as what it is," she said. "A door, offered early, before the fire makes other doors. I have watched you for weeks, courier. You are careful, you are decent in your small unnoticed ways—you fixed Maren's lamp bracket when you thought no one saw; you stack the drying racks at night because Tomas's hands ache. You are a good man being wasted on a dead cause. I am offering to waste you on a living one instead."

He looked at her hand on his wrist. Then he looked up, and for the first time since the pass had swallowed him she saw the courier entire—not the blankness, not the calm, but the thing underneath, which was neither fear nor anger but a kind of terrible attention, as if he were memorizing her. Every line of her face. The clasp at her throat. The face-down sheet with its six-stroke drawing of nobody.

"I'll think about it," he said.

"Of course you will." She withdrew her hand and smiled, and refilled his cup anyway, and he let her, and that small surrender told her more than anything he'd said. "Take all the time the winter allows."

* * *

After he was gone—escorted back by Tomas, politely, the way one escorts a guest one no longer quite trusts—Ilse sat alone at the table with the two cups and the face-down sheet, and did the accounting she always did after a negotiation, tallying what had been given away.

Too much, she decided. Not in facts—in feeling. She had shown him her grief, the story of Marrenhof, and grief was capital and she had spent it like a drunkard spends a final coin. Why? Because he had sat there refusing her wine with that steady pulse, and something in her had wanted, absurdly, to be *believed*—not obeyed, believed. That was the danger of this one. Buyers she could handle; buyers wanted things, and wanting was a lever. But a man who wanted nothing could not be bought and could barely be threatened, and so she had reached for the only remaining tool: the truth.

She turned the sheet over and studied the little drawing again. Two eyes that watched without being seen.

Maren had shown him the ledger. Ilse had known within the hour—known, and said nothing, because a girl's first betrayal of her mistress was a thing best left to ripen until it could be harvested whole. But now the harvest had complications: the girl had given the courier not information but *invitation*, and the courier had spent three weeks deciding whether to accept it, and tonight Ilse had interrupted at exactly the wrong moment, pushing the two of them toward each other by making herself the common enemy. Tomas, too—Tomas with his aching hands and his old flag folded somewhere in a drawer, Tomas who had begun avoiding her eyes in December. Loyalties in the house were moving like ice on the river: solid-looking, groaning, unreliable.

She snuffed the lamps except one, took up the sheet, and fed it to the candle flame, watching the drawn face curl and blacken. Then she stood a long while in the dark, listening to the wind and the distant, irregular thunder of the river ice, and admitted to herself the thing she had dressed in grey wool and served smoked fish to avoid admitting:

The offer had been real, but it had also been a probe, and the probe had come back wrong. He had said *I'll think about it*, and couriers who intended to sell said yes. Couriers who intended to fight said no. Only couriers who intended to do something else entirely asked for time.

She put on her apron, took the lamp, and went down through the sleeping house to check the locks on the copy room—finding, when she got there, that the lock was fine but the dust on the sill had been disturbed in a way she did not yet understand, and would, before the thaw.
