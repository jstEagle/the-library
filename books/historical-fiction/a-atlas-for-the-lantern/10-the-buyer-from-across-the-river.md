## 10. The Buyer from Across the River

The rider came up the valley road three days after the ice first sang at night — a sound Maren had been taught to hear as a child the way other children learned to hear bells: *groan and crack, groan and crack*, the river turning in its sleep. Too early. Everyone said so. The thaw was weeks away, and men did not cross mountains ahead of the thaw unless they were fools or were paid not to care which.

He was neither fool nor careless, this one. That was the first thing she noticed, watching from the engraving loft window while she pretended to sort plates. He rode a gray mare with a soldier's even walk, and he sat her the way sitting is done by people who have ridden through worse places than a winter pass. His coat was good wool, undyed, the color of river fog, cut in a fashion Maren had only seen in the foreign maps — broad-shouldered, high-collared, buttons of dull horn rather than the republic's bright tin. He did not look up at the workshop when he reined in at the gate. He looked at the yard, at the outbuildings, at the positions of doors, and then he looked up, once, briefly, the way a man glances at a shop sign to confirm what he already knows.

"Maren." Ilse's voice came from the foot of the stairs without its owner needing to be seen. "Come down. Take his horse. Say nothing."

Maren had learned long ago that Ilse Halvard's instructions arrived pre-trimmed, stripped of everything but the task, like boards planed for a press bed. She went down, took the gray mare's bridle. The animal smelled of lather under the winter coat; he'd been pushed hard across country that no sensible rider pushed a horse across.

"Careful of her near fore," the stranger said. Her language, accented but fluent, spoken quietly. "Ice. She'll favor it in cold mornings but she's sound."

He spoke to her the way people speak to grooms and innkeeps — not rudely, precisely, the way one confirms that a thing is functioning. Maren nodded and led the mare toward the stable, and felt, all down her back, his attention leave her the moment her back was turned, swinging instead to the workshop windows, counting lights.

She counted them too, from inside the stable, through the gap above the stall door. Four lanterns burning where usually three. Tomas's bench dark. And Vesper's window on the upper floor — Vesper kept his lamp late now; she'd seen it burn past midnight these last weeks, a small stubborn square of yellow against the snowfield — Vesper's window was dark. Someone had told him to put it out, or he had understood on his own.

That was the thing about the workshop, Maren thought, rubbing the mare down while her breath made ghosts in the cold air. It was a house built of signals. Who ate at the long table and who ate alone. Which ledger was open when a certain name was spoken. Whether Master Halvard came into the press room before noon or let the work find her after. Maren had spent nine years learning to read it, and only this winter — only since the courier with the forgettable face and the eyes that watched too carefully to be forgettable at all — had she understood that reading the signals and being unable to act on them were two different weights, and she was carrying both.

* * *

Dinner that night was not dinner. It was theater, and everyone knew their parts except perhaps the buyer, who seemed to know his better than anyone.

Ilse had the long table set with the good linen — the unbleached stuff, notably, nothing fine enough to suggest wealth worth robbing — and she seated the stranger at her right hand with Vesper's chair, the one at the far end, left empty and conspicuously so. The apprentices served. This was Maren's place tonight, along with Piet and young Anselm: standing at the sideboard, clearing plates, filling glasses, invisible in the manner of furniture.

She hated how well she knew this vantage. From below — from the serving height, the clearing height — faces arranged themselves differently than they did at the bench or the ink stone. You saw jaws more than eyes. You saw what people did with their hands between sentences. Ilse's hands lay folded and still; she was listening more than speaking, and when she spoke it was to offer things — another pour, the last of the preserved cherries, a remark about the quality of the paper stock produced by mills on the far bank — each offering a rung laid flat, so that climbing it would seem the guest's own idea.

"The pass held, then," Ilse said. "I confess I didn't expect anyone before the thaw."

"I didn't come over the pass." The buyer broke bread with neat, unhurried fingers. "I was already on this side of it since midwinter. Waiting for weather I could trust, or an invitation I couldn't."

"And which did you get?"

"Both, I think." He smiled. It was a good smile, level, without warmth in it anywhere. "Your letter reached me in the twelfth month. I confess I admired it. Not a word out of place, not a word committed. A letter written by someone who has written many such letters and buried the ones that went wrong."

"Maps teach economy," Ilse said. "Every line costs something."

"So I'm told." He set down the bread and leaned back a fraction — a small movement, but Maren saw Ilse register it, saw her fold her hands tighter. "Let's economize, then. You have sheets. I have a client who wants them before the river opens, because after it opens, what they describe stops being secret and starts being history, and history is worthless. What's your number?"

From the sideboard, Maren filled a lamp that did not need filling. Piet stood beside her, breathing through his mouth. Anselm, fourteen and incapable of disguise, stared openly until Piet trod on his foot.

Ilse named a figure. Maren did not flinch, because apprentices at clearing height did not flinch, but the figure landed in her stomach like a dropped plate — not because it was large, though it was, large enough to buy the workshop twice over, but because it was specific. It was not a number invented that evening. It was a number prepared, carried downstairs in Ilse's breast pocket alongside the good linen, meaning this dinner had been set days ago, meaning the rider's early arrival had been expected, hoped for, planned around — and every hour Maren had spent since the ice began singing, telling herself there was still time, still the thaw, still weeks, had been a child's arithmetic.

The buyer named a smaller number. Ilse named one slightly below her first. They moved toward each other the way ice floes move, slowly, with grinding, with nobody yielding visibly and everything yielding underneath.

"Half on agreement," the buyer said. "Half on my inspection. I don't pay final coin on paper I haven't read, Madam Halvard, and you shouldn't take offense at that. In my trade, offense is just pride wearing a merchant's coat."

"And in mine," Ilse said, "inspection is arranged, not demanded. But yes. You may see them. Tomorrow, in daylight, in my workroom, with me present."

"Not with your courier present."

The room did not change. That was the terrible thing — the candles didn't gutter, the soup course didn't pause, Anselm didn't stop scraping the tureen. Only two small adjustments happened at once: Ilse's chin dipped a half-degree, and at the far end of the table, Vesper's empty chair seemed suddenly less like an absence and more like a subject.

"He is not my courier," Ilse said. "He is a guest wintering with us. He knows nothing of trade here."

"Then he knows nothing, and it costs nothing either way." The buyer drank, set down the glass, aligned it with the table's edge. "But my client asks about him by description. Short man, quiet, came over the pass in the first snows carrying a courier's satchel and no luggage worth the name. When a man is described to me before I meet him, Madam, I assume others are paying for descriptions too. So either he stays ignorant, in which case keep him so, or he isn't ignorant, in which case I'd want to know whose ignorance we're purchasing."

"I see," said Ilse, and Maren heard, beneath the smoothness, the sound of a woman recalculating everything. "You'll stay the night, of course. We have the blue room ready."

"I'll stay several nights, I think." He smiled again, that level smile. "My client pays for certainty. Certainty ripens slowly."

* * *

Afterward, in the scullery, the three of them cleared up in the particular silence of people who have all heard the same dangerous thing and cannot say so. Maren scraped plates. Piet stacked. Anselm washed, badly, and was allowed to be bad at it, which was itself a kind of mercy.

It was Anselm who finally cracked. "Is it true?" he said to the water. "What he said — that people are paying for descriptions of guests?"

"People pay for descriptions of everything," Piet said. "Horses. Weather. Other people's letters."

"But Vesper—"

"Plates, Anselm."

But Maren had stopped scraping. Because she was remembering the dinner differently now, replaying it from below the way you replay a map from memory to check a bearing. *Not with your courier present.* The buyer had said it as a condition. Ilse had accepted it as a condition. And yet Vesper's chair had been left at the table, empty, displayed — and Ilse, who managed every signal in that house down to which ledger lay open on which desk, did not leave anything at a table by accident.

She was showing the buyer the chair. She was saying: *there he sits, there is your described man, and look how completely I hold him.* Everything at that dinner had been double-ledgered, even the seating. Even the emptiness.

Maren set down the scraper. Through the scullery's small window she could see the yard, blue with moonlight on snow, and beyond it the workshop's dark bulk, and — she counted without wanting to, the habit of a lifetime in a house of signals — one lamp burning where none should be. Not the copy room this time. The workroom. Ilse's own room, where tomorrow, in daylight, the true sheets would come out for inspection.

And moving along the gallery above the yard, wrapped in a coat too thin for the season, a slight figure she knew by the way it walked — quietly, close to the wall, the walk of a man practicing being nobody — going not toward the sleeping quarters but toward the stairs that led down to the press room.

Tomas was already down there. She had seen his light go out an hour ago and had assumed he'd gone to bed.

She dried her hands. She took the lamp from the scullery hook, the little one, the one that wouldn't show under a door, and she followed, because she had shown Vesper the second ledger in autumn hoping — she had never finished the sentence even inside her own head, but it ended something like *hoping someone would break it* — and hope, she understood now, standing at the top of the dark stairs with the buyers' voices still ringing in her ears and half a fortune changing hands over soup, hope that never finishes its sentence is just complicity wearing a cleaner apron.

Below her, faintly, she heard the press room door open, and Tomas's voice, low and urgent, saying the one word she could catch:

"—tonight."
