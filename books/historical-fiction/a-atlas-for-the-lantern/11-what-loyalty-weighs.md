## 11. What Loyalty Weighs

Tomas Brandt had been engraving the republic's seals for thirty-one years, and he could tell you the exact hour he stopped believing in them.

It was not a dramatic hour. It was not the night the purges came to his street, or the morning his brother's name appeared on a list, or any of the moments a novelist would choose. It was an afternoon in autumn, eleven years ago, when he sat at his bench cutting the great seal of the Chancellery into a copper plate, and he noticed that his hand no longer trembled at the fine lines of the laurel wreath. Once, in his apprenticeship, the wreath had taken him four days because his respect for it made him slow. Now it took him four hours, and the difference was not skill.

Skill had nothing to do with it. You did not need to believe in a thing to render it beautifully. That was the whole secret of his trade, and perhaps of his life: belief was for the people who read the documents. The engraver only needed to know where the light fell.

He thought about this now, standing at the window of the drying room with a cup of chicory going cold in his hand, watching Vesper Joubert cross the yard below through the falling snow.

The courier moved like a man who had been taught that being seen was a form of injury. He walked close to the walls, kept his shoulders angled as if perpetually preparing to turn sideways through a door narrower than doors actually were. Ilse had put him in the north room, the one with the good lock on the *outside*, and everyone in the workshop understood what that arrangement meant except, perhaps, the courier himself. Or perhaps he understood it perfectly and simply had nowhere else to be. That was the more likely thing. Tomas had watched men accept gilded captivity before; they always understood the terms. Understanding them and having alternatives were different matters entirely.

The bridge had been closed nine days. Nine days of snow, of lantern hours, of the codebreakers working in shifts behind the door at the end of the corridor that Tomas himself had fitted with its double lock two winters past — a commission he had accepted without asking why a map workshop needed a strongroom, because by then he had long since stopped asking why.

Maren had shown the courier the second ledger. Tomas knew this the way he knew most things in the house: by watching hands. He had seen the girl's face at supper, the particular stillness she wore when she had done something brave and was waiting to find out whether bravery would be punished. And he had seen Joubert's face across from her, the courier's eyes moving over nothing while his mind moved over everything.

Tonight, Tomas decided, he would talk to him.

Not because Ilse had asked. She had not asked, and that itself was information — Ilse Halvard left things unasked when she wanted them done but wanted her hands clean of the asking. No. He would talk to the courier because he was sixty-three years old and tired in a way that sleep did not touch, and because somewhere under thirty-one years of beautiful, meaningless line-work there remained a man who had once cut a laurel wreath in four days out of love, and that man wanted, before the end of him, to argue with someone honestly. To say the worst of it aloud. To hear it answered.

* * *

He found the courier in the map archive, which was where Joubert went every evening after supper, though he was forbidden to touch anything. He stood among the flat cabinets like a man visiting graves — reading titles, tracing borders with one finger held a careful inch above the paper, never touching. A man honoring a rule so as not to forget he was capable of keeping rules.

"The Sereval delta," Tomas said, coming up beside him. "You've looked at it four nights running."

Vesper did not startle. Tomas gave him credit for that. "I crossed there once," the courier said. "Years ago. I didn't know it looked like this from above."

"Nobody does. That's the point of maps. They show you the shape of your own life from a height you'll never have." Tomas set his cup down on a cabinet. "Come to the bench room. I want to show you something."

"I'm not permitted—"

"You're not permitted the strongroom. The bench room is where old men complain." He was already walking. Behind him he heard the pause, the small calculation, and then footsteps following. Of course they followed. What else was there, in this house, in this winter, but to follow?

* * *

The bench room smelled of acid and beeswax and the particular cold-metal sweetness of copper. Tomas lit the lamp over his own station — the oldest station, the one nearest the window, kept out of habit rather than rank — and took from the drawer beneath it a plate wrapped in oilcloth.

"Careful," he said, unwrapping it. "Edges will take your thumb off if you're careless."

Joubert leaned over the plate without touching it. It was a seal — the old seal, the first republic's seal, the one struck before the Chancellery redesigned everything after the purges. A lantern inside a circle of wheat. Under it, in letters small enough to require a loupe: *Light Enough to Walk By.*

"My master cut the original," Tomas said. "I cut this one. There were three hundred impressions pulled from plates like it, back when I was twenty-two, and every one of them went onto a document that someone believed in. Land grants. Commissions. Pardons." He turned the plate so the lamp caught the wheat. "A pardon is a beautiful thing to engrave. Do you know that? All those curves. Mercy has better lines than almost anything."

"And you don't cut them anymore."

"I cut whatever Ilse tells me to cut." He said it plainly, without self-pity, because self-pity was a luxury he had priced long ago and found too expensive. "That's what I want to talk to you about, courier. Not the ledger Maren showed you — I know she showed you, the whole house knows, the whole house knows everything and says nothing, that's how we survive each other. I want to talk about what you're deciding. Because you are deciding something. I can see it eating you. You sit at supper doing arithmetic behind your eyes, and it isn't days until the bridge anymore. It's something heavier."

Vesper straightened. In the lamplight his face was young and old at once, the way faces got when a man had been trained out of expression and then felt something too large to train away. "What would you do?" he asked. "If you were me."

Tomas laughed — a short, dry sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who had been waiting all evening for exactly this question and dreaded it anyway. "Sit down," he said. "This takes longer than you want it to."

* * *

"When I was your age," Tomas began, "I engraved for the republic and I meant it. Understand that. I wasn't a cynic who found religion late. I was a believer who lost it slowly, the way you lose a coat — leave it here, mislay it there, and one day you're cold and you can't name the moment it happened." He turned the old plate over in his hands, feeling the grooves with his thumb the way a blind man reads. "My brother Anders worked the river ferries at Sereval. Good man. Stupid in the way honest men are stupid — he thought the law was a wall that protected everybody equally, because it had always protected him. Then the purges came, and somebody denounced somebody who owed him money, and Anders's name went on a list, and the list went to the Chancellery, and the Chancellery's seal — my seal, an impression from a plate I had cut — authorized the men who took him."

Vesper had gone very still. "What happened to him?"

"He was released in the spring. Most of them were, eventually. That's the cruelty nobody warns you about — not the killing, the releasing. He came back wrong. Quiet. He'd been made to sign things in there, confessions, denunciations of other men, and he signed them, because in there signing was breathing, you did it to stay alive, and afterward he couldn't forgive himself for the arithmetic of it. He drowned himself in the delta six years later. They found him near the reed beds." Tomas set the plate down with exaggerated care, as if his hands might otherwise do something with it. "So when I tell you I have thought about loyalty, courier, understand that I have paid tuition."

"And what did you learn?"

"That truth is a luxury of winners." He said it without heat, the way a man states the price of bread. "That's the whole lesson. Everything else is commentary. When the republic was strong, my seals meant something, and meaning was cheap for me because the state supplied it. Now the republic is dying — you know it, I know it, your handler knew it when he sent you, or why send a message of surrender dressed up as diplomacy? — and the question isn't what's true. The question is who will be alive next winter to say what was true. Truth needs survivors, courier. A truth spoken by dead men is called a rumor, and rumors don't feed anyone."

"And so you copy ciphers for buyers across the river," Vesper said quietly. "And call it survival."

"I call it fidelity." Tomas met his eyes. "Say it plainly if you mean to judge me. Yes. I cut the duplicate sheets. I cut them well — that's the shame of it, I cut them *beautifully* — and they go to men who will sell them to whichever army pays, and soldiers will die holding maps I drew, and I sleep at night. Do you want to know how I sleep?"

"Yes."

"Because I count." Tomas held up his hand and folded fingers down one at a time. "Ilse feeds eleven people in this house. Apprentices whose families would be begging in the border towns. A cook with a bad heart. Maren, who would be — I don't let myself finish that sentence. Eleven people eat because the workshop serves both sides, and if we served only one side, the other side would burn us out by spring, and then the true cipher dies with us anyway, and the message you carry dies with us, and the war goes on exactly as it was going, minus eleven souls. That is the arithmetic. I have done it every night for six years. Some nights the numbers hold. Some nights they don't. But I do the sum honestly, courier, which is more than your Chancellery ever did."

"The Chancellery is finished," Vesper said. "You said so yourself. So what are you being faithful *to*?"

"To the living." For the first time Tomas's voice roughened. "You think loyalty means dying for a flag that's already half-lowered? Loyalty is a weight, boy, and weights are carried by bodies, and bodies need bread. Your republic used you precisely because you weigh nothing — no name, no keepsakes, no attachments, a man built to be disowned. Well. I am the opposite of you. I am all attachment. Every person in this house is sewn into my coat, and I will not tear them out to keep my hands clean for a state that wouldn't print my obituary."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the house a door closed, softly, the way doors closed in this place — everyone listening, everyone careful.

"And the buyers?" Vesper asked. "The men across the river? Are they living too? The soldiers who'll die because their positions were sold?"

"They are not mine to carry." It came out harder than Tomas intended, and he heard the crack in it, the hairline fracture where the argument failed even as he made it. He pressed on anyway, because a man must finish his own testimony. "No one can carry everyone. That is the first lie of every idealist — that the burden is shareable. It is not. You carry what your arms can hold and you set down the rest and you live with the sound it makes when it drops."

Vesper was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and steady and it frightened Tomas more than anger would have. "The message I carry," he said, "is a surrender. Did you know that? Not orders. Not reinforcements. A confession that it's over. And maybe that's worth nothing — maybe you're right and the republic's last truth is just a rumor spoken by a dying man. But it's *true*, Tomas. It may be the last true thing that government ever says, and I am the only body it's sewn into, and if I trade it for passage — if I let it become one more sheet in your buyer's portfolio next to forged maps and sold ciphers — then it stops being true and becomes merchandise. And I don't think I get to set that down. I think some weights aren't chosen. I think they're assigned, and the assignment is the loyalty."

"Assigned by whom?" Tomas asked, gently, because this was the soft place in the boy's armor and he hated himself for finding it. "By the men who built you to be invisible? By handlers who told you the mission was a formality and sent you anyway? Courier — they spent you. They are spending you right now, across a closed bridge, in a house full of thieves, and when spring comes they will either welcome you back or erase you, and you will have no way to know which until it happens. Fidelity to a thing that spends you isn't loyalty. It's inventory."

"And survival is?" Vesper's eyes were bright now, wet-bright, furious-bright. "Ilse spends this whole house the same way. She calls it mercy. You call it arithmetic. Everyone in this building has found a gentler word for being bought."

"Yes," Tomas said. "We have. That's what words are *for*, at the end of a war." He looked down at the old plate, the lantern in the wheat, *Light Enough to Walk By*, and his throat tightened around something thirty-one years deep. "You want me to tell you I'm damned. I won't give you the comfort. I'll tell you instead that I don't know. I wake at night and I don't know, and the not-knowing is my penance, and I serve it out daily, and it is *enough*. It has to be enough. Ask me again in the spring, if either of us is alive to answer."

* * *

They stood a while longer among the benches, the argument hanging unfinished between them like smoke that would neither rise nor clear. Nothing had been resolved. Tomas had known it wouldn't be — arguments between honest men rarely resolve; they only reveal the load each man is carrying, and loads cannot be argued lighter.

At the door, Vesper paused. "The lantern," he said, nodding at the plate. "Your master's design. Why a lantern?"

Tomas almost smiled. "Because a lantern doesn't claim to be the sun. It only claims enough light for the next step. My master said a country should promise its people no more than that — and no less." He wrapped the plate back in its oilcloth. "The republic promised suns. Look how that ended."

Vesper left. Tomas heard his careful footsteps fade down the corridor toward the north room with its outside lock, and he stood alone in the lamplight with the old seal in his hands, feeling the grooves of the wheat, the little ring of light, all that hope cut into metal by a young man who had believed.

He was still standing there when he heard, faintly, from the direction of the front hall — boots on the packed snow of the yard, more than one pair, and the low voice of the gatekeeper challenging, and then Ilse's voice rising to meet whoever had come, warm and welcoming and an hour past any honest hour for visitors.

Tomas set the plate in its drawer and closed it gently, the way you close a coffin.

The buyer from across the river had arrived early.
