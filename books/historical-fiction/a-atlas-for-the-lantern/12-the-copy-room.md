## 12. The Copy Room

The buyer slept in the blue room.

Vesper learned this the way he learned everything in the workshop — by being small enough that no one thought to hide things from him. He carried the man's boots to be dried by the stove, and they were fine boots, city boots, stitched with a maker's mark from a capital Vesper had never been allowed to visit. He collected the man's coat, heavy as a dead animal, and felt in its lining the shape of papers already signed. He poured the man's wine and heard him say, over the rim of his glass, that the thaw could not come soon enough for his taste, that winter was bad for business, and Ilse laughed and said winter was *wonderful* for business, because winter kept honest people honest.

Vesper smiled at the correct moments. He had spent eleven days smiling at the correct moments, and each one had cost him something he could not name but could feel leaving him, the way heat leaves a stone — slowly, then all at once when you finally touch it.

He did not sleep that night.

He lay in the narrow room they had given him — the room without a lock, the room whose door he had long ago wedged with a sliver of candle wax so it would open silently, a habit so old it preceded thought — and he listened to the house breathe. A workshop sleeps differently than a home. It sighs through its presses. Its beams tick with the cooling of the drying room. Somewhere below, always, there was the faint mineral smell of ink, which had gotten into the walls themselves, into the grain of the wood, so that even in darkness the house smelled of things being reproduced.

At what he judged to be two hours past midnight, the last lantern in the corridor went out. Tomas's footsteps passed his door and faded toward the apprentices' loft — slow steps, an old man's steps, pausing once outside Vesper's threshold for the length of three breaths before moving on. Vesper could not decide whether the pause was warning or apology. Perhaps, he thought, in Tomas they were the same thing.

He waited another quarter hour. Then he rose, dressed in the dark, and took the wax sliver from the door.

* * *

The copy room was at the end of the east wing, down a corridor where the workshop's oldest work hung framed on the walls: survey maps of passes that no longer belonged to anyone, river charts of channels since shifted, a great engraved plate of the republic's first atlas, its margins crowded with the names of towns that had burned or surrendered or simply been renamed by whoever held them the longest. Vesper had walked this corridor a hundred times in daylight and never once looked at the frames. Now, by the light of a single shuttered lantern turned to a thread, he looked at all of them. It was like walking past a cemetery of places still standing.

The copy room door was locked. Of course it was locked. But locks were made for apprentices and thieves, and Vesper had been trained to be neither — to be a man whose presence in a corridor explained itself, who could stand behind a locked door with a lantern thread and look, to anyone passing, like a reason rather than a problem. No one passed. The lock was old brass, older than Ilse, and it gave to the thin wire he had taken from Maren's binding kit three days ago, telling himself even then that he was only preparing, only keeping options open, the great lie of every courier who has ever decided to stop running.

Inside, the cold was different — drier, sharper, the cold of paper. Paper breathes out its own climate; anyone who has slept in a map archive knows it. Rows of flat presses stood along the walls like sarcophagi. Drying racks rose to the ceiling, sheets pinned in place, and in the lamplight they looked like the shed skins of enormous pale insects. Along the far wall, the south-facing one, built deep to keep the temperature even, stood the cabinet of drawers where the workshop kept its plates and its most delicate commissions — the drawer into which, four nights ago, Vesper had watched Tomas slide the copper plate of the pass survey, closing it gently, the way you close a coffin.

Vesper set his lantern on the central table and stood very still and let the room tell him what it was.

It was a factory. That was the truth of it, stripped now of lantern-hour charm. Not a scriptorium, not a library, not a house of learning — a factory for the manufacture of certainty. Every instrument had its place marked on the bench in painted outline, the way a surgeon's tray is laid out. There were trays of type in forty hands, including three distinct imitations of official ministerial scripts, and Vesper recognized one of them — recognized it with a lurch in his stomach — as the hand in which his own commission had been written. The republic's own secret hand, hanging here in labeled rows like herbs.

They had not needed to steal it. They had only needed, over twenty years, to be asked to print things in it, until the asking became routine and the routine became ownership.

He crossed to the cabinet of drawers. His heart was behaving strangely — not racing but slowing, each beat deliberate, the body's old trick of making time pass more slowly when it wanted to see everything. He opened the drawer Tomas had closed.

The plate was gone from it. In its place lay paper.

He lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a newborn or a wound. Six sheets, laid between felt blotters, still carrying the faint damp swell of fresh impression. And across the top sheet, in the precise gray hand of the workshop's finest engraving transfer, ran the opening line of his cipher.

His cipher. The seals he had carried through the pass strapped against his ribs, the packet he had handed to Ilse Halvard with ceremony he no longer believed in. Here it was, half-born: the first substitution row rendered perfectly, the second begun and abandoned mid-line where the copying hand had paused — he could see the hesitation, a slight thickening of the stroke where the pen had lifted and considered. Someone had worked late and stopped. Someone would finish tomorrow.

Or tonight. Or perhaps never again, if tonight went the way he was suddenly, terrifyingly, certain it must go.

He stood holding the sheets and understood that he had come here to burn them.

He had known it, he realized, since the buyer's boots arrived in the yard. Since before that — since the second ledger, since Ilse's offer of lodging *as one of us*, since the morning he'd woken in this house and found himself thinking of its rhythms as comfort instead of captivity. He had told himself he was gathering information, weighing options, being the professional his training demanded. But couriers do not gather information. Couriers carry. And somewhere in the past eleven days the thing he carried had changed weight without changing size, and tonight he had come down through the sleeping house like a man walking to a well he had already decided to fall into, and the fire was in his pocket — he touched it, the little tin of matches, the flammable wax cubes meant for sealing letters, repurposed — the fire was in his pocket and the copies were in his hands and it would take four minutes, five at most, and then the buyers across the river would purchase six sheets of beautifully rendered nothing.

The match tin was warm from riding against his body all evening. That warmth seemed obscene now, a small dishonest animal.

He knelt beside the central table, away from the drying racks — fire spreads, he was not a fool, he would contain it, the stone hearth in the corner where they burned spoiled impressions was right there, three steps — and he drew out a match.

And then his hand stopped, because his mind had finally caught up with the rest of him, and showed him the flaw.

The sheets in his hands were copies of the cipher. But a cipher is only a lock. And somewhere in this house — in Ilse's study, most likely, in the locked case where she kept active commissions — sat the decoded message. The translation. The plain-language text that the cipher existed to protect, extracted by the codebreakers in their lantern hours while he was fed dinner and forbidden to know what he carried.

He had come to burn the lock. But the message — whatever the dying republic had needed said badly enough to send a forgettable man through a winter pass — that would remain. Sitting in a drawer. In a workshop that served both sides. Waiting for the next buyer, the spring buyer, the buyer after that, to purchase it alongside everything else Ilse Halvard had decided the war was worth.

Burn the copies and you bought nothing. You bought a delay of weeks. You bought yourself a clean feeling and left the actual words — the surrender, the withdrawal, whatever terrible necessary sentence his country had composed in its last competent hour — lying in this house like a purse left on a table.

And worse. He saw it with the clarity that comes only at the worst moments, when understanding arrives like ice water: if he destroyed the reproductions, the work would simply be done again. The cipher original was here. The codebreakers were here. Tomas's hands were here. He could burn these six sheets every night for a month and the workshop would produce six more, and after the first burning there would be no pretense left, no hospitality, no winter lodging offered kindly. Ilse would know exactly what he was, and what she did to problems she knew about, she would do quickly and quietly, and in spring the bridge would open onto a world in which Vesper Joubert had vanished as completely as his doctrine required — except that this time the disappearing would not be service. It would just be erasure. The republic used men like him precisely because they could be disowned. No one would ever ask where he'd gone. That had once felt like safety. Kneeling on the cold floor of the copy room with an unlit match in his fingers, it felt like the trap's final bar.

No. If the message was going to leave this house, it had to leave whole — cipher and translation together, or better, translation alone, carried out under the noses of everyone, and the copies left intact only until the moment they could be replaced rather than destroyed. Replaced. Forged. Fed to the buyers as poison instead of bread.

The idea arrived not as a plan but as a shape, a silhouette of a plan, and Vesper knelt there and let it take form around the stubborn ember of the one thing he still possessed: he was invisible. He had been made invisible by professionals, at cost, over years. It was the only skill he owned outright. And invisibility, he saw now, was not the absence of action. It was action wearing the dark.

He struck the match anyway — because a man kneeling at a hearth at three in the morning must be doing something, and he needed, for one heartbeat, to have been doing it — and touched the flame to a spoiled impression sheet pulled from the wastebasket, a botched map of some coastline, worthless, and let it curl and go up in the hearth where such sheets belonged. Smoke, a little. Evidence of industry, if anyone asked. Ash among ash.

Then he put the six cipher sheets back between their blotters, squared them as Tomas had, and closed the drawer gently, the way you close a coffin.

* * *

He was almost at the door when the lantern-thread of light under it darkened, and someone knocked — softly, twice, with a fingernail, the way you knock when you are not supposed to be awake either.

Vesper stood motionless, match tin in his pocket, heart a closed fist. Through the door came a whisper, thin as the lamp-flame he had killed.

"I know you're in there." A pause. "I've known since you borrowed my wire."

Maren's voice. Steady, and underneath the steadiness, something shaking.

"Tomorrow," she whispered, "there's a storm coming. We'll all be shut in together. Nobody in, nobody out, nobody watching anybody." Another pause, longer, and he heard her swallow. "I need to show you something. And you can't tell Tomas I asked."

Her shadow shifted away from the crack beneath the door. Vesper stood in the dark of the copy room, among the drying skins and the sleeping presses and the six sheets of his own betrayal resting ten feet behind him, and realized that for the first time since he'd crossed the pass, someone in this house had spoken to him as though he were visible — and that it was the most dangerous thing that had happened yet.
