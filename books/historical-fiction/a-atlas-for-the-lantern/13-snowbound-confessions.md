## 13. Snowbound Confessions

The storm came down off the pass in the night like something that had been waiting all winter for permission.

Maren woke to the sound of the workshop groaning—not the settling creak of a house in cold, but a deeper complaint, timber and slate flexing under the weight of snow that fell without wind at first, straight down, in quantities that erased the world. By the hour before dawn the windows of the apprentices' loft had gone white and featureless, and when she pressed her palm to the glass it met not cold air but a soft, dense resistance, as though someone had packed the panes with wool from the outside.

By midmorning no one could say where the yard ended. The drying lines between the outbuildings had become strings of white, sagging. The path to the privy was a trench someone dug and someone else filled behind them. Ilse stood at the great south window for a long time with her arms folded, looking out at nothing, and then she said, in the voice she used for weather and for orders, "No one goes beyond the wood store," and turned back to her ledgers, and that was the whole of the ceremony. The storm had closed the workshop like a fist.

Maren felt the change in the house the way one feels a change in pressure before pain. Everyone felt it, though no one named it: the buyer—Voss, he was called, a soft-spoken man who ate slowly and looked at hands more than faces—could not leave, and so he did not leave his room except to eat, and his presence at the long table each evening had become a kind of sediment the whole household settled around. And Vesper Joubert could not leave either. His quarters had been moved after the thaw began its first false start, moved upstairs, nearer to Ilse's own rooms, and his satchel had gone with the move and had not come back. He sat at meals now with the particular stillness of a man being watched by furniture.

Three days the storm held them. On the second evening the lanterns were lit at four o'clock because the daylight had failed to arrive at all, only a dim bruise of grey where the sky should be, and Tomas lit the stove in the engraving room and let the apprentices abandon their plates early because the damp made the ink misbehave anyway. Maren ground pigment until her wrist ached and then found there was nothing left to grind that needed grinding, and stood at the window with her stained hands hanging at her sides, watching snow bury the world.

It was Vesper who spoke to her first. That mattered to her later; she kept it, afterward, like a coin kept in a boot against bad roads—that he had come to her, and not the other way around.

"You've stopped watching me," he said. He had come up beside her quietly, which she should have resented and did not. Outside, the snow made a sound like breathing if you listened too long. "For two months I could feel your eyes on my back every time I touched a door. Now nothing."

"Everyone knows everything now," Maren said. It came out flatter than she meant. "There's no point watching a man everyone already watches."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," she agreed. She turned her head and looked at him properly. Up close he was exactly what his trade required him to be—a brown-haired man of no particular age whose face slid off the memory—but his eyes didn't cooperate with the rest of him. They had never cooperated. From the first week, when she'd caught him reading the spines of the locked cabinets, she had thought: *there is a person inside the courier, and someone has been very careless with the lock.* "The answer is that I'm afraid of you now instead of curious about you, and fear is quieter."

Something moved across his face—hurt, maybe, or the recognition of hurt owed. "You showed me the ledger," he said, very low. The engraving room hummed around them: the stove, the muffled storm, Tomas humming an old marching song under his breath with all the march taken out of it. "In autumn. You left the cabinet unbarred and you waited where I would see you waiting. Don't tell me I imagined that. Why?"

Maren looked out at the whiteness for a long moment. Then she said, "Come to the drying loft tonight. After the house sleeps. Not because I'll tell you anything new." She swallowed. "Because I want to know if I'm alone in what I know, or if knowing is enough to make two people into something."

* * *

The drying loft ran the length of the workshop's upper floor, above the press room, where sheets of freshly printed maps hung from lines like strange laundry: mountain ranges and river deltas and coastlines, hundreds of them, ghost-pale in the dark, all of them slightly wrong these past two years in ways only insiders would ever notice—a ford marked where no ford existed, a road drawn through forest that had burned a decade ago. The wrongnesses were the product. The wrongnesses were what crossed the border in diplomatic pouches and merchant bales, and somewhere on the far side of them men made decisions about bridges and supply lines based on paper made honest-looking by people she loved.

Maren climbed up through the trapdoor at midnight with a shuttered lantern and found Vesper already there, sitting on the floorboards beneath the hanging sheets with his knees drawn up, looking less like a courier than like a boy playing truant. She lowered herself beside him and set the lantern between them, hooded to a coin of light.

"The house is asleep," she whispered. "Tomas sleeps like the dead. The buyer took wine up with him—he does that when he's bored. And Aunt Ilse—" She hesitated.

"Aunt Ilse doesn't sleep," Vesper finished quietly. "I've noticed. Go on."

"Aunt Ilse reads until the candle fails. Her rooms are over the map library. We're directly above the press room, which is empty, and the stairs creak at both ends, and we have perhaps an hour before anyone comes looking for heat or water. So." Maren pulled her shawl tighter. In the coin of light the hanging maps swayed faintly, stirred by the draft from the trapdoor, and shadows moved across Vesper's face like pages turning. "You asked me why."

"I asked you why."

"Because you're the first person to come into this house in three years who wasn't already bought." She said it fast, before her nerve went. "Do you understand? Tomas sold his politics the winter they shot his brother—the price was that we keep printing and he keeps quiet and alive. Anselm sold his curiosity; he's been told not to have any and he obeys. The boys downstairs sell their labor and don't ask what for, and that's a sale too, everyone sells something, I understand that, I'm not a child. But *you*—you came carrying something you weren't supposed to read, for a republic that you must have known was dying, for wages I could guess, and you didn't sell it. Even when it would have bought you out of here by spring. Even when Aunt Ilse offered. You sat across the table from her and said no with your face while your mouth said you'd think about it, and I thought—" Her voice cracked and she pressed on, angry at the crack. "I thought, *here at last is someone the ledger can't account for.*"

The snow filled the silence. Underneath them, faintly, the workshop ticked and settled, ice expanding in the gutters.

"The ledger," Vesper said at last. His voice had changed—careful, careful, the way a man walks on river ice. "Tell me what it means. Not what's written in it. I've seen the copies going out; I can count. Tell me what it *means*, Maren. Because I think you've understood it longer and better than I have, and I think it's eating you alive, and I want to know if I'm right."

She laughed, or something near laughter, and it came out wet. "It means we are the war," she said. "Don't you see? The armies are just—the armies are just weather. Men move where the maps say the ground allows them, and the bridges stand or fall where the paper says, and for two years this house has been selling the ground itself to whoever pays. First one side, then both, and now—" She gestured upward, helplessly, at the hanging sheets, at the pale ghost-geography of a country being redrawn for profit. "My father built half of these plates. True ones. He used to say a map was a promise you made to strangers, that the world was *knowable*, that a traveler with one of ours would live to see home. He died in the purges same as Aunt Ilse's family, did you know that? Same year, same edicts. She built this place to be *neutral*—she said it so often I believed it. Neutral ground. No flags in the print room. And neutrality is just—" Maren stopped. Breathed. "Neutrality is just a shop that sells to both queues. And the queues got longer, and the prices got better, and somewhere along the way neutral became *this*. A ledger in a locked drawer with columns for buyers on both sides of a bridge that closes every winter so nobody can check the accounts."

"And you copied the ledger," Vesper said softly. "Before you showed me. Didn't you."

It wasn't a question. She hadn't known it was visible, that thing in her, but apparently it had been visible for months.

"There's a copy," she admitted. "Hidden where no one will find it, not even you, so don't ask. Names, dates, payments, plate numbers. If anything happens to me, it finds its way out. I told myself that was courage. Mostly it was arithmetic—I am one apprentice and Aunt Ilse owns the roof over my head and the town below us and the ferryman and probably the priest, and courage without arithmetic is just dying loudly." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "But then you came. An outsider. Someone they *needed* to leave alive in the spring, someone whose disappearance would raise questions even Ilse can't afford. And I thought—if I show him the ledger, if I put the weight of it on someone from outside this house—maybe he'll do what insiders can't. Maybe he'll carry it away. It's what you *are*, isn't it? A carrier. So carry *this*, I thought. Carry the truth of this house out over that bridge in the spring, and let it land wherever truths land, and let it fall on them."

"And if it falls on you?"

"Then I chose it," Maren said simply, "which is more than most people in this war get to do."

The lantern guttered and she trimmed the wick with a fingernail. Above them the maps hung motionless again, the draft gone, the whole loft holding its breath. Vesper was silent so long she began to count the sounds of the storm to keep from counting his heartbeats, and then he spoke, barely above the snow-sound.

"When the bridge opens," he said, "they mean to send the cipher sheets out with Voss's own escort. Ilse promised him passage ahead of anyone else. The decoded message—my message—they'll hold it back, or copy it altered, or use it as leverage against whatever government still stands to receive it. Everything true leaves this house as a lie and everything false leaves looking true. You know that."

"Yes."

"So carrying the ledger isn't enough anymore. A ledger proves theft after the theft. What's in this house is going to kill people *before* anyone reads any accounting of it." He turned to look at her fully, and in the small light his forgettable face was entirely unforgettable, and Maren thought, absurdly, *so that's what it looks like when a courier stops being invisible.* "We need to take the truth out of the buyers' hands, not just document it. Which means forgery, and timing, and at least one man inside the copy room who can reproduce a master's hand, and it means trusting each other past the point where trust is sane. Do you understand what I'm asking?"

"Tomas," Maren breathed. Her mind was already running ahead, terrified and quick. "He engraved half those plates. He can forge Ilse's hand in his sleep—he taught her the letterforms himself, before—"

"Before," Vester agreed grimly, "whatever 'before' broke. Will he?"

"I don't know." Honest. It cost her. "He's been quiet so long I don't know what's left inside the quiet. But he watched you refuse Ilse at dinner twice, and the second time he stopped chewing, and I saw his hands, and old engravers' hands always tell you what they're thinking. He hasn't decided yet. But he's *considering*, and Tomas hasn't considered anything on Ilse's behalf in a year."

Vesper almost smiled. "Then we have a conspiracy of two and a half."

"Two and a half conspiracies and a stolen lantern," Maren said, "in a house where the mistress doesn't sleep."

And that was when she heard it—or rather heard the absence of it, the way you hear a stopped clock. The stair at the far end of the loft, the third step, the one that always sang. It had not sung. No footstep retreated, no board eased. But beneath the trapdoor, in the dark of the empty press room, there had been—for a moment, just at the edge of the lantern's coin, just once—the smallest shift of weight, of a body standing very still on boards that knew it, and then nothing. Only the storm, and the maps swaying gently overhead like a field of pale wheat, and somewhere below them, moving away without sound, someone who now knew everything.

Maren's eyes found Vesper's across the light. Neither of them said her name. Neither of them had to.

Ilse Halvard climbed back down through the sleeping house with the patience of a woman who had all winter, and said nothing—yet.
