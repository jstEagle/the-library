## 14. Ilse's War

The winter Ilse Halvard learned that maps could kill, she was seventeen and living in a house with blue shutters on the Vellstrasse, in the quarter of the capital where civil servants and engravers kept their hedges trimmed to identical heights. Her father drew coastlines for the Admiralty. Her mother bound books in a shop below their apartment, and the smell of glue and leather came up through the floorboards like a second weather. Her brother Anselm, fourteen, wanted to be a soldier, which in those first months of the republic seemed to everyone a harmless ambition, the way a boy might want to be a lighthouse keeper or a man who rode the mail coaches.

She remembered the shutters because of what happened to them. They were painted over in gray by men with government stencils, one October morning, while her father stood at the window with his hands behind his back and watched them work. *Neutral colors,* they told him. *The committee has determined that certain hues are ideologically suggestive.* Blue, apparently, suggested something. Her father never said what. He was a careful man; he had spent thirty years drawing the exact edge of things, and he knew better than anyone how a border drawn wrong could drown a village.

Her name then was not Halvard. It was Ilse Ferrejn, and it would be struck from three registries within two years.

* * *

The purges did not come like a storm. That was the lie everyone who survived them told afterward—that they had been sudden, a squall out of a clear sky. No. They came like cartography itself: slowly, patiently, line by line, until one day you looked up and discovered that the country had been redrawn around you and your street now stood on the other side of belonging.

First it was the names. Then it was the commissions—her father's coastlines declared suspect because he had once corrected a naval chart under the old monarchy, and accuracy, the committee decided, was a form of nostalgia. Then it was the neighbors, who stopped meeting her mother's eyes in the market because meeting eyes had become a transaction, and everyone was suddenly poor.

Anselm joined the youth brigade in the spring because he believed it would protect the family. He was fourteen and he believed things at full volume. Ilse could still hear him rehearsing his recitations in the courtyard, the revolutionary verses he delivered with such earnest fury that the words broke apart into pure sound, and she loved him so much in those evenings that the memory of it would later feel like an injury.

He denounced a teacher in November. He wept about it privately, only to Ilse, and made her swear not to tell their parents, and she understood even then—he was fourteen, but she understood—that denunciation was the only form of loyalty the republic would accept from a boy whose father drew suspect coastlines. He was trying to buy them all back with the only coin he was permitted to hold.

In February they took the father anyway.

* * *

There is a version of this story in which Ilse rages, pounds doors, writes petitions. There is no truth in that version. What happened instead was quieter and more terrible: nothing she did made any difference, so she stopped doing, and the stopping became its own education. She learned the arithmetic of fear. She learned which clerks accepted bribes of coffee and which accepted silence, and that these were often the same clerks. She learned that the republic, for all its talk of the people, ran on an endless supply of small mercies purchased at compound interest.

Her mother lasted eight more months in the binding shop before the guild expelled her—for associations, the letter said, adverse to the health of the state. Health. As if her mother's hands, which could set a spine so true a book would open flat after fifty years, were a disease.

They came for Anselm last, in the summer. Not the enemy—their own side, his side, the brigade he had bled his conscience to join. A paperwork error, someone said later. A list copied wrong. Or perhaps no error at all; perhaps a boy who wept after denunciations had been noticed weeping. Ilse never learned which, and the not-knowing did more to shape her than any certainty could have. Certainty can be fought. Ambiguity only ferments.

By autumn she was alone in the apartment with the gray shutters, and the smell of glue still rose through the floorboards from some other family's shop, and she sat at her father's drafting table with his instruments laid out before her like relics—ruling pen, beam compass, the burnisher he had kept oiled for forty years—and she understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only when there is nothing left to protect, that she had been studying all along without knowing it.

She had studied how power moves. It moves like ink on wet paper: along the fibers already present.

* * *

She left the capital with a forged travel pass she had drawn herself, and the forgery was good enough that it taught her something: the state's authority was, in the end, a matter of typography. Stamps, signatures, the weight of paper, the confidence of a straight line. Anyone who could reproduce belief convincingly enough could carry it across any border. She was nineteen years old and carrying this thought like contraband when she walked into the mountain town of Sereval and found, at the far end of it, a shuttered map works with a slate roof and a view of the river gorge, for sale by a widow who asked half its value because the war had emptied the roads and no one believed in maps anymore.

No one believed in maps anymore. Ilse bought the building with her father's last remaining silver and moved in before the ink on the deed dried.

The early years she would remember as the only honest ones. She hired Tomas first—a journeyman engraver ten years her senior, hollowed out by the same purges, who arrived with tool-chest and tremor and asked no questions because questions were the currency that had ruined him. Then the Osten brothers to pull the press. Then a girl from the valley to grind pigments, then another, then a boy who could draw birds so precisely the ornithological society in the capital wrote to request plates. The workshop filled with lantern light and the smell of oil and lamp-black, and word traveled down from the pass, as word does: at Halvard's, they don't ask where you came from. At Halvard's, a name struck from the registry will still get you paid on Fridays.

That was the founding promise, and she had kept it in her chest like a coal. Neutral ground. The workshop served the republic's survey office because the survey office paid, and it served refugee forgers and exile printers and, eventually, officers of the very committees that had unmade her family, because—

Because the first time a committee man came asking whom she employed, she showed him a ledger with two sets of entries, and he left satisfied, and she lay awake all night understanding that she had discovered the shape of her life. Not vengeance. Something colder and more durable. The republic had killed her family with paperwork; she would survive it the same way, and survival, done well enough, done long enough, began to look like a kind of justice. Every secret she held was a weight on the scale. Every buyer served was a buyer owed, and owed men are slower to inform.

*It is not corruption,* she told herself in those years, and the sentence had the ring of her father's voice, precise as a ruling pen. *It is counterweight.*

* * *

The ledger grew its second volume the winter the war came south over the pass.

She had not planned it. She resisted the phrase *planned it* the way she resisted all flattering lies about herself. What happened was that a buyer arrived—an agent, soft-spoken, with the river accent of the eastern states—and asked, over the tea she poured because hospitality was armor, whether the workshop's famous facsimile work extended to documents other than charts. And Ilse heard herself pause exactly long enough. Not too eager. Not refusing. The pause of a woman weighing, which every buyer reads as a woman bargaining, and which is in fact a woman standing at the edge of her old life looking down.

She said yes because saying yes meant the workshop lived, and the workshop was Anselm's apprenticeship that never happened, was her mother's hands, was the blue shutters repainted in every color the committees had banned. She said yes and told herself the buyers' secrets would be her arsenal, and for a long while this was even true.

But ledgers do not distinguish between arsenal and merchandise. Entries accrete. The second volume thickened. Buyers recommended buyers; factions learned that whatever one side commissioned, the other could acquire, priced according to urgency; and Ilse Halvard found that she had built, brick by necessary brick, the exact inverse of her founding promise. Neutral ground had become common ground—market ground—where every cause shopped and no truth was safe simply because everyone's money was green.

She knew it. That was the thing no one guessed, watching her move through the workrooms with her lamp and her unhurried step. She was not deceived about herself; deception was a luxury for people with clean hands. She knew the workshop had become a place where the dead of both sides were, in a sense, printed and sold by the ream. Some nights she stood in the copy room among the drying sheets and felt her father beside her, feeling the paper the way he used to, testing its tooth, and saying nothing, which from him had always been the loudest rebuke.

She kept him at the table anyway. Sentiment, her buyers would have called it, if they had known he existed. But sentiment was not the right word either. He was the reason. Not the excuse—the reason. Everything the workshop had become, every compromised page, traced back through the chain of survivals to the day the men with stencils painted over the blue, and she would rather stand damned at his table than forgiven anywhere else.

This is what she would want understood, though she confessed it to no one: villains believe they are settling accounts. Ilse Halvard had simply gotten very good at arithmetic that always rounded in her favor.

* * *

Now, present tense again, the winter of the courier:

She stood at the copy room door after midnight with her lamp turned low, watching Vesper Joubert sleep in the chair they had given him, and she saw her own seventeen-year-old self sitting there—someone else's instrument, trained into invisibility by people who would disown him by morning. The republic wasted nothing. It consumed its children twice: once for labor, once for blame.

Tomas had told her, days ago, that the courier watched too carefully. Maren had shown him the second ledger—Maren, of all people, her steadiest hand, and Ilse had known about that within the hour and chosen, for now, to say nothing, because a girl testing a stranger's soul was not yet treason, and because part of Ilse wanted to know what the courier would do with what he'd seen. She had been running that experiment all winter without admitting its name: *hope.* Hope that an outsider, seeing the machine whole, would refuse it in a way she no longer could.

The thaw was weeks away. Ice groaned on the river at night like old furniture in an empty house. The buyer from across the water paced the guest quarters and extended his stay and asked sharper questions, and the courier's satchel hung now in her own locked cabinet, and everything was arranged, balanced, entered in both volumes of the ledger as she had entered every winter for twenty years.

But tonight, watching him sleep with the tension of a man composing a report in dreams, Ilse felt the faintest wrongness in the workshop's hum—the press out of true by a hair, the apprentice steps a half-beat quick. Counterweights shifting somewhere below her notice.

She turned up her lamp and went down to check the map press, because her father had taught her that borders move first in places too small to see.
