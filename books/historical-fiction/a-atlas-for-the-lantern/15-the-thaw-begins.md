## 15. The Thaw Begins

The ice spoke first.

Vesper heard it before dawn, a long groan rolling up the valley like something vast turning over in its sleep. He lay in the narrow cot they had given him—third room he'd held since winter began—and listened to the river under its white lid, testing itself against the season. By midmorning the sound came again, sharper, a report like a musket fired beneath the snow, and the apprentices at their presses paused with ink-blackened fingers lifted, heads cocked, all of them reading the same message.

The thaw had begun.

He felt it as a loosening in his own chest, a door unbarred somewhere far off. Weeks now—not months. The bridge at Sereval would open when the ice broke, and everything that had been suspended through the winter would resume its motion: couriers, armies, debts, betrayals. Time, which had seemed to pool and stagnate in the workshop like water under ice, was moving again. And moving time was Ilse Halvard's element. She had built her house on the patience of winters; she would not waste this one's ending.

Vesper stood at the window of his small room above the drying loft and watched the light on the mountains. It had changed—thinner, whiter, the light of a season deciding to leave. Snow still lay deep on the roofs of the workshop compound, but along the southern eaves the icicles were dripping, ticking against the stone like a slow clock.

*Weeks,* he thought. *Perhaps less.*

And across the courtyard, in the guest quarters that were finer than any Vesper had been given, the buyer from across the river was extending his stay.

* * *

Vesper learned of it at supper, from the mouths of others, because that was how all news reached him now. He had been moved—without explanation—to a room at the end of the east range, near the bindery, where the walls were thick and the window looked onto nothing but the sheer drop behind the workshop. A room with one exit and no view worth climbing out of. He had not protested. Protest would have told Ilse how much he understood.

"The gentleman stays until the crossing," said Petra, the bindery forewoman, to no one in particular, ladling barley soup with the weary authority of a woman who had fed this household through a war. "Sent a rider down through the pass yesterday. His people will meet him at Sereval when the bridge opens."

"Then he'll want everything finished by then," said the younger engraver, Ferren, not looking up from his bowl.

"He'll want it finished before then," Petra said. "Gentlemen like that don't wait on bridges. Bridges wait on them."

Vesper kept his eyes on his soup and let the words settle into him like stones into a riverbed. The buyer was staying. The buyer's people would be at Sereval. Which meant the exchange—the sale of his cipher, the republic's last diplomatic code, copied sheet by patient sheet in the room he was never permitted to enter—was scheduled now, fixed to a date, no longer an abstraction drifting through the winter. It had become a thing with a day attached, the way his own mission had once had a bridge closing attached to it, and he remembered with a cold twist of shame how he had counted days like coins on the road north, hoarding them, spending them, certain that time was the only currency that mattered.

Time belonged to someone else now.

After supper he took his cup of chicory to the corner by the stove, as was his habit, and made himself small, and listened. This too had become habit: the discipline of invisibility turned inward, aimed at the house that had made use of it. He had spent eleven years learning to be forgotten the moment he left a room. Now he used it as a tool. People said things in front of furniture.

"They're doubling the watch on the copy room," Ferren was saying, low. "Two keys now. Halvard keeps one, the other goes to her at night."

"And him?" Petra tipped her chin, very slightly, toward Vesper's corner.

"What about him? He carries soup to his room and stares at the wall."

"Halvard doesn't move a man three times in two months for staring at walls."

Ferren shrugged. But later, when the table emptied, he lingered near Vesper long enough to say, without looking at him: "The old master's hand is getting worse. The tremor. You've seen it." And then he was gone, before Vesper could decide whether it was warning or bait.

Tomas's tremor. Tomas, who had argued over chess and brandy that truth was a luxury of winners, whose arguments had grown shorter lately, quieter, as if the sentences themselves had begun to shake. Vesper turned the words over all the way up the stairs to his exiled room. Either the old engraver was failing in ways that threatened the quality of the copies—which would matter enormously to buyers paying for perfect work—or something else was trembling in him besides his hands.

* * *

Ilse came for the satchel on a morning of hard blue sky.

She did not send anyone. That was the point of it, Vesper understood—she came herself, knocked herself, entered with the unhurried grace of a woman visiting a tenant, and stood in the middle of his bare room taking inventory of its poverty with one sweep of her grey eyes.

"You've been comfortable," she said, in the tone of a hostess apologizing for inadequate hospitality.

"Comfortable enough."

"I'm going to have to ask you for your satchel, courier." She said the word *courier* the way another woman might have said *darling*, tenderly, mockingly. "There's been some rearranging below stairs. Inventory. With the thaw coming we take stock of everything in the house." Her gaze rested on the satchel where it hung from the single peg by the door—his one possession, empty now of everything except a comb, a spare shirt, and the ghost of a mission. Everything that mattered had never been in it. Everything that mattered was sewn into the lining of his coat, flat against his ribs, a folded page of decoded surrender he had sworn to carry to a republic that might already be a name.

"It holds nothing of yours," Vesper said.

"It holds nothing at all, I imagine. That isn't why I want it." She crossed to the peg and lifted it down herself, unhurried, giving him every chance to object, and the chance itself was the message: *object, please, and show me what you are.* "A man who has nothing to hide gives up his bag without complaint. A man who complains—" she smiled, "—makes me curious. Curiosity is expensive in winter, Vesper. For both of us."

He let her take it. He even helped, holding the door, watching her turn the satchel over in her hands, feeling along the seams with fingers that had probably found every hidden pocket ever sewn in this house's long career of serving both sides. She found the false bottom—he had left it deliberately, containing nothing but a folded map of the pass, accurate and useless—and unfolded it, studied it, refolded it with a small approving nod.

"Clever," she said. "Empty. Even better than full, for my purposes. Now when I tell my buyer you surrendered your effects willingly, he'll believe you're broken." She slung the satchel over her shoulder like a schoolgirl's. "You'll have it back when you cross the bridge. I keep my bargains, Vesper. It's the only religion left to me."

"And if I don't cross?"

"Then it stays on its peg in my office, and so do you." At the door she paused, and for a moment the marketplace mask slipped, and he saw beneath it the woman from the flashback Maren had given him in fragments during the storm-bound weeks—the girl whose family had gone to the purges, whose neutrality had begun as a fortress and hardened into a shop. "The ice is talking," she said quietly. "Do you hear it? Every year it talks like that and every year I think: *this spring will be different.* It never is. The river does the same thing it has always done. So do I. So will you." Then she was gone, and the lock turned in the corridor outside, soft as a sigh.

* * *

His new room had a bolt on the outside.

He discovered it the first night—a sliding iron tongue set into the frame, painted over so many times it blended with the wood, fresh grease on the track. Someone had prepared this room for a guest who might need keeping. Perhaps years ago, for another courier, another witness, another piece of cargo that had learned too much. The thought did not frighten him exactly. It clarified. Ilse had stopped pretending, and there was a strange relief in that, the way there is relief when a diagnosis finally has a name.

Options narrowed like the pass in winter. He laid them out each night on the floor of his mind as he had once laid out route maps: the window, four floors above rock, watched by nothing only because no one believed it needed watching. The corridor, bolted. The household—could he trust any of them? Ferren's cryptic remark. Petra's incurious incuriousness that was perhaps not incuriosity at all. Tomas, who owed him nothing, who had defended betrayal to his face over the chessboard and yet whose hands shook when the copy room door closed behind him. And Maren.

Maren, who had shown him the second ledger in the first weeks, deliberately, watching his face as she did it, testing whether he would look away. Who had whispered to him through the storm nights that she wanted an outsider to end what insiders could not. Who now passed his door each evening on her way to the dormitory and knocked once, softly, a signal they had agreed meant *nothing tonight* or *all clear* or simply *I remember you exist*—the grammar of conspirators is thin, and grows thinner under strain.

Tonight the knock came, and then her voice, pitched for a distance of inches: "He asked about you at dinner. The buyer. Asked your name."

"What did she say?"

"A courier. Disowned. She showed him the letter of disavowal she intercepted from your handler's office before the pass closed." A pause. "It's genuine, Vesper. Your republic wrote you off in autumn. He laughed. He said disowned couriers make the best sellers, because they have nothing to go back to."

The cold that went through Vesper then was not the room's cold. He sat on the edge of the cot in the dark and saw it whole: the machinery, fully assembled at last. Ilse would offer him to the buyer as merchandise or as proof of cooperation. The buyer would find a use for a man trained to be invisible—a man who could carry anything across any border because no one would ever claim him. They were not going to kill him. They were going to *keep* him, the way the republic had kept him: useful, deniable, owned. His invisibility, which he had worn for eleven years like a second skin, was about to be confiscated too, and put to someone else's purpose.

"Maren." He kept his voice below the wind that had started worrying the eaves. "When the bridge opens. What happens to you?"

Silence for a moment. Then: "I run the ledger. Ledgers survive wars. That's what Tomas says." Another silence, longer, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "The forged sheets. Ferren's right—the old man's hand is failing. If the swap is going to happen, it has to happen soon. Before the buyer demands a final inspection. Before the thaw takes the choice away from everyone."

"The thaw takes nothing," Vesper said. "It just shows us what was already decided."

He heard her breath catch, or imagined it. Then her footsteps receded down the corridor, quick and light, and the bolt slid home outside his door with its soft oiled finality.

* * *

He slept badly and woke before dawn to the loudest report yet from the river—a crack like the sky splitting, followed by a deep resonant tolling, as if the whole frozen length of the valley had been struck like a bell. He went to the window that faced the drop and, leaning, could see past the cliff's shoulder to the gorge below, where a seam of black water had opened along the river's spine, steaming faintly in the freezing air. A wound in the winter. Through it he could see the current running fast and dark, impatient, carrying the season away downstream toward Sereval, toward the bridge, toward everything waiting on the other side.

Days now. Not weeks. He counted them the way he had once counted them on the road, coins again, but he found his hands were steady in a way they had not been since autumn. Fear was still there—it lived in him now like the bolt on his door, permanent, greased, ready—but around it something harder had set. Purpose does not remove fear; it gives fear a schedule.

Because Tomas had come to him the previous evening. Had come to the bindery end of the east range where no ears were seasoned, had sat down heavily on Vesper's cot like a man setting down a load carried too far, and had said, without preamble:

"My hand is not what it was. But my master taught me a thing before the purges took him. He said a forger's last work should be his truest—that the only honest forgery is the one that undoes its own trade." The old man's eyes had been wet and furious and afraid. "I can do it, Joubert. In Ilse's hand, so that she'll know, when it's done, exactly whose hand it was. But once it's done there's no way back for any of us. Not for me. Not for the girl. Not for you, and you have the farthest to fall—you're supposed to be nothing, and after this you'll be something, and somethings get caught."

Vesper had looked at the old engraver's shaking hands and thought: *they shake less when they're working.* And he had said the only thing worth saying:

"Then we do it before the thaw. Before the buyer asks to see the sheets. We finish the forgeries while the ice still covers the river, and we swap them under it, and when the bridge opens the only thing that crosses is the truth."

Now, at the window, watching the black water widen by inches, hearing the ice give its dying speeches down in the gorge, Visper pressed his palm flat against his coat, against the paper sleeping over his heart, and made his peace with the arithmetic. Days remained. Perhaps five. Perhaps six.

Somewhere below, in the copy room with its double-locked door, the true cipher waited in its press. Somewhere across the courtyard, Ilse was writing letters in a hand Tomas knew better than his own. And somewhere in the dormitory, a girl who kept the ledger was lying awake, doing the same countdown, arriving at the same number.

The ice boomed again, long and final, a sound like a door opening at the bottom of the world.

Three mornings later, at first bell, Vesper opened his eyes and knew immediately that something had changed. The corridor outside his bolted door was loud with footsteps, voices raised not in alarm but in ceremony. He heard Petra calling for hot water, Ferren swearing cheerfully at a stuck shutter, and above it all Ilse's voice, clear and bright as the thawing air, giving orders with the pleasure of a general whose campaign has finally arrived:

"Set the good plates in the small dining room. The green glass. Tell the kitchen the gentleman will dine formally tonight—he's received word his people have reached Sereval." A pause, and then, lighter, almost gay, the sentence that landed in Vesper's chest like ice cracking underfoot:

"And tell Tomas I'll want the finished sheets for inspection. Day after tomorrow. Before the gentleman's breakfast."
