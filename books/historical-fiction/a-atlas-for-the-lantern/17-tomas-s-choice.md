## 17. Tomas's Choice

The burin had been in Tomas's hand for fifty-one years. He knew its weight the way a priest knows his rosary—not by thought but by the grooves worn into the handle, the particular way it settled against the pad of his thumb when the light was right. His father had given it to him in another country, in another century almost, back when engraving was still called a trade and not a crime.

Now he sat before a sheet of Ilse Halvard's cipher paper, a blank rectangle of the finest rag stock, and he could not make his hand move.

* * *

He had told Vesper yes. That was the trouble with old men's promises—they came out of the mouth too easily, riding on forty years of grievance like a boat on a familiar current. *I will do it,* he had said, standing in the drying loft among the hanging maps, and he had meant it, or meant something adjacent to it, the way a man means a thing at night that looks different by morning.

By morning it looked like this: a lamp, a graver, a blank sheet, and the knowledge that everything he touched from this hour forward would be either treason or salvation, depending entirely on who won the war that was already over.

Tomas had been forging hands since he was nineteen. That was the secret shame of the workshop, the thing no buyer ever said aloud but every buyer understood: Halvard maps were trusted not only because they were accurate but because they were *signed*, and the signatures were perfect, and the signatures were lies. Ilse's father had taught him the trick of it—how to study a master's letterforms until they lived in your wrist, how to cut a capital H so that it trembled in exactly the way the old man's hand had trembled after his second glass of plum brandy. Forgery, properly done, was not imitation. It was sympathy. You had to become the other hand. You had to feel what it felt, hurry where it hurried, hesitate where its arthritis caught it on cold mornings.

For decades this had been the workshop's quiet genius, and Tomas had been its quiet instrument. He had forged border treaties onto maps that moved rivers. He had authenticated survey sheets for men who never walked the ground they claimed. Each forgery had been small, defensible, paid for in coin that fed apprentices through winters. Complicity, he had learned, did not arrive as a single decision. It arrived as a thousand small permissions, each one reasonable, each one followed by another, until the man you had been was somewhere far behind you on a road you could no longer see.

And now the skill itself had turned around and looked at him.

* * *

Vesper had brought him the original sheets two nights past, wrapped in oilcloth, carried the way the courier carried everything—as though the object weighed nothing and the carrying weighed everything. They had spread them on the bench under the north window, where the light was honest even in winter, and Tomas had bent close and read the hand.

It was Ilse's. Of course it was. The cipher sheets bore her mark—the small crossed keys she used for diplomatic work, the precise italic numerals she had learned from her father and improved upon, the way she broke her serifs at a slight upward angle, impatient, always impatient, even in ink. Tomas had watched that hand form for thirty years. He had inked plates beside it. He had held her fingers steady once, when she was young and grieving and learning to engrave through tears, teaching her how pressure must remain constant even when the heart would not.

He could forge it in his sleep. That was the horror of it. Somewhere in his wrist lived an entire copy of Ilse Halvard, complete and faithful, and all these years it had served her, and now he was being asked to turn it against her, and the wrist did not care about loyalty. The wrist only cared about truth of line.

"You're certain," Vesper had said, and it wasn't a question so much as a door held open.

"No," Tomas had answered honestly. "But certainty is for the young and the dead."

* * *

Now, alone in the engraving room with the banked stove ticking, he made himself do the thing he had avoided doing for two days: he made himself imagine the morning after.

Not the swap—that part had a shape he could hold, a plan with edges. He imagined instead the moment Ilse understood. Because she would understand. That was the price no one had spoken aloud, the figure beneath the figure. Maren might escape suspicion; she was young, and youth is legible as error. Vesper was already condemned in her ledger; a condemned man risks nothing further. But Tomas—Tomas was the one person in the house whose hand could have produced those sheets, and Ilse knew it, and worse than knowing it, she would *recognize* it. She would hold the forged cipher up to the lantern and she would see not a counterfeit but a signature, the way a mother knows a child's footsteps on a stair. She would know it was him the way he had known, all his life, which apprentice had been crying, which buyer had lied, which plate had a flaw no one else could feel.

She would know, and knowing, she would understand what it meant: that the last craftsman of the old school, the keeper of her father's methods, the man who had taught her to keep pressure constant—had chosen.

There was no version of this in which he survived her forgiveness, because there would be no forgiveness. There would be something quieter and more final. A word to the buyer. A rearrangement of duties. An accident in the press room, where accidents had a long and respectable history. Or simply the door, in spring, and his name struck from the workshop rolls, and fifty years erased with a pen stroke—which, he admitted, staring at the blank sheet, might be the gentlest of the options, and also the one he feared most, because it required nothing of him but continued existence, and continued existence was the one thing he had never been asked to risk.

That was the true arithmetic of it, and he made himself look at it plainly, the way he looked at a plate under the loupe: redemption priced in full meant paying in the only currency he actually possessed. Not death—he was too old to romanticize death, had buried too many friends to mistake it for a statement. Paying in usefulness. Paying in belonging. The old engraver would become, in the space of one evening's work, a man without a trade, because a forger whose forgeries are known is no forger at all, and a loyalist whose loyalty is discovered is no longer safe anywhere, and a craftsman cast out of his craft at seventy-one has nothing left but the waiting.

He got up and put another log on the stove, though the fire did not need it. His hands wanted occupation.

* * *

It was while the log caught that he remembered his father's face, and understood, finally, why he had hesitated.

Not fear of Ilse. Not fear of the buyer, nor of the republic falling, nor of any of the large historical weather that had battered them all for years. He had hesitated because the last time he had used his gift cleanly, without permission asked or given, had been for Ilse's father, old Aldric Halvard, in the year of the purges, when the family had come apart like wet paper and the daughter had gone silent for a season, and the old man had sat in this same room and said, *Tomas, make me something true.* And Tomas had engraved him a map—not for sale, not for any faction, just a map of the valley as it had been in the old man's boyhood, the river where it used to run, the orchards, the mill that burned—a map of things that no longer existed, rendered with total fidelity, a lie in the service of memory, which is perhaps the only honorable lie a mapmaker tells.

Aldric had wept over it. Then he had died, and the purges had eaten the rest, and Ilse had come back from her grief changed, hardened into neutrality, neutrality thickening into commerce, commerce calcifying into the double ledger—and Tomas had watched it happen the way one watches ice form on a window, gradually, then all at once, and had told himself each stage was natural, was weather, was nothing a servant could oppose.

His father had told him once, in the old country, holding out the burin: *A tool is innocent. The hand is not. Whatever you engrave, you will have engraved.*

He picked up the burin.

* * *

He worked through the hours before dawn, and the work was terrible and beautiful, as the best work always is.

He began not with the cipher but with waste paper, warming the hand, running through the letterforms like a singer running scales. *H* with its impatient serif-break. The crossed keys, drawn small, drawn twice, because Ilse always drew the mark twice—once lightly to place it, once firmly to commit—and Tomas had forgotten he knew that until his own hand did it without instruction. Sympathy. Becoming the other hand. Thirty years of watching had deposited themselves in his muscles, and now he drew upon the deposit like a man drawing poison from a well.

The difficulty was not accuracy. Accuracy was the easy part; he could match her line weight to within a hair's tolerance, could reproduce the slight rightward lean of her numerals, the way her fives closed with a small flat stroke rather than a curve. The difficulty was *error*. A perfect forgery convicts itself. Ilse's hand, especially in recent years, especially late at night, especially when she worked under the pressure of buyers and deadlines and the slow collapse of everything east of the pass—her hand made mistakes. Her threes went wobbly when she was tired. Her horizontal rules drifted a degree off true, because she'd injured her right shoulder in the fire year and never fully recovered the reach. A flawless Halvard cipher sheet would announce itself to Ilse the way a stranger's voice announces itself in a dark house.

So Tomas had to forge not her best but her tired. He had to sit with his own body and find the tiredness in it, borrow it, translate it into her idiom. He let the lamp burn low so his eyes strained as hers would strain. He worked hunched, though his back protested, to bring the drift of the shoulder into his arm. He made the third sheet's threes wobble. He let one rule drift. He caught himself enjoying it—the puzzle of it, the deep craft pleasure of a problem worthy of his last good years—and the enjoyment frightened him more than the danger did, because it reminded him how easily delight had always led him across the lines he'd sworn not to cross.

Near the coldest hour, Maren came down with tea, moving quietly, saying nothing, setting the cup at the edge of the bench where it wouldn't drip near the paper. She stood a while watching the sheets dry, and her face in the lamplight was the face of someone carrying something heavier than paper, and Tomas understood that she was thinking of the night of the swap, and what it would cost her, and that she had decided already, the way the young decide—completely, without the luxury of revision.

"Your hand is shaking," she said.

"It's supposed to shake. Hers shakes." He paused. "Though tonight it isn't only the forgery."

She looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she saw made her reach out and touch the back of his wrist, briefly, the way one steadies a plumb line. "Ilse will know it was you."

"I know."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"The tool is innocent," Tomas said. "I'm trying to give the hand one clean thing before it's finished."

Maren nodded slowly, and left the tea, and went back up the stairs without sound, and Tomas understood that he had just been witnessed, and that being witnessed was part of the price too. A sin committed alone can still be renounced. A sin committed before a witness belongs to you forever.

* * *

Dawn found the forge-light strengthening against the frosted panes, and four sheets drying on the rack, and Tomas standing before them with ink to his elbows and the burin resting in its groove of worn wood, feeling like a man who has set fire to his own house to warm a stranger.

They were good. He examined them with the mercilessness he had spent a lifetime applying to apprentices' proofs, and they were good—tired where she would be tired, confident where she would be confident, bearing the crossed keys drawn twice, the drifting rule, the wobble in the threes. If he had been shown them blind, he thought, he would have believed them. More: he would have believed them *hers*, would have felt the presence of the woman in them, her impatience, her grief, her terrible competence. He had made ghosts of her and set them loose on paper, and the ghosts would go out into the world carrying worthless numbers to men who killed for numbers, and the true message would travel the other way, sewn into a courier's coat, toward a republic too far gone to be saved by any message—but saved or not, it would arrive, it would be *said*, and saying it would be somebody's act of fidelity in a season that had forgotten the word.

He cleaned the burin. He put it away in its case, and then, after a moment, took it out again and laid it on the bench, uncovered, where anyone entering the room would see it first. Let it be evidence. Let it testify.

Then he heard the stair, and Ilse's voice above, calling down the ordinary morning words—*the buyer wants proofs by noon*—and Tomas stood very still in the room full of his own treachery, listening to her footsteps cross the floor overhead, directly above him, and understood that the clock had started, and that from this hour forward every glance she gave him would be an examination, and every kindness a question, and the only way out was through the night of the swap and whatever waited on its far side.

He blew out the lamp. The daylight was enough. It was, he thought, the first honest light the room had seen in years, and he turned his face toward it like an old plant toward a window, and waited for noon.
