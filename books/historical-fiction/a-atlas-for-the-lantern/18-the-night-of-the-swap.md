## 18. The Night of the Swap

Maren had learned to measure her fear in small units. Not the whole night at once—that way lay paralysis—but in the space between one lantern passing a window and the next, in the time it took Ilse's boots to cross from the stair to the map room and back. Fear in inches. It was survivable that way.

The plan lived in three heads and nowhere on paper. Vesper had been clear about that: nothing written, nothing spoken above a whisper, nothing that could survive a search. Tomas had made his forgeries over six nights with the door barred and shavings burned as they fell, and now the false sheets lay wrapped in oilcloth inside the cold stove of the copy room, waiting to become true by simple exchange. All that remained was the doing of it, and the doing belonged to tonight, because the buyer—Colonel Amsel of the eastern delegation, though no one believed that was his name or his nation—had announced at supper that he would inspect the work personally before dawn. He was leaving early. Ahead of the thaw. Ahead, perhaps, of anyone's chance to stop him.

"He'll look at every sheet," Vesper had said in the dark of the engraving loft, his voice pitched under the sound of the wind. "He saw the originals once, in Ilse's office, when he thought we weren't watching. He has the eye. If he catches one false line, it's over—all of us, all at once."

"Then he won't catch one," Tomas said, and did not explain, because there was nothing to explain. Either his hand was faithful enough or it was not. Faith had never entered into it.

What Maren contributed she had not told them fully, because they would have refused. She knew the house. She knew its sounds the way a musician knows an instrument—which floorboard sang in the corridor outside the copy room, which hinge on the press-room door had gone dry in November and had never been oiled because Ilse liked hearing who came and went. And she knew where the house kept its interruptions: the fuse box in the old wing, the gas lines for the lanterns, the great drying kiln beneath the paper store that had once, two winters ago, scorched a whole ream of finished sheets and filled the upper floors with smoke enough to empty the building in ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds. That was what she would give them. Ninety seconds of smoke and shouting and running feet, while Vesper knelt inside the press cabinet and changed the truth for a lie.

* * *

The evening came down white and windless, the worst kind—the kind where every sound carried. Supper was laid late and cleared slowly; Amsel ate little and drank less, and watched the apprentices with the flat patient attention of a man pricing horses. Maren felt his gaze pass over her twice, light as a fly, and kept her eyes on the plates. Clearing dishes gave a person reasons to move, to turn her back, to be furniture. Furniture was safe. Furniture had been her armor for years, and she hated how well it still fit.

Ilse sat at the head of the table and performed serenity the way she performed everything else—beautifully. She spoke of the thaw, of the bridge, of the buyer's long journey home. Her voice never wavered. But Maren had seen her at noon, standing at the window of the map room with the second ledger open in her hands, not reading it, only holding it closed very tightly, the way one holds shut a door against a draft—or against something trying to get out. Ilse suspected something. Maren could not say what or how much. Perhaps nothing more than the general suspicion that falls on a woman who has spent twenty years buying loyalty and knows exactly what loyalty weighs.

At eleven the household banked its fires. At midnight, by the workshop clock, whose slow tick Maren could feel through the joists of her attic room like a second pulse, she put on her coat.

She had left the window latch oiled days ago, one small kindness to herself among so many calculations. The snow took her weight without complaint. Below her, the yard lay blue under the moon, crossed by the long shadows of the drying racks, and beyond the wall the river muttered in its channel of ice, groaning now at longer intervals, like a large animal turning in sleep. The thaw was coming. Everything was coming. There was only tonight to get through first.

Vesper's signal was a candle in the third window of the south range, lit for ten counts and shuttered. She counted against her heartbeat, which was faster than any honest clock. Then she moved along the wall to the old wing, let herself in through the paper store, and stood a moment in the dark, letting her eyes adjust, letting her fear arrange itself into its small survivable units.

The kiln room smelled of char and linseed. The fuse box was where it had always been, behind the shelf of ruined plates, a black iron cabinet with a porcelain heart, and beside it the valve that fed the kiln's burners. The plan was simple almost past believing: starve the flame, flood the pipe, let the accumulated gas find the dying embers of the kiln bed. It would not be an explosion. It would be a soft whump, a cough of smoke up the flue, and the flue served the chimneys of the whole south range. Every alarm bell in the house would speak at once.

Her hands were steadier than she expected. That frightened her more than shaking would have—how easily the body learns treachery, how naturally it fits the shape of the thing you've sworn you are not. She thought of her father then, unexpectedly, a printer's devil in a city she would not see again, who had taught her that ink comes off but stains stay. She closed the valve until it whispered. She waited, counting, counting, listening to the gas breathe out into the dead kiln like a held breath being taken.

Then she cut the fuse, and ran.

* * *

In the copy room, Vesper heard the whump come up through the floor like the house clearing its throat.

He had been kneeling in the dark between the press and the cabinet for an hour already, the true cipher sheets against his chest inside his shirt, warm as a living thing, the forgeries loose in their oilcloth at his knee. Ninety seconds, Maren had promised. He had not argued. Arguing would have meant admitting what it would cost her to buy those seconds, and he could not afford to think about cost tonight. Cost was a currency he would settle later, if there was a later.

The alarm bells began—first the yard bell, then the hall, then old Pieter's voice cracking across the courtyard, *fire, fire in the old wing.* Doors slammed. Boots rained down the stairs. In the corridor outside the copy room someone ran past at full speed, and the singing floorboard sang, and Vesper counted to five and slipped out into the empty hallway.

The house emptied itself toward the smoke the way water finds a hole. He crossed the map room in four strides, past the great tables where the republic's rivers and mountains lay pinned under lamp glass, and knelt at the flat cabinet where the cipher work slept under lock. Tomas's key turned without sound—he had cut it weeks ago from a wax impression, another small treason filed smooth—and the drawer rolled open on its runners.

The sheets lay in order, numbered in Ilse's neat gray hand. Vesper lifted them out in a single block, laid the forgeries in their place, and aligned the edges with fingers that would not hurry. Hurry was the enemy. Hurry was the one witness that could not be bribed. He checked the numbering—Tomas had matched even the slant of the numerals, the tiny hesitation in the sevens—and he checked the watermark by touch alone, tilting each page against the moonlight through the shutter slit, feeling the raised ghost of the bridge stamp rise and fall. Identical. Or near enough that only a man looking for falsehood would ever find it.

And Amsel would be looking for falsehood. That was the arithmetic Vesper could not make friendly. Everything depended now on the difference between a man glancing and a man reading, and he had no control over which kind of man came up those stairs when the smoke settled.

He locked the cabinet. He buttoned the true sheets inside his shirt, where they lay over his heart like a second, colder heart. And he went out to fight the fire.

* * *

They fought it with buckets and wet blankets and the strange calm of people who have rehearsed disaster without admitting it. The kiln had flared and choked itself; the damage was smoke, mostly, and a scorched lintel, and the paper store saved by Pieter's fast thinking with the sand barrels. By the time the bells went silent, the household stood in the yard coughing and counting itself, and the sky to the east had gone the color of pewter, which meant dawn was less than an hour off, which meant Amsel's inspection would happen now, this morning, before the adrenaline had drained out of anyone's blood.

It was Ilse who spotted her.

Maren had meant to be in her room. She had meant to come down with the others, wide-eyed, asking questions. But the gas had caught sooner than she calculated—a miscalculation, her one miscalculation—and she had been crossing the yard when the flare lit the old wing windows orange, and she had done the stupid thing, the human thing: she had stopped to look. And in the crush at the sand barrels, pushing a heavy cask with the men, she had taken a falling hook-chain across the shoulder and gone down into the trampled snow, and by the time hands found her the fire was dying and everyone was looking anywhere else.

Ilse looked at her. That was all. Looked at her lying in the snow with her coat torn at the shoulder seam, and looked a moment too long, and said nothing, and the nothing was louder than all the bells.

"She was helping with the casks," Pieter said gruffly. "Chain came off its pulley."

"Did it," Ilse said. It was not a question, and it did not need to be, and Maren felt the ground tilt under the certainty that Ilse knew—not suspected, knew—and was choosing, for reasons Maren could not read, to file the knowledge rather than spend it. She was helped inside. Her shoulder was bound by the cook, who clucked over it and asked no questions either; in a house built on secrets, silence was a communal craft, passed down like an engraver's hand.

Amsel inspected the work at half past four, by lamplight, in the map room.

Maren was not supposed to be there. She was concussed and bandaged and should have been upstairs. But Vesper had been given the task of holding the lamp, and Tomas of unlocking the cabinet, and Ilse of presiding, and the tableau required witnesses—witnesses were the point; a sale conducted before witnesses could not be repudiated—and so Maren sat in the corner with her arm in a sling, ostensibly sorting proofs, actually watching the most important minutes of her life unspool across a table of numbered papers.

Amsel read the way winter comes: thoroughly, without haste, indifferent to what it covers. He lifted each sheet with two fingers. He tilted it to the lamp for the watermark. At the fifth sheet he paused, and the pause stretched, and Maren discovered that terror and triumph are not opposite states at all but a single braid, twisted tight around the spine; her heartbeat was a drumroll for both of them at once. Vesper held the lamp perfectly steady. Only his eyes moved, from Amsel's face to Ilse's and back, a spectator at his own execution hoping for a pardon.

"The seventh numeral," Amsel said at last.

Tomas did not breathe. Ilse said, smoothly, "Yes?"

"It hesitates." Amsel set the sheet down. "Here—" he touched the paper with one blunt finger, "—and here. On the originals, your master's sevens hesitate. I remember it. It is why I remember them."

The silence lasted three seconds. Later Maren would replay those three seconds many times, and each time they would be longer.

"They hesitate," Ilse agreed, "because Master Halvard's hand shook at the end of his life, and Tomas inherited the shake with the hand. It is why we keep him. Fidelity of imperfection, Colonel. Anyone can forge a clean line. Who can forge a tremble?"

Amsel looked at Tomas. Tomas, magnificent, ancient, guilty, spread his ruined hands on the table and said, "The tremble is honest, sir. God gives it. I merely preserve it."

Something moved at the corner of Amsel's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a different man. He gathered the sheets, squared them, and slid them into the leather case he had brought for the purpose, and the click of the buckle was the smallest sound in the room and the largest.

"The money transfers at the bridge," he said. "When it opens. You have done careful work." His eyes crossed the room, touched Maren in her sling, rested there a half-second too long, and moved on. "I trust the fire cost you nothing valuable."

"Only paper," Ilse said. "Paper grows back."

Afterward, when the case and the colonel and the lamps had gone, Tomas sat down heavily on a stool and put his face in his hands. Vesper stood by the window with the shutter cracked an inch, breathing the cold air, and did not look at anyone. The true cipher lay warm against Maren's own skin now—no; against Vesper's. Against his. She had sewn the new hiding seam into his coat lining herself, two nights ago, by candlelight, her good hand guiding the bad, and neither of them had spoken while she worked, because some stitches are a kind of vow and words would only cheapen them.

It was done. The lie had walked out the door wearing the truth's clothes, and the truth sat hidden over a beating heart, waiting for ice to break.

Maren got up to go to bed, because shaking legs are best hidden in motion, and at the door she met Ilse coming in. They stood a moment, close enough that Maren could smell the smoke still in her mistress's hair.

"You should not have been in the yard," Ilse said quietly.

"No, ma'am."

"And yet I find I am glad you were." Ilse's eyes were unreadable as a closed ledger. "A house that burns a little teaches you what it's made of. Go and sleep, child. The thaw is coming fast now, and after a thaw, everything melts."

She passed on down the corridor, and Maren stood listening to her footsteps fade, and understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, that the next fire would not be a diversion—and that when it came, it would be lit on purpose.
