## 1. The Weight of Sealed Paper

The snow came down on the ministry courtyard like ash from a fire no one would admit to having set.

Ansel Pemberton stood beneath the colonnade and watched it settle on the shoulders of the sentries, on the black lacquer of the state carriage, on the bronze eagle above the doors whose wings had been gilded once, in a year when there had been money for such things, and were now going green at the tips like something left too long in water. The cold found the gap between his collar and his jaw. He did not move to close it. A courier learned early that discomfort was a kind of discipline — that the body's small complaints could be stacked away like coins in a drawer, counted later, spent when there was nothing else.

Inside, the Ministry of Correspondence was dying by degrees, though no one had thought to post an obituary. Ansel climbed the stairs past the second-floor landing where three clerks' desks stood empty, their inkwells dry, their chairs pushed in with the neatness of men who had left in a hurry or been made to leave in one. Nobody spoke about the empty desks. That was the fashion of the season: not speaking about things. Not speaking about the deputies who did not attend sessions. Not speaking about the names struck from the payrolls. Not speaking about spring, when the purges had begun in the ministries before moving outward into the districts, the way frost moves through a wall — quietly, invisibly, until one morning the plaster is buckled and the whole structure is unsound.

On the fourth floor, in a room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, Undersecretary Halloran waited behind a desk bare except for a single document case and a lamp turned low, as if even light were being rationed now.

"Pemberton." Halloran did not look up. He was a man built like a ledger himself — narrow columns, ruled lines, everything entered twice. "You'll take the eastern road."

"The eastern road is closed at Grey Ford," Ansel said. "It's been closed since the first week of December."

"The eastern road is closed to smugglers and deserters," Halloran said. "It is open to the republic's business." He looked up then, and Ansel saw that he had aged since autumn — not in the face, which had always been old, but in the hands, which lay flat on the desk as though pressing something down that wanted to rise. "Sit."

Ansel sat. He kept his gloves on. It was that kind of cold, and besides, a courier did not remove anything in another man's office; you never knew what might be expected of your hands next.

Halloran drew the document case toward him but did not open it. For a long moment he only looked at Ansel, and Ansel understood that he was being measured — weighed against some standard that had changed without notice, the way standards do.

"Twenty-two years old," Halloran said. "Six years in service. Fourteen runs completed. No losses, no delays, no questions asked." He said *no questions asked* the way other men said *God willing*. "Do you know why I give you the difficult pouches, Pemberton?"

"Because I don't lose them."

"Because you don't *read* them." Halloran leaned back. "There are couriers who open seals. Curiosity. Fear. Sometimes both wearing each other's coats. You've never done it. In six years you have carried things that could unmake ministers, and you have carried them the way a mule carries grain — without tasting it. That is the rarest quality in this building. Rarer than honesty. Honesty we can manufacture. We have forms for it."

Ansel said nothing. There was a compliment buried in there somewhere, or perhaps an indictment; with Halloran the two often shared a wrapper.

"This will be the last run of the year," Halloran went on. "Possibly the last of the administration, though I'd thank you not to quote me." He opened the case at last.

What came out of it was not what Ansel expected. He had imagined the usual dispatch box — iron-bound, lockable, heavy with the dignity of its own secrecy. Instead Halloran lifted out a book. A great book, bound in oxblood leather gone dark at the corners, with brass fittings at the edges and two straps crossing the cover, each strap ending in a wax seal the size of a plum. One seal was the republic's — the eagle, the wreath, the seven stars. The other bore a device Ansel did not recognize: a compass rose, quartered, with a single star at its center.

"A ledger?" Ansel asked.

"An atlas." Halloran set it on the desk between them, and even the sound of it — dense, final, like a door closing in an empty house — seemed to argue for its importance. "Made by Elias Brandt. You may remember the name."

Ansel did remember it, distantly, the way one remembers a street from childhood: Elias Brandt, cartographer royal, whose maps hung in schoolrooms and ministry corridors alike, whose hand had drawn every border the republic claimed and several it merely hoped to claim someday. A name from the calm years. From before.

"He died in October," Halloran said. "You won't have heard. His death was not announced. Neither was the manner of it." He paused, and the lamp guttered, and neither of them moved to trim the wick. "This atlas was found among his effects. Sealed by his own instruction. It is addressed — insofar as it is addressed to anyone — to a party whose identity is indicated inside, under seal. The ministry has determined that the atlas must be conveyed east, to the delegation at Vellmark, where it can be... assessed. In context. By persons qualified."

All of which, Ansel thought, meant that no one in this building dared open it themselves, and no one dared destroy it either, because no one knew what destroying it might cost. So it would travel east, wrapped in other people's courage, until someone braver or more expendable could be made to bear it.

"And my instructions?"

"Carry it sealed. Deliver it sealed. If the seals are broken by any hand but yours at the point of delivery, note the fact in your report and continue regardless. If you are searched—" Halloran's mouth thinned. "You are a clerk traveling to inspect granary accounts. The book is your working papers. Bureaucrats are tedious, Pemberton. Be tedious. Tedious men cross borders."

He slid the atlas across the desk. Ansel took it.

It was heavier than it should have been. Not the weight of paper — though there must have been three hundred leaves in it, good rag stock, the kind that outlives its makers — but some other weight, denser, as though the thing had been packed with more than ink. He settled it against his forearm and felt the brass fittings bite cold through his glove. Somewhere in the binding, faintly, pages shifted, the way a sleeping animal shifts.

"You'll want the receipt," Halloran said, and wrote it out in his ruled, doubled hand: *One (1) atlas, sealed, conveyed to courier A. Pemberton, this 21st day of December, 1798.* He signed it, blotted it, and held it out — then did not release it when Ansel took hold.

For a moment they stood like that, joined by a slip of paper, and Halloran looked at him with an expression Ansel had never seen on him before. It took Ansel a moment to name it. Pity, perhaps. Or its cousin, warning.

"Pemberton. Do you know what's happening to the east?"

"There's unrest," Ansel said carefully. "The districts are — dissatisfied."

"Dissatisfied." Halloran released the receipt. "Yes. That's the word we use." He turned back to his papers, and the audience was plainly over. "The inn at Hessel Cross takes ministry vouchers. Travel by day. And Pemberton—"

Ansel paused at the door.

"—history is happening rather quickly just now. Try not to be interesting."

* * *

The city let him go reluctantly, the way a debtor lets go of a creditor's letter.

He walked the last mile to the east gate rather than take a cab, because the atlas rode better against his chest than in any vehicle, and because walking gave him time to perform the ritual he performed at the start of every run: the folding away of his own life. It was a small life, and folded small, it vanished entirely. He had a room on Tanner's Lane with a leak over the window; he had a landlady who would assume he had gone to visit his aunt in the provinces and would keep paying his coal account out of the deposit, because she was honest in the way of people who had never been tested; he had no family worth the word — a father dead of the fever, a mother remarried north and silent, a sister whose letters arrived twice a year like comets. None of it needed to come with him. That was the trick of the work. You became a man made entirely of errands, and errands had no homes to burn down.

At the gate the guards checked papers with a slowness that was itself a message. Beyond them the road ran white between hedgerows furred with snow, and the sky hung low and the color of pewter, and somewhere far off — east, he supposed, in the direction of all his trouble — a sound like thunder rolled across the plain, though there were no clouds heavy enough for thunder. Artillery, then. Or quarry blasting. He chose quarry blasting, the way one chooses a coat that fits.

That was the whole art of it, he told himself, settling the atlas into the oilskin satchel and drawing the strap across his body so it lay against his heart like a second, harder heartbeat. The art of surviving history was simply the art of mishearing it. Wars happened to other people. Treaties happened to other people. Purges happened to clerks on the second floor whose desks you stepped around, and you stepped around them because looking too long was how a man became *involved*, and involvement was the one contagion for which the ministry offered no inoculation.

He had carried dispatches announcing troop movements and never seen a troop. He had carried treaties and never met a signatory. He had once conveyed a pouch containing, by its weight and its consequences, an order of arrest for a minister whose portrait had hung in the corridor outside Halloran's office — and Ansel had delivered it to a way-station, taken his supper, slept soundly, and read in a newspaper a week later that the minister had died before trial, and had felt nothing sharper than the mild chill of seeing a familiar face in an obituary. History, for Ansel Pemberton, was a country he transited without a visa. Its wars were weather in distant counties. Its dead were names in columns, printed small.

The atlas bumped against his ribs with every stride, patient as a debt.

By dusk he had covered nine miles and the snow had thickened, and the lights of Hessel Cross appeared ahead like a handful of coins dropped in dark water. The inn was older than the republic — half-timbered, sagging eastward as though leaning toward its own future — and warm inside in the way of places where warmth is the principal product. Smoke, roasting meat, wet wool steaming by the stove. A dozen travelers, none of them looking at one another, which was the custom of the times: everyone studying everyone's boots.

Ansel took a corner table, ordered soup and whatever the tap offered, and arranged his persona around himself like blankets. Granary accounts. Eastern circuit. Tedious. He set the satchel between his feet, where it rested against his ankles, solid and secret and heavier than any bag of working papers had a right to be.

He was halfway through the soup when the door opened and let in a column of blue evening air and a woman carrying a ledger of her own.

She was perhaps thirty, dressed in the plain dark coat of a government functionary, with snow melting in the seams of her sleeves and a pair of spectacles pushed up into hair the color of wet slate. She stamped the snow from her boots with the thoroughness of a person who believed in doing small things correctly, crossed the room, and took the table nearest the stove. Then she opened her book, uncapped her pen, and began, without preamble and apparently without appetite, to work.

A census taker, by the badge on her coat. Ansel noted it and dismissed it, the way one notes weather. Census takers were harmless. They counted hearths and livestock and heads of households; they were the dullest instruments of a dull department. He finished his soup. He drank his wine. Around him the inn murmured its evening murmur, and outside the snow fell on the republic, on its closed roads and emptied desks and gilded eagles going green, falling equally on everything, asking nothing of anyone, promising nothing to anyone.

Then she rose, tucked her pen behind her ear, and began moving from table to table with her book, and the murmur of the inn changed key — a half-tone lower, the sound of men remembering to be careful.

She reached the farmer two tables down and asked him four questions. She reached the drover by the window and asked him five. Her voice carried without effort — clear, unhurried, precise, the voice of someone writing things down exactly as said and intending to keep them exactly as written. At the merchants' table there was laughter at some answer, and she did not laugh with them, but wrote, and moved on.

And then she was standing beside Ansel's table, and her pen was already uncapped, and her eyes — gray, level, lit from the side by the stove — were already taking in the satchel between his feet, the cut of his coat, the ink stain on his right cuff that no amount of granary-account fiction could quite explain.

"Name," she said.

"Pemberton. Ansel Pemberton. Clerk."

"Clerk of what office?"

"Provisions. Granary accounts." He produced the lie smoothly, well-worn, comfortable as an old glove. "Eastern circuit."

"Mm." She wrote. "Occupation of father?"

"Deceased. He was — a scrivener."

"Destination?"

"Vellmark. Inspections."

She wrote that too, and he watched the letters form under her pen — quick, upright, merciless — and felt, for the first time in six years of professional invisibility, the strange vertiginous sensation of being *entered into a record*. Of becoming, in someone else's hand, a fixed and retrievable fact.

She capped her questions with one more, casual as a knife laid flat on a table:

"And tell me, clerk — why does a man auditing granaries travel armed with two seals instead of one?"

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere behind the bar a cork came out of a bottle with a small, obscene pop, and Ansel Pemberton, who had never in six years been interesting, who had made a religion and a livelihood of being nobody, sat perfectly still while a stranger's gray eyes waited on his answer — and understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, that the road east had just begun counting him.
