## 2. One Question Too Many

The snow began an hour before the inn did.

Ansel Pemberton saw it first as a grey smudge among the greyer hills, a dark shape that resolved slowly into walls and a leaning chimney, smoke standing straight up from it in the windless cold. The sign above the door was a painted boar's head, half flaked to bone, and beneath it the words *The Boar's Rest* had been written and rewritten so many times by so many hands that the letters no longer agreed with one another about their alphabet.

Ansel stopped at the edge of the yard and stood a moment in the falling snow, letting the horse blow, letting the silence settle back over the road behind them. Empty, all of it. Twelve days out from the capital and not once had anyone looked twice at a thin clerk in a grey coat on a tired horse. That was the whole art of it — Ansel's art, the only art they had ever been asked to learn. Be dull. Be furniture. Be the kind of person a guard's eye slides past the way water slides past a stone.

Inside the coat, against the chest, the ledger-atlas rode in its oilcloth wrapping like a second heart. It had a weight out of proportion to its pages. Sometimes, when the wind gusted, Ansel imagined they could feel it shifting, turning over in its sleep.

*History is something that happens to other people.* Their father had said it; their father's superiors had believed it; the ministry had built its whole quiet architecture on the principle. Wars were for generals. Treaties were for men whose signatures were worth more than their word. Clerks carried paper, and paper, however heavy, was only paper.

Ansel dismounted, stamped feeling back into their boots, and pushed open the door of the Boar's Rest into a wall of warmth that smelled of tallow, wet wool, and onions fried in old fat.

* * *

Marguerite Voss had claimed the table nearest the fire at four o'clock precisely, because four o'clock was when travelers began to believe the day's driving was done and the ones with something to hide began to think about beds instead of roads. She had been sitting there for two winters now, in this district and the three adjoining, with her ledger, her pen, her tin badge of office no larger than a playing card, and she knew the rhythm of an inn the way a physician knows a pulse.

The Boar's Rest filled the way it always filled: carters first, loud and early; then a wool merchant who would overstate his takings by a third because he always did; then, near dusk, the solitary riders, and it was these she watched. Most solitary riders were exactly what they appeared — tired men, or frightened ones, or both. But fear had shapes, and Marguerite had learned to read them the way other people read weather.

She was on her second cup of small beer and her fourth page of entries when the door opened and the clerk came in.

That was her first note, made mentally before the stranger had crossed half the room: *clerk*. Everything about the figure said clerk — the grey coat cut for sitting rather than riding, the ink-stain ghosting the cuff of the right sleeve, the careful way the newcomer hung the wet cloak at a distance from the fire, as if heat itself might be a thing requiring permission. A soft face, youngish, unremarkable in every particular except that unremarkableness itself, worn with such diligence it became a kind of costume.

And yet. The boots were wrong. Riding boots, broken in hard, mended at the heel with soldier's stitching. And the newcomer chose a table with the wall at the back and the door and both windows in view, and did it without appearing to choose anything at all, the way a sleeper turns over.

Marguerite dipped her pen.

She worked through the carters first, then the wool merchant, whose takings she recorded accurately and without comment; accuracy was the whole of her religion, and she kept it privately, the way some women kept saints' medals sewn into their hems. Name, trade, origin, destination, purpose of travel. Five columns, neat as teeth. The law required it. What the law intended to *do* with twelve thousand names from three rural districts, no one had explained to her, and she had learned not to ask, because asking questions was how her brother had come to the attention of people who took notes of their own.

The clerk ordered soup and bread and ate standing the tray between himself and the room — herself, Marguerite corrected; the hands were fine and the jaw soft, a woman or someone very good at seeming one, and either way it went in the ledger as observed, not assumed — and ate the way travelers eat who have been eating alone for many days: quickly, mechanically, without pleasure.

Then Marguerite closed her ledger, squared it on her knee, and crossed the room with her badge already in her hand.

"District enumeration," she said pleasantly. "Travelers are required to be recorded. It takes two minutes."

* * *

Ansel had seen her watching. Of course Ansel had seen her watching; twelve days of being nobody gave a person a great deal of time to notice who declined to look away. The census taker was perhaps thirty, sharp-faced, with ink on her fingers and the particular stillness of someone whose work is done with the eyes. Her badge said what she was. Her posture said something else entirely, something patient and appraising, like an auditor at a market scale.

"Name," she said, pen ready.

"Pemberton." The lie sat where it always sat, smooth with use. "Ansel Pemberton."

"Trade?"

"Clerk. Ministry correspondence." Half-true, which was the best kind.

"Origin?"

"The capital."

"Destination?" The pen hovered.

"A relative. In the north." This was the answer Ansel had rehearsed on the first morning and had never yet needed, and it came out now with a strange taste, like coin held too long in the mouth.

Marguerite Voss wrote it all down in her quick upright hand, and Ansel watched her write it and felt the peculiar discomfort of watching one's own erasure performed so neatly. *Pemberton, clerk, capital, north.* Four words, and the person they described had never existed. Somewhere in a ministry archive a real Ansel Pemberton would eventually surface in some list, or wouldn't; by then the name on this page would have long since dissolved into the general grey porridge of the enumerated republic.

It should have felt safe. It had always felt safe.

"Two minutes," Marguerite said, "as promised," but she did not close the ledger. She turned it slightly, angling the last column toward the firelight, and looked at Ansel with the frank professional interest of a woman reading aloud from a page only she could see. "One more question. It's irregular, I'll admit, but the district office likes completeness."

Ansel waited.

"You're armed." It was not quite a question. "A knife under the left side of the coat, I'd guess, from the way you've been favoring that arm since you came in. Which is unusual for a clerk. Unusual enough that I'd have noted it and thought nothing more." She turned the page. "Except that when you signed the stable register downstairs — most travelers don't bother, but clerks generally do — you pressed a seal into the wax. That's also normal. Couriers carry seals." Her eyes came up. "But the register is a public book, Mr. Pemberton, and I read public books the way other people read scripture. There were two impressions in your wax. Two seals, overlapping. Nobody carries two seals unless the second seal belongs to something that outranks the first."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the room the wool merchant laughed at his own joke.

"And a clerk," Marguerite finished softly, "travels armed with two seals instead of one — why?"

* * *

Later, Ansel would not be able to say why the fear arrived so late. It should have flooded in at *two seals*, at the first syllable of it, cold as the yard outside. Instead there was a moment — brief, absurd, almost sweet — in which Ansel simply felt *seen*. Twelve days of being furniture. Twelve years, if it came to that. And here was a woman with a tin badge and ink-stained fingers who had looked straight through the grey coat and the dull face and the rehearsed lies as though they were windows with the shutters open, and what she had found inside was interesting enough to ask about.

Being seen felt, for one heartbeat, like warmth.

Then the heartbeat ended, and it felt like danger.

"I don't know what you mean," Ansel said, and heard how badly it landed, how late, the way a foot lands wrong on stairs in the dark.

"No," Marguerite agreed, not unkindly. "I don't expect you do." She wrote nothing. That was somehow worse than writing. She simply let the pen rest against the ledger's spine and studied Ansel the way she might study a household whose accounts didn't sum. "Here's my situation, Mr. Pemberton, since you've gone pale and I'm not a monster. I record things. Names, trades, roads. I have recorded eleven hundred and forty travelers this winter, and of those, perhaps thirty wore faces like yours — faces doing a job. Faces with stitching under them." She closed the ledger at last, and her voice dropped to something almost private. "I don't report faces. I report names, as given. What the office does with them after that is between the office and its conscience, if it has kept one."

"And if I told you nothing?"

"Then you'd be Mr. Pemberton, clerk, bound north to a relative, and you'd have soup cooling and a bed upstairs, and tomorrow we'd be strangers again." She rose, smoothing her skirt, and paused. "I'm not your enemy. I'm only a person who counts. But I'll tell you one thing for free, since you look like a man who has been counting his own footsteps too long to count anything else." She leaned down, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "Whatever you're carrying — and you're carrying something, you sit like a person carrying something — the roads ahead of you are worse than the roads behind. The district office received new instructions this week. New categories. Travelers are to be recorded by *category* now, not just by name." She straightened. "Category four is couriers of unregistered correspondence. I haven't used it yet."

She walked back to her table by the fire and reopened her ledger as though nothing had passed, and began, with terrible calm, to enter the evening's remaining arrivals.

* * *

Ansel did not eat the rest of the soup.

Upstairs, in the narrow room with its window rattling in a frame swollen with frost, Ansel sat on the edge of the bed and unlaced the coat and drew out the oilcloth bundle and held it on both knees like a sleeping animal.

*Category four.*

The words turned over and over. Not *courier* — the republic had ten thousand couriers, honest grey furniture like Ansel, and had never troubled itself about them beyond the routine seals and countersigns. *Couriers of unregistered correspondence.* Someone, somewhere in the machinery, had begun keeping lists of a different kind. Lists of people like Ansel. Which meant someone suspected that things were moving through the dark that shouldn't be. Which meant the pouch against Ansel's chest was no longer merely heavy paper. It had become evidence. It had become a *reason*.

Ansel thought of the ministry corridor where they had accepted it, the last mild afternoon of autumn, the Director sliding the bundle across the desk without ceremony, saying only: *Routine delivery, Pemberton. North. Before the passes close.* Routine. The Director had not looked at Ansel while saying it, which Ansel had taken at the time for weariness and understood now, sitting in a freezing room above a burning inn, for something much older and much sadder: the look of a man handing off a thing he did not wish to be holding.

Snow ticked against the window. Below, voices rose and fell; a chair scraped; the wool merchant laughed again at something, perhaps still at himself.

Ansel pressed a palm flat against the oilcloth. Beneath it, the leather cover of the atlas, and beneath that, whatever Elias Brandt had spent his last good years committing to paper. *Deliver it,* the old man had said, in another life, in peacetime, with the sun coming through his workroom windows and dust turning gold above the map-tables. *To whoever keeps the maps honest.* A promise made in calm weather, when it had cost nothing and meant everything and seemed, like all promises made in sunlight, safely abstract.

The trouble with history, Ansel thought, was that it insisted on introducing itself personally.

Downstairs, the outer door opened, and the cold came in with it — Ansel heard it in the sudden hush of the common room, in the way the voices dropped. Boots on the boards. More than one pair. The measured, unhurried tread of men who entered buildings expecting everyone inside to wait for them.

Through the floor came the sound of a chair being pushed back, and then Marguerite Voss's voice, level and clear, saying words Ansel could not distinguish but whose shape was unmistakable: a question being asked of soldiers, politely, formally, by someone whose pen was already moving across a page.

Ansel pulled the laces tight, wrapped the atlas back against their heart, and stood in the dark, listening.
