## 4. Roads Declared Enemy

The snow came down in the manner of official correspondence — steadily, impersonally, covering everything equally — and Ansel Pemberton walked through it thinking of nothing at all, which was a discipline he had practiced for eleven years and had nearly perfected.

It was the fourth day out of the capital and the second since the inn where the census woman had looked at him too closely. He had left before dawn, before the porridge, before her questions could resume their patient excavation. Two seals instead of one, she had asked him. He had given her an answer that was true and useless, and had carried it with him ever since like a stone in his boot — small, persistent, impossible to ignore for longer than an hour at a time.

The pouch rode against his chest under his coat, strapped where a heart would be if a heart were made of leather and paper and weighed four pounds. The ledger-atlas inside it was Elias Brandt's life's work, and Ansel had promised, years ago, in weather so mild it seemed to belong to another country, that he would deliver it to whoever kept the maps honest. Whoever that proved to be. Those had been Brandt's words, delivered with the sly precision of an old man who knew exactly how much trouble he was bequeathing.

Ansel had thought, then, that whoever would prove to be some functionary. Some office. A door he could knock on, a hand he could shake, a receipt he could file. Promises were easy when they resolved into paperwork.

The road descended toward Vellmark through stands of birch that held the snow the way clerks held documents — carefully, without warmth. Below him the town showed its roofs and its smoke, and at its center the ministry annex, a squat gray building that had been a granary before it was a government and would probably be a granary again after. That was the thing about republics, Ansel had learned: they moved into buildings the way travelers moved into inns, leaving no mark, expecting to be turned out.

He stamped his boots at the door. The guard there was a boy he did not know, which was itself a kind of news; Vellmark's garrison had been old men and farm boys for as long as Ansel had traveled this route, but this boy stood like the capital stood, shoulders squared against something coming.

"Name."

"Pemberton. Courier, third circuit. I'm expected."

The boy checked a list. Ansel watched him read and saw the moment the boy found the name and the moment after it, when something in the boy's face closed like a ledger being shut.

"You're expected," the boy said, and stepped aside. It was not the yes Ansel had asked for. He climbed the stairs anyway.

* * *

Deputy Minister Corvane Harrow had an office on the second floor with a window facing the granary yard, and he occupied it the way he occupied everything — thoroughly, and with visible resentment at its imperfections. He was a heavy man gone lighter over the past year, as though the republic's troubles were being drawn directly out of his flesh. When Ansel entered, Harrow did not look up from the papers before him, and Ansel stood, dripping, and waited, because waiting was also a discipline and Harrow liked to see it observed.

"Close the door."

Ansel closed it.

"You came by the north road." Not a question.

"The north road, yes, sir. Through Halben and the ford at—"

"I know the geography, Pemberton. I drew your circuit." Harrow looked up then, and his eyes were the worst thing in the room. They had the flatness of a man who had spent the autumn reading lists of names, and had stopped hearing them as names. "Did anyone stop you? Question you? Anyone at all?"

There was no reason to lie and every instinct to. Ansel felt the census woman's pen moving in his memory, recording. "A census taker. At the Crossed Antlers, two days out. Routine enumeration."

"What did she ask you?"

"Name, origin, business, destination. The usual columns."

"And?"

Harrow's voice had not risen. That was the terrible thing about it; it never rose. It simply continued, like water finding the low place.

"She observed that I carry two seals," Ansel said. "Most couriers carry one. She asked why."

The silence that followed had texture. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below, a cart unloaded barrels, each one landing with a soft wooden concussion, like a slow drum counting something.

"And what did you tell her?"

"That the second seal is ministerial and none of her concern." Ansel heard his own voice do what it always did: become smoother, more official, a surface. "She recorded my answers and wished me good roads."

Harrow leaned back. His chair complained. He picked up one of the papers from his desk — Ansel could not read it upside down, but he recognized the format, the dense justified columns, the little stamped crest at the head — and held it out.

"Then you will want to sit down," Harrow said, "before you read what everyone else in the republic has known for six days."

Ansel took the paper. He sat down. He read it.

It was a proscription list. He had seen proscription lists before, in the way that one sees weather reports from distant provinces — names, districts, offenses ranging from *correspondence with foreign agents* to *sentiments injurious to the state*. This one was headed by the seal of the Committee for the Conservation of Public Order, which had been founded last summer to protect things and had immediately begun destroying them, protection and destruction having proven, in practice, to be the same activity conducted at different speeds.

Halfway down the second column, in a neat clerk's hand, he found:

**BRANDT, ELIAS — deceased. Sedition (posthumous): cartography injurious to the state. All works seized or destroyed on discovery.**

And beneath it, in the same hand, the same ink:

**BRANDT, ILSE (m. Kessler) — daughter. At large. Enemy of the republic.**

**BRANDT-KESSLER issue, two minor children. Dependent status: enemy stock.**

The room did not change. That was the strangest part. The fire went on burning in the grate; the barrels landed below; Harrow watched him with those flat eyes; the snow fell on Vellmark with its customary impartiality. Ansel read the entry three times and understood that Ilse Brandt — Ilse Kessler now, married name, false name perhaps, *at large* — was the person to whom he had promised, in mild weather, to deliver her father's atlas. And that delivering it had become, on some day six days past while Ansel was walking innocently through the snow believing history happened elsewhere, an act of treason.

Not near-treason. Not ambiguity. Treason, specified, itemized, punishable.

"The old man died in the spring," Harrow said, into Ansel's silence. "You knew that."

"He wrote to me. April." Ansel's voice sounded far away to himself, a report filed by someone else. "He said the work was finished. He didn't say what the work was."

"No. He wouldn't." Harrow took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and for one moment he looked tired rather than terrible, human rather than institutional, and Ansel clung to that moment the way a drowning man clings to anything. "Pemberton, I am going to tell you what happens next, and you are going to find it very simple, because it is. You will surrender the pouch. Now. To me. It will go into the annex strongroom tonight and be burned tomorrow with the other recovered materials, in the yard, with witnesses. You will sign a receipt stating you surrendered it unopened — have you opened it?"

"No."

"Good. Unopened. Your signature here attests that the contents remain unknown to you, and that fact, properly witnessed, constitutes your entire protection. Do you understand? Your ignorance is going to save your life. Sign your ignorance and go home."

Ansel looked at the paper again. **Enemy stock**, they had written, of children. The clerk who wrote that line had excellent penmanship. Someone had trained that hand.

"Sir," he said, "the pouch carries diplomatic seals. Two of them. One is yours — this office's. The other—"

"—is dead," Harrow said. "The Foreign Section no longer exists. It was dissolved on the ninth. Its personnel are being enumerated." He let the word sit there a moment; Ansel felt it land. Enumerated. The census woman's word, repurposed, turned from counting into hunting. "Its functions have passed to the Committee. Which means that seal authorizes nothing anymore except inquiry into whoever presents it. You are carrying a piece of paper that has changed species, Pemberton. It was a passport. It is now bait."

* * *

They gave him a room in the annex — they always did, courier's lodging, narrow bed, a stove that worked if you understood its opinions — and Ansel sat on the bed with the pouch across his knees and did not sleep.

The stove ticked. The wind worked at the shutters. Down in the yard, late, he heard voices and the scrape of a sledge, and once, briefly, laughter — soldiers' laughter, ordinary, human, the sound of people who had eaten and were warm — and then quiet again.

He should give them the atlas. That was the whole of it; there was no version of the arithmetic that came out otherwise. Harrow had laid it out with the clarity of a man who had already written the sentence Ansel would die of if he refused. Surrender the pouch. Sign the receipt. Go home. *Home* — the word itself was a small mercy Harrow had offered, and Ansel turned it over in the dark and found it had a hollow sound, like a coin worn smooth. Home was a set of rooms in the capital that he slept in forty nights a year. Home was a profession. Home was a promise he had made to a dead man about a book that was now, officially, a weapon.

Loyalty said: surrender it. Loyalty had a long and respectable pedigree. Loyalty had fed him for eleven years, clothed him, given his wandering a shape and his silences a purpose. Loyalty said the republic, whatever it was becoming, was still the thing he had sworn to, and that a courier who opened his cargo had already taken the first step down a road with no returns, and that Elias Brandt was dead regardless, and Ilse Brandt was *at large*, which meant she had sense enough to hide, and that Ansel Pemberton could not save any of them by feeding himself into the fire after them.

Truth said — and truth, Ansel noticed, did not argue so much as simply stand there, in the corner of the dark room, the way Marguerite Voss had stood there with her pen poised, asking one question too many — truth said: *you have carried this thing for three hundred miles without opening it, and you told Harrow you hadn't, and both statements are true, and yet.*

And yet he knew. He had known since the night in Brandt's workshop, nine years ago, when the old man had shown him a single page — just one, and covered it again quickly, as though ashamed — and Ansel had seen that the map on it was not of any border he recognized. It had been of the city, the capital, district by district, and along its margins ran columns of figures in Brandt's cramped hand: sums, dates, names. Debts. The republic's promises, written down where promises are usually allowed to evaporate. Whose grain had been requisitioned and never paid for. Which families had been guaranteed passage and then refused it. Who was owed what, and by whom, forever, unless someone kept the record.

Brandt had mapped not the country but its conscience, and consciences, unlike borders, could not be renegotiated at treaty tables.

Ansel had not opened the ledger in all these years. He had told himself it was duty. Sitting on the narrow bed in the annex at Vellmark with treason cooling against his chest, he admitted the truer thing: he had not opened it because opening it would make it real, would make the promise real, would make him a participant in history instead of its errand boy. He had wanted, his whole life, for wars and treaties to be things that happened to other people. The sealed paper had been his instrument for achieving exactly that — a life spent carrying other people's consequences without ever having to hold one.

Now the consequences had his name on them either way. Harrow had made sure of that. If he surrendered the atlas, he surrendered it knowingly — he had read the proscription list; he knew what it was; the receipt's careful fiction of ignorance would be a fiction he signed, and fictions signed by couriers had a way of resurfacing, in tribunals, in bad winters, with the signer's name attached. And if he did not surrender it—

If he did not surrender it, then somewhere out there in the frozen provinces a woman named Ilse Kessler was hiding with two children and the surname *enemy stock*, and the only inheritance her father had left her was strapped to the chest of a man whose government wanted it burned.

The stove ticked. Toward the coldest hour, Ansel got up, dressed fully, and sat by the window with the pouch in his lap and his hands flat upon the leather, feeling for the first time in eleven years the actual weight of the thing he carried — not four pounds, he discovered, but considerably more.

He tried the argument one final time, giving loyalty its best case, because he was fair-minded even with himself. *The republic is ill,* he told it. *Not evil. Ill. Illness passes. Institutions survive their fevers if their servants keep faith through the fever. Burn the atlas and the fever breaks someday, and the maps can be drawn again by honest hands.*

But truth only had to say one thing, quietly, in Marguerite Voss's level voice, the pen already moving:

*And until it breaks, who counts the disappeared?*

Ansel sat until the window went gray. Below, in the yard, soldiers were building a pyre of confiscated papers in the snow, stacking them with the methodical care of men doing a job, and he watched them and understood that tomorrow morning he was expected to walk down those stairs and add the last honest map of the republic to that fire, and sign his name to having done so.

He looked at the second seal — the dead ministry's seal, the bait, the passport that had changed species — and he thought about roads. About how a road is only a direction until someone declares it closed; then it becomes a choice.

The knock came at dawn, two sharp raps, and the boy-guard's voice through the door, formal and slightly frightened: "Courier Pemberton. The deputy minister requests the pouch. He's waiting in the yard."

Ansel stood. The pouch went back against his chest, under his coat, where it had ridden three hundred miles, and his hands were steady, which surprised him.

"One moment," he called, in the smooth official voice that had never once told the whole truth. "I'll bring it down."

Then he picked up his pack, and began looking, very calmly, for the window at the back of the building that faced away from the yard.
