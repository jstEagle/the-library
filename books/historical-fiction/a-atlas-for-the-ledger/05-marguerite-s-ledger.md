## 5. Marguerite's Ledger

The census book had forty-one names in it by the time the snow began again, and Marguerite Voss hated every one of them for how easily they had given themselves up.

She sat in the corner of the inn's common room with the table pulled close, her ink bottle warming between her palms because the cold had thickened it to tar. The fire spat and settled. Somewhere above her a floorboard groaned under someone's restless weight — the clerk, she thought. The one with two seals.

She had been writing names into ledgers for six years. Names, ages, trades, parishes, the number of livestock, the number of children, the number of things a household could lose and still be counted as existing. She had learned early that people told you everything if you asked in the right order, with the right pen, at the right hour of a tired evening. A ledger was a kind of confession booth where the penitent didn't know they were kneeling.

Her brother had understood that too. Tomas had laughed about it once, turning her completed district book over in his big printer's hands. *You know what you're making, Marguerite? You're making a shopping list.* He'd said it lightly. He'd been wrong about almost nothing else in his life.

Now she dipped the pen and wrote, in her small upright hand: *Pemberton, Ansel. Clerk. Travelling on ministry business. Papers in order.*

The lie of that last line sat on the page like a fly in amber.

Because the papers were not in order. Not quite. She had seen enough papers in six years to read them the way a farrier reads a hoof — the flaws invisible to the owner, screaming to anyone who looked. The courier's travel writ bore the seal of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Perfectly genuine; she knew its watermark by heart, had copied its particulars into her own reports a hundred times this autumn. But folded behind it, glimpsed when he'd opened his coat to retrieve his papers, had been a second seal. Older. Wax the color of dried blood, pressed with a crest she did not recognize from any current ministry — and she recognized them all, because recognizing them was, as it happened, her actual work.

One seal meant a clerk. Two seals meant a courier carrying something worth more than his life, or hers.

*Why does a clerk travel armed with two seals?*

She had asked him that at the table, three nights ago now, or four — the days had gone soft-edged since the roads closed — and she had watched the question land on him like a stone dropped into still water. He hadn't flinched, exactly. Flinching was for amateurs. What he had done was worse: he had gone *careful*, the way a man goes careful when he steps onto ice he'd sworn was solid ground. And then he had smiled, and the smile had been very good, good enough to fool the innkeeper, good enough maybe to fool a tribunal. It had not fooled her. She had spent too many years watching faces decide whether to tell her the truth.

Marguerite closed the census book and pressed her thumb against the edge of the pages until they bit.

Under the floorboards of her old life there had been a second ledger. Not paper — memory, which is the only archive no one can burn. In it she kept the things she had written down and could never unwrite: the miller's daughter marked *dependent, female, age 16*, who had been taken in the spring sweeps. The widow Harsch, recorded with *two goats, one cow, sympathies unknown*. The Kessler household, entered in her neat hand as *brother abroad, correspondence suspected*, and emptied of its people before the month was out.

And beneath all of those, deeper than any of them, one entry she had never made in ink but carried anyway: *Voss, Tomas. Printer. Age 29. Taken the third week of April. No trial recorded. No grave assigned.*

The state had used her lists to find its enemies. Her brother had been found without needing a list — printers were easy, printers practically announced themselves — but the others, the miller's daughter, the Kesslers, they had been found through the patient, accurate, dutiful work of enumerators like her. Accuracy, she had learned in the worst way a lesson can be learned, is not a virtue. It is a tool. And tools do not care whose hand holds them.

The fire cracked. Across the room, Pemberton sat hunched over a cup of wine he wasn't drinking, and Marguerite studied him the way she studied everything: openly, methodically, under cover of paperwork.

He was younger than his composure suggested. Twenty-five, perhaps twenty-six, though he moved like a man who had decided long ago that history was a weather happening in some other country. She recognized that posture. It had been her own posture for years — the belief that if you kept your head down and your entries tidy, the century would pass over you like a cloud. She had believed it right up until the morning a black carriage stopped outside her office and two men with very clean boots asked her, very politely, to recite the contents of her own district book from memory.

They had wanted to know if she remembered recording any couriers.

That had been in October. Since then her instructions had changed shape without ever changing words. The Director of Enumeration — a soft-spoken man who smelled of church incense and had never once looked at her while speaking — had explained, over tea she did not drink, that the republic's roads were full of persons moving documents contrary to the public safety. That enumerators, being trusted observers present at every inn and crossing, were ideally positioned to note such persons. That a certain class of traveler — young, composed, travelling alone, bearing ministerial seals — might be noted in the margins of the census book using a simple notation: a small circle drawn beside the name.

*A small circle,* the Director had said, stirring his untouched tea. *Nothing dramatic. We are not asking you to arrest anyone, Mademoiselle Voss. Merely to count.*

Count. As though a name inside a circle were still just a name.

She had drawn three circles since October. She did not know what had happened to the three men inside them. She knew only that their names had left her book and entered a larger book, and that no one ever came back to ask follow-up questions, and that in her trade, silence from above was itself an answer.

And now here was a fourth, sitting across a dying fire with a blood-colored seal folded against his heart, and Marguerite Voss found that her pen would not move toward the margin.

She thought of Tomas again. She thought of him every hour of every day, but tonight the thought had teeth. Tomas, who had set type by lamplight and said that ink was the only honest army. Tomas, who had been arrested because a compositor in another town, frightened or ambitious or simply accurate, had written his name down somewhere it should not have gone. One name. One line in someone's ledger. And the machinery had done the rest, the way it always did, efficiently, without malice, the way a threshing floor separates grain from chaff without caring which is which.

She had been chaff once. She refused to be the thresher.

Marguerite rose, took her candle, and crossed the room.

* *

Pemberton watched her come with the wariness of a man who has already decided she is dangerous and is now calculating how dangerous. Good, she thought. Let him calculate. Accurate fear was more useful than comfortable trust.

"Mademoiselle Voss." He inclined his head. "More questions for the census? I warn you, I've told you everything true I own."

"That's precisely the problem," she said, and sat down across from him without being asked, setting her candle between them so their shadows leaned together on the wall like conspirators. "You've told me everything *true*. It's everything else I'm interested in."

His cup paused halfway to his mouth. To his credit, he finished the motion and drank, unhurried. "I don't follow."

"Then let me follow for both of us." She kept her voice low, level, the voice she used for reading numbers back to farmers — the voice that promised nothing except precision. "Your travel writ bears the seal of the Foreign Ministry. Genuine — I've handled forty of them this year. But yesterday, when you opened your coat for your papers, I saw a second seal. Old wax. Red. A crest I don't know, and I know all of them." She let that sit. "Clerks carry one seal, Monsieur Pemberton. Couriers carry two. So my question stands, the one I asked you at supper: why does a clerk travel armed like a diplomat?"

The fire chose that moment to collapse, a soft avalanche of embers, and in the sudden flare of light she watched him decide — watched the whole interior argument cross his face the way weather crosses a valley. Deny. Deflect. Dignity. She catalogued each option as it presented itself and waited past all of them, because she had learned that most men's lies had a lifespan of about ten seconds if you simply declined to rescue them.

"You saw that," he said finally. Not a denial. An inventory of damage.

"I see everything. It's my profession."

"And what do you do," he asked quietly, "with what you see?"

And there it was — the real question, underneath both their questions, hanging between the candles like breath in cold air. *What do you do with what you see.* She could lie. She was good at lying; she had learned recently that she was excellent at it, that six years of exactitude made falsehoods from her mouth strangely convincing. She could say she would write his name in her book and be done with it, and watch him bolt at dawn into the frozen hills where the patrols would find him inside two days.

Instead she reached into her satchel and brought out the census book — her own copy, the working draft — and turned to the page with his name.

*Pemberton, Ansel. Clerk. Travelling on ministry business. Papers in order.*

No circle in the margin. She turned the book so he could read it upside down, the way clerks read, and watched him understand what the absence of the mark meant — which required, first, that he understand the existence of the mark.

"There is a notation," she said. "Used this autumn. A small circle beside a traveler's name. Do you know it?"

Something went out of his face. Not color — something deeper, some assumption about the shape of the world. "No," he said, and then, honestly: "Yes. I've heard rumors. At the ministry they called it—" He stopped himself.

"The counting," Marguerite supplied. "They call it the counting. I know, because I was taught to do it in October, by a man who stirred tea he never drank and never once looked at me while telling me what my pen was for." She drew a slow breath. The next part was the part she had rehearsed for three days and still could not make easy. "I have drawn three circles. Three names. I don't know what became of them, and I want you to appreciate what it costs a person trained in accuracy to admit she doesn't *want* to know, because wanting not to know is the first dishonest thing I have ever done in this book." Her finger rested beside his name, in the empty margin. "This is the fourth. And I find I cannot draw it."

"Why?" His voice had gone careful again, but a different care now — the care of a man handling something live. "You don't know me. By your own accounting I'm a stranger carrying sealed paper through a country that shoots strangers carrying sealed paper. Turning me in is the safest act available to you. It buys goodwill. Maybe a transfer to a southern district. Maybe—" he gestured faintly at the burning inn, the snow, the whole collapsing apparatus of the republic— "maybe just another winter of being left alone."

"Yes," she agreed. "It does buy all of that." She closed the census book and laid her flat palm on its cover, the way one lays a hand on a coffin lid. "My brother was a printer. Tomas Voss. In April he was taken from his shop between the Tuesday edition and the Wednesday one. No trial. The tribunal records show a number, not a name. I wrote to three offices to ask which. Two never answered. The third answered with a form confirming that no further information was available to persons not directly concerned." She heard her own voice go dry and precise, the enumerator's voice, and was grimly grateful for it, because the alternative was the other voice, the one that wept into pillows in lodging houses across four districts. "Persons not directly concerned. My brother. I filled out the form correctly. I am very good at filling out forms correctly. It's the only skill the republic has ever praised in me."

Pemberton said nothing. He was watching her with an expression she distrusted and needed anyway: attention. Real attention, undivided, the kind nobody paid an enumerator because enumerators were furniture with pens.

"In May," she continued, "I understood something. They did not need to hunt Tomas. Someone wrote his name down. One line, one ledger, one accurate little entry — and the machine did the rest. And I sit in inns every night of my life making accurate little entries, and I told myself it was different, my work, my book, because I only record what *is*, and what is cannot hurt anyone." Her laugh came out with frost on it. "What is can hurt everyone. It's the only thing that ever has."

The innkeeper snored behind his counter. Wind worked at the shutters like a patient creditor. Above them, the unseen floorboard groaned again, and both of them glanced up, and in that shared glance — two professionals checking a sound — Marguerite felt the first unchosen thread of alliance tie itself.

"So here is my offer," she said. "Not friendship. I don't trust you, and you would be a fool to trust me, and trust between us would probably get us both killed faster than distrust would. This is arithmetic." She opened the book again to his page and held her pencil above the margin. "I do not draw the circle. Your name stays exactly as it is — a clerk, papers in order, boring beyond persecution. In exchange, you give me honesty. Not your secrets — I don't want your secrets, secrets are just lies wearing uniforms. Honesty. When I ask you a direct question, you either answer it truthfully or you say *I cannot answer that*, and both answers are acceptable, and anything else ends our arrangement and I become exactly what they trained me to be. Can you live inside those terms?"

For a long moment he looked at the pencil hovering over his name, and she saw him weigh it — really weigh it, the way he'd weigh a document — and something in his face shifted from calculation to something almost like relief, which surprised her. People usually found surveillance simpler than honesty. Surveillance required nothing of the soul.

"If I say 'I cannot answer,'" he said slowly, "will you accept it? Truly? Or will you spend the night inventing what I'm hiding?"

"I will spend the night inventing what you're hiding regardless," Marguerite said. "But I'll write down only what you actually say. That's the covenant. Between what we know and what we record, there must be a gap, and the gap must be *ours*." She realized, saying it, that she was describing something she had never articulated even to herself — the private territory every hunted person needs, the inch of darkness no ledger reaches. Tomas had died in the part of the world with no gaps left. "Do we have terms?"

"We have terms," Pemberton said.

She withdrew the pencil. Then, because accuracy was a discipline and she was disciplined even in treachery, she made one final entry in the day's column, aloud, so he could hear the terms performed: "Pemberton, Ansel. Clerk. Departure tomorrow, weather permitting. Nothing further to report."

"Nothing further to report," he repeated, and there was something almost like a smile at the edge of it, tired and disbelieving.

"Don't thank me." She closed the book and pinched out her candle, leaving them in the red glow of the embers. "Gratitude is a debt, and I don't collect debts, I count them. Just remember the covenant runs both directions. If soldiers come to this door asking after couriers — and they will, Monsieur Pemberton, sooner than you think, because the roads are closing and closed roads make everything that moves look guilty — then whatever story we tell, we tell it together, and neither of us improves upon it alone."

"Soldiers," he said. "Here?"

Marguerite gathered her satchel and stood. Through the frost-furred window, far down the white road, she could see the last light of evening failing — and on the road, or perhaps only against her own retinas, a smudge of movement that might have been wind-driven snow, or might have been riders keeping easy pace through the drifts.

"They were already asking at the toll bridge yesterday," she said. "About a courier traveling south with diplomatic seals." She slung the strap across her shoulder and met his eyes in the ember-light, and gave him the only kindness left in her inventory: the truth, delivered plainly, like a well-kept figure. "Sleep in your boots, clerk. And keep that second seal somewhere closer than your coat."
