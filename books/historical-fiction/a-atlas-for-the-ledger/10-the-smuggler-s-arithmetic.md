## 10. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

The smuggler's warehouse smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular sourness of river ice thawing against stone. Ferro had chosen it well — a salt store at the edge of the ford town, half-derelict, its windows bricked over generations ago so that the only light came in through gaps in the roof slates and fell in long, dusty blades across barrels that had not held anything legal in years.

Ansel sat on an upturned crate and tried not to look at the satchel on the table between them. It was difficult. The leather ledger inside it seemed to have weight beyond its pages, the way a sleeping animal has weight — a warmth, a presence, a thing one is aware of even when facing the other direction.

Ferro poured three cups of something from a clay jug without asking whether anyone wanted any. He was a compact man, grey at the temples, with hands like knotted rope and a face that suggested every expression had been negotiated out of it long ago as an unnecessary expense. He pushed one cup toward Ansel, one toward Marguerite, who stood near the door with her back to the wall and her eyes moving in the old census rhythm — exits, windows, the number of men visible, the number of shadows that might be men.

"You came to me," Ferro said. "That's worth remembering. I didn't come to you."

"We were told you cross the river," Ansel said.

"I cross many rivers." Ferro drank. "The question is what I carry, and for whom, and what it costs. The first two are my business. The third is yours." He set down his cup and looked at the satchel the way a butcher looks at a carcass before naming a price. "You have no money. I know this already, so don't insult us both by pretending. You have papers — worthless, or worse than worthless, since your warrants travel faster than your feet. What you have that is worth anything is on this table."

Ansel's hand moved before thought did — not to open the satchel but to cover it, palm flat on cold leather, like a man steadying himself against a wind he hadn't seen coming. "This isn't for sale."

"Everything is for sale," Ferro said, without heat. "Some things are just priced badly."

* * *

They had found him through a chain of whispers Ansel could no longer reconstruct: a baker who asked no questions, a woman who hung a red cloth from her window, a boy who led them through alleys where the snow lay undisturbed and the walls pressed close enough to touch on both sides. Marguerite had done most of the talking. She had a talent, Ansel had noticed, for speaking to people who did not wish to be spoken to — a courtesy, a patience, the manner of someone whose profession had been to ask and wait. The census taker's gift turned inside out: now she gathered names for no ministry at all.

Three days since the salt house. Three days since the man named Kovac had stepped out of the dark between them and the secret police with a boat-hook in his hands, and bought their escape with whatever a boat-hook buys, and not come back out of the alley. Ansel still heard it sometimes in the hour before waking — not a sound exactly, more the shape of a sound, the memory of violence arriving and then not being there to witness its end.

Marguerite had not spoken of it. Ansel had not spoken of it. Between them the silence had grown a texture, like frost on glass: beautiful if you didn't look too closely, and impossible to see through.

Now Ferro refilled his own cup and began, in the tone of a merchant displaying wares, to talk about the atlas.

"I know what it is," he said. "Don't look surprised. I knew before you reached the ford town, which tells you how careful you've been, which is to say: not very. A courier with no office left, a census woman with no district, traveling together and carrying nothing worth stealing except one leather case they never let out of arm's reach." He shrugged. "I sell passage across a river at war. Knowing what people carry is the whole of my trade."

"Then you know it's not money," Ansel said.

"I know it's better than money." Ferro leaned forward, and the light from the roof gaps caught the grey stubble along his jaw. "Let me tell you about gold, clerk. Gold is honest the way a hammer is honest. It buys what it buys, and then it's spent, and then it's gone, and everyone forgets what it bought. But a map—" he tapped the table with one scarred finger, once, like a judge's gavel—"a map of debts, a map of every promise a republic made and broke, with names attached, dates attached, sums attached—that is not a purchase. That is a lever. You put it under the right wall and you can move anything. Governments. Prices. Wars. Men like me spend our lives hauling small loads for small profits. That book is a load that hauls itself."

Ansel kept his voice level, though something in his chest had begun to move like water finding a crack. "You know what's in it."

"I know what people are willing to kill for. That's usually sufficient."

* * *

He should say no. The word was right there, simple as a nail. And yet Ansel sat in the cold warehouse doing arithmetic instead, and hated himself for it, and kept doing it.

Because here was the sum: two lives, weighed against one book. Here was the other column: safety, warmth, a crossing to a country where neither warrant nor history had yet learned their names — purchased not with coin they did not have but with a truth they had carried four hundred miles and bled for twice. Ferro would use the atlas, yes — squeeze payments out of frightened officials, sell secrets back to the men who'd broken the promises recorded in its pages. But it would *survive*. It would be used. Wasn't use better than loss? Wasn't a lever in a smuggler's hands better than a lever at the bottom of a frozen river with two corpses beside it?

And beneath the arithmetic, quieter, a third column Ansel did not want to read at all: *you never wanted any of this.* The old wish, the founding wish, rising one last time from wherever wishes go to die slowly. History had happened to other people for twenty-six years because Ansel Pemberton had made a religion of small offices and sealed paper — carry this, sign that, ask nothing, be the hinge and not the door. Sell the atlas and it stops being yours. Sell the truth and you are only a courier again, delivering danger into someone else's keeping, and walking away clean.

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it. Not that temptation wore a monstrous face, but that it sat across a barrel-table pouring bad wine, and spoke reasonably, and offered exactly what a tired person wanted most.

"You're calculating," Ferro observed. There was no mockery in it. If anything there was a kind of professional respect. "Good. The ones who refuse instantly are either saints or liars, and saints don't come to salt warehouses."

"What would you do with it?" Marguerite asked. She hadn't touched her cup. Her voice filled the space between the roof blades of light the way her silences always did — precisely, completely.

Ferro spread his hands. "Use it. Carefully. A page at a time. There are men on both banks who signed those promises and would pay to see certain entries disappear. I would let them pay. And while they paid, I would copy everything, because a secret sold is only worth selling once, but a secret *kept* earns forever." He looked at her, then at Ansel. "Is that what you wanted to hear? That I'm a vulture? Fine. I'm a vulture. Vultures keep the roads clean. Ask yourself what your republic was, if not a room full of respectable men feeding on the same corpse with better manners."

"The atlas isn't a corpse," Ansel said quietly.

"No?"

"It's a promise that hasn't finished being kept."

* * *

Silence, then, of the kind that has weight. Somewhere outside, the river groaned under its ice — a long, deep sound like a door in the earth opening and closing. Snow ticked against the bricked-over windows. Ansel became aware of how cold the warehouse was, how far the light had fallen since they arrived, how much of the day had gone into this single conversation.

Ferro drank again and set the cup down with the care of a man concluding a transaction he already understood.

"Here is my offer, stated plainly, because plainness costs me nothing and flattery costs me time. Passage for two. Papers good enough to pass you through the eastern districts. Safe lodging until the crossing, and my personal word — such as it is, and it is worth exactly what my reputation says it's worth — that you reach the far bank alive and unmolested by me or mine. In exchange: the atlas, entire, tonight."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then you walk out of here into a town where the secret police hang notices on the church doors, and one of those notices describes a tall courier with burned hands, and I will have done nothing to help them and nothing to hinder them, because that is what neutrality means." He said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "I am not your enemy, clerk. I am arithmetic."

Marguerite shifted by the door. Ansel read her the way he had learned to read her over these weeks of shared frost and shared flight: the slight narrowing that meant she was composing; the stillness of her hands that meant she had decided something and was waiting for the right moment to make it cost as little as possible. When she spoke, her voice was the census voice, mild and exact.

"You said you copy everything you sell."

"I said a great deal of things."

"You said a secret kept earns forever." She stepped forward, out of the wall's shadow, and laid her own hand flat on the table — not on the satchel, but near it, framing it. "So here is our arithmetic, Mr. Ferro. We will not sell you the atlas. But we may be persuaded to lend you its shadow. Copies. Pages selected by us, delivered after we cross, through means we control. You get leverage to begin your work; we keep the spine of the book. You profit slowly instead of all at once — which, I'm told, is what separates merchants from gamblers."

Ansel looked at her and felt the ground shift under everything. She was bargaining away promises Ansel alone had made, offering a thing she could not fully deliver, lying with the smoothness of a woman who had spent months recording truths and had discovered how much power lived in the opposite. He should have been afraid. Instead he felt something adjacent to awe.

Ferro was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years.

"A census woman," he said. "Of course. You count people for a living. You know exactly how much anything is worth." He turned to Ansel. "She negotiates. Do you decide?"

"That depends," Ansel said, "on what you want the pages *for*. And that answer decides whether we leave this warehouse as partners or as strangers."

"Strangers walk slower," Ferro agreed pleasantly. "Partners sleep under my roof tonight. Choose."

Outside, the light was failing altogether now, and the river spoke again beneath its ice, and Ansel put both hands around the cold clay cup and understood that whatever he answered next would follow him across the water and past it, into whatever century waited on the far bank.

But before he could speak, boots sounded in the alley outside — not one pair, several, moving with the unhurried coordination of men who believed the building already surrounded — and Ferro's face changed for the first time all evening, and Marguerite snuffed the candle with her fingers, and in the sudden dark the smuggler said, very quietly:

"Well. It seems someone else wants to do business with you, clerk. And they've brought soldiers."
