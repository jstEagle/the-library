## 11. Marguerite Counts the Fallen

The road into Sainte-Aurelle smelled of wet ash and old bread, and Marguerite knew before she saw a single house that something had gone wrong with her arithmetic.

She had counted this district once — in the spring, when the lilacs were still permitted to bloom along the fence lines and the republic still pretended its paperwork was a form of care. She had walked these lanes with her ledger under her arm and her pen behind her ear, knocking on doors with the flat, apologetic confidence of a woman who believed that to count a thing was to keep it safe. *How many souls reside at this address? Names, ages, trades. Any lodgers? Any kin away at service or at sea?* She had asked her questions gently. She had written small in the margins — *widow, deaf in left ear; boy of nine, limps but strong; keeps bees, will offer you honey if you praise them* — because she had believed that accuracy was a kind of devotion, and that a district recorded well was a district held in the state's memory like a coin held in a closed hand.

Now the closed hand had opened, and the coin was spent.

* * *

They had come into town on the back of Ferro's coal cart, wedged between sacks of salt and a crate of candle stubs, because Ferro refused to carry them openly and Ansel had agreed too quickly, as Ansel agreed to everything now, with the hollow obedience of a man who had lost his ministry and could not stop reporting to it. The cart left them at the crossroads north of the market square. Ferro chewed his unlit pipe and looked at Marguerite a long moment.

"Your district, isn't it?" he said. "You've got the face of a woman coming home to a funeral."

"I'm passing through," she said.

"Everyone's passing through." He tipped his hat with two fingers, an oddly courtly gesture from a man who smelled of tallow and gun grease. "Mind the church square. They've been posting papers there."

They had not needed to mind the church square. The papers found them anyway, because the papers were everywhere — nailed to doors, pasted over shop windows, fluttering from the pump handle like pale leaves out of season. Marguerite read the first one from ten paces away, because she knew the typeface, knew the wide official margins, knew the particular weight of ink the prefecture used.

*By order of the Committee for Public Reconciliation, the following households are declared suspect of harboring sentiments contrary to the unity of the republic—*

And then the names.

The Delacroix household. Rue des Tanneurs, number four. Four souls: Odile, widowed; her son Baptiste, nineteen; her daughters Camille and Sylvie, eleven and seven.

Marguerite had written those names herself. She remembered the afternoon — the smell of tanned leather and tallow from the yard behind the house, Odile's cracked hands, the way little Sylvie had hidden behind her mother's skirts and then peered out, curious about the woman with the book. *Four,* Marguerite had written, in her careful, upright hand. *One hearth. No lodgers. The boy works the tanning vats; the mother does piecework for the chandler.* She had added in the margin, because she could not help herself: *Family poor but proud. Do not offer charity; offer work.*

Someone had read that margin. Someone with a different understanding of what a margin was for.

The paper on the door of number four bore a red slash across the whole entry, and beneath the names, in a clerk's efficient hand: *Enumerated. Verified. Processed.*

Processed. The word sat in Marguerite's stomach like a swallowed stone.

* * *

Number four stood with its door off one hinge. Inside, the furniture had been taken apart with methodical violence — not looted, she understood at once, but searched, and the searching had been done by people who had read her notes. The floorboards near the hearth were pried up where she had recorded that Odile kept her savings. The chandlery account book lay open on the table, marked with a ribbon, exactly where her marginal note would have directed an interested reader to look.

There was no one home. There was no one home anywhere on the street, or it felt that way — faces at windows drawn back the instant she turned her head, shutters closing like eyelids, the whole lane holding its breath around her. She stood in the wrecked parlor of a family she had once counted so carefully, and the cold came up through the holes in the floor, and she understood with a clarity that felt like falling:

They had not needed informants. They had not needed spies, or patrols, or traitors. They had needed only her. Her patience, her thoroughness, her gentle marginalia. Every kindness she had written down had been a signpost. Every accurate number had been a door marked for opening. She had gone door to door through the spring with the best intentions a person could carry, and she had delivered each household, wrapped and labeled, to the exact people who would unmake them.

Ansel appeared in the doorway behind her. She heard him stop when he saw her face.

"Marguerite."

"She wrote me her accounts," Marguerite said. Her voice came out very level, which frightened her more than weeping would have. "Odile Delacroix. She showed me where she kept her money because she thought — she thought being counted meant being *known*. Being known meant being protected. That's what I told her. I told her the census was how the republic kept faith with its people." She picked up the chandlery ledger. The ribbon was prefecture blue. "I drew them a map, Ansel. Of every family on this street. With annotations."

"You couldn't have known—"

"No." She turned the word over, examined it, found it rotten. "No is what I'd have written if you'd asked me yesterday. But knowing isn't the question anymore. The question is what my hands did while I wasn't looking." She set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might go off. "I used to think accuracy was a virtue. I used to think a lie in a ledger was the only sin a clerk could commit."

* * *

They found the church register in the square, and it was worse than the doors, because the church register was her own work returned to her transformed.

The parish had kept its own rolls, of course — baptisms, marriages, burials, generations deep — and last spring the prefecture had demanded copies reconciled against the civil census. Marguerite had done that reconciliation herself, three nights of cross-checking at the district office, matching the curé's spidery Latin against her clean columns, noting discrepancies. *Pierre Aubry, baptized 1761, listed by family as born 1763 — discrepancy of two years, likely to defer conscription of elder son.* She had noted it without malice. She had noted it because a discrepancy unnoted was a flaw in the record, and flaws offended her the way loose threads offend weavers.

The new posting on the church door listed Pierre Aubry among men liable for fraudulent documentation of age. It listed four others whose discrepancies she had caught. It listed them with dates and parishes, in an order she recognized — her order, her sequence, the path her own feet had walked through the registers, first page to last.

"They didn't even reorganize it," she whispered. "They just... followed me."

Ansel stood beside her with his collar up against the wind, and she could feel him trying to assemble comfort out of words and failing, and she loved him a little for failing, because anything he managed to say would have been made of the same paper as the postings.

A woman came out of the shadow of the church porch. Old, bent, headscarved — and then Marguerite's memory performed its terrible, faithful work: Madame Gireaux, rue des Tanneurs, number eleven, aged sixty-eight, lives alone, son executed at Toulon, refuses pity, grows beans.

Madame Gireaux looked at her for a long moment with eyes like frozen puddles.

"I know you," she said. "You're the counting woman."

"Yes," Marguerite said.

"You came to my kitchen. You wrote down my name and my boy's name, though my boy is dead and they killed him, and you wrote it all down *neatly*, I remember, neat as prayer." The old woman's voice did not rise. That was the worst of it. It stayed level as a carpenter's line. "Then men came with papers. Your papers. They knew my son's name. They knew the year. Nobody living knew the year except me and God and you."

Marguerite did not say *I didn't give it to them.* The sentence was true and it was worthless, and Madame Gireaux deserved better than true, worthless things.

"What do you want from me?" Marguerite asked.

The old woman considered. Wind moved the postings on the church door with a dry rattle, like the pages of some enormous, obscene book turning.

"I want you to count what's left," she said finally. "Since you're good at counting. Count the empty houses. Count the taken. Write *them* down for once, in your neat hand, and don't stop until there's no more room in the book." She gripped Marguerite's wrist with surprising strength, fingers like root wood. "Because somebody has to hold the numbers, girl. And God has very bad handwriting."

Then she let go, gathered her shawl, and walked back up the lane toward her beans, and Marguerite stood in the square holding her wrist as though the grip had been a seal pressed into wax.

* * *

That night they sheltered in the loft of a barn two fields outside the town, because returning to any address in the district was no longer survivable — another lesson her own records had taught her; every barn, every hayrick, every outhouse within six leagues had an owner, and owners had entries, and entries had numbers, and numbers could be checked. They lay in the hay with their coats pulled up to their chins and the wind worrying the roof boards overhead, and Ansel, bless him, did not speak for a long time.

When he finally did, it was only: "Tell me."

So she told him. Not the story he already knew — brother arrested last spring, brother vanished into the machinery, sister reassigned to courier-identification duty and quietly refusing — but the other story, the one underneath, the one she had never said aloud even to herself.

"When they gave me the courier list," she said, "I told myself it was different. Finding travelers' names, that's just registration. That's weather recording. But the truth is I'd already stopped believing in the ledger before that. Last spring. When they took Tomas." Her brother's name still fit strangely in her mouth, like a key to a house that had been sold. "They took him using the trade guild rolls. Rolls I had helped reconcile the winter before, as a favor to the district office. His name, his apprenticeship, his lodging. All in my handwriting somewhere, in fair copy, under my eye." She listened to the wind. "I told myself the rolls would have existed whether or not I copied them. I told myself refusal would only have gotten me taken too, and then who would have been left to notice what was happening. Both true. Both useless. Do you understand? I built a whole architecture of both-true-and-useless and lived inside it, and it was warm in there, and I slept well."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the house they built with my bricks." She rolled onto her side to look at him. In the dark his face was only an arrangement of shadows, but she could feel him listening the way some people pray. "I keep thinking about what a census actually *is*, Ansel. We tell ourselves it's a mirror. The state looks at itself, sees how many hands, how many mouths. But a mirror doesn't remember. A ledger remembers. A ledger is a promise that says: *these people existed, here, at this hour, and someone swore to it.* And when the state breaks its promises, it uses the ledger to find the people it promised to. We are indexed, do you see? We are paginated. They can call us up by name and number like demons."

Ansel was quiet. Then, in the careful voice he used when touching the atlas: "Brandt's book is a ledger too."

"Yes."

"Of debts. Promises made and broken. Names still owed protection."

"That's what you told me you found in the chapel."

"Then maybe—" He stopped. Restarted. "Maybe it matters who holds the remembering. Maybe a ledger in the wrong hands is a hunting map and in the right hands it's a witness. Same pages. Same ink."

Marguerite stared up through a gap in the roof boards at a hard scatter of stars. *Same pages. Same ink.* Her whole life she had believed the danger lay in lying — in false entries, forged numbers, padded counts. She had never imagined that the deepest sin available to a recorder was perfect honesty in the service of predators. Accuracy was not a virtue. It was a capacity, like strength, like fire, and capacities served whoever grasped them.

Well then. She would be grasped by no one again. If her counting had armed the state, her counting would arm the ones the state hunted. She did not yet know what that meant in practice — a reversed census, a register of the disappeared instead of the resident, names gathered in secret, carried in secret, sworn in secret — but she felt the shape of the idea settle over her like a coat cut precisely to her shoulders, and she was almost afraid of how well it fit.

"There are households on my lists," she said slowly, "that haven't been touched yet. Ones the prefecture hasn't processed. I know their composition. Their ages. Which sons are away, which cellars are deep, which wives can pass messages as laundresses without being questioned. I carry all of it still, Ansel. Up here." She touched her temple. "They burned nothing. They never thought to search the counter."

"The counter?"

"A census taker's second ledger. The one clerks aren't supposed to keep. Corrections, doubts, observations too soft for the official record." Her heart was going fast now, the way it went as a girl running downhill. "I kept one anyway. Habit. Or conscience. I don't know. But I know where it is buried, and I know every name in it, and tomorrow I am going to start writing those names down again — the taken, the fled, the hidden — and I am going to keep writing until there is no more room in the book, like the bean woman said."

In the dark, Ansel reached over and found her hand, and they lay like that, two people trained to record rather than trust, holding on as though the other were the only surviving copy of something precious.

* * *

The storm came at dawn out of the northwest, a grey wall shouldering the hills aside, and it changed all their plans in an afternoon.

They had meant to push east along the river road toward Ilse Brandt's hiding place, two days' travel, three in bad weather. By noon the bad weather had become a verdict: roads white-blind, hedgerows turned to ice-sculpture, the river itself smoking where the wind stripped its surface. Ferro, whom they had arranged to meet at a charcoal burner's camp, took one look at the sky and pronounced the river route dead for a week.

"So we wait," Ansel said.

"We wait *here*?" Marguerite looked around the charcoal burner's clearing: a low turf-roofed hut, a lean-to of stacked cords, a sky pressing down like a lid. "In a hut with one door and your warrant in every post office from here to the coast?"

"In a hut with one door," Ferro said cheerfully, feeding sticks to the stove, "you'll always know where your enemies are coming from."

But the storm had its own arithmetic, and by evening it had penned them in entirely, wind screaming across the clearing like a congregation in mourning, snow sealing the door drift half up the jamb. There would be no travel, no news, no movement for days. Nothing to do but sit close to the stove with the atlas between them, that leather-bound weight of other people's broken promises, and Marguerite — who could not bear idleness, who had never in her life endured an unrecorded hour — found herself saying:

"Read it to me. All of it. The letter especially. I've carried the fear of this book for weeks and I've never once heard its actual voice."

Ansel hesitated, unwrapped the oilcloth, turned the heavy pages to the folded letter at the front — Elias Brandt's covering letter, written in peacetime, in a calm hand, to whoever came after. Outside, the storm leaned on the hut. Ansel cleared his throat, and began to read aloud into the wind.
