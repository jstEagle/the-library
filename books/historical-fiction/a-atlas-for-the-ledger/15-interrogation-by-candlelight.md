## 15. Interrogation by Candlelight

The house Ilse Brandt kept was smaller than Ansel had imagined, and colder. It stood at the end of a lane where the snow lay undisturbed except for one set of boot prints, already half-filled with fresh fall, leading away from the door rather than toward it — as though its owner had rehearsed departure so often that even her comings were shaped like flight.

Inside, the room smelled of tallow and cold iron and, beneath it, faintly, of ink. A single candle burned on the table, set in a dish that had once held fruit. Two chairs faced each other across the table's width like witnesses in a trial who could not be trusted to sit together.

Ilse Brandt did not look like the daughter of the man Ansel remembered. Elias Brandt had been all softness at the edges — a belly that shook when he laughed, hands stained to the wrist with cartographer's ink, a voice like a hearth. His daughter was thin as a drawn line, her hair scraped back so severely it seemed an act of discipline rather than fashion, and her eyes moved over them the way a customs officer's eyes move over luggage: cataloguing, weighing, searching seams.

"Sit," she said.

It was not hospitality. It was procedure.

Ansel sat. Marguerite did not; she remained standing by the door, her back against the wood, one hand still on the latch — the posture of a woman who had learned, over a long winter, exactly how quickly rooms could become traps.

Ilse took the other chair and placed, deliberately, between herself and Ansel, a second candle. She lit it from the first with a taper, unhurried, until three flames stood in a row across the table like a small tribunal.

"You have something of my father's," she said.

"Yes."

"I didn't ask if you'd admit it. I asked you to confirm what I already know." She folded her hands. "You have something of my father's. Describe it. Do not open anything. Tell me what you see when you look at the outside of it."

Ansel described the ledger-atlas: the calfskin cover gone dark with handling, the brass corners worn round, the spine cracked in three places where it had been opened most often — near the front, near the middle, near the end. The strap that closed it was not original; it had been replaced with a soldier's belt-leather, stitched by someone with more determination than skill.

"The stitching is mine," Ansel said. "Elias taught me to sew leather the winter my hands froze. He said a courier should know how to mend what they carry, because no one else will love it enough to."

Something crossed Ilse's face then — not softening, but the memory of softness, the way light crosses a shuttered window without entering. It was gone before Ansel could name it.

"Elias Pemberton," she said. "That was the name he used for his apprentice. In letters. *My boy Elias.* He never said your family name."

"He gave me his own for safety. He said names were coats — you wear what fits the weather."

"And now the weather has changed again." She leaned back. "So. You carry his atlas. Across four hundred miles of a country eating itself. Through checkpoints and burned inns and whatever happened to leave those marks—" her eyes flicked to the scorch shadow along Marguerite's coat sleeve, "—on the people traveling with you. Why?"

Ansel opened their mouth to give the answer prepared somewhere in the deep habit of service: *duty*, or *promise*, or *the mission*. The words assembled themselves in the old order, courier's grammar, report-shaped.

Ilse watched the assembly happen. She had the patience of someone who had been interrogated before and had studied her interrogators closely.

"Take your time," she said. "I have candles."

* * *

The first hour she spent on facts. Where Ansel had been born — not the name on any document, but the place, the street, the sound of the church bells, whether the well water tasted of iron. Where Elias had kept his workshop. What he paid for lamp oil. Whether he whistled while he worked (he did; badly; always the same four bars of a marching song he claimed to despise). What he said the last time Ansel saw him.

Ansel answered everything, and the answers came easier than expected, because memory, once unlocked, poured like meltwater. And through it all Ilse listened without expression, correcting nothing, confirming nothing, occasionally asking a question so precise it proved she knew the answers already — this was not fact-finding but verification, the way a mint tests coins against known weights.

Marguerite, by the door, interjected only twice: once to correct a date, once to say, quietly, "That's true. I've seen her knowledge of the roads. It's real." Ilse noted her voice the way she noted everything, filed and unspent.

Then, past midnight, with two candles burned to stubs, Ilse changed the weather entirely.

"Now tell me why," she said. "Not the route. Not the seals. Why does a clerk of a republic that has forgotten my father ever existed walk into winter carrying his life's work? Give me the true reason. Not the one you'd write in a report."

And Ansel, who had crossed mountains and outlasted fire and lied to soldiers with a steady voice, found there was nothing left to lie with.

* * *

"I wanted it not to matter," Ansel said finally.

The words came out small. They sounded worse spoken than they had sounded in the privacy of Ansel's own skull, where they had lived unexamined for years, dressed up in better clothes.

"When Elias gave me the atlas, he made me promise to deliver it to whoever keeps the maps honest. And I promised, because it was Elias, and because promises made in peacetime feel like decorations — pretty things you hang up and forget. I thought the war would pass around me. Wars always seemed to happen to other people. Other couriers carried the dispatches that mattered; mine were inventories and trade schedules. I built a whole life on being unimportant. Invisible. I told myself invisibility was a kind of virtue. Humility." A laugh escaped, bitter and unfamiliar in their own ears. "It wasn't humility. It was cowardice wearing humility's coat. Same tailoring as the names-as-coats speech. I learned from a master."

Ilse's eyes narrowed slightly. "Go on."

"I carried the atlas for months without opening it. Not because I was honoring a seal — because I didn't want to know. If I never looked inside, it was still just paper. Paper can be delivered. Paper can be handed off. But if I opened it and found it mattered — really mattered — then I would matter, and everything I'd done to stay safe would have been wasted. So yes. I carried your father's work across a burning country, and half of why was duty, and half of why was fear, and I honestly could not tell you which half signed the promise first."

The third candle guttered. No one moved to trim it.

"And her?" Ilse tilted her chin toward Marguerite.

"She asked one question too many at an inn," Ansel said. "Two seals instead of one. She saw me when I had built my whole survival on not being seen. Then soldiers burned the inn down around us, and we've been running since. She was ordered to hunt couriers. Instead she became one."

"That's her story. I'll take it separately, and I'll know if the seams don't match." Ilse turned back. "But here is what I don't believe yet, Elias-the-second: that a coward carries stolen state property through a war zone for six hundred miles. Cowards find ditches. Cowards sell the atlas to the first smuggler who offers warm beds and a border crossing. Did no one offer?"

"Ferro," Ansel said quietly.

"And?"

"And we said no. And Ferro sold us anyway, and a man named Oskel died in a salt house so that we could keep walking. So perhaps I'm not a coward anymore. Or perhaps cowardice just got expensive."

"No." Ilse's voice cut clean. "That's not it either. Don't dress it up as arithmetic. A man died. Say it plainly."

"A man named Oskel died," Ansel said, and their voice broke on the name, and they let it. "He died facing the door so we could go out the window. He laughed at Ferro's jokes and he owed me nothing, and I will carry him longer than I carried any dispatch. Is that plain enough?"

For a long moment, the only sound was the candleflame eating its wick.

Then Ilse said, softer, "Why are you telling me all this? You could have performed conviction. Duty. Most couriers can produce it on demand, like a second seal. I would have doubted it, but you'd have slept better tonight."

"Because you asked for the true reason. And because—" Ansel stopped. Started again. "Because your father told me once that the worst thing a mapmaker can do is draw the country he wishes existed instead of the one under his feet. I've been drawing myself as a person I wished existed. Someone dutiful. Someone whose history happens elsewhere. You deserve the survey, not the wish."

* * *

Ilse rose. She went to the cold hearth and stood looking into it as though reading ash. When she spoke again, her voice had lost the tribunal's edge and gained something worse: grief, long-compressed, speaking through procedure because it had no other language left.

"They came for him in the spring," she said, to the fireplace rather than to Ansel. "Not soldiers. Clerks. Men with lists. They read out his name wrong — some minor official's mispronunciation — and he corrected them. Politely. Twice. Because he was a man who believed accuracy mattered." Her hand tightened on the mantel. "They took him away for the correction. For insisting on the truth of his own name. And do you know what I thought, afterward? I thought: if he had only let it go. If he had only been a coward, like everyone else, like me — I hid, you understand. I changed my name and let them erase him from every record, and I told myself surviving was the higher duty. So don't stand in my kitchen confessing your cowardice to me, courier. I hold the patent on it."

"Then you know," Ansel said, "that cowardice isn't the end of the story. You're still here. You still asked the questions."

"I asked questions to protect myself. To make sure you weren't police."

"Perhaps. But you also asked what I see when I look at the atlas's cover. That's not police questioning. That's a daughter checking whether a stranger handled her father gently."

Ilse turned. In the candlelight her face was unreadable, but her eyes were wet, and furious about it.

"The atlas killed him," she said. "He wouldn't stop working on it. Wouldn't hide it. Wouldn't burn it, though I begged him — I begged him, the night before they came, and he said, *Ilse, a map of broken promises is the only honest map there is, and honest things must survive their makers.* He chose it over us. Over himself. So forgive me if I don't weep with gratitude that two strangers dragged it through the snow to my door like a relic."

"It isn't a relic," Ansel said. "It's a debt. Your father mapped every promise this republic made and broke — every pension unpaid, every protection withdrawn, every name still owed shelter. There are people in it, Ilse. Living people, hiding right now, who are owed protection under laws that still technically exist. The atlas knows where they are. And the republic's own records, the ones the census-takers built—" a glance at Marguerite, who flinched almost imperceptibly, "—are being used to hunt them. Your father's book might be the only counter-record left."

Silence. The candles burned low, all three, drowning in their own wax.

Marguerite spoke from the door, her voice steady in the way that cost her visibly. "She's right. I've seen the lists used. My own district. My own hand."

Ilse looked from one to the other, and Ansel watched her weigh it — the same weighing she had done all night, coin after coin against known weights. But this time what she weighed was not their story. It was her father's last sentence, the one Ansel had just quoted, the one she must have heard a thousand times at a workbench smelling of ink.

*Honest things must survive their makers.*

"I need to think," she said at last, hoarse. "Alone. You'll sleep in the back room. The door doesn't lock — from either side. Understand what that means."

"It means trust with the lock removed," Marguerite said.

"It means I haven't decided yet," Ilse said. "Don't mistake one thing for the other."

She gathered the candle dish and moved toward the stairs, then paused, her back to them.

"The four bars he whistled," she said, without turning. "What were they?"

Ansel hummed them. Badly. The same four bars.

Ilse stood very still. When she finally climbed the stairs, her steps were slower than her interrogation had been.

* * *

The back room held one cot, one blanket, and a window whose shutter had been nailed shut from the inside — a detail Ansel noted with the weary expertise of someone who had slept in many houses that expected visitors with warrants. Marguerite laid the atlas on the floor between them, equidistant, neither guarding nor surrendering it.

"She'll refuse it again tomorrow," Marguerite whispered. "Or she'll take it. Either way, we've done what we came to do."

"We've done what we came to do," Ansel agreed. But sleep would not come, and after a while Marguerite's breathing slowed into the shallow rhythm of someone else also failing to sleep, and the house creaked around them with the particular grammar of old buildings and old grievances.

Near dawn, Ansel heard footsteps on the stairs. Light ones. Pausing at the back-room door — which, as promised, had no lock. A long silence followed, the length of a person standing with her hand raised to knock, or to open, or simply to listen to two strangers breathing in her father's absence.

Then, softly, through the wood, Ilse Brandt's voice:

"The middle crack in the spine. What page does it fall open to?"

Ansel was fully awake now. They had not needed to check. "The debts of the northern garrisons. The ones the treasury swore were paid in '93 and never were."

Another silence. Longer. When the voice came again it was barely above the sound of the wind finding the eaves.

"He showed me that page when I was twelve. He said, *remember this one, little owl — this is the page that will get me killed someday.*"

The stairs creaked as she turned. And then, from the darkness beyond the door, words that rearranged the entire shape of the night:

"There's a print shop in the river quarter. Cellar of a chandler's, blue door. If I do this — if I do *this* — it won't be delivery. It will be copying. Hundreds of copies. Hand to hand until the republic chokes on its own broken word." A breath. "Be ready to travel again by Friday. Both of you. And courier—"

"Yes?"

"If you ever draw yourself as a coward again, I'll correct you. Twice. Politely."

Her steps receded upward. Ansel lay in the dark beside Marguerite, listening to the house settle, and understood that the interrogation had ended hours ago — that somewhere in the confession and the grief and the four whistled bars, a verdict had already been reached.

But Friday was four days away, and the road behind them was not empty. Somewhere south, a warrant with Ansel's true name on it was moving north faster than any courier had ever carried anything.

Outside, the snow began again, covering the lane's single set of footprints, erasing the evidence that anyone had ever come to this door at all.
