## 16. The Census Reversed

Marguerite had not slept in Ilse Brandt's house. She had not been offered a bed, and she would not have taken one if it were offered. Instead she sat in the kitchen through the small hours with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold an hour ago, listening to the wind work at the shutters and to the sound of Ansel's voice in the next room — low, steady, sometimes breaking — as they answered Ilse's questions one by one.

She knew how those questions felt from the other side of the table. She had asked them herself for six years: names, ages, trades, places of birth, kin, loyalties, the color of the door, the number of animals in the yard. She had asked them with a smile that made people forget they were being measured, and she had written their answers down in a hand so fine and so regular that her supervisors had twice used her ledgers as models for the new enumerators.

Her brother's name was in none of those ledgers. That was the thing she returned to, over and over, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. Tomas had died in the spring purge, and his name appeared in no census she had ever taken, because the men who took him had not bothered to record it. He had simply ceased, administratively, to exist. The republic was very good at counting what it wished to keep and very good at forgetting everything else, and Marguerite Voss had spent six years sharpening its forgetting into an instrument.

The candle guttered. Outside, snow had begun again, fine as sifted flour, hissing against the glass.

In the next room, Ansel said something she could not hear, and then there was silence, and then the creak of a chair, and then footsteps — Ilse's, heavy and deliberate — crossing toward the kitchen.

Marguerite did not look up when the door opened. She looked at her own hands instead, at the ink stain in the crease of her right thumb that no amount of washing had ever fully lifted. Six years of writing other people's lives into columns, and the ink had gotten into her the way iron gets into well water.

"You're still awake," Ilse said. It was not a question. Nothing Ilse said was ever a question; she interrogated even the weather.

"I don't sleep well in houses where I'm being judged."

"Then you'll be tired for a long time." Ilse crossed to the stove, fed it a split of birch, and stood watching the flame take. She had her father's hands — broad, capable, scarred at the knuckles — though her face was all her mother's, angular and unyielding as a winter shoreline. "My father kept a rule," she said, without turning. "A map is a promise made in ink. Break the promise and the ink becomes a lie, and a lie drawn accurately is more dangerous than any ignorance." She turned then, and her eyes were gray and cold and lit orange at their lower edge by the stove. "You are a woman who makes promises in ink, Fräulein Voss. Or you were. So tell me why I should believe your ink is any different from theirs."

Marguerite set down the cup. Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a kind of distant surprise, the way one notices the stillness of a pond just before a stone breaks it.

"Because mine has already been used to kill," she said. "And I know exactly how it was done."

* * *

Ilse did not sit. She leaned against the stove's warm flank with her arms folded, and Marguerite understood that this was to be a confession delivered standing, like a charge read before a tribunal, and that she should not ask for mercy because none would be extended until the reading was done.

"In my third year of service," Marguerite began, "the Enumeration Office changed its instructions. Quietly. There was no proclamation; there never is. A circular came down, printed on cheap paper, signed by a deputy we'd never heard of. It said that certain categories of persons — foreign-born residents, holders of lapsed licenses, families with kin across the border — were henceforth to be flagged in our ledgers. Not arrested. Flagged. A small mark in the margin. A red dot, when red ink was available."

"And you made the marks."

"I made the marks." Her voice came out flat and strange, as though someone else were speaking through her. "I told myself it was clerical. That a dot in a margin was not a verdict. That I recorded what the state required and the state alone decided what the recording meant. That was the whole architecture of my innocence — that the pen was only a pen." She looked down at her stained thumb. "In the autumn, soldiers came to the district I had finished in August. They went to forty-one houses. Every house carried the mark. They took ninety-three people. I know the number because I checked, afterward, against my own ledger, which the Office later burned along with three hundred others — but I had copied my pages out of habit, because I am careful, because accuracy was my pride, and so I am one of perhaps four people alive who knows exactly who was taken and when and from which door."

Ilse had gone very still. The stove ticked and settled behind her.

"My brother was not among them," Marguerite continued. "Tomas was a printer. He printed pamphlets the state disliked, which meant he printed truths, which in this republic are now the same category as weapons. They came for him in April. No mark preceded him. No dot, no flag, no warning in any ledger. He was simply erased — no record, no trial entry, nothing. And I sat in my office that spring filing forms about grain allotments while my own brother vanished from the world without leaving so much as a line for me to find him by." She finally raised her eyes. "Do you understand what I learned from that? The state counts what it intends to keep. Everything else it simply stops writing down. My dots were the beginning of the killing, yes. But the erasure — the refusal to record — that is the deeper crime, and it is the crime no one punishes, because you cannot prosecute a blank page."

For a long moment there was only the wind and the fire.

"And so?" Ilse said at last. "You confess beautifully, Fräulein. Confession is also a form of self-comfort. What do you intend to do with all this fine guilt?"

Marguerite reached into the satchel at her feet — the satchel that had not left her body since the inn burned, since Grey Ford, since the salt house — and drew out the atlas. She laid it on the scrubbed pine table between them, its leather cover darkened with road grime and one water stain shaped like a country that existed nowhere.

"I intend to count," she said. "Backward."

* * *

She opened the atlas to the middle sections, the ones Ansel had shown her in the chapel, the ones that had curdled her awe into dread. Elias Brandt's maps were not maps of territory. Page after page, in the old cartographer's meticulous hand, the republic rendered itself as it truly was: a lattice of debts. Here, the grain promised to the northern cantons in the famine year and never delivered — each undelivered ton marked with a small cross, and beside each cross, names. Here, the pensions voted to maimed veterans and quietly defunded — names again, columns of them. Here, the protections sworn to border families under the old treaties, protections now voided by decree — and here, in the margins, in fresher ink, additions in a different hand: *taken*, *fled*, *dead?*, dates.

"He didn't stop mapping when the state turned," Marguerite said. "He turned with it. Look — these last entries are two years old. He kept recording the broken promises right up until they killed him for it." She touched one name gently, the way one touches a bruise. "This is a census, Frau Brandt. Not of property or persons-of-record, but of what the republic owes and whom it has failed. Your father counted the disappeared before anyone called them that."

"I know what my father made." But Ilse's voice had lost some of its armor; it had gone quieter, rougher at the edges.

"Then let me tell you what I can make of it." Marguerite pulled a blank folio from her own bag — her working papers, the ones she had risked her neck retrieving from her office the night before she fled — and smoothed it flat. "Every census taker learns the same first lesson: a population is not a number. It is a method. You define a household, you define a person, you define absence — and once you have definitions, you can count anything. The state taught me to count its subjects. Fine. The same methods, reversed, will count its victims."

She began to draw as she spoke, quick sure lines, the geometry of a form taking shape: ruled columns, marginal spaces, a header block.

"A register of the disappeared. Name, last known residence, date last seen, manner of disappearance — arrest, flight, death witnessed, death reported. Kin who may be searching. What they left behind. Who remembers them." Her pen moved without hesitation; six years of habit had built this machinery into her hand. "The state keeps no such register because the register is an accusation. Every name in it is evidence that the republic lied. That is precisely why it must exist. You cannot demand restitution for persons who officially never were. So first they must be made to exist — on paper, in ink, in numbers the state cannot argue with, because numbers compiled by its own methods are the one language it respects."

Ilse stared at the forming grid. "You propose to build the thing that will hang them."

"I propose to build the thing that will convict them," Marguerite said. "There's a difference, and it matters. Hanging requires power we do not have. Conviction — proof, testimony, a record that outlives whoever tries to burn it — that requires only discipline. Which is the one thing I have never lacked." She heard the bitterness in her own voice and did not soften it. "They trusted me to be accurate. They gave me authority, routes, access to every parish roll and garrison manifest in three districts. I will spend every ounce of that training against them now. Let my precision be poison in their ledger for once."

"And the atlas?"

"The spine." Marguerite laid her hand flat upon the open page. "Your father already gathered what would take us ten years to find — the debts, the names, the dates, the broken promises, each tied to real households and real villages. Every entry here is a seed for a file. We copy the names, verify what we can, extend outward through the parishes and the surviving neighbors and the smugglers' networks. Ferro's people move goods across the river; they can move paper too. Within a year there could be copies in four cities, in cellars, in false bottoms, passed hand to hand. Burn one, and another survives. That is how you make a truth unkillable — not by hiding it, but by distributing it past the reach of any single fire."

The stove popped. Somewhere above them a beam contracted in the cold with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and neither of them moved.

"You understand," Ilse said slowly, "that this is not deliverance. This is not my father's promise kept and finished. This is a war conducted with stationery." There was something in her voice Marguerite had not heard before — not warmth, not yet, but the loosening of a knot held too long. "You understand that the moment the first copy leaves this cellar, every person named in it is in danger, including everyone who helps make it. Including the two sleepless idealists upstairs in my guest room who brought me this book like children bringing home a wounded bird."

"I have been complicit in danger already," Marguerite said. "I would rather be complicit on the correct side. And I would rather do the work than atone for it. Atonement is passive. It lets you feel cleansed while changing nothing. Work changes things." She met Ilse's gaze and held it. "You asked why your ink should differ from mine. It shouldn't. It's the same ink, the same craft, the same terrible usefulness. The only question is whose hands hold the pen, and for whom the counting is done. Your father chose. Now we choose."

Ilse looked down at the atlas for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice was almost too low for the room.

"He used to say that a mapmaker's first duty was not to the prince but to the territory — that the land does not lie, only the surveyors do." Her jaw tightened. "He would have hated what you're proposing. Too dangerous. Too many civilians holding too much evidence." A pause. "He would have started it anyway. By Thursday."

Despite everything — the dead inn, the dead contact at the salt house, the warrants with Ansel's name on them, the whole frozen collapsing weight of the century's last winter — Marguerite laughed. It came out of her like a cough, unexpected and raw, and Ilse's mouth twitched at one corner, the first concession of a besieged fortress.

"Then we start tonight," Marguerite said, and uncapped the ink.

* * *

They worked until the window lightened from black to slate to the pale washed gray of a snowbound dawn. Marguerite ruled the master grid herself, forty lines to the page, and entered the first eleven names in her finest hand — the eleven she could verify from memory alone, the households of the forty-one doors, the ones whose absence she had carried like stones in her coat for over a year. Ilse copied atlas entries onto slips for cross-checking, her father's debt-maps fanning across the table like the plumage of some enormous slain bird. Upstairs, around five o'clock, a floorboard announced that Ansel was awake; he came down an hour later, hair wild, took one look at the transformed kitchen, and asked no questions — only fetched more candles from his pack and sat down and began, in his clerk's copperplate, transcribing the covering letter.

By full morning the first register-page was complete. Eleven names. Eleven absences made briefly, stubbornly present. Marguerite blotted the final entry and sat back, and found that her eyes were wet, and did not trouble to hide it.

"It's so few," she said. "Eleven. Out of thousands."

"It's a beginning of a count," Ilse said. "Counts begin at one. That is their entire virtue — they cannot be argued with once begun."

It was Ansel, quiet at the far end of the table, who broke the moment — not with comfort but with the flat tone Marguerite had learned to dread, the voice he used for reports from the world's worse edge.

"There's smoke south of the tree line," he said. "Too much for a chimney. And Ferro was supposed to send word by dawn."

Marguerite was on her feet before he finished, her hand already finding the strap of the satchel. Through the frost-furred glass, beyond the white fields, a thin dark column rose against the brightening sky, bending east with the wind — bending, she noted with the cold automatic part of her mind that never stopped measuring, directly away from the river road.

Away from the crossing. Toward the salt house. Toward the ashes of the man who had died there covering their escape.

"They've found him," she said. "And they'll find what he was carrying next."
