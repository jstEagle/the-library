## 20. Delivered

The print shop announced itself by smell before Marguerite saw it: ink and tallow, wet paper and iron, the cold mineral breath of a cellar that had never once been warm. They came down to it through a cooper's yard off the Rue des Tanneurs, past vats stinking of oak tannin, down a stair so narrow Ilse went first with a shuttered lantern held low, its single blade of light sliding along walls furred with frost.

Marguerite counted the steps. It was habit — she had counted everything for years, doors and chimneys and souls — but now the counting felt different in her hands, like a tool returned after long borrowing. Fourteen steps. A landing. A door with a latch wrapped in wool so it would not sound.

Below, the cellar opened wider than the street above suggested. Three presses stood against the far wall under canvas, and when Ilse pulled the cloth away, the light caught the iron of them and made two brief silver ghosts in the dark. A composing stone. Trays of type, sorted and slotted with the obsessive neatness of a person who needed one thing in her life to stay ordered. Reams of paper wrapped in oilcloth, hoarded like bread in a siege. And everywhere, on every surface, pamphlets in various stages: broadsheets half-dried, their columns of type still glistening, arguing some point of doctrine or other with the furious confidence of ink.

"You print," Ansel said from the stairs, and there was something almost wounded in it, the tone of a man discovering that a quiet neighbor kept a wolf.

"I print," Ilse said. "My father drew maps for men who lied with them. I learned early that paper is never neutral. So." She set the lantern on the composing stone and turned, and in the small light her face was all planes and verdicts, her mother's jaw and Elias Brandt's eyes — eyes Marguerite had seen only once, in a miniature sewn into the lining of an atlas, but knew instantly all the same. "Show me what you carried across a river under fire."

* * *

They had crossed at dawn, three days ago, or what they believed was dawn — the sky had been the color of dirty wool, and the fog on the river so thick that Ferro's boat seemed to travel through milk. The soldiers' muskets had spoken twice from the far bank, flat sounds swallowed by water, and then Ferro's voice rising out of the fog, telling jokes to the soldiers in a voice pitched to carry, holding their attention the way a man holds a lantern before a bull. He had not come back over.

Marguerite did not let herself think of him in full. She thought of him in pieces: the arithmetic he did aloud with any available object as counters — buttons, walnuts, the knuckles of his own hand. The way he'd said, *a map of broken promises is worth more than gold, courier, because gold you can steal but a debt you can only collect*, and how she had hated him for wanting the atlas and understood him completely. The ledger had been meant as his payment. In the end he took nothing but the fog and the soldiers' attention, and left them the whole of his fee unpaid.

She would enter him in the register, when she began it. Under *lost*. She was already composing the line in her head, and hated that this was how she mourned now — by formatting grief into columns.

In the cellar, Ansel unbuttoned their coat and drew out the atlas wrapped in sacking and waxed linen, and even now, after everything, held it a moment before offering it. Marguerite watched the hesitation. She had come to know it well on the road: the way Ansel's hands behaved around the ledger like hands around a sleeping animal that might be grateful or might bite. Two months of carrying it had changed the courier's body. The shoulders sat differently. There were new lines around the mouth that had not been there at the inn at Grey Ford, when a census taker asked one question too many and two careful lives began to burn behind them.

Ilse took the atlas the way a magistrate takes evidence.

She unwrapped it without ceremony, laid it on the composing stone beneath the lamp, and opened it — and Marguerite saw her stop breathing.

The pages were Brandt's hand, unmistakably: the same upright, patient script that filled the miniature's backing paper. But where a common atlas would show rivers and ranges, these pages showed names. Loans recorded and repudiated. Grain requisitioned and never paid for. Villages promised exemption from levy and levied anyway within the season. And threaded through every page, in red, a second geography: the names of persons owed protection under old treaties — witnesses guaranteed safe conduct, informants promised passage, families promised pensions whose fathers had died in the republic's service. Each name located precisely. Each promise dated, cited, and marked, in Brandt's small red hand, with a symbol Marguerite had not learned to read until the chapel, until Ansel read the covering letter aloud in a voice that shook: the mark meaning *unpaid*.

"It isn't a map of the country," Ilse said at last. Her voice had gone very quiet, which Marguerite would learn was worse than loud. "It's a map of what the country owes."

"He called it honest cartography," Ansel said. "He said borders are the first lie. Debts are the truth underneath."

"My father is dead because of the truth underneath." Ilse closed the atlas. Her hand rested on the cover as if holding a door shut against weather. "They shot him in the courtyard of the Survey Office. Do you know why? Not for the maps. For a list. Someone told the Committee that Brandt kept a list of persons the state still owed, and the Committee understood immediately what such a list is. It is a mirror. You cannot let people see what they are owed; they begin to collect." She looked up. "So no. I don't thank you for it. I have spent four years being no one's daughter, and you have walked it across a frozen country to make me his again."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, the cooper's dog barked twice and stopped.

"Then burn it," Ansel said.

Marguerite turned. She had heard Ansel argue with Ferro, lie at checkpoints, refuse a superior's pardon through most of one endless night — but she had never heard this flat calm.

"Everything it cost is already spent," Ansel went on. "Ferro. the boy at the salt house. My seals, my name, my whole ridiculous belief that history happened elsewhere. If it means nothing to you, burn it, and we will have paid for a fire."

"That is blackmail by corpse," Ilse said.

"Yes," Ansel said. "I've become the sort of person who does that. You should know it before you decide about me."

* * *

Marguerite left them to it.

Not far. She climbed six of the fourteen steps and sat in the dark on the cold stone, hearing the murmur below — two voices circling each other like animals deciding whether the other was predator or kin — and she did what she always did when feeling exceeded her: she took out her book.

Her census ledger. The official one, with the republic's stamp on the flyleaf, the one she had used at the border inn to record travelers' details in her upright hand. She had not been able to abandon it. Even knowing — *especially* knowing — what it had done. In her old district, three weeks ago, she had walked her own rounds again and seen the consequences of her accuracy: the houses with fresh whitewash over doors, the neighbors who would not meet her eye, the absence where the Aubry family had been, entered in her own book as *household of five, all present* and entered in someone else's book as *five arrests, confirmed*. Her careful pen had been a weapon aimed by other hands. She had sat that night in a ditch and been sick, and then, because she was who she was, she had begun, in the back pages of the same ledger, a different column.

Now she turned to those back pages and read what she had so far: *Voss, Mathias. Taken spring '98. No charge recorded.* Her brother. Then eleven more names gathered along the road, each with place, date, last seen, and the blank she refused to fill: *status unknown*.

That was the thing the republic understood and she had not, until the ditch: a census is never neutral. It can count people to find them, or count them to keep them. Every list is a moral instrument; the question is only whose hands hold the pen and what the pen is for.

She went back down.

They were standing exactly where she'd left them, the atlas between them on the stone like a border both sides claim. But Ilse had opened it again, and Marguerite saw that her finger rested on a page near the middle — and that her lips were moving.

"This name," Ilse said. "Marthe Aubry and household, five persons, village of Saint-Rève, promised protection under article eleven of the armistice terms, marked unpaid." She looked up at Marguerite, and something moved behind the verdict-face, something older and more frightened. "You know this family."

"They were on my rounds," Marguerite said. "I enumerated them. Five, all present, in the spring." Her throat closed. "I walked their street three weeks ago. The house is whitewashed. Nobody would speak of them."

The cellar was very quiet. Then Ilse pulled a second chair — a printer's stool, actually — to the composing stone, and pointed at it.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. I want to understand exactly what my father built, and I think the two of you are the only apparatus in Europe that can explain it. One of you carries his debts. One of you carries the method for finding the debtors." She looked at Marguerite's ledger, sticking out of her coat where she had never thought to hide it, and her mouth did something that was almost, almost a smile. "And apparently one of you has already started."

* * *

They worked until the candle was a thumb's width and then worked past it, by feel and by memory, and Marguerite forgot to be afraid, which she noted the way a physician notes a symptom.

What emerged, spread across the composing stone in the lantern light, was not one document but a machine. Brandt's atlas gave the debts: who had been promised what, by whom, under which article, and where. Marguerite's census methods could give the living: how to enumerate quietly, house by house, using the state's own grammar — *household of*, *all present*, *occupation* — so that a hidden register could hide inside the shape of an ordinary one. And Ilse's press could multiply it. Not printed — printing was for declarations, and declarations invited the gallows — but copied, in fair hand, page by page, so that the atlas existed in ten places instead of one and could not be killed by burning any single cellar.

"A counter-census," Marguerite said, and heard how strange the words were in her mouth, words that had no office behind them, no stamp, no authority except the authority of having been done carefully. "We count the disappeared. We record what was promised to them and never delivered. And we put it in the same order the republic uses, so that someday — if there is a someday with laws in it — every page of ours can be laid against every page of theirs."

"And until that someday?" Ilse asked.

"Until then, it means the names exist somewhere other than the heads of people who loved them."

Ilse was silent a long moment. When she spoke, she was looking at her father's red marks, the little symbols marching down the margins like birds on a wire.

"He used to say a map is a promise you can hold," she said. "I thought it was sentiment. Cartographers' talk." She straightened. "All right. I accept it. Not because of your corpses, courier — though I will carry those too, you're right, they're real. Because if I burn it, I am doing exactly what the Committee does: deciding that some truths are too heavy for people to hold. I won't. People get to know what they are owed." She put out her hand, flat on the atlas, the way one swears on a book in court. "This is his office now. I'm taking it up."

Ansel put a hand over hers. Marguerite, after a breath, laid hers on top of both, and the three hands stacked on the leather cover looked, in the lantern light, like something being weighed and found sufficient.

* * *

They began that night, because Ilse did not believe in beginning tomorrow.

Ansel slept — truly slept, for the first time since Marguerite had known them, curled on sacking behind the presses with the deep, shameless exhaustion of a soldier after the war. Ilse unlocked the first press and showed Marguerite how to pull proofs of a single atlas page onto cheap paper, working copies, so that the original need never be handled again. The press demanded a rhythm: set the sheet, pull the bar, breathe, release. Ink, pressure, breath. The page of debts came up ghostly and reversed, Marthe Aubry's name printed backward like a reflection in dark water, and Marguerite found it beautiful, the way things are beautiful when they are finally being used for what they were made for.

While the proofs dried she opened her ledger to the back pages and ruled a new section properly, with a straightedge, because if the work was resistance it would still be done neatly. Columns: *Name. Last known residence. Promised, and by what instrument. Status. Source.* At the top of the page, where the republic's ledgers bore their stamp, she wrote instead, in letters she pressed hard enough to leave grooves through three sheets:

*Register of Persons Owed. Begun by M. Voss, enumerator. Continued by whoever keeps the maps honest.*

Behind her, the press thumped. Thumped again. Ilse working in the dark like a heart in a chest.

By the time the cellar's air went grey with approaching morning, they had copied four pages and entered thirty-one names, and Marguerite's hand ached in the good way, the way of a tool well used. She blew on the wet ink of the thirty-first name — *Aubry, Marthe, and household; promised protection, armistice art. xi; status unknown; source: Brandt atlas p. 47, Voss enumeration spring '98* — and it was then that the knock came.

Three blows on the wool-wrapped door at the top of the stairs. Paused. Three more.

Ilse froze at the press, bar half-pulled, ink to her elbows. In the silence Marguerite heard the cooper's yard above: boots, several sets, moving fast across frozen mud, and a voice she knew — a voice that had offered a pardon across a table one long night and been refused — saying, with terrible mildness:

"Pemberton is down there. I'd take it as a courtesy if no one made me break this door."
