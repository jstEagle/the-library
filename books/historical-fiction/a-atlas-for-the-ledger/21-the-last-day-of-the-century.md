## 21. The Last Day of the Century

The bells began at dusk, and they were wrong.

Ansel heard them from the window of the print shop cellar, where the light came down through a grate in the street above like something rationed. The bells of Saint-Aubin had always rung in thirds — the old pattern, the one every child of the city learned before they learned their letters. Now they rang in quarters, the new pattern, decreed by a ministry that no longer existed, ordered by men who had fled or burned, kept by a bell-ringer who perhaps did not know which republic he served anymore, or whether any remained to be served. So the sound that rose through the grate was neither old nor new but something broken between them, and Ansel stood listening to it with their coat already on, as though the bells were a summons and not a celebration.

Below the grate, in the street, people were carrying wood.

That was the other thing. Bonfires. The city had decided, with the particular stubbornness of cities, that even if the year was ending badly and the century with it, the ending would be marked. Ansel watched bundles pass beneath the grate: fence rails pried up, a chair with only three legs, an entire doorframe from some house that would never be whole again. A boy ran past carrying an armload of laths, laughing, and behind him came his mother with her arms full too, and she was not laughing, but she was walking fast, toward the fire, because that was what you did tonight. You walked toward the fire. It was the only direction left that everyone could agree on.

"Ansel." Marguerite's voice from below, from the workroom where the presses slept under their dust sheets. "Ilse wants you. She says it's time."

Time. For a year now everything had been time — time to move the atlas again, time to change the hiding place, time to copy another signature of pages before the wax cooled on the world. Ansel took the stairs down into the lamplight of the workroom, where Ilse Brandt sat at the long table with the ledger open before her, and Marguerite came after, and the three of them were briefly a government: one cartographer's daughter, one census taker, one courier whose office had died without ever sending word.

Ilse turned the atlas around so its spine faced Ansel. The leather had gone soft with handling now, a year of hands, a year of copying; forty-one pages had been duplicated in fair hand and hidden in six places Ansel knew of and three, he suspected, that only Ilse knew. What lay on the table was becoming a relic, and relics needed keeping, and keeping was a kind of surrender.

"The last pages are copied," Ilse said. "The register Marguerite is building has its spine." She tapped the atlas once, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Which means, courier, that your commission is discharged. Whatever promise you made to my father — I think you've kept it twice over. Once by bringing it. Once by not selling it to Ferro."

"Ferro," Ansel said, and could say nothing else for a moment. The river. The muzzle flash across the water. The smuggler standing up out of cover with his arithmetic finally done, holding the soldiers' eyes while two figures and a wrapped bundle went into the reeds. "Yes."

"He'd have charged you double for the sentiment," Ilse said, and there was almost a smile in it, the kind she allowed herself now, small and hard-won, like coins counted out one at a time.

Marguerite pulled a stool to the table and sat, and her hands — ink-stained at the knuckles always, the hands of a woman who wrote numbers for a living and names for a resistance — folded themselves flat on the wood. "There's more," she said. "Tonight of all nights, there's news."

* * *

The news was this: the republic had formally dissolved itself at noon. Not fallen — dissolved. There was a difference, Ilse explained, spreading the printed broadsheet on the table beside the atlas, and the difference mattered to people who dealt in paper. A republic falls when someone takes it. A republic dissolves when it admits there is nothing left to take. The Assembly — what remained of it, eleven men meeting in a rented hall because their chamber had been sold for timber — had voted the state out of existence with the same pens that had once voted it into being. Effective at midnight. All offices closed. All commissions void. All warrants, Ansel read with his heart going strangely quiet, null and unenforceable.

Null and unenforceable. He read his own name in the list of persons so relieved: *Pemberton, Ansel, formerly of the Office of Dispatches.* Formerly. The word did what a year of running had not done. It set him down somewhere.

"They'll reprint this by morning," Marguerite said. "Half the city will burn these in their bonfires tonight and never know what they're burning."

"And half will keep them," Ilse said, "and be right to."

Ansel looked at the broadsheet and then at his own hands, and found that he did not know what they were for anymore. That was the truth of it, the plain truth he had been outrunning since a border inn and a woman with too many questions: he had been a courier, and couriers existed only in transit, only in the space between sender and receiver, and both ends of him were gone now. The Office of Dispatches had died unmourned. The atlas was delivered. Even the warrants had expired like milk.

"So what am I?" he said, and heard how young the question sounded, younger than he was, young as the clerk who had once believed history happened to other people.

Marguerite answered without looking up from the broadsheet. "Tomorrow? Whoever you choose. Tonight—" she glanced at the window-grate, where the light had gone orange, firelight rising off the street like weather — "tonight the whole city is asking the same question. Come and see. We've earned one night of being nobody's."

* * *

They went up as the bells finished their broken pattern and the fires took hold.

The square had become a country of its own. Three bonfires, great and crackling, threw heat across the cobbles in waves that made the winter feel briefly like a rumor. People stood close enough to warm their faces, and the light moved over them — the baker with flour still in his hair, called out of his ovens by the bells; the widow Aubert who had hidden Ilse's press plates for a month in her cellar of pickled things; two soldiers of the dissolved army, out of uniform already, their coats looking suddenly like costumes. A fiddle somewhere. Children running between legs with torches too big for them, supervised by no one, because tonight was a night when supervision relaxed its grip.

Ansel stood at the edge of it all with a cup of hot wine pressed into his hands by a stranger, and let the strangeness wash over him. A year ago — less than a year — he had crossed this same city by back ways with a leather ledger against his ribs and his collar up, invisible by craft and by conviction. Invisible, and believing invisibility was safety. He had thought that if he made himself small enough, flat enough, sealed enough, the century would pass over him like weather over a stone. History was a thing that happened in assembly halls and border provinces, to ministers and generals and other people named Pemberton. His job was only to carry the paper between one elsewhere and another.

He looked at his hands around the cup. They had carried it. They had opened it in a chapel with soldiers' voices in the street outside, and read Elias Brandt's letter aloud while Marguerite listened with snow melting in her hair, and understood, line by line, that the atlas was not a map of the republic but of its conscience — every debt, every broken promise, every name still owed protection, written in the patient hand of an old man who had measured the distance between what states said and what they did, and found it worth mapping. They had refused Ferro's gold with these hands. They had held Marguerite's counter-census together, page by page, the register of the disappeared growing in the cellar like something planted.

And tomorrow—

"Look," Marguerite said softly, at his shoulder.

Down the street, a procession came: neighbors carrying an effigy on a litter, the way you'd carry a scarecrow to a field. It was dressed in a clerk's black coat, and its head was a paper sack with a face drawn on in charcoal — a face with a frown of official importance, seals hanging from a ribbon around its neck. Someone had written across its chest, in careful letters: *THE OLD YEAR.*

They fed it to the nearest fire. It went up in a bloom of sparks, and the crowd cheered, and the drawn face curled and blackened and was gone, and Ansel felt the heat of it on his cheeks and understood, with a laugh that surprised him by coming out ragged, that the city had burned his old self months ago and he was only now catching up.

"You're crying," Marguerite said. Not accusing. Recording. That was her way; she recorded even tenderness, entered it in whatever ledger she kept behind her eyes.

"It's the smoke," Ansel said, and she smiled, and let him have it.

* * *

Later — much later, when the fires had burned down to red architecture and the fiddle had gone home — Ansel climbed back down to the cellar and lit the lamp at the worktable and took out the thing he had carried longest of all.

The seals. Two of them, brass and horn, worn smooth by his palm: the great seal of the Office of Dispatches, and the lesser seal, the personal one, given to him at nineteen by a superior whose name was now on a dissolution broadsheet. He laid them side by side in the lamplight. Around them, faintly, the smell of the day's ink and the cold breathing down through the grate.

He had promised himself this act for a year, in the way you promise yourself a confession. But confessions need witnesses, or they're only talking. And so it was that Marguerite found him — she seemed always to find him, the census taker, the woman who counted things, who had once asked him one question too many and changed the shape of his life — coming down the stairs with two cups and the last of the wine.

She saw the seals. She sat. She waited. Waiting was also a thing she was good at; she had interrogated Ilse's suspicions and the secret police's patience with the same unhurried competence.

"I used to write reports," Ansel said. "Every week. To the Office. Condition of route, condition of cargo, condition of self." He turned the great seal over. Its face caught the lamp. "I kept writing them after the Office stopped existing. Did you know that? Mailed them to a building that had been sold. Habit, I told myself. Grief, Ilse said. I think it was prayer, actually. The only kind I knew how to make."

"And the last one?"

Ansel drew a sheet of good paper toward him — Ilse's paper, print-shop paper, clean — and dipped the pen, and wrote, while Marguerite drank her wine and the firelight flickered orange in the grate above:

*Final report of Ansel Pemberton, courier. To be delivered to: no office, no minister, no ministry, none of which exist. Addressed therefore to whoever finds it.*

*Condition of route: ended.*

*Condition of cargo: delivered. The atlas of Elias Brandt rests in the hands of his daughter, who keeps the maps honest, as her father instructed and as I promised. Copies circulate. The register of the disappeared grows weekly. The count is being taken, at last, of those the state preferred not to count.*

*Condition of self: formerly of the Office of Dispatches. Formerly invisible. Formerly convinced that history happens to other people. I was wrong about all of it, and the correction cost me everything inherited, and I find — this is the part I could never have reported to the Office, which would have filed it under irregularity — that I have been paid back in a currency I didn't know existed. Belonging. It does not come from nation or blood. It comes from one person asking one question too many, and staying for the answer.*

*This office closes willingly.*

He signed it, dated it — the last day of the century — and then, because a report must reach its addressee, he folded it and set it under the brick at the mouth of the cold stove, where the draft would find it eventually, or not, and either way it would have been sent.

Then he picked up the great seal, and the hammer was already waiting — Ilse kept tools the way she kept secrets, within arm's reach — and he struck it once, and the brass face split along its device, and the republic's emblem broke into pieces no herald could reunite. The lesser seal he fed to the stove's cold ashes, unbroken, just buried, a seed that would never sprout.

The sound of the hammer rang in the small room like a single honest bell.

"There," Ansel said. His voice was steady. His eyes were not. "It's done. I'm no one's courier now."

"No," Marguerite said. She reached across the table and turned his broken seal over, studying the fracture with a professional's interest, the woman who had spent her life reading documents. "You were never anyone's. That was the lie we both believed — you the sealed paper, me the counting pen. Tools don't get to choose." She looked up, and the lamplight was in her eyes, and the year was dying in the street above, and she was smiling the smile she saved for true entries. "We chose anyway. Both of us. That's the whole report, isn't it? Everything else is condition of route."

Outside, faintly, the first voices began to count down toward midnight, and somewhere a new bell — an old bell, rung newly — prepared itself.

Ansel blew out the lamp, because the firelight through the grate was enough, and because some things are better witnessed by the light they'll live in.

Above them, the century ended.

And in the dark of the print shop cellar, Marguerite Voss took his hand — not the courier's hand, not the clerk's, just his — and the last bell of the old world rang out over the city, and somewhere in the reeds of a frozen river a dead smuggler's arithmetic balanced at last, and morning, when it came, would come with no office to report to and no seals left to break, only the first blank page of a new count, waiting for whoever kept the maps honest to begin it.
