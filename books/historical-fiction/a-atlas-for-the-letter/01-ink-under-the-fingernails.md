## 1. Ink Under the Fingernails

There were three ways to be invisible in the counting house of Harrow & Lyle, and Quentin Vane had mastered all of them before his sixteenth birthday.

The first was posture. A clerk who stooped over his desk like a gull over a fish stall invited notice — someone might wonder if he slept, or wept, or plotted. But a clerk who sat with his spine straight and his shoulders settled became furniture. The second was rhythm: dip, scratch, blot; dip, scratch, blot. Men's ears forgave regularity anything. It was the irregular sound — a cough, a dropped pen, a laugh — that turned heads. And the third, the most reliable, was simply to owe nothing and own nothing and expect nothing, because in the whole history of commerce no one had ever looked twice at a man who wanted nothing from them.

Quentin Vane owed, owned, and expected almost exactly nothing, and he had long ago made his peace with that arithmetic. He was nineteen years old, ink lived permanently under the nails of his right hand like a second set of fingernails grown dark, and he believed — quietly, without bitterness, the way one believes in tides — that ledgers made the world turn.

People, in his experience, were unreliable. People got sick, married badly, drank, drowned, forgot. But an entry in the Harrow & Lyle debt-book was eternal. If it said that Jorunn Bekker owed four crowns eleven shillings for net-mending advanced in March of some forgotten year, then Jorunn Bekker owed four crowns eleven shillings until the company said otherwise, and the company never said otherwise, and the sun rose anyway. There was a comfort in that. The world was run from distant capitals by men whose names Quentin copied daily into the header columns — *Harrow & Lyle Trading Company, Registered London, Agents for the Northern Pearl Fishery* — and those men would never know Quentin existed, and this seemed to him not a tragedy but simply the correct arrangement of things. History did not belong to clerks. History belonged to whoever kept the books.

He kept them beautifully.

"Vane." Mr. Petch, the senior factor, stood at the end of the long room holding a ledger against his chest the way a bishop holds a psalter. "The Saltrow accounts. Are they current?"

"They are current, sir."

"Errors?"

"None since Tuesday, sir."

Petch nodded once, satisfied, and moved on. That was the entire conversation, and Quentin found it entirely satisfactory. Eleven words. Some men needed speeches; Quentin needed only to be correct.

Through the tall windows behind him the harbor lay flat and silver in the early light, and beyond the harbor the sea, and beneath the sea — Quentin did not let himself think about what lay beneath the sea, or rather he thought about it constantly but in the disciplined, quarantined way a man thinks about a wound that has healed crooked. His mother was down there right now. He could not see her, but he knew the shape of her morning: down at first light, before the other divers, walking backward into the shallows until the water took her shoulders, then gone for two minutes at a stretch while her skiff bobbed empty on the swell and the gulls screamed at her as though insulted by her absence.

Two minutes. He timed her sometimes, when he came down to help beach the skiff in the evenings. Two minutes and ten seconds was her longest, and she claimed she could do better but saw no reason to show off for fish.

* * *

At noon the clerks ate together on the loading step, and Quentin learned everything worth knowing in Saltrow by saying nothing.

"—heard my cousin say the war party's got the whole southern coast under requisition orders," said Tam Whitley, the youngest apprentice, through a mouthful of bread. "Gunboats. Real ones."

"There's always gunboats," said old Merrick, who had copied freight manifests for thirty-one years and had opinions about nothing else. "Last time they came for the herring tax. Gunboats are weather. They come, they go, the ledgers stay."

"The ledgers *stay*," Tam repeated, delighted by the phrase, and Quentin felt a small warm pride, as if Merrick had quoted scripture.

That was another thing Quentin believed: the counting house was permanent in a way the town was not. Saltrow smelled of tar and fish guts and, on certain winds, of nothing at all; its houses leaned against each other like tired drunks; its dock had been rebuilt twice in living memory and neither time well. But the counting house had slate floors and iron shutters and a fireproof cabinet from London, and inside that cabinet lived every debt, every indenture, every promise anyone in Saltrow had ever signed. You could burn the town and the books would survive it. Quentin took a real, quiet solace in this, though he was wise enough not to say so aloud on the loading step where the fishermen's sons could hear him.

"What's the company paying your mother for pearls these days?" Tam asked suddenly, aiming the question at Quentin with the innocence of the very young or the very careless.

Merrick snorted into his tea.

"Standard rate," Quentin said evenly.

"Half rate," said Merrick, not looking up. "Standard for the company. Robbery for everyone else. Same as it's been twenty years." He said it the way he said everything, in the tone of a man reading a freight manifest, and that was somehow worse than if he'd shouted it.

"The company provides boats," Quentin said. "And gear. And the market in the south—"

"The company provides half a boat," said Merrick, "and charges them for the other half. I've written the entries, boy. I know what I've written."

Quentin shut his mouth. This was the trouble with clerks: you couldn't argue with them, because they'd seen the numbers. He finished his bread, brushed the crumbs from his waistcoat with two precise swipes, and went back inside to be furniture.

* * *

His mother came up from the water at dusk with three oysters in her bag, two of which contained pearls, one of which was merely hungry.

They ate the hungry one.

Their house sat at the top of Diver's Stair, which was ninety-eight steps cut into the cliff between the harbor and the shacks above — Quentin had counted them as a boy during a fever, and the number had lodged in him like a splinter. From the single window you could see the whole harbor mouth, the counting house lantern, and, on clear nights, the lights of the company steamer anchored out past the point like a patient animal.

"Two today," Maren said, laying the shells on the table. She worked them open with a knife, her hands still wrinkled white from the water, her forearms marked with the faint pale scars of a hundred small encounters with coral and urchin spines. She was forty-three and looked older on land, younger in the water; Quentin had heard men say both and considered both wrong. She was simply Maren, the same in every element, exacting and dry-humored and impossible to surprise. "One good one. One the size of a peppercorn, which will buy us — let me guess, Petch's scale — a peppercorn."

"You could sell south," Quentin said, because he said it most nights, because it was nearly true. "Independent brokers in Corvane pay triple."

"And Corvane is nine days' sail, and a woman diving alone with a boat the company technically owns—" she tapped the hull visible through the window, "—would find her cargo inspected at the point, and her pearls 'held for assessment,' and her assessment lost. Your Mr. Petch invented that trick before you were born. I've watched it done to the Halvorsens." She held the good pearl up to the lamp, turning it. Her face did something complicated and brief — a flicker, quickly put away — and then went back to its usual wry calm. "Still. Pretty little thing. All that patience, wrapped around a bit of grit."

"That's how pearls work, Mother. The grit comes first."

"I know how pearls work, Quentin. I make them." She set it in the tin box with the others, which held perhaps forty in total, saved across years against some future she never described. "I meant people are the same. Something gets in. Hurts. They wrap it up nice and round and wear it on a string and call it jewelry."

Quentin, who had a piece of grit of his own wrapped somewhere deep and round and never discussed, changed the subject. "Tam Whitley says there are gunboats coming. Requisition orders, maybe. For the war."

Maren paused with her hand still on the tin box lid.

"Gunboats," she said. "For a pearl town? We're not worth a gunboat. We're barely worth a rowboat with a flag."

"The war doesn't ask what a town is worth. It just takes the accounting."

She looked at him then, a long look, the lamplight catching the salt still dried in the lines at the corners of her eyes. "You say that like it comforts you."

"It does," Quentin admitted. "If it's all written down, then it's all — ordered. Fairly distributed. Nobody suffers more than their entry says they should."

Her expression did the complicated thing again, longer this time, and he realized with a small cold start that it wasn't grief exactly and wasn't anger exactly, but some alloy of the two that had been smelting for ten years and never poured out. Ten years. Since he was nine. Since the *Providence Belle*, his father's diving sloop, came back to the dock towed by another boat, riding high and empty, with a page torn from its log flapping loose in the bailing bucket and no explanation anyone would give except "accident," and the company entered the outstanding debt of Edvard Vane, deceased, into the master ledger in fair round figures, and transferred the remainder of the indenture to his widow and son as was policy, and Quentin had cried exactly once, at the funeral, and had decided standing at the grave's edge that tears were not entries and therefore did not count.

"Well," Maren said at last, closing the tin box. "When the gunboats come, you can copy their names into your book, and I'm sure that will protect us all."

It was said gently, mostly. Quentin smiled the way you smile when you decline to argue with someone you love. "More tea?"

"Please."

* * *

He stayed late at the counting house that night — he often did; lamps cost oil, and oil cost money, and the counting house had both in institutional quantities that individual clerks were quietly permitted to borrow. The great room after hours was Quentin's favorite place in the world, though he'd have died before admitting it. Daylight belonged to the factors and their voices, but night belonged to the entries themselves. In the dark, with only his desk lamp making a yellow island in the blackness, the ledgers stopped being instruments of the company and became something closer to what Quentin privately believed they were: the true biography of Saltrow. Here was Jorunn Bekker's net, bought on credit in a hard spring. Here was the Halvorsen inspection fee. Here — and his pen slowed, here — was page 411, the Vane account, his own name in the family column, his father's debt carried forward year upon year like a stone rolled forward by hand.

Balance due: eighteen crowns, six shillings. It had been eighteen crowns, six shillings for as long as Quentin could remember, which was strange, when he thought about it — and he rarely let himself think about it, because interest compounded and debts grew, that was the first law of the books. Every other debt in the room grew. His father's sat frozen at eighteen crowns, six shillings, decade-still, like something dead.

A clerk's mind noticed such things and filed them under *curiosities*, next to the fact that the page's lower corner had been mended with a strip of paste and matching paper, professionally, by someone skilled — not the sort of repair apprentices did.

Quentin dipped his pen. Scratch, blot. Dip, scratch.

Outside, far out past the point, a light moved where no light should be — low, deliberate, traveling against the tide. Then another beside it. Then, faintly, borne over the water a full minute later, a sound he had never heard in Saltrow harbor in his life: the deep, unhurried pulse of a steam whistle. Once. Twice. A third time, long and flat, like a sentence being dictated to the dark.

Quentin set down his pen carefully, the way his father had taught him to set down tools — blade inward — and stood at the window with the ink still wet behind him, drying into permanence on the page.

The lights came on toward the harbor mouth.
