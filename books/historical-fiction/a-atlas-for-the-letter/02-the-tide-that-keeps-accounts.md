## 2. The Tide That Keeps Accounts

The tide turned an hour before the sun did, and Maren Vane was in the water before either of them finished making up their minds.

She went down the ladder of rocks at Sallow Point the way her mother had gone down it, and her mother's mother before that, which was to say without ceremony and with one hand on the wet rope and a curse ready in her mouth for the barnacles that had grown since last season. The water took her breath the way it always did — not cruelly, but like a creditor: *I'll have that back later.* She let it take it. Breath was the only thing she gave up for free.

Below her, the light came down in long grey columns, and the oyster beds spread along the shelf like a field of sleeping sheep. She worked by touch mostly, at this hour, feeling along the ridges for the heft of a shell worth opening, tucking each one into the net bag at her hip. Her hands knew the work so well they had stopped consulting her about it years ago. That left her mind free, which was not always a kindness.

Her mind went, as it did most dawns, to Aldous.

Eleven years now. Eleven years, and still the first thought underwater every morning was his, and the second was usually something practical, like *mind the urchins*, and the third was him again. Grief at depth was a strange companion. It weighed nothing down here. Everything weighed nothing. That was the mercy of diving, if you wanted to call it that — the sea suspended your sorrows along with your body, and you could almost pretend they were weightless instead of merely postponed.

*Almost*, said Maren, and pried another oyster loose.

* * *

Aldous Vane had drowned with a page of paper in his fist.

That was the detail everyone in Saltwreck Harbor knew and nobody in Saltwreck Harbor discussed, because it was too strange to be comfortable and too true to be forgotten. A man doesn't drown holding paper. A man drowns holding rope, or rock, or another drowning man, or nothing at all. But when they brought Aldous up off the Shambles — three days after the *Meridian's Pride* went down in the squall, tangled in her own anchor line — his right hand was closed around a leaf of ledger paper gone soft as skin from the salt, and his fingers had to be pried open one by one, which Maren had made them do gently, as though he might still be reading.

The factor's man, Odell — smooth Odell, silver-tongued Odell, whose handwriting looked like it had been raised in a church — had come to the house within the week, wearing the face men wear at funerals when they are also thinking about lunch. He expressed condolences on behalf of Harrow & Lyle. He noted, with regret, that Aldous's account stood open. Two hundred and forty crowns outstanding against the company, he said, plus the boat's lease, plus the advance against the season's catch that Aldous had drawn before the *Pride* sailed. In sum: Maren and the boy were bound to the company until the debt cleared, which at current pearl prices and current company rates would be — he consulted nothing, because he already knew — some nineteen or twenty years.

"Or the boy can take indenture now," Odell had said, kind as a knife. "Clerk's work. Clean work. It clears faster."

Quentin had been eight. He'd been sitting on the step outside the door, pretending not to listen the way children pretend not to be cold, and Maren remembered thinking: *he is listening, and it will go into him deeper than any of us knows.*

She had signed. What else does a widow with a child and a debt sign? She signed with her dive-name, the mark she used on bills of sale, three lines like gulls in flight, and Odell blotted it carefully, and thanked her, and left, and she had stood in the doorway hating him with a hatred so clean and quiet she hadn't even recognized it as hatred for another ten years. She'd thought it was just how she felt about arithmetic.

The page in Aldous's fist had gone into the sea chest with everything else of his, unreadable — the ink had bled to ghosts — but Maren had held it up to the lamp more times than she could count, those first years, trying to make the shadows say something. And once, once, she'd seen enough of the column figures to know what troubled her: the numbers were small ones. Small sums, close-set. Not the running total of a great debt. More like a tally of payments.

Like a record of a debt being paid off.

She'd told herself a hundred explanations since. That she'd misread the ghost-ink. That Aldous kept his own accounts separate from the company's. That a drowning man grabs whatever's near, and paper is near, on a ship, if you're a clerk-man who loved his books even as the sea closed over them — and Aldous had loved his books. He'd taught himself letters off cargo manifests, sitting on the dock with his lips moving. It was why Quentin could read before he could swim, an order of accomplishments the whole harbor found frankly alarming.

But explanations are cheap at low tide, and Maren had stopped believing most of hers around year six. What she believed now was simpler and uglier: Aldous had died with proof of something in his hand, and the company had known it, and the debt that bound her family had been written by the same hand that wrote so beautifully in church script.

She could prove none of it. Proof lived in the counting house, in the great debt-book behind the iron grille, and no diver's wife walked in there except to pay.

So she dove. Every dawn, every tide the weather allowed. She brought up pearls and the company claimed them at half price — *half*, for her, because her mark was bonded-mark, and bonded divers got bonded rates, and wasn't that the whole trick of it, that the ledger made its own justice and then charged you for the ink.

"Maren," she said to herself, thirty feet down, mouth full of regulator-less silence. "You are talking to oysters about fraud."

An oyster declined to comment.

* * *

She surfaced when the bag grew heavy, and sat on the flat rock warming herself in the first proper sunlight, shucking the catch open with a knife older than Quentin. Most shells held nothing. Some held the small seed pearls that were the bread of the trade. Now and then the sea, in one of its moods of careless generosity, put a proper pearl in a proper shell, round as a moon and cool as a lie, and Maren would look at it for a moment before dropping it in the tin — because every proper pearl was a day off the debt, and also never a day off the debt, because Odell's scale always found a flaw, and the flaw always halved the price, and the debt always breathed on.

There were others out on the water this morning. Old Sefa's skiff to the north, and the Kell cousins working the shallows, and young Brin Osta out past the point where her mother had forbidden her to go, which meant Brin was getting a hiding or a promotion, depending on the catch. Maren watched them and felt the thing she always felt, which was half love and half arithmetic: eleven boats, eleven families, all of them bonded the same way, all of them paying the same patient tithe, all of them keeping count in their heads because the company kept count everywhere else.

Divers kept tallies the way other people kept saints' days. It was in the blood. Sefa could recite what she'd paid in any given year going back thirty, and the Kells sang their debts at weddings like ballads, verses of crowns and seasons, everyone laughing and no one forgetting. When the company's man came around with his book, the whole harbor would nod along politely, and then afterward, over supper, the real accounting would happen — the oral one, the one passed mother to daughter, the one that said *no, we paid that in the spring of the yellow tide, I remember because Tomas broke his arm the same week and the doctor's fee went into the same remittance.*

Two sets of books, in effect. One on paper, owned by Harrow & Lyle. One in the heads of the women of Saltwreck Harbor, owned by nobody, and therefore by everybody.

Maren had never thought much about which set was truer. Paper had always seemed to her like weather — distant, official, something that happened above your head and decided whether you ate. But lately, and she couldn't have said what had changed except perhaps the slow accumulation of eleven years of small wrongs, she'd begun to wonder what would happen if the two books ever disagreed.

Which of them would the world believe?

She snorted at herself, loud enough to startle a gull. *The world.* As if the world had ever been asked to choose between Saltwreck Harbor's memory and a company ledger, and might, if pressed, come down on the side of memory. The world didn't even know Saltwreck Harbor existed. The world knew the harbor produced pearls, in the way a table knows it has a leg.

Still. She wrapped her hands around the tin and let the sun get at her shoulders, and she let herself think the thought she'd been circling for a month now, careful as a woman walking a wet deck:

If Aldous's debt was false — if Odell had written it into the book with his beautiful hand — then somewhere in that book, in the entries before the falsification, there was the truth. Settlements. Payments. Maybe even a final payment, a *paid in full*, entered fair and square by some clerk who didn't know he was writing the only honest sentence in the room.

And if the army requisitioned the town — and the gunboats had been sighted off the capes two days ago, smoke on the horizon like a bad weather sign, and the harbormaster had gone quiet in the way of men who've heard things — then soldiers would come for the ledgers. Everyone knew that was how it went now; it had gone that way in Corvel Bay, in Anselm's Reach. Armies wanted paper. Paper told armies who owed what to whom, who could be squeezed, who could be moved. The first thing a requisition seized was always the records.

Maren sat very still on her rock.

Because there was a thought forming, and it was a dangerous shape of thought, and it had Quentin's face on it. Quentin, who sat inside that counting house six days a week among the ledgers. Quentin, who could read a column of figures the way Brin Osta read water. Quentin, who was — and here her heart did the painful double-beat it always did, pride and fear braided together — invisible. Nineteen years old and invisible, a boy who copied other people's names into books and had never once understood that he might put his own name anywhere.

If anyone could get into that debt-book and find what Aldous had died holding —

"Maren," she said aloud, sternly, to the rock, the gull, the tin of pearls. "You are a widow with two hands and a mortgage on your own spine. You cannot afford treason."

But she counted the pearls anyway, the way she did every morning, marking them in her head against the number Odell's clerk would write down and the smaller, truer number she'd carry home in her skull where no requisition could reach. Forty-one. A good morning. Enough, at company rates, for maybe a week of freedom. At honest rates, a month.

She was still doing the sums when she saw the smoke again, farther in now, past the capes, riding low on the water where smoke shouldn't ride unless it was coming to stay.

Three columns. Grey, deliberate, unhurried.

Maren stood, and the tin nearly went into the sea, and she swore and caught it, and stood a moment longer with her heart doing something worse than arithmetic.

Then she began packing up her gear, fast, with the particular efficiency of a woman who has just decided something enormous and needs the ordinary motions of her body to buy her soul time to catch up.

Quentin would be at the counting house by now. Quentin, with his ink-stained fingers and his gentle belief that ledgers made the world turn.

"Well," said Maren Vane to the tide, hauling her bag onto her shoulder. "We'll see what it turns."
