## 3. Gunboats at the Harbor Mouth

The morning the gunboats came, Quentin Vane was copying the name of a man who had been dead for two years.

*Hollis Bramm, cooper, balance outstanding: eleven crowns, four shillings.* The ink was old-fashioned and brownish in the pen, the kind the company bought cheap by the barrel, and Quentin had long ago stopped wondering whether a dead man's debt was a debt at all or merely a habit the paper refused to break. Eleven crowns, four shillings. Somewhere in the capital — he imagined it as a single vast room with no windows, staffed entirely by men like himself but better fed — that figure existed now as fact. It would outlive Hollis Bramm's widow, his sons, the very cooperage where he'd once made barrels. Figures did that. People didn't.

"Your hand's gone crooked," said Mr. Petch from the next desk.

Petch was sixty, half-blind, and had been copying entries since before Quentin's father could swim, which in Maren's telling was roughly the age of three. He squinted across the aisle now with the expression of a man who considered bad handwriting a personal affront to civilization.

"The light," Quentin said. It wasn't the light. The light that morning came through the counting house windows low and strange, gilded at the edges, and kept flickering — though not like sun on water. More like sun on something moving.

"Hmph." Petch returned to his columns. "Light. In my day we copied by whale oil and were grateful."

In your day they also hanged men for less than I'm about to think, Quentin thought, and bent again over *Hollis Bramm, deceased, eleven crowns four*, and for one unguarded moment let himself imagine what the world would look like if all the ledgers of Harrow & Lyle were dropped into the harbor at once. He imagined the figures dissolving like sugar. He imagined the freed space where the debts had been. Then he imagined Mr. Odell's face, and the fantasy curdled, as it always did, into the ordinary dread of a clerk who needed his wages.

He was nineteen. He had been invisible for approximately as long as he could remember, and had come to regard invisibility less as a curse than as an accommodation, like a bad knee. Merchants walked past his desk without seeing him. Factors dictated over his head. Once, when he was fifteen, two company directors had conducted an entire argument about his mother's diving contract while standing close enough that their coat buttons brushed his shoulder — and neither had thought to lower his voice, because why would you lower your voice for furniture?

The flickering light got worse.

It was young Tam Wicker who first ran in from the dock road with his cap in his fist and his face gone the color of the ledger margins. Tam delivered messages, fetched lunches, and served generally as the counting house's barometer; when Tam went pale, weather was coming.

"Ships," he said. "Steamers. In the harbor mouth. Three of them. Grey ones."

"Coastal packet?" asked Petch without looking up.

"With guns, Mr. Petch. Great black guns sticking out the sides like — like teeth." Tam swallowed. "And soldiers on deck. Hundreds. Nobody signaled them. They just — came."

The counting house did not stop work, because the counting house never stopped work; that was rather its whole theology. But there was that particular silence that falls over a room when every man in it is listening while pretending not to. Pens slowed. A drawer closed too softly somewhere.

Quentin set down his pen. He crossed to the window — a liberty permitted to clerks only because clerks were invisible, and being invisible meant your movements required no explanation — and looked out over the tiled roofs toward the water.

Three steamers sat in the harbor mouth, exactly as Tam had promised: grey-hulled, black-funneled, riding the swell with the heavy stillness of things built to be somewhere else. Smoke stood above them straight as masts. Along their rails, small and neat as punctuation marks, stood ranks of soldiers. And beneath the ships' bows, unmistakable even at this distance, lay the shadows of guns — not fishing guns, not signaling guns, but the kind of ordnance that made the sea itself seem briefly like a mistake the navy was prepared to correct.

"They're flying the Crown ensign," said a voice behind him. It was Odell, the factor, arriving with the news already half digested, as if he'd eaten it for breakfast. Odell was a compact man with a tidy beard and eyes like two beads of sealing wax; he had a way of looking at people that suggested he was totaling them. "War steamers. Well." He adjusted his cuffs. "Well, well."

"War?" Petch's pen had finally stopped. "There's no war here."

"There is a war," said Odell, "somewhere else. Which is precisely how wars prefer to arrive." And he smiled — a thin, professional smile, the smile of a man watching an opportunity come up the harbor on the tide — and went out through the front doors to meet it.

Quentin stayed at the window a moment longer. Out past the mole, far below the grey hulls, a single sail was coming home: one of the divers' boats, late off the beds, his mother's boat, the sail patched at the leech with canvas she'd sewn herself during a winter of bad pearls. It seemed very small. It seemed, in fact, to be sailing directly toward the teeth.

He got his coat.

* * *

By noon, the whole town had arranged itself along the harbor front in the manner of towns everywhere when history arrives: too close to be safe, too curious to leave, packed shoulder to shoulder among the fish carts and the bollards. Quentin found Maren at the edge of the crowd, her hair still wet from the morning's last dive, salt drying white along her forearms. She did not ask what he was doing away from his desk. She simply moved her elbow so he could stand beside her, which between the two of them constituted a lengthy conversation.

The middle steamer had lowered a boat. It came across the bright water with twelve oars rising and falling in perfect agreement, and in the stern sheets sat a single officer, upright, gloved, wearing a hat that must have cost more than the counting house roof.

"That's an expensive hat," Maren said quietly.

"Mm," said Quentin.

"When a man wears an expensive hat to a poor town," Maren said, "he's either selling something or taking something. And sellers don't bring guns."

The boat grounded. The officer stepped ashore onto planks laid for him by sailors who had clearly done this before, in other towns, and walked up through the parted crowd with an unhurried stride, reading the town as he came — the roofs, the drying nets, the faces — the way Quentin read a ledger page: quickly, completely, and without affection.

He stopped at the customs house steps, produced a folded document sealed with a wafer of red the size of a plum, and unfolded it with two crisp motions, as though the paper had been drilled as thoroughly as the soldiers.

"My name is Colonel Elias Ashe," he said. His voice was not loud, and yet it carried, because he had the gift — Quentin would learn this about him later, and dread it — of speaking as though silence had been ordered specifically for him. "By authority of the War Office and the Emergency Powers Act, I am instructed to inform you that this port, its harbor, its warehouses, and all property within it are hereby requisitioned for the service of the realm."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through barley. Someone near the front — old Greta Sool, who was deaf enough to shout — demanded, at volume, *requisitioned for what?*

"For the war," Colonel Ashe said, with the mild patience of a schoolmaster repeating himself to a slow pupil. "The war which, I am given to understand, some of you believe is happening elsewhere. It is happening elsewhere. That is precisely why your harbor matters. Coal, timber, rope, grain, hulls, wharves, and labor will be taken as needed, at rates of compensation to be determined. Resistance will be treated as sabotage. I regret the necessity of saying these things plainly." He paused, and something almost gentle crossed his face, which somehow made it worse. "I am told this is a hardworking town. Good. Hardworking towns make the fewest difficulties."

He folded the proclamation. Behind him, sailors were already carrying crates ashore, and Quentin saw with a lurch that two of them wore the brass badges of the Army Records Corps — a branch of the service whose entire function, he knew from the trade circulars that crossed Odell's desk, was the inventory and transport of documents. Paper soldiers. Men whose weapon was the manifest.

"All commercial records are to be surrendered for audit," Ashe continued. "Ledgers, charters, registers, indentures, deeds. Every book, every page. Owners will be compensated for the paper." A dry beat. "Not for what is written on it."

Beside him, Maren had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Quentin knew his mother's frights the way he knew tides; this was the other thing, the stillness she went into before a deep dive, when the whole body agrees at once to stop arguing with the water.

"They'll want the debt-books," she said, barely above the sound of the waves.

"Of course they will," said Quentin. "Records are property. Property gets requisitioned. It's in the act, probably."

"Quentin." Maren turned and looked at him fully, sea-grey eyes in a face browned by thirty years of salt, and for once she looked not at her son the clerk but at the clerk himself, which unsettled him more than the guns had. "Whose names are in those books?"

"Everyone's," he said. "Everyone in the parish, going back forty years. Debts, payments, contracts, indentures—"

He stopped. The word sat between them on the air like a gull that had landed and would not leave.

*Indentures.*

His own family's papers lived in those books. The Vane indenture — his grandfather's signature, his father's mark, the terms by which three generations of Vanes had been bound to the Harrow & Lyle Trading Company for debts incurred, discharged, renegotiated, and incurred again — all of it written in the same brownish cheap ink Quentin himself had spent years copying forward, entry after entry, year after year, without ever once — he realized it now with a sensation like stepping off a stair that isn't there — without ever once actually *reading* the pages he copied.

Somewhere in that counting house was a line recording whether his father's final dive had cleared the family account or sunk it deeper. He had copied around it a hundred times. He had never read it. Reading, apparently, was for the people who owned the books.

On the water, the third steamer sounded its whistle — a long, flat, industrial note that rolled across the harbor and set every dog in town barking at once, and under it, faintly, came the rattle of the first requisition wagons being unloaded.

"We should get back," Quentin said. "Odell will want the ledgers made presentable. There's a protocol. There's always a protocol."

Maren was still looking at the ships. "Quentin," she said. "When they take those books — do they take the truth in them, or just the paper?"

"I don't know what that means."

"No," said Maren. "I don't expect you do. Not yet." And she turned back toward the diver's quarter, leaving him standing in the crowd with the whistle dying away and the expensive hat receding up the customs house steps.

* * *

The counting house received the army the way a body receives cold: first a shiver, then a fever, then a grim adjustment.

They came at four o'clock, precisely, in the manner of everything Colonel Ashe did. Two sergeants, six privates, and a lieutenant with a clipboard, and behind them, carried in like relics, a dozen empty iron-bound chests stenciled with the Records Corps crest. Odell met them at the door rubbing his hands together in a manner he presumably believed conveyed patriotic cooperation and which in fact conveyed the joy of a man greeting business partners.

Quentin was ordered to fetch the current year's debt-book from the fireproof cabinet. He did it mechanically — shelf, key, leather spine, weight in his arms — but somewhere between the cabinet and the lieutenant's table the book became, in his hands, a different object entirely. An hour ago it had been work. Now it was a cell full of prisoners: Hollis Bramm's eleven crowns, Widow Ferris's lien on her own net-loft, the Okafor brothers' boat mortgage, and — bound into the same covers, sharing the same paper, breathing the same air as every debt in town — whatever the truth was about his father.

"Careful with that," said the lieutenant, who needn't have bothered; Quentin held it the way you hold a sleeping child you're about to give away.

They catalogued by candlelight. The sergeant — a broad, grey-tempered man named Ivo, whose name Quentin learned from the way the privates addressed him and not from any introduction, since sergeants did not introduce themselves to furniture — checked each volume against a master list and called out numbers in a voice like a door closing. *Debt register, 1861 through '68. Charter of incorporation. Indenture rolls, volumes one through nine.* Nine. Quentin tried to remember which roll held the Vanes. Third? Fourth? He genuinely did not know. He had filed his own family the way he filed everyone: alphabetically, sight unseen.

"You're the copyist," Sergeant Ivo said suddenly, appearing beside him with the abruptness of weather.

"Yes, sir."

"Fast?"

"Adequate, sir."

Ivo grunted, which might have meant anything, and moved on.

By midnight the chests were full and locked and stacked in the requisition office — the old charter room, now with a soldier posted at the door and an official seal on the keyhole. Odell signed the surrender inventory with visible relish, as though handing over the ledgers were itself a promotion. The lamps were doused. The counting house stood dark for the first time in living memory, and Quentin walked home through streets patrolled by men who saluted the harbor instead of the church, and lay awake in his narrow bed listening to the steamers' furnaces breathe offshore like enormous patient animals.

History had come. Not elsewhere — here. Not to capitals, not to newspapers he copied excerpts from, but to his own desk, to his own cabinet, to the exact shelf where his family's freedom had been gathering dust in brown ink while he dusted around it.

* * *

He found his mother on the rocks below the diver's quarter at dawn, mending a net she did not need mended, which was how she prayed.

"They've got everything," he said. "Charter, registers, indenture rolls. Sealed in the charter room under guard."

Maren's needle paused. "All nine rolls?"

"All nine."

"And nobody read them first?"

"Read them? They *inventoried* them. Weight, count, condition. That's all anyone's ever done with them." He heard his own bitterness arrive a sentence too late, as usual.

Maren set down the net. She looked out at the harbor, where the grey ships sat exactly where they had sat yesterday, as though they had grown there overnight, permanent as rocks, temporary as nothing. When she spoke, her voice was quiet in the way the sea is quiet an instant before it changes.

"Your grandfather signed that indenture with a cross, because he couldn't write. Your father paid it off — I know what he brought up the last five seasons, I counted every shell myself, and I know what the company credited, and the two numbers don't agree. I've known it for years, Quentin. I just couldn't prove it, because proof lives on paper." Her jaw tightened. "And now every scrap of that paper belongs to the Crown, sitting in a locked room, in the keeping of men like Odell — and Odell has wanted our family bound to that company since before you could walk."

"So what do we do?" Quentin asked. It was the question of a clerk. Even now, even here, some part of him reached for procedure: petition, appeal, extract, certified copy. There must be a form.

Maren turned and looked at him for a long moment, and what passed over her face was not grief, though grief lived underneath everything she did like groundwater. It was decision. It was the look she wore stepping backward off a boat into black water.

"We get it back," she said. "Whatever page says what my husband paid. We get it back before they ship it off to some capital where our names become somebody's filing problem forever." She picked the needle back up. "You work in that building, Quentin. You're furniture to them. Furniture can walk anywhere it likes."

Quentin stood on the cold rock with the tide coming in around his boots and felt fear settle over him — real fear, the specific kind, the kind with a shape and a date — and underneath the fear, faint but undeniable, something else stirred. Something that felt disgracefully like relief. As if a window he hadn't known was painted shut had just, with a small cracking sound, opened.

The steamers blew for the morning watch. Down the coast, the first requisition wagons rolled toward the counting house, and the town woke to find its history had been confiscated overnight — all of it, every page, except what lived in the heads of divers and the son of a drowned man who had spent his whole life copying other people's names, and had never once written his own.
