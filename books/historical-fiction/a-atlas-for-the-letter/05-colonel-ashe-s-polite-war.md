## 5. Colonel Ashe's Polite War

The counting house smelled different with soldiers in it. Quentin couldn't have said at first what was wrong, and then he understood: the smell of ink had been joined by the smell of boot oil and pipe tobacco and, faintly but unmistakably, of men who had been marching since before dawn. The Harrow & Lyle Trading Company had always prided itself on smelling like nothing at all — like money, which is to say like absence.

Colonel Ashe arrived on the third morning after the gunboats, and he did not arrive the way armies usually arrive. There was no clatter, no shouting. He came up the steps of the counting house at half past nine, precisely when the clerks were meant to arrive, wearing a grey coat brushed to a soft shine and an expression of mild, paternal regret, as though he had come to inform them that a beloved aunt had taken ill.

"Good morning," he said to the room at large, and the room — eleven clerks, two factors, and old Pell the book-keeper, who had worked at Harrow & Lyle for forty years and had assumed nothing could surprise him — said good morning back, because politeness is a reflex even in the presence of artillery.

"I want to thank you all," Ashe continued, removing his hat, "for your patience during what must be a disorienting week. The war effort requires this port. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise, nor by pretending it requires anything less than everything: your harbor, your warehouses, your ledgers, and — for a time — some portion of your labor." He smiled. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "I am told the company's books are thorough to the point of obsession. Excellent. Obsession is exactly what we need."

Quentin stood at his desk near the back with his pen still in his hand, ink drying on the nib, and felt something turn over inside him. A week ago he had believed — had built his whole quiet life on the belief — that history happened elsewhere, in capitals, between men whose names appeared in dispatches. Now a colonel stood four desks away from him talking about ledgers as if they were cannon.

"You'll find me reasonable," Ashe said. "I have no interest in cruelty. Cruelty is inefficient. What I require is order, and order, gentlemen, is what you do here every day already. We are not so different, the army and the trading house. Both of us keep accounts. The difference is only what we count."

He let that sit in the room like smoke. Then he began assigning quarters.

The counting house itself would house a garrison detachment — thirty men, sleeping among the desks, their packs slung over chairs where clerks had once hung coats. The warehouse loft would take another company. Every able-bodied man in the town between sixteen and fifty would be registered for harbor labor within the week: dredging the channel, loading coal, building the fortifications at the harbor mouth that would, in time, close it.

"Registration begins tomorrow," Ashe said, replacing his hat. "I encourage full cooperation. Cooperation, I find, is almost always rewarded." And then he left, as quietly as he'd come, pausing at the door to nod at old Pell and say, "Fine shelves," as though complimenting a garden.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Tam Weller, who copied bills of lading and had never in his life had an original thought out loud, said, "Fine shelves," in a strangled voice, and the whole room broke into the kind of laughter that is mostly fear looking for somewhere to put itself.

* * *

They came that evening with their packs and their bedrolls, and the counting house became a barracks by lamplight. Quentin watched from the doorway, having been told — politely — to collect anything he needed and return at seven sharp.

It was strange, seeing soldiers among the desks. One of them had set his boots on Pell's stool. Another had draped a shirt to dry across the great iron safe, and the sergeant supervising the quartering kept glancing at the safe with the frank professional interest of a man estimating weight. Quentin thought of the debt-books inside it — his father's name in one of them, or what remained after whatever Odell had done — and his stomach tightened.

"You live here?" asked a voice, and Quentin turned to find a young soldier setting down his pack, round-faced, maybe a year older than Quentin himself, with the raw-knuckled hands of someone who had been a farm laborer a month ago.

"I work here," Quentin said. "Clerk."

"Clerk." The soldier said it the way other men said *sailor* or *priest* — a trade belonging to another country. "Lucky. Dry work. I'm Ivo," he added. "Sergeant Ivo, if you're being formal, which the colonel says we're always being."

"Quentin Vane."

"Well, Quentin Vane." Ivo looked around the counting house, at the rows of desks, the ranked shelves, the ledgers standing spine-out like a choir. "You know what's funny? Back home I couldn't read. Still can't, much. My mother could, though. She used to read the rent book out loud to my father every quarter-day like it was scripture, and he'd swear at it, and she'd say, *you can't swear at a book, Aldous, it doesn't hear you.*" He grinned. "First place I ever saw a book matter more than weather."

Something about that lodged under Quentin's ribs. He carried it upstairs with him to the room over the chandlery where he boarded, and found Maren waiting, which was unusual enough; she rarely came into town after dark, and never without reason.

She sat at his small table with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't drunk. Her hair was still damp from the evening tide. She looked up when he came in, and her eyes went through him first to the window, where the harbor lights of the gunboats burned steady and wrong.

"The harbor labor lists start tomorrow," she said.

"So you've heard."

"Everyone's heard. Tomas wants to know if divers count as harbor labor." She said it flatly, without inflection, the way she reported tides. "And I want to know what you're going to do about the ledger."

Quentin sat down across from her. Outside, a patrol went by, boots unhurried on wet cobbles. "Mother—"

"It's our freedom in that book. Your father's paid debt, and ours. And it's sitting in a strongbox with a soldier drying his shirt on top of it."

"And how would you have me get it? They've sealed the requisition office. Colonel Ashe has—" He stopped, because his mother was watching him with that expression she wore at sea, the one that meant she had seen the shape of a thing beneath the water long before he did.

"Then get inside the office," she said. "Not the strongbox. Not yet. Just inside. You've been invisible in that place five years, Quentin. Invisible men are worth more than pearls right now."

He wanted to argue. Instead he heard himself say, "They'll need clerks. To copy the requisition registers. Ashe said order, he wants everything accounted for, and they don't have enough of their own people who can write."

"There," said Maren, and drank her cold tea at last, as though sealing a bargain. "There's my son, the volunteer."

* * *

Volunteering, it turned out, required less courage than lying. Quentin simply presented himself at the requisition office the next morning, when the registration queues stretched down the quay, and told the harried lieutenant in charge that he had five years' experience in double-entry book-keeping and knew the company's filing system better than anyone alive, including — he did not add, though he thought it loudly — anyone the army had brought with them.

The lieutenant looked at him with the gratitude of a drowning man thrown a rope. "Name?"

"Vane. Quentin Vane."

"You're hired, Vane. Sit there. Copy. Don't stop copying until I tell you to stop, and God help you, don't make errors."

So Quentin sat, ten feet from the strongbox, and copied the names of his neighbors into labor registers. That was the vertigo of it, the thing he could never afterward explain properly: he had spent five years making himself invisible in that building, and now invisibility had become a weapon, and he had no idea whether he was strong enough to hold it.

He wrote *Tomas Ferro, diver* and watched Tomas, three queues ahead, give the registering officer his name in a voice pitched carefully neutral. He wrote *Tam Weller, bill-clerk,* and *Aldous Brine, cooper,* and forty other names, and each stroke of the pen felt like something — a small theft, a small treason, a small prayer.

At noon Sergeant Ivo brought the clerks bread and salt fish on tin plates, and stopped beside Quentin's desk.

"Vane. Counting-house boy."

"That's right."

Ivo set the plate down and stood a moment, looking at the register. "Good hand," he said. "My mother had a hand like that. Round letters. Letters that look like they'd be kind if they talked." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on Quentin a moment longer than lightness warranted, and then he moved on down the row, and Quentin ate his lunch tasting copper.

That afternoon Colonel Ashe toured the office.

He came through with Odell at his elbow — Odell the factor, silk-collared even now, wearing the new expression of a man who has decided to be on the winning side of whichever side wins — and paused at each desk as though inspecting fruit. When he reached Quentin, he stopped.

"Vane," he said, reading the register's flyleaf. "You were in this building before us, I believe."

"Yes, sir. Five years."

"Five years." Ashe picked up a completed page and held it to the light, and Quentin's heart performed a slow, deliberate somersault, because the page was innocent, only names and trades, and yet. "Excellent work. You know, Mr. Vane, most men in your position would resent us. It's the natural feeling. And yet here you are, helping." He set the page down and smiled the smile that reached his eyes. "Either you're the rarest kind of patriot, or you're the sensible kind of man. In my experience they look identical from the outside, and it hardly matters which is true, so long as the work gets done."

"No, sir," said Quentin, meaning *no, sir, it doesn't matter,* and hating the smoothness of his own voice.

Ashe moved on. Odell lingered half a step behind, and his gaze crossed Quentin's — brief, flat, and knowing in a way that made the room tilt. *He remembers me,* Quentin realized. *The son of the drowned diver. He knows exactly whose hands are copying these pages.* And Odell said nothing, and walked on, and that silence was louder than any accusation.

When they had gone, Quentin discovered his own hand was shaking so badly he had spoiled a line. He blotted it, copied the last entry again, and understood, with the clarity of cold water, two things at once.

The first was that Odell knew. Knew what Quentin might come looking for, knew what the missing settlement page would prove if it ever surfaced — and had known it for years, since the day Quentin's father drowned with paper in his fist.

The second was that this made Quentin dangerous, and that Odell would move against him the moment he gave cause — and that none of it mattered, because tomorrow morning he would walk back through that door and copy more names, and wait, patient as tide, for his chance at the strongbox.

Correction — the queue was shorter than yesterday. Quentin noticed it at the end of the second week, the way you notice a tide turning: not by any single wave but by the mark on the pilings. Fewer men reporting. More empty stools at the chandlery. And on Friday, a notice nailed to the requisition office door in the round, kind-looking hand of a clerk:

*By order of Col. Ashe, commanding: the port of Harrow Reach is closed to civilian traffic effective immediately. All vessels wishing clearance must apply in writing.*

Closed. Not requisitioned, not regulated — closed. The word *evacuation*, which until now had lived only in rumors, appeared in the notice's final line, followed by a phrase that made Quentin read it three times: *priority passage will be arranged for persons of record.*

Persons of record.

He stood in the doorway with the drizzle settling on his shoulders, and old Pell came up beside him, squinting at the paper under the lamp.

"Persons of record," Pell repeated, and gave a dry laugh with no humor in it anywhere. "Fifty years I've kept books, boy, and I'll tell you the one truth in them free of charge. Sooner or later, every list becomes the world. Whoever's on it, is. Whoever isn't—" He tapped the notice twice and shuffled back inside to his warm desk and his ordered columns, and Quentin stood alone in the rain, understanding that somewhere in that building, behind the strongbox steel, a berth list was already being drawn up —

— and that he had just volunteered to copy it.
