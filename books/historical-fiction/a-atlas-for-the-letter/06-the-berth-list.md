## 6. The Berth List

The requisition office smelled of lamp oil and other people's certainty.

Quentin had been copying in it for six days now — Colonel Ashe's clerks being, in his private estimation, worse penmen than any he had ever supervised, and that included a one-armed factor at the Saltwick branch who wrote exclusively with the paper tilted at an angle suggesting he expected it to bolt. The army men pressed too hard on their nibs. They wrote as though the paper owed them money.

Perhaps, Quentin thought, dipping his pen for the four hundredth time that morning, everything did.

He had learned a great deal in six days, most of it by saying nothing. He had learned that Sergeant Ivo took his tea bitter enough to strip barnacles and drank it standing up, as if sitting were a luxury he hadn't earned. He had learned that the two young lieutenants who shared the corner desk argued nightly about a woman named Constance, whom neither appeared to have met. And he had learned that when Colonel Ashe walked into a room, the room rearranged itself around him without being asked — chairs shifted, voices dropped half a tone, and even the lamp flames seemed to stand straighter, as though they'd been drilled.

Ashe came in now, at half past ten, with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been late for anything that mattered.

"Master Vane."

"Colonel." Quentin rose. One did not salute; one was not sure what one did, so rising seemed safe.

"You have good eyes, I'm told."

"I have spectacles, sir."

Ashe almost smiled. It was a thin thing, his humor, like frost on a window — you could see through it clearly to something much colder. "Good. Then you can read this out. My clerk has handwriting like a spider drowning, and I'll not have numbers misread in front of the factor."

He laid a single sheet on the table before Quentin. It was longer than most pages, written in two columns, and headed in Ashe's own hand — precise, unornamented, faintly beautiful in the way of things made by people who did not waste motion:

*Manifest — S.S. Meridian — Priority Berths.*

"Read," said Ashe.

Quentin read.

"Factor Odell, Mister Halloran of the company, Mrs. Halloran, two children. Surgeon Pryce. Mister Fenwick, senior clerk. Mister Ashby, junior clerk—" Quentin paused. Ashby was a fool who filed invoices upside down, but he was also the nephew of a director in the capital, which apparently outweighed competence by several tons. "—Mister Quill, harbor master. Ensign Doyle. Ensign—"

"Continue."

He continued. Name after name. Some he knew: the company men, their wives, their children, their children's governesses. A naval paymaster. Two engineers whose names he didn't recognize and whose presence he couldn't explain until he saw the notation beside them — *essential, gun-carriage contract* — and understood that they were not people on this list so much as skills wearing people about.

The list ran to thirty-one names. Thirty-one berths.

Thirty-one, in a town of some four thousand souls.

Quentin finished reading. The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a soldier was whistling a tune that kept losing its way, like a man walking home drunk.

"Well?" said Ashe.

"It is a very complete list, sir."

"It is," Ashe agreed, "a very short list." He took the page back, folded it once along its crease with the exactness of a man folding a map of somewhere he intended to go. "You're wondering why I've had you read it aloud."

The truthful answer — *because you wanted to watch my face, sir, and here I am watching yours* — seemed inadvisable.

"No, sir."

"Liar," said Ashe, without heat. "Good. A clerk who doesn't wonder is furniture, and I have enough furniture." He looked at Quentin for a long moment, the way he might look at a bridge before deciding whether to march troops over it. "The Meridian arrives within the week. She takes these thirty-one, and the company's records — every ledger, every deed, every bill of lading in that warehouse — and she goes south before the blockade closes. After that, Master Vane, no ship larger than a fishing skiff leaves this harbor for the duration."

"The duration of what, sir?"

"The war." Ashe said the word the way other men said *Tuesday*. "The town stands. The town always stands; towns are where we store the things armies need. What the town does not do is leave. You will make fair copies of that manifest — three copies, your best hand — and you will speak of it to no one. Is that understood?"

"Yes, sir."

"Splendid." Ashe turned to go, then stopped, as though remembering something trivial. "Your mother. Maren Vane. The diver."

Quentin's heart performed a small, cold maneuver, like a coin dropped down a drain. "Yes, sir."

"She's spoken of. The divers' quarter thinks highly of her." Ashe's face gave away nothing whatsoever. "A woman people listen to. Those are worth knowing about." And he left, boots ringing on the boards, taking the warmth out of the room with him like a man carrying off the last lamp.

Quentin stood a while among the drowned-spider handwriting and the arguing lieutenants and the whistling outside, and thought: *thirty-one.*

Then he sat down, and began, with hands that only shook a little, to make his fair copies.

* * *

He read the list eleven times that afternoon, and by the twelfth reading he had stopped seeing names and started seeing arithmetic, which was worse.

That was the thing nobody warned you about ledgers — or lists, which were only ledgers that had learned to be cruel. Numbers were patient. You could look at them all day and they would simply wait, holding their meaning out flat in their palms like stones, until you finally picked them up and felt how heavy they were.

Thirty-one berths. Factor Odell, who signed falsified settlements with a flourish like a rooster's tail. Surgeon Pryce, who had once treated Quentin's split knuckle for free and was therefore, by the strange mathematics of kindness, the person on the list Quentin minded least. The Halloran children, five and seven, who had done nothing to anyone except be born into the right family, which was, Quentin supposed, the entire principle under discussion.

And not on the list: Maren Vane, who had dived twenty years for a company that paid her half price and called it generosity. Not Tomas, her brother, who mended nets and raised his daughter Liss on herring and stories. Not Liss herself, eight years old, who could name every boat in the harbor by the sound of its engine and had once, memorably, told a customs officer that his hat was stupid with total sincerity. Not old Signe, not the Widmark brothers, not the four hundred souls of the divers' quarter who had spent their lives pulling wealth out of the sea floor and handing it upward, hand over hand, until it reached a list like this one — where it became thirty-one names, none of theirs.

Lives priced like cargo. No — that wasn't quite right, and Quentin was honest enough with himself to notice it wasn't. Cargo got weighed carefully. Somebody cared about the accuracy of cargo. On this list the weights were wrong, and everybody aboard knew it, and the wrongness was not a mistake but a method. Rank first. Debt second. Skill third, if the skill could be fitted into a gun carriage. People fourth, insofar as they were attached to any of the above.

His family, Quentin calculated, ranked somewhere below the governess. Below the governess's trunks, possibly.

He copied the list a third time, and his pen slowed, and he found himself doing something he had never done in nine years of clerking: he let his mind wander while his hand worked. His hand, loyal servant, went on producing perfect letters — *Ensign Doyle, essential, Mrs. Pryce, essential* — while the rest of him drifted out over the harbor, over the grey water, doing sums that no column could hold.

What was a berth worth? A place on a ship, a passage south, survival. Say that was everything — say a berth was life itself, whole and indivisible. Then thirty-one lives were being saved and four hundred were being stored, like grain, against the war's appetite.

But suppose you divided differently. Suppose a berth was worth one mother, or one child, or one net-mender who happened to be the only father figure a girl had ever known. Suppose you started weighing people not by rank but by — what? Need? Love? The number of people who would come apart if they were gone?

There was no column for that. There was no column for anything that mattered. That was the discovery, and it arrived in Quentin with the quiet, ringing finality of a ledger balancing: *the books are accurate about everything except the truth.*

He thought of his father, then — unexpectedly, painfully. Aldous Vane, who had gone down with a ledger page in his fist, gripping it hard enough, Maren always said, that they'd had to pry it loose. Drowning with paper. Believing, down at the very bottom of the world, that paper would save him.

Quentin set down his pen.

"Vane." Sergeant Ivo was standing over him, tea in hand, watching with those flat grey eyes that missed less than they let on. "You've written *essential* twice in the same entry and crossed one out badly. Colonel doesn't like bad crossings. Makes him think someone changed their mind."

"Sorry, Sergeant."

Ivo looked at the crossed-out word a moment longer than he needed to. Then he moved on down the row of desks, and Quentin breathed again — slowly, and not entirely steadily.

* * *

He found Maren at the tide pools behind the divers' quarter, prying limpets off the rocks for supper, because the company's half-price pearls bought little else these days and pride bought less.

She listened to the whole of it without pausing — the Meridian, the thirty-one, the blockade, the manifest with its careful columns — cracking limpets from their shells with a small stone tap, tap, tap, like a clock counting down something.

"Not us," she said at last. Not a question.

"Not us. Not Tomas. Not Liss."

Tap. Tap.

"There'll be a second ship," Quentin said, and heard immediately how it sounded — the voice of every factor who had ever promised a diver fair prices, warm and reasonable and hollow to the floorboards. "After the war, perhaps. When the records—"

"When the records come back." Maren stood, brushing rock-grit from her knees. Her hair was coming loose from its braid the way it always did by evening, silver threading through the brown, and her hands — Quentin had never really looked at his mother's hands before, he realized; they were mapped with old cuts and the pale scars of coral, hands that had held more of the sea than any list would ever hold. "Quentin. Your father believed in the second ship too. He believed in the company's promise the way other men believe in God, and it held him underwater at the end." She handed him the bucket of limpets, matter-of-factly, as though handing him her grief to carry for a moment. "You'll take these home. And you'll tell me tomorrow everything you hear about that ship — every name, every number, every time they change the list. Because they will change it. Men like Ashe always change the list."

"How do you know?"

"Because," said Maren, turning back toward the water, where the evening tide was coming in soft and relentless over the pools, filling everything it touched, "the sea changes its list twice a day, and even the sea gives warning. Men never do."

* * *

That night Quentin lay awake in the narrow room above the counting house — quarters now, technically, though the army had not troubled to evict its own clerk from his own bed — and listened to the garrison settling around him like weather. Boots on stairs. A laugh, cut short. The whistling soldier again, finding his tune at last, somewhere past midnight: a slow southern song about a girl and a river, played over and over as though the whistler were trying to wear a hole in it.

Thirty-one names. He had copied them until he knew them by heart, which meant he now carried the list inside himself, in the one place no requisition could reach. *Factor Odell. Mister Halloran. The governess.* He could recite them in the dark like a rosary, and he did, and somewhere around the twentieth name he understood what was happening to him: he was learning the list the way you learn an insult. Keeping it. Turning it over. Filing it where it would keep.

Below him, through the floorboards, he could smell the counting house — ink and dust and lamp oil, the smell of his whole working life. And beneath that, fainter, the salt of the harbor coming through the shutters, and beneath *that*, if he was honest, the memory of Maren's voice: *they will change the list.*

He was still awake when the knock came — two soft raps at the counting-house door, past two in the morning, when no honest boot in this town had business moving.

Quentin sat up. Downstairs, a lamp flared. He heard Sergeant Ivo's voice, low and flat, and another voice answering — and then footsteps on the stairs, quick ones, and a fist against his door.

"Vane." It was Ivo. "Get dressed. Colonel wants the manifest amended. Tonight."

"Amended how?"

Ivo paused, and through the door Quentin could hear him breathing, could picture the flat grey eyes narrowing at the wood between them.

"Downward," he said.
