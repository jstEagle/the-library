## 7. Salt and Promises

The tide came in before dawn, as it always did, indifferent to armies. Maren Vane stood at the edge of the shallows with her knife belt buckled and her diving hood rolled down around her neck, and she watched the steamers' lanterns burn out on the black water like a string of fallen stars.

Three days now since the gunboats had come. Three days of soldiers in the lanes, of the counting house flying a flag it had never flown, of her son coming home each evening with ink still under his fingernails and something new and frightened behind his eyes that he thought he was hiding well.

The other divers were already gathering. She could hear them — low voices, bare feet on wet shingle, the clink of shells being dropped into baskets without much hope of finding anything worth finding. The company claimed half of everything they brought up anyway, and lately the company's half had grown, the way the company's half always grew, by arithmetic no diver had ever agreed to.

Maren had called them here before first light, and they had come. That was the thing about the diving families, the thing the merchants in their dry wool coats had never understood: you did not need paper to summon them. You needed only a promise made in front of witnesses, and the sea itself for a notary.

Old Sef was there, of course, though he hadn't dived in six years. He sat on his upturned skiff like a king on a throne someone had forgotten to take away, his hands folded over a belly that had once been hard as coral. Beside him sat his daughter-in-law Petra, who could hold her breath longer than any man on the coast and said so at funerals. The Aldous brothers were there, all four of them, standing in a row the way they always stood, as if they might be inspected. Little Nell Okafor, sixteen and already better at reading the water than her father had ever been, perched on the breakwater with her legs dangling, pretending she wasn't listening, which meant she was listening harder than anyone.

"Thank you for coming," Maren said.

"We didn't come," Sef rumbled. "We were already here. Tide's ours before it's theirs." He nodded at the steamers. "Whatever else has changed."

There was a murmur of agreement, and Maren felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight for three days. It was true. The army could requisition the harbor. They could seize the ledgers, quarter soldiers in the counting house, put names on lists. But the tide came in when it pleased, and the people who read it had never needed anyone's permission.

"I'll come to the point," she said. "Quentin told me what he saw in the requisition office. There's going to be one ship. One. And it won't be taking us."

Nobody gasped. That was the worst part, Maren thought. Nobody gasped. They had all known, the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the wind, long before any clerk writes it down. Nell stopped swinging her legs. The Aldous brothers looked at each other in their row, which was as close to speech as they came in public.

"The berth list is drawn up by rank," Maren went on. "Company men first. Officers. Factor Odell, I'm told, has two berths reserved, one for himself and one for his hat trunk." A grim laugh went around the shingle at that. "Then favored clerks. Then creditors who can prove their claims. We are not on it because, according to the books, we don't exist except as debts. And our debts are owed to the company, and the company is being evacuated along with everything else it owns. Which includes, if you follow their arithmetic, us."

"They can't evacuate people like crates," Petra said.

"They can do whatever the list says they can do," said Sef. "That's the whole trick of lists, girl. Been saying it for forty years. Why do you think I never learned my letters?" He patted his belly contentedly. "Nothing written down about me means nothing true can be written down about me."

"You're a fool, old man," Petra said fondly.

"I'm an unlisted fool. There are worse things."

Maren let them have their laughter, thin as it was, because laughter was also a kind of tally. You kept count of the days you could still laugh. Then she raised her hand, and the shingle went quiet, because Maren Vane raising her hand was not a common thing and everyone knew what it cost her.

"I'm going to tell you something my husband told me," she said, "the night before he drowned. And then I'm going to ask you for something no one has asked you for in your whole lives."

She had rehearsed this speech a hundred times in the dark of her kitchen, and now, standing in front of them, she threw most of it away. That was the difference between paper words and spoken ones, she thought. Paper words stayed where you put them. Spoken words moved with the water.

"You all keep tallies," she said instead. "Don't look at the sand. I know you do. My mother kept one. Her mother before her. When the company wrote that Aldous Tom owed nine crowns for his boat repair, somebody in this town remembered it was four. When they wrote that the Okafor family was three seasons behind on their lease, somebody remembered the lease was paid the year the fever came through, paid in full, in pearls, and the factor at the time pocketed the difference and wrote it down as arrears." She looked at Nell. "Your grandmother remembered. She told my mother, and my mother told me, and I'm telling you now, so it doesn't die with me."

Nell had gone very still on the breakwater.

"That's what a tally is," Maren said. "It isn't paper. Paper gets requisitioned. Paper burns, or gets torn out, or gets carried off on a ship by men in good coats. A tally lives in mouths. As long as one of us is breathing, the count is true. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Every debt they claim we owe — somewhere in this circle, someone remembers the truth of it. Between all of us, we remember everything. We have *always* remembered everything. That is why they fear us, though none of them would ever say so. A man with a ledger can be robbed. A woman with a memory cannot."

Sef had stopped smiling. The Aldous brothers had stopped looking like they were awaiting inspection. Even the tide seemed to be holding its breath.

"Now," Maren said. "The asking."

She took a breath, and dove.

"There is a cache," she said. "Pearls. Not company pearls — *our* pearls. Everything we've skimmed off the top for thirty years, everything we've hidden in the place only divers know, everything the books say never came up from the bottom. My husband started it. I've kept it. Some of your own kin have added to it, whether you knew it or not." She saw Petra's eyebrows go up and pressed on. "It's enough. It's enough, if we're clever and if the army wants what I think it wants, to buy berths. Not just for me. For us. All of us, or near enough. The company's records are going on that ship, and Colonel Ashe wants the war paid for, and wars are not paid for in promises. Wars are paid for in pearls."

Silence, but for the water working at the stones.

Then Sef began to laugh. It started deep in his unlisted chest and rolled out across the shingle until his eyes were streaming, until Petra smacked his arm, until even the Aldous brothers cracked, one after another down the row like dominoes.

"Oh, that's beautiful," Sef wheezed. "Thirty years of thieving from the company — pardon me, thirty years of *correcting the record* — and now we sell it back to them at full price. Maren, girl, your husband is dancing in his grave right now, and I mean that in the best way."

"He'd want a cut," Petra said.

"He'd want the whole cache and a ship of his own and Odell's hat trunk thrown overboard," Sef said. "Aye. Let's do it."

But it wasn't that simple, and everyone knew it wasn't that simple, and so they talked while the sky went from iron to pearl — a word nobody used, that morning, without a small private smile.

Petra worried aloud that the cache would be counted as company property the moment it surfaced, and she was right to worry; they argued through three schemes for moving it, two of them mad, one of them merely dangerous. The Aldous brothers, speaking in their odd relay fashion — one beginning a sentence, the next finishing it — pointed out that the soldiers counted heads at the dock gates now, and where would they even find a colonel willing to bargain with divers? It was Nell, finally, who said the thing that stopped them all:

"My gran used to say the company keeps its promises the way the sea keeps footprints."

"And your gran," said Sef, "died rich in memory and poor in everything else. So we make them put it in writing." He said the word *writing* the way other men spat. "If they want our pearls, let the great Harrow & Lyle Trading Company sign for them, in ink, with a colonel's name on it. Let the ledger serve us for once. There's a justice in it that would make a saint weep."

Maren watched them argue and plan and quarrel and laugh, these people who owned nothing that any book acknowledged, and she felt the grief she had carried for twenty years shift its weight inside her — not lighter, exactly, but differently balanced, the way a boat rides different when the cargo is finally stowed right. Eirik had died clutching a page that proved their freedom, and she had spent two decades hating the paper for it. But paper had never been the enemy. Forgetting was the enemy. And her people had never once forgotten.

They dispersed as the sun cleared the headland, each to their tasks, carrying the plan the only way it could safely travel: mouth to mouth, breath to breath, unwritten. Nell lingered on the breakwater until the others were gone.

"My gran's tally," the girl said quietly. "The Okafor lease. Is it really true?"

"It's true," Maren said. "I heard it from my mother's own lips, and she heard it from yours."

Nell nodded slowly, and Maren saw something settle in the girl's face — the particular gravity of a person accepting an inheritance. Sixteen years old, and already a keeper of the count.

That evening, Maren walked home past the requisitioned counting house, where lamplight burned late behind shuttered windows and a sentry stamped his feet against the cold. She paused in the lane, just long enough to memorize the position of the guard, the number of horses, the hour the officer's boy came out for a bucket of swill.

Because there was one thing she had not said on the shingle, one thing that was hers alone and would stay hers alone until she knew how Quentin's errand had gone: the cache could be traded for berths, yes.

But the pearls were also the only leverage on earth that could make a man like Odell sit down at a table and answer, under witness, for what he had torn out of a ledger twenty years ago.

And Maren Vane had waited twenty years to hear him say it.
