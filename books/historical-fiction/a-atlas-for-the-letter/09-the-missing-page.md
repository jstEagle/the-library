## 9. The Missing Page

The requisition office smelled of tallow candles, wet wool, and the particular sourness of men who had been awake too long and were proud of it. Quentin had been copying for six hours. His fingers ached in the old familiar way — the ache he'd carried since he was nine years old, when his father first set him on a stool at Harrow & Lyle and told him that neatness was a kind of honesty.

*Neatness is a kind of honesty.* His father had said it while balancing a column of pearl weights, and young Quentin had believed it the way children believe the sun will rise: completely, without examination.

Now he copied requisition manifests by candlelight for an army that had taken everything, and he wondered whether his father would have found that funny or simply sad.

"Vane." Sergeant Ivo stood over him, holding a lantern too close to the page. "You've transposed two entries. Barrel staves, forty-one, not fourteen."

Quentin looked. He had. In eight years of clerking he could count his errors on one hand, and this made eleven.

"My apologies, Sergeant."

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to the quartermaster who'll be looking for thirty-seven staves that don't exist." Ivo set the lantern down but didn't leave. He was a broad man with a conscript's slouch and an officer's frown, which is to say he wore the army like a coat that fit badly and he knew it. "You were company clerk before all this?"

"Since I was eleven, sir."

"Hm." Ivo chewed on something — tobacco, or a thought. "You'll be here tomorrow?"

"Unless you requisition me elsewhere, Sergeant."

A sound that might have been a laugh escaped him and was immediately recaptured. "Get some sleep, Vane. Paper doesn't tire, but paper-men do."

He left. Quentin sat alone among the seized ledgers of Harrow & Lyle, stacked in ranks along the walls like soldiers awaiting orders, and felt his heart begin its slow, traitorous drumming.

Because that was the thing about being invisible: nobody watched the invisible man work. And Colonel Ashe's requisition office needed a clerk who knew the company's hand, its abbreviations, its maddening habit of recording debts in shillings and payments in pounds so that every sum required conversion and every conversion required checking against the original debt-book. Quentin had volunteered for exactly this reason. Maren had ordered it. *Find our page,* she had said, her voice flat as a becalmed sea, which was how he knew she was more frightened than she'd ever let him see. *Find what your father paid.*

And now, at last, everyone else had gone to bed.

* * *

The debt-books lived in a locked cabinet, but the cabinet key hung on a nail behind Ashe's desk because Colonel Ashe did not believe in thieves. He believed, he had announced at supper the second night, in order. *Men steal when they believe nothing belongs to them,* he'd said, carving a chicken with surgical calm. *Give them a place in the order of things and they become honest out of vanity.* The officers had laughed. Quentin, refilling wine, had thought: *You have never been poor, Colonel, and it shows.*

The lock turned without complaint. Inside, the ledgers waited in their calfskin bindings, spines stamped in gold leaf that had faded to the color of old teeth. Quentin knew them the way other men knew their own hands. Debt Register A through F, 1838 to present. The Vane family indenture was recorded in Register C, under the letter V, in the year his father signed it — Quentin had copied that entry himself a hundred times in practice, had dreamed in its phrasing:

*Elias Vane, diver, indebted to Harrow & Lyle Trading Co. for advance of £14, gear, and boat share; to be discharged upon delivery of pearl weight equal to £42 gross, per terms of settlement.*

Per terms of settlement. Which meant there was a settlement page. A single sheet, somewhere in the correspondence files or appended to the register, recording the day Elias Vane walked into the counting house dripping harbor water and laid out pearls worth fifty-one pounds — nine pounds past his debt, as Maren swore it, though the company had claimed only forty-two had ever arrived.

One page. That was all that separated the Vanes from freedom papers they'd never known existed, from proof that his father had died a free man swindled rather than a debtor drowned in arrears.

Quentin lifted Register C from the shelf. It fell open naturally to the V section, the binding worn soft there by years of the same hands seeking the same names. He found the entry. He read it twice, three times, mouthing the words.

Then he turned to the back of the volume, where the settlement appendices were bound — pages sewn in chronological order, each one initialed and dated.

There was a gap.

Not an empty page. A *gap*. The stub of a torn leaf, ragged at the gutter where someone had gripped and ripped with intent. The stitching around it was disturbed, threads pulled loose like whiskers. Whoever took it hadn't been careful. They'd been certain no one would ever come looking.

Quentin stared at the stub for a long time. Outside, a sentry coughed. Somewhere down the hill a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, or at the general condition of the world.

"No," he said aloud, quietly, to the ledger. "No, no, no."

He checked Register B, in case the appendix had been misfiled. Then D. Then he went through the loose correspondence boxes, folder by folder, his candle burning lower, finding bills of lading, wage schedules, a complaint about weevils in the company biscuit that Odell had answered with three paragraphs of magnificent pomposity —

Odell.

Quentin sat back on his heels.

*Factor Odell.* Cyril Odell, keeper of the counting house, arbiter of half-price pearls, a man built like a well-fed parson with a smile to match. Odell who had patted eleven-year-old Quentin on the head and said, *Your father was a good worker, boy, shame about the arithmetic of the sea.* Odell who had signed the receipt for the company's papers when the army seized them, standing beside Colonel Ashe, already transformed — Quentin saw it now with terrible clarity — not from factor into prisoner or bystander, but from factor into *adviser*. Civilians didn't survive requisitions by accident. They survived by making themselves useful.

Useful how?

Quentin pulled the correspondence box back toward him and began reading Odell's letters differently. Not for content. For hand.

He knew that hand the way he knew his mother's face. The tall, narrow letters with their affected little flourishes on the capitals, the *O*s closed with a small inward spiral like a snail retreating into its shell. Odell had taught Quentin his own copybook hand, correcting his slants with a pencil, saying, *Again, boy, again — a clerk's letters are his reputation.*

Quentin found a letter dated four days after his father's death. It was addressed to the Harrow & Lyle head office in the capital, and it concerned, of all things, a shipment of dried cod. But Quentin wasn't reading content anymore. He was reading the *O* in *Odell*, signature at the bottom — the spiral shell, the narrow spine, the flourish drawn with the confidence of a man who has never once feared an audit.

Then he found another letter, dated the week before the gunboats came. Odell to the company's insurers, regarding "certain irregularities anticipated in the event of military seizure." Irregularities *anticipated*. As if the requisition had been known before the gunboats anchored. As if Odell had been preparing the books for inspection — and preparing himself.

Quentin's hands were shaking. He set the candle down before he set something on fire prematurely, which he suspected was a crime even in wartime.

*Think,* he told himself. *Be the clerk. Be neat. Neatness is a kind of honesty.*

So he did what he had done ten thousand times: he made a record. On a scrap of wrapping paper he noted the register, the date range of the appendix, the position of the torn stub between the Aldous settlement and the Wren settlement. He described the disturbed stitching. He wrote down the dates of the two Odell letters and where he'd found them. His hand steadied as it moved — the old comfort of the work, ink going down clean and straight, the world reduced to things that could be written and therefore held.

But this time the writing didn't comfort him. This time every neat line was a wound report.

His father. Drowned with a ledger page in his fist — Maren had said it a hundred times, and Quentin had always imagined it was a page of *debt*, some final demand Elias had clutched as the water took him. But what if it wasn't? What if his father had gone into the water holding the settlement itself — proof he was free — trying to bring it home? What if the page had been torn out *before*, and Elias had stolen it back, and the sea had kept it for him?

Or worse. What if Odell had torn it out years ago, quietly, and the theft had nothing to do with the army at all? What if the requisition was just weather, and the real storm had been blowing through that counting house for a decade, and Quentin had sat in the middle of it sharpening pencils?

He thought about Odell's hand on his head. *Good worker, boy.* The warmth of it. He had *liked* Odell. Odell had given him sweets at Midwinter and once defended him when a warehouse foreman accused him of miscounting — defended him loudly, magnificently, in front of everyone.

Had that been kindness? Or had that been a man tending his instrument? You kept your best knife sharp. You kept your best clerk loyal.

"The arithmetic of the sea," Quentin whispered, and heard it now for what it must always have been: Odell blaming the ocean for numbers Odell himself had cooked.

* * *

He blew out the candle when the sky went grey, because a lit candle after dawn was a confession. He locked the cabinet, returned the key to its nail, squared the manifests he'd been assigned, and left a note for the day clerk in his best hand — *neatness is a kind of honesty* — reporting progress on the stave discrepancy. Forty-one, not fourteen. Even now. Especially now.

Maren was waiting for him in the alley behind the cooperage, where the divers' families had begun gathering in ones and twos, trading news the way gulls traded scraps. She read his face before he got within ten feet. Nineteen years of practice; she could read him at parade distance.

"You found it," she said. Not a question.

"I found where it was."

Her jaw tightened. Around them the morning sounds went on — hammers, gulls, a soldier whistling something tuneless near the quay — indifferent as ever to the business of one family's ruin.

"Torn out," Quentin said. "Stitching cut. Years ago, I think — the binding's settled around the gap like it's been there a long time." He hesitated, then made himself say the rest, because his mother deserved unsoftened truth the way she deserved bread: daily, and whole. "I think Odell took it. His hand is on the correspondence around it. And Mother — there's a letter to the insurers, before the gunboats ever came. He *knew*. He was getting ready."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. A woman who had spent twenty years diving into cold water had learned patience the way monks learned prayer.

"That man," she said finally, "gave you sweets."

"Yes."

"He gave your father a debt that was already paid." She looked out toward the harbor, where the evacuation ship rode at anchor, black-hulled and patient as a coffin. "Paper can be torn, Quentin. Remember that. Whatever he tore out — he couldn't tear it out of everyone."

But Quentin, walking home through streets full of soldiers who saluted nobody, kept seeing the ragged stub in the gutter of Register C, and thinking less like his mother and more like what he was: a clerk, born and bred, who knew in his bones what every bookkeeper knows.

A missing page isn't silence. It's a sentence with a hole in it — and somewhere, sooner or later, the man who cut it out will try to fill that hole with something worse.

He was still turning it over when he rounded the corner by the chandlery and nearly walked into Cyril Odell himself, resplendent in a new coat, standing in the morning light as though he owned it.

"Why, Quentin Vane," Odell said, smiling his parson's smile. "Just the clever young man I wanted to see."
