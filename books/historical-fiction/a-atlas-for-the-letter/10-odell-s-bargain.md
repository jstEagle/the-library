## 10. Odell's Bargain

The summons came the way all of Odell's summons came: written in his own immaculate hand, folded twice, and delivered by someone who pretended not to know what it said.

Quentin found the note tucked under his inkwell when he returned from the evening ration queue. *Mr. Vane — my office, eight o'clock. Bring nothing. Tell no one. O.*

"Bring nothing," Quentin read aloud to the empty clerk's room. "Tell no one." It had the flavor of an invitation to a conspiracy, which, he supposed, was exactly what it was. He considered telling Maren. He considered telling Ivo, who by now hovered at the edge of Quentin's days like a dog that had smelled something on the wind and couldn't decide whether it was dinner or death.

He told no one. The note had said so, and Quentin Vane had spent nineteen years obeying things that were written down.

* * *

Odell's office occupied the corner of the requisition building with the good light and the good view — harbor on one side, burning-lamp streets on the other — and Odell himself occupied the chair behind the desk the way a man occupies a fortress he has only recently taken and does not entirely trust to hold. He was smaller than Quentin remembered from the counting-house years, when the factor had been a voice from the inner room, a signature at the bottom of rejections. Up close he had soft hands and eyes like two coins left out in the weather: valuable once, now going green at the edges.

"Mr. Vane." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit. Tea?"

There was tea. Actual tea, in an actual pot, with leaves visible floating in it like small drowned things. Quentin could not remember the last time he'd seen tea outside the officers' quarters. He sat, and accepted a cup, and hated himself for how eagerly his hands closed around the warmth.

"You're wondering why you're here," Odell said.

"The note didn't say."

"No, it wouldn't." Odell smiled faintly, as if Quentin had passed some small examination. "You copied the berth list."

It was not a question, and Quentin's cup paused halfway to his mouth for precisely one heartbeat too long before continuing its journey.

"I see I'm right," Odell said, pleased. "Don't look so alarmed, boy. I copy things too. It's how I've made my living. You copy the berth list; I copy everyone who copies the berth list. We are, in a sense, colleagues."

"We're not colleagues," Quentin said. "I'm a clerk. You're —"

"A factor. Yes. Was." Odell turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a small fastidious gesture. "The army doesn't recognize factors. Or clerks. Or debt. Colonel Ashe has no use for any of it — he'd burn the ledgers himself if they took up space needed for gunpowder, and sleep beautifully afterward." He leaned back. "Which is why men like you and me have to look after ourselves."

Quentin set down the cup. His heart was going hard against his ribs, but nineteen years in a counting house teaches a person to keep their face still while their insides do arithmetic. "What do you want, Mr. Odell?"

"Straight to business. Good. I always liked that about your father."

The name landed in the room like a dropped weight. Odell watched him take it, and Quentin understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that this was deliberate — that every word tonight would be placed as carefully as entries in a ledger.

"Your father," Odell repeated, gently, "was owed a settlement. Paid in full, three years before the *Kestrel* went down. I know because I wrote the entry. I also," and here he turned his cup another quarter-turn, "removed it."

* * *

Later — much later, walking home through streets that smelled of tar and requisitioned horses — Quentin would try to remember whether he had felt surprise. He did not think he had. That was the terrible part. Some part of him, some quiet bookkeeper buried deep behind his breastbone, had known for years that the page was missing for a reason, that pages did not tear themselves, that debts did not outlive settlements without help. Knowing it and hearing it said aloud were different things, though. Hearing it was like discovering a sum you'd suspected was wrong and finally carrying the column: the answer was worse than the suspicion, but at least the columns balanced.

"Why?" he asked. His voice came out steady. Small miracle.

"Because your father's settlement threw off the quarter's figures." Odell said it as if explaining rain. "Harrow & Lyle reported debt as income to the home office — deferred income, technically. Every diver who paid off his indenture early made my quarterly statement look sick. Your father paid early. So I moved the payment forward into the next ledger year, and then, when the auditors came through Greyharbor, I moved it rather further than that." A thin smile. "Into oblivion. It was very tidy work, if I say so myself. I've always been proud of it."

"You destroyed his freedom papers," Quentin said. "For a quarterly report."

"For a *career*, Mr. Vane." For the first time something real moved behind the coin-green eyes — something old and hungry. "Do you know what it is, to start as an office boy copying bills of lading and end as factor of the whole Greyharbor station? Do you know what that costs? Your father wanted freedom from a ledger. I wanted freedom *into* one. We are not so different."

"We are entirely different."

"Mm." Odell refilled his own cup. Steam curled up between them like something escaping. "Well. Be that as it may. Here is my proposition, and I'll ask you to hear all of it before you spit at me, because it is, I flatter myself, generous."

Quentin heard it. All of it. He sat with his hands flat on his knees and listened the way he'd been trained to listen — for figures, for errors, for the small lie hidden in the long column.

One berth. Guaranteed. Not subject to the cuts Ashe kept making, not ranked by debt or usefulness, but sealed — Odell had the authority, as civilian adviser, to add names to the list, and no one questioned the adviser's paperwork, least of all the adviser. One berth for Quentin Vane. And in exchange: the divers' pearl cache — yes, Odell knew about the cache, Odell knew about everything, the divers' grandmothers gossiped to soldiers' wives and soldiers' wives gossiped to factors — and Quentin's silence regarding a certain missing page, a certain falsified debt, a certain dead man whose papers had gone conveniently to oblivion.

"And your mother," Odell added mildly, "keeps her opinions to herself. She's already half the town's conscience. If she starts preaching falsified ledgers in the fish market, Ashe will want proof, and I'd rather the proof stayed where it is. Which is nowhere."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then there is no berth. For you or anyone named Vane." Odell spread his soft hands. "The list gets cut again tomorrow, I expect. Blockade's closing. Names come off, Mr. Vane; that is the whole of naval arithmetic. Yours comes off easily enough. A stroke of the pen." He smiled his thin smile. "I've always been good with a pen."

* * *

Here is what Quentin did not say, sitting in that warm room with the good light: *my mother has spent thirty years underwater pulling shells open for half their worth; my uncle Tomas has a daughter with a cough that turns her ribs into a barrel; there are forty families in the divers' quarter who have never held a piece of paper that said anything good about them.* Here is what he did not say: *one berth is my mother's life.* Here is what he did not say, because he could barely stand to think it: *one berth might be worth more than the truth.*

He almost accepted.

He wants, in memory, to dress the moment up — to claim he thought of his father's drowned hand clutching a torn page, of the town's faces in the ration queue, of justice itself standing at his shoulder in the lamplight. He does not. What he remembers is the tea, still warm in his cup, and the arithmetic running without his permission: *one berth, guaranteed, versus nothing, for certain.* Odell had chosen his bait well. Not money. Not advancement. Just the single most precious object in Greyharbor, wrapped in paper and tied with a condition.

"How do I know you'd honor it?" Quentin asked at last, which was, he realized afterwards, the sound of a man negotiating the price of his own soul.

Odell laughed — genuinely, warmly, almost fondly. "You don't. But consider: I've kept your father's secret for six years. Keeping things is what I do. Burning things is what other people do." He drew a sheet toward himself and began, with evident pleasure, to write. "I'll draw up the berth certificate tonight. You have until tomorrow's tide to bring me word about the pearls. Not the pearls themselves — I'm not a fool, I don't go near that beach — just word. Where. When. My people will handle the rest."

The pen scratched. Quentin watched it move across the page and thought, absurdly, of all the thousands of hours he had spent watching pens move across pages, making other people's lives legible, and how it had never once occurred to him that a pen could be a knife.

"One more thing," Odell said, without looking up. "Your mother. Does she know you're here?"

"No."

"Good boy." He finished writing, sanded the page, folded it, and held it out. "Take it. Consider it a token of my confidence. And Quentin —" the first time he'd used the given name, and it landed wrong, familiar as a hand on the shoulder from a stranger — "your father was a good man. I want you to know that. I falsified him anyway. That is simply the world. The world is a ledger, and somebody has to keep it, and the keepers eat first."

* * *

Quentin walked home the long way, along the sea wall, because the direct route passed the requisition office and he could not bear to look at it yet.

The certificate sat inside his coat like a coal that hadn't decided whether to burn him. One berth. Guaranteed. His mother's name, in Odell's confident hand, safe from every future cut of the naval pen. He took it out twice under the streetlamps and read it both times, and both times his chest did something complicated and painful, the way it does when a thing you want desperately arrives wearing clothes you're ashamed of.

He tried the argument on, the way you try on a coat in a shop window's reflection. *The pearls were never theirs. The company claims them anyway; Odell claims them either way; at least this way the claiming buys a life.* It almost fit. It fit the way ill-fitting boots fit — walkable, for a while, if you didn't think about where you were going.

And underneath the argument, quieter, was the other thing, the thing he'd carried since the counting house, since chapter upon chapter of copied names: the knowledge of what the divers' quarter was. A neighborhood built on unwritten tallies. Forty families whose entire history lived in grandmothers' mouths and mothers' memories, because nobody with a pen had ever bothered to write them down except when writing them down meant owning them. The pearls weren't a treasure hoard. He'd seen the cache once, years ago, when Maren let him hold one — a grey pearl the size of a thumbnail, warm from her pocket. She'd said, *That's Sela Jonnsdottir's dowry. That's old Piet's funeral. That's four winters of my life.* The cache was the quarter's memory, rendered in nacre. Selling it wasn't theft. It was selling the only history those families owned that no one had requisitioned.

And buying it with silence. Silence about his father. About the page.

He stopped at the sea wall and looked out at the black water, where the blockade ships would be, somewhere past the point, closing like a hand.

"Gemini," he said aloud — his father's name, spoken to the sea the way Maren spoke it — "what would you have done?"

The sea, being a sea, declined to enter negotiations. But Quentin found, standing there, that he already knew the shape of the answer, and it frightened him less than the certificate in his pocket, and more. His father had died holding paper. Quentin did not intend to live the same way — bought, bound, filed.

He got home late. The house smelled of kelp broth and lamp oil. Maren sat at the table mending a diving suit, needle flashing, and she looked up when he came in and looked at him for a long moment — the way she looked at water before a dive, reading what was underneath.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me a berth. One berth. Guaranteed. For the cache, and for silence about Father's page." Quentin put the certificate on the table between them, face up, like a card he'd been dealt and didn't want. "I didn't say yes."

His mother put down the needle. He braced for anger, or worse, for relief — but she only looked at the paper, and then at him, and her face did something he couldn't read at all.

"Sit down, Quentin," she said quietly. "There's something I should have told you years ago. Something I've been keeping better than any ledger ever could."

She turned the certificate over, blank side up, and reached for the lamp.
