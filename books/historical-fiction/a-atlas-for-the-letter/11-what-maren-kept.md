## 11. What Maren Kept

Maren Vane had never trusted a thing she could not hold in her hands, and even then she preferred to hold it twice.

She was mending a net on the seaward steps of Tomas's house when Quentin came down to her, and she knew before he said a word that something had happened to him. He had his father's walk when he was frightened — shoulders up, chin tucked, like a man trying to make himself smaller than the world. Odell had that effect on people. She had seen him do it to better men than her son, and to worse ones too, though the worse ones usually deserved it.

"Sit," she said, without looking up from the net.

"Mam—"

"The step's not going anywhere, and neither is the net. Sit."

He sat. The evening was doing its slow work on the harbor — fishing boats coming in with their lamps lit early, gulls arguing over the day's leavings, the black shapes of the army steamers sitting out beyond the point like guests who would not take the hint. Somewhere behind them in the quartering streets a soldier was badly playing a concertina, and Maren found she almost liked it. It was good to know the enemy had at least one thing as miserable as they were.

"He offered you a berth," she said. "Odell."

Quentin went very still. Nineteen years old and thought he could keep things off his face; he'd been doing sums in his head since he was six and had never once managed to keep the answer out of his eyes. "How did you—"

"Because he offered me one first. Two days ago, through the fishwife Brannagh, who didn't know what she was carrying." She bit the thread and tied it off. "One berth, for the pearls, and for my silence about a debt that died before you learned to walk."

The sound Quentin made was small and wounded. "Mam, I have to tell you something. About Father. About the ledger. His debt — the company claims it was never settled, but I found the entries, I found the payment record from the year he drowned, it doesn't balance, Odell tore the settlement page out and—"

"I know."

He stared at her. In the lamplight from the window above he looked, for a moment, about seven years old again, the age he'd been when they'd pulled his father's coat out of the water and Maren had made him hold it all the way home because she could not carry both the coat and the boy.

"You *know*?" he said.

"I've known for eleven years." She set the net down in her lap and looked at him properly, because this part deserved looking-at. "Quentin. What do you think I've been doing all this time?"

"Diving. Working. Being—" He gestured vaguely at everything. "Being Mam."

"Well. Yes. But a woman can chew and think at the same time."

* * *

She should tell it plainly, she decided. Her whole life she had watched men wrap simple truths in enough cloth to sink them, and look where the town was now — quartered, requisitioned, priced by the berth. Plainness was the only honest currency left.

"When your father drowned," she began, "they gave me back his effects in a company sack. His knife. His boots, which were ruined, which they charged me for replacing, mind you, out of his estate — such as an estate is when it fits in a sack. And a scrap of paper, sodden through, that had been clenched in his fist so hard the fingers had to be opened by the surgeon."

Quentin had gone white. "The ledger page."

"A corner of one. Enough to show an entry and a date. I can't read more than my name and the numbers, but numbers I can read fine, thank you, I was keeping accounts of shell and pearl since before your factor Odell could grow the beard he hides behind now." She let that sit a moment. "The entry was a payment. Dated three weeks before he drowned. A payment that cleared the family account to nothing — paid off, closed, done. And your father, who knew it, who must have been holding that page like a torch, went under anyway with the divers saying he dove strange that morning, dove like a man with nothing left to prove."

"You never showed anyone."

"To who?" She laughed, short and salt-bitter. "The company clerk? That was you, love. Odell? The man whose hand wrote half those entries? The magistrate who leases his cellar to Harrow & Lyle for rum? I held that scrap of paper and understood, right there on the dock with my husband's coat dripping on the planks, that paper only speaks if there's someone willing to listen, and every ear in this town belongs to the company."

"So why keep it? Why not burn it and be done?"

"Because it was true," she said simply. "Truths are heavy to carry, but they're the only ballast worth having."

* * *

She got up, joints complaining the way they did after thirty years of cold water, and went inside. Quentin followed. She took the lamp down from its hook and carried it to the pantry, and from behind the flour crock — where any thief would look, which was the point — she moved aside a loose board and drew out a tin that had once held ship's biscuits.

Inside were no papers at all.

"Go on," she said. "Look."

Quentin lifted out a bundle of knotted cords — fishing line, different colors, knotted at intervals — and beneath them, worn soft as cloth, a set of counting shells, each one scratched with marks. He turned them over in his hands like relics, which, she supposed, was exactly what they were.

"What is this?"

"That," said Maren, "is every debt, every payment, every death-duty and birth-credit owed between forty-one families in this town and the Harrow & Lyle Trading Company, going back to before you were born. That's the Okonkwos' boat loan, cleared in '41, two seasons early. That's the Brannagh grain debt, which Odell doubled in '43 and I watched him do it. That's your father's account, marked paid in full, three weeks before they told us otherwise." She tapped a red cord near the middle. "Knots are payments. Shells are balances. The scratches on the underside are dates, by tide-season, because I don't trust calendar years — calendars get printed in the capital same as ledgers do."

Quentin was holding the cords the way he held anything precious: too carefully, as if it might be taken from him. Which, she thought, was fair. Everything else had been.

"Mam. This is — how long?"

"Since the funeral. Since the week after." She lowered herself onto the bench by the cold stove. "I couldn't sleep, those first months. You remember. You thought I was walking the shore from grief, and I was, some nights. But other nights I walked to houses. Old Sera Okonkwo, who remembered her mother's accounts word for word. Tam Broadfoot, who kept his tally notched on his doorframe until the company collector pried the frame out for 'inventory.' The diver widows, who each knew their own men's debts by heart because the collectors used to shout them across the beach like insults. I gathered it. Piece by piece, year by year. Every family's tally against the day the paper failed."

"And today the paper failed," Quentin said quietly.

"Today the paper failed." She looked at him — her boy, who had spent his life copying other people's names into books, believing the books were the world. "Quentin. Do you know why I did it? Not for spite, though there was plenty of that, and it kept well. I did it because your father came up out of the water dead with proof in his fist, and it did him no good at all. Paper can be torn out. Paper can drown. I decided that night that our family would keep its history somewhere the company couldn't requisition it." She touched her own temple. "In here. And here." She touched his chest, lightly, over the heart. "And in Sera's head, and Tam's doorframe, and the widow Kess's mouth, and forty-one families' worth of remembering."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Out in the street the concertina had stopped, or been stopped; the silence it left was enormous.

"All these years," Quentin said, and his voice cracked on the second word. "All these years I thought you didn't understand the ledgers. I'd come home talking about entries and balances and you'd nod along, and I thought — Mam, I thought you weren't interested."

That stung more than she expected, and she let him see it sting. "Boy. Who do you think taught you your numbers? Who counted your father's catch aloud on the dock every evening while you sat in the sand drawing figures in it? You learned arithmetic from a woman the ledgers forgot, and then you grew up and married the ledgers instead." She softened — he was pale, and young, and carrying Odell's offer like a stone in his belly. "I'm not angry at you. I'm telling you what we have. Sit down. We're going to need it."

He sat. "The pearls," he said. "Your plan. Trading the cache for berths. If Odell knows about the cache—"

"He knows I *have* one. He doesn't know where, and he doesn't know the size, and most importantly he doesn't know that the cache isn't the real treasure." She gathered the cords back into the tin, knot by knot, in their proper order — she could have done it blind, had done it often enough in dreams. "Odell thinks he's bargaining for pearls. Let him. Pearls run out. But a town that remembers what it's owed — that, Quentin, is a debt no one ever collects against."

* * *

They worked until late. That was the part she hadn't anticipated, the sweetness of it: spreading the tallies out between them on the kitchen table, her knots and his letters, matching memory to ink for the first time in eleven years. He read the copied pages he'd risked his neck for; she recited against them. Where the paper agreed with the memory, they marked it. Where they disagreed —

"They doubled the Brannaghs'," Quentin said, staring at the page, then at her. "You said '43. Here it is. There's a credit entered and then reversed the same season. No explanation."

"There's always an explanation," Maren said. "It's usually that someone wanted more rum that winter."

He laughed — actually laughed, tired and startled out of him — and she felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the gunboats came. This, she thought. This is what I was keeping it for. Not the revenge. The *unclenching*. The look on her son's face when the numbers in his head and the numbers in his blood finally agreed.

By midnight they had verified twenty-two families' tallies. By one o'clock, thirty. Quentin's eyes were burning and his hand cramped from writing, and at some point Maren realized he wasn't copying anymore — he was composing. Making a new document, neither company book nor spoken word, but something third: the town's account of itself, written down at last.

"It should be a book," he murmured, half-asleep over the table. "Not debts. People. Names and what they survived. An atlas..."

"An atlas of what?" she asked, but he was already gone, face down on his own fresh pages, nineteen years old and snoring gently in ink.

Maren banked the lamp. Through the window the steamers sat on the black water, and beyond them, faint, the last light of the evacuation ship at anchor — the ship with the berth list, the ship with her name absent from it, the ship that everyone in town now spoke of the way sailors speak of weather coming.

She put the tin away behind the board, then reconsidered, and put it inside her sea-chest instead, under her diving dress, where it would go wherever she went. Memory traveled better than furniture.

Then she stood at the window a long while, watching the harbor, and it seemed to her — though she was a practical woman and did not hold with omens — that there were fewer lights on the water than there had been at dusk. As if ships were moving in the dark. As if the blockade were closing early, ahead of schedule, the way the company always moved when it sensed the townspeople were beginning to count for themselves.

She woke her son with a hand on his shoulder, gentler than what was coming deserved.

"Quentin," she said. "Get your shoes. Something's wrong with the water."
