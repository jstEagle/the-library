## 12. The Blockade Closes

The morning the navy came, Quentin Vane was copying the same line for the fourth time.

*One crate naval biscuit, spoiled in transit, value assessed at—* He had written *spoiled* and crossed it out and written *damaged* and crossed that out too, because Colonel Ashe's clerks did not like the word *spoiled*. It sounded like an accusation against the army's own provisioning. *Damaged* was neutral. *Damaged* could have happened to anyone.

He was reaching for his penknife to scrape the third attempt clean when the sound came through the window — a low, grinding boom, felt in the floorboards before it was heard, the way thunder is felt in the soles of children's feet.

Quentin looked up. Sergeant Ivo was already at the window, and Ivo never went to windows. Ivo believed windows were where trouble found you standing still.

"Signal gun," Ivo said. His voice was flat as slate. "From the *Meridian*."

"The evacuation ship?"

"Was." Ivo turned from the window, and for a moment — just a moment — something moved behind his face that Quentin had never seen there before, and would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It looked almost like apology. "Harbor's closed. They've laid the chain across the mouth early. Nobody in, nobody out."

"But the ship —"

"Gone to anchor inside the boom." Ivo picked up his cap from the corner of the desk where he always kept it, squared it on his head with two precise tugs. "And you'd best come down to the office, clerk. There'll be lists to amend."

Lists to amend. Quentin sat very still while the words arranged themselves into their true shape behind them, the way a sentence sometimes means more than its grammar allows. Lists to amend meant names coming off. Names coming off meant people staying. And staying, now, meant staying behind a chain boom and a ring of gunboats, inside a town requisitioned down to its biscuit crates, for the duration of a war nobody in Harrow's Reach had been consulted about.

He followed Ivo out into a street that had already changed weather.

* * *

Panic, when it comes to a port town, does not scream. It whispers, and it counts.

That was what struck Quentin most, hurrying through the quartering streets behind the sergeant's grey back: how quiet it all was, and how much arithmetic was happening in doorways. A fishwife stood in front of her stall not selling anything, her lips moving — counting, he realized, counting her children, counting her coins, counting whether either sum could be made to reach a berth. Two old men by the chandlery had stopped mid-conversation and were simply looking at the harbor, where the *Meridian*'s masts stood up above the warehouse roofs like three fingers raised in a promise that was being quietly renegotiated.

A woman was scrubbing a doorstep that was already clean. Quentin passed her twice on the same street. Both times she looked up at him with the same expression — not hope, exactly, because hope requires somewhere to put itself, but the raw nerve of a person who has heard news secondhand and is waiting for someone official-looking to either confirm or kill it.

"You there," she called on his second pass. "You work for them. Is it true about the berths? Have they cut the list?"

"I don't know yet, mistress."

"They've cut it." She said it with terrible certainty, wringing her rag. "They always cut it. My mother told me — in the famine year, when they evacuated the north shore, the list started at four hundred souls and the ship left with one hundred and sixty. The rest were told the tide was wrong. The *tide*, boy. As if the tide hadn't been turning on schedule since before anyone's grandmother was born."

Ivo, ahead of them, did not slow down. But Quentin saw the sergeant's shoulders climb half an inch toward his ears, the way a man's do when a stranger says aloud the thing he has been thinking in private.

At the requisition office — the old Harrow & Lyle counting house, now flying the army's colors from the roof Quentin had spent six years under — the scene was orderly the way a flooded cellar is orderly: everything in its place, and all of it ruined. Clerks at every desk. Colonel Ashe himself standing at the master ledger stand, reading, unhurried as a man reading scripture of a Sunday.

Ashe looked up when Quentin entered, and smiled his small, courteous smile — the smile of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice because everyone around him has learned to listen.

"Ah. Vane. Good pen, good hand. You're wanted." He turned a page. "Situation has evolved. The fleet command moved the blockade forward by nine days. Their reasoning is classified; their effect is not. The *Meridian* sails tomorrow evening, at high water, instead of ten days hence."

Quentin heard himself say, stupidly, "Tomorrow," as though repeating the word might change it into something longer.

"Tomorrow." Ashe said it pleasantly, the way he said everything. "Which presents us with a small embarrassment of arithmetic. Two berths must come off the list."

The room did not go silent, because the room was full of pens scratching, and pens scratch through anything. But Quentin felt the silence anyway, the way you feel cold coming off ice before you touch it.

"Two, sir? Out of how many?"

"Out of forty-one." Ashe closed the ledger and rested both hands flat upon it, a gesture Quentin had seen a hundred times in merchants, and which he understood now, watching Ashe, was not about the book at all. It was about showing everyone your hands were empty and steady. "The ship's tonnage was calculated against the original schedule. With the shortened timeline, we lose capacity for stores — and stores, Vane, are not negotiable. Soldiers who cannot eat cannot hold a harbor, and a harbor unheld is worth precisely nothing to anyone, including the forty-one."

"And the two who come off?" Quentin kept his voice level. It was the voice he used for reading entries aloud. Inside, something was moving fast, like a diver beneath ice. "How are they chosen?"

Ashe regarded him with mild interest, the way a man regards a dog that has asked a question.

"By rank first. By debt second. By—" he paused, selecting the word with visible care, "—*usefulness* third. It is not a cruel system, Vane. It is simply a system. Cruelty would be choosing by lottery, which wastes the choice. Choice is the only currency this office actually controls, and I intend to spend it well." He lifted one finger. "The company factor advises me on the debt rankings. Mr. Odell is drawing up the amendment now."

Odell. The name landed in Quentin's chest like a stone dropped down a well — he listened for it to hit bottom, and it took a long time, and when it did, the sound was his father's name.

"Sir," Quentin heard himself say, "the families in the divers' quarter — many of them aren't on the list at all. If berths are being amended, might some be—"

"Might some be *added*?" Ashe almost laughed, a dry, brief sound with no warmth in it anywhere. "Vane. In nine days' war, nothing is added. Things are only taken away, and occasionally redistributed among people whose paperwork pleases me." He returned to his page. "Thank you. That will be all."

* * *

He ran.

Not all the way — a clerk running through quartering streets draws eyes, and eyes draw questions, and questions were the one commodity Quentin could no longer afford. So he walked, fast, with his satchel clutched to him like a breastplate, and every step of the way the town told him what was happening faster than any proclamation could.

By the chandlery, the two old men had been joined by a dozen others, and someone had chalked something on the doorpost — Quentin didn't stop to read it, but he knew the shape of it. Numbers. Always numbers. The fishwife's stall was abandoned, her fish already going soft in the sun, and that frightened him worse than shouting would have, because Maren Vane had raised him to believe that no fishwife alive leaves her catch to spoil unless something bigger than hunger is at stake.

Outside the cooperage, a knot of young men had gathered around Tomas Eriksen's cousin, who was saying, in the carrying voice of a man who wants the whole street to hear: "—sail tomorrow, I'm telling you, my wife's sister works wash for the garrison and she heard the sergeant say it plain. Tomorrow at high water. And the list is cut." A murmur ran through them like wind through wheat. "Cut, I say. And who d'you think they cut? Not the factors. Not the officers' cooks. They cut us, friends. They cut us the way you cut bait."

Quentin should have kept walking. Instead he stopped, and the young men noticed him — noticed the satchel, the ink stains, the counting-house pallor — and the murmur changed key.

"That's one of them," someone said. Not hostilely. Worse than hostilely. *Hopefully.* "That's the Vane boy. Maren's son. He sees the books." A broad-shouldered man pushed forward — Halloran, Quentin thought, Halloran who mended nets and had once paid off his indenture two years early by diving through a winter. "Vane. Tell us straight. Are they cutting the list?"

"I — the amendments haven't been posted yet—"

"So it's true."

"I don't know the names yet—"

"But it's *true*." Halloran wasn't angry. That was the awful part. He looked at Quentin the way men look at a barometer. "Two berths. Off forty-one. And forty-one was already a lie, because there's better than four hundred souls in this town and the ship holds fifty at a crush. We all did the sums months ago, lad. We just hoped we were bad at sums." He looked past Quentin then, toward the harbor, toward the three masts standing in their iron necklace of gunboats. "My girl's eight years old. She's not on any list. She was born here, worked here, and there's no line in any book in that building with her name in it, because nobody ever thought to write her down until somebody wanted to decide if she gets to leave."

Quentin said, "I'm sorry," and hated how it sounded — the sound of a man reading an entry aloud.

Halloran surprised him. The netmender put one huge hand briefly on Quentin's shoulder, gentle as a man steadying a lantern. "Don't be sorry, boy. Be quick. Whatever you're going to do — and I've watched your mother's face long enough to know her son is going to do *something* — do it tonight. Because come high water tomorrow, doing becomes a thing that other people did to us." He turned back to the crowd. "Now go on. Go on, before they see you talking to the likes of us."

* * *

Maren was waiting at the door of their rooms, and she had been waiting some time. He could tell by the way she stood — not leaning, never leaning, but planted, arms folded, the stance she took at the water's edge when she judged a dive too dangerous and intended to win the argument before it began.

"They've sealed it," she said. Not a question.

"Chain across the harbor mouth. Ship sails tomorrow at high water. Two berths cut from forty-one."

"Tomas?"

"On the list. Still. As far as I know."

"And us?"

"Not on the list. Still." He made himself say the rest. "Mother — Odell is drawing up the amendment. Ashe said rank, then debt, then usefulness. Odell knows every number in our family's history. If he's cutting two names, and he can choose which two cost him least—"

"He'll cut ours." She said it without drama, the way she said *the current's turned* or *your father's boat leaked*. Facts of the water. "And he'll sleep well after."

Inside, their single room held the evening's small inventory: the cold hearth, the two chairs, the sea chest where Maren kept what couldn't be drowned. On the table lay the thing Quentin had copied at night over three fearful, exalted evenings — his pages, his own hand, the first sentences in the world that belonged to him. The berth list. The key ledger entries. The tallies as the divers kept them, which Maren had recited and he had set down like a man taking dictation from history herself.

Maren looked at those pages for a long moment. Then she did something he had seen her do perhaps five times in his whole life.

She sat down.

She sat down heavily, in the middle of the day, with hours of light still left, and put her palms flat on the table, and stared at the wall where a map of the coast used to hang before the requisition took it.

"Nine days," she said quietly. "We had nine days. Nine days to get the pearls up, nine days to trade them, nine days to argue, bribe, beg, and shame our way onto that list. Your father used to say the sea gives you exactly enough time and not a minute more, and that the trick of a life is learning which kind of minute you're in." Her jaw tightened. "This is the other kind."

"Then we move tonight," Quentin said. "The cache. If we bring it up tonight—"

"Bring it up into what?" She turned and looked at him, and her eyes were the grey-green of deep water, and for once there was no hardness in them at all, only exhaustion, and beneath the exhaustion, fear — real fear, the kind she usually dove below. "Quentin. Odell knows about the cache. Maybe not where, but he knows it exists, he's known for years, and now he needs it. A man who falsifies a dead man's debt doesn't stop at debts. If we raise those pearls tonight, we're not traders anymore. We're thieves caught red-handed in company waters, and there's no page in any ledger that will say otherwise, because the page that would have said otherwise is *torn out*."

The words filled the room like cold water filling a boot.

"And if we leave them down there," Quentin said slowly, working it through the way he worked through a difficult column, "then the only thing we could have traded for berths stays buried, and we stay off the list, and Tomas—"

"Tomas goes." Maren said it instantly, fiercely. "Tomas and the child. That was settled before you were born, effectively."

"And you."

"I'm a strong swimmer," she said, with the ghost of her rare smile, "and the water and I have an arrangement."

But the joke died between them, because they both knew the water's arrangements had already taken one parent from Quentin, and the sea honors no agreements that matter.

Silence. Down in the street, footsteps hurried past their window — many sets, going the same direction, toward the harbor, toward the ship they could no longer reach, drawn to it the way mourners are drawn to a grave they're forbidden to enter. Somewhere a child was crying, not loudly, the tired crying of a child who has absorbed the adults' fear without understanding one word of it.

"Mother," Quentin said. "There were two berths cut. From forty-one. Which means thirty-nine names on that amended list, and Odell writing it, and every one of those thirty-nine families will thank the company for the mercy of it. And the rest of the town — Halloran's girl, the fishwife's boys, the old men at the chandlery — they'll watch that ship sail knowing the books decided, and the books were written by the men who robbed them." He looked down at his hands, at the ink under the fingernails that no scrubbing ever fully reached. "Every choice now costs somebody. Ashe chooses by rank. Odell chooses by grudge. Somebody has to choose by — by what's *right*, and if none of us does it, then nobody does."

Maren studied her son for a long moment. Something shifted in her face — the look, he would remember later, of a woman seeing the water clear suddenly above a depth she hadn't known was there.

"What are you thinking, Quentin Vane?"

He was thinking of his father, drowning with a ledger page in his fist. He was thinking of Halloran's enormous gentle hand on his shoulder. He was thinking that he had spent nineteen years believing that ledgers made the world turn, and that maybe — just maybe — the world turned on its own, and the ledgers merely wrote down where it had been, and a man who could write could also—

Footsteps on the stair. Heavy, deliberate, familiar ones.

Quentin was on his feet with the copied pages swept into his shirt before the knock came, and Maren was upright beside him as if she had never sat down at all.

Sergeant Ivo's voice through the door, low and even: "Clerk. Open up. Mr. Odell wants you at the office within the hour." A pause, and then, quieter, almost gently: "He says it's about your family's entry. And clerk — bring your best pen."

Through the window, beyond the roofs, the *Meridian*'s masts stood motionless against a sky going the color of old paper. High water tomorrow evening. One night left. Every hour now had a price tag hanging from it, and Quentin Vane, who had spent his life pricing other people's losses, was about to learn what his own cost.
