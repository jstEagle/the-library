## 13. Ivo's Question

The copies were gone from their hiding place on a Tuesday.

Quentin knew it the way a man knows his own hand has been taken from him — not by looking, but by the sudden wrongness of the empty space. He had tucked them behind the loose brick in the counting house cellar, beneath the shelf where the company kept its ceremonial brandy and its more ceremonial accounts of charitable giving, and when he pried the brick free at the end of his shift there was nothing there but cold mortar dust and the smell of old salt.

He did not panic. Panic was for men whose names were still written down somewhere that mattered. What he felt instead was something quieter and considerably worse: the particular chill of having been *watched*. All those nights bent over the requisition office desk with a stub of pencil and his heart hammering like a dockworker's mallet — all those nights, someone had been counting him the way he counted everyone else.

*So this is how it feels,* he thought, closing the brick back into place with fingers that had gone very steady, the way they always did at precisely the wrong moments. *To be an entry.*

He walked home through the quartered streets as calmly as he could manage, past soldiers playing cards on the Harrow & Lyle steps, past Mrs. Aldous-Prynne's boarding house with its new sign reading MILITARY MESS — RATIONING ENFORCED, which had caused Mrs. Aldous-Prynne to weep openly into her chowder for two days before discovering that feeding hungry conscripts paid better than feeding indigent pearl clerks ever had. The town had begun, in its stubborn way, to digest the army rather than be digested by it. Quentin took no comfort in this. His copies — the berth list, the ledger pages, the tallies Maren had dictated to him in her flat diver's voice while he wrote faster than he'd ever written in his life — were somewhere they should not be.

Or, more precisely, somewhere exactly where he feared.

He found out that evening, because Sergeant Ivo was waiting for him at the door of his mother's house, sitting on the low sea-wall with his long red hands folded in his lap like a man attending a funeral for someone he hadn't much liked but respected anyway.

"Evening, Vane," said Ivo.

"Sergeant."

"Nice night." It was, in fairness, a spectacular night — the kind of amber-and-violet sunset the harbor produced when it wanted to remind everyone why nobody had ever willingly left. "Been meaning to have a word with you."

Quentin sat down on the wall beside him, because his legs made the decision without consulting him. From inside the house came the sound of Tomas arguing with his daughter about bedtime, the argument being conducted in the universal language of fathers losing.

Ivo reached into his tunic — slowly, deliberately, the way you reach for something when you want the other man to have time to consider running — and drew out a fold of paper. Quentin recognized the cheap gray stock. He recognized his own cramped handwriting on it, the letters going slightly uphill when he was frightened, which was all the time.

"I found these," said Ivo, "behind a brick."

"Did you," said Quentin.

"In this town," Ivo went on, turning the pages over without appearing to read them, "there are forty-one soldiers under my command. Thirty-eight of them are conscripts. The other three are thieves, so effectively conscripts too. Do you know what a conscript is, Vane?"

"A man who didn't choose."

"A man," said Ivo, "who was entered into a book he never got to read. That's what the recruiting officer called me once, before I broke his nose. *An entry.* He said it like it was nothing. Like being written down was the same as agreeing." He looked out at the water, where the evacuation ship rode at anchor, black-hulled and patient as a coffin. "You've got my whole war in your pocket, Vane. The berth list. The requisition orders. Names, tonnage, dates. Enough to hang you and half of me."

"I can explain—"

"Don't," said Ivo, and there was no menace in it, only a tiredness so deep it sounded almost like kindness. "Don't explain. I've heard explanations from better liars than either of us. I want to ask you something else."

* * *

What he wanted to ask, it turned out, could not be asked quickly, so the two of them sat on the sea-wall while the light failed and the lamps came on one by one along the dock, and Ivo told Quentin about his mother.

Her name was Serafina Ivo — she had given her son her own name as surname, having never gotten around to the other business with his father, whom Ivo described only as "a fiddler, and gone before the tune ended." She lived in a river town two hundred miles inland, up where the war was still just a rumor with a uniform on it, and she kept a shop that sold rope and tar and advice, the advice being free and the most profitable line. Every month since he'd been marched away she had sent him a letter, and every letter contained, among other things, a list.

"The tally," Ivo said. "That's what she calls it. Everything owed and everything paid, going back three generations. Who lent us flour in the bad winter and whether we paid it back with interest or with labor. Which neighbor's funeral we contributed to and which neighbor skipped ours and is therefore owed a certain coldness until the debt of rudeness is discharged. My grandfather's boat, and who actually built the hull, which the county records get wrong." He shook his head. "She writes it like scripture. She thinks if the paper ever burns, the truth goes with it, and then whoever holds the new paper owns everything."

Quentin's throat had gone tight. "Your mother sounds like—"

"She sounds like a woman who's never trusted a ledger in her life," said Ivo, "and keeps one anyway. Because the alternative isn't trusting people, Vane. The alternative is trusting the people who write the ledgers." He turned and looked at Quentin directly for the first time, and in the lamplight his face was older than his years and younger than his rank, somehow both at once. "Here's my question. Here's what I dragged you out here to ask."

He held up the stolen pages.

"This list. This berth list. If I asked you right now what my mother's name would be worth on it — what it would take, what it would cost, who you'd have to bribe — could you tell me?"

Quentin opened his mouth.

"And don't say it doesn't work that way," Ivo added. "Because you copied it. You know exactly how it works. Rank first, then shareholders, then creditors of good standing, then skilled tradesmen, then — at the bottom, if there's room, if the tide cooperates, if God is feeling generous — the rest. Where does a rope-seller from upriver fall on that list? Where does a sergeant fall? I'll save you the arithmetic. We don't. We're not entries. We're margins."

The tide breathed against the pilings. Inside the house, Tomas had evidently lost the bedtime argument and was now singing, badly, a lullaby that Maren used to sing, which meant Maren was home, which meant Quentin was sitting six feet from his entire remaining family with a soldier between him and them, and could not have moved if the pier caught fire.

"No," Quentin said finally. "I couldn't tell you. Not because I don't know how the list works. Because the list is wrong."

Ivo grunted. "Wrong how? It's accurate. I checked the tonnage myself."

"It's accurate the way a knife wound is accurate," Quentin said, and heard his own voice do something it had never done before in the counting house: rise. "It tells you exactly where the blade went and says nothing about whether anyone deserved it. That's what I spent four years learning, Sergeant. I thought ledgers were how the world stayed honest. I thought if you wrote everything down — every debt, every payment, every name — then the numbers would protect people. Numbers can't perjure themselves, my father used to say. Except he was wrong, because somebody tore out his page, and now he's a blank in a book, and the book gets to decide he never existed."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. Out on the water, a ship's bell rang the hour — nine o'clock, the harbor curfew, though neither of them moved.

"You know what's funny," Ivo said at last. "I reported the papers. Day before yesterday. Wrote it up proper, told Lieutenant Grange I'd found documents hidden in the cellar wall, suspected company clerk, couldn't identify him." He smiled faintly. "Grange thanked me and filed the report in the requisition office, in a cabinet, in a drawer, behind a form. It will sit there until the ship sails or the sun burns out, whichever comes first. I betrayed you to the army, Vane, and the army lost the betrayal on the way to the filing room." He tapped the pages against his knee. "Then I sat with these all night and read every word. And do you know what I found?"

"What?"

"My name." Ivo's voice was very even. "On the requisition roster. *Ivo, Sgt., 3rd Harbor Co., rations drawn, wages deferred.* Deferred. You know what deferred means? It means they're keeping a record of money they intend to never pay me, and the record is the payment. That's my whole compensation, Vane. A well-kept account of my own robbery." He laughed — a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. "My mother would laugh herself sick. She'd say, *At least they spelled it right.*"

Despite everything — despite the pages in Ivo's hand and the gallows implied by their existence — Quentin laughed too. It came out ragged, but it came out real, and something crossed the sea-wall between them in that moment that neither of them would ever quite have a name for: the recognition, mutual and unforced, of two men who had spent their lives being written about by others.

"I should arrest you," Ivo said.

"Yes."

"It would be correct. Procedure. There's a form."

"There's always a form," said Quentin.

"And instead," Ivo went on, "I'm going to give you these back, and I'm going to forget where I found them, and tomorrow morning I'm going to assign myself to the night watch at the requisition office, alone, unsupervised, because I am famously reliable and no officer has ever once questioned it." He pressed the fold of pages into Quentin's hands and stood, brushing off his trousers with the brisk embarrassment of a man who has just done something enormous and wishes to pretend it was small. "But I want something in return. Two things."

"Name them."

"First: when this ends — however it ends, fire or flood or paperwork — you find some way to write my mother's name down somewhere it counts. Not on their list. On yours. Whatever yours turns out to be." He said it lightly, but his ears had gone red. "Serafina Ivo. Rope and tar and advice. She'd hate to be forgotten by professionals."

"I will," Quentin said, and found that he meant it the way he had never meant the words *I certify* or *received in good order* — completely, in the part of himself that predated ink.

"Second." Ivo paused at the gate, silhouetted against the lamplit street, and his voice dropped. "There's a rumor moving through the officers' mess. The navy wants the blockade closed early — day after tomorrow, maybe sooner. When it closes, the ship sails within the hour, and the list shrinks again. Two berths cut, maybe more. Nobody knows whose yet." He looked back at Quentin, and his face held none of the sergeant now, only the conscript, the entry, the man in the margin. "Whatever you're planning, Vane — and don't tell me you're not planning, I've watched you for three weeks, you plan like other men breathe — whatever it is, it needs to happen before the water closes. After that there's no choosing anymore. There's only arithmetic."

He let himself out. Quentin sat on the sea-wall in the dark with the pages warm against his chest, listening to Tomas's terrible lullaby through the window, and understood that the question Ivo had really asked him had not been about his mother's name at all.

It had been: *when the moment comes, whose names will you spend?*

And now, with the blockade two days out, Quentin had a spare berth in his pocket — one, single, unassigned — and two names in the house behind him who each believed it belonged to the other.
