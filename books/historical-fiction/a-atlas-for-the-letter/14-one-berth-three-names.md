## 14. One Berth, Three Names

The berth list hung in the requisition office like a weather forecast for a storm that had already drowned everyone. Quentin had copied it three times now — once for the record, once for Ivo's files, and once for himself, folded into his shirt where it lay against his chest like something he'd stolen from a church. Which, in a way, he had.

Two names had been struck through that morning in Colonel Ashe's neat red ink. The blockade had moved up the departure by two days, and arithmetic being arithmetic, two berths had to die so the paper could balance.

Quentin had spent the walk home rehearsing how he would say it.

He had not expected Tomas to already know.

* * *

His uncle was sitting on the step of the Vane house with his boots off, drying his feet in the last of the afternoon sun as though it were an ordinary evening and he were an ordinary man who had simply walked far that day. He was a big man, Tomas — big hands, big laugh once, though the laugh had been rationed lately — and he looked up at Quentin with an expression of such terrible calm that Quentin knew before a word was spoken.

"You've seen the list," Quentin said.

"I've seen Ivo's face," said Tomas. "Ivo has an honest face. It's his worst quality." He wiggled his toes toward the sun. "Sit down, lad. Your mother put the kettle on before she went to the divers' hall. She does that when she wants to be somewhere else when the talking starts."

"She knows too?"

"Everyone knows. That's the trouble with a town this size. News travels faster than ships, and it doesn't need a harbor."

Quentin sat on the step beside him. Between them lay the whole problem, and neither of them looked at it directly, the way you don't look at the sun, or at a hole in a boat.

"There's one berth unclaimed," Quentin said finally. "A surgeon's assistant deserted yesterday — ran for the hills with a mule and half the medicine chest. His name can be replaced. One name. Mine to write in."

"Ah," said Tomas. "So they've made *you* the ledger now."

The words landed harder than Tomas probably intended, or perhaps exactly as he intended; his uncle had always had a diver's sense for where things actually rested on the bottom, beneath what could be seen from the surface.

"It's my choice," Quentin said. "Ivo can enter any name I give him. Nobody will check."

"Nobody ever checks," Tomas agreed. "That's how your father died, more or less."

That silenced them both. Down the lane, someone's goat got loose and a woman swore at it with real artistry, and for a moment the world seemed almost normal — a town going about its business, ignoring the gunboats at the mouth of the harbor the way you ignore a tumor you've decided not to name.

"Maren," Quentin began.

"—wants it to be me, yes." Tomas reached over and gripped Quentin's shoulder, and his hand was heavy as an anchor. "She told me this morning while I was mending the net. She told me very loudly, the way she tells people things she's decided. And I told her no, and she told me louder, and here we are, both of us still breathing, which is the only argument either of us has won against her in thirty years."

"She has the pearl knowledge. The tallies. The whole quarter's memory is in her head—"

"And yours is in yours," said Tomas. "And mine is in mine. Memory isn't a pearl, boy. It doesn't get rarer because one oyster dies. She taught me that herself, back when she was young enough to think teaching me anything was possible."

"You have Elsi," Quentin said quietly.

Tomas was silent for a moment. Elsi was six. She had her mother's chin and nobody's patience, and she believed, with total conviction, that her uncle Quentin worked in a building made entirely of paper because paper was the most valuable substance on earth. No one had corrected her. In the current circumstances it felt rude to.

"Yes," Tomas said. "I have Elsi. And that's exactly why it should be your mother on that ship."

"That makes no sense."

"Course it makes no sense. Sense is what people use to dress up fear so it can walk around in public." He pulled his boots back on slowly, wincing at the cold leather. "Listen. If Maren goes, she goes alone. Her brother stays, her nephew stays, and everything she loves is on this dock when the shooting starts. You'd be sending her away safe and hollowed out. But if *I* go — I go with a purpose. I keep Elsi alive, I get her somewhere green, somewhere the war hasn't chewed yet, and every day I'm alive out there is a debt your mother can see paid. Hope travels better than safety. Ask any sailor."

"That's the stupidest argument I've ever heard."

"It's the best I've had since breakfast. I'm working with limited materials."

* * *

Maren came back from the hall with the tide, smelling of salt and lamp oil, and found them still on the step, having progressed nowhere except to the point where Tomas had begun listing, with mock scholarship, all the previous occasions on which he had been right and she had been wrong. The list was short. He was stretching it.

"The answer is no," she said, before the door had even shut behind her.

"The question was whether you want tea," said Tomas.

"The answer is still no."

She sat down across from them, hands folded, and Quentin saw that she had been crying earlier and had done the work of hiding it, the way she did everything: thoroughly, without witnesses, before anyone thought to look. Nineteen years of watching her and he still almost missed it.

"Mama—"

"Don't." She held up one finger. "Don't *Mama* me. I've been diving since I was eight years old, Quentin. I know exactly what I'm worth in open water and I know exactly what your uncle is worth on land, which is nothing, because the man once got lost walking home from the church we can see from this doorstep."

"That was fog," said Tomas with dignity.

"It was Tuesday."

"Foggy Tuesday."

Quentin let them have their minute of it. It was how they loved each other — through the thinnest possible veil of insult, so that neither would ever have to be caught saying the true thing aloud. Then Maren turned to him, and the veil dropped, and underneath it her face was bare as the seabed at low tide.

"You're the one holding the pen," she said. "So hear me plainly. If you write Tomas's name, I will grieve you both from this shore and I will survive it, because surviving is the one thing our family has never failed at. If you write *my* name, I will walk onto that ship knowing my brother's daughter is behind me, and I will spend the rest of my life diving in some foreign sea trying to wash her face out of my eyes. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There is no version of this where I thank you. There is only the version where I can live with it, and there's only one of those."

"That's not fair," Quentin said. "You're making me choose between two ways of breaking your heart."

"Yes," said Maren. "Welcome to being the eldest of something. It's the whole job."

"He's not—" Quentin stopped. Then, because the house felt suddenly too small for honesty: "I'm not choosing for you. That's the thing none of you will say. I'm choosing for *me*. Whoever I leave off that ship is whoever I have to look at every morning after, knowing I wrote them out of the world. You're asking me which of you I can bear to fail."

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere outside the goat achieved its freedom and could be heard celebrating in a neighbor's garden.

Tomas cleared his throat. "Well," he said. "When you put it like that it sounds less noble."

"It wasn't meant to sound noble."

"No, it's good. Honesty's underrated in family disputes. Your grandmother used to say—" He stopped, and the color went slightly out of him, because Quentin's grandmother had drowned too, and the joke that had been forming died quietly in the air between them like a match pinched out.

Maren stood up abruptly and put the kettle on after all. It was what she did instead of screaming, and everyone present respected the craft involved.

* * *

They argued until dark, then argued by lamplight, then argued in the pauses while eating bread and arguing. Positions were entrenched. Reinforcements were summoned — old Sela from next door, whose counsel Maren sought, arrived and took Tomas's side within four minutes purely out of lifelong spite; the widow Ansker, who owed Tomas money, took Maren's side for reasons she stated openly. By nine o'clock the kitchen contained five opinions, two of them borrowed, and the berth remained unwritten.

It was Elsi who ended it, by wandering downstairs past bedtime for water and asking, with a six-year-old's flawless instinct for the exposed nerve, "Is somebody dead?"

The room went quiet.

"Somebody's being difficult," said Tomas.

"Same smell," said Elsi, and drank her water, and went back up, leaving behind her a silence of a new and unbearable composition.

Quentin looked at his mother. He looked at his uncle. And he understood, with a clarity that felt less like wisdom than like a blade being handed to him handle-first, that the argument had never been about who deserved to live. It was about who each of them could stand to owe. Maren could carry grief but not guilt. Tomas could carry guilt but not grief. And Quentin — Quentin had spent his life copying other people's debts into ledgers, and knew in his bones that the worst debt is the kind that gets forgiven too easily, the kind nobody writes down, the kind that just sits in you, compounding.

"Both of you stop," he said.

They stopped. Even Sela stopped chewing.

"You're both trying to lose," he said, "because losing feels cleaner than choosing. Well, I'm the clerk. Choosing is my trade now. So here is what's written." He took out the folded copy of the berth list and laid it flat on the table, smoothing it with both hands like a man laying out a body. "One berth. One name. And I'll tell you my reasoning, so that neither of you can pretend afterward that it was cruelty or favor. I'm not choosing who I love more. I'm choosing who the town needs more."

"Tomas needs to go with Elsi," Maren started—

"Neither of you," said Quentin. "I'm writing in a third name."

The silence that followed had actual texture.

"The surgeon's berth goes to Old Nan Corrigan," Quentin said. "She's the only midwife left in the quarter. Eleven women on that dock are carrying, and wherever that ship lands, those children will need delivering, and Nan is the only person whose death tonight would cost us more than one life at a time. That's the arithmetic. I learned it from the company books, God forgive me, but it holds."

"But—" Tomas began.

"Elsi isn't sailing," Quentin said, and his voice didn't shake, which surprised him as much as anyone. "None of us are. Not yet. Because the pearls are still in the water, Mama, and Odell still wants them, and a man who wants something can be traded with. We have two days before the blockade closes for good. Two days and one hidden cache worth more than every berth on that list combined. We don't spend our last coin on one seat. We buy the whole boat."

Nobody spoke. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

"That's insane," said Sela at last, with obvious admiration.

"It's bookkeeping," said Quentin. "The only honest kind I've ever done."

Maren looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes that he couldn't read — pride, or terror, or the diver's instinct that recognizes, far below, the shape of something enormous rising toward the surface.

Then she smiled, slow and dangerous, and said:

"Then we dive tomorrow night. And God help anyone standing between my family and the water."

Down in the harbor, unseen in the dark, the first searchlight of the blockade swept the black water once, twice — and went out.
