## 15. The Pearl Cache

Maren Vane had not been inside the old salt-house since the year the roof fell in, and she had forgotten how loudly a ruined building could speak. Every step set the boards complaining underfoot, and the wind came through the broken slats in a long, wheedling whistle, like a beggar who would not take no for an answer.

"Hush," she told the building, and behind her she heard Sela snort.

"You're talking to architecture now, Maren."

"I talk to whoever listens. It's more than the company ever did."

There were nine of them moving through the dark above the tideline — divers mostly, and the grown children of divers, and old Piet who was neither but had carried things for the diving families for forty years and considered it a sacred office. They came in ones and twos along the shingle, no lanterns, the moon doing its thin best through torn cloud. Below them the harbor lay full of army steamers, black hulls against black water, their riding lamps like a string of cold beads. Somewhere on those decks men were sleeping who believed, because paper told them so, that this town belonged to them.

Maren touched the locket at her throat out of habit and found, as always, that it wasn't there anymore. She had traded it to Quentin's father thirty years ago for a courting ribbon and never gotten it back, which she had told him at the time was the worst bargain he'd ever make. He had drowned holding worse things than lockets.

*Not tonight,* she told herself. *Tonight you are only a woman walking to work.*

The cache lay beneath the floor of the salt-house, in a cavity between two foundation stones where the old curing brine had once drained. Her mother had shown it to her when Maren was eleven years old, kneeling on these same rotten boards, lifting them one by one as though they were the lid of a chest in a story.

"Paper burns," her mother had said. "Pearls don't. And people remember where pearls are buried better than they remember where papers are filed."

Her mother had been right about nearly everything and wrong about the most important thing, which was that burying something deep enough would keep it safe. Nothing stayed buried forever. Not debts. Not grief. Not the men who came to collect either.

"Here," she said, and knelt.

Piet brought the pry bar down gently, the way he handled everything, as though the world might crack if he were careless. The board came up with a groan that seemed to Maren far too loud, loud enough to wake the whole garrison, and everyone froze and listened to the wind whistle and the tide breathe and nothing else.

"Well," whispered Sela, after a decent interval, "if they didn't hear that, they're deafer than Colonel Ashe's conscience."

Maren reached into the cavity. Her fingers found sand first, then sacking, then the cool greasy roundness of the jar within. She lifted it out and set it on the boards between them, and for a moment nobody spoke.

Even in the half-dark the pearls looked alive. They sat in their brine-clouded glass like a handful of moons someone had pried loose from the sky and stored against hard times. There were larger caches — the company's own warehouses held barrels of them — but this one was theirs, gathered grain by grain over three generations, skimmed from the company's half-price takings by women who could hold their breath longer than any clerk could hold his pen.

"That one there," said Piet, pointing with a cracked fingernail, "your grandmother called the Widow's Tear. Sold it twice and bought it back twice."

"And this flat one," said Sela, "my mother swore it was cursed. Swore it until I sold it and gave her the money, and then she swore it was blessed."

Maren let them murmur. This was part of it too — the naming. Every pearl in the jar had a name and a story, the way every family in the diving quarter had a tally in her head: what they'd been owed, what had been stolen, what had been paid four times over and charged for anyway. Odell thought the books were the truth of this town. Let him. Books were just ink. Ink didn't know anybody's name.

"We go tonight, all the way?" asked Piet. "Or split it?"

"All together, all the way." She wrapped the jar in sacking and stowed it in the basket lined with wet kelp, which kept the pearls cool and, more importantly, made the basket smell like a hundred other baskets on a hundred other boats. "The *Cormorant* sails in two days. We put the jar in Captain Reyes's hands, he puts us in berths, and we all watch this harbor from somewhere the guns can't reach."

"And if he wants more than the pearls?"

"Then he can want," said Maren. "Reyes has wanted things his whole life. It's never once made him brave."

They went out along the tideline in single file, quick and low, and Maren felt — she would remember this afterward, how she had felt it, the last easy feeling for a long while — she felt *light*. Forty years of fear set down on the sand. The thing was done. The treasure was out of the ground and moving toward the sea, and tomorrow she would see Quentin and tell him, *we did it without your ledgers, love; we did it the old way*, and he would make one of his serious young faces and say something about risk, and she would let him think he was the careful one in the family.

She was halfway to the boats when Piet stopped walking.

He didn't say anything. He just stopped, the way a deer stops, and that was worse than shouting. Maren turned. Behind them, up on the shingle ridge, a lantern kindled — one lantern, held high, unhurried.

"Beautiful night for it," said a voice she knew.

Odell came down the slope alone, or appeared to be, and that was how Maren knew he wasn't. The factor had always traveled with shadows arranged around him. He wore a civilian coat now instead of company gray, but he wore it the same way, buttoned to the throat, as though his own skin embarrassed him.

"You followed us," Maren said.

"I've been following you for weeks, Mrs. Vane." He raised the lantern higher, and its light crawled across the faces of the divers, one by one, cataloguing them. "Since the counting house. Since your son began asking after pages that weren't his business. You're a family of questions, you Vanes. Questions are expensive."

"The pearls aren't yours."

"They're on land the army requisitioned, recovered from a structure requisitioned, by persons employed — however loosely — by a company whose property is now under military administration." He smiled with the bottom half of his face. "I believe the legal term is *theft*. But then, you people don't trouble much with legal terms, do you? You trouble with memory. And memory, Mrs. Vane, is not collateral. Memory cannot be posted as bond. Nobody at the bank will take it."

"Memory is the only thing you can't steal," said Sela, "which is why it frightens you."

"It doesn't frighten me." Odell crouched, slow and courteous, and set the lantern on the sand so its light came up under his face and turned him into something from a child's bad dream. "It bores me. Now. Here is what happens. You give me the basket, quietly, and I forget this evening. Your families stay off the berth list, of course — that list is closed, quite closed — but you stay free, and free people can always find boats. Fishermen are sentimental. Ask any of them."

"And if we say no?"

Soldiers came over the ridge then, six of them, rifles unslung, boots loud on the shingle, and Maren understood that the question had only been politeness, the way everything Odell did was politeness laid over iron like gilt over lead.

She looked at the basket. She looked at her people — Piet with his pry bar, useless and precious; Sela with her chin up, twenty years old and furious; the others pale in the lantern light, watching her, waiting for her, because for forty years these families had come to Maren Vane with their grievances and their griefs and she had kept them all, every debt and every death, in the ledger behind her eyes.

If she handed over the jar, they lost their berths and their bargaining coin and three generations of skimming and saving. If she didn't, they lost her.

*Well,* she thought, with a calm that surprised her, *there it is. Every tally comes due.*

"No," she said.

"Maren—" started Piet.

"No." Louder now, so the soldiers could hear it, so the whole listening dark could hear it. "This jar is ours. We dove for every pearl in it, and our mothers before us, and the company took half of everything we ever brought up and called the taking fair. I am not handing my grandmother's grave to a man who falsifies dead men's debts. Take it if you must. Take it and explain to Colonel Ashe why his requisition includes robbing widows on a beach."

Odell sighed, genuinely, deeply, the sigh of a man disappointed in a subordinate. He gestured.

The soldiers came forward. Piet raised the pry bar and a corporal simply took it away from him, almost kindly, the way you'd take matches from a child. Sela spat. Someone began to cry, softly, furiously, hating herself for it. And the basket passed out of Maren's hands — she made them take it, made it cost them something, her fingers locked white around the handle until a sergeant pried them loose one at a time, respectful as a man handling rigging in a storm — and Odell held it up to the light and looked inside and smiled.

"There she is," he murmured, to the pearls, to the night, to nobody. "Forty years, Mrs. Vane. Did you truly think I didn't know?"

"If you knew," said Maren, "why wait?"

"Because a cache in the ground is a rumor. A cache in my hand is an asset." He handed the basket to the sergeant. Then he turned back to her, and the smile was gone, and what was underneath was older and colder and much more honest. "But you — you're not an asset, Mrs. Vane. You're a witness. You've spent forty years telling every diver on this coast what the company owes them. Witnesses are dangerous. Witnesses get tried."

"Tried for what? Walking on a beach?"

"Tried for theft of company property." He said it gently, almost regretfully, a doctor delivering a diagnosis. "Recovered goods, seized in violation of military requisition, by a known agitator, in the company of unauthorized persons. The evidence is remarkably complete. I have the basket right here."

The soldiers took her arms. She didn't pull. That surprised her too — some steady thing in her simply declined to thrash, the way she had learned, all those years diving, that panic spends air faster than fear does. She stood in their grip on her own beach, on stones her mother's mother had walked, and she looked at Odell for a long moment.

"You'll hang me with a ledger," she said. "That's your way. No knife, no gun. Just ink."

"Ink has kept the peace for five thousand years, madam."

"Ink kept *you* in peace. It kept the rest of us in debt." She raised her voice then, past the soldiers, past the lantern, out over the dark water where the steamers rode at anchor, and she pitched it the way she pitched it on the dock, the way she had pitched it at a hundred funerals and a dozen company offices, so that it carried. "Hear me, Odell! You can cage me. You can try me with your books. But every woman on this shore knows what's in them better than you do. You hold the paper. *We* hold the count. And the count says you owe."

"Take her," said Odell.

They marched her up the shingle, and the town fell in behind her without being asked — Piet, Sela, the crying girl, then others from the doorways and net-sheds as the news ran ahead, bare feet and lamp flames gathering like gulls behind a boat. Maren walked straight, chin level, giving them what they needed from her, which was a back that didn't bend.

Only at the guardhouse door, when the lamplight fell through the bars across her hands, did the steadiness finally crack — not into tears, she was too angry for tears, and too frightened, and the two feelings crushed together into something dry and burning. It was this: her head, her memory, the forty-year ledger. Everything the quarter knew about itself lived in her. What Sela's mother was owed. What the Okonjo brothers had paid twice. What Quentin's father had paid with his life, and the page that proved it, torn out by the very man now locking the door.

If they tried her by their books, their books would win, because their books were built to win. Unless —

Unless there was a page. Quentin had copies. Quentin had the tallies. Quentin had, God help him, his father's stubbornness and her own arithmetic and nineteen years of copying other people's names.

They put her in the cell and took the lamp, and the door closed with the soft, well-oiled click of a thing designed to close ten thousand times without complaint.

Maren stood in the dark and listened to the guards settle outside, and the tide beyond them, and her own heart going hard and even as a diver's, down, down toward the bottom of the night.

*Quentin,* she thought. *They have taken the keeper. Come and find the count.*
