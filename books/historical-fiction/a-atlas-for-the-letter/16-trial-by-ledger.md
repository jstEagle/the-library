## 16. Trial by Ledger

They tried my mother in the counting house, which I suppose should not have surprised me. Where else does the Harrow & Lyle Company hold its judgments? The great room had been cleared of desks — all but one, Odell's, which sat at the front like a small altar — and in their place stood rows of borrowed chairs from the Salt & Anchor tavern, still smelling of spilled beer and old arguments. A trestle table served as the bench. Colonel Ashe presided over it with a cup of coffee he never drank, and behind him, stacked on the shelves like witnesses who could not lie, stood the ledgers.

Forty-one volumes. I had counted them since I was eleven years old, first as a boy sent to dust them, later as the clerk whose hand filled three of them myself. I knew their spines the way other sons know their mothers' faces. And now they were going to be asked to condemn her.

The charge was read out at nine in the morning by a lieutenant who kept mispronouncing *Maren* — *Marren*, *Marin*, once memorably *Moron*, at which half the soldiers bit their knuckles and the other half looked at the ceiling — as *theft of company property, being one hundred and twelve pearls, value unassessed.* Value unassessed. That was the army's way of saying they did not yet know how badly they had been robbed, and it occurred to me, standing at the back with my hands folded like a good little clerk, that this was the single honest phrase spoken all day.

* * *

My mother sat in the dock — which was actually a packing crate marked FRAGILE: CHINA, HARROW & LYLE — with her hands loose in her lap and her chin up, the way she sat waiting for a tide. She had been given no counsel, because the town's only lawyer was on the berth list and had sailed two days prior, waving from the rail with what I can only describe as insufficient shame. So when Ashe asked if she wished to speak in her own defense, she said, "I'll want my bookkeeper," and pointed at me.

"Your—" Ashe turned. "The clerk?"

"He knows the books better than the man who signed them," Maren said. "That's the trouble, isn't it? Everything here turns on the books."

I felt every eye in the room swing to me, and I will confess something: for one long breath I wanted to be anywhere else on earth, including the bottom of the harbor. Then I caught my mother's gaze, steady as ballast stone, and walked forward.

It was Odell who rose to prosecute. Of course it was. He had traded his factor's coat for a civilian's dark suit, but he wore the suit like a uniform anyway, buttoned wrong at the top because he had never once in his life dressed himself without a ledger telling him where buttons went. He gave the court a small bow.

"The facts are simple," Odell said. "On the night of the eleventh, soldiers acting on information discovered a cache of pearls concealed beneath the floor of the Netmender's Hall. These pearls are, by every contract ever entered in the company's registers, the property of Harrow & Lyle. The divers are paid wages against the value of their finds. Every pearl they bring up belongs, by law and by ink, to the company. What Madam Vane buried under a dance floor is stolen goods."

"And the contracts?" Ashe said. "You can produce them?"

Odell smiled the smile of a man opening a safe. "Colonel, I have brought every relevant volume. Entries spanning nineteen years. Wages advanced, pearls received, debts carried forward. The record is complete."

Complete. I heard the word and felt something turn over in my chest, slow and cold, like a hull coming off its moorings.

Because I had spent four nights in the requisition office copying those very volumes, and I knew what was missing from them. One page. One settlement record, dated the spring before my father drowned, showing that the debt of Alric Vane had been paid in full — paid, and signed for, and then torn out. If it existed, it proved the company had collected my father's freedom and drowned him in debt anyway. It proved Odell had falsified twenty years of entries. It would acquit my mother of theft, because you cannot steal what was owed to you all along, plus interest.

And Odell had just promised the court the record was *complete.*

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing there in the room where I had grown invisible: a man does not tear out a page unless the page threatens him. I had assumed — God help me, I had *hoped* — that the page had been lost carelessly, misfiled, ruined by harbor damp the way everything in this town was eventually ruined by harbor damp. But a man who loses a page says so. A man who loses a page orders a search. Odell had done none of those things. When I had asked him, weeks ago now, why the settlement records for 18— were thin, he had told me there'd been a flood in the archive room, and I had believed him the way I believed everything then, the way a ledger believes whatever is written in it.

He hadn't lost the page. He had destroyed it. And he had destroyed it not to spite my family — spite is cheap, and Odell never spent anything cheaply — but to save himself. Because that page, entered into evidence, did not merely prove my father free. It proved that the man who had signed the false entries afterward was a forger, a thief of wages, and — if the war office ever looked closely — liable to exactly the kind of arithmetic that ended with a rope.

My mother wasn't on trial. My mother was the lever, and Odell meant to use her to crack open the divers' quarter, to find whether more pearls lay under more floors, and to bury his own crime under hers while the shovels were convenient.

I stood at the prosecution table with my copied pages hidden in my shirt and thought, with a clarity that frightened me: *this is no longer a war of paper.* Paper had been on Odell's side for nineteen years. Paper was his native country. If we fought him there we would lose, because he owned the country and spoke the language and had already burned the map.

But people. People remembered. My mother had spent years memorizing every family's tally against exactly this day. Old Sef the netmender could recite the winter the company doubled the salt prices. Widow Kess could name the boat, the date, and the weather the day the company claimed her husband's catch and paid him a third of its worth. There were forty families in the divers' quarter and every one of them carried a ledger in their skulls more accurate than anything Odell had ever signed, because theirs had been checked against hunger, and hunger does not tolerate errors.

The paper war became a human one. And humans, unlike paper, could be asked to stand up in a room full of soldiers and say what they knew.

* * *

The morning proceeded. Odell called Sergeant Brack, who testified to finding the cache. He called the hall's caretaker, who confirmed the Vane family had access. He produced volume after volume and read entries in a voice like a man counting coins into a beggar's hat — *advanced to M. Vane, wages against finds; received, pearls, thirty-one count; balance carried* — and each entry landed in the silent room like a coin on wood.

Then it was my turn.

I had planned, in the sleepless hours before dawn, to be careful. To be a clerk. To answer only what was asked. But when I laid my hand on the first volume and saw Odell watching me with that patient, ledger-ruled face, something in me went quiet and cold and clear, the way the water goes at sixty feet down when my father used to say you could hear your own heart deciding things.

"Madam Vane," I said, "you've heard the entries read. Do you dispute them?"

"Not the numbers," Maren said. "The sums are right. My husband's son copied most of them." A ripple of something — laughter, almost — went through the benches. "It's the story the numbers tell that's a lie."

"Explain," Ashe said.

"My husband died owning the company eleven pounds, fourteen shillings. That's what the book says. I don't dispute it. What I dispute is that the debt was still alive. He paid it. Paid it off whole, in the spring, with the season's take. There was a settlement drawn up. He signed it. The factor signed it. And then the page went away, and the debt came back from the dead, and worked him underwater for two more seasons until the water kept him."

"You have proof of this settlement?" Ashe asked.

"It would be in the books," Maren said mildly. "If the books were complete."

Every head turned to Odell. I watched him do it — the tiny recalibration, less than a heartbeat, before the smooth surface closed again. "Volumes do lose pages," he said to Ashe, shrugging gently. "Harbor damp, rats, careless clerks. I'm sure if such a settlement existed—"

"I copy these volumes," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Four nights a week, since the requisition. I can testify to the condition of every binding. There is no flood damage in the archive room. There are no rats. There is one page razored out of the settlement register, cut close to the stitching, by someone who knew the volumes well enough to know which one."

"Which would be you," Odell said softly.

The room went very still.

"Yes," I said. "It would easily be me. I'm the only person besides yourself with the key, isn't that so? Which is why I've wondered, sir, why you never reported the loss. A man of your exactness. I've seen you reprimand a junior clerk for a smudged figure. Yet a whole page — a *settlement* — vanishes, and you file nothing, ask nothing, search nowhere." I turned to Ashe. "Colonel, ask him why. Ask him what was on it."

Ashe set down his untouched coffee. For a moment I thought — hoped — feared — he might. His eyes moved between us, two clerks with our pens drawn across each other's throats, and I saw the pragmatist in him doing sums: one dead diver's account against the smooth running of a requisitioned port, one suspicious page against a functioning harvest of labor and tonnage. I watched him decide not to care, and it was one of the loneliest calculations I ever witnessed.

"This court is sitting on the matter of stolen pearls," Ashe said. "Not the archival hygiene of a trading house. Continue with the charges, Mr. Odell."

* * *

The verdict took the tribunal eleven minutes. Guilty. Sentence deferred pending — and here Odell's voice achieved a warmth I had never heard in it — *further inquiry into the extent of the divers' concealed holdings,* meaning my mother would sit in the guardhouse and be squeezed like a fig until she named every family that had ever buried so much as a button.

As the soldiers took her arms, she paused beside me. Up close she smelled of salt and soap, unhurried as ever, as though the guardhouse were simply another tide she'd wait out.

"The tallies," she murmured. "All forty families. You know where I keep them."

I did. Not in a floor cavity, not under a dance floor. In her. In the way she'd made me recite them back to her over supper for years — *again, Quentin, the Kess account, again* — and I had thought it was a game, or a mother's odd idea of arithmetic drills. Forty families, every advance, every cheat, every death at sea, every pearl brought up wet into the light and shorted at the scale. Kept the only place requisition orders couldn't reach.

"Visit me Thursday," she said, louder, for the soldiers' benefit. "Bring my mending. And tell Tomas the roof wants seeing to." Which meant nothing to anyone in the room except me, and Tomas, and the forty families, and possibly — knowing her — the tide itself.

They marched her out through the double doors, past the shelves of ledgers, and she did not look at the books once. That was the thing I understood last of all that long day. She didn't look at them because to her they had never been witnesses. They were only the opposition's bad memory, and she had the good one.

* * *

That night I sat in the requisition office with a candle stub and my copies spread out around me, and I let myself feel the fury properly, all of it, the whole burning weight — at Odell, at Ashe, at the lieutenant who couldn't spell my mother's name, at myself for nineteen years of believing that whoever held the pen held the truth.

Then I blew out the candle, because fury makes poor lamplight, and began to think instead.

Odell had destroyed the page to protect himself. Very well. The page was gone beyond recovery — but not beyond *reconstruction.* I had copies of the surrounding register entries, dated, initialed, in my own hand. The settlement would have followed the company's own formulas; I had copied enough settlements to reproduce the arithmetic in my sleep. And the divers' tallies — my mother's living ledger, checked against hunger for twenty years — could supply the amounts, the dates, the witnesses. Between my ink and their memory, the missing page could be rebuilt, line by line.

Rebuilt as what, though? A forgery. If exposed — and Odell would flay it open in an hour, he knew every quirk of the original hand, because the original hand was *his* — it would hang Quentin Vane alongside whatever they did to Maren. Sergeant Ivo's face floated up in the dark, that question he'd asked me on the quay: *what would your mother's name be worth?*

Worth a gallows, apparently. Mine, possibly both of ours.

I struck a match to find my pen, and my hand was shaking, and I noticed — with the strange detached humor that had lately taken up residence in me like a lodger who pays no rent — that for the first time in my life I was about to write an entry that was false in every figure and true in every fact.

The candle needed relighting after all. There was a great deal of work to do before Thursday, and the company's own forms, thank God, came in triplicate.
