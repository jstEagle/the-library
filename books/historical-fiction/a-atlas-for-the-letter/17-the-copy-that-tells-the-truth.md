## 17. The Copy That Tells the Truth

The lamp in Sergeant Ivo's quarters had a cracked chimney, and the light it threw was the color of weak tea. Quentin sat at a table meant for mending boots, and across from him, in his shirtsleeves with his tunic hung over a chair like a man who'd given up on being an army for the evening, sat Ivo Danner, conscript, son of a laundress in some northern garrison town, keeper of a mother's name he could not stop asking about.

Between them lay the materials of what Quentin's grandmother would have called a mortal sin and what Quentin had come to think of as bookkeeping.

"Say it again," Ivo said. "Slowly. Like you're explaining it to a magistrate. Because one day you might be."

"The settlement page," Quentin said. "Entry for the vessel *Kestrel's Wake*, diver Aldous Vane, account settled in full on the ninth of March, four years past. Payment received by the company factor Odell, countersigned—" He stopped.

"Countersigned by whom?"

"That's the trouble. I never saw the countersignature. The page was torn out before I ever knew to look for it."

Ivo rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "So we are going to certify a document that neither of us has seen. You understand that if they catch us, they won't hang me for forgery. They'll hang me for treason, because I'm wearing the uniform, and treason is the more economical charge. One rope, no paperwork."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since you put that copied page on my table." He poured something into two cups — not army issue; it smelled of plums and desperation, a private contraband. "My mother used to say that a lie told carefully is just a story with good posture. She told stories beautifully. She also spent six months in the stocks for it." He pushed a cup toward Quentin. "Drink. If we're damned, we may as well be warm."

* * *

They worked three nights.

The first night was for the paper itself. It could not be requisition-office stock — Ashe's clerks counted their foolscap like ammunition. Ivo solved this with the shameless efficiency of a career soldier: he simply stole it, a dozen sheets at a time over a week, folded inside dirty laundry sent down to the garrison wash. "No one searches linen," he said. "It's beneath everyone's dignity, including mine."

Quentin brought the ink. Not the counting house ink, which had a violet cast under lamplight that any factor would know at a glance, but a bottle of oak-gall he mixed himself in Maren's kitchen, following a recipe she remembered from her father, who had been a boat-painter before he was anything else. The color came out brown-black and honest-looking, like something that had been sitting in a drawer for years.

"It needs to look *lived with*," Quentin said, holding a trial page up to the lamp. "A document pulled from the files during a requisition wouldn't be crisp. It would be handled. Creased where someone folded it badly. Coffee-ringed, maybe."

"Coffee-ringed," Ivo repeated, delighted despite himself. "You're a natural criminal, Vane. Has anyone ever told you?"

"My employer used to tell me I had no imagination whatsoever."

"And look at you now."

The second night was for the hand. This was the hard part, and Quentin knew it before they began, because the page had to carry two hands: the clerk's entry and Odell's signature. He had spent six years copying other men's writing — that was the whole of his trade — and he had copied Odell's more than most, because Odell had signed everything, invoices, receipts, the margin notes that ruined divers without their knowing it. Quentin closed his eyes and let his hand remember.

The first attempt was too neat. The second too nervous. On the third, he stopped thinking about Odell and started thinking about the specific boredom of the man — how Odell's pen grew heavy at the ends of words, as though each signature were a small weight he was setting down and walking away from.

"There," Ivo said quietly. "That's him. That's the man who shorted my pay twice."

"You never met him."

"I don't need to have met him. I know him. Everyone poor knows him."

* * *

On the third night, Maren came.

She arrived after the harbor curfew, which meant she arrived the way the divers always arrived, along the sea wall at low tide, wrapped in oilskin, smelling of salt and woodsmoke. She stood in the doorway of Ivo's quarters dripping on the floorboards, and Ivo, to his credit, only looked briefly alarmed.

"Sergeant," she said. "You're the one who asked my son what his mother's name would be worth."

"Yes, ma'am."

"It's Maren. And tonight it's worth whatever you say it is." She set a bundle on the table and unwrapped it: the divers' tallies, not written but spoken, and now — for this night only — spoken aloud while Quentin wrote them down in a fair hand. Old Sef's count of the *Kestrel's Wake*'s catch in its final season, kept in forty-one knots of cord and recited every solstice. The widow Brakke's memory of the payment date, because she had been on the dock when the money changed hands and had remarked upon it, as widows do. Her own recollection — flat, unhesitating — of watching Odell tear the page from the book with his own hands, four years ago, in the back office, believing no one saw.

"I saw," Maren said. "I said nothing, because saying something gets a diver's family struck from the rolls, and the rolls were bread. But I remember it the way I remember my own wedding day. Worse, actually. I liked my wedding day."

Quentin wrote it all. And here was the thing he had not expected — the thing that made his pen tremble slightly against his will: the oral record *agreed*. Knot-count matched widow's memory matched Maren's witness. Four independent keepings of the same truth, none of them written, all of them intact, converging on the ninth of March like three rivers finding one mouth.

"They'll say it's collusion," Ivo warned.

"They'll say whatever they like," Maren said. "Paper lies easily. People have to work at it. Forty-one knots, sergeant, tied by a man who couldn't write his own name, and every knot agrees with a widow who can't read. Explain that away."

* * *

They assembled the extract near dawn.

Quentin laid out the certification exactly as Harrow & Lyle certified everything: the heading, the file reference, the phrase *true and correct extract taken from the debt-book of the port accounts*, the date, the two signatures required — one clerical, one authorizing. For the authorizing signature he used the name of old Master Corben, the senior clerk, dead these two years, whose hand Quentin knew better than his own and whose authority no one living at the requisition office would think to question, because dead men's signatures are the most trusted signatures there are. No one audits the dead.

"That's the part that hangs us," Ivo observed, watching the name dry. "Not Odell. Not even the forgery itself. Corben. They'll take the desecration of a respected man's name personally."

"He'd have signed it," Quentin said. "If he were alive, he'd have signed it. He was the one who taught me that a ledger is only as honest as the last person to open it."

"Did he teach you that?"

"No. I'm teaching myself. But he'd have agreed."

Then the aging. Maren took the finished page out to the kitchen and held it over the smoke of a damp fire until it took on the soft gray-brown of a document that had slept in a drawer through damp winters. She creased it once, badly, on purpose. She pressed it under a wet kettle to give it a gentle warp. When she brought it back, Quentin held it beside his memory of the original files and felt something between pride and vertigo.

It was beautiful. It was a lie. It was truer than anything else in the building it was about to be entered into.

"I keep waiting to feel worse," he admitted.

"Don't wait," Maren said. "Feeling worse is a luxury for people whose mothers aren't in a cell awaiting transport. Feel accurate instead." She touched the page once, lightly, the way she might touch a hull checking for rot. "Four years I carried that day in my head with nowhere to put it. Now it has somewhere to go. Paper's just a bucket, boy. Tonight we're carrying water in it."

* * *

The problem of delivery solved itself in the worst possible way, which is to say it arrived wearing a uniform and knocking politely.

Sergeant Ivo answered the knock at mid-morning two days later and found a junior officer with orders: the tribunal reconvening for the Vane woman's case requested all supporting documents filed within twenty-four hours, and further, that any evidence of company malfeasance be submitted through proper channels, meaning through Odell, meaning through the wolf.

"They want it channeled through him?" Quentin said afterward, white-knuckled on the table's edge.

"Of course they do. Ashe runs a tidy war. Everything goes through channels. Odell *is* the channel." Ivo sat down heavily. "Which means we can't smuggle it in. It has to arrive officially. Certified, stamped, logged."

"Logged by whom?"

Silence. Then both men looked at each other, and Quentin saw the shape of it land on Ivo like a dropped pack.

"I'm a garrison clerk," Ivo said slowly. "Documents get logged through me. It's the single most boring fact of my existence." He laughed — a short, unhappy sound. "Do you know I volunteered for clerk duty because I thought it would keep me off the walls? Safest post in the army, I told my mother. Nothing ever happens to a clerk."

"Something is happening to one now."

"Yes." He stood, straightened his tunic, and became, visibly, a soldier again — spine first, then shoulders, then the flat professional face that Quentin had learned to read underneath rather than through. "Here's how it goes. The extract arrives in tomorrow's document run, properly sourced — I'll mark it as recovered from the Harrow & Lyle annex files, which conveniently burned in Tuesday's small fire, so no one can check the original. There *was* a small fire Tuesday. A candle in the records room. Terribly careless of someone."

"You set a fire."

"I burned a wastepaper basket, Vane, let's not promote me to arsonist. But it gives the extract a provenance and the annex a plausible absence." He picked up the forged page with two fingers, delicately, as though it might go off. "One thing remains. It has to pass Odell's eye before it reaches the tribunal. He'll see it in the morning bundle. And if he recognizes the trap — if he looks at that signature and knows what it means —"

"He'll destroy it."

"He'll try to destroy it quietly, and then he'll come looking for whoever filed it, and he will find me, because I filed it, and I am expendable in ways you are not." Ivo set the page down and slid it into an official folio with a steadiness that Quentin suspected cost him considerably. "So before I do this, I want to hear you say it plainly. What does your father's name buy? Walk me through it. Because my mother's name bought me a beating from my first sergeant and a lifelong suspicion of uniforms, and I've never regretted the price. I want to know what I'm buying at this rate."

Quentin thought of his father, of whom he had only the ledger line and the drowned fist and Maren's face when she spoke of him. He thought of forty-one knots. He thought of the word *settled*, which he had copied ten thousand times in other men's accounts and never once in his own.

"It buys the truth entering the record," he said. "Officially. Stamped. Countersigned. Once it's in the file, Odell can't tear it out again without tearing out himself — because now people will be *looking*. That's all a record is, sergeant. It's a room full of people looking. He's ruled four years in the dark."

"And if the tribunal throws it out anyway?"

"Then it's still in the file. Ashamed, maybe. Disbelieved. But filed. And files outlive tribunals." Quentin heard his own voice and was startled by it — steady, certain, a clerk's voice, a believer's voice. "I spent my whole life thinking ledgers belonged to the men who owned the paper. I was wrong. They belong to whoever tells the truth in them. We're telling it."

Ivo regarded him for a long moment. Outside, a patrol went by, boots keeping time on the cobbles, the whole requisitioned town marching to somebody else's count.

"Dangerous faith," Ivo said at last, almost gently. "But faith's the only supply the army never issues, so I suppose a man has to find his own." He tucked the folio under his arm. "Tomorrow morning, then. Pray to whatever keeps books in heaven, Vane. And pray it's a forgiving auditor."

He left. Quentin sat alone in the tea-colored lamplight, listening to the tide come in below the window, and did not sleep.

In the morning, the document run went out. By noon, word reached the divers' quarter that moved through it faster than any official dispatch: Odell had been seen at the tribunal offices before dawn, alone, with a lantern. And by evening, the summons came — not for Ivo, not yet — but for Quentin Vane, clerk, to appear before Colonel Ashe at eight o'clock, regarding *a matter concerning the integrity of certain documents*.

Maren squeezed his arm at the door of the guardhouse. Whatever waited inside, one thing was already certain: the page had landed. The water was in the bucket, and someone upstairs had gotten wet.
