## 18. Ashes in the Counting House

The word *unofficial* was spoken by a man with flour still on his cuff, and it undid everything.

Quentin stood at the back of the requisition office, among the clerks and the smell of cold tallow, and watched Colonel Ashe turn the certified extract over in his long fingers as though it were a fish of doubtful freshness. Sergeant Ivo stood to one side, at attention, his face arranged into the particular blankness Quentin had come to recognize as armor. Maren sat where they had put her — a straight-backed chair between two soldiers, her wrists chafed from a night in the cell below the customs shed. She did not look defeated. She looked like a woman doing arithmetic, and Quentin knew whose columns she was running, because he was running them too.

"This extract," Ashe said, "claims to be drawn from the Harrow & Lyle debt-book, folio forty-one. Is that correct?"

"It is a true copy," Quentin said. His voice held. He had practiced holding it all night, lying on the floor of Ivo's quarters, staring at the underside of a bunk. "Every figure. Every name. The settlement of Aldous Vane's indenture, entered the eleventh of March, seven years past."

"By whom?"

"By the company's own hand. Countersigned by the factor."

"Ah." Ashe set the paper down with terrible gentleness. "And the original page?"

"Missing," said Odell.

Odell had not been asked to speak. He rose anyway from his place near the window, smoothing his waistcoat, and Quentin saw that he had dressed for the occasion — dark wool, sober cravat, the costume of a grieving administrator. Seven years ago that same man had sat across a desk from Quentin and explained, kindly, that boys without fathers learned arithmetic faster. Now he folded his hands and addressed the colonel as though delivering a sermon.

"The original page cannot be produced, sir, because it does not exist. It has never existed. What we have here is a fabrication — a clever one, I grant you — assembled by a clerk who spent years inside our books and a woman who has spent years nursing a grievance against the company that fed her." He turned to Maren and inclined his head, almost courteously. "I understand the temptation, Mrs. Vane. Grief makes forgers of us all."

"You made a forger of me before I ever grieved," Maren said.

"There," Odell said softly, to the room rather than to her. "You see."

Ashe looked at the extract again, then at Quentin. There was no cruelty in his face; that was the worst of it. There was only the mild, tired attention of a man balancing an army's accounts against a town's lives, and finding the arithmetic inconvenient either way.

"Clerk Vane," he said. "Who witnessed this extract?"

"I did," said Ivo.

"And who else? A magistrate? A company officer? Anyone whose signature carries weight outside this room?"

The silence answered him. Quentin felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way the harbor pilings shifted when a heavy ship passed. Every lawful door, closing one after another down a long corridor. He heard each latch click.

"No one," he said.

"Then it is a curiosity," Ashe said, "and curiosities do not acquit thieves. The pearls were company property, taken from company waters, hidden in company ground. Mrs. Vane's possession of them is not disputed — only her story about why." He rose, and the room rose with him. "She will be transported with the records aboard the *Meridian*. As will you, Clerk Vane. You have demonstrated a talent for improvisation that the company may find useful elsewhere, and a loyalty that this town has evidently finished with."

"Sir—" Ivo began.

"Sergeant." Ashe did not raise his voice. "You vouched for this man's copying. I am not blaming you for being deceived. But if you interrupt me again about it, you will explain yourself at a court-martial instead of a mess table. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Both Vanes sail with the records. Odell, prepare the manifests. This office closes its accounts tomorrow night." Ashe gathered his papers, paused, and — Quentin would remember this all his life — added, almost as an aside: "For what it is worth, Clerk Vane, I believe your mother believes her own story. Belief is a fine thing. It simply weighs nothing."

* * *

They were given until evening to settle their affairs, under escort, which meant they were given an hour in their own kitchen with a soldier in the doorway picking at his nails.

Maren put water on to boil though neither of them wanted tea. It was something her hands needed to do. Quentin sat at the table where he had copied his first letters, tracing the grain of the wood, and tried to feel what a man was supposed to feel when every door closed. He found, to his surprise, that he mostly felt tired — and beneath the tiredness, something small and stubborn that would not lie down.

"He tore out the page," Quentin said quietly. "Years ago. Before the army ever came. He must have — when father's settlement went through, or just after. Why keep the book at all, why keep *us* at all, if he'd already—"

"Because a debt unpaid is a leash," Maren said. "Your father paid it. Every pearl, every season, eleven years of my husband's lungs filling with salt water so that Odell could write *paid* in a book and then eat the page." She poured the water over nothing — there was no tea left in the house; the army had requisitioned that too — and watched it steam. "He kept the leash because leashes are useful. And now the army holds the collar end."

"They're sending us away tomorrow."

"Yes."

"With the records."

"Yes." She looked at him then, and Quentin saw that she had already walked further down the road than he had. Mothers always did. "And once we are on that ship, my love, we are ink. We become an entry. Transported per manifest, debts outstanding. And nobody, nowhere, ever, reads an entry and wonders what happened next."

The soldier in the doorway coughed. Maren raised her voice pleasantly. "Would you like tea? We haven't any, but I could boil you some water, it's very restful."

The soldier declined. Quentin caught himself almost smiling, and the smile frightened him more than despair would have, because it meant some part of him was still planning.

* * *

Night came down on the harbor like a lid. From the kitchen window Quentin could see the *Meridian* at her moorings, her ports glowing, crates going up her side in a slow procession of swinging lanterns — the town's paper life ascending into the hold, folio by folio. Somewhere in those crates sat seven years of the divers' seasons, Tomas's boat-debt, old Wrenna's widow's portion, every family in the quarter reduced to figures in a factor's hand. And somewhere among them, the gap where folio forty-one should have been, torn ragged at the spine, the one absence that proved everything and could prove nothing.

Ivo found him at the window. There was no knock; there hadn't been a knock between them since the trial, since the forged extract, since the question Ivo had asked in the dark of the guardroom — *what would your mother's name be worth?* — and Quentin had answered, *everything,* and Ivo had said nothing for a long time and then, *mine too.*

"They move the crates at dawn," Ivo said. "Manifests sealed tonight. After that, even I can't open a hatch without three signatures." He set something on the table: a ring of keys, dull brass, unremarkable. "Requisition office. Side door off the coal passage. The sentry rotates at the half hour; you'll have four minutes of blind spot, no more."

Quentin stared at the keys. "Ivo. If they find out—"

"They won't find out. That's rather the point of giving them to you instead of using them myself." Ivo's jaw worked. "My mother's name is Brigit. She lives above a bakery in Corren's Cross and she thinks I enlisted because of the bounty, and she writes to me about the price of flour." He laughed, briefly, horribly. "Four minutes, Vane. Whatever you mean to do in there, do it in four minutes."

"What do you think I mean to do?"

"I've carried your copies around in my jacket for a week." Ivo looked at him steadily. "A man doesn't copy a ledger that carefully unless he means to answer it somehow. And a man whose every lawful door has shut—" he shrugged, a small, exhausted motion, "—starts looking at windows. I'm telling you the window's open. What you climb through is your own affair."

He left the way he had come, and Quentin sat alone with the keys, and the lanterns rising up the *Meridian*'s side, and the small stubborn thing inside him finally standing all the way up.

* * *

His mother stopped him at the door with one hand flat against his chest, the way she had when he was six and the tide was wrong.

"No," she said.

"You don't know what I—"

"A mother knows." Her palm was rough as wharf timber. "Whatever you've got in your pocket, whatever Ivo gave you, whatever is turning behind your eyes — no. They'll hang a diver's son for less than what you're thinking. They'll hang him and call it tidying up."

"Then tell me another door," Quentin said, and his voice cracked on it, and he hated that, and kept going. "Tell me the lawful door, Mam. Tell me the magistrate who'll hear us. Tell me which entry in their books says a drowned diver's wife gets to keep her house. You told me yourself — once we're on that ship we're ink. Well, I am done being ink. I am done watching other men write us."

Maren was silent a long moment. Out in the street, a patrol went by, boots keeping time, and both of them stood still as furniture until it passed.

"When your father died," she said at last, very quietly, "they gave me his effects in a canvas bag. One boot, one knife, and a piece of ledger paper gone soft with seawater, fused to his fist. I couldn't read it then. I can read it now — I've had seven years to learn every figure that man ever wrote." She took her hand from his chest. "That page was the settlement, Quentin. He died holding proof the debt was paid, and Odell let us believe otherwise for seven years because a believing debtor dives cheaper. So no — I don't have another door for you." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "But you'll look at me first, and you'll tell me true: what happens to you after?"

"I don't know."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have."

Maren studied him the way she used to study the water before a dive — reading weather underneath weather. Then she did something she had not done since he was small: she pulled him to her, salt and soap and lamp oil, her chin hard on the top of his head.

"Four minutes," she said into his hair. "Ivo told you four minutes and he undersold it because he loves you. Take six. Your father always took six, and he was right every time but the last."

* * *

The coal passage smelled of tar and old fires. Quentin went through the side door on the half-hour bell, exactly as instructed, and the requisition office received him with the particular silence of rooms full of paper — a listening silence, shelf upon shelf of it, thousands of pages that had spent lifetimes deciding what people were worth.

He had planned this the way he planned everything: precisely, twice, in the dark behind his eyes. He would take folio forty-one's neighbors — the binding stub, the ragged edge, physical proof of the tear. He would take Odell's private day-book, which Ivo had seen locked in the factor's desk and which Quentin was certain held the other half of the story. He would take enough, and no more, and they would have their case by morning, and every lawful door would swing open again, and—

His candle found the ledgers.

Row upon row of them. The debt-books, the tallies, the indenture rolls, the freight manifests, the berth list itself with its careful ranks and prices. Seven years of his handwriting in the margins. Twenty years of the town's breathing pressed flat between covers, each life rendered as a sum, each sum held over a family like weather. If he took the pages that proved Odell's theft, the rest stayed whole. The debts stayed whole. Tomas's boat-debt, Wrenna's widow's portion, the divers' seasons owed at half price forever — all of it sailing away intact aboard the *Meridian*, lawful and load-bearing and drowning them just the same.

And if he burned it —

The thought arrived whole, the way his mother said the sea sometimes hands you a wave you didn't see coming, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. Burn the books and the debts die with them. Burn the books and Odell's proofs die too — every falsified figure, every eaten page, gone to ash where no court could ever resurrect them. Burn the books and the town owed nothing to anyone, ever again, except what people remembered — and the divers remembered. Maren remembered. Twenty families' worth of tally recited on docks and in kitchens, handed down like songs. Memory, kept in people rather than paper.

Burn the books and lose his own name in every record — transported per manifest, deceased per rumor, unwritten. A boy who copied other people's names into ledgers all his life, ending as a blank margin.

The candle guttered. Somewhere down the passage, boots. The sentry, early, or the half-hour come round twice while he stood dreaming.

Quentin looked at the nearest ledger — folio forty-one's cousins, its siblings, its accomplices — and did the only arithmetic that had ever come easily to him.

He struck the candle into the crate of packing wadding beside the shelves, and the flame stood up in the dark like something that had been waiting years for the invitation.

"Six minutes," he whispered, to his father, to his mother, to whoever kept count now that the books wouldn't. "I'm taking six."
