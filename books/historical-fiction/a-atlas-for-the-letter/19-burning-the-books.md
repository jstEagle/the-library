## 19. Burning the Books

The lamp in the requisition office had burned down to its last finger of oil, which meant Quentin had perhaps an hour of darkness left before the sentries changed and the world insisted on existing again. He stood at Odell's desk with a candle stub he'd taken from the quartermaster's drawer — stolen from one pocket to be hidden in another, which was, he reflected, more or less the story of his whole life.

The ledgers were stacked in front of him. Eleven volumes, bound in gray calf, their spines cracked from years of his own hands opening them. He knew these books better than he knew his own face. He knew that Volume Six had a water stain shaped like a fish on page forty. He knew that Volume Nine's binding had been repaired with sail thread, badly, by some clerk before him who must also have been tired and underpaid and invisible. He had copied ten thousand names out of these pages, and not once — not once — had anyone copied his.

He thought about that now, standing there with the candle trembling in his fingers.

For nineteen years he had believed what every clerk believes: that the ledger is the world. That what is written down is true, and what is true gets written down, and everything else — the smell of salt on Maren's shawl, the way Tomas laughed through his nose, the sound the divers made when they came up gasping on winter mornings, half laugh and half prayer — all of that was just weather. Unrecorded. Therefore unreal.

Then Odell tore a page out of Volume Four and proved that the ledger could lie. And tonight, when Quentin had gone looking for the forged extract's fate — the little document he and Ivo had built out of memory and desperation — he had found instead Odell's private correspondence, stacked neat as communion wafers: letters to the colonial office, letters to Harrow & Lyle's home office, each one a small elegant knife aimed at the town. *Irregularities among the diving population. Recommend transportation of principal agitators.* Agitators. That was Maren. That word meant his mother.

The lawful doors had closed, one after another, all evening. The extract was unofficial; Odell had seen to it within hours. The magistrate would not hear testimony without certified records; the certified records said what Odell wanted them to say. There was no door left except the one nobody used because it led nowhere — the truth that none of it mattered if the paper stopped existing.

You cannot falsify a debt that has no record. You cannot transport a woman for stealing pearls from a company that can no longer prove it owned them. And you cannot hang a clerk for erasing a page that was already torn out — or so Quentin told himself, in the voice of a man walking toward a cliff with excellent posture.

He set the candle down. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He had expected them to shake like Ivo's did when Ivo talked about his mother's name, that soft terrible question — *what would yours be worth?* — but his hands were calm as two birds settled on a rail.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty office, because it seemed wrong to do this in silence and wronger to do it without witnesses, even witnesses made of furniture. "Gentlemen of the counting house. I have an announcement."

The filing cabinets did not object. In eleven years of service, they never had.

* * *

He began, perversely, with Volume One, because it was where he had begun at nine years old, copying his first entry in a hand so careful it looked frightened. *J. Pruitt, rope-maker, owes for canvas, 3 pounds 4 shillings.* Old Pruitt was dead six years now. What was owed to a dead man? Nothing. What did a dead man owe? Also nothing, if you asked the right person, and Quentin had finally found the right person, and the right person was a boy with a candle.

There was a brazier in the corner — the army kept coals in it for sealing wax and for the officers' tea, because Colonel Ashe considered lukewarm tea a form of insubordination. Quentin carried Volume One over and opened it to the first page.

He had planned to feed it in slowly, page by page, methodical, the way he did everything. Instead his hand simply let go, and the book fell open into the coals, and the fire took it with a sound like a long-held breath released.

It went faster than he'd imagined. That was the first surprise. Paper wants to burn; it has been waiting its whole life to burn; ink and vellum go up like a rumor in a small town. The second surprise was the light. It filled the room the way no lamp ever had — orange and greedy and warm, throwing shadows up the walls until the shelves looked like a forest at sunset. Quentin stood in front of it and felt something he had no word for, and he was a man whose entire trade was words, so he made one up on the spot: *unburdenment.* The feeling of setting down a sack you had forgotten you were carrying.

Volume Two. The fish-shaped stain on Volume Six's page forty darkened, curled, and became smoke. He fed them in one by one, and each one whispered as it went — the whisper of ten thousand debts being forgiven by nobody, which is the only way debts are truly forgiven, since forgiveness granted by an authority always arrives with conditions stapled on.

"Goodbye, J. Pruitt," he murmured. "Goodbye, Mrs. Calloway's egg money. Goodbye, four generations of the Sandoval family owing eleven shillings for a boat that sank in '41." He paused, candle-light flickering across his face. "Goodbye, Vane."

His own name was in Volume Four. Had been. Entry after entry, his father's debt compounding like a tide coming in, and then — nothing, because the proof of its settlement was the page Odell had torn out and burned years ago, or hidden, or eaten, for all anyone knew. Well. Now both halves matched. Now the ledger agreed with the sea, which had never kept accounts anyway.

He was three volumes from the end when the door handle turned.

* * *

Sergeant Ivo came in sideways, the way he did everything, a big man trying to take up less room than he did. He had a rifle he never seemed to remember he was carrying and a face built for worrying. He saw the fire. He saw the books. He saw Quentin, sleeves rolled, feeding Volume Eight into the flames with the tenderness of a man burying a friend.

Ivo did not shout. That was the thing about Ivo; the army had given him a uniform and forgotten to give him a temperament. He shut the door behind him very quietly, which was somehow louder than slamming it.

"I thought you might," he said.

"You thought I might?"

"Burn them. Since Tuesday." Ivo crossed the room and stood beside him at the brazier, close enough that the heat pressed against them both like a third conspirator. "You've had the look. My dog had it once, watching a rabbit."

"That's very flattering."

"He caught it, in the end." Ivo nodded at the flames. "So."

"So."

They stood there while Volume Nine went. Quentin could feel the questions stacking up behind Ivo's teeth: *Are you mad? Do you know what they'll do? Is my mother's name in there?* — because it was, everyone's mother's name was in there, that had been the whole horror of it, the ledger held the entire town by the throat and called it bookkeeping.

"The extract," Ivo said finally. "Our beautiful forgery. The one we risked our necks making."

"Worthless now. Odell saw to it."

"And this?" Ivo gestured at the fire with his chin. "This is worth something?"

"This is worth everything, or nothing, depending on tomorrow." Quentin watched the last of Volume Nine curl. "But it's worth the same for everyone. That's new. Everything else in this town has been worth different amounts depending on who's holding the pen."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, startled bark, like a man surprised by his own opinion. "My gran used to say, when the priest's account book got wet in the flood, that God had done the parish a favor." He unslung his rifle and leaned it against the wall, deliberately, a man taking off a coat. "Volume Ten's still on the shelf. You're going slowly. Sentimental?"

"Methodical."

"Worse." But there was no judgment in it. Ivo dragged a stool over and sat, warming his hands, a soldier on picket duty over a fire that was eating his own enlistment papers' cousins. "Odell will have your head for this."

"Odell's proof died with the ledgers. Every falsification, every torn page, every clever little adjustment — it all lived in those books and nowhere else. Without them he isn't a thief; he's just a man with opinions." Quentin fed in Volume Ten. "Without them, *Maren* isn't a thief either. Nobody is anything anymore. We get to start from scratch."

"Nobody gets to start from scratch," Ivo said, but gently. "Somebody always remembers."

"That's rather the point, Ivo."

* * *

The trouble with fires, Quentin discovered around midnight, is that they are bad listeners. He had intended a controlled, dignified destruction — eleven volumes, one brazier, done. But Volume Ten had been fat with pasted-in appendices, company seals, tax schedules, and it burned hot and unhappy, and somewhere around the time the last of it went up, a tongue of flame leapt the brazier's iron rim and found the stack of requisition drafts on Odell's desk.

Quentin slapped at it. The flame went through his fingers like a cat slipping a net and settled into the papers with an air of having found home.

"Oh no," said Quentin.

"No," agreed Ivo, and moved with unexpected speed, hauling the water bucket from the corner — except the bucket held the mops, because the requisition office did not believe in ready water, only in ready paperwork, and paperwork does not put out fires.

They tried the tea urn. Cold. They tried a coat, then a rug, then beating it with the rug, which spread it to the coat. Within minutes the desk was alight, then the drafts, then the shelf of Odell's immaculate correspondence — and Quentin, who should have been horrified, stood back and watched the letters curl and felt the strangest urge to applaud.

"There goes *irregularities among the diving population,*" he said. "There goes *recommend transportation of principal agitators.* Burn well, you miserable little knives."

"You're enjoying this."

"A bit. Yes."

"It's spreading to the wall, Quentin."

That sobered him. Fire, unlike debt, does not respect property lines; it takes everything in the district equally, which was a kind of justice, but not the kind a town needs at two in the morning. Smoke was pooling along the ceiling now, and the flame had found the old varnish on the shelving, and the shelving had found the floorboards, and the counting house — built of sixty-year-old pitch pine and greed — was proving flammable in the way of all things built to hold value rather than people.

"The chimney," Ivo said. "Old buildings burn slow if you keep it low. Or we run."

"If it burns fast, it jumps the alley to the quartering houses. Half the town sleeps next door." Quentin was already moving, dragging the water cart hose from the corridor, thinking with the cold clear part of his mind that had copied ten thousand entries without error: *the fire is doing the necessary work; my job is only to keep it honest.* "Wet blankets on the dividing wall. Go wake the harbor watch — no, stay, I'll go, they know your face and they'd arrest it."

"Charming arrangement," said Ivo, and threw the first blanket.

* * *

What followed, Quentin would remember afterward in fragments, the way you remember weather. Running down the alley hammering on doors, shouting *fire* in a voice he didn't know he had. Mrs. Calloway emerging in her nightcap with the same bucket she'd once hoarded egg money in. The divers coming up from the beach road in twos and threes, silent, efficient, forming a line to the harbor the way they formed lines for the boats, passing buckets hand to hand with a rhythm older than any ledger. Someone's child asleep on someone's shoulder. Tomas, barefoot in the cold, hauling the hose, glancing once at Quentin and once at the burning counting house and saying only, "Yours or theirs?"

"Both," Quentin said, and Tomas nodded as though that were the most reasonable answer in the world, and went back to hauling.

By dawn the counting house was a black ribcage smoking against a gray sky, and the requisition office with it — every draft, every order, every elegant letter — and the town stood around the ruin in the wet morning air, exhausted, singed, and strangely light. Quentin heard old Sandoval say to his son, quietly, "Eleven shillings," and his son say, "What eleven shillings, Da?" and the old man laugh until he had to sit down on a barrel.

Colonel Ashe arrived at seven, impeccable, and surveyed the wreckage with the expression of a man finding a stain on his cuff. Odell was beside him, gray-faced, clutching at nothing, because there was nothing left to clutch.

"Mr. Vane," Ashe said, when the crowd parted and Quentin stood before him smelling of smoke and freedom. "Would you care to explain how the empire's accounting records came to be ash?"

Quentin looked at the colonel, and at Odell, whose eyes were moving in quick animal arithmetic — *the proof is gone, the theft is unprovable, the witness knows it* — and Quentin understood with perfect clarity that the next hour would decide whether he was a criminal or the reason Odell was.

"The ledgers are destroyed," he said steadily. "All of them. Which means every debt in this town is destroyed too. Including," he added, meeting Odell's eye, "the ones that were never honestly entered in the first place."

Behind the crowd, Sergeant Ivo stood at ease with soot on his hands and his face perfectly, professionally blank. And down at the quay, faint on the morning wind, came the whistle of the evacuation ship — sounding early, sounding urgent, sounding, Quentin thought with a drop of the stomach, like a departure that would not wait for anyone, least of all for whatever the colonel decided to do with the clerk who had just confessed to arson.

Ashe looked at the ruin a long moment. Then he took off his glove, finger by finger.

"Start talking, Mr. Vane," he said. "And I would begin with why Mr. Odell looks like a man whose ledgers contained more than arithmetic."

Quentin took a breath, tasted smoke, and began.
