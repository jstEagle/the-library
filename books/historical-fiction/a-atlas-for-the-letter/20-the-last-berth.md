## 20. The Last Berth

The fire announced itself the way all important things in Saltwreck Harbor did: first as rumor, then as argument, and only finally as fact.

Quentin heard it before he saw it — a rising murmur from the quartering streets, voices stacking on voices like tide over tide — and then a glow came through the requisition office windows that had no business existing at two in the morning. He stood among the shelved ledgers with the lamp oil still slick on his hands, and he understood, with the strange calm of a man who has already jumped and is simply waiting to hit water, that there was no undoing it now.

The counting house was burning. The books were burning. Every debt in Saltwreck Harbor was turning into light.

He walked out of the requisition office without hurrying. It seemed important not to hurry. A clerk who runs is a thief; a clerk who walks is perhaps only a man stepping out for air. Two soldiers passed him at a dead sprint in the other direction, buckets swinging, and neither gave him a glance. For nineteen years he had been invisible in this town, and it appeared the gift held even now, when he most needed to be nobody at all.

Then he rounded the corner by the salt sheds and stopped being calm, because the harbor was a riot of orange and shouting, and the *Providence* — the last ship, the berth ship, the arithmetic-made-timber that had governed every waking thought for a week — was warping off the quay with her gangway still down and half her lamps lit, and people were running along the dock beside her like gulls chasing a fishing boat.

"Fire stations!" someone bellowed. "Form fire stations! Lively now!"

Nobody formed anything. The counting house roof collapsed inward with a sound like a great book closing, and a column of sparks went up over the town, and Quentin thought — absurdly, helplessly — of his father's ledger page going up with them, the one Odell had torn out, the one Quentin had rebuilt from memory and ink and Ivo's trembling hand. Well. Let it burn. It had done its work. Maren was free of the transport order; Ashe's men had dragged Odell out of the trial hall an hour before the first flame, and whatever happened next, the paper that had caged her was ash.

The paper that had caged everyone was ash.

"Quentin!" Tomas caught his arm out of the crowd, soot-faced, his daughter Wren riding his shoulders with both fists locked in his hair like a sailor clinging to rigging. "Have you seen her? Your mother — they took her to the trial hall and then everything went sideways and I've lost her in this—"

"She'll be at the dock," Quentin said. "She'll be trying to put you on that ship."

"I'm not going."

"You are," Quentin said, "and so is Wren, and we haven't much time to argue about which of us is right."

* * *

They found Maren at the foot of the *Providence*'s gangway, arguing with a ship's officer who had the harried look of a man whose passenger manifest had just become kindling.

"The list is gone," the officer was saying. "The master's list, the company list, the army list — all of it was in that building, madam. I have berths and I have no authority to fill them. Do you understand what that means? Anyone can board. No one can be refused. And everyone in this town knows it, and they're all coming."

"Then fill them fast," Maren said, "starting with a child."

She turned and saw them — Tomas, Wren, Quentin — and something crossed her face that Quentin had never seen there before, or rather something he had only ever seen aimed outward, at the company, at the sea, at bad weather: a fierce, unguarded tenderness that she would deny to her dying day.

"There you are," she said. "You're filthy. Both of you." She looked past them at the burning counting house, and back. "Was that you?"

Quentin had prepared a lie. He'd been preparing it since sundown, actually — something about sparks, about accident, about soldiers knocking lamps. What came out instead was the truth, plain as a copied line.

"Yes."

Maren studied him for a long moment. Behind her the crowd surged against the quay barriers; ahead of her the ship groaned toward open water.

"Well," she said. "Your father always said you'd amount to something the books couldn't hold." Then, briskly, before the moment could grow any larger than either of them could survive: "Tomas. Wren. Up the gangway. Now."

"No," said Tomas.

"Yes."

"Maren—"

"You have a child," Maren said, and her voice did not rise but somehow filled the whole dock anyway. "I have a town. That's the whole argument, brother, and I've been winning it since you were four years old. Go."

"I won't leave you standing on a quay with a harbor full of soldiers and my sister's son with nothing—"

"He's got more than nothing," Maren said. "He's got me, and forty diving families who owe each other their lives on a tally nobody wrote down. You've got a daughter. Up. The. Gangway."

And that was when Quentin understood that the choice everyone had been dreading — one berth, three names, the terrible arithmetic — had dissolved the way the debts had dissolved, into smoke and freedom. There were no berths anymore. There was only a gangway, and a ship pulling away from a burning town, and the question was no longer *who goes* but *who stays*, and Maren had answered it before anyone thought to ask.

Except.

Except Quentin looked at the gangway, and looked at Wren — six years old, solemn-eyed, with pearl-shell combs in her hair that Maren herself had traded three dives for — and felt the old cold arithmetic reach up one last time and close its hand around his heart.

Because there was room for two. Perhaps three, if people pressed together the way frightened people do. But not five. And his mother was staying. And his uncle would die on that gangplank before he left her. Which meant—

"Come on, Wren," Quentin said, and lifted her off Tomas's shoulders, and carried her up the tilting ramp himself before anyone could stop him, because if he had waited for permission he would never have gone.

The deck was chaos — families without lists, sailors hauling lines, a lieutenant waving people bodily toward the holds. Quentin set Wren down by the rail where Tomas could reach her, and Tomas grabbed his wrist hard enough to bruise.

"You're coming back down," Tomas said. It wasn't a question, but Quentin answered it anyway.

"Yes."

"Quentin—"

"She stays with you," Quentin said. "That's the whole point of me. If I stay down there, then Maren isn't alone, and you're not alone up here, and the family's split even instead of broken. Somebody has to keep the account, Uncle. You know how I am about accounts."

Tomas made a sound that was half laugh and half something much worse, and pulled him into a hug that smelled of salt and soot and Wren's hair.

"Tell your mother—" he started.

"Tell her yourself," Quentin said. "In the spring. Overland. I'll write to you." He hesitated. "Well. Someone will write to you. Half the town will write to you. You'll be sick of letters."

Wren was crying, quietly, the way children cry when they understand more than they say. Quentin crouched to her level.

"Look after your dad," he said. "He gets muddled with accounts. You'll have to keep the tally."

"I can count to a hundred," Wren said, with the wet dignity of the very young.

"Then you're better qualified than the whole Harrow & Lyle Company," Quentin said, "and good riddance to them." He kissed the top of her head, memorizing — the smell of her, the weight, the exact stubbornness of her small arms around his neck — because that was the thing he had learned at last, the thing the whole burning year had been teaching him: you cannot requisition a memory. You can only carry it, or give it away.

Then the sailors were easing the last passengers aboard, and the gangway was swinging free, and Quentin ran down the ramp with the gap widening under his feet and jumped the last four feet to the quay, and behind him he heard Wren shout his name once, high and clear over the water, and did not turn around, because if he turned around he would go back aboard and that was no longer the man he had decided to be.

* * *

The *Providence* cleared the harbor mouth at dawn.

Quentin watched her go from the end of the north mole, standing beside his mother, who had not cried, and beside Sergeant Ivo, who had, briefly and furiously, and claimed afterward that it was smoke.

The ship grew small. The sunrise came up behind her, gold and ordinary, as though the world had not ended and remade itself in a single night. On the quarterdeck, if you squinted, you could see a small figure at the rail, and Maren raised her hand, and Quentin raised his, and the distance swallowed everything else.

"She'll be all right," Maren said. "Your uncle's useless in a crisis but excellent the week after one. She'll be fine."

"I know."

"You did the right thing."

"I know that too," Quentin said. "It doesn't feel like anything yet. Is that normal?"

Maren considered this. "Nothing worth doing ever does. Not at first. Ask me about the day I burned the company's pearl scales sometime — everyone said it was a great victory and I mostly remember being cold." She squeezed his shoulder, once, hard, which from Maren was an embrace sufficient for most funerals and all weddings.

Down the mole, the town was stirring. Not fleeing — stirring. Men were already on the counting house roof with hooks and ladders, pulling down what could be saved, and women were carrying out scorched ledgers — the empty ones, the harmless ones — and laying them open in the sun to dry like fish. Someone had started a kettle. Someone else was arguing loudly that the fire had started in Odell's private office, which everyone agreed was fitting, and which no one intended to investigate further, since the answer might require arresting somebody the town had rather liked lately.

"They'll want to know who did it," Ivo said quietly. He was out of uniform — he had abandoned it somewhere between the trial hall and the dock, folding the jacket neatly over a fence post as though returning a borrowed tool. "Ashe will want a name. There'll be questions, Quentin. There may be a gallows."

"Let them ask," Quentin said.

"You don't understand. They'll ask *you*. You were the clerk. You had access. When they trace it—"

"Then let them trace it," Quentin said. "Here's what I've learned about records, Sergeant: they only hold what someone is willing to enforce. The ledger held our debts because the company had guns. The berth list held our lives because the army had a manifest. Burn the paper and the enforcement goes with it." He shrugged, feeling lighter than he had any right to feel with a harbor full of ash and a family scattered across the sea. "There's no page left that says what I did. Which means it's not a crime anymore. Which means it's just a story. And stories—" he smiled, tired and true, "—stories belong to whoever tells them."

Ivo stared at him. Then he laughed — a short, disbelieving bark, the laugh of a conscript discovering he was free. "You've become a dangerous man, Quentin Vane."

"No," said Maren, already walking back toward the town, toward the smoke and the kettle and the forty families who would need feeding before noon. "He's become a *writer*. God help us all."

* * *

By evening the fires were out and the town had done something Quentin had never seen it do: it had held a meeting that ended in agreement.

They gathered on the ruined dock — divers and fishermen, clerks and netmenders, a few deserting soldiers hovering uncertainly at the edges until Ivo waved them in — and someone brought out the salvaged tally-sticks and knotted cords that the diving families had kept for generations, and Maren stood on an upturned crate with the sunset behind her and began, of all things, to read aloud.

Not from anything. From memory.

"Ferrelo's family, owed eleven days' labor taken without pay, winter of the blockade. The widow Sarn, owed her husband's dive-share, three seasons back. The Okan family, owed—" and on, and on, name after name, debt after debt, and here was the miracle that Quentin felt in his chest like a second heartbeat: nobody checked her. Nobody could check her. The proof had burned. And it didn't matter, because forty families nodded at each name and said *yes, that's true, that's ours,* and the truth of it lived in the nodding, in the crowd, in the place no requisition officer could ever search.

When she finished, the silence lasted a long moment, and then Ferrelo — old, half-deaf, dive-bent Ferrelo — said: "Someone should write those down. Before we old ones go and take the tallies with us."

And every head on that dock turned toward Quentin.

He looked at the faces. His mother's, expectant and amused. Ivo's, encouraging. Old Ferrelo's, patient as stone. Somewhere beyond the horizon, a girl of six was learning to count to a hundred, and a ship was carrying his family toward a life he would only ever know through letters.

"All right," Quentin said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he didn't care. "But I'm warning you all now — I'm done writing what people owe. From tonight it's only what people did, and what they were, and what they refused." He found a charred stick in the rubble of the counting house steps, and a plank dry enough to take a mark, and he set the stick against the wood and discovered that his hand was steady.

"First entry," he said. "'Maren Vane, pearl diver, kept every debt in her head for nineteen years, and when the day came, forgave most of them.'"

"I forgave *some* of them," Maren said.

"*Most*," Quentin repeated, writing, and the dock laughed, and far out past the mole the tide came in over the ashes as it always had, keeping its own accounts, owing nothing to anyone.

He was three names in when the boy arrived — breathless, barefoot, one of the harbor-watch children — with word that changed the shape of the spring: a rider on the coast road, flying Colonel Ashe's colors, carrying papers for the town of Saltwreck Harbor, and asking, by name, for the clerk Quentin Vane.

Maren and Quentin looked at each other across the plank.

"Well," she said. "History's at the door again."

Quentin put down the charred stick, and this time — for the first time in his life — he went to answer it himself.
