## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The watch lay open on the bench like a small wounded animal, its heart exposed to the lamplight.

Ansel Nakamura bent over it, his loupe screwed into his right eye, and lifted the balance wheel with tweezers that had been his father's before they were his. The spring beneath was fouled with grit—sand from the harbor road, carried in on some gentleman's waistcoat pocket, worked deep into the mechanism until the whole watch had begun to die by inches. He could fix it. He could fix almost anything with gears in it. That had never once been the problem.

The problem was the shop around him, and the man who owned it, and the town beyond both.

"Garrick's," said the sign above the door in flaking gilt letters, and below that, smaller: *Clocks & Watches Repaired. Prompt Attention Given.* The word *prompt* had been repainted twice in Ansel's three years there; the rest had been allowed to weather. The shop itself was narrow as a coffin stood on end, shelved floor to ceiling with clocks in every stage of surrender—a grandfather case missing its pendulum, a carriage clock with a cracked crystal, a wall regulator whose face had gone yellow the color of old teeth. They ticked against one another out of rhythm, a room full of hearts beating at cross purposes, and Ansel had long ago stopped hearing them the way other people heard noise. To him it was weather. It was simply what the air was made of here.

He seated the balance jewel, adjusted the beat, and held the watch to his ear.

*Tick. Tick.* Steady as a heartbeat. He set it down, closed the case back over the movement with a soft press of his palm, and only then looked up at the boy waiting on the stool across the counter—the fishmonger's son, twelve perhaps, watching him with the frank, unguarded stare children still permitted themselves before they learned better.

"Is it true you're half Japanese?" the boy asked.

Ansel's hands did not pause. He had learned that much in his first month at the shop: never let them see your hands pause. "It's true my mother was Japanese," he said, folding a chamois cloth around the watch. "And my father was English. Which makes me entirely me."

The boy frowned at this arithmetic, found nothing in it to argue with, and left with his father's watch and a coin less in his pocket. Through the window Ansel watched him go down the cobbled lane toward the harbor, past the fish carts and the rope walks, and saw him turn to say something to a friend, and saw the friend look back at the shop window—at *him*—with that particular expression he knew better than his own reflection. Not hostility, exactly. Curiosity with a wall built around it. The look people gave a dog they weren't sure wouldn't bite, or a clock they weren't sure kept honest time.

He turned back to the bench.

There were eleven more jobs waiting in the drawer, each wrapped in its paper docket in Garrick's cramped hand. A mantel clock from Trewin the solicitor that struck seven when it meant six. A lady's pendant watch with a mainspring like a snapped nerve. Ansel sorted them into order—not by when they'd arrived but by how long each owner could be made to wait without complaint—and began on the mantel clock, because Trewin complained to everyone, and Garrick cared about complaints the way a ship cares about barnacles: not at all until they slowed him down.

His father would have started differently. His father would have started with the hardest one, the one that taught him something, because Kenji Nakamura had believed—or had said he believed, in his careful accented English—that a craftsman's morning should begin with the thing most worth doing. But his father was four years in the churchyard on the hill now, under a stone that read *KENJI NAKAMURA, CLOCKMAKER*, and no more, and the shop where he had briefly been partner before the partnership soured belonged wholly to Ambrose Garrick, and Ansel had learned to work the way Garrick wanted or not at all.

Not at all was the alternative. He tried not to think about it too precisely, but the shape of it sat always at the edge of things: no position, no reference, a half-Japanese apprentice with a foreign name in a coastal town that tolerated him the way a tidepool tolerates a crab—because he was small, and because removing him seemed like effort. There had been talk, after his father died, of sending him to an aunt in London he had never met. The aunt had never written back. So he stayed, and swept the floor, and fixed the watches, and told himself every morning that this was patience rather than captivity, though some mornings he could not have sworn to the difference.

The doorbell rattled.

Mrs. Penhale came in trailing the smell of lavender and coal smoke, wanting her kitchen clock looked at—"it loses a quarter hour a day, Mr. Garrick, I'm sure it's spiteful"—and Ansel explained, for the fourth time, that a clock did not lose time out of spite but out of worn pivots, and that Mr. Garrick would attend to it, and Mrs. Penhale looked past him through the curtain to the back room where Garrick took his noon brandy, and said, "Well, if he can spare a moment from his ledger," in the voice people used about Garrick when they thought the apprentice couldn't hear. Ansel heard everything. Hearing everything and saying nothing was most of his trade.

She paid her sixpence for the estimate and left, and on her way out she paused at the counter, and Ansel braced himself, because he knew the pause.

"You're looking well, Ansel," she said, which was not what she had come in to say. "For all that's happened." *All that's happened* being his father, being the war in the east his mother had sailed back into and never returned from, being whatever else the town had decided had happened to him. She patted the counter twice, as one does to a horse or a child, and went.

Ansel stood a moment with his hand flat on the bench, feeling the vibration of forty clocks through his palm, and then he picked up the tweezers and went back to work.

* * *

Garrick emerged at half past two, smelling of brandy and ink, and stood in the doorway of the workshop with his thumbs hooked in his waistcoat, surveying the benches the way a landlord surveys tenants.

"The Trewin clock," he said.

"Striking wrong. Worn escape teeth. It'll want two days."

"It'll want tomorrow." Garrick crossed the room and stood behind Ansel's shoulder, close enough that Ansel could feel the warmth of him, and watched the wheelwork with the uncomprehending eye of a man who had spent thirty years among clocks and understood none of them. "Trewin asks questions. Trewin talks to the trustees. We give him his clock when we give it to him, not when the fancy takes us." He tapped the bench with one finger, twice, a habit Ansel hated with a quiet, bottomless hatred. "You've the hands for it. Use them."

*You've the hands for it.* It was, Ansel reflected, the nearest thing to praise Garrick produced, and it managed to praise his hands while implying that everything above the wrists was incidental. He had heard it a hundred times. *You've the hands for it, boy.* As though he were a pair of gloves Garrick had inherited and wore because new ones cost money.

"And the Penhale clock?" he asked.

"When there's time."

"There'll be time Thursday."

"There'll be time when I say." Garrick drifted toward the front, then paused, and Ansel felt the particular stillness that meant the day's real business was arriving. "There's been a letter."

"A letter."

"From the observatory." Garrick said the word the way he said all words connected with the observatory: with a thin varnish of deference over something harder. The observatory sat on the hill above the town, white dome catching the light, keeper's cottage tucked beneath it, and it was the one institution in the parish whose business Garrick could not afford to slight, because the observatory kept the harbor time, and the harbor time was law, and the men who enforced it had long memories and short tempers. "Their master clock has failed. Stopped dead, they say. Edmund Whitcombe writes asking—asking, mind, not telling—for someone to come up and see to it."

Ansel kept his eyes on the wheelwork. "I can go this afternoon."

"You can go when I've thought who ought to go." Garrick's finger tapped the bench again. "It's a day's work, likely two, up that hill and back, and the Whitcombes pay late and argue the bill. And their clock is a fine piece, French-built, temperamental. One wrong turn of a key and you've broken something worth more than you'll earn in a year."

"I know the movement. Father showed me the pattern once—he'd seen one in—"

"I don't care what your father saw." The words came out flat and quick, a door shut on a draft. Garrick never spoke of Kenji Nakamura if he could help it; the partnership's ending was a country he refused to draw maps of. He straightened his cuffs. "But it wants doing, and I can't climb ninety-nine steps these days with my knee, and Peters is useless with anything finer than a church bell. So it'll be you."

"Yes, sir."

"And you'll say nothing up there beyond what's needful. You're there to mend a clock, not to make conversation with the keeper's daughter. I know how girls of that house carry on—friendly as sparrows, all of them. You keep your head down and your mouth shut and you come back with the clock going and a signed docket. Understood?"

Understood. Ansel said it, and Garrick grunted and withdrew to his ledger, and the shop settled again into its ticking weather. But something had shifted in Ansel's chest, small and warm as a gear seating into place, and he recognized it with a kind of embarrassment: hope. Flicker of it, no more. A commission of his own—not sweeping, not estimates, not the jobs Garrick didn't want—a fine French movement, a master clock that governed the harbor, work that would be seen by people who mattered, done by hands that mattered even if nothing above the wrists supposedly did.

He caught himself smiling at the escapement and put the smile away, the way he put away most things.

* * *

He worked until the light failed. In October the dark came early off the sea, pressing gray faces to the windows, and the shop lamps drew their little yellow islands on the benches. Ansel finished the Trewin clock's striking train, oiled the pivot holes with a wire dipped in oil so fine it left barely a shine, and set the clock to run on the test stand, where it ticked with the smug steadiness of a thing that knows it has been forgiven.

While it ran he cleaned his tools. This was ritual more than necessity—his father's discipline, absorbed so deeply it had become the shape of his evenings: every file wiped, every screwdriver bladed and wrapped, the tweezers laid in their velvet grooves like instruments in a surgeon's case. His father's tools. The only inheritance that had survived the lawyers and the debts, and Garrick had let him keep them only because good tools cost money and dead men's tools come free.

Through the curtain he could hear Garrick adding figures aloud, the small satisfied sounds of a man balancing books that balanced in his favor. Ansel had never been invited to understand those books. He suspected he would not like them if he did. There was a way Garrick had of going quiet when certain customers came in—the ones from up the coast, the ones who paid in coin and took no receipts—and a way the shop's back room smelled of pipe smoke at odd hours, and a ledger page Ansel had glimpsed once, years ago, with columns that matched no clocks he'd ever repaired. He had looked away. Looking away was a skill he'd practiced so long it was nearly a virtue.

He was wrapping the last file when the knock came at the door—three raps, brisk, official—and a boy in customs-house blue stood on the step with a note, breathless from the hill.

"For Garrick's shop. For the clockmaker. It's marked urgent." The boy thrust it forward and was gone before Ansel had fully taken it, boots slapping down the lane.

The envelope bore the observatory's seal, pressed hard, and inside was a single line in a firm upright hand:

*The master clock stands still since dawn. Come tonight if you can come at all. — E.W.*

Tonight. Not tomorrow, not when convenient. Tonight, after dark, up ninety-nine steps to a hilltop where the wind came raw off the Channel, to a clock whose stopping was apparently urgent enough to spend a second letter on.

Ansel carried the note through the curtain. Garrick read it twice, and Ansel watched his master's face do something complicated—the calculation of fees against trouble, the flicker of fear of the observatory's trustees, the knee that couldn't climb—and settle at last into resignation.

"Well," Garrick said. "You'd best get up there before they send for Peters instead. Take the French tools. Take the lantern. And mind what I said—mend the clock, sign the docket, say nothing you don't have to." He was already turning back to his ledger when he added, without looking up, "And take your coat. Hilltop in October kills fools and apprentices alike."

Ansel got his coat. He packed the tool roll with the care of a man packing for a journey longer than an evening, blew out the bench lamps one by one, and stepped out into the street.

The town at night was a different animal. The shops were shuttered, the fish carts gone home, and the gas lamps burned in their halos of mist all the way down to the water, where the masts of the harbor moved gently against the lights like a congregation at prayer. Somewhere out in the roads a ship's bell rang the hour—eight o'clock, struck slow and deep—and Ansel realized, with a strange lurch, that the harbor bell and the observatory clock between them governed every hour of every life in this town, and one of those two voices had gone silent this morning, and he was the one being sent to find out why.

He began to walk. The hill rose ahead of him, black against the last gray of the sky, the observatory dome just visible above the trees like a pale closed eye. Ninety-nine steps, they said. He'd climbed them twice before, delivering parts with Garrick wheezing behind him, and had counted without meaning to, because counting was what his hands and mind did when they were nervous.

At the foot of the steps he paused and looked back once at the town, at the lit windows and the dark water and the shop where his father had worked and where he himself had spent three years learning to be invisible.

Then he put his hand on the iron rail, and began to climb.
